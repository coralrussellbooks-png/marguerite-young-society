Inviting the Muses, Part 9

← Part 8 · Inviting the Muses: A Reading, Part by Part · Parts 10 and 11 →

Part 9 covers the end of "The Anarchic Principle," then "Excursus on War," "Plumbing the Irrational," "Comfort's Work," "Joseph Smith, Mormon Prophet," "Metaphysical Fiction," the Sartre review, and most of "An Indiana Book." The 1945 pieces show something new about Young: three of them appeared in Conscientious Objector, the pacifist paper, which places her plainly on the side of war resisters in the last year of the war.

"The Anarchic Principle," conclusion (Sewanee Review, 1945)

The rest of the roundup is quick sorting. Elizabeth Hardwick's The Ghostly Lover is "scrupulously well done." A novel satirizing advertising fails because "satire, to be effective, must be intellectual." In Harry Sylvester's book about the New Mexico Penitents, the best scene is not the flagellation but priests playing cards, and Young ends dryly: "Ace, it seems, even when the players are priests, takes all."

"Excursus on War" (Conscientious Objector, 1945)

A review of Henry Miller's Murder the Murderer, a book whose publisher printed a disclaimer of responsibility for its views. Young defends Miller's right to speak ("there is nothing in the least immoral in what Miller has to say") and observes that after every war "a wave of conscientious objection seems always to start," while only the brave object during one, against the mentality of "Remember Pearl Harbor." Yet she finds Miller "a good deal less radical and less thoughtful than Alex Comfort." Miller still believes in a unity "God-given in nature" and in a guilty superman and innocent little men; his flaw is "romantic individualism, which also blows trumpets and waves flags." Comfort's The Power House, by contrast, shows "the death of individualism" in a mechanized civilization conditioning everyone. What Young wants from a pacifist is not heat but reason, "positive, forceful." It is a principled, unsentimental position.

"Plumbing the Irrational" (Conscientious Objector, 1945)

A short art review of Howard Mitcham at the Jane Street Gallery, a cooperative started by men from Civilian Public Service, the program for conscientious objectors. Young reads his painting through her usual philosophy: "Elephants dissolve into smoky light beams," "the red rose is not red," and there is "no absolute standard available to us." Mitcham "makes a game of our terror," and his art "evades the total logic of nonentity, populating vacuums with extraterrestrial refugees." The atomic bomb appears again, with its "diseased atoms in contagion," and she argues that only "the myth of infinite distended variety" can answer "the spiritual vacuity which our unthinking technicians have presented to us."

"Comfort's Work" (Conscientious Objector, 1945)

Young returns to Comfort a third time, reviewing his poetry collection The Song of Lazarus while complaining that The Power House never found its American audience. The Book-of-the-Month Club passed it over for Young'un, "the story of a little pioneer girl who made her own party dress." She suspects readers feared the novel encouraged conscientious objection, then writes one of the sharpest lines in the book: "Now that, since the atomic bomb, every man is a conscientious objector, or almost every man, that fear should certainly have dissolved like the doomed cities of Japan." She compares Comfort to the public Shelley and calls his poems "elegies for the human race."

"Joseph Smith, Mormon Prophet" (Nation, 1945)

Young had praised Fawn Brodie's No Man Knows My History in "Complex Life," and here she treats Smith's life as proof that America is anything but plain: "Nothing could be more baroque." He founded a church "by a series of accidents" that no one could have predicted. Her tone is amused and sly: unlike most angels of the time, Moroni "was not in favor of an arid celibacy," and "for every wife, another star in heaven." She gives Emma Smith her due, noting she "astutely objected to all this carnality." Set beside "Horse Utopia," it is another portrait of a male visionary building a world that serves him, though Smith gets more wonder than judgment.

The ending is mythic. Smith is killed at thirty-nine, and as a man rushes forward to cut off his head, "the sun came out from behind the cloud, and the people fled silently away." Young takes Brodie's title literally: he remains "unknown, unknowable," a mystery "even to himself."

"Metaphysical Fiction" (Kenyon Review, 1947)

McCullers has appeared throughout the book, and this is Young's full reading of The Member of the Wedding. She insists it is not what it first seems, "a study of turbulent adolescence," but "a curiously spiritual book" built on "carefully erected symbols." Frankie wants to join her brother's wedding in order "to break down all barriers of atomic individualism," which Young compares to "a union of all the nations," and Berenice tells her such a dream is possible "only inside her own creative head."

Her details are well chosen. Berenice married three times looking for her dead first husband, once for a man's thumb and once for the coat she had pawned. Berenice's ideal world has Black and white people living "equally and freely" and includes that lost husband. The three characters play three-handed bridge with cards missing from the start. The "wedding of three" echoes Riley's triangle in "The Old Sweetheart," all three loving each other and held apart. The ending turns on Frankie announcing that she "just loves Michelangelo," and Young's last line is perfectly flat: "Either she will grow up, or she will not grow up."

"To Be or Not" (Tiger's Eye, 1947)

The only truly scathing review so far, of Sartre's The Age of Reason. Young's objection is not to its pessimism but to its thinness. "The thesis that life is meaningless is, in itself, quite meaningless," she writes, since meaninglessness would need rich paradox, dream, and contradiction to be shown. Instead she finds "blank, empty, unvisionary pages" marked only by "the guilty thumbprints of Jean-Paul Sartre," characters who "live as paper dolls," and writing "all too near the slick," like a Hollywood version of France. Dreiser, in her view, knew "more of life's phantasmagoric base" than Sartre, and Sartre's pessimism is "only the reverse of greeting-card optimism," the same phrase she used against Merton's weaker poems. The complaint that there is "no buzz, no boom of reality" echoes her Moore essay's "buzzing and booming and blooming of existence," borrowed from William James.

"An Indiana Book" (first part)

A review of John Bartlow Martin's history of Indiana, and Young is on home ground. Martin hunts for the Hoosier "type," and she answers that "the 'type' is always a laziness of vision" and that Indiana "has given birth to many individuals but to no types." She teases him for insisting Hoosier is an insult, suggesting the habit might come from his being "born in Ohio," and offers a lovely correction: in Indiana people speak of "the outside world," not from isolationism "but through recognition of the interior world," where "the distance between Indianapolis and heaven" may be "shorter than that between Indianapolis and Cincinnati." Still, she calls it "an utterly delightful book" with a fine eye for "the strangest birds in Indiana's history." Part 9 breaks off in Martin's survey of Riley and Lew Wallace's Indianapolis.

Across part 9

The war pieces reveal Young's politics more directly than anything earlier: a pacifist who wants conscientious objection grounded in reason rather than romance, and who reads the atomic bomb as the end of every comfortable certainty. Her literary judgments follow the same line. She values McCullers and Comfort for their depth of symbol and their refusal of easy wholeness, and she dismisses Sartre not for doubting meaning but for writing doubt without any texture. The Indiana review returns to the argument of "The Midwest of Everywhere": individuals are real, types are a failure to look.
