Chapter 65

On Chapter 65 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Christianity and Religion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 24, 2026

← Previous · Religion in MMMD Home · Next →

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Titus Bonebreaker

Allusions: Angels and Archangels · Genesis · Judaism · Revelation and the Apocalypse · The Book of Job

“the old King James version which was, in her pale esteem, befogged”: The King James Bible (1611) was made at the order of James I, the Stuart king; Miss MacIntosh objects to it as she objects to all royalty, “even to king crabs, kingfishers, king posts.” See The Bible.

“And the Arabs had their topknots by which God might fish for them”: The topknot hairstyle has ancient origins in various cultures, often symbolizing status, maturity, or spiritual significance. Historically, it was adopted by samurai in Japan for practical reasons, such as securing their helmets, while in ancient China it was worn by scholars and nobility. In South Asian traditions, it holds religious meaning, and in Korean society, it was used to signify adulthood. Many Muslim men in Egypt once shaved the head but left a small tuft, the “shoosheh,” on the crown; Edward William Lane was told it was kept so that a Muslim slain by an unbeliever would not have his head carried in an unclean way (An Account of the Manners and Customs of the Modern Egyptians, 1836). The belief Miss MacIntosh repeats, that God would draw the faithful up to heaven by the tuft, is folk belief rather than Islamic teaching; the hadith forbid qaza’, shaving part of the head and leaving the rest (Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of Dress). Western travelers told of Muslim men, Turks especially, who shaved the head but for one lock by which they would be drawn up into paradise; Young gives the belief to “the Arabs.”

“to wrestle with the dark angel of death”: “And Jacob was left alone; and there wrestled a man with him until the breaking of the day” (Genesis 32:24, KJV). See Genesis. Earlier in Chapters 3, 6, 15 and 53.

Mr. Bonebreaker's long sermon is a tissue of scripture. He gathers "the stray lamb to the fold" (Isaiah 40, Luke 15), calls God's discipline love, "for whom the Lord loveth, the Lord chasteneth" (Hebrews 12), and promises a man will be "as the shadow of a great rock in a weary land" (Isaiah 32).

“as dead leaves are cast forth and burned”: “If a man abide not in me, he is cast forth as a branch, and is withered; and men gather them, and cast them into the fire, and they are burned” (John 15:6, KJV). See The Bible.

His apocalypse is Revelation's: the Last Day "with trumpets blowing and angels shouting," the good divided from the evil "as the sheep from the goats, as the wheat from the tares" (Matthew 25 and 13), the saved riding "into heaven on cherubim" through the ivory gates, the lake turned to blood and to "sea of crystal," the unsaved cast out like "whited sepulchres" (Matthew 23).

“that which eye has not seen and ear has not heard”: “Eye hath not seen, nor ear heard, neither have entered into the heart of man, the things which God hath prepared for them that love him” (1 Corinthians 2:9, KJV). See The Bible. Earlier in Chapters 2 and 6.

“and the numbers of the hairs in her head, and the least sparrow”: “But the very hairs of your head are all numbered” (Matthew 10:30, KJV), said to a woman who has none. See The Bible and Hair, Wig, and Baldness.

“lest we likewise perish”: “Except ye repent, ye shall all likewise perish” (Luke 13:3, KJV). See The Bible.

“Weep not, Hagar, for thy little son”: According to the Bible, Hagar was the Egyptian slave of Sarah, Abram’s wife. Sarah had been barren for a long time and sought a way to fulfill God’s promise that Abram would be father of many nations, especially since they had grown old, so she offered Hagar to Abram to be his concubine. Hagar bore Abraham’s son, Ishmael. “What aileth thee, Hagar? fear not” (Genesis 21:17, KJV). Where the King James has “he will be a wild man” (Genesis 16:12), the Hebrew reads “a wild ass of a man,” as Mr. Bonebreaker has it; see Ishmael in the Chapter 62 resources. See Genesis and Ancient Egypt. Earlier in Chapter 62.

His figures run through the whole of Scripture: Hagar and her son the "wild ass among men" (Genesis 16), Eve's fig leaf and Cain's murder of Abel, a God who "has reproved kings," the promise "to hoar hairs will I carry you" (Isaiah 46), and "God speaking out of the whirlwind" (Job 38).

Between prophecies he drops into the language of baseball, urging the crowd to "pitch for God's sake at the eleventh inning," and claims to have "put away childish things" and "triumphed over death," his reward a "heavenly pavilion." His creed is that "the perishing meat of this world" is "nought," and only "love will take away an aspect of death."

“when God prepared the heavens and set a compass upon the face of the depth”: “When he prepared the heavens, I was there: when he set a compass upon the face of the depth” (Proverbs 8:27, KJV). See The Bible.

“the ravens of the valley shall pluck us”: “The eye that mocketh at his father… the ravens of the valley shall pick it out” (Proverbs 30:17, KJV). See The Bible. Earlier in Chapter 37.

“Unfruitful are the works of darkness”: “And have no fellowship with the unfruitful works of darkness” (Ephesians 5:11, KJV). See The Bible.

“One woe is past”: “One woe is past; and, behold, there come two woes more hereafter” (Revelation 9:12, KJV). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.

He warns that God "is tired of man" and "will wipe old Chicago clean as a plate, just as He did the old Jerusalem, the old Nineveh," cities judged and destroyed, and that "men shall be crushed before the moth" (Job 4), for "the wrath of God is revealed from heaven" (Romans 1).

“we who are saved are not like to whited sepulchres”: “Ye are like unto whited sepulchres” (Matthew 23:27, KJV). See The Bible.

“man caught as a wild bull in a net”: “As a wild bull in a net” (Isaiah 51:20, KJV); God speaks “out of the whirlwind” in Job 38:1. See The Book of Job. Earlier in Chapters 14, 21 and 29.

“where the canker cannot touch us”: “Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth, where moth and rust doth corrupt” (Matthew 6:19, KJV). See The Bible. Earlier in Chapter 3.

“for his bones were out of joint”: “I am poured out like water, and all my bones are out of joint” (Psalm 22:14, KJV). This biblical reference is consistent with Mr. Bonebreaker’s character, who is deeply religious yet flawed and struggling, using scripture to make sense of his life and to assert his role as a preacher of impending judgment and salvation. The phrase encapsulates both his physical frailty and his spiritual earnestness in a poetic, scriptural metaphor. “I may tell all my bones” (Psalm 22:17, KJV).

“since in heaven nobody remembers anybody”: Mr. Bonebreaker tells Miss MacIntosh that in heaven he would not remember her or her face. His heaven erases earthly bonds and memory, a bleak promise to a woman already alone. “For in the resurrection they neither marry, nor are given in marriage” (Matthew 22:30, KJV). See On since in heaven nobody remembers anybody. Earlier in Chapter 6.

“our fathers have eaten sour grapes, that the worm is mother of us all”: “The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children’s teeth are set on edge” (Ezekiel 18:2, KJV); “to the worm, Thou art my mother, and my sister” (Job 17:14, KJV). See The Book of Job. Earlier in Chapters 10 and 18.

“the Egyptians were spoiled”: “And they spoiled the Egyptians” (Exodus 12:36, KJV): the Israelites, leaving Egypt, carried off the Egyptians’ silver and gold. See The Bible and Ancient Egypt. “the Egyptians were spoiled” implies that the Egyptians, once a mighty and revered people, became corrupt or morally ruined in his religious worldview. This phrase is part of Mr. Bonebreaker’s sweeping religious rhetoric about the decline of the world and the need for salvation before the Last Day.

“Babylon was sunken into the sea”: In Revelation Babylon is the great city of sin, thrown down by divine judgment, and Mr. Bonebreaker uses its fall as a sign of the coming end of the world. “And a mighty angel took up a stone like a great millstone, and cast it into the sea, saying, Thus with violence shall that great city Babylon be thrown down” (Revelation 18:21, KJV). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.

“he had dwelled for a long time in the Land of Ham”: In the Psalms the land of Ham is Egypt: “Israel also came into Egypt; and Jacob sojourned in the land of Ham” (Psalm 105:23, KJV). See The Bible and Ancient Egypt.

“God gave snow like wool”: “He giveth snow like wool: he scattereth the hoarfrost like ashes” (Psalm 147:16, KJV). See The Bible.

“her hands were the pureness”: “Consider the lilies of the field, how they grow; they toil not, neither do they spin” (Matthew 6:28, KJV). See The Bible.

“She had gone down into the valley of the shadow to get him”: “Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death” (Psalm 23:4, KJV). See The Bible. Earlier in Chapters 6, 43, 45 and 58.

“Her head was filled with dew, he said”: Mr. Bonebreaker courts her with the Song of Solomon: “my head is filled with dew” (5:2), “thy hair is as a flock of goats” (4:1), “Thy neck is as a tower of ivory” (7:4), “Many waters cannot quench love” (8:7), and Job’s “eyelids of the morning” (41:18), all KJV. Every compliment is to her hair or her beauty. See The Bible and Hair, Wig, and Baldness.

“John Knox MacIntosh, her father”: John Knox MacIntosh and Mercy MacIntosh are Georgia MacIntosh’s parents. See On John Knox MacIntosh. John Knox was a Scottish minister, Reformed theologian, and writer who was a leader of the country’s Reformation. He was the founder of the Church of Scotland. Young claimed descent from John Knox on her mother’s side. While writing the novel she “discovered that the Macintoshes, this really astonished me, were descendants from a prince, a bald prince, who was washed up on the shores of Scotland,” so that “Miss Macintosh is really a bald prince, disguised with a red wig and the woman’s gear” (Marguerite Young, interview, WNYC radio, New York). See John Knox MacIntosh, Miss MacIntosh, and Marguerite Young on Her Craft.

“the mountains full of horses”: “The mountain was full of horses and chariots of fire” (2 Kings 6:17, KJV). See The Bible.

“Miss MacIntosh was the brightest star in his crown”: Revival hymns promised the soul-winner a star in his crown for each soul saved, as in Eliza Hewitt’s “Will There Be Any Stars in My Crown?” (1897), after “they that turn many to righteousness as the stars for ever and ever” (Daniel 12:3, KJV). See American Religious Movements. “The star of the Turk” most likely refers to the crescent and star on the Ottoman flag and emblems. It could also recall the Gallipoli Star (Ottoman War Medal), a WWI Turkish military decoration for bravery, or the famous Mechanical Turk, an 18th-century chess-playing automaton.

“the star of the dirty Jew”: “Dirty Jew” is a widely recognized and offensive antisemitic slur with historical roots in hate speech and discrimination. The term is a standard antisemitic taunt used in real-world attacks and verbal abuse against Jewish people. During the Holocaust, the yellow Star of David identification marker that Jews were forced to wear encouraged people to use slurs like “dirty Jew” and to harass them. The phrase draws on ancient anti-Jewish themes that falsely associated Jews with filth or spreading disease. Miss MacIntosh objects, “quite flatly,” that the Jews should not be singled out. See Judaism.

“the neighborhoods of Old Jewry”: “Old Jewry” in London was the center of England’s medieval Jewish community, thriving from the Norman Conquest (1066) through the 13th century as financiers and merchants, but faced increasing persecution, discriminatory laws like the 1275 Statute of Jewry, and accusations (e.g., coin clipping), culminating in King Edward I’s expulsion of all Jews from England in 1290, ending centuries of Jewish life in that area until their readmission under Cromwell in 1657. In Chicago the Jewish neighborhood around Maxwell Street on the Near West Side was known from the 1880s as the ghetto. See Judaism.

“first the sermon and then the soup”: Rescue missions such as Chicago’s Pacific Garden Mission (1877) fed the poor after the sermon, not before. The Salvation Army promised “soup, soap, and salvation,” and Joe Hill’s union song “The Preacher and the Slave” (1911) mocked the arrangement as “pie in the sky.” See American Religious Movements.

“It was most deranging. The yoke of God”: “Take my yoke upon you” (Matthew 11:29, KJV).

He turns even Miss MacIntosh's body into text, quoting the Song of Solomon over her mastectomy, "we have a little sister and she hath no breasts," bidding the barren rejoice and "enlarge their baldness like the eagle" (Micah 1), for "God loves the shorn lamb."

“the barren who had borne seven times”: “So that the barren hath born seven” (1 Samuel 2:5, KJV); “Rejoice, thou barren that bearest not” (Galatians 4:27, KJV). See The Bible.

“He was on the baseball diamond again”: Compare Billy Sunday, who played outfield for the Chicago White Stockings, was converted at Chicago’s Pacific Garden Mission in 1886, and became the most famous revival preacher in America, preaching in the slang of the ballpark. See American Religious Movements.

“Chicago was as fat as grease”: “Their heart is as fat as grease” (Psalm 119:70, KJV); “What ailed thee, O thou sea, that thou fleddest?” (Psalm 114:5, KJV). See The Bible.

“Love lifted me”: The hymn “Love Lifted Me,” written by James Rowe in 1912 with music by Howard E. Smith, tells a story of salvation using the biblical account of Peter walking on water (Matthew 14) as a metaphor for being pulled from sin by Jesus’s love, with Smith composing the melody despite severe arthritis, creating a powerful testament to divine rescue and devotion. See On Love Lifted Me and Music and Composers.

“In his father’s house were many mansions”: “In my Father’s house are many mansions” (John 14:2, KJV). See The Bible. Earlier in Chapters 26 to 28 and 39.

“the locusts have no king”: “The locusts have no king, yet go they forth all of them by bands” (Proverbs 30:27, KJV). See The Bible. Earlier in Chapter 35.

“As in water, face answers to face”: “As in water face answereth to face, so the heart of man to man” (Proverbs 27:19, KJV). See The Bible.

“his was the shield of the unicorn”: In medieval art, unicorns were tamed only by virgins, making them symbols of purity, often featured on shields alongside figures like the Virgin Mary. The unicorn, often chained and rearing, appears in heraldry, notably with the lion in the British Royal Arms and Canadian symbols, representing royal power.

“He was poor as Tobit’s dog”: In the Book of Tobit, found in the Apocrypha of the 1611 King James Bible and in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles, a dog faithfully accompanies Tobias, Tobit’s son, and the angel Raphael on their journey: “So they went forth both, and the young man’s dog with them” (Tobit 5:16, KJV). In Christianity, dogs represent faithfulness. See The Bible.

“the day he figured would be the day after the world would end”: Compare the Millerites, who followed William Miller’s prophecy that Christ would return in 1843 or 1844; when October 22, 1844, passed, they called it the Great Disappointment. The Seventh-day Adventists, who grew out of them, also urged a meatless diet, as Mr. Bonebreaker keeps. See American Religious Movements. Religious food restrictions, seen across Judaism (Kashrut), Islam (Halal/Haram), Hinduism (no beef), Jainism (strict vegetarianism, no roots), and early Christianity (abstain from idol-sacrificed food, blood), have ancient roots, often linked to divine command, health/sanitation (pork, blood), ecological reasons, or cultural identity, evolving over time from specific scriptural interpretations (like Jesus declaring all foods clean) to broader ethical principles like non-violence, shaping practices from what to eat to how it’s prepared. Religions emphasizing vegetarianism include Jainism (which mandates it for non-violence), Buddhism and Hinduism (strong traditions rooted in Ahimsa or non-violence), and certain Christian denominations like the Seventh-day Adventists, alongside practices in Sikhism, some Jewish traditions, and Eastern Orthodox Christianity during fasts. The core principles often involve compassion for animals, spiritual purity, and minimal harm (Ahimsa). Vegetarians, and vegans especially, must find other sources for nutrients most plentiful in animal foods, such as vitamin B12, iron, zinc, and omega-3 fats. Some anthropologists hold that eating meat, and later cooking, fueled the growth of the human brain (Richard Wrangham, Catching Fire: How Cooking Made Us Human, 2009). Flag: after 1964, Wrangham (2009). See Buddhism and Hinduism.

“Instead of a girdle, there would be a rent”: “And it shall come to pass, that instead of sweet smell there shall be stink; and instead of a girdle a rent; and instead of well set hair baldness” (Isaiah 3:24, KJV). See The Bible and Hair, Wig, and Baldness.

“He himself had often said, quoting the Bible, that the wife should stand forth before her husband”: “Whose adorning let it not be that outward adorning of plaiting the hair, and of wearing of gold… But let it be the hidden man of the heart” (1 Peter 3:3-4, KJV). See The Bible and Hair, Wig, and Baldness.

“You, too, can be washed as white as the new-fallen snow”: “Though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow” (Isaiah 1:18, KJV). See The Bible. Earlier in Chapter 47.

“much like the blind leading the blind”: “And if the blind lead the blind, both shall fall into the ditch” (Matthew 15:14, KJV). See Eyes and Blindness.

“a Moody’s Bible chained to a post”: Young’s “Moody’s Bible” recalls Dwight L. Moody, the Chicago evangelist whose Bible Institute opened in 1886; the Gideons began placing Bibles in hotel rooms in 1908. See American Religious Movements.

His apocalypse keeps its images to the end, "the end of the world... like a shadow running backward," the sun going out and the stars falling and "the serpent bite without enchantment."
