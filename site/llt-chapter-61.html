Chapter 61

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: The Margins Reveal the Truth

The Questions

For Mr. Spitzer “his night was his day, and his day was his night.” What is left of an opposition once each side becomes the other?

An old mute “spoke only with his hands,” then one night speaks the Lord’s Prayer aloud. What has happened to the line between the silent and the speaking?

Why does the book put this exchange in a thieves’ café, among the outcast and the night people?

If every pair folds into itself, is there any fixed point left to stand on?

The chapter opens on oppositions folding into each other until none of them holds. For Mr. Spitzer “his night was his day, and his day was his night, for his twilight was his dawn… and his beginning was his end.” Every pair that is supposed to sort experience, night and day, dawn and dusk, beginning and end, has collapsed, each side becoming its opposite. This is the dissolving of distinctions the book has tracked in him, carried to a kind of totality: not one line blurred but every line, the grid of opposites folded flat, so that there is no fixed point of orientation anywhere in him. He lives where the categories have stopped dividing anything.

The mute who speaks is the chapter’s sharpest instance. Mr. Spitzer meets in the thieves’ café “an old mute” who “spoke only with his hands, his nervous hands continually making signs,” and then, one night out walking, the mute “had suddenly spoken” aloud, the Lord’s Prayer coming “out of the shadows.” The line between the mute and the speaking, as fixed a category as any, does not hold either. Language does not sit where it is supposed to; the silent man carries speech, the hands that were his only voice give way to a voice, and the built distinction between one who cannot speak and one who can comes apart like all the others. The grammar of who has a voice is itself a construction, and it slips.

The setting carries the book’s standing claim. This folding of every opposition, this mute who speaks, happens in a thieves’ café among the outcast, the night people, the ones the settled day has no place for. The Margins Reveal the Truth holds: it is at the margin, in the company of the mute and the thief, that the constructed quality of the ordinary distinctions shows plainest. The center keeps its opposition crisp because it needs them; at the edge, where the categories were never a good fit, they are seen to fold, and Mr. Spitzer, himself a night creature by now, sees the folding from inside the company that lives past the day’s divisions.

So the chapter leaves no fixed point, and treats the leaving as truth rather than loss. A man whose day is his night and whose mute companion speaks has nothing steady to orient by, no pair he can trust to stay sorted. This is not vertigo presented as breakdown but the honest end of a long unsealing: once one stops believing the oppositions are written into the world, they fold, and what remains is not a firmer ground beneath them but the folding itself, lived in. Mr. Spitzer stands nowhere fixed, among people the day discards, watching the distinctions dissolve, and the chapter asks him to stand nowhere firmer than that.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Mr. Spitzer’s folded oppositions for mere confusion, and the reading that takes the speaking mute for a simple miracle to marvel at. Neither the confusion nor the marvel is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that the oppositions sorting experience, night and day, beginning and end, are constructions that fold once one stops believing them written into the world, so that no fixed point remains and the folding itself is what is lived in; that the line between the mute and the speaking is one more such construction, slipping when the silent man finds a voice; and that the margin, the thieves’ café, is where the folding shows plainest, the edge seeing the seams the center keeps crisp. The distinctions are made, not found, and they fold at the edge. Mr. Spitzer stands nowhere fixed among the night people, and the chapter offers him no steady pair to sort the dark by.

For Reflection

Which oppositions that seem written into the world would fold if one stopped believing in them?

What fixed distinction in one’s own life, silent and speaking, waking and sleeping, has quietly stopped dividing anything?

Why is the constructed quality of the ordinary categories clearest from the margin rather than the center?
