Characters: Esther Longtree

Definition from research

This is the great grotesque comic set piece of the closing stretch, the little toy salesman. He is a fully grown man who behaves as an infant. He drinks milk "from a baby bottle" warmed to skin temperature, needs his chin wiped, "could not tie his own shoe-laces," bawls, waddles "like an old diapered baby," waves a toy popgun, and has only three teeth "like tiny snowdrops." Esther, ever maternal, plans to marry him and correct his habits, teaching him "by light blows" to sit still, tie his laces, and above all "to shed tears," since he has no tear ducts. The romance between a woman perpetually pregnant with nothing and a man who is essentially a baby is pure grotesque comedy.

His shipwreck monologue extends it into a grand comic catastrophe. He sits safe in a rubber life boat "tipping his straw hat to women and children whose faces and hands crowded the sea," politely bidding each drowning passenger goodbye. The doomed ship is stocked with absurd figures: four cardsharps who cheat with mirrors and discover "there was a way for all to win," each holding "the perfect flush" as they go down; "a woman who thought that she was two women"; a glass-eyed oculist who insists he sees better with his glass eyes; and a mad German captain who, sinking, keeps conducting, his withered arm lifting to produce "more beautiful music than ever was heard by the living." The whole drowning becomes a stately, ridiculous funeral. It should be said that the salesman's monologue also curdles into paranoid, hateful raving about the sea and sky as a conspiracy against him, a delusion Young presents as the ugly, monstrous underside of the comic figure rather than as anything to laugh with. The grotesquerie and the menace are deliberately inseparable.
