Chapter 55: Carpe Diem / Hoc Opus, Hic Labor Est

Research notes: Chapter 55

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Mr. Mouse · Peron Spitzer

Themes: Duality and Doubles · Music, Sound, and Silence

Symbols: Horses and Carriages · Music and Instruments · The House · Twins and Doubles · Weddings and Funerals

Allusions: Ancient Egypt · Gambling and Playing Cards · Music and Composers · Suicide in History and Literature · Women's Suffrage

Peron's motto was carpe diem: seize the day. Appropriate for a gambler, a chameleon, a man whose entire philosophy was the embrace of the present moment and the absolute indifference to consequence.

Joachim's motto was hoc opus, hic labor est: this is the work, this is the labor. It comes from Virgil's Aeneid: spoken by the Sibyl when she tells Aeneas that descending to the underworld is easy, but returning to the upper air, that is the task, that is the work. The return. The difficult part is not the going down but the coming back.

Young gives the brothers these two mottoes and lets them stand without commentary. They are not just personality types; they are philosophical positions on what a life is for. Peron: the moment seized and released, over and over, no accumulation, no residue, pure now. Joachim: the work sustained, the labor endured, the descent made and the attempt to return. One died by apparent suicide. The other is still in the underworld, still trying to find the way back up.

What actually happens in Chapter 55

Chapter 55 gives us the most complete biographical account of the Spitzer brothers that the novel offers: their life together in the twin house, the financial disaster of Peron's death, and the specific shape of the resentment and grief that has organized Joachim's existence ever since.

The house was extraordinary: symmetrical, mirrored, every room doubled. Twin portraits, twin gardens, twin arrangements of objects: a house built to contain two lives simultaneously, to give each brother his half without requiring them to overlap. Joachim planned it meticulously. He envisioned the life it would support: the music, the philately, the careful conservative investments that would preserve enough stability to allow the work to continue. He designed the music as a tomb: an immortal structure in sound that would outlast his physical existence.

Peron disrupted all of this. He borrowed money he did not repay. He forged checks in Joachim's name. He accumulated debts that he did not acknowledge as debts, because for Peron debt was simply another form of tomorrow's problem, and tomorrow was not real to a person who lived entirely in the present tense. When Peron died, Joachim paid for the funeral. He paid the debts. He went bankrupt. He lost the twin house. He moved into a beachcomber's house.

And then he wondered, as he has been wondering through every chapter since, which brother had really died.

The twin house

Young's image of the twin house (each room mirrored, every arrangement doubled, the structure built to make two lives symmetrical) is the architectural rendering of what the brothers were supposed to be: complementary halves of a single whole, each one complete, each one matching the other without requiring either to dissolve.

Joachim designed it. He is, remember, the orderly one: the man who draws maps, makes plans, tries to exclude chance. The twin house is the map he made for the life he intended to live. It has his music and his stamps and his careful china shepherdesses and his butterfly collection and his conservative investments in gilts and estates. It is a house designed to support the work.

Peron lived in the other half and did not treat it as a mirror. He borrowed Joachim's half. He forged documents with Joachim's name. He introduced chance into every room. The house that was designed to make the two lives symmetrical became, under Peron's occupancy, a house where one half constantly threatened the structural integrity of the other.

The house is gone now. Joachim lost it paying the debts his brother left. He lives in a beachcomber's house: the opposite of the twin house in every way: improvised, coastal, exposed, belonging to whoever washes up. He is the beachcomber, picking through what remains after the tide.

Peron's forgeries

That Peron forged checks in Joachim's name is not simply a betrayal. It is the specific shape of the identity confusion that has haunted every chapter since.

A forged check in Joachim's name is Peron signing as Joachim. It is Peron making himself Joachim, at least on paper, at least for long enough to extract the money. This is the literal version of everything the novel has been asking about their relationship: which one is the real Spitzer? Which signature is authentic? When Peron signs Joachim's name, is the check more or less counterfeit than any other document? All money was counterfeit, Mr. Spitzer thinks: “turned out at the great Mint,” valuable only because an institution said so. Perhaps all signatures are the same: assigned value, not inherent truth.

Joachim was furious. He was invisible to Peron, never acknowledged, never appreciated, his order and sacrifice treated as infrastructure to be used and ignored. The resentment in this chapter is real and carefully rendered by Young. She does not let Joachim be simply noble. He is also wounded. He is also the person who planned everything carefully and watched it be dismantled by someone who did not understand that plans had value.

The music as tomb

Joachim's great project (the opus, the dirge, the elegy) was designed from the beginning as a tomb. Not a tomb in the sense of a mausoleum, cold and sealed, but a tomb in the sense of the ancient Egyptian model: a structure built to sustain life beyond death, equipped with everything the person would need to continue existing in another form. His music was to be the architecture of his survival.

Young gives this serious and exact weight. The composition was not a vanity project. It was Joachim's version of what Cousin Hannah's suffrage campaigns were: a structure built to outlast the body that created it, a form of being-in-the-world that could continue after the physical self had ended. The music as tomb is the musician's version of the legacy problem. It is, among other things, the thing Mr. Mouse is eating.

The curse of the ninth hovers over it: the superstition, rooted in the deaths of Beethoven, Schubert, and others after completing a ninth symphony, that the ninth will be the composer's last. Joachim knows the curse. He has organized his life around surviving long enough to finish the work. The bankruptcy, the loss of the house, the move to the beachcomber's cottage: all of these have set back the schedule. He is still in the middle. He has not finished. The tomb is incomplete.

Which brother really died

This is the question the chapter turns over at its close, and it is the question the novel has been building toward since the first hints about the Spitzer twins in the early chapters.

Peron died by apparent suicide. Since Peron's death, Joachim has been living at a second remove, absorbing Peron's knowledge and world, being mistaken for Peron by people who knew them both. He has been using Peron's existence as the scaffolding for what remains of his own. He has been, in the language of philosophy, becoming his brother.

Which means: who is the person living in the beachcomber's house? Is it the man who planned the twin house and designed the music as a tomb and lived by hoc opus, hic labor est? Or is it the man whose motto was carpe diem, who forged the checks and accumulated the debts and moved through every environment like a chameleon, and who now inhabits the cautious twin's body because the cautious twin's body is what survived?

Young does not decide. The novel has never decided. The question which brother really died is the question whose answer would change everything about how you read all the preceding chapters, and Young preserves the ambiguity because both answers are true in their way. The reckless twin died. The cautious twin survived by becoming, in some sense, the reckless one. Or: the cautious twin died in the bankruptcy and the loss and the long years of the second remove, and what is left is the ghost of the reckless one, still gambling, still trying to seize a day that keeps arriving a minute late.

The skeletal coachman

The chapter gives us an image of Joachim unknowingly traveling in the wrong carriage, driven by a skeletal coachman through unfamiliar landscapes. He is the wrong passenger. He does not know where he is going. The coachman does not explain.

The skeletal coachman is the novel's most direct invocation of Death as driver: the Etruscan Charun, who ferries souls across the boundary, or the danse macabre's Death who leads everyone in procession. But Joachim is not being driven to death exactly. He is being driven through a landscape that no longer corresponds to any map he made. He is the wrong passenger because the right passenger, the Joachim who designed the twin house and planned the music and drew the precise financial arrangements, is not the person who ended up in the carriage. The person in the carriage is whatever survived the bankruptcy and the forgeries and the grief. The coachman drives on regardless. The landscape is unfamiliar. The destination is unknown.

What to hold onto

Chapter 55 is where we finally understand, in practical terms, what happened to Mr. Spitzer. The twin house, the debts, the forgeries, the bankruptcy, the move to the beachcomber's cottage; these are the specific material events that produced the liminal figure wandering the fog-bound city a minute late, carrying an alpenhorn and composing an elegy in scraps. He did not begin this way. He was methodical. He had a plan. The plan was sabotaged from inside the house, by the other half of himself, who did not believe in plans.

What to carry forward: the two mottoes, which are the chapter's philosophical spine. Carpe diem versus hoc opus, hic labor est. The seized moment versus the sustained work. The man who lived entirely in the present versus the man who organized his life around a future he would never fully reach. One is gone. The other is still in the underworld, still trying to find the way back up, still doing the labor that the Sibyl named as the difficult part: not the descent, but the return.
