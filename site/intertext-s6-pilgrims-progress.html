Definition from research

Category: obligatory. The novel builds on Bunyan's allegory, and a reader who does not know it misses part of the meaning.

John Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress, published in 1678, follows Christian from the City of Destruction along a narrow road to the Celestial City. On the way he passes the Slough of Despond and other trials. It was for centuries one of the most widely read books in the English language, and a child of Miss MacIntosh's generation would have known it almost as well as the Bible.

In the novel it is Miss MacIntosh's chosen guide. She prefers it even to Scripture, because "the Word of God was that which should be taken with a grain of salt," whereas "old Bunyan was fairly safe even though not altogether trustworthy" (chapter 3). She reads life through Bunyan's map. The world is "a guide through this great wilderness, this mortal life which was a path of thorns and very narrow and very uncertain" (chapter 3). She speaks of her own past as "wallowing in a Slough of Despond, almost the gutter itself" (chapter 3). She and Vera are "two grey pilgrims climbing the naked rock" (chapter 18).

Young keeps Bunyan's road and removes its ends. The book itself is found on the beach, "water-logged with marks of sea shells on its pages" (chapter 3). In chapter 63 Vera remembers Miss MacIntosh as "the surefooted Pilgrim, the one person never dreaming where all might dream." In that same chapter Miss MacIntosh names the journey for what it has become: "ours is a Pilgrim's Progress from nowhere to nowhere."

Bunyan promises arrival. Young refuses it, and yet she does not mock the pilgrim. Miss MacIntosh still urges that we walk "as if we knew where we were walking" (chapter 63). The essay "The Drowned Guide" in Section 5 follows the same pattern through her other guidebooks.
