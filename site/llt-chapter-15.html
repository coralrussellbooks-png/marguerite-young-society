Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 15

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

The Questions

Vera thinks “if everything should be false and nothing true, then all false things should be true.” Is that nonsense, or the sealed idea of truth coming apart?

She asks what Miss MacIntosh could “know of life as it might be lived.” What does the blueprint miss that the sea knows?

Why does Vera turn toward the “outer darkness” and the “roaring tide” rather than the safe house?

On the night she rejects the “hackneyed proverbs,” what is she reaching for instead?

On her fourteenth birthday Vera lets the sealed idea of truth come apart in her hands. “What if everything should be false and nothing true…Then all false things should be true.” It reads like a riddle, but it is the finished blueprint of truth dissolving: once you stop believing there is a single fixed true-and-false laid into the world, the rigid scaffold falls, and what looked false and what looked true trade places. This is the unfreezing of the last chapter carried further, from farewell into active doubt.

She turns that doubt on Miss MacIntosh directly. For all her wisdom and her stock of facts, “what did she know of life as it might be lived?” Vera asks. The blueprint knows the tons of coal and the correct time; it does not know the “undertow which carries away the strongest swimmer.” Vera is naming exactly what the sealed construction cannot hold, the depth, the current, the life that exceeds the tally, and she is refusing to mistake the tally for the whole thing.

So she turns toward the edge. She wants out of the house of “old people” and “adult fantasies,” out into the “outer darkness,” the “tumultuous sea,” the “roaring tide.” She is no longer afraid of the dark, and the loss of that fear is the loss of the sealed house’s hold on her. The edge, the darkness and the water, is where she now expects to find life as it might be lived, because the settled center has just been shown not to know it.

And she faces death without the proverbs. The “white skull drifting” moon, the “black, whirling waters,” Miss MacIntosh’s own sayings that “our cradle stands in the grave,” Vera meets with laughter rather than dread. She has thrown out the “hackneyed proverbs and moral aphorisms” that would package mortality into something manageable, and what she reaches for instead is the direct thing, unpackaged, the sea and the skull and the immensity, held open rather than sealed into a saying.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Vera’s doubt for adolescent nihilism, a mere rejection of everything, and the reading that takes her turn to the sea and the dark for a death-wish. She is not embracing meaninglessness or courting the end.

The footing is that the sealed idea of truth is what comes apart here, not truth itself, and its coming-apart frees Vera to reach for life as it might be lived, the undertow the blueprint cannot count. The edge, the darkness and the tide, is the place of that reaching, and the discarded proverbs are the sealed packages she will no longer mistake for the thing. The fixed true-and-false is a construction, and the life that matters exceeds it. Vera turns toward the open sea and the chapter lets her go without telling us what she finds there.

For Reflection

What comes apart when one stops believing truth is a fixed thing laid into the world?

What does the tally of facts about a life leave out of the living of it?

What proverb has one used to package something, grief, death, love, that would be truer left unpackaged?
