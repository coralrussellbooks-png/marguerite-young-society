In the research: Chapter 31, Chapter 32, Chapter 64, Chapter 69

Definition from research

Chapter 31

  • Ecclesiastes 6:12 states life is short and meaningless, and it fades away like a shadow. It serves as a poignant reminder of the human condition's vulnerability and the pervasive sense of impermanence that haunts the characters throughout the book.

Chapter 32

  • Ecclesiastes 1:18 “For in much wisdom is much grief: and he that increaseth knowledge increaseth sorrow” The paradox of knowledge - challenges the common belief that knowledge is always a positive thing. It acknowledges that wisdom, while valuable, might also bring greater awareness of the problems, and limitations of human existence. It serves to underscore the novel's exploration of wisdom as a double-edged sword: enlightenment brings clarity but also increases one's sensitivity to life's tragedies and existential sorrows. It expresses the tragic insight that knowledge and sorrow are intimately linked, and that to be truly wise is to be deeply aware of life's inherent griefs. The verse encapsulates a key philosophical reflection in the novel: that the pursuit of wisdom and knowledge, rather than providing comfort, often exposes one to the profound sorrows of human existence.

Chapter 64

  • “Vanity, vanity, all is vanity…” Ecclesiastes 1:2-11 "Vanity of vanities, saith the Preacher, vanity of vanities; all is vanity." This phrase conveys the preacher’s meditation on the fleeting nature of worldly things and human endeavors under the sun. It expresses the idea that much of what people strive for (wealth, wisdom, pleasure) is ultimately empty or futile because it does not provide lasting meaning or satisfaction. It is used by Miss MacIntosh to reflect on the futility and emptiness she perceives in life, especially in relation to her own condition and experiences. The phrase captures a sense of existential weariness and the recognition that many pursuits and concerns, such as appearances, social acceptance, or even personal desires, are ultimately transient and meaningless.

Chapter 69

  • "Naked, we come into this world, and naked, we go out of it." The quote comes from the Bible, found in both Ecclesiastes 5:15 and Job 1:21. The phrase reflects the inevitability of human vulnerability at birth and death, stripping away all pretenses or material concerns, which is a poignant theme running through the novel’s exploration of mortality, identity, and human frailty.
