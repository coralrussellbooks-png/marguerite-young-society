Chapter 77

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Esther Longtree

Pillars: Grief Doesn't End

The Questions

Esther asks “who had killed the heavens… who had killed the green earth… who had killed the love.” What is grief doing when it asks who killed the world?

She decides “astronomy… was to blame,” her children “formations… hailstones.” Why does the loss diffuse outward into the stars?

The old Doctor said “all was wrong with the world, a broken clock.” What is a grief that indicts the whole of time?

Her cries are “not heard even by her.” What is a sorrow that outruns even the one who feels it?

The chapter opens on grief asking the largest possible question of blame. “Who had killed the heavens, rolling them up like a scroll, and who had killed the green earth… and who had killed the love which could not defend itself.” Esther’s loss has grown until it is not the death of her children only but the death of the heavens, the earth, love itself, and she asks who is responsible for all of it. This is Grief Doesn't End at its most cosmic reach: a sorrow so large it indicts creation, that cannot find a killer small enough to blame and so charges the whole world with the murder, the heavens rolled up, the earth killed, the love struck down unheard.

The blame diffuses outward into the stars because there is nowhere smaller for it to land. Esther decides “astronomy… was to blame—the stars, the vortices,” that her children were “formations… hailstones,” natural phenomena of a cosmos gone wrong rather than losses caused by anyone. The grief cannot find a guilty party, as the last chapter showed, and so it climbs, past men, past the ghostly child, all the way up to the stars, blaming astronomy, blaming the vortices, blaming the physics of the universe. This is what grief does when the loss exceeds any human cause: it diffuses into the cosmos, holds the stars responsible, because a loss this size can be laid at no smaller door.

The whole of time stands indicted. The old Doctor’s verdict, that “all was wrong with the world, a broken clock,” makes the diagnosis total: not a particular wrong but the wrongness of everything, time itself broken, the clock of the world stopped or running false. Esther’s grief has reached the point where it does not blame an event within time but time itself, the whole broken mechanism, “the snowy Poles… being murdered by the snowy Poles,” the world at war with itself. This is grief that has stopped looking for a cause inside the world and started indicting the world entire, the broken clock, the wrong creation, the cosmos that produces stillbirths as it produces hailstones.

And the sorrow outruns even the one who feels it. Esther’s cries are “not heard even by her,” the grief so vast it exceeds her own capacity to register it, crying past the crier, a woe larger than the woman who carries it. This is the furthest the book takes Grief Doesn't End: not only untetherable, not only cosmic, but exceeding even its own sufferer, a sorrow so large that the one who feels it cannot fully hear it. Esther stifles cries she cannot herself hear, grieving a loss that has outgrown her, and the chapter holds her there, at the edge of a grief that has become bigger than the person, bigger than the cause, as big as the broken world.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Esther’s cosmic blame for mere delusion, a madwoman faulting the stars, and the reading that would shrink her grief back to a manageable, locally caused sorrow. Neither the delusion nor the manageable loss is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that grief can grow until it indicts creation itself, asking who killed the heavens and the earth and love, a sorrow too large to find a killer small enough to blame; that the blame diffuses into the stars because a loss this size can be laid at no smaller door, the children become formations and hailstones of a cosmos gone wrong; that the whole of time stands indicted, the broken clock, the wrong world; and that the sorrow outruns even the one who feels it, cries not heard even by her. Some grief is cosmic, uncaused, and larger than its sufferer. Esther grieves a loss bigger than herself and the world, and the chapter does not cut it down to size.

For Reflection

What does grief reach for when the loss is too large to blame on any person or event?

Is there a sorrow that indicts not a wrong within the world but the whole broken world itself?

Can a grief grow so large that it outruns even the one who carries it?
