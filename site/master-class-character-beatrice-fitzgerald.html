Beatrice Fitzgerald: The Guide at the Washtub

← Mrs. Hathaway · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · The Stone Deaf Man →

Characters: Beatrice Fitzgerald · Homer Capehorn · Jock Cartwheel · Justice O’Leary · Madge Capehorn · Moses Hunnecker · Musidora Tidings · The Stone Deaf Man · Vera Cartwheel

She hauls the town's dirty linen through the snow on a child's red sled, half blind and turning more blind, while two strangers on a bus argue about whether she is clean or filthy, beloved or a hag, a mother or a woman who never had a son. Beatrice Fitzgerald is the laundress of the Indiana town, and she is assembled entirely out of that argument, because being made of two accounts that cannot both be true, by people who never once looked at her.

Homer's Beatrice is a saint of labor. "She's clean," he says. She "washes the clothes so clean," and "no one ever worked so hard," and in winter she drags the wash "on a little red sled over the snow," "up the hill and down the hill," until "her face turns blue." Madge's Beatrice is the reverse. She is "one-eyed," "the ugliest old hag for miles," "nearly blind, so what can she see," her clothes "streaked with dirt." She is "losing the other eye," and "she never did want anybody to see her," and so "she turns out the light." Both are speaking of the same old woman dragging the same sled, and the book lets neither win.

Ask the wrong question and Beatrice is a scrap of local color, a poor laundress the bus passes in the dark. Ask the right one and she carries two of the book's claims at once. She is the second pillar of Radical Immanence, reality constructed rather than found, built out of contradictory rumor the way Mrs. Tidings is built out of an invented and murdered husband. And she is the fourth, the truth at the margin, the person beneath all notice, sister to Musidora and to the clam-digger, the one who does the lowest work and is discounted for doing it.

Her name is where the book plays its flat joke and its deepest one, the way it does with Justice O'Leary and Moses Hunnecker. Beatrice is Dante's guide, the beloved who leads the pilgrim up out of the dark and into Paradise, and an engraving of "Dante wooing Beatrice" hangs in the baby doctor's room, over the bed. Here the guide to the light is a half-blind old laundress in the dark who "turns out the light" so no one will see her. The book is saying, in a name, that if the celestial guide came to this town she would come as the woman nobody looks at, hauling wet sheets uphill in the cold, and that the way to find Beatrice is to look at the drudge and see the guide.

Her blindness belongs to the book's strange company of the sightless who perceive. Friday the blind dog sees the angel his clear-eyed mistress cannot, the stone deaf man "heard with his eyes," and Beatrice, losing both eyes, is dismissed by Madge with "so what can she see," as if the one who cannot see does not count. But she is the keeper of the town's soil. She takes in what everyone wants made clean and unseen, the streaked and stained and private linen, and gives it back white, and drags the burden herself. She knows the town the way a launderer knows it, by its dirt, and she asks nothing, and no one ever sends her beautiful clothes. She washes the world and stays in rags. Hers is witnessing without the reward of being witnessed, the pillar Spitzer performs with his cheroots and Vera performs with her looking, worn now by an old woman at a washtub who serves the whole town and turns out the light rather than be seen serving it.

Around her the book grows its usual tangle of uncertain sons. Homer swears she had a boy who "joined the Navy and went to sea," who "sends her postal cards from all over the world," who "has seen all the world's great ports," Borneo and Singapore and Gibraltar, who "never forgot her," and whose "little red sled she dragged through the snow." Madge swears the son never existed. A third account makes him "young Doc," fathered by "old Doc" who "never claimed him" and "never knew that he was a father," though "it could have been anybody's son." Even Moses the bus-driver, stopping near her place, is proposed for a moment as "a faithful son" come for his mother's clean clothes. She is Jock's counterpart in the register of motherhood, a woman whose child is assembled from postcards and denials and may be three men or none, and the red sled is the emblem of it, a boy's toy become an old woman's labor, the absent son present only as the thing she still drags through the snow.

The turn for a writer is that Beatrice is made the way the whole book says people are made, out of the testimony of those who did not look. Two riders who have never spoken to her build her on a bus, one giving her cleanliness and a devoted world-traveling son, the other giving her filth and blindness and no son at all, and the character is neither report and both, alive in the gap between them exactly as a real person is. And the name is the instruction. A writer can hang the highest figure in literature, the guide who leads to Paradise, on the lowest laborer in the county, and let the irony carry the argument, that the guide, if she is anywhere, is at the washtub, blue-faced in the cold, dragging the clean and the soiled up the same hill, turning out the light because she does not want to be seen. To write Beatrice is to refuse the town's two easy verdicts, saint and hag, and to see instead the plain old woman who takes in what everyone wants hidden and hands it back clean, and to understand that finding the guide in the drudge is not a trick of the name.
