Research notes: Chapter 4 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 4

Characters: Miss Pebbles · Moses Hunnecker

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The bus-driver is never one person. He is simultaneously:

  • The driver at the wheel
  • A man in a four-posted bed dreaming he is driving
  • His mother's unweaned baby
  • Penelope weaving, not Ulysses wandering
  • An Arctic whaler who missed his calling
  • A Republican prophet
  • A man who may already be dead

The text makes this ontological multiplicity explicit: "perhaps he only thought he was the bus-driver...and he was just turning and tossing upon his feather mattress". The self here is not fragmented in the sense of being broken; it is constitutively multiple, each version equally valid, none canceling the others. He is Penelope and the drunken driver and the dead man and the unborn. The text does not resolve these into a single identity. It insists on holding them all at once.

The pregnant woman on the bus mirrors this: she is simultaneously real and a projection, her moaning "accusing everybody, all the Toms, Dicks, Harrys...every man but her husband"; she is one woman and all women, specific and archetypal, present and symbolic. The old doctor, similarly, is both a man in a hotel room and a figure of cosmic medical intervention, both senile fool and the only one who understands what is actually happening.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter is structured around a sustained, irresolvable question: what is real? The bus-driver insists the doctor's patients are illusions, "there are none of these pregnant women you think there are", while the narrative simultaneously presents the pregnant woman on the bus as physically present. The doctor thinks he is practicing medicine; the bus-driver thinks he is driving; the narrator is a passenger on a journey whose destination may be death. None of these perspectives is privileged.

The key passage: "the old doctor, poor old fellow, the bus-driver said, was drunk in his imagination, imagining he was drunk on ether and starlight". But this description applies equally to the bus-driver himself, who may be in his four-posted bed. The cave has no outside. The illusion-making is recursive: the bus-driver's accusation of the doctor's delusion is itself delivered from within a delusion. The text offers no Archimedean point from which to adjudicate.

The Burma-Shave signs are gone, the landmarks vanished, the sign-posts removed. Without external reference points, navigation becomes purely interior: "driving along with only his memory of the polar star". Reality is what memory and imagination construct in the absence of the external. There is no other kind.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The chapter is saturated in a grief that has no object and no resolution; it is simply the climate of the world being described. The grey miles, the washed-out bridges, the flooded roads, the dead orchards, the cherry trees "bending and breaking under their weight of snow"; these are not symbols of grief so much as grief itself made landscape.

The bus-driver's mother appears repeatedly: she is dreaming she is young, she has milk in her breasts, her baby is in her arms, but the baby is the bus-driver, who is old and drunk and driving through the night. The grief here is the grief of time itself, of the impossibility of return. He has "never left his mother" in one sense, while in every practical sense he has left her completely. These two facts coexist without resolution.

The undertaker's side-business in honeymoon furniture has collapsed: "now things had gotten so bad, even he was going to seed, and there were no calls for tables or chairs, no calls but for the beds", the beds of death, not of marriage. The generative has been replaced entirely by the terminal. This is not a temporary condition. It is the permanent weather of this world.

The dead watch dog "who had been shot to put him out of his misery, barking at a shadow": dead and still barking. Grief does not end with death in this text. It continues, accumulates, echoes through the thin partition between soul and soul.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator's role in this chapter is almost entirely that of witness. She asks only two questions, "How much longer?" and "Why don't you cut your hair?", and otherwise simply receives the bus-driver's monologue, his philosophy, his grief, his politics, his cosmology. She does not judge, does not resolve, does not explain. She stays in the room.

This is itself an ethical stance. The chapter is approximately ten pages of the bus-driver's consciousness, and the narrator does not interrupt it to provide psychological diagnosis or narrative conclusion. The text around the bus-driver is the practice of attention: full, unflinching, unrushed attention to a man who is drunk, possibly delusional, politically reactionary, and also, simultaneously, profound.

The sign above the driver's head, which "urged that no passenger engage in conversation with the bus-driver whose eyes were always on the road", is itself a meditation on witnessing. The instruction is to watch but not interfere. The narrator violates it only minimally. To witness fully is the chapter's primary ethical act.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The bus route itself is the formal embodiment of recursive construction. The bus "detour[s] a thousand miles...to advance one", passes "the same things again and again, the same rail fences broken down and horse fountains", circles back through "the same rail fences broken down" again. Progress is not linear. The road returns to its beginning.

The bus-driver's oath, not to cut his hair until Roosevelt leaves office, is a construction he builds and rebuilds each time he tells it. His argument with the barber, his argument with the doctor, his political manifesto: these are rehearsed, repeated, refined. "He had kept his word, keeping also even those promises he had not made". He builds his identity through recursive promise-keeping, through returning to the same commitments with what he has learned (or drunk) in the interim.

The hair itself is recursive construction made literal: it grows, it is the ongoing project, it is never finished, it is the building that cannot be completed because completion would mean death, "when you cut my hair, I said to him, I will be dead".

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter has no resolution. The bus does not arrive. The birth does not occur. The argument with the doctor is never concluded. The political situation is not resolved. The mother does not wake. The hair is not cut. The chapter simply continues, sentence after sentence, clause after clause, in Young's characteristic style of the ongoing, never-concluded sentence.

The demand for completion (the barber's scissors, the doctor's forceps, the undertaker's final bed) is figured throughout as a kind of violence. The bus-driver resists all of them. "When you cut my hair, I said to him, I will be dead". Completion equals death. The ongoing, unfinished sentence is life.

"You died every minute that you lived...and you were born every minute that you died": the ethics of incompletion is not merely aesthetic here but ontological. To be alive is to be incomplete. The finished self is the dead self. The ongoing sentence, the uncut hair, the bus still moving: these are the forms integrity takes.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

The bus-driver is the central figure of the chapter, and he is constitutively marginal: drunk, politically reactionary in a New Deal world, possibly delusional, possibly dead, driving a route that goes nowhere through a landscape of the abandoned and forgotten. His hair (the sign of his marginality, his refusal of social normalization) is precisely where the constructed nature of reality becomes most visible.

"God liked a little variety, something to keep God awake, and God must have been asleep when He created man out of the dust. God forgot to create some of us, and some of us created ourselves". The marginal person is not the exception to creation; they are the evidence that creation is incomplete, ongoing, and partly self-made.

The pregnant woman, the old doctor with his empty practice, the deaf Miss Pebbles with her broken earphones, the dead watch dog still barking; these are all figures at the edges of categories: alive/dead, sane/insane, practicing/retired, hearing/deaf. The chapter populates itself with the marginal precisely because the marginal is where the seams of the constructed real become visible.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This is perhaps the pillar most fully realized in the chapter. The past does not recede in this text; it accumulates, presses upward, refuses to stay buried.

The most explicit statement: "Sometimes, their graves opening in the spring floods and rains, they were washed up, years afterward...and then it could be seen that they who had been buried alive had torn out their hair". The dead return through the literal action of the earth: the floods wash up what was buried. The sediment gives back its contents.

The bus-driver's hair is itself sedimentary time: "His hair had grown three inches since he left Persia at sunset", and will grow three more before dawn. The hair is the accumulation of time made visible on the body. It is the past that will not be cut away, that grows even after death: "his hair would not molt as would this mortal hair".

The mother in the bridal chamber is dreaming of her youth while her son is old and drunk. Past and present coexist in the same hotel room, separated only by "the thin partition between soul and soul". The past is not behind; it is beneath, in the next room, dreaming.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Young's prose style is itself an enactment of this pillar. The sentences are enormously long, self-qualifying, self-interrupting, recursive; they name their own uncertainty as they proceed. The language does not pretend to speak from nowhere. It speaks from inside the bus-driver's drunk, hair-obscured, politically compromised, possibly-dead consciousness, and it knows this about itself.

The bus-driver's political language is particularly interesting here: his anti-Democrat, anti-New Deal rhetoric is presented not as truth but as his construction of reality, shaped by his location (his mother, his bus, his hair, his whiskey). The language of political certainty ("I'm no Democrat", "they will tax my hair") is shown to emerge from the same dream-logic as everything else he says. Language that claims to speak from political principle is shown to speak from grief, from the mother, from the fear of the barber's scissors.

"What's communication?": the bus-driver's own question, asked after describing his inability to telephone the police. Communication is a construction, a technology, a fiction. The chapter's own extraordinary communicative achievement rests on this acknowledged impossibility.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter does not point outward to a beyond. It points deeper into the grey bus, the drunken driver, the flooded roads, the dead mother's dream. The transcendent is not elsewhere; it is here, in the most ordinary and degraded circumstances, intensified.

"Giddy up, giddy up, little cherub dogs...little churchyard owls, little churchyard mice. Giddy up, little rabbits, little stars, for we are coming home". The cherubs are churchyard owls. The angels are moths. The sacred vocabulary is applied to the most humble and marginal creatures (rabbits, mice, owls) and this is not ironic diminishment but genuine elevation. The sacred lives here, in these creatures, on this road.

"His hair was his angel, his dead mother who lived in him, his dream of life, his angel's downy feathers". The angel is the hair. The transcendent is the physical, biological, growing thing. The mother who is dead lives in the hair that grows. This is immanent transcendence in its most precise form: the sacred is not beyond the mortal body but is the mortal body, its growth, its warmth, its refusal to be cut.

The "elegiac music of the spheres" and "heavenly harmonies no mortal ears could hear" are immediately juxtaposed with the bus-driver's drunken singing: "Oh, the old grey mare, she ain't what she used to was". The heavenly harmonies and the drunken song are not opposed. They occupy the same acoustic space. The sacred is not above the drunken song. It is in it.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is the pillar that gives the chapter its deepest structural logic. The bus-driver is explicitly identified as a crossing point: between birth and death, between his mother's past and the unborn passenger's future, between the living and the dead.

"He was not responsible. He had not created the world nor the distance between one place and another", yet he carries everyone across it. The obligation is not chosen. He did not choose to be the bus-driver. He is the bus-driver because the doctor brought him into the world, because his mother dreamed him, because the route exists and someone must drive it.

The four passengers are "Matthew, Mark, Luke, John": the four evangelists, the four gospel writers, the four who carry the story forward. The bus-driver carries the carriers of the word. He is the ground on which transmission stands.

"One as yet unborn": one of the four passengers has not yet arrived. The bus-driver is carrying the not-yet-born. He is the crossing point between what has been and what has not yet come. "This womb of time, of time which was his dream": the bus itself is the womb, the driver is the medium of passage, and the obligation to carry continues regardless of whether he understands it, regardless of whether he is drunk or dead or dreaming.

The ancestors are in the sediment: his mother's milk, the doctor who delivered him, the promises made before he knew what promises were. The carrying continues. Moses Hunnecker drives on.
