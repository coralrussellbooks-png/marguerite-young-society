Chapter 4

On Chapter 4 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of The Wheel and the Spokes.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 16, 2026

← Chapter 3 · Wheel and Spokes Home · Chapter 5 →

Research notes: Chapter 4 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 4

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

Vera opens this chapter in two bodies at once. In one, grief has made her heavy and still: "my hair cropped, my body slothful and immense, my flesh increasingly vapid and vacant", lying on the sand as "the fat, stupid, indolent girl, great balloon of meaningless flesh with negligee ribbons streaming". In the other, the years have worked the opposite way: "I had grown thin, and my hair had grown long, sometimes hanging like a curtain before my eyes". The chapter does not tell us which Vera is the real one or how one became the other. Both are her.

When she finally names herself, the name opens rather than settles:

"I, Vera Cartwheel, daughter of Catherine Helena Cartwheel, nee Snowden, and of a father unknown, perhaps of many fathers, would be a part of that other world"

A self with a father unknown, perhaps many fathers, is plural from its origin. And the sentence is in the conditional: she would be part of that other world. Even her declaration of who she is points toward a self she has not yet become.

The dead girl whose letters set Vera on the road is plural in the same way. She wrote to farmers she had never seen, "she being different according to each description she gave of her face and of her height". To Cuckoo she was one woman, to Bill another. None of the versions was false, and none was the whole.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Catherine states the pillar in a single question:

"For what, she would ask, had ever been real, and who had ever known reality?"

Her answer is the opium paradise, "that great play of illusion which must take the place of the world thereafter, all things changing their forms, their shapes, all things blurred as if seen under water". Vera refuses it. But the chapter is honest about what she chooses instead: not reality, but another dream. Through the long years on the beach her mind is "employed by nothing but the dream, the dream of what was normal".

That normal world is built from paper. Indiana is "the promised land of which I had read in catalogues of wild flowers and seed catalogues", and the kind country people waiting for her are "those simple persons I now imagined". Then the fat, soft-faced lady on the train builds the same place in the opposite direction: "a place out of this world", "nothing but desolation and weeds", deaf old people and funeral wreaths on every door. Two women describe one destination, and neither has the plain truth of it. Vera rides on between the two constructions, and the bus window, streaked with rain, shows her neither.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The chapter's first words set its weather: "Long years after her disappearance or death, I had dreamed of no one but Miss MacIntosh, my darling". Grief here is measured in years, and the years do not thin it. Vera searches long after others have given up:

"I had searched for her everywhere, and yet I had not given up the search even when others had said that she would never be found, neither in a great sea of humanity nor in the sea. Yet years after she was gone, I had found her ivory knitting needles in the sand."

The knitting needles are grief's evidence. The sea gives back one small, real thing and keeps the woman. Even the sounds of Miss MacIntosh outlast her: "an old dog barking at her heels as I could hear him barking long after she had gone".

And yet the grief changes shape without ending. Late in the chapter Vera says that she no longer searches for Miss MacIntosh as she once had been, "for she was dead, and my search was for life, this life as it would become". The mourning has not resolved. It has turned into the direction of her journey. She carries it onto the bus.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Vera's long search is an act of witness. She asks everyone the sea has touched:

"had talked to old fishermen drying their silvery nets on the beach, had talked to the red-faced lobster-trappers red as the lobsters they trapped, to codfish fishermen returning from the Great Banks with the full boats followed by the empty boats, to sailors of the deep, to all who were wayfarers of the sea"

She gets no answers, and she keeps looking. Witness, in this chapter, is the refusal to stop attending to someone who cannot be found.

The chapter is also honest about where Vera's witnessing fails. When Catherine's beauty collapses, Vera looks away:

"few should see her with her eyelids swollen and blackened, her face swollen like that of the drowned—and if I saw her thus, then I closed my eyes in order not to remember her"

The daughter who will not stop searching for her nursemaid closes her eyes on her mother. Young lets both stand. Witnessing is not a virtue Vera simply has; it is something she practices unevenly, the way everyone does.

The dead girl is witnessed only after her death, through what she left behind: "a dead girl's love letters strewn on the floor of a room like a cell". From the letters come the names of the men she wrote to ("Cuckoo, Bud, Jim, Bob, Henry, Bill"), and Vera carries the story with her. As Vera tells it, the girl's last evening is given in full detail, from the taxi under a shower of rice to the long fall from the bridge. Someone has to keep her.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

One sentence holds this pillar whole:

"I built a house of driftwood, though only the seagull came to the door."

Vera builds anyway. The house is made of what the sea has already broken, it shelters no one, and the only visitor is a bird. She also lights a fire on the beach so that Miss MacIntosh, far out at sea, might see it: "I lit a fire upon this beach that Miss MacIntosh might know where I was", "long years after she had gone". Neither the house nor the fire can do what she builds them for. She builds them all the same.

The book she tries to write goes the same way. She writes "by the wavering light of the sea", but "I could not compete with four snails crawling across the open pages, crawling into the sea, for their writing was more beautiful than mine. So I left the book by the tide." And then she begins again in a different form: the bus journey itself, another provisional structure, taken up with what the beach has taught her.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Vera does not go to Iowa. She explains why, and the explanation is the pillar:

"I knew what I had always known, that the unknown had always been, must always be her place. That was why I had come this circular way—by no direct route."

To go straight to Iowa and ask at every door would have been, she says, "too suicidal and foolhardy a journey". It would close the question, and Vera cannot bear to close it. So she stops short: "This was only the stopping ground, Indiana", "as near as I could go without being confronted once again by the bitterness of the facts". The unfinished journey is not cowardice in Young's telling. It is the only way to keep faith with a woman whose place is the unknown.

The dead girl's journey is unfinished too. She leaves for Grand Central Station under rice and old shoes, then changes her mind and turns the taxi back "perhaps because of the love which would always change its face, the love of which the only face was a cloud". Her story ends in the water with her suitcase still in her hand. No one will learn which of the farmers she meant to marry.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

The person this chapter stops to honor is a woman no one else noticed. The dead girl's mother "had been an Irish wash woman". She has "no father who would give her away in holy matrimony, for she had lost her father at the age of three". She lives on a waterfront street of "painted streetwalkers leering under the lamp-posts" and shop windows full of "waxen brides in their wedding gowns". Her yearnings are for "only the simplest things imaginable, marriage, the union of body with soul, children, chickens, a little farm with a vine-covered gate".

At the margin, the constructed nature of the ordinary life shows plainly. The girl can only reach it by writing letters to strangers, describing herself differently each time, and the wax brides in the window are as near as she comes to a wedding. Young surrounds her with others at the edge: "tramps sleeping in doorways or on the sidewalks or in the gutters, even in enormous baby carriages". And the fat lady on the train places the deaf old people of the interior at the farthest margin of all, where "the human voice was not heard". The margins are where the promise of normal life is tested, and where it most often fails.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Vera hopes the past will stay buried: "I would be freed of the past and of its obsessions, those driving ghosts white sheeted with ice, those voices calling my name upon the many winds", and "The dead past would be buried, and my eyes would be awakened". The chapter shows how little that hope can hold. The voices she means to escape are the ones that brought her here. She has come, she says, "because of a dead girl's love letters", and "all those persons who had ever pointed the way, all were dead, and some had been suicides". The dead are not behind the journey. They are steering it.

Miss MacIntosh's own past rises in the same way, through the stories she told of Iowa winters. One of them is the chapter's most exact image of what this pillar means:

"Once a farmer driving his wagon with a team of four horses went through a hole in the ice of a frozen lake, and in the spring when the ice was melting, when the great boomers were booming, he was brought to shore with his hands frozen to the reins and his four horses rising through the foam caused by the breaking ice, the mist and spray."

What went under in winter comes back in spring, still holding the reins.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Vera's name is a claim about language. In the life she imagines, "All people would greet me by my name, Vera, that which stands for truth and not merely the fickle promise or dead substitute for that life which was never to be realized". She wants a world where a word means what it says. The chapter keeps showing her how rarely words do.

Her own writing loses to the snails, whose trails across the page are "more beautiful than mine". The fat lady on the train speaks almost entirely in borrowed phrases: "there were just as many fishes in the sea as ever were caught", and "there was a second husband for everybody". Her warning ends in the words of Ecclesiastes, used as if they were her own report:

"The voice of the turtle-dove was not heard in all that land, for the golden bowl was broken and the wheel broken at the cistern. Nobody knew what time it was, for all the clocks were stopped."

Scripture and cliché carry the same weight in her mouth. Language here makes a place before anyone has seen it, and Vera travels into a country already built out of other people's sentences. The broken wheel at the cistern is worth noticing in a study called The Wheel and the Spokes.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Vera's dream of the sacred is made entirely of ordinary things. She is a rich girl, and she knows how strange her longing sounds:

"Others might be surprised by what a rich girl dreamed, that she did not dream of riches. I did not dream I lived in marble halls, for I lived in marble halls. For years I dreamed of nothing but a shoemaker's awl, an old tramp's shoe which needed mending, a hammer, a nail"

Earlier she lists the life Miss MacIntosh spoke of: "the routine of Monday following Sunday, Tuesday following Monday, the washings on the lines, the voices of little children, baking day, recipes, knitting for the mission at some far Arctic post". This is the sacred as Young keeps finding it, in the week's plain order and the work of hands. When Vera imagines her own rebirth, she reaches for a Gospel figure and sets him in a farmyard: "I would be a Lazarus of these awakening joys, these country pleasures".

Even the heavens in this chapter do not point beyond themselves. The commonplace life Vera longs for "asks no questions of the moon or sun or stars. For they are their own answer, shining through clouds."

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Catherine's offer of opium is the chapter's crossing point, and Vera names what it costs:

"This was my mother's greatest act of love, the only sign that she had a child, her acceptance of me when I was no more, her acknowledgment that I existed only when I had ceased to exist"

What Catherine deposits in Vera is an inheritance of withdrawal: the compromise "which she, a poor invalid sleeping in a charity ward or under the roofless heavens, had made years before me". Miss MacIntosh deposited the opposite inheritance, the dream of "that normal life which she had always spoken of in no uncertain terms". Vera stands where the two meet. She did not choose either inheritance, and she cannot set either down.

On the bus she wonders whether she has chosen at all:

"Had I made, after all these long, circular windings, the wrong choice, the dread suspicion crossing me now that we had passed, long ago, the choice point, a dead star, a dark star, that there was no choice?"

The question is the pillar's own. Vera is constituted by what came before her, her mother's bed, her nursemaid's Iowa, a dead girl's letters, and she is also moving toward a life not yet lived, "life, the unreachable". The carrying continues on a bus in the dark.
