Definition from research

"My religion is poetic and cosmic and not theological."

Marguerite Young, Paris Review

Marguerite Young held a religion all her own, and it shaped everything she wrote. She came down from two figures who could hardly have agreed on anything, Brigham Young, who led the Latter-day Saint movement west, and John Knox, who drove the Scottish Reformation, and out of that unlikely descent she drew a lifelong pull toward utopias, toward faith, and toward the line where belief shades into illusion.

Her grandmother raised her and marked her early life. Late in life the old woman suffered a stroke, and her delusions filled with "angels and other such creatures," a household haunted by the fantastic. The child took it in and never quite set it down. Young was taught young to ask the angels for help and to cast Satan away, and when someone said "Satan, get behind me," she would whirl around on the spot, hoping to catch a glimpse of him before he could hide. That reflex, the sense that the unearthly stood close at hand rather than far off, runs straight through the work that followed.

The books trace the same preoccupation from every angle. Angel in the Forest: A Fairy Tale of Two Utopias (1945) digs into the Rappist and Owenite communities that rose and fell at New Harmony, Indiana, and her deep research there springs from the same fascination with utopian dreams and the search for truth. Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965) is a mythological journey through illusion and reality, in which Vera hunts for the Christian nursemaid who stands for solid ground and finishes by accepting that life is shot through with ambivalence instead. Harp Song for a Radical (1999), her biography of the socialist Eugene V. Debs, ties his labor movement back to the communal, mutual-aid spirit of the nineteenth-century utopian sects, the Mormons and the Shakers among them. The Complete Poems of Marguerite Young (2022) still waits for a close reading of its own, one that would test how her spirituality speaks in the earliest work.

Nature, for Young, was never simply beloved. She prized it for what it meant. Her "birds and beasts" stand in for human traits and cosmic ideas, a habit of symbolic reading she first worked out in her master's thesis on Elizabethan and Jacobean literature. Asked once by the Paris Review to name her faith, she called it "always poetic and cosmic," which is about as close as she came to a creed.

This second edition takes Miss MacIntosh, My Darling as its ground. It works through the novel reference by reference, gathering every mention of Christianity and of the other faiths and beliefs the book carries, in order to read Young's religious imagination closely and, in the end, to say what her Christianity and her religion finally amount to.
