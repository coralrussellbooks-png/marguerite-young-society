Research notes: Chapter 68 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 68

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Weed

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

"Who was I?"

The narrator does not ask this rhetorically. She asks it with genuine ontological uncertainty, sitting in a rocking chair in a tavern lobby, watching her face change while seeming changeless, feeling herself pass "from my youth to my age without so much as a sigh." She is simultaneously the child who loved Miss MacIntosh, the woman who has journeyed through the night, the widow with no husband, the mother with no children, the old woman tottering at the edge of a grave: all at once, in the same body, in the same chair.

Not One Self is not a metaphor here. It is the literal condition of the narrator's experience. She inhabits multiple temporal selves simultaneously: the nine-year-old watching sleigh bells in a cherry tree, the woman screaming that Miss MacIntosh is drowned, the ancient figure with brown spots on her arms, and none of these selves cancels the others. They are all present, layered, irresolvable.

The hangman, Mr. Weed, enacts the same plurality in a darker register. He is simultaneously the humanitarian missionary, the man who stopped to put on his galoshes while his wife hanged, the dreamer who boils his children in a pot, the father who nearly beats his children with the branch from their mother's death-tree. He insists: "I am a good father,’ he said. ‘I always was." This insistence is not hypocrisy; it is the desperate attempt to resolve the plural self into a single, manageable identity. The chapter refuses to let him.

Even the landlord is multiple: the married man who ties lovers' knots, the sailor who drowned four times and survived, the man who is "unofficially alive", the innkeeper who has seen everything and feigns deafness to manage what he cannot stop hearing.

Not One Self is the chapter's population principle. Every character is irresolvable. That is not their flaw. That is what they are.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

"For there was only one definition of reality, that it was real, real as my mother's opium dreams of scallop boats and the feet of satyrs and sails upon the waves, real as Mr. Spitzer's brother who might be the musician or the gambler, real as Miss MacIntosh's sand-colored face which had no hair-line, real as a sea which had no surf line, and real as that rock which was perched upon an angel's wing."

This is the chapter's philosophical thesis statement, delivered at its opening. The narrator does not distinguish between opium dreams and waking perception. She places them in a list of equivalences. The only definition of reality is that it is real, and everything experienced is, by that definition, real.

The second pillar states that "the line between illusion and reality is itself a construction." The narrator goes further: she dissolves the line entirely. The unreal things are real. "Even the unreal things were real, even those who were ignored, rejected, despised, abandoned, unloved, perhaps even unknown, never of fruit or flower."

This is not relativism. It is a more radical claim: that the hierarchy between the real and the illusory is itself an ideological construction, and that the honest mind refuses it. Miss MacIntosh, with her sand-colored face and her insistence that sleigh bells belong only in snow, represented the attempt to maintain that hierarchy: to put everything in its proper place, to keep the cave well-lit and orderly. And then she walked into the sea, leaving her galoshes on the shore, leaving everything in "such a wild confusion." The woman who most insisted on the boundary between real and unreal destroyed herself on it.

The tavern itself operates as a space where illusion has become primary. The sign reads "Bride Ahead" when it means "Bridge Ahead." The miner sits with his headlight out, mining only his dreams. The lobby contains bald men studying what may or may not be a map. Nothing is confirmed. Everything is possible. The larger cave is more carefully lit, but it is still a cave.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

"Perhaps even before a river is seen, its invisible presence distends all things, much like a bellows blowing up many fires of many dreams, dead dreamers and their dreams."

Grief in this chapter does not arrive as an event. It is the atmosphere: the distending pressure of the river not yet seen, the accumulated weight of every lost thing pressing outward into the present.

The narrator's grief for Miss MacIntosh is not a past event she is processing. It is the condition of her perception. She sees everything through it. The sleigh bells in the cherry tree, the galoshes on the shore, the swollen copy of Pilgrim's Progress; these are not memories she visits. They are the medium through which she sees the tavern, the signboards, the hangman, the landlord.

The hangman's grief operates the same way. His first wife Marie hanged herself years ago, and yet she is present in every scene he describes: in the peach tree that still stands, in the children who play at hanging, in the second wife who wears the first wife's clothes, in the dream where he boils his children alive. Marie has not receded. She has accumulated. She is the weather of his domestic life.

Most devastatingly, the narrator confesses:

"I was frightened, frightened, frightened by my own defective character which had loved imperfections and distortions and mad dreams more than it had loved that reality which I did not know, the flesh and blood, perhaps might never know, for I had loved only the dead. I had loved only the departed."

Grief as permanent weather means that the beloved dead are not behind you. They are the climate you live in. The narrator does not grieve Miss MacIntosh. She is grief for Miss MacIntosh. The distinction between the griever and the grief has collapsed.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

"I sat with my face glued against the bus window, studying a not unusual wilderness architecture."

The narrator's primary activity throughout this chapter is watching. She watches the tavern from the bus before she enters it. She watches the boy and girl walk up the street. She watches the hangman and the landlord from her rocking chair at the edge of the circle of light. She does not intervene. She does not conclude. She does not rush to judgment.

This is the ethical stance of the Witness: to stay in the room, to see fully, without flinching, without rushing toward resolution.

The hangman's stories are grotesque: a woman's severed head in a wheat field wearing rose-colored sunglasses, children hanged like dolls from a peach tree, a man who boils his children in dreams. The narrator does not recoil from these. She does not editorialize. She presents them with the same flat, accumulating attention she gives to the signboards and the weeds growing between broken pavements.

The landlord models a cruder version of witnessing: he listens, plucks at his deaf ear, occasionally interjects, but mostly allows the hangman to speak. He has heard these stories before, "You tell the same damned stories every time you come here, Weed", and he has not resolved them. He simply keeps the room open.

The refusal to simplify is the act. The chapter's extraordinary length, its refusal to summarize the hangman's monologue, its insistence on following every digression about poker chips and peach trees and the race-horse tout who kept crying "Casho!"; this is not self-indulgence. It is the formal enactment of the Witness. Attention is the practice. The chapter will not look away.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

"All might have been like the extension of one of my mother's opium dreams."

The narrator has been journeying. She is arriving. But the arrival does not end the journey; it reveals that the journey was always already the destination, and that the destination is simply a new point from which the journey continues.

The hangman's procedure is explicitly recursive: he rehearses every hanging, practices with a sandbag dummy weighted to the condemned man's exact measurements, corrects his form letter "over a period of years", returns again and again to the same peach tree, the same orchard, the same stories. He is building, knowing the building is provisional. The peach tree still stands. He has always intended to cut it down. He never does.

The landlord's hotel "sinks every night", and yet it stands each morning. The recursive construction is not a metaphor for the hotel. It is the hotel. The building is never finished. That is how it works.

The narrator's own construction (this novel, this journey, this search for Miss MacIntosh) is recursive by definition. She is returning to a beginning she never left. The journey toward the real was always already a journey through the constructed. She builds anyway.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

"The bus, in the middle of the street now, was as motionless as the old ghost buggy. Nobody made a move to get out of the bus. We three might have been corpses in an undertaker's hearse, it seemed to me as we waited, perhaps waited for the journey to begin when it had ended."

The journey has ended. The journey has not ended. Both are true simultaneously, and the chapter refuses to resolve them. This is Unfinished Is Honest in its purest form: the journey does not conclude because conclusion is a violence against the experience of journeying.

The hangman's memoirs, which he announces he is writing, will be "quite sketchy, an outline". He cannot include everything. "Every leaf that falls" cannot be included. The memoirs are incomplete by necessity, and the hangman's acknowledgment of this is, paradoxically, the most honest thing he says.

The narrator's account of herself is similarly incomplete. She does not know if the woman whose black hat hangs on the rocking chair exists or is a phantom. She does not know if her stone foetus is real or imagined. She does not know who she is. The unfinished self is not a failure of self-knowledge. It is the only honest self-knowledge available.

The demand for completion (Miss MacIntosh's insistence that there is "a place for everything", that sleigh bells belong in snow and not in cherry trees) is revealed as its own violence. Miss MacIntosh walked into the sea rather than live with incompletion. The chapter suggests this was the wrong choice, or at least an unnecessary one.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

"Even the unreal things were real, even those who were ignored, rejected, despised, abandoned, unloved, perhaps even unknown, never of fruit or flower."

The chapter is populated entirely by marginal figures. The narrator herself (the daughter of an opium addict, the lover of a vanished woman, the widow with no husband, the mother with no children) is marginal by every social category. The hangman is marginal in the most literal sense: he operates at the edge of the law, the edge of life, the edge of the community's ability to acknowledge what it requires. The landlord has been officially dead in multiple shipwrecks and continues to exist as a kind of ontological surplus.

The chapter's most sustained marginal figure is the unnamed Negro who was cut down from the rope a split second too soon and then seen pushing a baby buggy across a field. He is "unofficially alive": existing in a category that the social order cannot accommodate. He is the chapter's most devastating figure, because his marginality is not chosen or accidental. It is imposed, and it is the direct product of a society that hanged him for "sleeping with a white woman as he surely never did." He is the evidence that the categories are constructed, that the margins are where the construction becomes most visible, and that the cost of that construction is borne by specific bodies.

The hangman's humanitarian self-narrative cannot survive contact with this figure. He entered the profession because of the unnecessary pain of that inefficient hanging. But the unnecessary pain was not primarily the pain of an unskillful knot. It was the pain of a man hanged for a crime that "never happened and yet happened, happened every day in America." The hangman sees the technical problem. He cannot see, or will not see, the structural one. His marginality to his own moral narrative is the chapter's quiet indictment.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

"The streets were splotched as if with rain or a disease."

The village the narrator enters is not a present place. It is a sedimentary accumulation of every past that has happened in it. The Tavern was built in 1827. The signboards are tattered. The chassis are rusted. The mattresses are sodden. The bed-springs are "like old musical instruments never to be played on again." The past has not receded. It has deposited itself as the physical substance of the present.

The hangman carries the past literally in his pocket. The black poker chip from an old unsolved case has been in the lining of his coat for years, forgotten, and then recovered. He carries, in the same coat, the Parker fountain pen given to him by a condemned man's relatives, the Jew's harp, the stopped watch from the family driven off a cliff. He is a walking sedimentary deposit of other people's catastrophes.

The narrator's own sedimentary time is organized around Miss MacIntosh's galoshes:

"Her galoshes, thrown up by the tide, were water-logged. Seaweed, the color of coral, adhered to them. On the sole of one, there was plastered a green leaf."

This image has been deposited in the narrator so deeply that it surfaces here, in a landlocked tavern, far from any shore. The past is not behind her. It is beneath her, pressing up. The green leaf on the sole of the galosh is still wet.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

"Far up the street, which climbed uncertainly toward the indistinct sky, Josh's Place, a wreath of fuzzy blue neon lights, was like a funeral wreath both for Josh and the world. Beyond this, another sign read Gentle Incline — Bride Ahead — but obviously what was meant was Bridge Ahead, a warning of the river which must be crossed — surely no aromatic river with clouds of bridal lace hanging over it and the white skull moon like the face of the bride, dead bride."

Language fails, and in its failure, it reveals. The misspelled sign, Bride for Bridge, opens a space where the literal and the figurative collapse into each other. The bridge that must be crossed becomes the bride, the dead bride, the marriage with death that the hangman will later name explicitly. The misprint is not corrected. It is followed: the narrator pursues the error into its implications, and finds there a deeper truth than the intended meaning would have provided.

The hangman's language is a sustained exercise in this dynamic. He speaks in euphemisms (helping "a fellow creature out of his misery", "the painless knot", "I come in toward the end and tie the knot") that simultaneously reveal and conceal. His language shapes what can be seen: by calling himself a humanitarian, he makes it possible not to see the violence. By calling his procedure efficient, he makes it possible not to see the bodies.

The narrator's language, by contrast, is language that names its own location. It says: I am speaking from grief, from fear, from a defective character that loved imperfections. It says: I do not know if what I see is real. It says: the journey may have ended or may not have ended. This is language that refuses to pretend to speak from nowhere.

The Tavern's sign, "built in 1827 by God's servants who were believers in the lamb", is language that claims the authority of the universal while speaking from a very particular location. The narrator's question, "Who, I wondered, were God's servants, and why should they advertise themselves?", is the exact question the ninth pillar demands we ask of all language that claims to speak from nowhere.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

"My mother's face would never be old, I thought even now when surely I knew that my mother had grown old. Hair, hair, that was the great deception, hair, a woman's crowning glory, hair which keeps growing in the grave. The bald woman was the woman I loved."

The sacred in this chapter does not point outward to a beyond. It points inward and downward, into the ordinary, the broken, the mortal. The bald woman (Miss MacIntosh, stripped of every conventional sign of femininity and beauty) is the object of the narrator's deepest love. The sacred is located precisely in what social convention marks as deficiency.

The hangman's Jew's harp is a sacred object of this kind. He takes it out, plays a soundless tune, puts it back. It is a child's instrument, associated with no tradition of seriousness, and yet it is the thing he reaches for in moments of difficulty. It is the thing that cannot console him and yet he keeps reaching for it. The sacred is here, in the inadequate instrument, in the gesture that knows it will not work and makes it anyway.

The Tavern itself, "built in 1827 by God's servants who were believers in the lamb", is now a commercial establishment that sinks every night and rises every morning, full of tattered streamers and rusted signboards and a landlord with ships tattooed on his arms. The transcendence has not departed. It has become immanent. It lives in the creaking chain and the scraping sign and the hobnailed boots of the sleeping miner whose headlight has no light in it.

The chapter's most explicit statement of immanent transcendence is the narrator's vision of herself as simultaneously young and old, living and dying, in the rocking chair:

"Now in a split second, in the flicker of an eyelid, here as I sat rocking in a chair which creaked, I seemed to myself to be passing from my youth to my age without so much as a sigh."

The sacred is this: the flicker of an eyelid in which all of time is present. Not heaven. Not resolution. The grey bus, the permanent grief, the plural self in all its unresolved reality. This is where the sacred lives.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This pillar finds its most complete expression in the chapter's final image, or rather, in the chapter's refusal of a final image:

"The landlord and the hangman climbed the stairs together — while I, rocking back and forth, waited for service. A clock should have struck somewhere, telling the hour. No clock struck."

The narrator waits. Time does not mark itself. The clock that should have struck does not strike. She is not moving through time toward a destination. She is the place where time has stopped marking itself, where the accumulated past and the unbegun future press against each other without resolution.

The chapter's opening establishes this structural position explicitly:

"To come into a village by night may mean perhaps never to know it in the same way as if one came in the incandescence of sunlight. Perhaps never would the darkness lift, I thought then — that the shadows of the night might always remain."

She arrived in darkness. The darkness is constitutive. She is constituted by what was deposited before she arrived (her mother's opium dreams, Miss MacIntosh's galoshes, the Negro pushing the baby buggy, the peach tree where Marie hanged herself) and she is constituting the ground the not-yet-born will stand on. The chapter she narrates will become sediment.

The hangman's stopped watch makes this explicit. It stopped at the exact moment the car went over the cliff. The crystal was not broken. The watch preserves the crossing point, the instant when one thing ended and another began, and the hangman carries it in his pocket, wound down, refusing to wind it again. He is carrying the crossing point with him. He does not know what to do with it. None of them do.

The obligation is not chosen. It is structural. The ancestors are in the sediment. The carrying continues.
