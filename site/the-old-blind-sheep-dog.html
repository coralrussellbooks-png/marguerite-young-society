The Old Blind Sheep Dog

Part of To All My Darlings, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Home

In the research: Chapter 15, Chapter 18, Chapter 21, Chapter 22

Definition from research

Chapter 15

Listed as: The old, blind sheep dog

  • The sheep dog’s barking is repeatedly mentioned as an ominous sign, something that "had not barked for nothing," unlike a young dog who barks merely for pleasure. This suggests that the dog's barking is a deliberate warning of danger lurking nearby, perhaps a threat to Vera's innocence or safety.
  • Miss MacIntosh explicitly warns Vera to be careful because "someone who would take a young girl by the hair and drag her into the shadows where her cries should not be heard" might be near.
  • The dog’s barking seems to symbolize a protective instinct, alerting to the presence of real dangers in the night that Vera might be tempted to ignore or not fully understand.
  • The dog is described as "old" and "blind," which adds complexity to its symbolism. Blindness here can be interpreted as a metaphor for the limitations of perception that come with age or experience.
  • While the dog cannot see, it still hears and senses danger, which may suggest that wisdom or vigilance does not solely depend on sight or youthful alertness.
  • The dog’s blindness could also represent the older generation’s partial understanding of dangers, aware but not fully able to prevent them. This aligns with Vera’s reflections on how older people, including Miss MacIntosh, fail to grasp the "heart of youth" and the deeper currents of life and death.
  • The dog’s old age and blindness also evoke mortality and the approach of death, consistent with the chapter’s pervasive meditation on death as a "bridegroom" and the many faces of death.
  • The old blind sheep dog is isolated in the night, barking at waves and wind, much like Vera’s own sense of separation from the older generation and their "adult fantasies."
  • On a narrative level, the dog's barking serves as a tension-building device. It punctuates the chapter’s meditative and philosophical tone with a concrete, audible signal of danger. It brings the abstract themes of death, danger, and innocence into a tangible presence, grounding Vera's lofty reflections in the physical reality of the night and the environment.
  • “The old sheep dog barking in the night had not barked for nothing”: The proverb is “An old dog barks not in vain,” from George Herbert’s Outlandish Proverbs (1640).

Chapter 18

  • The old blind sheep dog appears only briefly and indirectly. It may function as a potent symbol of failing protection, limited perception, and the human struggle to confront elusive and overwhelming existential truths. Its presence underscores the chapter’s meditation on vulnerability, loss, and the elusive nature of reality and memory.
  • Vera hears him barking “perhaps at a spar, perhaps at the last falling star… or some danger greater than any I could have imagined.”

Chapter 21

  • “the old, blind sheep dog running ahead of us on three legs”: The dog still “guarding the pasture which was no more against the thief who would not come.”

Chapter 22

  • “An old dog barks at nothing”: This turns around the proverb of Chapter 15, “An old dog barks not in vain.”
  • “Whose dog did not bark in the night?”: May refer to a Sherlock Holmes story Silver Blaze, by Sir Arthur Conan Doyle, where the fact that a dog did not bark during the night when a race horse was removed from a stable was an important clue to solve the mystery of who removed the horse from the stable. Young said in an interview how much she loved murder mysteries, and she is said to have minored in criminology. It encapsulates a meditation on missed warnings, silent tragedies, and the profound isolation felt by characters like Vera and Miss MacIntosh as they navigate a world filled with unseen dangers and unspoken sorrows.
