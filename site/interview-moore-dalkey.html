"Remembering Marguerite Young," a memoir by Steven Moore (Dalkey Days)

See also: The Unfinished Harp: Reading Harp Song for a Radical

A reading for the Miss MacIntosh project.

The source at a glance

This is a first-person recollection, "Remembering Marguerite Young," by the critic and editor Steven Moore, who worked at Dalkey Archive Press and its journal, the Review of Contemporary Fiction. He wrote it for Constance Eichenlaub's "Who Is Marguerite Young?" website in March 1999 and reprinted it in his memoir "Dalkey Days." It is the publisher's view of the 1990s revival of Young's work and of Moore's dealings with her in her last years, so it should be weighed as candid recollection rather than as Young's testimony. Its value for the project is a documented publishing history of the reissues, a frank late-life portrait from someone who negotiated with her, and confirmation, from the inside, of several threads that recur in the interviews: the excised novellas, the troubled Debs biography, and the archive's fate. Eichenlaub, who commissioned the piece, keeps the Young web archive, and the festschrift Moore mentions was edited by Miriam Fuchs, so this source ties the surrounding materials together.

How the revival happened

Moore came to Young in the early 1970s through Anais Nin's "The Novel of the Future," but did not read Miss MacIntosh until the late 1980s, when copyediting a Review of Contemporary Fiction issue on Acker, Brooke-Rose, and Young sent him to it. He fell for it at once, admiring what others dismiss as purple prose and writing that "no one's prose is purpler than Marguerite's," that she turned 1930s America into "Arabian Nights enchantment" and left her prose "roaring through my dreams." The reprint began with a phone call from a young editor at Oxford University Press who knew Young and had already floated Dalkey to her; John O'Brien, Dalkey's editor, trusted Moore's judgment even though Young "wasn't his type of author." Moore documents the chronology: Dalkey published Miss MacIntosh in two volumes in 1993, with before-and-after author photos on the covers, and in August 1994 followed with three more books, the essay-and-story collection "Inviting the Muses," a reprint of "Angel in the Forest," and Miriam Fuchs's festschrift. He also records the loose ends: contracts Young never signed, so the books appeared on her verbal permission alone, and a Young bibliography by Martha Sattler that O'Brien canceled. Moore left Dalkey in 1996.

Young's conditions and regrets

Two of Young's own wishes come through clearly. She insisted the reprint be in two volumes, over Moore's preference for a single book like the original, because the one-volume edition "intimidated people, scared them off," and she wanted something portable people could "read on the subway," so insistently that she threatened to withdraw the offer otherwise. And she voiced a real regret: she wished she had not waited until the whole novel was finished, but had published it in installments through the 1950s and early 1960s as British writers did, naming Powell, Durrell, and Lessing as models. She told Moore the final Esther Longtree section would have been titled "Mule," which matches the "mule woman" she describes in the interviews, and repeated that the book holds many novellas that might have made a different reputation had they appeared separately. She also mentioned having come across a few old novellas, perhaps sections cut from the novel, that she never sent, the same phantom collection that surfaces across the interviews.

The Debs book and Knopf

Moore gives the clearest publishing account of the Debs biography's troubles. In 1994 and 1995 Young doubted that Knopf truly meant to publish "Harp Song for a Radical," since the house said the long book would "have to be cut," and she asked whether Dalkey would bring out "the uncut book" instead. O'Brien saw the value in publishing so "legendary" a work and agreed, but when Young called Knopf to take it back, her editor there, Victoria Wilson, insisted the house wanted it, so it stayed. The negotiations were confused by her failing health, Moore recalling that she was "out of it most of the time" and increasingly worked through a friend or cousin as go-between, and then she died. This corroborates from the publisher's side what the Neville profile and the Dowling chapter report from elsewhere, that the Debs book was published posthumously and cut down rather than as Young intended, a caution for anyone who would cite it as her finished work.

The person a publisher met

Moore's portrait of Young in 1992 is affectionate but unsparing. They met in a West Village diner favored by transvestites, one of whom greeted her, and Moore notes that the novel was a favorite of the gay literary community. Asked for her Social Security number to sign a contract, she dug in her purse and produced what turned out to be her dentist's phone number. Invited to amend the novel's dedication in his copy, she crossed it out and then chose to leave it as it was, and she handed him a packet of old photographs that became the reissue covers and the festschrift inserts. Walking to her Bleecker Street apartment she held his hand and moved very slowly; inside were books, "boxes and boxes of manuscripts" she was readying for sale to Yale's Beinecke Library, and countless dolls. Moore could get little concrete from her, since every question drew "a flight of fancy" that was "entertaining but not always informative," the same storyteller the Neville profile describes. He is candid, too, about a vanity that the reverent sources soften: she made sure he knew of her descent from Brigham Young and relayed her "dear friend" Saul Bellow's praise, and she treated the Oxford go-between coldly, at times "like a lackey." It is the least idealized close-up of Young in the whole set, and a useful corrective.

Set against the other sources

As the publishing capstone, this memoir documents the machinery behind the revival that the Neville profile and the Dowling chapter only reference, and it confirms three recurring threads with new specificity. It fixes the fate of the archive, Young's papers to the Beinecke, where the Marguerite Young collection now sits. It corroborates the cut, posthumous Debs biography, adding the detail that the Knopf editor Victoria Wilson held onto it against Young's wish to reclaim it. And it extends the excised-novellas theme that runs from the 1965 interviews onward, giving the Esther section its intended title, "Mule," and recording Young's own regret that she did not serialize the book. Set beside Neville's tender deathbed portrait, Moore's franker account of a vain, declining, sometimes difficult woman in her final years rounds out the picture rather than contradicting it, and his framing of her neglect, as the result of critics' fixed notions of canon and genre that she "stands outside of," names the same problem the Fuchs essay diagnoses from the critical side. For the compilation this is the source to cite on the publishing history, the archive, and the honest texture of her last decade.
