Summary

Catherine's memories of Hannah are vague and clouded by her dreams of an immortal love. She has reinterpreted Peron's neglect as a devotion too large to express. We learn that Catherine never believed in women's suffrage, the cause of the woman who tried hardest to save her. Mr. Spitzer adds his anecdotes, and Hannah appears in memory with keys and jeweled sword. Vera names the house for what it has become: a loony bin by the sea.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens by portraying Cousin Hannah as a formidable, almost mythic figure who never reconciled with life and whose identity remains blurred in memory. This passage sets the tone for the chapter’s exploration of memory, identity, and legacy. It emphasizes Hannah’s singularity and the elusive nature of her life and spirit, framing her as a person who lived on the outermost edges of experience.
  • Catherine is described as indefinite, clouded by dreams of immortal love and escape, living in a world of opium-induced fantasies and unawakened romance, in contrast to Cousin Hannah’s stark reality and fiery spirit. This contrast highlights two modes of existence: one engaged with harsh realities and causes (Hannah), and the other retreating into beautiful but unreal dreams (Catherine).
  • The chapter repeatedly emphasizes Cousin Hannah’s adventurous spirit and her sacrifices for the suffrage movement, portraying these efforts as both heroic and, from Catherine’s perspective, somewhat futile or unworthy of Hannah's potential. This passage reveals Catherine’s ambivalence (envy mixed with guilt) as she reflects on Hannah’s committed activism versus her own life of dreams and withdrawal.
  • Catherine’s reflections on love are poignant and laden with sorrow. She imagines a love greater than any realized affection, a "dead love which never was and never would be real," expressed in silence and absence rather than presence. She interprets Peron’s neglect as a form of love out of fear of her power, suggesting a profound but unspoken emotional tension. This theme of unattainable love permeates the chapter.
  • There are vivid passages describing Catherine’s dreams of travel and escape to exotic places (Egypt, Italy, the land of the Kurds) and her missed opportunities for a different life. These passages symbolize longing, regret, and the tension between the dream of freedom and the reality of confinement, both physical and psychological.
  • Catherine’s mind is portrayed as wandering and unreliable, filled with illusions and shifting memories. The narrative suggests that her dreams, though inaccurate, might hold a deeper truth than conventional reality. This raises philosophical questions about the nature of truth, memory, and perception.
  • Cousin Hannah is depicted as "sharp-witted, dashing, wild and reckless," a figure of masculine virtues and relentless energy, "like a finely tempered blade" who never stopped despite adversity. This passage celebrates her fortitude and contrasts it with Catherine’s frailty and passivity.
  • The chapter discusses mysterious, mystical elements: the whisperings of the dead, the music of the spheres, and spectral visions, suggesting a world where life and death intermingle and where memory and existence are fluid. Cousin Hannah’s belief that the dead are silent and that her battles were with the living underscores the existential tension between life’s visible struggles and the ineffable beyond.
  • Despite her admiration for Cousin Hannah’s strength and purpose, Catherine is disappointed that Hannah is not the romantic hero she imagined, a knight or prince who could awaken her from her dreams. This passage reflects Catherine’s deep yearning for connection and meaning, mingled with a sense of loss.
  • The chapter closes with reflections on the instability of time and identity, suggesting that even the most certain facts of time, place, person may be illusions or dreams. Mr. Spitzer’s observations about disorientation and the blurring of reality and illusion reinforce the chapter’s overarching meditation on perception

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Cousin Hannah

  • Described as a "great captain of extremes," a resolute and brave suffragette who sacrificed much for her cause.
  • Adventurous and daring, having traveled "to the outermost reaches of space," symbolizing exploration beyond physical and societal boundaries.
  • Exhibits masculine virtues: sharp, shrewd, incisive, relentless and compared to a "finely tempered blade."
  • Seen as singularly devoted to her cause, seemingly indifferent to love or personal attachments.
  • Her presence is associated with powerful imagery: whistling sounds, iron keys, jeweled swords, and snow storms.
  • A figure of mystery and paradox; she weeps for subtle reasons (e.g., a snowflake touching her hand), showing a hidden emotional depth.
  • Her life is characterized by perpetual motion and battle, never fully reconciled with peace or rest.
  • Represents strength, fortitude, and the harsh realities of activism and sacrifice.

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • Portrayed as dreamy, opium-addicted, lost in illusions and fantasies of an "immortal love" and lost romances.
  • She envies Cousin Hannah’s adventures but remains passive, bound to memories and dreams rather than action.
  • Her mind is clouded, veiled by dreams that are as much about escape as about longing.
  • She imagines herself as a great courtesan with many imaginary lovers, embodying a spectral beauty trapped in time.
  • Her love life is described as unfulfilled, marked by silence and absence, especially in relation to a man named Peron.
  • She distorts reality, unable to fully grasp or engage with the present moment.
  • She has a sense of guilt about her withdrawal and dreams, contrasted with Cousin Hannah’s active life.
  • Her life is marked by isolation and a yearning for an "unearthly romance" free from mortal change.
  • Represents passivity, longing, and the desire for transcendence.

Mr. Spitzer

  • His mind is clouded and dwells on abstract, cosmic music and metaphysical concepts.
  • Symbolizes a detached intellectual or mystical observer, contrasting with the passionate activism of Cousin Hannah and the dreamy passivity of Catherine.
  • His perspective reflects on the limitations of human understanding and the ineffability of life’s mysteries.

Research: Themes

Memory and Reality

See: Illusion and Reality · Memory and the Past

  • The novel blurs the lines between memory, dream, and reality.
  • Catherine’s recollections are described as "inaccurate," "clouded," and "untrustworthy," reflecting the elusive nature of truth.
  • The narrative questions the reliability of memory and whether dreams may contain a deeper accuracy than factual truth.
  • Time is fluid, with events and identities slipping and merging, e.g., Catherine confusing visitors or historical periods.

Love and Absence

See: Love · Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • Love is portrayed as an unfulfilled, silent, and distant force.
  • Catherine’s love is for an absent or dead man, expressed through silence and neglect rather than conventional gestures.
  • Peron’s love is described as “greater than hatred” but unexpressed, a "dead love which never was and never would be real," paradoxically eternal.
  • Cousin Hannah is described as never having loved, or only loving the “image of the dead,” emphasizing emotional coldness or detachment.

Sacrifice and Activism

  • Cousin Hannah embodies sacrifice for a cause, dedicating her life to suffrage and battling societal "ogres."
  • Her life is one of relentless struggle and fortitude, marked by physical and emotional trials.
  • Catherine contrasts this with her own withdrawal and guilt for not engaging in such struggles.

Escapism and Fantasy

See: Illusion and Reality · Dreams and Imagination

  • Catherine lives largely in fantasy, opium dreams, and romanticized illusions.
  • Catherine imagines exotic travels, lost loves, and mystical experiences that contrast sharply with her sedentary reality.
  • Cousin Hannah’s life, though real and harsh, also contains elements of myth and legend, as perceived by Catherine..

Death and Immortality

See: Death and Mortality

  • Death is omnipresent but paradoxically intertwined with immortality in the narrative.
  • Catherine and Cousin Hannah seem caught between life and death, reality and dream.
  • The novel references the “perennial dead” and the idea that death does not end mystery or existence.
  • The dead’s silence contrasts with the living’s whisperings, with Cousin Hannah focusing on the living and Catherine on the dead.

Research: Resources

  • Musica Universalis (music of the spheres) concept incorporates the metaphysical principle that mathematical relationships express qualities or “tones” of energy that manifests in numbers, visual angles, shapes and sounds which are all connected within a pattern of proportion. This has been explored by Pythagoras, Plato, Aristotle (he rejected the idea), Boethius developed three categories of music (musica mundana, musica humana, and musica quae in quibusdam constituta est instrumentis), Keppler expounded on the idea and cherry picked data to support his claims. They have since been refuted. Boethius developed imagery to go with his writings and I believe Young took inspiration from these illustrations for the novel. Musica universalis serves as a profound metaphor for the cosmic order, the spiritual unity beyond earthly fragmentation, and the haunting, silent music that connects life, death, memory, and the infinite. It highlights the novel’s exploration of metaphysical questions about existence, identity, and the search for meaning beyond the limits of human perception and experience.
  • Human Will or Free Will is the capacity of humans to make choices and decisions independently of external forces. Philosophy has debated the existence and nature of free will with arguments for and against. Determinism is the opposite of free will. Religions grapple with the concept of free will and attempt to reconcile it with divine sovereignty and predestination. Free will is closely linked with moral responsibility which is used to hold individuals accountable for their actions. Self-determination is part of free will where individuals choose their thoughts, words, and deeds. The environment a person is surrounded by also strongly influences human actions as well as instincts and inherited dispositions. The act of will allows individuals to direct and control their thoughts and actions and help them formulate and work towards fulfilling goals. In the chapter the significance of human will or free will is explored in a complex and nuanced manner, reflecting the novel’s broader themes of illusion, fate, and the limits of human understanding. Humans are not the architects of fate or destiny, and their freedom is limited by the ordered, but often harsh, conditions set by a God or nature. Individuals should "mind their own knitting" and live in the present moment, carefully attending to their responsibilities without chasing impossible dreams. At the same time, the chapter acknowledges the tension between this acceptance and the human desire for freedom and change. Miss MacIntosh is depicted as someone who neither denies the reality of suffering nor yields to despair; rather, she embraces labor, self-forgetfulness, and service to others as the true expression of human will within the bounds of mortal existence. Her will is not about imposing arbitrary control over life’s uncertainties but about perseverance and making the best of one’s circumstances. The novel also suggests that the illusion of complete free will is just that, an illusion. The individual is bound by the web of life’s circumstances, time, and fate, and the ultimate truth might be that the "individual is the one illusion". This idea challenges the notion of absolute free will, instead positing a universe where human wills are intertwined and limited within a grand cosmic logic that often eludes understanding. Human will or free will is significant as it embodies the struggle between acceptance of life's inevitable limitations and the desire for autonomy and meaning. Miss MacIntosh represents the choice to embrace reality with clear eyes and firm resolve, finding dignity and purpose in the acceptance of one’s mortal condition, while the narrative acknowledges the profound ambiguity and complexity of free will within the greater cosmic order. Human will is real but bounded, expressed best through practical endurance, moral clarity, and service, rather than through fantasies of absolute freedom or control over fate.

It can be understood as representing the eternal, unchanging spiritual realms or dimensions, where the soul and spirit exist beyond physical mortality and temporal constraints. It highlights the novel's recurring theme of the interplay between mortal life, death, and the possibility of immortal spiritual existence.

  • Catherine does not believe in suffrage and that was common among rich, white women from elite social and political backgrounds at the time. They argued it would disrupt gender roles and the family structure, they expressed doubts as to women’s political capacity, they opposed broader progressive reforms (prohibition), racism because they feared the right to vote would spread to women of color, they argued it would simply double the size of the electorate without adding an new value or perspective. Opposition also came from business and industry since it may lead to increased regulation and reforms that would hurt their profits.

  • Heraclitus and the Flux - he emphasized the constant state of flux and change in the universe, implying that perpetual motion is also a metaphor for the ever-changing nature of reality. Heraclitus’s philosophy in this context conveys that the world and ourselves are never static but perpetually evolving, and this continuous movement is both the source of life’s mystery and its challenge. The chapter meditates on how characters like Mr. Spitzer and Vera’s mother (Catherine) experience this flux (caught between reality and dreams, life and death, presence and oblivion) highlighting the fragile, transient, and often ambiguous nature of human existence.
  • Parmenides and the Paradox of Motion - Parmenides, an ancient Greek philosopher, argued that change and motion are illusions and that reality is unchanging and indivisible. This paradox (the question of how motion and change can exist if reality is fundamentally fixed and eternal) echoes the novel's exploration of the fluid boundaries between life and death, reality and illusion, presence and absence. In this chapter, the narrative reflects on the elusive nature of being, the difficulty of grasping a stable self or reality amid constant flux and transformation. The novel discusses how perceptions and memories shift, how the self might be divided or multiple, and how time and space themselves seem unstable or illusory. The paradox of motion in the context of the novel underscores the characters' struggles with their own existence, the impermanence of life, and the search for meaning in a world that seems to continuously dissolve and reform, much like Parmenides' philosophical dilemma. The mention of Parmenides and the paradox of motion highlights the novel's deep engagement with questions about the nature of reality, the illusion of change, and the elusive quest for permanence amidst inevitable flux.
  • Philosophical debate around perpetual motion explores the limits of human knowledge, the nature of reality, and the relationship between theory and practice. The chapter presents the idea that life and death are intertwined in a kind of endless cycle or flow, with no clear boundaries or finality. The chapter also reflects on the paradoxes of identity and existence, the relations between body, soul, and spirit, and the elusive nature of reality and perception. This is analogous to the concept of perpetual motion in that the philosopher or thinker is caught in an unending process of motion and change, never reaching a final resolution. The chapter discusses how music, as a metaphor for life and the universe, is a continuous flow that never fully resolves or ends, akin to perpetual motion. The novel philosophically explores the themes of endless cycles, the ambiguity of beginnings and ends, and the continuity of existence, all of which resonate with the concept of perpetual motion as an unceasing process without final rest or resolution.
  • Zeno’s paradoxes - the finite can contain the infinite. Neil deGrasse Tyson “there are an infinite number of steps to a finite destination”. Young discusses Zeno's paradoxes in the context of the characters’ reflections on life, death, and existence. They are trying to understand the idea of progress and the nature of time, for example, by questioning how two parallel lines might meet or how one might justify sorrows if one had experienced nothing. The chapter conveys a sense of confusion about the continuity of life and death and the elusive nature of reality. It refers to Zeno's paradoxes by highlighting the puzzlement about motion and the passage of time, such as the difficulty in knowing if one has truly moved forward or if one is caught in an endless cycle, like walking in circles or being lost between two polarities. They reflect on the idea of being between two worlds, the living and the dead, and the uncertainty of identity and existence, which echoes the philosophical challenge posed by Zeno's paradoxes regarding infinite divisibility and the nature of motion. This discussion illustrates the novel's broader themes of ambiguity, the interplay between reality and illusion, and the complexity of human consciousness and experience.
