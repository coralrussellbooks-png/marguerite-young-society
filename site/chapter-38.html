To All My Darlings

Chapter 38

On Chapter 38 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 3, 2026

← Chapter 37 · Home · Chapter 39 →

Summary

Catherine’s memories of Cousin Hannah are clouded by opium and by her dream of an immortal love, and Mr. Spitzer, being as one dead, must be prodded to remember anything at all. Catherine thought the lonely cause of suffrage unworthy of Hannah’s adventurous spirit and a waste of time. She would have been happy, she insists, if only Peron had lived, and she explains his neglect as a love too great to express: “This dead love which never was and never would be real was the one undying love.” Hannah had invited her to Egypt to show that a woman could be both beautiful and independent of man, and Catherine imagines the life she might have had, picketing Buckingham Palace, sleeping in silken tents, escaping from moated castles. She remembers Hannah’s sharp, masculine virtues with envy and guilt, wonders whom Hannah had loved, and is sorry that Hannah was not a knight or a Prince Charming come to wake her. Mr. Spitzer observes that most people are disoriented as to time, place, and person, and Vera calls the house what it has become, “this loony bin.”

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens by portraying Cousin Hannah as a formidable, almost mythic figure who never reconciled with life and whose identity remains blurred in memory. This passage sets the tone for the chapter’s exploration of memory, identity, and legacy. It emphasizes Hannah’s singularity and the elusive nature of her life and spirit, framing her as a person who lived on the outermost edges of experience.
  • Catherine is described as indefinite, clouded by dreams of immortal love and escape, living in a world of opium-induced fantasies and unawakened romance, in contrast to Cousin Hannah’s stark reality and fiery spirit. This contrast highlights two modes of existence: one engaged with harsh realities and causes (Hannah), and the other retreating into beautiful but unreal dreams (Catherine).
  • The chapter repeatedly emphasizes Cousin Hannah’s adventurous spirit and her sacrifices for the suffrage movement, portraying these efforts as both heroic and, from Catherine’s perspective, somewhat futile or unworthy of Hannah’s potential. This passage reveals Catherine’s ambivalence (envy mixed with guilt) as she reflects on Hannah’s committed activism versus her own life of dreams and withdrawal.
  • Catherine’s reflections on love are poignant and laden with sorrow. She imagines a love greater than any realized affection, a “dead love which never was and never would be real,” expressed in silence and absence rather than presence. She interprets Peron’s neglect as a form of love out of fear of her power, suggesting a profound but unspoken emotional tension. This theme of unattainable love permeates the chapter.
  • There are vivid passages describing Catherine’s dreams of travel and escape to exotic places (Egypt, Italy, the land of the Kurds) and her missed opportunities for a different life. These passages symbolize longing, regret, and the tension between the dream of freedom and the reality of confinement, both physical and psychological.
  • Catherine’s mind is portrayed as wandering and unreliable, filled with illusions and shifting memories. The narrative suggests that her dreams, though inaccurate, might hold a deeper truth than conventional reality. This raises philosophical questions about the nature of truth, memory, and perception.
  • Cousin Hannah is depicted as “sharp-witted, dashing and wild and reckless,” a figure of masculine virtues and relentless energy, “like a finely tempered blade” who never stopped despite adversity. This passage celebrates her fortitude and contrasts it with Catherine’s frailty and passivity.
  • The chapter discusses mysterious, mystical elements: the whisperings of the dead, the music of the spheres, and spectral visions, suggesting a world where life and death intermingle and where memory and existence are fluid. Cousin Hannah’s belief that the dead are silent and that her battles were with the living underscores the existential tension between life’s visible struggles and the ineffable beyond.
  • Despite her admiration for Cousin Hannah’s strength and purpose, Catherine is disappointed that Hannah is not the romantic hero she imagined, a knight or prince who could awaken her from her dreams. This passage reflects Catherine’s deep yearning for connection and meaning, mingled with a sense of loss.
  • The chapter closes with reflections on the instability of time and identity, suggesting that even the most certain facts of time, place, person may be illusions or dreams. Mr. Spitzer’s observations about disorientation and the blurring of reality and illusion reinforce the chapter’s overarching meditation on perception.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • Catherine believes “this dead love which never was and never would be real was the one undying love.” Why would an unreal love outlast a real one?
  • “Whichever one had lived, she would have missed the absent one.” What does Catherine need absence for?
  • “Perhaps it was that great captain who was weak, my mother who was strong.” Could Vera be right?

Characters

  • Hannah invited Catherine to Egypt to show “that one could be both beautiful and independent of man.” Why does Catherine refuse?
  • Hannah “had wept if she heard a skirt whirling, rustling in the wind.” What is she mourning?
  • Mr. Spitzer must be “prodded and forced to remember.” What does he gain by forgetting?

Connections

  • In Chapter 34 Hannah wore “iron keys” at her belt. Here she rattles them while “fighting a mirror with her jeweled sword.” What is she fighting?
  • Catherine wished Hannah were “the Prince Charming to awaken this Sleeping Beauty.” Who else in the novel waits to be awakened?
  • Mr. Spitzer asks whether he is “a man or a butterfly.” How does this echo the dreamer of Chapter 37?

Reflection

  • Is action “no necessary criterion or measure of life”?
  • Can a life lived for a cause be lived “in only one dimension”?
  • Who in the story is the sane one in “this loony bin”?

Research: Characters

Cousin Hannah

  • Described as a “great captain of extremes,” a resolute and brave suffragette who sacrificed much for her cause.
  • Adventurous and daring, having traveled “to the outermost reaches of space,” symbolizing exploration beyond physical and societal boundaries.
  • Exhibits masculine virtues: sharp, shrewd, incisive, relentless and compared to a “finely tempered blade.”
  • Seen as singularly devoted to her cause, seemingly indifferent to love or personal attachments.
  • Her presence is associated with powerful imagery: whistling sounds, iron keys, jeweled swords, and snow storms.
  • A figure of mystery and paradox; she weeps for subtle reasons (e.g., a snowflake touching her hand), showing a hidden emotional depth.
  • Her life is characterized by perpetual motion and battle, never fully reconciled with peace or rest.
  • Represents strength, fortitude, and the harsh realities of activism and sacrifice.
  • She greeted Catherine with “Babylon is fallen.”
  • She invited Catherine to Egypt “to show… that one could join her movement for reasons other than being ugly and old, that one could be both beautiful and independent of man.”
  • “She had burned like a live coal. Snow buried many cities but did not bury her.”
  • She wept “if she heard a skirt whirling, rustling in the wind.”

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • Portrayed as dreamy, opium-addicted, lost in illusions and fantasies of an “immortal love” and lost romances.
  • She envies Cousin Hannah’s adventures but remains passive, bound to memories and dreams rather than action.
  • Her mind is clouded, veiled by dreams that are as much about escape as about longing.
  • She imagines herself as a great courtesan with many imaginary lovers, embodying a spectral beauty trapped in time.
  • Her love life is described as unfulfilled, marked by silence and absence, especially with Peron.
  • She greeted Hannah, “Hello, traveller! And where do you come from now?”
  • “Oh, how happy, happy, happy my mother would have been… if only the great gambler had lived.”
  • She dreamed “she had found a stairway to heaven,” by which she could “over-hear the decrees and whisperings of the dead.”
  • Her eyes were “blurred, cobwebbed by her predisposed illusions, by nightshade and bella donna.”
  • She distorts reality, unable to fully grasp or engage with the present moment.
  • She has a sense of guilt about her withdrawal and dreams, contrasted with Cousin Hannah’s active life.
  • Her life is marked by isolation and a yearning for an “unearthly romance” free from mortal change.
  • Represents passivity, longing, and the desire for transcendence.

Mr. Spitzer

  • His mind is clouded and dwells on abstract, cosmic music and metaphysical concepts.
  • Symbolizes a detached intellectual or mystical observer, contrasting with the passionate activism of Cousin Hannah and the dreamy passivity of Catherine.
  • His perspective reflects on the limitations of human understanding and the ineffability of life’s mysteries.
  • “Being as one dead,” he “must be prodded and forced to remember.”
  • He is “writing now his nocturne in memory of her.”
  • Most people, he observes, “were at some level disoriented, unaware of the existing situation with reference to such apparently clear matters as time, place, identity of persons.”

Peron Spitzer

  • He was “this man of great wit but little brain—no legal knowledge in his head, no intellectual pretensions whatever.”
  • Catherine believes “he had expressed his love by his silence, his distance.”
  • “Sometimes she thought that only he lived.”

Research: Themes

Memory and Reality

See: Illusion and Reality · Memory and the Past

  • The chapter blurs the lines between memory, dream, and reality.
  • Catherine’s recollections are described as “inaccurate,” “clouded,” and “untrustworthy,” reflecting the elusive nature of truth.
  • The narrative questions the reliability of memory and whether dreams may contain a deeper accuracy than factual truth.
  • Time is fluid, with events and identities slipping and merging, e.g., Catherine confusing visitors or historical periods.

Love and Absence

See: Love · Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • Love is portrayed as an unfulfilled, silent, and distant force.
  • Catherine loves an absent, dead man whose love, she believes, was expressed through silence and neglect rather than conventional gestures.
  • Peron’s love is described as “his love greater than his hatred” but unexpressed, a “dead love which never was and never would be real,” paradoxically eternal.
  • Cousin Hannah is described as never having loved, or only loving the “image of the dead,” though she weeps at a snowflake or a rustling skirt.

Sacrifice and Activism

See: Women’s Suffrage

  • Cousin Hannah embodies sacrifice for a cause, dedicating her life to suffrage and battling societal “ogres.”
  • Her life is one of relentless struggle and fortitude, marked by physical and emotional trials.
  • Catherine contrasts this with her own withdrawal and guilt for not engaging in such struggles.

Escapism and Fantasy

See: Illusion and Reality · Dreams and Imagination

  • Catherine lives largely in fantasy, opium dreams, and romanticized illusions.
  • Catherine imagines exotic travels, lost loves, and mystical experiences that contrast sharply with her sedentary reality.
  • Cousin Hannah’s life, though real and harsh, also contains elements of myth and legend, as perceived by Catherine.

Death and Immortality

See: Death and Mortality

  • Death is omnipresent but paradoxically intertwined with immortality in the narrative.
  • Catherine and Cousin Hannah seem caught between life and death, reality and dream.
  • The chapter references the “perennial dead” and the idea that death does not end mystery or existence.
  • The dead’s silence contrasts with the living’s whisperings, with Cousin Hannah focusing on the living and Catherine on the dead.

Research: Resources

  • “the beautiful music of the spheres”: Musica Universalis (music of the spheres) concept incorporates the metaphysical principle that mathematical relationships express qualities or “tones” of energy that manifests in numbers, visual angles, shapes and sounds which are all connected within a pattern of proportion. This has been explored by Pythagoras, Plato, Aristotle (he rejected the idea), Boethius developed three categories of music (musica mundana, musica humana, and musica quae in quibusdam constituta est instrumentis), Kepler expounded the idea in Harmonices Mundi (1619). Boethius’s writings were later illustrated, and Young may have taken inspiration from these illustrations for the novel. See the Character Hubs. Musica universalis serves as a profound metaphor for the cosmic order, the spiritual unity beyond earthly fragmentation, and the haunting, silent music that connects life, death, memory, and the infinite. It highlights the novel’s exploration of metaphysical questions about existence, identity, and the search for meaning beyond the limits of human perception and experience. See Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres, Plato and Platonism, and Aristotle.
  • “its sound as of the human will”: Human Will or Free Will is the capacity of humans to make choices and decisions independently of external forces. Philosophy has debated the existence and nature of free will with arguments for and against. Determinism is the opposite of free will. Religions grapple with the concept of free will and attempt to reconcile it with divine sovereignty and predestination. Free will is closely linked with moral responsibility which is used to hold individuals accountable for their actions. Self-determination is part of free will where individuals choose their thoughts, words, and deeds. The environment a person is surrounded by also strongly influences human actions as well as instincts and inherited dispositions. The act of will allows individuals to direct and control their thoughts and actions and help them formulate and work towards fulfilling goals. Miss MacIntosh’s view of the human will is in the Chapter 3 resources.
    • Schopenhauer made music the direct image of the will: it “is as immediate an objectification and copy of the whole will as the world itself” (Arthur Schopenhauer, The World as Will and Idea 1.52, 1819, trans. R. B. Haldane and J. Kemp, 1883). See Schopenhauer.
    • Augustine defended the freedom of the will against predestination’s critics in On Free Choice of the Will (388-395). See Augustine and Christian Theology.
  • “seven deathless eternities”: “Seven deathless eternities” may recall Dante’s Divine Comedy, where the seven terraces of Purgatory purge the seven deadly sins. Seven heavens are found in Judaism and Islam, and Hindu cosmology has seven upper worlds, the lokas. The seven virtues join the four cardinal virtues of Greek philosophy to the three theological virtues of Paul (1 Corinthians 13:13). It can be understood as representing the eternal, unchanging spiritual realms or dimensions, where the soul and spirit exist beyond physical mortality and temporal constraints. It highlights the novel’s recurring theme of the interplay between mortal life, death, and the possibility of immortal spiritual existence. See Dante, Judaism, and Hinduism.
  • “the seven-stringed harp”: The seven-stringed harp is the lyre, which the poet Terpander was said to have given seven strings. See Greek Mythology.
    • The seven strings bearing “soul and spirit and astral body” recall Theosophy’s seven principles of man, which include the astral body (H. P. Blavatsky, The Key to Theosophy, 1889). See Spiritualism and the Occult.
  • “polar lights accompanied by strong sounds”: Northern peoples and Arctic travellers have long reported that the aurora crackles, hisses, and whistles, a sound scientists long doubted. See Astronomy.
  • “The lonely cause of suffrage had seemed to my mother to be quite unworthy”: Catherine does not believe in suffrage, and that was common among rich, white women from elite social and political backgrounds at the time. They argued that it would disrupt gender roles and the family structure, doubted women’s political capacity, and opposed broader progressive reforms such as prohibition. Racism drove them too, for they feared the vote would spread to women of color. They argued that it would simply double the size of the electorate without adding any new value or perspective. Opposition also came from business and industry since it may lead to increased regulation and reforms that would hurt their profits. See Women’s Suffrage.
    • The National Association Opposed to Woman Suffrage was founded in 1911 by Josephine Dodge.
  • “Babylon is fallen”: “Babylon the great is fallen, is fallen” (Revelation 18:2). It symbolizes the collapse of old worlds and the onset of a new, uncertain reality: one marked by fragmentation, loss, and the haunting presence of what once was but is no longer. It is a metaphor for the disintegration of familiar orders and the inescapable passage into a realm where past certainties give way to mystery and ambiguity. See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
    • “Babylon is fallen, is fallen” (Isaiah 21:9). See The Bible.
  • “an Alpine crystal-hunter who had cut great steps in ice”: Crystal hunters, the Strahler of the Swiss Alps, have climbed for rock crystal since the Middle Ages. See Mountains.
  • “the heavenly bridegroom none should see”: “Behold, the bridegroom cometh; go ye out to meet him” (Matthew 25:6). See the Chapter 33 resources and The Bible.
  • “this great courtesan with a snow pompadour”: The pompadour, hair swept high off the forehead, is named for Madame de Pompadour (1721-1764), mistress of Louis XV.
  • “death’s dateless night”: “For precious friends hid in death’s dateless night” (Shakespeare, Sonnet 30). See Shakespeare.
  • “Yet though there was no time, though all were immortal”: Parmenides and the paradox of motion: Parmenides, an ancient Greek philosopher, argued that change and motion are illusions and that reality is unchanging and indivisible. This paradox (the question of how motion and change can exist if reality is fundamentally fixed and eternal) echoes the novel’s exploration of the fluid boundaries between life and death, reality and illusion, presence and absence. In this chapter, the narrative reflects on the elusive nature of being, the difficulty of grasping a stable self or reality amid constant flux and transformation. The novel discusses how perceptions and memories shift, how the self might be divided or multiple, and how time and space themselves seem unstable or illusory. The paradox of motion in the context of the novel underscores the characters’ struggles with their own existence, the impermanence of life, and the search for meaning in a world that seems to continuously dissolve and reform, much like Parmenides’ philosophical dilemma. The mention of Parmenides and the paradox of motion highlights the novel’s deep engagement with questions about the nature of reality, the illusion of change, and the elusive quest for permanence amidst inevitable flux.
  • “the cloud-capped towers”: See the Chapter 35 resources and Shakespeare.
  • “picketed Buckingham Palace”: Buckingham Palace is the monarch’s official London residence and a working royal palace.
    • On May 21, 1914, Emmeline Pankhurst was arrested at the palace gates as suffragettes tried to bring a petition to King George V. See Women’s Suffrage.
  • “the land of the somnolent Kurds”: Kurds or Kurdish people are an Iranian ethnic group native to the mountainous region of Kurdistan.
  • “like the greatest escape artist”: The greatest escape artist was Harry Houdini (1874-1926), who escaped from handcuffs, straitjackets, and his Chinese Water Torture Cell.
  • “lived all her life in Italy”: Italy’s history has influenced the world with its art, architecture, and legal systems: Roman Empire, Catholic Church, Renaissance, Art and Architecture, Culture. In the chapter it is a poetic and symbolic passage illustrating Catherine’s inner life: her dreams, her memories, and her yearning for a place and state of being beyond her present limitations. It contrasts sharply with her physical reality and underscores the novel’s themes of illusion, memory, and the search for meaning in suffering.
  • “There were jeweled daggers and poisoned cups”: The poisoned cups recall the Borgias, Pope Alexander VI and his son Cesare, whose enemies were said to die of poison.
  • “easeful dreams of a lost romance”: “I have been half in love with easeful Death” (John Keats, “Ode to a Nightingale,” 1819). See Romantic Poets.
  • “a funeral gondola black as a blackbird”: Franz Liszt wrote La lugubre gondola, “the funeral gondola,” in Venice in 1882, and the next year a gondola carried Wagner’s coffin down the Grand Canal. See Music and Composers.
  • “donjons and walls and angle towers”: Donjon is the great tower or innermost keep of a castle.
  • “that great cavalier servente”: In eighteenth-century Italy a cavalier servente, or cicisbeo, was a married woman’s acknowledged escort. Byron describes the custom in Beppo (1818). See Romantic Poets.
  • “all base metals were transmuted by time into finest gold”: The alchemists sought to transmute base metals into gold. See Astrology, Alchemy, and Esoterica.
  • “the aqua fortis of memory”: Aqua fortis is an archaic term for nitric acid.
    • Aqua fortis, “strong water,” dissolves silver but not gold, so assayers used it to part the two. See Astrology, Alchemy, and Esoterica.
  • “Life contained death as a cloud contained a passing star”: Zeno’s paradoxes: the finite can contain the infinite. Aristotle reports Zeno’s argument “that that which is in locomotion must arrive at the half-way stage before it arrives at the goal” (Aristotle, Physics 6.9, trans. R. P. Hardie and R. K. Gaye, 1930). The chapter conveys a sense of confusion about the continuity of life and death and the elusive nature of reality. The characters reflect on the idea of being between two worlds, the living and the dead, and the uncertainty of identity and existence, which echoes the philosophical challenge posed by Zeno’s paradoxes regarding infinite divisibility and the nature of motion. This discussion illustrates the novel’s broader themes of ambiguity, the interplay between reality and illusion, and the complexity of human consciousness and experience. See Aristotle.
  • “She had wept if she heard a skirt whirling”: Young told Studs Terkel that Cousin Hannah came partly from an Alpine story in which two bloomer girls shed their skirts at a summit, and the younger, going back for hers, was lost: “the lady lost her life for a skirt” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See the Chapter 31 resources. See Cousin Hannah.
  • “never oblique, never obtuse”: Catherine is described as obtuse (“But she was obtuse”), but Cousin Hannah is described as the opposite, acute. These descriptions are called “masculine virtues.” Catherine could not help admiring these traits, though she could not compete with them. The contrast between obtuse and acute highlights the difference between Catherine and Mr. Spitzer’s more passive, bewildered, or dreamlike states on the one hand, and Cousin Hannah’s active, clear-headed, and vigorous character on the other. It underscores the theme of clarity versus confusion, engagement versus withdrawal, and the tension between different ways of facing reality in the novel.
  • “a widely netted seine catching only the biggest star or fish”: There have been numerous studies on human memory with conclusions that it is very accurate or very inaccurate. They suggest although people don’t recall a large fraction of what they experience, what they do remember is accurate at least for a few days (Nicholas B. Diamond, Michael J. Armson, and Brian Levine, “The Truth Is Out There: Accuracy in Recall of Verifiable Real-World Events,” Psychological Science 31, no. 12, 2020). Memory is portrayed as both a source of knowledge and confusion, a bridge between the past and the present, and a key element in the characters’ understanding of life, death, and identity, but one that can never be fully trusted or comprehended.
  • “Not even when trains were stopped, buried by snow”: In the Great Blizzard of March 1888, trains were stranded in drifts from New York to New England. See Snow, Ice, and Winter.
  • “her grand idée fixe”: An idée fixe is an obsession. Berlioz gave the name to the melody that haunts his Symphonie fantastique (1830). See Music and Composers.
  • “the rescue of pale ladies held by one-eyed ogres”: See the Polyphemus entry in the Chapter 36 resources. See Homer’s Epics.
  • “Mr. Spitzer writing now his nocturne in memory of her”: The nocturne, a night piece for piano, was invented by John Field in 1812 and perfected by Chopin. See Music and Composers.
  • “she had found a stairway to heaven”: “And he dreamed, and behold a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven: and behold the angels of God ascending and descending on it” (Genesis 28:12). See Genesis.
  • “Cousin Hannah must have believed that the dead were silent”: “The dead praise not the LORD, neither any that go down into silence” (Psalm 115:17). See The Bible.
  • “being never twice the same”: Heraclitus and the flux: he emphasized the constant state of flux and change in the universe, implying that perpetual motion is also a metaphor for the ever-changing nature of reality. Heraclitus’s philosophy in this context conveys that the world and ourselves are never static but perpetually evolving, and this continuous movement is both the source of life’s mystery and its challenge. The chapter meditates on how characters like Mr. Spitzer and Vera’s mother (Catherine) experience this flux (caught between reality and dreams, life and death, presence and oblivion) highlighting the fragile, transient, and often ambiguous nature of human existence. See Heraclitus and Flux.
    • “Heracleitus is supposed to say that all things are in motion and nothing at rest; he compares them to the stream of a river, and says that you cannot go into the same water twice” (Plato, Cratylus 402a, trans. Benjamin Jowett). See Plato and Platonism.
  • “the word perpetual was that which had always frightened my mother”: Philosophical debate around perpetual motion explores the limits of human knowledge, the nature of reality, and the relationship between theory and practice. The chapter presents the idea that life and death are intertwined in a kind of endless cycle or flow, with no clear boundaries or finality. The chapter also reflects on the paradoxes of identity and existence, the relations between body, soul, and spirit, and the elusive nature of reality and perception. This is analogous to the concept of perpetual motion in that the philosopher or thinker is caught in an unending process of motion and change, never reaching a final resolution. The chapter discusses how music, as a metaphor for life and the universe, is a continuous flow that never fully resolves or ends, akin to perpetual motion. The novel philosophically explores the themes of endless cycles, the ambiguity of beginnings and ends, and the continuity of existence, all of which resonate with the concept of perpetual motion as an unceasing process without final rest or resolution.
  • “action was no necessary criterion or measure of life”: Those that take action, do things, and are preoccupied with accomplishments are rewarded. The mid-western, Protestant work ethic is set in these standards. The Protestant work ethic is implicitly reflected through the character of Miss MacIntosh and her attitudes toward life, labor, and morality (Max Weber, The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism, 1905). In 2021 young people in China began “lying flat” (Elsie Chen, “These Chinese Millennials Are ‘Chilling,’ and Beijing Isn’t Happy,” New York Times, July 3, 2021). It recalls Diogenes the Cynic, a Greek philosopher, who lived in a large storage jar in Athens, often called a barrel, scorning comfort and convention. Work is considered “a false idol” and people instead rest, exercise, and relax as a form of “resistance.” See Miss MacIntosh.
  • “these irrational, mystical, four-faced doorways”: Janus, the Roman god of doorways, had two faces, and Janus Quadrifrons, the four-faced Janus, had a temple with four doors. See Greek Mythology.
  • “Clovis or Cuthbert or Crispin or Charles the Mad or the Beloved or the Bold”: The use of important rulers and kings in the chapter serves to explore themes of mortality, the quest for order amid chaos, the limits of power, and the complex interplay of identity, illusion, and reality. It situates personal and political struggles within a larger cosmic and existential framework, deepening the novel’s meditation on life, death, and meaning.
    • Clovis was the first king of the Franks to unite all of the Frankish tribes under one ruler.
    • Cuthbert was an Anglo-Saxon saint in the Celtic tradition.
    • Crispin is the patron Saint of cobblers, curriers, tanners, and leather workers.
    • Charles the Mad was king of France. He is known for his mental illness and psychotic episodes. He was also called Charles the Beloved. He at times believed that he was made of glass.
    • Charles the Bold was the Duke of Burgundy.
  • “sans peur et sans reproche”: Sans peur et sans reproche means without fear and beyond reproach.
    • The phrase was the epithet of Pierre Terrail, seigneur de Bayard (1476-1524), “le chevalier sans peur et sans reproche.”
  • “a French chevalier who had travelled great distances”: French chevalier is a French title originally equivalent to the English knight.
  • “a Red Cross knight”: Catherine is sorry that Hannah “was not a French chevalier… or a Red Cross knight.” The Redcrosse Knight of Edmund Spenser’s The Faerie Queene (1590) stands for holiness. With Una he slays the dragon that has imprisoned her parents.
  • “Richard Coeur de Leon”: Richard Coeur de Leon, Richard the Lionheart, was King of England. He had the reputation for being a great military leader and warrior.
  • “the early French kings carrying their oriflammes”: The oriflamme was the battle standard of the kings of France in the Middle Ages, kept at the abbey of Saint-Denis.
  • “some old Rinaldo riding”: Rinaldo is a legendary knight in medieval France.
    • He is Renaud de Montauban, a paladin of Charlemagne, and the Rinaldo of Boiardo’s Orlando Innamorato (1483) and Ariosto’s Orlando Furioso (1516).
  • “his bayard, his wonderful bay steed”: Bayard is a magic bay horse ridden by Rinaldo. It had the magic ability to adjust its size to fit the rider.
    • It could stretch to carry all four sons of Aymon at once.
  • “the Prince Charming to awaken this Sleeping Beauty”: In Perrault’s “Sleeping Beauty” (1697) the whole household sleeps for a hundred years with the princess, until the prince comes. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “the life-sized chessmen”: In Through the Looking-Glass (1871) Alice finds the chessmen alive and walks into a life-sized game of chess. See Alice in Wonderland.
  • “whether he was a man or a butterfly”: Zhuangzi’s butterfly dream. See the Chapter 37 resources and Taoism and Zhuangzi.
  • “a lost psyche drifting down the wind”: In Greek psyche means both soul and butterfly. Apuleius tells the story of Psyche and Cupid in The Golden Ass. See Greek Mythology.
  • “most people, it had been Mr. Spitzer’s unfortunate observation, were at some level disoriented”: Doctors still test whether a patient is “oriented” to person, place, and time, the same three matters Mr. Spitzer names. See Madness and Sanity.
  • “by nightshade and bella donna”: Deadly nightshade, Atropa belladonna, enlarges the pupils and blurs the sight. Italian women are said to have used it to make their eyes beautiful, hence bella donna, “beautiful lady.” See The History of Opium.
  • “Lunacy reigned supreme”: Lunacy comes from the Latin luna, the moon, which was long believed to cause madness. See Moon and Stars and Madness and Sanity.

Vocabulary

Amulet
A charm worn against evil.
Astral
Having to do with the stars.
Barouche
A fashionable four-wheeled carriage with a folding half-hood and two seats facing each other.
Begetting
Fathering; producing offspring.
Bisect
To cut or divide into two parts.
Cavalier
A horseman or gallant gentleman; also offhand and disdainful.
Centaur
In Greek mythology, a creature with the head and upper body of a man and the body and legs of a horse.
Chamois
A goat-antelope of the Alps.
Chandelier
A branched hanging light.
Conquistador
A Spanish conqueror of the Americas.
Courtesan
A mistress of a rich or noble man.
Crystal-hunter
A prospector in the Alps who climbs into rock clefts to find quartz crystals.
Divertissement
A diversion or entertainment.
Donjon
The great tower or innermost keep of a castle.
Elegiac
Mournful, like an elegy, a poem of lament for the dead.
Espousal
The taking up of a cause; also marriage.
Eunuch
A man who has been castrated. Eunuchs were employed for centuries to guard harems and to serve as officials in royal courts in the Middle East, China, and Byzantium. See The eunuch (Ch 35).
Fitful
Starting and stopping; irregular.
Flagstone
A flat paving stone.
Fortitude
Courage in bearing pain or trouble.
Freebooter
A pirate or plunderer.
Frigid
Very cold; stiffly unfriendly.
Imperious
Proud and commanding.
Incisive
Sharp and clear in thought.
Ineffable
Too great or wonderful to be put into words.
Innumerable
Too many to count.
Insurrectionist
A rebel.
Jasper
An opaque, often red, variety of quartz.
Labyrinthine
Like a labyrinth; intricate and confusing.
Limitary
Of or at a limit.
Luminous
Giving off light.
Lute
A plucked string instrument with a rounded back shaped like half a pear, popular in Europe from the Middle Ages through the 1700s.
Misanthrope
A hater of humankind.
Mottled
Marked with spots or blotches of different colors.
Nomadic
Wandering from place to place.
Obdurate
Stubborn, unyielding.
Oblique
Indirect; slanting.
Obtuse
Slow to understand.
Ogre
A man-eating giant of folktale.
Perennial
Lasting or returning year after year.
Pinnacle
The highest point; a tall, pointed mountain peak or the slender spire on a building.
Plaintive
Sad and mournful in sound.
Precarious
Unstable and likely to fall or fail.
Preternatural
Beyond what is natural.
Renunciation
The act of giving something up.
Revivification
A bringing back to life.
Rivulet
A small stream.
Sahib
Master, a title for Europeans in colonial India.
Seine
To fish with a large hanging net.
Shades
Ghosts; the spirits of the dead.
Somnolent
Sleepy; causing sleep.
Spectre
A ghost.
Suffragette
A woman who campaigned for women’s right to vote. The word was coined by a London newspaper in 1906 for militant British activists. Most American activists called themselves suffragists. See Women’s Suffrage.
Tenebrous
Dark, shadowy, obscure.
Transient
Passing quickly; not lasting.
Transmutation
A change from one form or substance into another, especially the alchemists’ dream of turning base metal into gold.
Trenchant
Sharp, incisive.
Tryst
An appointed meeting, as of lovers.
Turnkey
A jailer.
Turret
A small tower on a building or castle.
Vagrant
Wandering, with no settled home.
