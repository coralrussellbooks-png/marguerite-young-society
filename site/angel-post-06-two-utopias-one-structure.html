Chapter 6. Two Utopias, One Structure

Part of a reading of Angel in the Forest (1945), Marguerite Young’s history of the two failed utopias at New Harmony, Indiana.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 5 · Angel in the Forest: A Reading in Fourteen Chapters · Chapter 7 →

Marguerite Young / Angel in the Forest, Chapter 6

On paper, Rapp and Owen could not be more opposite. One was a German peasant, the other an English cotton lord. One built his community on Scripture, the other on science. One was a tyrant who demanded submission, the other an egalitarian who dreamed of liberation. One enforced total celibacy, the other invited free love. One promised heaven, the other promised rational happiness on earth. If you were looking for two men who represented opposite answers to the question of how human beings should live, you could hardly assemble a cleaner pair.

Young put them on the same ground they inhabited in New Harmony, in the same half-mile square, one after the other, and the reason she did is to show that underneath the opposition they were the same error, made twice.

One fixed account, handed down before the people arrive

Both communities rest on a single, fixed account of what a human being is, settled in advance and imposed from above.

Rapp's account is theological. Man is fallen, the fall was sexual, the path back is renunciation, and the destination is the sexless angel. Owen's account is mechanistic. Man is nothing but the product of his circumstances, the path is correct social design, and the destination is rational happiness. These accounts contradict each other on every point of content. What matters is that they are identical in kind. Each is a complete and closed answer to the question of human nature, worked out before a single actual person walks through the gate of their utopia, and each is held by its author to be not one possible view but an absolute truth. And so neither account can make room for the people who actually show up. Rapp cannot see anyone who is not becoming an angel. Owen cannot see anyone who is not a machine awaiting adjustment. The specific blindness differs. The structure of their blindness is the same: a total theory of the person, and then an inability to perceive the persons who exceed it.

The Committee and the celibacy edict are the same instrument

Both communities require the individual to be pruned back for the sake of the group's coherence, and the pruning is the same operation whether it is done with a scalpel or with a clipboard.

In Rapp's community this is overt and brutal. The edict of celibacy, enforced on everyone, backed by the threat of the most drastic measures, is the suppression of individual difference in its rawest form. In Owen's community the same operation is gentler and looks nothing like it, which is exactly why it is worth noticing. There is a Committee that decides what counts as correct behavior, and when Thomas Pears is dismissed, the real reason, as Young sees it, is that the Committee objected to his soul, to his idea of the pursuit of happiness. That is the celibacy edict in a business suit. A central body has decided what a member is permitted to be, and a man who wants something the account does not authorize is corrected out of the way. Celibacy and free love are opposite rules. They are enforced by the same machine, a body that decides in advance what a person is allowed to want, and removes whatever does not fit.

The transfer, where the sameness becomes literal

The proof of all this is not an interpretation Young imposes. It is a fact of the historical record, and it is one of the most brilliant things in the book. In 1825, Robert Owen bought Harmony from George Rapp for a little under a hundred and fifty thousand dollars. He bought the houses, the factories, the lands, the goods in the store, the horses and cows and steers, the orchards, the furnace, the fire engine, the wharf. Rapp took almost nothing with him. In Young's grim detail, he took all but the castrated dogs, which he carried away.

So the physical place is perfectly continuous. The same granaries, the same workshops, the same dormitories, the same geometrical streets laid out in the shape of the New Jerusalem. Nothing material changes hands except the ownership. What is replaced is only the invisible thing, the entire metaphysical superstructure. The cruciform church where celibates had prayed for the angelic state became a dance hall where the Owenites danced in rational patterns feeling the confluence of beauty and truth. The dormitories that had enforced chastity filled with free love. The identical buildings now housed the exactly opposite belief. And then, in those same buildings, the Owenite community failed along the same arc the Rappite dream had traced, coming apart from within, its people strangers to one another, its fields going to weeds.

This is the argument made visible. Strip the two metaphysics off the top and what remains is one infrastructure and one collapse. The continuity of the place demonstrates that the difference in doctrine, enormous as it looked, made no difference at the level where these communities actually broke. Both were single fixed accounts pressed onto the same plural humanity in the same square half-mile, and both broke on the same rock. Rapp himself confirms the pattern without meaning to: when he moved to Pennsylvania he simply rebuilt Harmony and called it Economy, laid out exactly like the town he had left, because for a man who holds one fixed account, every new place is only the same place again.

The error is the certainty, not the content

The philosophy this study follows would say that the mistake in both cases is not being religious and not being rational. The mistake is the moment either man looked at his own constructed account of human life and called it simply the way things are, the given world, reality itself. Rapp did this with heaven. Owen did it with reason. The content is interchangeable, and that interchangeability is the whole point. Once you have mistaken your construction for the bedrock, the people who do not match it stop looking like people with a different view and start looking like defects to be repaired. That single move, treating a made thing as a found thing, is the error, and it is one error, not two. Young placed the utopias side by side so that the shared move would show through the opposed vocabularies.

Three men, one mistake

The reason to be sure this is structural and not just a quirk of utopian dreamers is Owen's own conservative partner, William Allen. Allen was a Quaker who entered the New Harmony partnership reluctantly, by his own account only to have the strength to oppose the infidel plans of Robert Owen. And Allen held a view that is the mirror image of Owen's radicalism while being the identical error underneath. He believed that the existing order, the whole division of society into governors and governed, rich and poor, learned and unlearned, was God's plan, the handwriting of God on every wall, and therefore not to be tampered with. Where Owen was certain the correct account of human life required tearing the old order down, Allen was certain the correct account required leaving it exactly as it stood.

Line the three of them up. Rapp is certain the true account of man is renunciation toward heaven. Owen is certain it is a rational redesign toward happiness. Allen is certain it is the existing hierarchy as divine arrangement. Three incompatible theories of human nature, and each man holds his own to be the truth rather than one construction among possible others, and each is wrong in the same way. Allen matters because he proves the error has no political home. It is not a disease of radicals or of conservatives. The revolutionary who imposes a new total account and the conservative who defends the existing order as God's own plan are performing the identical act: taking one built version of reality for the given world, and refusing to see the seams. The mistake is not left or right. It is the certainty itself.

And yet the building was not the error

Here the philosophy makes a turn that keeps all of this from collapsing into mere futility. The certainty was the error. The building was not.

Both communities failed, and both left something real behind that outlasted them. The Rappites' relentless productivity fed into the commerce of the whole Mississippi valley. Owen's experiment, for all its collapse, seeded the free schools, the public education, and the property and social rights of women that his son Robert Dale would spend a career fighting for, calling the temple of freedom not marble or carved pillars but a humble schoolhouse. Further out, Owen's labors ran into the British labor movement itself. None of these outcomes needed the original metaphysics to be true. The angel was a fiction and the science of society was a fiction, but the granary held real grain and the schoolhouse taught real children.

That is the distinction the book is built to hold. What was wrong in Rapp and Owen was the fixed certainty, the account of human nature mistaken for reality and enforced against actual people. What was worth something was the labor, and the labor survived the doctrine. You can build seriously, pour a life into the work, and still hold your account of things as a construction rather than the bedrock, and when you do, the building can leave something real behind precisely because you never needed it to be eternal or true forever to be worth doing. The lasting things came out of Rapp and Owen almost sideways, from the work rather than the creed, from everything they did that did not depend on their being right.

Two utopias, three certainties, one structure, one error. And the way past it is not a fourth and better account of human nature waiting to be found. It is the refusal to hold any account as the final one, the willingness to keep building on ground you know to be provisional, without ever again mistaking the scaffold for the earth.
