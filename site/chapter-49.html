On Chapter 49 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 5, 2026

Summary

In a frozen cosmos where ice covers the rivers, the boats, the archer, and the constellations, Mr. Spitzer lives when he is dead and wakes only when he sleeps. He weeps for others who do not weep for him, and above all for Martha, the last passenger pigeon, whose death he dates to September 14, 1918, “in a war-torn world” (she died September 1, 1914). He arrived too late to see her alive, rode all night on the train that carried her body to Washington, and begged the old caretaker of the Washington Museum to set a mirror before her so that she would not know she was alone; the caretaker refused, saying she had no soul and that it was the incapacity to be deceived which caused extinction. Mr. Spitzer would have indicted America before the Senate for what it had done to the pigeons and to the Indians, but he would have been threatened with the Federal pen. He remembers meeting an old sailor, the sole survivor of a shipwreck, and telling him that the greatest shipwreck is to sink when there is no storm. He comes to trust only the false, the blind, and the deaf, all who have lost, and walking through the rain among the pigeons he cannot tell whether he is one or two, or which brother he is.

Key passages

From the research

  • Mr. Spitzer experiences a profound disorientation where he “was disoriented by his great experience of living when he was dead, waking only when he was sleeping, sleeping in a watery grave or in an ice hole.” This passage encapsulates the paradoxical state of existence and non-existence, blurring the boundaries between life and death, wakefulness and sleep.
  • The imagery of landscapes and continents being lost or carried away like feathers or lace handkerchiefs symbolizes the transient, fragile, and mutable nature of reality itself.
  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates vast cosmic elements: “Ice had frozen so hard that it was like a dome or like the shell of an egg… Ice was frozen into shapes of frozen roofs over the winding rivers and the seas and the lakes… ice piled up like weird spires and minarets and bell towers.” This grand imagery conveys a frozen, timeless cosmos, a metaphor for stasis, death, or suspended animation of existence.
  • The passage also reflects on the impossibility of finding solid ground or stable meaning in a world dominated by illusions and transformations.
  • Mr. Spitzer mourns the death of Martha, the last passenger pigeon, noting the poignant historical moment: “Martha, who expired September 14, at one o’clock in the afternoon just as the clock was tolling one, 1918, A.D., at the Cincinnati zoo” (Young’s date; Martha died September 1, 1914). This event symbolizes the extinction of a species and, metaphorically, the loss of innocence, beauty, and connection to nature.
  • His grief for Martha becomes a symbol for his own loneliness and the universal sorrow at loss and disappearance: “He wept for others who wept not for him.”
  • The novel frequently questions the nature of the self: “He knew not whether he was himself or an image contained within an image… one of whom no unitary statement could be made.” This reflects a fragmented identity, the multiplicity of selves, or even the illusion of a stable self.
  • The motif of doubling and mirrored selves recurs: “how often he had nearly collided with himself though this would seem impossible.” This evokes existential ambiguity and the fluidity of personal identity across time and space.
  • Mr. Spitzer embraces a paradoxical philosophy: So he had come “to trust only the false… For why… should he trust the truth which had already failed him if the false would never fail.” This reflects a deep skepticism about knowledge, reality, and the reliability of perception or memory.
  • The passage suggests that constancy is found only in inconstancy, and that faith in truth is always tentative and fraught.
  • The chapter emphasizes the unreliability and fragmentation of memory: “His memory… had omitted so much, perhaps the whole content of mortal life, of certain death.”
  • Time is portrayed as fluid, uncertain, and perhaps even illusory: “Perhaps there was no time… What calendar keeps the sea of the months flowing into the months…?.”
  • Reality is likened to a dream or a fog: “the fog concealed the barnacle, the rose, the wing, the star… obliterated the obvious differences between the living and the dead.”
  • Mr. Spitzer reflects on the interrelatedness of life’s opposites: “He died through sickness, died through health… died through happiness which should have saved him, died through sorrow, sorrow by which he lived, died by not dying, died by living, lived by dying.”
  • Love is depicted as immortal and paradoxical: “when love lived, it died—but having died, it lived and could not die.”
  • The text repeatedly returns to Mr. Spitzer’s profound loneliness and his longing for understanding and communion: “He trusted those who had lost… all those who were dispossessed and wandering through the storm of years as he was. They drew him nearest to the city. They were all his loves because he could not love or could love only by indirection.”
  • His existential isolation is underscored by the metaphor of walking “through the rain of the city where the rain of the city drummed upon the dark or golden porches and roofs” and feeling himself “oblivious to them.”
  • Water, ice, and the sea are pervasive symbols throughout the chapter, representing both life and death, fluidity and stasis, memory and oblivion: He was like a shipwreck, “his hull broken… paying out more water than the seas contained.” The rising waters symbolize overwhelming forces beyond control and the engulfing nature of death and oblivion.
  • Near the end, Mr. Spitzer is described as “the living lighthouse at that one moment when he saw his light, moment when he was dying… his own light shining, shining from him.” This image captures the simultaneous presence of life and death, light and darkness, as well as the enduring, though fragile, spark of consciousness amid cosmic dissolution.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “There was nothing he feared so much as the light of the sun.” Why does Mr. Spitzer live by fog and shadow?
  • “Greatest shipwreck is this… to sink when there is no storm.” What is a shipwreck in calm water?
  • Mr. Spitzer’s paradox is “to trust only the false.” How can falsehood be more faithful than truth?

Characters

  • The old caretaker says “It was this incapacity to be deceived which caused extinction.” Why does Mr. Spitzer want a mirror placed before Martha?
  • Mr. Spitzer would indict America for “what America had done to passenger pigeons… and had done to Indians in their lonely tepees.” Why does he join the two?
  • “He trusted all persons suffering some fatal defect.” Why are the blind and the deaf his loves?

Connections

  • Young dates Martha’s death to 1918, “in a war-torn world,” though she died in 1914. Why might the date move to the last year of the war?
  • In Chapter 48 Mr. Spitzer imagined himself a stowaway on a ship that went down; here he is the shipwreck itself. How has the image changed?
  • “He did not know which brother he was, and this was the crux of his mystery.” How does this chapter answer Chapter 48’s question of whether the twins are one and the same?

Reflection

  • “Rarely had there been known in the annals of time the date of the death of the last member of an entire species.” What does it mean to be the last of one’s kind?
  • “Perhaps perfect silence was the perfection of love.” Is it?
  • “He was forgotten now, so should he be remembered then?” What makes a life remembered?

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • A complex figure symbolizing the human struggle with mortality, memory, and meaning.
  • Described metaphorically as a shipwreck, a shadow, a lighthouse, a phantom, and a musician whose music is unheard.
  • His physical ailments (e.g., irregular pulse, short breath) parallel his metaphysical disintegration and confusion.
  • He functions as a “mourner” and witness to extinction, loss, and the fading of worlds.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s paradoxical existence of alive yet dead, remembering yet forgetting makes him a liminal, almost mythical figure.

The Old Caretaker

  • Represents skepticism, finality, and the harsh reality of extinction.
  • His views on Martha the passenger pigeon emphasize the finality of death and the absence of spiritual continuation.
  • “It was this incapacity to be deceived which caused extinction, the old caretaker had announced.”

Peron Spitzer

  • “His brother could never have accepted an ineluctable reality such as Mr. Spitzer feared. His brother had thought that there was something he could do to change things, to make for greater hope or greater hopelessness.”
  • “They were born together.” Mr. Spitzer “did not know which brother he was, and this was the crux of his mystery.”

Martha (Passenger Pigeon)

  • Symbolizes lost beauty, extinction, and irretrievable loss.
  • Her death is mourned as the death of a species, a cosmic event, and a personal loss for Mr. Spitzer.

Research: Themes

Life, Death, and Immortality

See: Death and Mortality

  • Mr. Spitzer’s state blurs the boundary between living and dead. He lived “when he was dead, waking only when he was sleeping.”
  • Death is portrayed as a paradox: he “died at his zenith because he was at his nadir, died at his nadir because he was at his zenith.”
  • The text questions the reality of death and life, suggesting both may be illusions: “He knew there was no death because there was no life.”
  • The theme of immortality is paradoxical, with Mr. Spitzer’s spirit existing in a liminal state, neither fully present nor gone.

Memory and Forgetfulness

See: Memory and the Past

  • Mr. Spitzer struggles with remembering and forgetting, his past and identity constantly shifting.
  • His “forgetfulness veiling his forgetfulness” and “oblivion hiding behind his oblivion” illustrate layers of fading consciousness.
  • He questions the reliability of memory, pondering if he ever truly lived or if life was just a “mistaken dream.”

Identity and Self-Perception

See: Identity and the Self

  • There is a profound ambiguity regarding Mr. Spitzer’s identity, “He knew not whether he was himself or an image contained within an image.”
  • The motif of doubling, shadows, and reflections recurs, contributing to his fragmented self-understanding.
  • He often quarrels with himself or his shadow, suggesting an internal conflict and fractured self.

The Natural World and Extinction

See: Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos

  • The extinction of the passenger pigeon, Martha, serves as a poignant symbol of loss and mourning.
  • Nature is depicted as vast, mystical, and fragile all subject to decay and disappearance.
  • The natural world mirrors the human condition as being both transient, vulnerable, and caught in cycles of life and death.

The Cosmic and the Mystical

See: Faith, God, and the Supernatural · Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos

  • The cosmos, stars, and celestial imagery permeate the text, emphasizing vastness, mystery, and the unknowable.
  • Mr. Spitzer imagines his existence woven into universal patterns, yet feels lost and disoriented within them.
  • The narrative contemplates the idea of multiple dimensions, alternate realities, and the eternal recurrence of life and death.

Isolation and Connection

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Despite being surrounded by crowds, Mr. Spitzer experiences profound solitude and alienation.
  • His relationships are ambiguous since he mourns lost loves, mourns a lost species, and even quarrels with his own shadow.
  • The idea of failed communication and the search for understanding pervade, especially in his interactions with the blind and deaf.

    • Problem of Universals: The problem of universals is an ancient question from metaphysics that has inspired a range of philosophical topics and disputes. “Should the properties an object has in common with other objects, such as color and shape, be considered to exist beyond those objects? And if a property exists separately from objects, what is the nature of that existence?” The problem of universals relates to various inquiries closely related to metaphysics, logic, and epistemology, as far back as Plato and Aristotle, in efforts to define the mental connections a human makes when they understand a property such as shape or color to be the same in nonidentical objects. Universals are qualities or relations found in two or more entities. As an example, if all cup holders are circular in some way, circularity may be considered a universal property of cup holders. Further, if two daughters can be considered female offspring of Frank, the qualities of being female, offspring, and of Frank, are universal properties of the two daughters. Many properties can be universal: being human, red, male or female, liquid or solid, big or small, etc. Philosophers agree that human beings can talk and think about universals, but disagree on whether universals exist in reality beyond mere thought and speech. (Wikipedia)
    • There are many philosophical positions regarding universals (also found in Indian and Buddhist philosophy): Platonic realism is the view that universals or forms in this sense, are the causal explanation behind the notion of what things exactly are which is that universals are real entities existing independent of particulars. Aristotelian realism is the rejection of extreme realism. This position establishes the view of a universal as being that of the quality within a thing and every other thing individual to it which is that universals are real entities, but their existence is dependent on the particulars that exemplify them. Anti-realism is the objection to both positions. Anti-realism is divided into two subcategories; (1) Nominalism and (2) Conceptualism. (Wikipedia)
    • Realism: Realists tend to argue that universals must be posited as distinct entities in order to account for various phenomena. A common realist argument said to be found in Plato’s writings, is that universals are required for certain general words to have meaning and for the sentences in which they occur to be true or false. Take the sentence “Djivan Gasparyan is a musician” for instance. The realist may claim that this sentence is only meaningful and expresses a truth because there is an individual, Djivan Gasparyan, who possesses a certain quality: musicianship. Therefore, it is assumed that the property is a universal which is distinct from the particular individual who has the property. (Wikipedia)
    • Nominalism: Nominalists assert that only individuals or particulars exist and deny that universals are real. Four major forms of nominalism are predicate nominalism, resemblance nominalism, trope nominalism, and conceptualism. One with a nominalist view claims that one predicates the same property of/to multiple entities, but argues that the entities only share a name and do not have a real quality in common.Nominalists often argue this view by claiming that nominalism can account for all the relevant phenomena, and therefore, by Occam’s razor, and its principle of simplicity,nominalism is preferable, since it posits fewer entities. Different variants and versions of nominalism have been endorsed or defended by many. (Wikipedia)
    • Conceptualism: Conceptualism is a position that is meshed between realism and nominalism. Conceptualists believe that universals can indeed be real, but only existing as concepts within the mind. Conceptualists argue that the “concept” of universals are not mere “inventions but are reflections of similarities among particular things themselves.” For example, the concept of ‘man’ ultimately reflects a similarity amongst Socrates and Kant. (Wikipedia)
    • Taking “beauty” as example, each of these positions will state the following. Platonic realism: Beauty is a property that exists in an ideal form independently of any mind or description. Aristotelian realism: Beauty is a property that exists only when beautiful things exist. Conceptualism: Beauty is a property constructed in the mind, so exists only in descriptions of things. (Wikipedia)
    • Porphyry of Tyre was a Neoplatonic philosopher born in Tyre, Roman Phoenicia during Roman rule. He edited and published the Enneads, the only collection of the work of Plotinus, his teacher. He wrote original works in the Greek language on a wide variety of topics, ranging from music theory to Homer. His Isagoge or Introduction, an introduction to logic and philosophy, was the standard textbook on logic throughout the Middle Ages in its Latin and Arabic translations. Porphyry was, and still is, also well-known for his anti-Christian polemics. Through works such as Philosophy from Oracles and Against the Christians (which was banned by Constantine the Great), he was involved in a controversy with early Christians. Important works and philosophy attributed to him. His Isagoge set the question for the Middle Ages, whether genera and species exist in reality or only in the mind. (Wikipedia) See Plato and Platonism.
    • William James: Though James certainly agreed with Peirce and against Berkeley that general ideas exist as a psychological fact, he was a nominalist in his ontology: “From every point of view, the overwhelming and portentous character ascribed to universal conceptions is surprising. Why, from Plato and Aristotle, philosophers should have vied with each other in scorn of the knowledge of the particular and in adoration of that of the general, is hard to understand, seeing that the more adorable knowledge ought to be that of the more adorable things and that the things of worth are all concretes and singulars. The only value of universal characters is that they help us, by reasoning, to know new truths about individual things.” (William James, The Principles of Psychology, 1890, chapter 12).     • Roscellinus was born in Compiègne, France. Little is known of his life, and knowledge of his doctrines is mainly derived from Anselm and Abelard. He studied at Soissons and Reims, was afterwards attached to the cathedral of Chartres and became canon of Compiègne. As a monk of Compiègne, he was teaching as early as 1087. He had contact with Lanfranc, Anselm, and St. Ivo of Chartres. Roscellinus’s exposition of Nominalist doctrines, and especially his application to the dogma of the Trinity, attracted broad attention. He maintained that the three persons of the Trinity were not referred to as three substances or Gods only by convention, arguing that if they were really one substance then God the Father and the Holy Spirit would have become incarnate along with God the Son. Roscellinus cited Lanfranc and Anselm in support of this doctrine. According to Otto of Freisingen, Roscellinus “was the first in our times to institute the theory of words,” but the chronicler of the “Historia Francia” mentions before him a “magister Johannes,” whose personality is much discussed and who has not yet been definitively identified. The “sententia vocum” was one of the anti-realist solutions of the problem of universals accepted in the early Middle Ages. In accordance with a dichotomy set out by Porphyry, the first medieval philosophers regarded genera and species either as things or as having no existence. The philosophers described this using terminology drawn from Boethius, referring to such abstract concepts as either things or words. Nominalists held that only the individual exists and that universals are merely words. (Wikipedia)

According to Anselm, he maintained that colour does not exist independently of the horse which serves as its support and that the wisdom of the soul is not outside of the soul which is wise.     • The passenger pigeon or wild pigeon is an extinct species of pigeon that was endemic to North America. Its common name is derived from the French word passager, meaning to pass by, due to its migratory habits. Passenger pigeons were hunted by Native Americans, but hunting intensified after the arrival of Europeans, particularly in the 19th century. Pigeon meat was commercialized as cheap food, resulting in hunting on a massive scale for many decades. The last captive birds were divided in three groups around the turn of the 20th century, some of which were photographed alive. Martha, thought to be the last passenger pigeon, died on September 1, 1914, at the Cincinnati Zoo. The eradication of the species is a notable example of anthropogenic extinction or extinction of species directly due to human activity. (Wikipedia)

  • “how shall I perceive there what was not perceived here?”: Mr. Spitzer seems to be struggling with the philosophical question, if a tree falls in a forest, which asks about the possibility of unperceived existence. Can something exist without being perceived by consciousness? Can the unobserved world function the same as the observed world? There is a division between perception of an object and how an object really is. What does existence mean and what is the difference between perception and reality? George Berkeley held that for things to exist is for them to be perceived, esse est percipi (A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge, 1710, section 3), and the riddle took its familiar form in a physics textbook: “When a tree falls in a lonely forest, and no animal is near by to hear it, does it make a sound?” (Charles Riborg Mann and George Ransom Twiss, Physics, 1910). See Empiricism (Locke, Berkeley, Hume).
  • “It is possible that we are illusions in the light-streaked fog”: Human beings, their identities and experiences, may be insubstantial and transient, like shapes seen in a fog lit only for a moment by streaks of light. The fog is the ambiguity surrounding existence: perceptions, memories, and realities are not fixed but shifting and elusive, and what seems substantial may be ephemeral or constructed by the mind. The line holds the novel’s blurred boundaries between reality and dream, life and death, and the elusive nature of identity.
  • “Often he could be heard talking to himself”: Mr. Spitzer is exhibiting two psychological issues: 1) internalized conflict which often involves rehearsing disagreements, replaying past interactions, or formulating responses to potential future arguments. This can stem from unresolved conflicts, a need to feel in control, or a tendency to project negative feelings onto others. These mental arguments can be a way to process emotions, prepare for potential conflicts, or even act out suppressed anger or frustration. 2) self-talk is a common and often beneficial behavior as a form of cognitive processing, aiding in problem-solving, decision-making, and memory recall. It can also be a way to regulate emotions, manage stress, and even practice social interactions. Both, of course, taken to excess, could be a mental health condition.
  • “For why, as he might often ask himself, should he trust the truth”: This passage encapsulates Mr. Spitzer’s existential uncertainty and the paradoxes he faces: the instability of what is usually trusted (truth, constancy, solidity) and the endurance of what is usually mistrusted (falsehood, change, fragility). It expresses the elusive and contradictory nature of reality as he perceives it, where clear distinctions between truth and falsehood, permanence and change, are blurred or inverted.
  • “Why should he suppose his musical murmur should remember him when he was gone?”: Mr. Spitzer doubts he will be remembered after death, since he already feels forgotten in life. His “musical murmur” is his life’s work and presence, subtle, ephemeral, barely noticed, and he expects to be forgotten after death as before. The question challenges the assumption that memory or legacy outlasts the body and accepts oblivion, part of the novel’s exploration of impermanence and the fragile boundary between presence and absence.
  • “the dream of the dream”: Zhuangzi’s “Butterfly Dream” is a famous philosophical anecdote about a dream where the philosopher Zhuangzi transforms into a butterfly and then questions whether he is now a man who dreamed of being a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming he is a man. This story explores themes of self, reality, and the fluidity of existence within Daoist philosophy. Zhuangzi recounts a dream where he is a butterfly, flitting around happily and unaware of his human identity. He wakes up and realizes he is Zhuangzi, but is then filled with doubt about whether his current state is the true reality or just another dream. The core question arises: Is he the man who dreamed he was a butterfly, or is he a butterfly dreaming he is a man? “Once upon a time, I, Chuang Tzŭ, dreamt I was a butterfly, fluttering hither and thither, to all intents and purposes a butterfly… Now I do not know whether I was then a man dreaming I was a butterfly, or whether I am now a butterfly, dreaming I am a man” (Chuang Tzŭ, trans. Herbert A. Giles, 1889, chapter 2). See Taoism and Zhuangzi.
    • Relativity of Reality: The story suggests that the distinction between dreaming and waking reality might not be as clear-cut as it seems.
    • The Fluidity of Self: It questions the fixed nature of identity, implying that the self might be more fluid and transformative than it is usually taken to be.
    • The Unity of All Things: Some interpretations connect the story to the Daoist concept of the “Transformation of Things,” suggesting that all things are interconnected and constantly changing.
    • Beyond Duality: The story can be seen as an invitation to transcend dualistic thinking (e.g., self versus other, dream versus reality) and embrace a more holistic view of existence.
  • “Their yesterday was a pointing backward over the shoulder”: Young seems to use actual ASL sign language gestures but some seem to have changed over time. In American Sign Language time runs along the body: the past is signed behind, over the shoulder, and the future ahead. See Deafness and Sign Language.
  • “What cenotaph was there for him”: Mozart was buried in 1791 in a common grave at St. Marx in Vienna whose place was soon lost; a memorial stands near the likely spot, and a monument was raised to him in Vienna’s Central Cemetery in 1891. See Music and Composers.
  • “the porphyry clouds”: Porphyry, from the Greek porphyra, purple, is an igneous rock with large crystals set in a fine-grained matrix, the mark of two-stage cooling. The purple porphyry of Egypt was prized by Rome and Byzantium for imperial sculpture and architecture, since purple was the color of royalty, and a Byzantine emperor’s child born in the palace’s porphyry chamber was porphyrogenitus, “born in the purple.” See Porphyry of Tyre, above. See Ancient Egypt.
  • “he had seen the other side of the tapestry”: “Translating from one language into another… is like looking at Flemish tapestries on the wrong side; for though the figures are visible, they are full of threads that make them indistinct” (Miguel de Cervantes, Don Quixote, Part II, chapter 62, trans. John Ormsby, 1885). The chapter ends with Mr. Spitzer as the weaver woven into his own tapestry. See Don Quixote.
  • “He was this old fan-tail fanning his tail”: Darwin bred fancy pigeons and opened On the Origin of Species (1859) with them: the fantail with thirty or forty tail feathers spread upright and the pouter with its swollen crop. Mr. Spitzer is “pouter-breasted” too. See Birds.
  • “or perhaps he was the third”: “Who is the third who walks always beside you? / When I count, there are only you and I together” (T. S. Eliot, The Waste Land, 1922). See Peron Spitzer.
  • “the turf meets the surf”: Del Mar racetrack, founded by Bing Crosby with William Quigley, opened on July 3, 1937; “Where the Turf Meets the Surf,” written in 1938 by James Monaco and Johnny Burke with Crosby, is still played at the track.
  • “Venus shell”: In Botticelli’s The Birth of Venus (about 1485) the goddess rises from the sea standing on a scallop shell; “Was he rising from the sea, or was he sinking?” See Greek Mythology.
