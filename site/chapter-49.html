Summary

In the longest of his meditations, Mr. Spitzer moves through a frozen cosmos of the mind. He mourns Martha, the last passenger pigeon, who died at the Cincinnati Zoo in 1918, weeping for others who did not weep for him. He lists the contradictions of his existence, dying through life and living through death. He wonders whether he is one man or two, bound so closely to his brother that he cannot tell which he is.

Key passages

From the research

  • Mr. Spitzer experiences a profound disorientation where he "was disoriented by his great experience of living when he was dead, waking only when he was sleeping, sleeping in a watery grave or in an ice hole". This passage encapsulates the paradoxical state of existence and non-existence, blurring the boundaries between life and death, wakefulness and sleep.
  • The imagery of landscapes and continents being lost or carried away like feathers or lace handkerchiefs symbolizes the transient, fragile, and mutable nature of reality itself.
  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates vast cosmic elements: "Ice had frozen so hard that it was like a dome or like the shell of an egg... Ice was frozen into shapes of frozen roofs over the winding rivers and the seas and the lakes... ice piled up like weird spires and minarets and bell towers". This grand imagery conveys a frozen, timeless cosmos, a metaphor for stasis, death, or suspended animation of existence.
  • The passage also reflects on the impossibility of finding solid ground or stable meaning in a world dominated by illusions and transformations.
  • Mr. Spitzer mourns the death of Martha, the last passenger pigeon, noting the poignant historical moment: "Martha, who expired September 14, at one o’clock in the afternoon just as the clock was tolling one, 1918, A.D., at the Cincinnati zoo". This event symbolizes the extinction of a species and, metaphorically, the loss of innocence, beauty, and connection to nature.
  • His grief for Martha becomes a symbol for his own loneliness and the universal sorrow at loss and disappearance: "He wept for others who wept not for him".
  • The novel frequently questions the nature of the self: "He knew not whether he was himself or an image contained within an image... one of whom no unitary statement could be made". This reflects a fragmented identity, the multiplicity of selves, or even the illusion of a stable self.
  • The motif of doubling and mirrored selves recurs: "how often he had nearly collided with himself though this would seem impossible". This evokes existential ambiguity and the fluidity of personal identity across time and space.
  • Mr. Spitzer embraces a paradoxical philosophy: So he had come "to trust only the false... For why... should he trust the truth which had already failed him if the false would never fail". This reflects a deep skepticism about knowledge, reality, and the reliability of perception or memory.
  • The passage suggests that constancy is found only in inconstancy, and that faith in truth is always tentative and fraught.
  • The chapter emphasizes the unreliability and fragmentation of memory: "His memory... had omitted so much, perhaps the whole content of mortal life, of certain death".
  • Time is portrayed as fluid, uncertain, and perhaps even illusory: "Perhaps there was no time... What calendar keeps the sea of the months flowing into the months...?".
  • Reality is likened to a dream or a fog: "the fog concealed the barnacle, the rose, the wing, the star... obliterated the obvious differences between the living and the dead".
  • Mr. Spitzer reflects on the interrelatedness of life’s opposites: "He died through sickness, died through health... died through happiness which should have saved him, died through sorrow, sorrow by which he lived, died by not dying, died by living, lived by dying".
  • Love is depicted as immortal and paradoxical: "when love lived, it died—but having died, it lived and could not die".
  • The text repeatedly returns to Mr. Spitzer’s profound loneliness and his longing for understanding and communion: "He trusted those who had lost... all those who were dispossessed and wandering through the storm of years as he was. They drew him nearest to the city. They were all his loves because he could not love or could love only by indirection".
  • His existential isolation is underscored by the metaphor of walking "through the rain of the city where the rain of the city drummed upon the dark or golden porches and roofs" and feeling himself "oblivious to them”.
  • Water, ice, and the sea are pervasive symbols throughout the chapter, representing both life and death, fluidity and stasis, memory and oblivion: He was like a shipwreck, "his hull broken... paying out more water than the seas contained". The rising waters symbolize overwhelming forces beyond control and the engulfing nature of death and oblivion.
  • Near the end, Mr. Spitzer is described as "the living lighthouse at that one moment when he saw his light, moment when he was dying... his own light shining, shining from him". This image captures the simultaneous presence of life and death, light and darkness, as well as the enduring, though fragile, spark of consciousness amid cosmic dissolution.

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • A complex figure symbolizing the human struggle with mortality, memory, and meaning.
  • Described metaphorically as a shipwreck, a shadow, a lighthouse, a phantom, and a musician whose music is unheard.
  • His physical ailments (e.g., irregular pulse, short breath) parallel his metaphysical disintegration and confusion.
  • He functions as a “mourner” and witness to extinction, loss, and the fading of worlds.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s paradoxical existence of alive yet dead, remembering yet forgetting makes him a liminal, almost mythical figure.

  • Represents skepticism, finality, and the harsh reality of extinction.
  • His views on Martha the passenger pigeon emphasize the finality of death and the absence of spiritual continuation.

Martha (Passenger Pigeon)

  • Symbolizes lost beauty, extinction, and irretrievable loss.
  • Her death is mourned as the death of a species, a cosmic event, and a personal loss for Mr. Spitzer.

Research: Themes

Life, Death, and Immortality

See: Death and Mortality

  • Mr. Spitzer’s state blurs the boundary between living and dead. He lived “when he was dead, waking only when he was sleeping”.
  • Death is portrayed as a paradox: he “died at his zenith because he was at his nadir, died at his nadir because he was at his zenith”.
  • The text questions the reality of death and life, suggesting both may be illusions: “He knew there was no death because there was no life”.
  • The theme of immortality is paradoxical, with Mr. Spitzer’s spirit existing in a liminal state, neither fully present nor gone.

Memory and Forgetfulness

See: Memory and the Past

  • His “forgetfulness veiling his forgetfulness” and “oblivion hiding behind his oblivion” illustrate layers of fading consciousness.
  • He questions the reliability of memory, pondering if he ever truly lived or if life was just a “mistaken dream”.

  • porphyry is a type of igneous rock characterized by a distinctive texture that happens from a two-stage cooling process where magma cools slowly underground, forming the larger crystals, then erupts and cools rapidly at the surface, creating a fine-grained matrix. Porphyry is also known for its historical significance as a prized stone, particularly in ancient civilizations for sculpture and architecture. Porphyritic texture is the defining characteristic, indicating a complex cooling history. It involves two distinct crystal sizes: larger phenocrysts and a finer-grained groundmass. The phenocrysts form during the slow cooling of magma underground, while the fine-grained matrix forms during the faster cooling at the surface. Porphyry rocks are important hosts for mineral deposits, especially copper, molybdenum, and gold. Porphyry copper deposits, in particular, are economically significant sources of these metals. These deposits form when mineral-rich fluids concentrate in fractures around igneous intrusions. Porphyry, especially the purple variety, was highly prized in ancient civilizations like Egypt and Rome. It was used for sculpture, imperial architecture, and other prestigious objects. The color purple was associated with royalty and imperial power, further enhancing porphyry's value.

  • Problem of Universals - The problem of universals is an ancient question from metaphysics that has inspired a range of philosophical topics and disputes. "Should the properties an object has in common with other objects, such as color and shape, be considered to exist beyond those objects? And if a property exists separately from objects, what is the nature of that existence?” The problem of universals relates to various inquiries closely related to metaphysics, logic, and epistemology, as far back as Plato and Aristotle, in efforts to define the mental connections a human makes when they understand a property such as shape or color to be the same in nonidentical objects. Universals are qualities or relations found in two or more entities. As an example, if all cup holders are circular in some way, circularity may be considered a universal property of cup holders. Further, if two daughters can be considered female offspring of Frank, the qualities of being female, offspring, and of Frank, are universal properties of the two daughters. Many properties can be universal: being human, red, male or female, liquid or solid, big or small, etc. Philosophers agree that human beings can talk and think about universals, but disagree on whether universals exist in reality beyond mere thought and speech.
  • William James - Though James certainly agreed with Peirce and against Berkeley that general ideas exist as a psychological fact, he was a nominalist in his ontology: “From every point of view, the overwhelming and portentous character ascribed to universal conceptions is surprising. Why, from Plato and Aristotle, philosophers should have vied with each other in scorn of the knowledge of the particular and in adoration of that of the general, is hard to understand, seeing that the more adorable knowledge ought to be that of the more adorable things and that the things of worth are all concretes and singulars. The only value of universal characters is that they help us, by reasoning, to know new truths about individual things.” William James, The Principles of Psychology (1890). There are at least three ways in which a realist might try to answer James' challenge of explaining the reason why universal conceptions are more lofty than those of particulars: the moral–political answer, the mathematical–scientific answer, and the anti-paradoxical answer. Each has contemporary or near-contemporary advocates.
  • There are many philosophical positions regarding universals (also found in Indian and Buddhist philosophy):
  • Platonic realism is the view that universals or forms in this sense, are the causal explanation behind the notion of what things exactly are which is that universals are real entities existing independent of particulars.
  • Aristotelian realism is the rejection of extreme realism. This position establishes the view of a universal as being that of the quality within a thing and every other thing individual to it which is that universals are real entities, but their existence is dependent on the particulars that exemplify them.
  • Anti-realism is the objection to both positions. Anti-realism is divided into two subcategories; (1) Nominalism and (2) Conceptualism.
  • Realism - Realists tend to argue that universals must be posited as distinct entities in order to account for various phenomena. A common realist argument said to be found in Plato's writings, is that universals are required for certain general words to have meaning and for the sentences in which they occur to be true or false. Take the sentence "Djivan Gasparyan is a musician" for instance. The realist may claim that this sentence is only meaningful and expresses a truth because there is an individual, Djivan Gasparyan, who possesses a certain quality: musicianship. Therefore, it is assumed that the property is a universal which is distinct from the particular individual who has the property.
  • Nominalism - Nominalists assert that only individuals or particulars exist and deny that universals are real. Four major forms of nominalism are predicate nominalism, resemblance nominalism, trope nominalism, and conceptualism. One with a nominalist view claims that we predicate the same property of/to multiple entities, but argues that the entities only share a name and do not have a real quality in common.Nominalists often argue this view by claiming that nominalism can account for all the relevant phenomena, and therefore, by Occam's razor, and its principle of simplicity,nominalism is preferable, since it posits fewer entities. Different variants and versions of nominalism have been endorsed or defended by many.
  • Conceptualism - Conceptualism is a position that is meshed between realism and nominalism. Conceptualists believe that universals can indeed be real, but only existing as concepts within the mind. Conceptualists argue that the "concept" of universals are not mere "inventions but are reflections of similarities among particular things themselves." For example, the concept of 'man' ultimately reflects a similarity amongst Socrates and Kant.
  • Taking "beauty" as example, each of these positions will state the following:
  • Beauty is a property that exists in an ideal form independently of any mind or description.
  • Beauty is a property that exists only when beautiful things exist.
  • Beauty is a property constructed in the mind, so exists only in descriptions of things.
  • Charles Sanders Peirce was an American scientist, mathematician, logician, and philosopher who is sometimes known as "the father of pragmatism". According to philosopher Paul Weiss, Peirce was "the most original and versatile of America's philosophers and America's greatest logician". Bertrand Russell wrote "he was one of the most original minds of the later nineteenth century and certainly the greatest American thinker ever". Pragmatism as a philosophical movement originated in 1872 in discussions in The Metaphysical Club among Peirce, William James, Chauncey Wright, John Fiske, Francis Ellingwood Abbot, and lawyers Nicholas St. John Green and Joseph Bangs Warner (1848– 1923). He outlined four methods, ordered from least to most successful in achieving a secure fixation of belief: 1) The method of tenacity (policy of sticking to initial belief) which brings comforts and decisiveness, but leads to trying to ignore contrary information and others' views, as if truth were intrinsically private, not public. The method goes against the social impulse and easily falters since one may well fail to avoid noticing when another's opinion is as good as one's own initial opinion. Its successes can be brilliant but tend to be transitory. 2) The method of authority which overcomes disagreements but sometimes brutally. Its successes can be majestic and long-lasting, but it cannot regulate people thoroughly enough to suppress doubts indefinitely, especially when people learn about other societies present and past. 3) The method of the a priori which promotes conformity less brutally but fosters opinions as something like tastes, arising in conversation and comparisons of perspectives in terms of "what is agreeable to reason." Thereby it depends on fashion in paradigms and goes in circles over time. It is more intellectual and respectable but, like the first two methods, sustains accidental and capricious beliefs, destining some minds to doubt it. 4) The method of science is the only one whereby inquiry can, by its own account, go wrong (fallibilism), and purposely tests itself and criticizes, corrects, and improves itself.

  • The passenger pigeon or wild pigeon is an extinct species of pigeon that was endemic to North America. The eradication of the species is a notable example of anthropogenic extinction or extinction of species directly due to human activity.
