Definition from research

Chapter 6

  • “Death was birth, and birth was death”: Schopenhauer’s philosophy views death not as a final annihilation, but as the negation of the individual “will-to-live,” a fundamental force driving existence and causing suffering. He believed that life is inherently a state of suffering, a constant cycle of wanting and never truly being satisfied. Death, for him, is the end of this striving, the cessation of the individual will and a return to the underlying, impersonal Will. He argued that the fear of death is irrational, stemming from the attachment to the will-to-live. Although acknowledging the desire to escape suffering, Schopenhauer rejected suicide as a philosophical solution. He argued that suicide, like the will-to-live, is an act of will, preventing the potential for a more profound liberation through the negation of the will in death. Schopenhauer saw individuality as an illusion, a temporary manifestation of the underlying Will.

Chapter 7

  • “What is sorrow? Is it happiness?”: The exploration of happiness and sorrow may refer to Schopenhauer who provides a Western interpretation of Buddhism which includes: the will to life (why people strive to keep on living no matter what) as a major driving force; it is an error to believe people exist to be happy; his solutions are to become a monk or sage and to spend as much time as possible with art and philosophy.

Chapter 8

  • “Yet you can’t help giving him your hand when he gives his hand to you”: Schopenhauer places compassion as the foundation of his ethical system, arguing that it is the only truly moral motive because it is based on a genuine concern for the well-being of others. He sees indifference as the opposite of this, something to be overcome in order to live a moral life. Compassion allows individuals to transcend their self-centeredness and act for the benefit of others, whereas indifference is a form of egoism that hinders moral conduct. Moses mocks old Doc and still pities him: “It kind of makes your heart give.”

Chapter 25

  • “those heavenly harmonies”: The push and pull between silent music and the music of the spheres or harmonious music form much of Mr. Spitzer’s tale. Particularly how Schopenhauer considers music the highest form of art. The philosophy of silence in music is that silence can be a powerful tool for making a statement, conveying emotion, and organizing structure. The novel embodies Schopenhauer’s belief in music as the highest art through its intricate portrayal of Mr. Spitzer’s silent and profound music, the use of musical metaphors to explore existential themes, and the depiction of music’s transcendence beyond the physical and intellectual world. The novel’s deep engagement with music as both expression and mystery reflects the Schopenhauerian vision of music as the direct and ultimate language of the will. Schopenhauer sets this out in The World as Will and Representation (1818), Book 3, where music, unlike the other arts, is a copy of the will itself.
  • “this perpetual and unconsummated suicide”: The will-to-live by Schopenhauer could be used to rationalize why Mr. Spitzer did what he did and how Jock, the Black Coachman, Joachim and Peron are all the same person. Mr. Spitzer’s actions (his tireless searches, his care for others, and his internal conflicts) are driven by this fundamental will, compelling him to persist despite loss and disintegration. The multiplicity of characters such as Jock, the Black Coachman, Joachim, Peron, and Mr. Spitzer symbolically represents different manifestations of this singular will struggling within one being, blurring the lines between self and other, life and death, reality and illusion. Schopenhauer’s term is Wille zum Leben, from The World as Will and Representation (1818). “In psychology, the will to live is the drive for self-preservation, usually coupled with expectations for future improvement in one’s state in life. The will to live is an important concept when attempting to understand and comprehend why we do what we do in order to stay alive, and for as long as we can.” (Wikipedia)
  • “There had been still, it seemed, a spark of life in Mr. Spitzer”: Friedrich Nietzsche proposed the “will to power” as a driving force behind human behavior, instead of the “will to live” of his predecessor Arthur Schopenhauer. Nietzsche develops it in Thus Spoke Zarathustra (1883-85), “On Self-Overcoming.” Will to live: an irrational force drives humans to strive endlessly. Will to power: a dynamic force drives humans to overcome resistance and become who they truly are. Nietzsche believed that the will to power is more useful than the will to live for explaining human behavior. He used the will to power to explain both life-affirming and life-denying impulses, as well as master and slave morality. He believed that the will to power is the expression of self-realization, and that it is a condition for growth. He held that life itself is will to power, a striving that is never done. In the chapter it reflects Nietzsche’s “will to power” through its exploration of internal conflict, the striving for control amid chaos, the tension between life and death, and the dynamic, creative force underlying human existence, as embodied in Mr. Spitzer’s character and experiences.

Chapter 26

  • “perpetual grief” and two other quotes above may refer to the philosophy of Arthur Schopenhauer who believed that death is a natural part of life and a return to a state of non-existence that existed before birth. He thought that individual consciousness is impermanent and that the life of an individual is meaningless. His key ideas are will-to-live (the driving force behind our attachment to life and our fear of death), knowledge (shows the meaninglessness of life and the worthlessness of living), suicide (pointless because it affirms the will-to-live), palingenesis (suggest that the mind has always existed and will continue to exist), redemption (that death is an opportunity for redemption through the negation of the will). The portrayal of Mr. Spitzer's lifelong sorrow, his recognition of life as a series of losses and unfulfilled desires, and his contemplation of the illusory nature of reality all echo Schopenhauerian themes. The passage reflects a worldview where grief and suffering are continuous and intrinsic to life.

Chapter 37

  • Life as a state of experience and potential - Socrates thought death might be a dreamless sleep or passage to another realm where one can converse with the dead. Existentialism believes death is an inevitable aspect of human existence and that it can lead to a heightened sense of self-awareness and responsibility. Epicureanism argues death cannot harm a person, as it’s either not yet experienced (while alive) or not experienced at all (once dead). Death is defined as the loss of the capacity to engage in vital activities and the cessation of consciousness. Wittgenstein said that death is not an experience in life. Schopenhauer believed that understanding death as the ultimate end of life can provide a perspective on the meaning of life itself. Freud thought the life instinct (Eros) and the death instinct (Thanatos) are fundamental forces in human behavior. The chapter depicts life as a state of ongoing experience and potential characterized by uncertainty, illusion, and transformation. It is not a fixed reality but a mutable and often paradoxical condition where life and death, truth and illusion, identity and multiplicity coexist and where human understanding is limited and provisional.

Chapter 39

  • an old "idiot who had become a great philosopher, a philosopher who had become a great idiot," - Wittgenstein, despite his genius, was known for his unconventional behavior and challenging personality. He even questioned his own sanity, asking Bertrand Russell if he was an idiot. Russell asked for proof and after reading his philosophical piece declared he was not an idiot. His profound insights into philosophy, language, and logic led to his recognition as a major philosopher. The idea of a philosopher becoming an “idiot” is a common trope often referencing Diogenes of Sinope. Arthur Schopenhauer was also considered eccentric.

Chapter 47

  • “he was agonized by attributing consciousness to everything” - may refer to panpsychism which suggests that a mind, or mind-like aspect, is a fundamental and widespread characteristic of reality. It says that the subjective experience associated with human consciousness exists, in some form, throughout the universe, even in its most basic elements. It has its roots in ancient Greek philosophy with Thales and Plato and later with Spinoza, Leibniz, and Schopenhauer. It reflects Mr. Spitzer's profound and troubling awareness of the pervasive presence of consciousness or life-like qualities in all things around him. This means that Mr. Spitzer experiences a kind of existential distress or mental anguish because he perceives consciousness, intention, or awareness not just in people but extending to inanimate objects, natural phenomena, and abstract concepts. He senses life, emotion, or meaning in things that are traditionally considered lifeless, such as clouds, stars, waves, or even shadows and this blurs the line between what is truly alive and what is not. This overwhelming and almost mystical perception causes him suffering because it challenges ordinary reality and makes his own sense of self and understanding of the world unstable and confusing. This agonizing attribution is part of Mr. Spitzer's broader struggle with reality, illusion, memory, and identity. It underlines his sensitivity to the ambiguities and mysteries of existence, where the boundaries between life and death, the living and the non-living, and reality and dream are not distinct. His mind is burdened by this expansive and unsettling sense of interconnectedness and animation of all things, which he cannot easily reconcile or control. The phrase captures Mr. Spitzer's deep philosophical and emotional turmoil as he contemplates a universe filled with consciousness beyond human life, contributing to the novel’s themes of illusion, perception, and the elusive nature of reality.
