Chapter 12. The Detective and the Miners

Part of a reading of Harp Song for a Radical (1999), Marguerite Young’s life of Eugene Victor Debs.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Allan Pinkerton, the Molly Maguires, and the law as a trap

The chapters from about page 396 to 474 form the longest set piece in the book, and Debs is barely present in them. Young tells the life of Allan Pinkerton, the Scottish radical turned detective, and the destruction of the Molly Maguires, the Irish coal miners of Pennsylvania who were hanged on the testimony of his agent. Debs appears at the margins, as the young editor watching and as the old man remembering.

The Chartist who changed sides

Young first mentioned Pinkerton back in Part One, as a young Chartist agitator in Scotland who came to America "in the year of the gold rush" (29). Here she fills in the story. He fled Britain after the Chartist cause failed, settled near Chicago, and built the most famous detective agency in the country. He guarded Lincoln and ran intelligence for the Union Army. Then he became the railroads' and mine owners' private police.

Young's judgment is that Pinkerton's "social philosophy not bound by loyalty to principles" had simply "reflected the colors of the surrounding world" (399). The man who had marched for workers' rights became their most effective enemy. This is Not One Self in its darkest version, a man whose selves were bought and sold.

She also records the violence that trailed the agency: the lynching of the Reno brothers in Indiana after they were captured, and the 1875 raid on the James family farm in Missouri, where an incendiary device thrown by detectives killed Jesse James's eight-year-old half-brother, whom she calls "Little Archie" (426). Pinkerton believed in phrenology, measuring skulls to judge character (440). Young lets that detail speak for itself.

The law as a mine without an exit

The heart of these chapters is the case of the Molly Maguires, a secret society of Irish miners in the Pennsylvania coal fields. Pinkerton's agent James McParlan infiltrated them, and on his testimony, in trials prosecuted by Franklin Gowen, president of the Reading Railroad, which also owned the mines, a series of men were convicted of murder and hanged.

Young's verdict comes in one sentence: "The law was made into a mockery of law" (449). She compares McParlan's plot to "the design of the mines which had no exit for them" (449). The railroad owned the mines, hired the detective, and led the prosecution. The miners were caught in a legal structure built as tightly as the shafts they worked in.

This is Reality Is Constructed at its most lethal. The courtroom, the jury, and the gallows looked like justice, and they were built by the same company that paid the miners' wages.

What Debs remembered

Debs kept a private calendar of labor's martyrs, and the Mollies were on it (398). Years later, in the socialist paper Appeal to Reason, he wrote of the executed men: "Not one of them betrayed the slightest evidence of fear or weakening, not one of them was a murderer at heart" (399). He did not claim they were saints. He called them "ignorant, rough and uncouth," the products of their conditions.

Young notes that McParlan's career did not end in Pennsylvania. Three decades later, the same detective led the investigation that charged the western miners' leaders William Haywood, Charles Moyer, and George Pettibone with murder in Idaho. Debs defended them passionately (441). They were acquitted.

This is the Crossing Point again, with the same man standing at both ends. The detective who hanged the Mollies in 1877 came for the Western Federation of Miners in 1906, and the young editor who watched the first case became the old agitator who fought the second.

What the lens shows

Young gives these chapters to people Debs never met because they are part of his inheritance. The Margins Reveal the Truth belongs to the miners, who saw the law's machinery from underneath. Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act is Debs's calendar, a list kept for decades of names the country preferred to forget.

Checking the record

  • The number hanged. Young refers to "nineteen official executions" in 1877 (462), and her list of names includes John Kehoe, who was hanged in December 1878. Twenty men connected with the Molly Maguires were hanged between 1877 and 1879, ten of them on a single day, June 21, 1877.

  • John Kehoe's pardon. Young does not mention it. In 1979, Pennsylvania granted Kehoe a posthumous pardon, an official acknowledgment of the doubts she raises.

  • The Reno lynching. Young implies that Pinkerton's men took part. The Renos were seized from jail in New Albany, Indiana, in December 1868 by vigilantes. How far Pinkerton's agency was involved remains disputed.

  • McParlan and Haywood. Accurate. McParland, as the name is often spelled, directed the 1906 investigation in Idaho.
