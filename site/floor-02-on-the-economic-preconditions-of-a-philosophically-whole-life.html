The Floor and the Framework

On the Economic Preconditions of a Philosophically Whole Life

← Why This Constitution Exists and How It Can Change · The Floor and the Framework · Constitution of the United States, Revised Draft →

The Argument

Every philosophical system has assumptions buried in its foundation. Most of them are about who gets to use it.

The philosophy developed in Marguerite Young’s work rests on five pillars: the plural self, illusion as primary (the Working Story), grief as permanent weather (grief as climate), the witness (the Standing Still), and recursive construction (the Reef Method). Together they form a complete epistemology for living: not a prescription for what to do but a description of how consciousness actually works and how to inhabit that reality with more precision and less suffering.

But there is a condition each pillar requires that the philosophy itself does not name explicitly. It is not a sixth pillar. It is the ground beneath all five. Without it, the pillars do not fall: they simply become inaccessible. They become the kind of wisdom that sounds true when you read it and impossible when you try to live it.

That condition is material sufficiency. Not wealth. Not comfort in any luxurious sense. A floor: the minimum threshold of economic stability below which philosophical and psychological wholeness become structurally unavailable, not because people below that threshold are incapable but because the nervous system, the narrative capacity, and the temporal horizon that each pillar requires cannot function in sustained conditions of scarcity and threat.

The economic floor is not a political position added onto the philosophy. It is what the philosophy requires in order to be something other than a consolation for the already-comfortable.

This essay makes that argument pillar by pillar, then draws the implications for the constitutional and political framework that the philosophy generates. It is not a complete economic theory. It is a philosophical argument for why any serious engagement with Young's epistemology must include an account of the material conditions her pillars presuppose.

Part One: What Each Pillar Requires

The Plural Self Requires Safety to Be Multiple

Young's first pillar holds that the self is not unified and was never meant to be: that we are each a chorus of selves, a multiplicity that is the normal condition of consciousness rather than a symptom of dysfunction. The project is not to achieve unity but to become a more hospitable host to all the selves we carry.

This is psychologically accurate. Internal Family Systems, narrative psychology, and the broader literature on self-as-process rather than self-as-object all confirm it. But there is a condition the research also confirms: multiplicity is only available as a resource when the threat level is low enough.

Under sustained threat, the nervous system does not permit multiplicity. It collapses the chorus into whichever part is most equipped to survive the current danger. Polyvagal theory describes this as the shift from social engagement into fight-or-flight or freeze states: the higher-order capacities for complexity, nuance, and relational attunement become neurologically unavailable when the system is mobilized for survival. You cannot be multiple when all of you is needed for one thing.

Chronic economic precarity is a sustained threat. It is not an acute crisis that resolves and allows the nervous system to return to baseline. It is a permanent condition of low-level mobilization that forecloses the kind of psychological spaciousness in which the plural self can be inhabited rather than suppressed. Research on the cognitive effects of scarcity (Mullainathan and Shafir) demonstrates that bandwidth (the mental and attentional capacity available for complex self-reflection, narrative construction, and future orientation) is directly diminished by financial precarity in ways that are measurable and not attributable to character or intelligence.

You cannot host a chorus when all of you is needed for survival. The plural self is not a luxury, but inhabiting it requires conditions that are.

The floor required by this pillar is therefore: enough material security that the nervous system is not in sustained mobilization. Not comfort. The absence of chronic threat. Below that threshold, the plural self remains theoretically true but practically inaccessible.

The Working Story Requires Narrative Agency

The second pillar holds that illusion is not weakness but the medium through which consciousness constructs meaning. The question is not whether we live by stories but whether our stories are working: whether they expand or contract our capacity to act, love, and endure. The practice is auditing our stories rather than accepting or rejecting them wholesale.

This requires narrative agency: the actual capacity to author one's story rather than simply survive it. Narrative therapy rests on this premise. The shift from being authored by your story to authoring it is the therapeutic move. But narrative agency is not evenly distributed.

It requires, at minimum, enough temporal stability to have a narrative horizon. Stories require time: past, present, and a future that feels real enough to plan toward. Research on the psychological effects of poverty consistently shows that economic precarity compresses the temporal horizon. When the immediate future is uncertain, the capacity for long-range narrative construction is not a psychological failure: it is a rational adaptation to actual conditions. You do not build a Working Story when you cannot be confident there will be a tomorrow in which it operates.

It also requires that the story be, at least in principle, revisable. Narrative agency means the capacity to reauthor. But reauthoring requires that the material conditions of your life not be so fixed by external constraint that no story change produces any actual change in circumstances. The Working Story framework becomes a form of psychological gaslighting if it is offered to people whose actual circumstances are constrained by structural forces their story cannot touch. 'Change your story' offered without 'change the conditions that produced it' is not philosophy. It is a way of making oppression feel like a personal failing.

The Working Story is only a tool if you have enough authorial ground to stand on. Below the floor, there is no ground.

The floor required by this pillar: enough temporal stability to have a narrative horizon, and enough material agency that story revision can produce actual change in at least some domains of life.

Grief as Climate Requires Enough Stability to Feel the Weather

The third pillar holds that grief is not a stage sequence moving toward resolution but the permanent climate of a life fully lived. You do not finish grieving. You develop grief literacy: the capacity to read your own patterns, recognize the returning weather, and stop interpreting its return as failure.

This is one of the most humane reframes in Young's epistemology. But it contains an assumption that deserves scrutiny: that you have enough stability to feel the weather at all.

Grief in conditions of sustained crisis does not behave like climate. It behaves like emergency. The literature on cumulative trauma and complex grief shows that when losses are ongoing, unresolved, and compounded by poverty, discrimination, or chronic threat, grief does not settle into weather. It accumulates without the processing that weather metaphors imply: the ability to stand in it, read it, and return indoors. People managing multiple simultaneous losses under conditions of material precarity are not experiencing grief as climate. They are experiencing grief as flood.

Pauline Boss's work on ambiguous loss is particularly relevant here. Much of the loss that economically precarious people carry is ambiguous: the loss of a future that was possible before the factory closed, the loss of a family member to incarceration, the loss of a self that existed before chronic illness without access to treatment. These losses have no clear object, no social ritual, and no endpoint. They are not amenable to the continuing bonds framework because the bond itself is contested or invisible. Grief literacy, as a practice, requires enough external acknowledgment of the loss to have something to be literate about.

Grief requires enough stillness to become weather. In flood conditions, you do not develop literacy. You build levees.

The floor required by this pillar: enough material stability that losses are not continuously compounded by new losses; enough social acknowledgment that grief has an object; enough time outside survival mode that the processing grief requires is neurologically available.

The Witness Requires a Window

The fourth pillar holds that the witness is not detachment but presence: the capacity to be fully inside experience without being destroyed by it. The Standing Still is the practice of remaining in contact with what is happening without requiring it to be different before you can engage with it.

The psychological framework that maps most precisely onto this pillar is the window of tolerance, developed by Daniel Siegel and extended through trauma-informed clinical practice. The window of tolerance is the zone of nervous system arousal within which the higher-order capacities for reflection, integration, and relational attunement are available. Below it is hypoarousal: numbness, disconnection, dissociation. Above it is hyperarousal: flooding, reactivity, the collapse of the witness position entirely.

The window of tolerance is not a fixed property of a person. It is a dynamic product of the nervous system's current state, which is itself downstream of material conditions. Research on allostatic load (the cumulative physiological burden of chronic stress) shows that sustained economic precarity narrows the window of tolerance measurably and over time. The person who has spent years managing housing instability, food insecurity, or the chronic hypervigilance produced by discrimination is not a person with a narrow window by nature. They are a person whose window has been narrowed by load.

The witness, in Young's framework, is the part of you that can see clearly. But seeing clearly is a neurological capacity before it is a philosophical one. You cannot cultivate the witness when your nervous system does not have the resources to sustain the window of tolerance it requires.

The witness is not a moral achievement. It is a neurological condition. And neurological conditions have material preconditions.

The floor required by this pillar: enough reduction in allostatic load that the window of tolerance is not chronically narrowed; enough physical safety that hypervigilance is not the adaptive baseline; access to the conditions (rest, adequate nutrition, physical safety) that allow nervous system regulation rather than perpetual mobilization.

The Reef Method Requires That Return Be Possible

The fifth pillar holds that growth is not linear but recursive: we return to the same territory again and again, building layer by layer, nothing wasted and nothing finished. The Reef Method replaces the metaphor of the journey with the metaphor of accumulation. You are not going somewhere. You are becoming more of what you already are.

This is perhaps the most materially contingent of all five pillars, and the most quietly radical.

The Reef Method requires that return be survivable. You can revisit old territory when revisiting does not cost you everything. You can build recursively when the foundation is stable enough to build on. Post-traumatic growth research (Tedeschi and Calhoun) consistently shows that growth following rupture requires what researchers call 'assumptive world rebuilding': the capacity to reconstruct a coherent framework for meaning after it has been shattered. But this reconstruction requires resources: time, social support, basic material security. Without them, rupture produces not growth but prolonged fragmentation.

There is also a specifically economic version of this argument. The Reef Method assumes a kind of biographical continuity: that the self you were at twenty is available to the self you are at forty as material to build with. But biographical continuity is disrupted by economic catastrophe. The person who lost their home in 2008, or whose career was destroyed by a health crisis, or whose education was interrupted by the need to work: these are people for whom the reef has been repeatedly demolished rather than built upon. The philosophy does not account for this. The economic floor does.

You cannot build a reef on ground that keeps shifting. Recursive construction requires that the foundation hold.

The floor required by this pillar: enough biographical continuity that past selves are available as building material rather than lost; enough material stability that rupture does not permanently foreclose the possibility of reconstruction; access to the social and institutional resources that post-traumatic growth research identifies as necessary for the rebuilding of assumptive frameworks.

Part Two: What the Floor Actually Is

Having argued that each of the five pillars has a material precondition, the question becomes: what exactly is the floor?

It is not wealth. The argument does not require that philosophical wholeness be the exclusive property of the comfortable. The research on subjective wellbeing and income shows a floor effect rather than a linear relationship: below a threshold, material conditions directly impair psychological and philosophical functioning. Above that threshold, additional wealth produces diminishing returns to wellbeing. The floor is a threshold, not a gradient.

The floor consists of the following, understood not as luxuries but as the material preconditions of the capacities each pillar requires:

THE FIVE MATERIAL PRECONDITIONS

1. Physical safety and housing stability

The baseline precondition for nervous system regulation. Chronic housing instability produces sustained hypervigilance that narrows the window of tolerance, forecloses the plural self, and makes the witness neurologically inaccessible. This is not a metaphor. It is a measurable neurological effect of chronic threat.

2. Nutritional sufficiency and healthcare access

Allostatic load is a physiological accumulation. The body keeps the score, as van der Kolk's title insists, and the score includes hunger, untreated illness, and the chronic inflammation produced by poverty. Cognitive bandwidth, emotional regulation, and narrative capacity are all downstream of basic physical functioning.

3. Temporal sufficiency: time that is not entirely consumed by survival

Every pillar requires time: time to reflect, to grieve, to witness, to build. The person working three jobs to cover rent is not philosophically deficient. They are temporally dispossessed. The economic floor must include enough time outside survival labor that the inner life has room to operate.

4. Narrative sufficiency: enough stability that a future horizon exists

The Working Story and the Reef Method both require a future. Not certainty: a horizon. Chronic precarity collapses the temporal horizon to the immediate. The floor must include enough stability that planning beyond the next week is not irrational.

5. Social acknowledgment: enough recognition that losses and selves are legible

Grief literacy requires that grief have an object and that the object be socially acknowledged. The witness requires enough relational safety to practice. The plural self requires enough social context that multiplicity is permitted rather than punished. This is the most political of the five preconditions: it is not only about material resources but about the social and structural conditions that make inner life legible rather than invisible.

Part Three: Implications for the Constitutional Framework

If the economic floor is a philosophical necessity rather than a political preference, then a constitution derived from Young's epistemology must encode it structurally. This is not a matter of redistribution as charity. It is a matter of philosophical consistency.

A society organized around this epistemology is organized around the premise that each person's inner life has value and that the conditions for its full functioning are a legitimate civic concern. The plural self, the witness, grief literacy, the recursive construction of identity: these are not private achievements. They are the basic activities of a conscious life. A society that does not provide the material conditions for them is not a neutral society that leaves people free to pursue them individually. It is a society that makes them structurally unavailable to a significant portion of its members.

The economic floor is not generosity. It is the precondition for the kind of society the philosophy describes.

What the Constitutional Society Must Encode

The constitutional framework derived from this philosophy must therefore include, at minimum, the following structural provisions:

HOUSING

Not as a market commodity but as a precondition of personhood. The constitutional society encodes access to stable housing as a right rather than a reward. This does not require uniformity of housing or the elimination of private ownership. It requires that the floor exist: that no member of the society is in chronic housing instability that produces the neurological conditions that foreclose their access to the philosophy's capacities.

TIME

The economic floor must include temporal sufficiency. This implies labor protections that are not merely about compensation but about the preservation of non-survival time: the hours in which a person can be more than their economic function. This connects directly to cooperative economic structures, universal basic income arguments, and the broader literature on time poverty as a distinct form of deprivation.

HEALTHCARE INCLUDING MENTAL HEALTH AND NEUROLOGICAL SUPPORT

Window of tolerance, allostatic load, nervous system regulation: these are not metaphors. They are physiological conditions with material causes and material interventions. A constitutional society that does not provide universal healthcare is not merely unjust in a redistributive sense. It is a society that has decided that some of its members' inner lives do not matter enough to maintain the bodies and nervous systems that house them.

EDUCATION AS INNER LIFE DEVELOPMENT RATHER THAN WORKFORCE PREPARATION

The Reef Method requires biographical continuity and narrative capacity. Education in the constitutional society is not job training. It is the development of the capacities that Young's epistemology describes: the ability to hold complexity, to witness, to grieve, to construct and reconstruct identity. This does not preclude practical education. It insists that practical education is not sufficient and that its sufficiency has never been the actual argument for it.

SOCIAL ACKNOWLEDGMENT STRUCTURES

The most difficult precondition to encode constitutionally is also the most important: the social conditions under which inner lives are legible. This includes anti-discrimination provisions, but it is larger than that. It includes the question of whose grief is publicly acknowledged, whose multiplicity is permitted, whose recursive self-construction is supported rather than punished. The constitutional society must build structures that actively produce legibility for the inner lives of all its members, not merely protect them from the most egregious forms of erasure.

Closing: Why This Matters for the Philosophy

The economic floor argument does not diminish Young's epistemology. It completes it.

Young's philosophy is not a philosophy for the comfortable. Its central insights: that the self is multiple, that illusion is generative, that grief does not end, that the witness is a practice rather than a position, that growth is recursive rather than linear: these are truths that resonate most precisely with people whose lives have not permitted the comfortable fiction of a unified, progressing self. They are truths that have been lived most fully by people whose circumstances forced an early confrontation with multiplicity, loss, and the necessity of building without a straight line.

But lived truth is not the same as accessible philosophy. The person whose nervous system is in chronic mobilization, whose temporal horizon has been collapsed by precarity, whose losses have no social acknowledgment, whose biographical continuity has been repeatedly demolished: that person may recognize every one of Young's pillars as accurate descriptions of their experience and still find the practice of inhabiting them structurally unavailable. Not because they lack capacity. Because the floor is missing.

The economic floor argument insists that the philosophy be honest about this. It insists that the work of building the conditions for a philosophically whole life is not separate from the philosophical project but central to it. And it insists that a constitutional society derived from this philosophy is not a society that leaves the floor as an individual problem to be solved by individual effort.

The philosophy describes what is possible. The floor is what makes possible available.

Young spent her life writing about the inner lives of people who had every reason to collapse and did not. The philosophy she embodied in that work is not a philosophy of transcendence. It is a philosophy of inhabitation: of living fully inside what is actually true, rather than fleeing toward what is more comfortable to believe.

The economic floor is what it looks like to take that philosophy seriously at the scale of a society.
