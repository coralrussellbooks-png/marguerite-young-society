Movement Two: The Five Pillars in the Traditions

Characters: Homer Capehorn · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Dante · Don Quixote · Exploration and the Poles · Fairy Tales and Folklore · Homer's Epics · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · Milton · Romantic Poets · The Bible

Definition from research

The preface to Miss MacIntosh, My Darling makes a striking defensive claim on Young's behalf: "Although she accomplishes for native American folklore the same immortality of the myth that Joyce accomplished for Ireland, Joyce was not her inspiration. Her inspiration was America." The defensiveness is telling. By 1965 the comparison to Joyce was both the most available compliment and the most limiting frame for any writer working at this scale of formal ambition, and the preface tries to redirect the conversation toward the American vernacular rather than the European modernist tradition. What neither the preface nor subsequent criticism has adequately addressed is the more fundamental question: not which tradition inspired Young, but what she does with tradition as such, how intertextuality functions in this novel not as allusion or homage but as primary construction method, the mechanism by which the recursive construction pillar actually builds.

The most shocking demonstration of this method arrives early, when Vera describes the bus journey through the Middle West: "We had passed a windmill, a leaning tower, Noah's Ark, the old woman who lived in the shoe." Noah's Ark and the Old Woman in the Shoe are not metaphors here. They are roadside buildings, pieces of rural American architecture dotting the landscape alongside coffee urns and wigwam nightclubs. Young has taken two of the most foundational intertextual resources of Western civilization, one biblical, one from the nursery rhyme tradition, and made them literal objects in the physical world of the novel, sitting alongside the melancholy buffalo tethered under a denuded oak and the faded washing on the line. This is the recursive construction pillar at its most methodologically explicit: the novel absorbs the entire Western literary tradition as material, refuses to keep it in the elevated register of allusion, and builds it into the ground-level American landscape as furniture. The sacred text and the nursery rhyme arrive in the same sentence, at the same scale, with the same grammatical weight. This is not irreverence. It is a structural argument about how all inherited texts function: as the architecture of the world we move through, as present and as ordinary as roadside buildings, available to be passed and noted and left behind.

Don Quixote appears early in the novel in exactly this flattened mode: Mr. Spitzer is "perhaps even some mad Don Quixote chasing windmills," and the windmill has already appeared in the landscape as literal roadside architecture two pages earlier. Young has the bus pass the windmill before she gives Spitzer the Quixote comparison, which means the intertextual reference arrives after the concrete object, reversing the usual direction: typically the object illuminates the reference, but here the physical windmill precedes the literary windmill, making the Cervantes allusion feel like recognition rather than elevation. Mr. Spitzer does not resemble Don Quixote because he is tragic and deluded; he resembles Don Quixote because the windmills are actually there, in the Middle West, just past the Ark and the shoe, and someone has to contend with them.

Pilgrim's Progress is the novel's most sustained intertextual structure, and it operates not as allusion but as inverted architecture. Miss MacIntosh prefers Bunyan to the Word of God itself, "fairly safe even though not altogether trustworthy as a guide through this great wilderness, this mortal life which was a path of thorns." Vera reads it to Miss MacIntosh, "sometimes I would begin at the end, reading straight to the beginning, working from redemption backward," which is the recursive construction pillar making its formal argument: the book that promises a linear progress from the City of Destruction to the Celestial City can be read backward, from redemption toward sin, and the journey is identical in either direction because Young's novel does not believe in linear progress. When Miss MacIntosh disappears, her Pilgrim's Progress is found "water-logged with marks of sea shells on its pages," left on the beach alongside her sea boots and broken umbrella. The book of Protestant virtue's end is not the Celestial City but the tide. Young gives Bunyan's pilgrim exactly the conclusion his own book refuses: dissolution into the sea, the body of water that throughout the novel represents everything Protestant plain-speaking cannot account for.

The inversion of classical epic is the intertextual operation Young performs most consistently through Mr. Spitzer. He is "Penelope weaving this web, not Ulysses who wandered over the wide seas, he just staying at home and minding his own business." The gender inversion here is not a feminist joke at Homer's expense. It is Young's structural claim about what the novel does with the epic tradition: the wanderer's role, the heroic quest, the return to Ithaca, these are all transferred to the absent figure, to Peron who has died and cannot return. The one who stays home, the one who waits, the one who weaves and unravels, he is the novel's consciousness, which means the novel's consciousness is Penelope's consciousness, the domestic intelligence that holds everything together in the absence of the adventurer. The Odyssey, filtered through this inversion, becomes not the story of a hero's journey but the story of the one left behind, which is the story the novel tells about grief.

The mother's canary exemplifies the most intricate intertextual architecture in the novel. She names her canary: Juniper, Tulip, Cellini, Solomon, Methuselah, Marco Polo, Admiral Peary, Shackleton, Dante. The list moves from flower to Renaissance goldsmith to biblical king to biblical patriarch to medieval explorer to polar explorer to medieval poet, covering five centuries and four continents in nine names applied to a single yellow bird that is, unbeknownst to her, actually many birds, each one secretly replaced when it dies. Young embeds the Ship of Theseus paradox inside the intertextual catalog: the bird that accumulates all these names is never the same bird, and the names that were given to one bird now apply to another, which means Dante the canary and Dante the poet share the same epistemological condition as the bird and the next bird: continuity asserted in the face of replacement, identity maintained by the refusal to acknowledge the substitution. This is what Young's intertextuality does structurally: it applies the names of the tradition to material that keeps changing underneath the names, and the names both illuminate the material and survive it, outlasting each particular instance of the thing they were attached to.

The literary dead appear in the mother's bedroom as houseguests: "crippled Mr. Alexander Pope, for example, he who had been scorned by Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, Lord Byron with his club foot, Shelley's bright hair like a comet in the wind, blind Milton dictating Paradise Lost to those indifferent daughters." Three of the four are identified by their physical impairments or personal humiliations rather than their literary achievements, and Paradise Lost arrives not as canonical text but as a domestic situation: a blind man dictating to daughters who do not care. Young does this throughout: the canonical text is always also a human situation, always embedded in a body with a club foot or a scorned love or a progressive blindness, always inseparable from the particular life that produced it. This is the plural self pillar applied to literary history: Pope is not only the author of the Essay on Man, he is the man Lady Mary Wortley Montagu did not love, and both are equally real, equally present in the room where Vera's mother is dreaming.

Virgil's "sunt lacrimae rerum," there are tears for things, arrives as the mother counts her pearls: "Sunt lacrimae rerum. But her tears were never for persons. To her, a human personality was a mere appearance, like a shadow upon the water." Young cites the Latin without translation, without footnote, as a fact in the prose the way the bus passes Noah's Ark as a fact in the landscape. The grief as permanent weather pillar is the translation: tears not for persons but for things, for the whole weight of existence rather than any individual instance of it, which is precisely what Young's grief-architecture requires. Virgil's line, from the Aeneid, is Aeneas weeping at the sight of a painting of the Trojan War in a foreign land, weeping because sorrow travels, because grief exceeds its origin, because the image of the dead persists in places the dead never reached. Young uses it to describe a woman who feels grief at the cosmic scale but cannot direct it toward any individual. They are the same grief, Aeneas's and the mother's, which is the claim recursive construction makes: all the grief in the tradition is one grief looking at itself in different mirrors across time.

"Although she accomplishes for native American folklore the same immortality of the myth that Joyce accomplished for Ireland, Joyce was not her inspiration." The preface is right that Joyce was not her inspiration, but it is wrong to limit her intertextual range to folklore. Young did for all of Western literary tradition what the American roadside did for Noah's Ark: made it a building you drive past on the way from here to there, present and weathered and ordinary and still, despite everything, the shape it always was. That is a different achievement from Joyce's. It may be a larger one.
