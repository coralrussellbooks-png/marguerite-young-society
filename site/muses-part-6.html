Inviting the Muses, Part 6

← Part 5 · Inviting the Muses: A Reading, Part by Part · Part 7 →

Part 6 finishes "The Artist as Wanderer," covers the two previously unpublished academic essays on Edward Young and Coventry Patmore, and ends partway into the "Memorial Speech in Honor of Mark Van Doren."

"The Artist as Wanderer" (untraced, ca. 1965–66)

The phrases "the work reproduced here" and "all the artists here" show that this essay was written to accompany a set of art images: the twelfth-century sculptor Gislebertus, Bernini, Rouault, Chagall, and Ben Shahn. Its argument is that the artist is the Wandering Jew, a figure who "never sleeps the sleep of death" and carries all of history in "an eternal now." The artist is "like every other dreamer except that he is more conscious of his dreams."

Young's handling of the legend is the heart of the piece. In the usual telling, a man mocks Christ on the way to the cross and is cursed to wander until the Second Coming. Young asks whether the words "you will be here when I return" might have been "a benediction" instead. She notes that the mocker only became a Jew in later versions, "a crime projected upon the Jew as scapegoat, a crime committed by all men who would kill the dreamer and the dream." By that reading the supposed punishment becomes a form of the eternal life Christ promised. It links directly back to "My Grandmother's Foot," where Jewish suffering and scattering become the source of "the great sanity of vision."

Her reasons for why the figure matters now are grounded in the century: "so many people displaced, so many flights, exoduses, barriers, walls, concentration camps," and "desert lands created by atomic tests to illustrate the feasibility of mass murder." The atomic tests have now appeared in three essays. Shahn's painting of a Jewish father fleeing the Nazis with a shrouded baby is read as the Wandering Jew and the Christ child fused into one image. She folds in Joyce's Bloom, Ishmael, Ahab, the Ancient Mariner, Don Quixote, and Huck Finn as versions of the same wanderer.

The ending is a creed. Art "by its very nature is an act of faith, of affirmation," and "cannot die as long as man endures." She takes Pasternak's claim that he was "the eyeball of a horse" as proof of a vision no state can wall in, and closes: "Better that it should crumble into man's creative dreams than into nothingness."

"The Theistic Mysticism of Young's Night Thoughts" (previously unpublished)

This essay and the next read like graduate-school work: denser, more formal, heavy on quotation, with little of her usual play. Their dates are unknown, which is why Moore placed them late in the section. They still matter, because they show where her convictions came from.

She places Edward Young's poem (1742–44) against the Augustan age of Pope and Addison, when poetry was treated as "machinery, like the Cartesian universe" and there was deep "distrust of any dark, irrational" knowledge. Young's courage, in her view, was to write a mystical poem inside a Newtonian cosmos that no longer offered Dante's or Milton's stage, so "the drama had to be interior." Lorenzo, the worldly young man the poem addresses, stands for "the prodigal philosophy," the realist position the poet argues against.

Two of her own sentences stand out in a crowded argument. One is "Sanity may consist only in sharing the hallucinations of our neighbors," which could stand as an epigraph for Miss MacIntosh. The other is her closing note that William Law called "the fund or bottom of the soul" eternity, a term taken from Boehme. The mystic's eternity and the modern unconscious turn out to be the same place, which is exactly the ground "The Artist as Wanderer" stands on.

"Coventry Patmore's Theory of Knowledge" (previously unpublished)

This is the more interesting of the two, because Young argues against the obvious reading. Patmore is known as a Catholic convert and religious poet, but she contends that in his work "God as God, if subtracted from that cosmos, would hardly be missed," since the poems would still be full of psychological detail and "the minutiae of quiet domesticities." The sexual imagery outweighs the religious meaning, and she notes, with some dryness, that "Freudian addicts" would find proof of their thesis throughout his odes.

Her real subject is how Patmore knows anything. She places him between idealism and realism, drawing on William James's "wild data" and Arthur Lovejoy's problem of where the image of a distant star is actually located. Patmore refuses to split reality into body and soul, shadow and substance. "In Patmore's world, the wild data continue to behave in a wild manner," and "even the nouns act like verbs." That last phrase could describe Young's own prose.

One passage speaks straight to the whole collection: memory implies a doubleness, "that the past, in being known, still keeps its distance." That is Riley's problem with the old sweetheart and Old James's problem with Indianapolis, stated as philosophy. It is also hard to miss that Patmore's best-known poem is The Angel in the House, and Young's own 1945 book on the New Harmony utopias is Angel in the Forest. The title looks like a deliberate echo.

"Memorial Speech in Honor of Mark Van Doren" (first part)

The speech reads Van Doren's Collected and New Poems: 1923–1963 through its imagery, starting with the old bestiaries whose emblematic beasts became, in later writers, "minute epiphanies of revelation." She hears in Van Doren "the muted undertone of a doubting intellect," but finds that his images build "a mystical lighthouse" toward a vanished world, so real in memory "that one may knock upon the door of a house which is already gone." In his poems "the skeptic thus meets with the mystic."

She pairs him with his hero Don Quixote, noting that Sancho's realism, in trying to correct his master, keeps falling for the illusions itself. Realism in Van Doren is valuable only when it "collaborates with the fading vision of the ideal." Part 6 stops in the middle of her reading of "Old Whitey," a poem about a dead horse turned to cloud, and her answer to whether Van Doren can prove the horse is gone: "The point of all his poems is that he cannot."

Across part 6

Here Young's underpinnings come into view. The academic essays show her working out, in scholarly terms, the positions her magazine pieces state with more flair: that reality is not split into matter and spirit, that the mind's "wild data" count as real, and that the unconscious is the modern name for the mystic's eternity. The two public pieces, on the Wandering Jew and on Van Doren, turn that philosophy into a moral stance for a century of camps and bombs. What survives, in her view, is what is remembered and imagined, whether that is a destroyed city under an Alpine meadow, a house already gone, or a horse that became a cloud.
