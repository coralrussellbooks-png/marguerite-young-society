Miss MacIntosh, My Darling as a Liberal Arts Education

Part of To All My Darlings, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Readers who return to Miss MacIntosh, My Darling again and again often come away with the sense that the novel has been educating them. Every chapter sends the curious reader somewhere else: to scripture, to Greek myth, to Roman history, to geometry and the stars, to Shakespeare and the Persian poets. Followed faithfully, those references add up to something close to a traditional liberal arts education, gathered inside a single story.

The seven liberal arts

From late antiquity through the Middle Ages, a liberal education meant the seven liberal arts. The first three, the trivium, were grammar, logic, and rhetoric, the arts of language and argument. The other four, the quadrivium, were arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy, the arts of number. Boethius, the Roman philosopher Young draws on in several chapters, gave the quadrivium its name.

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling touches all seven. Its characters argue in paradox and fallacy, count and measure, hear music in everything, and read the stars, and its long, looping sentences and torrents of questions are lessons in how language carries thought. See Nearly Four Thousand Questions, Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres, On musica universalis and Boethius (Ch 38), and On Boethius and the Consolation of Philosophy (Ch 51).

The course catalog at the end of this page sorts the novel’s references into subjects that grow out of those seven arts, with each chapter’s topics listed under its subject.

A traditional writer

Young claimed the word for herself. When Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs pressed her on the label “experimental,” she answered:

I see myself as traditional even though I know you see my work as experimental. I don’t really consider Sterne, Joyce, and Proust experimental either because the tradition of their writing goes back a long way. Traditional. The Grand Tradition. Clear back to Don Quixote.

Marguerite Young, in Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs, “A Conversation with Marguerite Young,” The Review of Contemporary Fiction (Fall 1989)

In the same conversation she named the books that formed her: Saint Augustine’s City of God, Tristram Shandy, Gogol’s Dead Souls, Dickens, Poe, and “the poetry of the Mormons.” Her own schooling was classical. At the University of Chicago she studied the epic under Robert Morss Lovett and the structure of the novel under Ronald S. Crane, with whom she read Tom Jones thirteen times. A tradition that runs from the Church Fathers through Cervantes, Fielding, and Sterne is the old humanist curriculum, and it is the one she wrote from. See the Friedman and Fuchs conversation and Reading Miss MacIntosh, My Darling for Its Structure.

Young denied any debt to T. S. Eliot. There are “no echoes of Eliot” in her work, she said, only “echoes of the things that influenced him.” Even so, his 1919 essay “Tradition and the Individual Talent” describes the kind of tradition she meant. A writer with a true historical sense, Eliot argued, feels “that the whole of the literature of Europe from Homer” has “a simultaneous existence and composes a simultaneous order.” That is close to the sense of time running through Young’s novel, in which every moment seems equally present.

Critics have since given a name to the line she placed herself in. In 1976 Edward Mendelson called it the encyclopedic narrative: a work that tries to hold the whole knowledge of a culture inside a story. His examples run from Dante’s Divine Comedy and Cervantes’s Don Quixote, the book Young named as the start of the Grand Tradition, to Goethe’s Faust, Melville’s Moby-Dick, with its chapters on the science of whales, Joyce’s Ulysses, and Pynchon’s Gravity’s Rainbow. When the 1965 reviewers reached for Melville and Joyce to describe Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, this is the company they were placing it in.

The teacher inside the book

Young taught for most of her life, from a high school classroom in Indianapolis to the Iowa Writers’ Workshop and the New School. The novel teaches the way a good teacher does. It does not lecture. It hands the reader a name, an allusion, or a half-remembered line and trusts the reader to go and find out. She was known for welcoming every arriving idea into her classroom with “Oh, come in,” and the novel does the same. See On ‘Oh, come in’: Young’s classroom (Ch 39).

Mr. Spitzer is almost a student in that classroom: always attending, often confused, and learning, as Chapter 43 puts it, only when “it was too late to do anybody good.” The essay The Novel as Classroom looks at how the book teaches its own craft of writing. This page is about the other education it gives, an education in the world.

Using this site as a course

Each chapter page in To All My Darlings is built to make the novel’s education usable:

  • Research: Resources explains the allusions, history, and ideas in the order they appear in the chapter.
  • Vocabulary gives short definitions of unfamiliar words.
  • The Dictionary gathers those words across the whole book, with every chapter where each one appears.
  • Questions and observations offers reflection questions for reading alone or in a group.
