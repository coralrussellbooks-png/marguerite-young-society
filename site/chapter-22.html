Summary

At a breakfast table laid, for no reason anyone names, with Haviland china, silver, and a bowl of white roses, Vera sits scratched, bruised, and aching, “seethingly irritated” that Miss MacIntosh never asks how she passed the night. Miss MacIntosh reads the water-stained newspaper aloud, a Brazilian rebellion, a needle-workers’ strike, gold to be drained from the sea, a famine in China, a new star, and steers the household like a ship (“All hands to the ropes!”). She scolds Vera about dust and unmatched socks and fires the old questions at her: the days of creation, which she counts from Sunday because Constantine “got us all off on the wrong day of the week,” and the world’s islands, mountains, boxers, and stars. Twice Vera asks whether the party dress was thrown away as a punishment, and twice she is answered with more questions. Miss MacIntosh scoffs at phantoms (“Who ever saw the ghost of his father?”), says she has not looked into a mirror in years, and mutters, almost asleep, “Baldness is no cure for sorrow, my dear.” Vera thinks of the two Mr. Spitzers, of the wreckage the sea casts up, and of her mother, who mistakes everything she sees for something else. “Was she all others? I wondered.”

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with Vera’s seething irritation and physical discomfort, describing scratches, aches, and a nameless fatigue that suggests both physical and spiritual malaise. This sets the tone of discontent and existential questioning that permeates the chapter. The sea is described as a mysterious, almost otherworldly presence, “an astral light as of some other planet.”
  • Vera’s feeling of disconnection from the usual routine and Miss MacIntosh’s cheerful insistence on continuing the day’s affairs despite Vera’s obvious distress highlight a fundamental tension between resignation and rebellion.
  • Miss MacIntosh reads aloud the morning newspaper, which is water-stained and blurred, symbolizing the blurred and unstable condition of the world and their lives. The newspaper covers a wide range of topics from international politics and economic troubles to mundane notices like shipments of coconuts and sports scores, reflecting the chaotic and fragmentary nature of modern life.
  • Her manner is repetitive and insistent on routine knowledge and memorization of things such as: biblical creation days, current events, and housekeeping duties. This shows her belief in order and discipline as a defense against chaos and despair.
  • The sea is a recurring symbol representing memory, oblivion, and the unknown. Miss MacIntosh doubts that the sea has memory, questioning “What does the sea remember when it swallows the ship?” This reflects Vera’s own questioning of existence and loss.
  • The house is depicted as a decaying, almost haunted place filled with relics, broken items, and ghosts of the past. The chapter describes the house’s vast but unused wealth (silver services, fine china) and its gradual ruination, symbolizing the decay of old values and failed dreams.
  • Miss MacIntosh is portrayed as brusque, pragmatic, and somewhat harsh, focused on housekeeping, moral discipline, and maintaining order. She criticizes Vera’s laziness and failure to perform her duties, insisting on sewing, cleaning, and learning practical skills for any future husband.
  • Her voice is described as grating and rasping, likened to rusted hinges and drowning sounds, emphasizing her toughness and weariness but also her refusal to indulge in illusions or sentimentalities.
  • The chapter is punctuated by Miss MacIntosh’s rapid-fire questions about the creation days, geography, history, sports, and the meaning of life and death. These questions serve as a kind of catechism, an attempt to impose order and knowledge on a world that feels chaotic and uncertain to Vera.
  • Vera reflects on the futility of these lessons and the disconnection between the external facts of the world and her internal sorrow and longing.
  • While Miss MacIntosh insists on routine and practical concerns, Vera is lost in a dreamlike state, haunted by memories, illusions, and phantasms. There is a growing sense of alienation and sorrow, Vera’s desire for “perfect happiness” contrasting with Miss MacIntosh’s grounded, if harsh, realism.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s dismissal of ghosts and phantoms as nonsense, and her readiness to confront any imaginary specter with her black umbrella, symbolizes her rejection of fantasy and acceptance only of the tangible and practical.
  • “‘Baldness is no cure for sorrow, my dear,’ she muttered sleepily, her voice like the continuation of a dream I had almost forgotten.” This is the only time Miss MacIntosh comes near the night.
  • “For many years now, she had never looked into a looking glass. She could not remember the last time she had seen herself.”

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • The table is laid “as if for some special occasion, the nature of which I could hardly imagine.” What is the occasion?
  • “There is no news today that was not yesterday.” Why does Miss MacIntosh read the news at all?
  • “Baldness is no cure for sorrow.” Whose sorrow?

Characters

  • Vera asks twice about the party dress and is twice answered with questions. What is Miss MacIntosh evading?
  • Miss MacIntosh “had never been to sea” but talks “as if we were far out at sea.” Why?
  • “Was she all others?” What has Vera begun to see?

Connections

  • Chapter 18’s dawn quiz returns: Skagerrak and Shiloh then, James Bay and Fingal’s Cave now. What has changed between the two quizzes?
  • In Chapter 18 she “had never seen it herself except in a mirror.” Here she “had never looked into a looking glass” for years. Which is true?
  • Catherine mistakes a fan for a door and Mr. Spitzer for an umbrella stand. How is Miss MacIntosh’s blindness different?

Reflection

  • What do we ask questions to avoid answering?
  • “It takes more than clothes to make the man.” What does it take?
  • Is a routine a comfort or a hiding place?

Research: Characters

Vera Cartwheel

  • Tired, disillusioned, and inwardly restless, she struggles with a profound sense of sorrow and alienation.
  • She questions the value of the tasks imposed on her and longs for a life beyond repetitive lessons and duties.
  • She exhibits moments of defiance and yearning for joy (“I shall dance again”).
  • She asks twice, “Did you throw away my party dress for a punishment?” and declares, “I shall dance again, even with my own shadow.”

Miss MacIntosh

  • A forthright, practical woman with a strong sense of duty, she is Vera’s caretaker and disciplinarian.
  • She is resistant to illusions and ghosts, skeptical of sentimentality, and focused on maintaining order.
  • Despite her harshness, she harbors her own doubts and vulnerabilities, reflected in her philosophical musings and physical frailty.
  • She counts the days of creation from Sunday, works every day of the week, and blames the Emperor Constantine for the Christian Sunday.
  • Her “old dollar watch… was given to her by her dead love.”

Richard, Miss MacIntosh’s brother (Mentioned)

  • A missionary who died trying to convert tribes to Christianity (told in a later chapter). Here she asks only, “Where is my brother Richard? Some said I looked like him. His hair was red.”

Research: Themes

Routine versus Inner Turmoil

See: Order, Chaos, and Discipline

  • Vera experiences deep emotional fatigue and existential questioning, feeling trapped in repetitive, meaningless tasks imposed by Miss MacIntosh’s rigid routine. The contrast highlights the human struggle between external order and internal chaos.

Memory and Forgetfulness

See: Memory and the Past · The Sea and Water

  • The sea, a recurring motif, is described as both full of “wrinkles” and yet possibly without memory. This paradox symbolizes the blurred lines between remembering and forgetting, life and oblivion.

Illusion versus Reality

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Miss MacIntosh dismisses phantoms and ghosts as nonsense, emphasizing the importance of facing reality and practicality, whereas Vera’s experience is haunted by memories, dreams, and elusive presences.

Mortality and Loss

See: Death and Mortality

  • References to death, the “angel of death,” and shipwrecks evoke the fragility of life and the inevitability of decay. Miss MacIntosh speaks of the absence of new life (“we shall have no baby of our own”) and the presence of old, broken things, symbolizing lost hopes.

Knowledge and Education

See: Order, Chaos, and Discipline

  • The insistence on memorizing Biblical genealogies, current events, geography, and sports reflects a traditional, perhaps oppressive, approach to learning as a way to impose order and meaning in a chaotic world.

Isolation and Connection

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Vera feels isolated, disconnected from the external world and even from Miss MacIntosh, who seems unable to perceive Vera’s sorrow. There is a tension between their companionship and emotional distance.

Technique

See: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

  • Symbolism: The chapter abounds with symbolic references to ships, storms, and phantoms, enhancing themes of memory and loss.
  • Stream of Consciousness: Vera’s thoughts flow freely between observation, memory, and philosophical reflection, emphasizing her inner turmoil.
  • Allusion: Biblical references to creation days, historical figures (Emperor Constantine, King Canute), and biblical figures (Jeremiah) deepen thematic complexity.
  • Contrast: Between Vera’s emotional state and Miss MacIntosh’s external cheerfulness and routine.

Research: Resources

  • “her morning newspaper”: In the lists of events and things there appear to be two time periods being dealt with: one paragraph is the 1800s and the other the early 1900s.
  • “a Brazilian rebellion”: Brazil’s revolts within Miss MacIntosh’s memory include the Naval Revolt (1893-94), the war at Canudos (1896-97), the Revolt of the Lash (1910), when Black sailors mutinied against flogging, and the Revolution of 1930.
  • “a needle-workers’ strike or a new garment union”: In the “Uprising of the 20,000” (1909), shirtwaist makers in New York, most of them young immigrant women, struck for months. The International Ladies’ Garment Workers’ Union was founded in 1900. See Women, Gender, and Marriage and Society, Class, and Race.
  • “the instability of islands”: Colonial rule in the Pacific islands, foreign intervention and control. Eventually the unstable conditions in the Pacific began to draw in European governments, all of which acknowledged some responsibility for the protection of their nationals and their property.
  • “the recent visit of a British prime minister”: This may be Ramsay MacDonald, who in 1929 became the first sitting British prime minister to visit the United States.
  • “the tonnage of water falling each day over Niagara Falls where was a honeymooners’ paradise”: Niagara Falls has a rich history as a honeymoon destination, with evidence dating back to the early 1800s. It was initially considered a destination for the wealthy, but became more accessible to a wider range of couples with the opening of the Erie Canal and the growth of railroads. The iconic Maid of the Mist boat tour, which takes visitors close to the base of the falls, has been a popular romantic experience for honeymooners for over 150 years.
  • “a great drouth in the sheep-raising lands of Western Alberta”: The 1920s and 1930s drought in Western Canada, particularly in areas of Alberta, was not solely a “Dust Bowl” event in the same way as the US Great Plains. While conditions were harsh, with severe droughts and dust storms, the period also saw a significant economic downturn related to agricultural challenges.
  • “a fall in sea island cotton”: Sea Island cotton, a long-staple variety cultivated on coastal islands of South Carolina and Georgia, was intimately linked to the institution of slavery. Its high quality and demand made it a lucrative crop, but its production relied entirely on the forced labor of enslaved Africans. The Sea Island cotton industry thrived from the late 1700s to the late 19th century, fueled by the brutal exploitation of enslaved people. The cultivation, harvesting, and processing of Sea Island cotton were entirely dependent on the labor of enslaved Africans. The Civil War ended slavery, but not the crop. Sea Island cotton was grown by freedpeople and others until the boll weevil destroyed it around 1920. See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “a project for draining gold out of the sea”: Humans have known that gold exists in the ocean since at least 1872, when British chemist Edward Sonstadt discovered it. People have been captivated by the idea of extracting gold from the ocean for over 100 years. In the 1890s, New England pastor Prescott Ford Jernegan claimed to have invented a device that could extract gold from seawater using mercury and electricity. His Electrolytic Marine Salts Company at Lubec, Maine (1897-98) was a swindle, and he fled with investors’ money. The ocean contains around 20 million tons of dissolved gold (Atlas Obscura, “There Is Gold in Seawater, But We Can’t Get at It”), but there is only about one gram of gold for every 100 million metric tons of ocean water (NOAA Ocean Service, “Is there gold in the ocean?”). There is also undissolved gold in and on the seafloor, encased in rock “a mile or two underwater” (NOAA). Getting gold from seawater is very difficult, so it is not expected to happen anytime soon. Scientists have also tracked the amount of gold in the ocean over the last 3.5 billion years (Ross R. Large and others, “Gold in the oceans through time,” Earth and Planetary Science Letters, 2015).
  • “the lost regatta”: Throughout the 1800s, hundreds of professional and amateur boat clubs were established in the United States. A select few that remain intact today, a few that merged with other clubs, and others that had brief but interesting lives. It gives a snapshot into what life was like in America as society’s elite established these clubs not just for athletics, but to build and maintain their social status, wealth, and political clout. Things weren’t so glamorous outside these boathouses in the US at that time.
    • The Henley Royal Regatta barred “mechanics, artisans, or labourers” until 1937. In 1920 it refused the American sculler John B. Kelly, a bricklayer.
  • “a shipment of cocoanuts”: DNA analysis of more than 1,300 coconuts from around the world reveals that the coconut was brought under cultivation in two separate locations, one in the Pacific basin and the other in the Indian Ocean basin (Gunn, Baudouin, and Olsen, PLoS ONE, 2011).
  • “the deposition of another Balkan king”: Serbia’s King Alexander Obrenović was murdered by his own officers in 1903. Greece deposed Constantine I in 1917 and declared a republic in 1924. Alexander I of Yugoslavia was assassinated in 1934, and Albania’s King Zog fled in 1939.
  • “the sea is full of wrinkles, though it has neither age nor grey hairs”: “Time writes no wrinkle on thine azure brow: / Such as creation’s dawn beheld, thou rollest now” (Byron, Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage IV, 1818). See Romantic Poets and The Sea and Water.
  • “the jeremiads”: Jeremiah means “a lamentation; mournful complaint,” or further, “a cautionary or angry harangue.” He was called the “weeping prophet” and was one of the major prophets of the Hebrew Bible.
  • “Hong Kong funds were frozen, and the British were rank imperialists”: Britain took Hong Kong in the First Opium War, fought to force the opium trade on China (Treaty of Nanking, 1842). See The History of Opium.
  • “there was a great famine in China”: The North China famine of 1876-79 killed some nine to thirteen million people, and famines came again in 1920-21, 1928-30, and 1942-43.
  • “A younger brother had been assassinated in Java”: The sultans of Yogyakarta:
    • Hamengkubuwono IV suspiciously died, maybe by poison.
    • Hamengkubuwono V was killed by his wife.
  • “A new star had been discovered in the Northern Hemisphere”: T Coronae Borealis may refer to the New Star that is seen every 80 years. “It was first discovered in an outburst in 1866 by John Birmingham, though it had been observed earlier as a 10th magnitude star. It may have been observed in 1217 and in 1787 as well. In February 1946 a 15-year-old schoolboy from Wales named Michael Woodman observed a flare up, subsequently writing to the Astronomer Royal and leading to the theory that the star flares each 80 years.”
  • “Mr. Spitzer’s close-mouthed old houseman with his clam buckets and his seine”: The old clam digger. See The Old Clam Digger.
  • “We loved no graven image”: “Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image” (Exodus 20:4). See The Bible.
  • “we were using the Haviland china”: Haviland & Co. was founded at Limoges, France, by the American importer David Haviland in 1842.
  • “cabbage roses such as grew, if not in What Cheer, Iowa”: Miss MacIntosh’s hometown. See Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest and Roses and Flowers.
  • “The first was to have begun what He could not finish”: The idea that God left things uncreated or unfinished holds deep philosophical and thematic significance. This concept reflects the novel’s exploration of imperfection, incompletion, and the inherent uncertainty of life. Because creation is incomplete, the characters, particularly Miss MacIntosh, emphasize the importance of practical, honest living in the present world rather than dreaming of distant, perfect worlds or afterlives. The unfinished world demands human effort and self-discipline, and moral clarity, rather than escapism or fantasy. The significance of God leaving things uncreated or unfinished is to highlight the imperfection and incompleteness of existence, the limits of human understanding, the blurred boundary between reality and illusion, and the call for grounded, practical living amid uncertainty. It encapsulates the novel’s meditation on mortality, faith, and the human condition.
    • The 8th day of creation is often used symbolically to represent a new beginning, a time of renewal or a significant event beyond the initial creation narrative, often associated with the covenant with Abraham and the practice of circumcision on the 8th day after birth, signifying a new life within the covenant with God. Some Christians interpret the 8th day as a reference to Jesus’ resurrection.
  • “no monsters guarding the waves at the edge of the world”: Old maps put sea monsters at the edges of the known world. The Hunt-Lenox Globe (c. 1510) is marked “HC SVNT DRACONES,” “here are dragons.”
  • “pheasants’ tongues served by imaginary servants”: Suetonius says the emperor Vitellius was served “the livers of pike, the brains of pheasants and peacocks, the tongues of flamingos” (Vitellius 13).
  • “triple-crowned griffins with gold eggs between their gilded paws”: Herodotus tells of griffins guarding gold in the far north (Histories 3.116). See Greek Mythology.
  • “My mother always insisted that the hotel entertained many dead men”: Catherine’s dead guests. See Catherine Cartwheel.
  • “what God had created on Wednesday”: “And God made two great lights; the greater light to rule the day, and the lesser light to rule the night: he made the stars also” (Genesis 1:16). See Genesis.
  • “the Emperor Constantine’s having changed the calendar”: On March 7, 321, Constantine ordered that “on the venerable day of the Sun” magistrates and city people should rest. The Seventh-day Adventists and Seventh Day Baptists teach that Rome changed the Sabbath to Sunday, and Miss MacIntosh’s quarrel with Constantine is theirs. See American Religious Movements and Augustine and Christian Theology.
    • The 8 day week and the alternate (older calendars) used by the Romans is an obsession for Miss MacIntosh. She is furious that they have been changed and thrown people off balance. Young may be telling us that we are out of balance with nature, the seasons, and the natural order. She seems to be using Roman paganism. In one of the links about the Roman calendar it says that they followed the seasons more closely. The significance of these things to Miss MacIntosh is to underscore the novel’s exploration of time as a human-made, mutable concept that reflects our struggle to impose order on life’s inherent uncertainty and impermanence. It reinforces Miss MacIntosh’s advocacy for common sense and living within the realities of the present moment, acknowledging the limitations imposed by time and mortality.
    • The Roman calendar was used by the Roman Kingdom and Roman Republic. The earliest Roman calendars were lunar calendars that tracked the moon’s 29.5 day cycle. It also had an eight-day week, with a market day every eight days.
    • The Julian calendar was a reform of the Roman calendar that was based on the Egyptian solar calendar. It had 365.25 days in a year, with leap days added every four years. It was replaced by the Gregorian calendar in 1582.
    • The Romans were generally pagans for thousands of years. Paganism is a term used to describe people who worship multiple gods and nature, rather than one god as in monotheistic religions. The Roman Empire eventually became a Christian majority state, and pagans were persecuted. In 391 AD, Emperor Theodosius issued edicts that abolished all pagan cults and ceremonies.

    • Jacob Riis: “A particularly important effort by Riis was his exposure of the condition of New York’s water supply.   • “every woman’s privilege and duty”: Women’s unpaid labor is the work that women do without financial compensation, such as cooking, cleaning, and caring for children. It’s often undervalued and underappreciated. Women do more than twice as much unpaid work as men. Women spend 2.8 more hours than men on unpaid care and domestic work globally.   • “a baseball autographed by a dead St. Louis Cardinal”: Young mentioned the St. Louis Cardinals and the Cardinals/Dodgers rivalry and this mention seems to be through the lens of racism. During this period, after the 1942 season, Branch Rickey, who had built up the Cardinals farm system as their general manager, moved to become the Dodgers’ general manager. In 1947, after Rickey broke the color line by signing Jackie Robinson to the Dodgers, there were rumors that southerners playing for the Cardinals were planning to boycott games against the Dodgers, although the players later denied it. In general, the Cardinals were latecomers to integration. Front-office executive Bing Devine said the owner from 1947 to 1953, Fred Saigh, refused to sign black players. There was a widespread belief that St. Louis was, in many ways, a Southern city. In the mid-1950s many of its stores and restaurants refused to serve black customers. The Cardinals, with baseball’s largest radio network blanketing the Midwest and South, had cultivated white Southern fans. Their ballpark was also the last in the majors to abolish segregated seating. Because of their lack of black players, the Cardinals’ play suffered on the field tremendously in the 1950s. Meanwhile, with the success of Robinson, the Dodgers doubled down on the opportunity to sign players of color from the Negro leagues. In the subsequent years after their pennant-winning season in 1947, they would sign Don Newcombe, Roy Campanella, and Jim Gilliam from the Negro leagues, adding to an already tremendous team. The Dodgers made the World Series in 1949, 1952, 1953, 1955, 1956, and 1959 (winning championships in 1955 and 1959) and were a historic pennant race away from making it in 1951, in part because they were the first to accept African American players. The 1951 season included a 14-game winning streak for the Dodgers against the Cardinals, the longest such streak in the rivalry. See Society, Class, and Race.

The All-American Girls Professional Baseball League played from 1943 to 1954. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.

    • Islands of the Blessed may refer to the Islands in Greek mythology which were a semi-legendary place in the western ocean where the souls of heroes and worthy people went after death. They were also known as the Fortunate Isles or Elysian Fields.
    • Islands of the Dead may refer to a concept from pre-Christian Europe of an island to the west where souls went after death. It is reported as being part of Celtic belief by several Roman historians, and evidence for this belief is also found in Welsh folklore. It also existed in ancient Germanic traditions where the British Isles were sometimes depicted as the isles to the west that the dead inhabited.
  • “Take time by the forelock!”: The saying means to act quickly and decisively, especially to take advantage of an opportunity. It comes from the Greek Kairos, Opportunity, carved by Lysippus with a forelock in front and bald behind, so that he can be seized only as he comes. The Roman fabulist Phaedrus tells it of Occasio (Fables 5.8). See Hair, Wig, and Baldness.
  • “the firmament in the midst of the waters to divide the waters from the waters”: “Let there be a firmament in the midst of the waters, and let it divide the waters from the waters” (Genesis 1:6-7). See Genesis.
  • “It’s a heavy sea running”: A nautical phrase meaning there are large, powerful waves crashing against a ship, indicating rough and dangerous sea conditions. Its origin lies in the literal description of a turbulent ocean with large waves, commonly used by sailors to convey the severity of a storm at sea.
  • “The longer the orbit, the longer the planetary year”: Kepler’s third law (Harmonices Mundi, 1619) is that the square of a planet’s orbital period is directly proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis of its orbit, meaning a larger orbit leads to a longer orbital period. See Astronomy.
  • “Time was circled in a single week”: Nietzsche’s eternal recurrence (The Gay Science 341, 1882): “This life as you now live it and have lived it, you will have to live once more and innumerable times more.” See Nietzsche and Time.
    • The block universe is usually traced to Hermann Minkowski’s lecture “Space and Time” (1908): “Henceforth space by itself, and time by itself, are doomed to fade away into mere shadows, and only a kind of union of the two will preserve an independent reality.” It describes the universe as a four-dimensional block that contains every event that has ever happened, past, present, and future all equally real. The growing block version of the theory states that the past and present are real, but the future is unreal. As future events become present, they are added to the growing block of reality.
  • “Siberian wheat”: Mennonites from the Russian steppes brought “Turkey Red” winter wheat to Kansas in 1874, and the USDA’s Mark Carleton brought more Russian hard wheats around 1900.
  • “The American divorces were increasing”: American divorce rates peaked in the 1980s. In American history, divorce was initially very difficult to obtain, primarily restricted to cases of adultery and was heavily regulated by religious and legal authorities during the colonial era. By the mid-19th century, divorce rates began to rise significantly as states enacted more lenient laws allowing for broader grounds like cruelty, desertion, and habitual drunkenness, leading to a major surge in divorce rates during the 20th century (“no-fault” divorce laws).

As the Wikipedia article on Pericles puts it, “Pericles marked a whole era and inspired conflicting judgments about his significant decisions. The fact that he was at the same time a vigorous statesman, general and orator only tends to make an objective assessment of his actions more difficult.” Pericles’ most visible legacy can be found in the literary and artistic works of the Golden Age, much of which survive to this day. In politics, Victor L. Ehrenberg (From Solon to Socrates, 1968) argues that a basic element of Pericles’ legacy is Athenian imperialism, which denies true democracy and freedom to the people of all but the ruling state. The promotion of such an arrogant imperialism is said to have ruined Athens. Pericles and his “expansionary” policies have been at the center of arguments promoting democracy in oppressed countries. Other analysts maintain an Athenian humanism illustrated in the Golden Age. The freedom of expression is regarded as the lasting legacy deriving from this period. Pericles is lauded as “the ideal type of the perfect statesman in ancient Greece” and his Funeral Oration is nowadays synonymous with the struggle for participatory democracy and civic pride. His Funeral Oration, especially this part (Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War 2.37, translation adapted from Benjamin Jowett, 1881): “Our form of government does not enter into rivalry with the institutions of others. Our government does not copy our neighbors’, but is an example to them. It is true that we are called a democracy, for the administration is in the hands of the many and not of the few. But while there exists equal justice to all and alike in their private disputes, the claim of excellence is also recognized; and when a citizen is in any way distinguished, he is preferred to the public service, not as a matter of privilege, but as the reward of merit. Neither is poverty an obstacle, but a man may benefit his country whatever the obscurity of his condition.”

  • “Old-fashioned celluloid collars were going two for a nickel”: The separable (paper) collar was introduced in the early 1800s as a way of allowing men of more modest means to look sufficiently presentable when the occasion warranted it. Wealthier folks could have their entire shirts laundered after every wearing, but such services were largely out of reach to those with less money, such as laborers, farmers, clerks, or ordinary storekeepers. Laundering in the era before washing machines and modern fabrics was far more labor-intensive than today. The new style collar made it possible for much larger numbers of people to present themselves in church, courts, offices, and stores without bringing embarrassment or revealing their more modest status. Celluloid collars came in the 1870s.

Vocabulary

Aberration
A departure from what is normal or expected.
Acanthus
A Mediterranean plant whose deeply cut leaves are carved as ornament, especially on Corinthian columns.
Aloes
A fragrant resin or wood used in perfume.
Antediluvian
Before the Flood; very old.
Astral
Having to do with the stars.
Balderdash
Nonsense.
Ballast
Heavy material, such as stones, carried low in a ship to keep it steady.
Barnacled
Crusted with barnacles, small shelled sea creatures that cling to rocks, pilings, and the hulls of ships.
Bayberry
A shrub of the Atlantic coast whose waxy berries were boiled to make fragrant candles.
Bellwether
The lead sheep of a flock, which wears a bell.
Brusque
Abrupt and blunt in manner.
Burnished
Polished until shining.
Calker
Also caulker. A worker who seals the seams of a wooden ship to make it watertight.
Camisole
A woman’s light undergarment for the upper body. The text has “camesoles.”
Candelabra
A branched candlestick.
Cassia
A spice bark like cinnamon.
Castanets
Pairs of small wooden shells clicked together in the hand, as in Spanish dance.
Celluloid
An early plastic.
Centaur
In Greek mythology, a creature with the head and upper body of a man and the body and legs of a horse.
Chandelier
A branched hanging light.
Chattels
Movable possessions.
Cherubim
Winged angelic beings. In art they are often shown as chubby winged children. The singular is cherub.
Combers
A nautical term for long, curling waves that roll in and break on a beach or reef.
Constitutional
A walk taken regularly for the sake of one’s health.
Corporeal
Having a body; physical.
Corset
A close-fitting undergarment, stiffened with whalebone or steel and laced tight, worn to shape the waist.
Counterpane
A bedspread.
Curdled
Thickened into lumps, like soured milk.
Delusive
Misleading; giving a false impression.
Denuded
Stripped bare.
Dirge
A slow song of mourning.
Discursive
Rambling from one subject to another.
Dope sheet
A racing sheet giving information and tips on the horses running.
Dory
A small flat-bottomed fishing boat.
Drouth
Drought.
Emissary
A messenger or agent.
Errant
Wandering; straying from the proper course.
Festering
Rotting and oozing; growing more corrupt.
Figurehead
A carved figure, often a woman, mounted on the bow of a sailing ship. See On the figurehead and intuition (Ch 40).
Firmament
The sky or the heavens, imagined as a solid dome overhead, as in Genesis 1.
Fly-specked
Spotted with the tiny marks left by flies.
Forecastle
The forward part of a ship below the deck, where the crew lived. Pronounced “FOHK-sul.”
Forelock
A lock of hair over the forehead.
Gargoyle
A carved grotesque figure on a building, often serving as a waterspout.
Gelding
A castrated male horse.
Genealogy
A record of a family’s descent.
Goldeneye
A diving duck with bright yellow eyes, called a whistler for the sound of its wings.
Gregarious
Fond of company; sociable.
Griffin
A mythical beast with the head and wings of an eagle and the body of a lion.
Helm
The wheel or tiller that steers a ship.
Hock
The joint in the hind leg of a horse, between the knee and the fetlock.
Homespun
Simple and plain; originally, cloth spun at home.
Hulk
The body of an old ship; a big, clumsy person or thing.
Imploring
Begging earnestly.
Incorporeal
Without a body; not physical.
Inordinately
Excessively.
Invidious
Hateful; likely to cause resentment.
Jeremiad
A long, bitter lament.
Laconically
Using very few words.
Laggard
Slow; falling behind.
Larboard
The old word for the left side of a ship.
Leeward
On the side sheltered from the wind.
Leviathan
A sea monster in the Bible; anything huge.
Livid
Bluish-gray, the color of a bruise. Also furiously angry.
Lucid
Clear; clear-headed.
Lunar month
The time from one new moon to the next, about 29.5 days.
Mackerel sky
A sky of rippled clouds like the markings of a mackerel.
Mackintosh
A raincoat of rubberized cloth, named for its inventor, Charles Macintosh.
Mandarin
A high-ranking official of imperial China.
Mantle
A loose sleeveless cloak; anything that covers or envelops.
Marcelled
Set in deep, regular waves with heated irons.
Mica
A mineral that splits into thin, shiny, nearly transparent sheets and makes rock glitter.
Minaret
A tall, slender tower of a mosque, from which the call to prayer is given.
Monsoon
A seasonal wind in South Asia that brings months of heavy rain.
Morosely
Gloomily, sullenly.
Mosaic
A picture or pattern made by setting small pieces of colored stone, glass, or tile together.
Mundane
Ordinary; of this world.
Myrrh
A fragrant resin used in incense and embalming.
Neap tide
The tide with the smallest difference between high and low water, twice a month at the quarter moons.
Obsolescence
The process of going out of use.
Palings
Pointed wooden stakes, or a fence made of them.
Paraphernalia
Personal belongings or equipment.
Patrimonial
Inherited from one’s father.
Peaked
Thin and pale, as from illness.
Percale
A fine, closely woven cotton.
Phantasmal
Ghostly; like a phantom or an illusion.
Pinnacle
The highest point; a tall, pointed mountain peak or the slender spire on a building.
Prism
A clear piece of glass with flat sides that splits light into the colors of the rainbow.
Proximity
Nearness.
Quail
A small, plump ground bird hunted as game.
Rabbit punch
An illegal blow to the back of the neck.
Regatta
A series of boat races.
Seine
To fish with a large hanging net.
Spar
A pole used as a ship’s mast, boom, or yard. Spar is also the name for certain shiny, easily split crystalline minerals, such as Iceland spar.
Spindrift
Spray blown from the tops of waves.
Starboard
The right-hand side of a ship, facing forward.
Steeplechase
A horse race over fences, ditches, and water jumps, first run cross-country from one church steeple to another.
Stridently
Harshly and loudly.
Sunday punch
A boxer’s hardest, best blow.
Supernal
Heavenly.
Tallow
Animal fat used for candles and soap. Tallow-colored is a dull, yellowish white.
Tarpaulin
A heavy waterproof cloth for covering things.
Tenement
A crowded, run-down apartment building.
Tonnage
The size or carrying capacity of a ship.
Tumult
A loud, confused uproar.
Tumultuous
Loud, wild, and disorderly.
Tureen
A deep covered dish for serving soup.
Turnstone
A shorebird that turns over stones to find food.
Urn
A tall vase; also a large metal container with a tap, for serving coffee or tea.
Vainglorious
Excessively proud and boastful.
Visage
A person’s face or expression.
Voluminous
Large, full, or great in volume.
Weathercock
A weathervane shaped like a rooster; also, a person who changes with every wind.
Widow walk
A railed platform on a coastal roof, from which to watch for ships.
Woolgathering
Idle daydreaming.
