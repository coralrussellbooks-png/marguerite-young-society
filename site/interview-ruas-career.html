Young's Interviews Analyzed

The Ruas Interview on Her Life and Miss MacIntosh, WBAI (undated)

← Previous · Young's Interviews Analyzed · Next →

A reading for the Miss MacIntosh project.

The interview at a glance

This is a second WBAI conversation with Charles Ruas, a companion to his interview on "Angel in the Forest," and where that one stayed with New Harmony, this one is the career interview, moving from her Indiana childhood through her arrival in New York to the making of the novel. The introduction notes that Ruas was then staging "a multimedia performance of the text of Miss Macintosh, My Darling," and Young speaks of "the two biographies I'm now writing" covering "1870 to 1926," her Debs-era work, which places the talk in the late 1970s or early 1980s, the same period as the other Ruas session. As in the WNYC recording, a few asides to a pet interrupt the transcript and are set aside here. This is the richest single source in the set on her literary life, and it gives the fullest and most candid version of two things the other interviews only touch: the opium lady, and the question of whether the book is autobiographical.

The Hoosier-poet myth and the grandmothers

Young calls Indianapolis "the Athens of the West" and places herself after Booth Tarkington, Meredith Nicholson, and James Whitcomb Riley, "the old Hoosier group," raised to believe "that to be born in Indiana was to be born a poet," a belief she now calls "a myth." Both her grandmothers were artistic, and the maternal one she describes as "a genius personality" who "could have been a surgeon or a poet or almost anything," who predicted the child would be "a writer or the first woman president of the United States, or would become a lawyer who would work to restore the lost family fortunes," fortunes so storied that she and her sister feared kidnappers who wanted "our gold and crowns." The genealogy repeats the WNYC claims with additions: the family pride ran to the French Huguenot and Scottish lines rather than to the father's Brigham Young, and to the John Knox descent she now adds David Hume and Jane Welsh Carlyle beside Byron, Boswell, Melville, and Stevenson, "I claim them all as relatives." New here is her French formation: she was "translating professionally" a French book on Cinderella by fifteen, learned to "think and dream and feel in French," and loved Anatole France so much that at her grandmother's death she had "a rather horrible thought" that the old woman could now tell him in heaven that her granddaughter admired him.

Cosmic, not regional

Ruas presses the regional question, and Young resists it firmly. "It was my fate to be born in Indiana," she says, "I probably would have chosen Edinburgh," and her aim was to "transmute this legend into a universal or cosmic statement," not "to be strictly a regionalist." She is blunt that she is not a nature writer: "if you really ask me if I love nature, I couldn't say I do. I love the sea and the sand," and she uses birds and beasts only "for their symbolic value, their value as icons," the interest behind her Chicago thesis on the creatures of Lyly's "Euphues." She is cool toward Willa Cather ("I like her. I don't love her") and treats Twain, Dreiser, and Sinclair Lewis as "great thinkers, great dreamers" rather than favorites. Her clearest formulation of the Midwest writer comes here: Southern writers seem "born to sing" amid daily ballads, while the Midwest, "more prosperous" and "middle class," forces its writers to "become highly sophisticated, highly educated," so that "the Middle West begets bizarre and beautiful writers who have been dipped and died in education." What she wanted was not Paris but to go "by mulesback through the mountains of Tennessee, collecting ballads," after the folklorist Stith Thompson, a plan the Depression ended. And she insists, then and now, that she never watches other writers: "if I knew there was a vogue, I would avoid it."

Coming east: the wartime literary world

Young came to New York almost by chance, as a teacher at Iowa who won "a fellowship from the American Academy of Arts and Letters, \$1,500, which was a fortune," and left "within 48 hours." A letter from John Crowe Ransom, who had been publishing her poems in the Kenyon and Southern Reviews, was waiting when she arrived. Sally Benson, who with Ransom had urged the "Eastern experience," introduced her on her first day to Harold Ross and James Thurber, "half the literary world that day." She remembers the wartime city as a golden age when exiled Europeans filled the town and "every publisher was asking every man on the street, have you got a book," so that contracts came "like the leaves from the trees." The scene, she says, was "under the domination of the new critics," Ransom, Allen Tate, Mark Van Doren, Lionel Trilling, and Van Wyck Brooks, "people of great generosity," and she denies any split between a New Yorker set and a Greenwich Village set, calling it "the flowering of American culture at every level." One durable fact for the literary record: as fiction editor of the avant-garde magazine "the Tiger's Eye," under founder Ruth Stephan and poetry editor Horace Gregory, Young was "one of the first publishers of a chapter of Anais Nin's novels."

Psychology, not personality

Young knew Gertrude Stein, a visitor at Chicago with Thornton Wilder as host, and admired the person while feeling "not the slightest empathy with her as a writer." She does not do automatic writing, she says, but is "controlled by intellectual ideas" and "obsessions with certain pathologies, almost a medical imagination," and counts Sterne, Gogol, Dickens, and Twain as her kin. The distinction she draws is between psychology and personality. "I'm never much interested in individuals, even my closest friends," she says, preferring "the many as opposed to the one," a "muralist imagination" she calls "very much William James." Her characters come "from the newspapers and from biography and medical history," never from invention: "I believe with Browning that the poet is a reporter," and she points to "The Ring and the Book," built on "a real murder in Rome," as her model. "I don't believe in inventing character."

The opium lady, in fuller detail

The opium lady gets her most detailed portrait here. Young reads Shakespeare aloud to her to work through school; the woman had been "under opium for about 15 or 20 years," unable to walk for ten, and was "one of the original patronesses of Poetry" and of "Jane Addams Hall House." Young witnessed the dreams: "the golden bird, which was the spirit of Heraclitus," a "conversation with the head of John the Baptist," an imaginary elephant, balloon fish over the bed. Two new facts stand out. First, Young had meant to write "another biography," of "the Toussaint L'Ouverture of the Haitian Rebel," and it was her publisher who "wanted me to write a novel" instead. Second, she still keeps the objects the dying woman's daughter gave her, at her own bedside now: "a silver drinking cup which had belonged to John Keats," a "mosaic Persian letter set," and "a beautiful bird with a seashell." The woman called her "the prosaic sprite" because she refused the opium she was offered nightly, needing no drug "to dream." The Wilder anecdote appears again, and in the form it takes in 1983 rather than 1965: told she was writing a novel with the opium lady in it, Wilder said "as a way of revealing the unconscious." Young adds a working principle: "I start where it's easy. Start with something strange and beautiful," because, "I'm a Scot. I'm economical."

Verifying the imagination, and the making of the book

This interview is unusually specific about method. Young worried that her strangest inventions were mere fantasizing, "the one thing I believe I have never done," and checked them against a psychiatrist friend, Dr. Harberstock, who confirmed that Miss MacIntosh's black gloves carried the right meanings of "black" and "gloves" in relation to guilt, and that the detective who will not touch a case where "anything black appears, even a black rooster or the ace of spades," was "the classic case of schizophrenia," backed with case histories. She read medical literature throughout. Reality kept confirming her: after she wrote the sea-walk, "stark naked, except for wearing a pair of black gloves, which she had carefully darned the night before, as if she were going to her wedding," with the red wig left "on the back of a horseshoe crab" and an "Admiral Dewey corset" on the sand, she read of a boy on Long Island who "was stark naked except for black gloves, walked into the sea," which made her feel "psychologically correct." A real window-washer appeared at her window as she wrote the mystical one she cut from the book, one of the excised pieces she says were accepted as a separate volume of novellas. The bookkeeping details line up with the other accounts, with one variance: Scribner's "commissioned" the novel "in 1944," probably on the strength of "Angel in the Forest" and "Moderate Fable," after she submitted "about 15 pages." She expected "about 200 pages" and two years, but the characters kept deepening and she "worked from nine to five for 18 years," never bored, hearing "music when I'm writing," toward "a book of books, a song of songs." She calls a novel "a holy commitment," and names herself "a comic writer" who nonetheless sees "life as an absolute unredeemable tragedy," so that "what comes out is the other side, the comedy." She credits Scribner's for never interfering, running from her first editor Maxwell Perkins through John Hall Wheelock to Burroughs Mitchell: "Scribners never whistled in my ear."

The autobiography she admitted

The most significant passage complicates her steady claim that the book is not autobiographical. She describes her method as pursuit of "the archetype," projecting into a heightened character all it might become. Then she concedes the turn: "only toward the end of a book do I ever realize that after all, it is my own project, and that I am really writing an autobiographical novel." Pressed on whether the opium lady is her mother, she answers that she "didn't know that until almost the end of the book, since my mother was a kind of Titania-like dreamer, and irrational," and that "in a way, it was my mother," adding that had she known at the start "I would not have written the book." That is the frankest version in the whole set of a tension the WNYC talk only cracks open. The interview ends in lighter registers that suit her love of the strange: the Norman Mailer story, in which Mailer, learning her half-brother "was a champion of the ring," called her "a gentle Hercules" for "punching away for 18 years," and a cartoonist drew "Hercules in high heels"; and her collections, the thirteen "China-headed dolls" she calls "images of the soul" and names after friends "not after characters," and the seashells she says she "worships," including her relish for the true story of a collector ruined when a shell believed extinct reappeared and "the market was broken."

Set against the other interviews

For the compilation, this interview both confirms the settled story and sharpens it. It restates the opium-lady origin, the missing-sense theme, the muralist method, the Browning creed, and the 18-year span, and it firms up the Wilder anecdote in the "revealing the unconscious" form shared with 1983, against the "eliminating" version of 1965. A few new drifts appear: the commission is dated to 1944 here and the start to 1943 in 1983, and the abandoned projects multiply, a Toussaint L'Ouverture biography named here beside the James Whitcomb Riley biography of the mid-1960s talk and the Debs work of the later years. The interview's real contribution is threefold. It supplies the literary-world context the others lack, from the New Critics to the early publication of Anais Nin. It documents a concrete method, the checking of imagined pathologies against Dr. Harberstock and the medical literature, that grounds her claim that everything is drawn from life. And it gives the clearest admission that Miss MacIntosh is, at bottom and by her own late recognition, an autobiographical book with her mother inside the opium lady, which is the single most important line to weigh against the flat denials elsewhere in the set.
