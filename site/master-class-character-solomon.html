Solomon: The Slow Wisdom Under the Bed

← Mr. Bonebreaker · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Friday →

Spoiler note: This portrait is written for readers who have finished the novel. It discusses the character’s whole story, including how it ends.

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

He is a turtle, and he is one of the few companions Vera has in the schoolroom by the sea. Her mother gave him to her "on my twelfth birthday," having received him herself "from one of her dead guests," and named him Solomon, a name the governess "did not approve of but which she could do nothing to change." Solomon is "quite ancient," "a great giant turtle who might have carried, as in some legendary preadamite time, the whole world on his back." Now he carries only "a clay flower pot in which I had planted a red geranium which smelled like tobacco," and wearing his small absurd crown he takes "his meditative constitutional" across the bare floor, scraping, slow, his own castle, drawing head and tail and legs into his shell "as if it carried its own bed covers always."

The two grown women read him in exactly opposite ways, and the split is an example of the whole book in miniature. Miss MacIntosh, the rationalist governess, "could see no wisdom in a turtle," only sloth, "an old turtle sleeping its life away and unaware of happenings on Wall Street or Threadneedle Street." A turtle, she rules, "should be no example for me." Vera's mother, the dreamer, gave him the name of the wisest king. Between the woman who trusts only the visible ledger and the woman who trusts the dream, the slow creature carrying his house is either nothing or everything. The book, which loves the axis that holds still while the wheel turns, is quietly on the turtle's side. Solomon is the vertical line made a small animal, unhurried, self-contained, carrying his whole world and his whole shelter with him, indifferent to the market, wise in the way the philosophy calls wise, by staying in slow contact with what he is made of.

He is also the sacred ordinary, the precious thing you do not notice until it is gone. When Miss MacIntosh "put him out-of-doors where he belongs," she tells Vera, "you will never miss him." She is wrong. His absence is "the absence of something which, though very precious, I had never appreciated before," and the bedroom without his soft padded footsteps is "filled with emptiness." He "had never done very much but push his head in and out of a hard shell," and that turns out to have been a companionship, a presence, a slow heartbeat in the room, and Vera sinks to her knees when he is taken away.

The turn for a writer is a small, steadying one. Solomon is the pace the work actually requires, the slow wisdom, the rationalist mistakes for sloth, the willingness to carry the whole weight and go at a scrape, "unaware of happenings on Wall Street," untroubled by the news and the noise. He is his own castle, needing little, wise by patience rather than by speed. And he is the ordinary companion that is not valued until the brisk sensible voice puts him out the door and the room fills with emptiness. The book lets the governess be wrong about him. The slow thing is not missed until it is gone. It should have been appreciated while it scraped across the floor, carrying its geranium, in no hurry at all.
