Lucie Bell: The Girl Who Was Too Loud

← The Stone Deaf Man · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Characters as Representations →

Characters: Lucie Bell · The Stone Deaf Man · Vera Cartwheel

Before Vera came, the stone deaf man loved a woman named Lucie Bell, and Lucie Bell was noise. Vera never sees or hears her, "she had left him before I ever came," and everything we know of her comes through his all-night lament against her, which is really a lament that she could not be silent. He loved silence, and she was "an alarm clock to awaken a man," "a four o'clock train," "a loud noise jangling in my ear," "too noisy a girl." "Be thou the silent bell," he begs the memory of her, but her whole nature was to ring.

She was a runaway and a hitchhiker, a woman who "waited by the side of the road" and held up her little thumb, who had been "running away from an old husband, an old man," and who would run again, "hitch-hiking on, Lucie Bell, come some other dawn." He cannot trust her because she is made of leaving: "if you was running away from an old husband, an old man, why should I think you would be faithful to me?" She loved noisy things, "a jukebox more than she loved" him, "a tired marathon skater" she would "hang around his neck like an albatross." She is the loud, living, faithless girl, the one who could not hold still in the silent world he wanted to keep her in.

There is a cruelty in his verdict that the book does not hide. He decides she "never" loved, that she was only "a loud noise," because she would not be quiet, because she woke him when he wanted "not awakening." He wants a woman who "slept in my arms when the cry was gone," and Lucie Bell was the cry. So he lets her go down the road and turns his love toward silence itself, toward Vera, who will spell her name on his hand and make no sound. Lucie Bell is what the silence-lover cannot keep, the one who is too alive, too loud, too much herself to be folded into his stillness.

The turn for a writer is quick and rueful. Lucie Bell is the living, jangling material that will not be quiet, that will not stay where the maker wants it, that loves the jukebox and the skater and the open road and moves on at dawn. The artist who is in love with silence, with the perfected still thing, loses her, and half-persuades himself she was never worth keeping, "a loud noise," never really loved. But she is the noise of a life that refused to be arranged, and the book lets her keep her thumb out by the side of the road, going somewhere, while the man who could not bear her ringing settles down with silence and calls it love. She is a small figure and a sharp one, the reminder that the thing too loud to keep may have been the living thing, and that calling it noise is how the maker forgives himself for letting it go.
