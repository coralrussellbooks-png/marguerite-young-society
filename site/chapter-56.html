To All My Darlings

Chapter 56

On Chapter 56 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 11, 2026

← Chapter 55 · Home · Chapter 57 →

Content note: This chapter mentions the suicide of Mr. Spitzer’s twin brother, Peron.

Summary

Mr. Spitzer remembers dressing Peron for burial in a black swallow-tail suit cut like his own, with a red rose to be known by at the gates of the great city, two coins for the ferryman, poker chips, dice showing only sevens and elevens, a racing form, and the six of hearts. A one-armed violinist and the track bugler played at the grave. Over the years he gave Peron’s enormous wardrobe to the tramps of Boston, Savile Row suits, hats of every kind, and twelve jockeys’ silks, and found among them the tiny suit of a little man no one could explain. He mourns for everything that changes, from the dew on a flower to the Dog Star, and comes to believe that reality is only clothing on a dream. He is a tailor’s dummy, a puppet whose puppeteer is dead, the scraps and patches of a man who does not know whether he is living or dead. He still carries the golden key to a door that is no more, and the dead musician in him will not stop singing.

Key passages

From the research

  • Mr. Spitzer meticulously arranges his brother’s funeral, dressing the corpse with the utmost sartorial elegance, symbolizing death as a formal occasion requiring proper attire and ritual. The detail about placing poker chips, dice, and a playing card in the grave represents the interplay of chance and fate in life and death. The presence of a violinist with an empty sleeve and an official track bugler who had played at renowned venues emphasizes the blending of the theatrical, the musical, and the ceremonial in mourning. Mr. Spitzer’s identity and grief are deeply intertwined with this performance of death, as he leaves only the red rose to Peron, symbolizing recognition at “the portals of the great city.”
  • Peron is portrayed as an elusive, shape-shifting figure, likened to a squid that evades capture by producing an inky cloud, while Mr. Spitzer is the shadow to Peron’s substance. This metaphor illustrates their complex, intertwined identities. Mr. Spitzer clings to his threadbare suit as a sign of his enduring self amidst chaos, while recognizing that both brothers have become all others except themselves by reflecting on themes of identity dissolution and the fluidity of self.
  • The act of giving away Peron’s lavish wardrobe to the homeless and the poor is laden with symbolic significance. Clothing, representing identity and presence, is dispersed, highlighting the transience of life and the fading of memory. The detailed description of the various garments and hats, especially the jockeys’ suits, connects Peron’s past life and interests to the present dispersal, symbolizing the scattering of a once vibrant existence into fragments of color and fabric.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s grief extends beyond personal loss to a cosmic mourning for all changing and ephemeral things: flowers, animals, natural phenomena, moments, and even the future. His grief is universal and diffuse, capturing an almost pantheistic sorrow for impermanence and transformation. The imagery of tides, stars, moths, and flowers conveys a deep connection between human sorrow and the natural world.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s philosophical reflections reveal his skepticism about reality, suggesting that what we call reality is merely appearances and dreams layered upon each other. He questions the existence of a stable self or a divine author, concluding that life and death might be inseparable or aspects of the same experience. The motifs of clothing, masks, and shadows reinforce the theme of identity as a fragile and mutable construct. Mr. Spitzer’s feeling of being a “dead man walking” clad in his black suit symbolizes his liminal state between life and death.
  • The imagery of Mr. Spitzer as a puppet, a tailor’s dummy, and a cloud with seams unraveling captures his fragmented existence and loss of agency. He is a “poor copy” of himself, patched and fragile, caught between madness and reality. His body and mind are portrayed as disconnected, with his identity slipping away like a dream or a reflection in a dark glass. This passage powerfully conveys the disintegration of self and the ephemeral nature of being.
  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates madness not merely as illness but as a state that blurs the boundaries between life and death, sustaining the semblance of health even as the mind is annihilated. This duality suggests that madness may be the only life possible for the dead, reflecting the profound ambiguity of existence in the aftermath of loss.
  • In the closing passage, Mr. Spitzer laments the absence of a guiding author or architect of being. The world is a place without an ordered design, and he carries the “golden key to the door that was no more,” symbolizing lost access to meaning or transcendence. The final image of the “dead musician” who lives despite attempts to silence him encapsulates the persistence of art, memory, or spirit amid chaos and decay.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “Death was a formal occasion, one for which a man should always be properly dressed.” Why does Mr. Spitzer dress his brother like himself?
  • “Reality was that which was unreal and was clothed with the dream of reality.” What does clothing have to do with reality in this chapter?
  • Mr. Spitzer “mourned for everything.” How does grief for one person spread to the whole world?

Characters

  • Peron was “the man of many coats and hats.” What do his clothes reveal about him?
  • Among Peron’s clothes is “the miniature suit of a tiny dwarf.” Who might the little man be?
  • “He only knew that he was Peron, his dead brother.” Has Mr. Spitzer become his brother?

Connections

  • “Ah, what mad tailor had tailored Mr. Spitzer.” How does this tailor echo the tailor retailored of Chapter 55?
  • “The great puppeteer was dead.” Where else in the novel do people keep moving after what moved them is gone?
  • “Those were pearls that were his eyes.” Why does Young bring in the drowned father of The Tempest here?

Reflection

  • “How sad it was, Mr. Spitzer remarked, when the body was no more, and only the clothing remained like the memory of a man.” What do the belongings of the dead mean to the living?
  • “Perhaps madness was the only life possible for the dead.” Can grief keep a person alive?
  • “There was no bridge but that bridge one built by walking where there was no bridge.” How does a person go on without a guide?

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • Mr. Spitzer is the grieving brother, depicted as a man shattered by loss, struggling with his identity and the nature of reality. He clings to his old, threadbare clothes as symbols of himself and his grief.

Peron

  • Peron is Mr. Spitzer’s deceased brother, described as eccentric with a flamboyant lifestyle, many coats and hats, and a lavish wardrobe. Peron’s death triggers Mr. Spitzer’s existential reflections.

The Tailor

  • The Tailor (mad tailor) is a metaphorical figure representing the forces shaping Mr. Spitzer’s identity and existence, questioning who “tailored” him in this strange life and afterlife.

Research: Themes

Death and Mourning

See: Death and Mortality · Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • Death is portrayed as a formal, solemn occasion requiring proper dress and ritual, exemplified by the funeral preparations and symbolic items placed with Peron (poker chips, dice, playing cards, racing forms).
  • Mr. Spitzer’s grief is all-encompassing, extending beyond his brother to encompass all things transient and ephemeral (flowers, moths, tides, stars, moments).
  • The boundary between life and death is blurred; Mr. Spitzer often questions whether he is alive or dead, and whether life and death are two sides of the same experience.

Identity and the Self

See: Identity and the Self

  • Mr. Spitzer struggles with his identity, feeling like a shadow or a poor copy of his brother, or even a puppet controlled by unknown forces.
  • He questions the nature of selfhood, pondering whether he might become his brother by adopting his clothing and mannerisms.
  • The metaphor of clothing is powerful: Peron’s elaborate wardrobe symbolizes identity and social roles, while Mr. Spitzer’s threadbare suit symbolizes decay, loss, and continuity of self.

Reality versus Illusion

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Reality is depicted as an illusion, a dream clothed in appearances.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s literal-mindedness causes him to confuse abstract qualities (love, beauty, truth) with physical objects, illustrating his disorientation.
  • He philosophizes that reality might be “unreal” and that existence is layered with dreams and shadows, making existence fragile and unstable.

Madness and Sanity

See: Madness and Sanity

  • Madness is presented ambiguously as both a form of death and a form of life, sustaining illusion and preventing the advance of time.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s mental state deteriorates as he questions who controls him, who “tailored” his existence, and whether he is merely a puppet or a phantom.
  • His inability to distinguish between reality and imagination is a symbol of his psychological collapse.

Clothing and Tailoring

White and Red Roses

The Mirror and Reflection

See: Mirrors and Reflections

  • Symbolize self-perception and identity confusion; often Mr. Spitzer sees distorted or absent reflections.

Clouds, Sea, and Tides

See: The Sea and Water · Clouds and Storms

  • Recurring natural motifs representing impermanence, change, and the fluid boundary between life and death.

Music and Instruments

See: Music and Instruments

  • The funeral bugler and violinist evoke the ceremonial aspect of death and a connection to the past.

Animals and Nature

  • Moths, barnacles, starfish, whales, and birds symbolize fragility, transformation, and the natural cycle of life and death.

  • “he bore the exact relation to his brother as the shadow to the substance”: In Aesop’s fable of the dog and its reflection, a dog carrying meat across a stream snaps at the meat in its reflection and drops its own; the moral is not to lose the substance by grasping at the shadow.
  • “in his Prince Albert cutaway”: “Prince Albert cutaway” frock coat is a formal men’s coat characterised by a knee-length skirt cut all around the base just above the knee, popular during the Victorian and Edwardian periods (1830s-1910s). It is a fitted, long-sleeved coat with a centre vent at the back and some features unusual in post-Victorian dress. These include the reverse collar and lapels, where the outer edge of the lapel is often cut from a separate piece of cloth from the main body and also a high degree of waist suppression around the waistcoat, where the coat’s diameter around the waist is less than around the chest. This is achieved by a high horizontal waist seam with side bodies, which are extra panels of fabric above the waist used to pull in the naturally cylindrical drape. As was usual with all coats in the 19th century, shoulder padding was rare or minimal. (Wikipedia)
  • “many tailored suits from Savile Row”: Savile Row is a street in Central London that was originally famous for men’s tailoring. See On Savile Row and the history of tailoring and Clothing, Hats, and Skirts.
  • “innumerable fine Steinkirk thrice-winding scarves”: Steinkirk is a kind of neckcloth worn in a loose and disorderly fashion. It is named for the Battle of Steenkerque (1692), where French officers, surprised by the enemy, went into battle with their cravats wound loosely and tucked through a buttonhole. See Clothing, Hats, and Skirts.
  • “the period of Beau Brummell”: Beau Brummell was an important figure in Regency England and known for promoting men’s fashion. He was born in 1778, the same year as Lord Brougham. See On Beau Brummell and the philosophy of dress.
  • “the brougham which was named after Lord Brougham”: Henry Brougham (1778-1868), Lord Chancellor from 1830 to 1834, helped pass the Reform Act of 1832 and the Slavery Abolition Act of 1833, which ended slavery in most of the British Empire. The brougham, a light closed carriage, was built to his design in 1838. Young’s dates for him are right. See Horses and Carriages.
  • “huntsmen’s jackets, both green and red”: Fox hunters wear scarlet coats, called hunting pink, while those who hunt hares with beagles and harriers traditionally wear green. See Clothing, Hats, and Skirts.
  • “hound’s-tooth”: The houndstooth coat pattern originated as a utilitarian “shepherd’s check” wool fabric in the Scottish Lowlands in the 1800s, named for its resemblance to dog teeth. The pattern gained widespread popularity in the 1930s and was later adopted by high fashion, with designers like Coco Chanel and Christian Dior making it a symbol of luxury and timeless elegance in the 20th century. Today, it remains a versatile classic, found in various colors, scales, and materials beyond its origins in woven wool.
  • “herring-bone”: Herringbone fabric has ancient roots, first seen in Roman road construction (Opus spicatum) and later in Egyptian textiles, with the name coming from the V-shaped herring fish bones. While its textile use began in the 19th century, the fabric gained prominence in the early 20th century for its use in tough, practical hunting and workwear, like the US Army’s herringbone twill (HBT) uniforms in the 1940s, and became associated with formal wear in the 1950s.
  • “Canton flannels”: Is a warm, cotton-based fabric, named for its origin in Canton (now Guangzhou), China, around the mid-to-late 19th century. It is characterized by a twill or plain weave on one side and a fleecy, napped surface on the other. The fabric was widely used in the American Civil War for military undergarments and later for sportswear and linings, becoming a staple of the textile industry by the early 20th century.
  • “fine merinos”: Merino wool’s history began in 12th-century Spain, where the breed was developed for its fine, luxurious fleece. Exporting Merino sheep was strictly forbidden and punishable by death, but occasional gifts and attempts to smuggle the sheep allowed the breed to spread. The sheep were later introduced to South Africa and then Australia by the late 18th century, becoming the foundation for Australia’s significant wool industry. Merinos eventually reached the United States in the early 19th century, sparking “merino mania” and a boom in sheep farming there.
  • “colored broadcloths”: Colored broadcloth has a long history from its origins in ancient civilizations to its widespread use in medieval Europe and colonial America, especially for trade and fashion. Originally, only the wealthy could afford it, but advancements in technology made it more accessible. Different quality broadcloths were available, and colors were produced using natural dyes. The fabric was used for clothing, and also in home decor, and trade goods.
  • “great astrakhans”: Astrakhan is the tightly curled fleece of young karakul lambs, named for the Russian city of Astrakhan on the Volga, through which it was traded; an astrakhan is a coat or collar made of it.
  • “camel’s hair”: Camel hair has a long history, dating back to biblical times when it was used by nomadic peoples for practical items like tents and cloaks, leveraging the hair’s natural insulation. Its use in Western fashion surged in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, popularized by polo players in England and later becoming a “glamour garment” in the U.S. through its association with the F. Scott Fitzgerald era. Today, it’s a luxury fiber from Mongolia and China, valued for its softness, warmth, and sustainable harvesting process, used in high-end apparel and other textiles.
  • “Bond Street fleece from India”: Bond Street is London’s street of luxury shops. The fleece from India is probably cashmere or pashmina, combed from the fine undercoat of Himalayan goats.
  • “Irish cambrics”: Primarily relates to the development of the Irish linen industry, which became a global leader for fine, high-quality fabric. French Huguenot Louis Crommelin modernized the production process in the late 17th century, establishing Lisburn as a major center for fine linen, including cambric-like textiles, and fostering linen’s importance in the Irish economy. While “cambric” also refers to a specific fine linen fabric, it became strongly associated with Irish lace, particularly Carrickmacross lace, from the mid-19th century, where it was used as an embroidered appliqué base. Cambric takes its name from Cambrai, in northern France.
  • “bright plaid shawls”: Emerged from the ancient Celtic tradition, evolving into clan-specific Scottish tartan, which symbolized kinship and was also functional as a blanket. After a ban in the 18th century, tartan gained royal endorsement in the 19th century, leading to its worldwide popularity. The word “plaid” itself comes from the Gaelic term for a blanket or piece of cloth. European weavers eventually mimicked the costly Kashmir shawls, creating westernized versions with similar patterns but using less expensive fibers like wool and silk, often produced in Paisley, Scotland.
  • “paisleys”: The Paisley pattern originated from the ancient buto or buta motif in Persia and India, possibly dating back to 2,000 years ago and associated with the Zoroastrian symbol of life and fertility. The design was popularized on Kashmir shawls in India during the 18th century and later became a significant textile industry in Paisley, Scotland, which gave the pattern its English name. Its journey continued through import to Europe, replication by local textile manufacturers like those in Paisley, and adoption by various cultural movements, becoming a global icon in fashion and design. Weavers in Paisley and other Scottish towns endured harsh conditions and were subjected to other forms of brutal treatment typical of the Industrial Revolution. The Calton weavers of Glasgow, for example, went on strike in 1787 to protest a 25% wage cut. During the protest, troops opened fire on the demonstrators, killing six weavers. Work conditions for textile workers in Britain were notoriously poor, with long hours, low wages, and unsafe machinery that led to frequent accidents.
  • “suits of twill and basket and plain and satin weaves”: Twill’s history as a suiting fabric stems from its ancient origins as a strong, durable weave that became popular for its strength and diagonal pattern. It evolved from early functional garments to status symbols and was crucial for military uniforms by the 18th and 19th centuries. During the Industrial Revolution, mass production made twill accessible for everyday wear, while its sophisticated aesthetic solidified its place in classic men’s tailoring and modern suiting for both formal and smart-casual occasions.
    • Basket weave is a fabric weave in which two or more threads pass over and under together, giving a checked, basket-like texture, as in oxford cloth and hopsack.
    • The plain weave, a fundamental and ancient technique also known as tabby weave, has a history stretching back to Neolithic times, with evidence of early forms found in the form of clay impressions from the Czech Republic around 27,000 years ago and textile fragments from the Turkish site of Çatalhöyük around 7000 BC. This simple criss-cross weaving pattern is created by interlacing warp and weft yarns and remains the most basic and common type of woven fabric, forming a stable and durable material used in countless textiles and industrial applications today.
    • Weave originated in medieval China around the 9th or 10th century in the port city of Quanzhou. The fabric was initially made from silk and named after the Arabic name for this city, “Zaitun.” Through the Silk Road, satin spread to the Middle East and then to Europe by the 12th century, where it became a luxury item for the aristocracy. Advances in the 19th-century Industrial Revolution, including the invention of the mercerization process for cotton, allowed for mass production and the creation of more affordable sateen, broadening its accessibility beyond the wealthy.
  • “several pairs of excellent knee breeches”: Were men’s trousers worn from the late 16th to early 19th centuries, ending around the knee, and were standard attire in Europe and America until they were replaced by long trousers, or pantaloons, which came to dominate by the 1820s. Originating from older leg coverings, knee breeches evolved from shorter versions worn in the Middle Ages to the dominant form of men’s lower-body wear. They were made of various materials like linen, cotton, and leather, were often embellished with ribbons or lace, and were seen in both everyday and formal wear, eventually surviving as court dress and in the livery of servants into the 20th century.
  • “excellent plus fours”: Are a specific type of knickerbockers, named for the four extra inches of fabric below the knee, popularized in the 1920s by the Duke of Windsor for golf and outdoor pursuits. They offered increased comfort and range of motion compared to shorter knickers and became a staple of the stylish, casual sportswear trend of the era. Though they declined in popularity in the 1930s with the introduction of walking shorts, they remained a recognized part of golf fashion.
  • “striped stockings”: Have a long and varied history, first appearing in the medieval period as garments for outcasts and criminals, but gaining respectability and popularity in the Victorian era as a fashionable statement. By the 18th century, vertical stripes were common, and the 19th century saw the advent of more colorful horizontal stripes for both everyday wear and sports like baseball. The 20th century brought new styles, with striped tube socks becoming a cultural icon in the 1960s and continuing to appear in various forms today for fashion and sports.
    • Have evolved over centuries, with their history tracing back to patterned plaids in ancient Celtic and Scottish cultures, famously refined into the Argyle pattern. However, smaller “checkered” or plaid patterns also featured on stockings in various forms, becoming prominent in specific eras.
  • “bright red fishermen’s hats”: The iconic bright red fisherman’s hat, most often a knitted beanie, has roots in 19th-century diving, Scandinavian folklore, and resistance movements. Its fame was cemented by legendary oceanographer Jacques Cousteau, who wore it as a tribute to early maritime tradition. The modern red beanie tradition traces back to oyster divers in the town of Whitstable, UK, in the 1820s. Early “hard hat” divers wore heavy metal helmets that contained a pressurized air valve, which was operated by bumping one’s head against it. A red wool cap was worn to prevent painful bruising from the valve. The red also made the caps visible in the dimly lit water. As diving technology advanced, divers continued to wear the red cap out of respect for the pioneers of their profession. Another thread of the red beanie’s history connects to Norwegian folklore and wartime defiance. In Norwegian fairy tales, the working-class hero Askeladden (the “Ash Lad”) is often depicted wearing a red knit cap with a pom-pom, symbolizing adventure and rebellion. During the Nazi occupation of Norway, the “nissebeanie,” a traditional red hat, became a silent symbol of patriotic resistance and Norwegian heritage. The German occupation forces considered the red hat such a threat that they eventually outlawed it. The red cap became famous globally due to the French oceanographer Jacques Cousteau, who proudly wore it on his televised adventures aboard the Calypso. Cousteau wore the red cap as a conscious tribute to the early diving pioneers. His highly popular TV series, The Undersea World of Jacques Cousteau, solidified the red beanie as a worldwide symbol of exploration and adventure in the 1960s. Flag: after 1964, Cousteau’s television series (1966-1976). See On the history of Peron’s hats.
  • “green-vizored yachtsmen’s hats”: The green-visored hat is most historically associated with accountants, gamblers, and other indoor professions that required reducing eye strain from harsh, early artificial lighting.
  • “Homburgs”: The homburg hat originated in the German spa town of Bad Homburg in the late 19th century and was popularized globally by Prince Edward VII of Britain around 1882, who encountered the style during a visit to the town. Known for its stiff brim, single center dent, and formal elegance, the homburg became a symbol of status and was adopted by politicians and business leaders, famously worn by Winston Churchill and, in a later cinematic resurgence, Al Pacino as the “Godfather hat.” Flag: after 1964, The Godfather (1972).
  • “Stetsons”: The Stetson hat company was founded by John B. Stetson in Philadelphia in 1865, after he created a durable hat for himself on a Western expedition that became known as the “Boss of the Plains.” This practical, wide-brimmed hat was a success and set the standard for cowboy hats, leading Stetson to become the world’s largest hat manufacturer by 1886. The company was known for its quality, innovation, and the creation of the first “cowboy hat.”
  • “plugs”: Plug hat was nineteenth-century American slang for a top hat or other stiff hat. Baltimore’s Plug Uglies, a gang of the 1850s, are said to have stuffed their plug hats with wool and leather to wear as helmets in street fights.
  • “trilbys”: Were named after a 1895 stage adaptation of George du Maurier’s novel “Trilby,” as the lead actress wore the hat, which then became known as the “Trilby.” The hat, with its short, downward-angled brim and slightly upward-turned back, rose to prominence in the 1920s and 30s, becoming associated with jazz culture and Hollywood.
  • “pork-pies”: The pork pie hat originated in the mid-19th century in America and Britain, deriving its name from its resemblance to a pork pie. It was first a women’s fashion item but later popularized for men by entertainers like Buster Keaton in the 1920s, evolving into a symbol of cool for jazz and blues musicians like Lester Young in the 1930s and 40s.
  • “brown bowler derbies”: The history of the brown bowler derby hat began in 1849 when London hatmakers Thomas and William Bowler created it for Edward Coke, a British nobleman, to provide a more practical and durable alternative to the tall top hat for his gamekeepers. Made from hard, dark felt (often black or brown), this hat’s stiff, rounded crown protected wearers from low-hanging branches. While the original was commissioned for British gamekeepers, its protective and distinctive style led to widespread adoption by British working and upper classes, eventually becoming the well-known American “derby” hat and a favorite of both lawmen and outlaws in the Wild West.
  • “all kinds of head coverings including the ass’s head”: In Shakespeare’s A Midsummer Night’s Dream, Puck gives the weaver Bottom an ass’s head, and Titania, under a love spell, falls in love with him (3.1). See Shakespeare.
  • “several beautiful Balaklava helmets”: The knitted balaclava is named for the Battle of Balaclava (1854) in the Crimean War, the battle of the Charge of the Light Brigade, where British soldiers are said to have worn knitted hoods against the Russian winter; the name is first recorded in the 1880s.
  • “les grandes neiges”: Means the great snow..
  • “many winter aconites”: Winter aconites is a flower in the buttercup family that grows in France, Italy, and the Balkans. It flowers yellow in late winter, often through the snow. See Roses and Flowers.
  • “twelve small jockeys’ bird-billed hats and colored suits”: Outfit. Historians suggest the practice of using colors to distinguish riders originated in Ancient Rome, where chariot racers wore colored capes to help spectators identify the different factions (Blues, Greens, Reds, and Whites). As horse racing became a more formalized sport in England, the need for rider identification grew. In 1762, the English Jockey Club made it mandatory for owners to register their unique color and pattern combinations. The earliest registered “silks” were actually made of expensive materials like silk from Asia or fine European wool. The fabrics were chosen for their vibrant colors and light weight, reflecting the aristocratic nature of the sport.
  • “much like beetles carrying the body of a dead bird”: Burying beetles really do this. A pair of Nicrophorus beetles will drag and bury the body of a small bird or mouse, strip it, and raise their young on it.
  • “harlequin designs, domino checks”: Harlequin of the Italian commedia dell’arte wore a suit of colored diamond patches; a domino is a loose hooded cloak worn with a half mask at masquerades.
  • “tremulous as clouds with silver linings”: “Was I deceived, or did a sable cloud / Turn forth her silver lining on the night?” (John Milton, Comus, 1634). See Milton and Clouds and Storms.
  • “the dew drop trembling on the lip of the helmet flower”: Helmet flower is monkshood, Aconitum, named for its hooded upper petal; every part of it is poisonous. See Roses and Flowers.
  • “the star-nosed mole burrowing under ground”: The star-nosed mole of eastern North America has a ring of twenty-two fleshy rays around its nose, with which it feels its way through dark tunnels and wet ground.
  • “the flower-faced bat flying out over the moving waters”: Is a species of bat in the family Hipposideridae. It is in the monotypic genus Anthops. It is found in the Autonomous Region of Bougainville of Papua New Guinea and in the Solomon Islands. This rare and little-known bat has been recorded from tropical moist forest and flying around village houses. (Wikipedia)
  • “the spouter whale spouting upon a far horizon”: A spouter is a whale seen blowing; Ishmael spends his first night in New Bedford at the Spouter-Inn (Herman Melville, Moby-Dick, ch. 2-3, 1851). See American Writers.
  • “the whelks and the wentletraps like staircases to heaven”: Wentletrap is from the Dutch wenteltrap, a spiral staircase. The precious wentletrap was so prized by eighteenth-century collectors that counterfeits were made of rice paste.
  • “the great phaetons”: Phaethon, son of the sun god Helios, drove his father’s chariot, lost control, and was struck down by Zeus; the light, open phaeton carriage is named for him. See Greek Mythology and Horses and Carriages.
  • “the Dog star which was the brightest star of the heavens”: Sirius, the Dog Star in the constellation Canis Major, is the brightest star in the night sky. See Astronomy.
  • “his whirling Inverness”: Is a form of weatherproof outer-coat. It is notable for being sleeveless, the arms emerging from arm holes beneath a cape. The sleeved version is an Inverness coat. The commonly held image of the cape as worn by Sherlock Holmes is one made of tweed, specifically in a grey hound’s tooth pattern. (Wikipedia)
  • “walking on water as if it were land”: “And when Peter was come down out of the ship, he walked on the water, to go to Jesus” (Matthew 14:29, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “the glass of fashion and the powdered fop”: Ophelia remembers Hamlet as “The glass of fashion and the mould of form” (William Shakespeare, Hamlet, 3.1). See Shakespeare.
  • “like the colors of a dying dolphin”: Byron: “Parting day / Dies like the dolphin, whom each pang imbues / With a new colour as it gasps away, / The last still loveliest” (Lord Byron, Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage, canto 4, stanza 29, 1818). The dolphin is the dolphinfish, whose colors change as it dies. See Romantic Poets.
  • “the rapidly changing colors as of some great color wheel”: Isaac Newton bent the spectrum into a circle in his Opticks (1704), the first color wheel.
  • “the spray of Venus hair”: Venus hair is maidenhair fern, Adiantum capillus-veneris, the hair of Venus.
  • “How often had he died”: “Cowards die many times before their deaths; / The valiant never taste of death but once” (William Shakespeare, Julius Caesar, 2.2). See Shakespeare.
  • “the golden cockerel weathervane”: In Pushkin’s “The Tale of the Golden Cockerel” (1834), a golden cockerel on a spire crows to warn the tsar of danger; Rimsky-Korsakov made it an opera (1909). See Music and Composers.
  • “The earth was moved by the moon like the tide”: The solid earth has tides too. Twice a day the ground under a person’s feet rises and falls, by up to about a foot and a half, as the moon and sun pull on it. See Astronomy.
  • “but who was the spinner?”: Clotho, one of the three Fates, spins the thread of each life, Lachesis measures it, and Atropos cuts it. See Greek Mythology and Fate and Chance.
  • “what mad tailor had tailored Mr. Spitzer”: See the tailor retailored, Sartor Resartus, in the Chapter 55 resources. See The Great Tailor.
  • “the watery firmament”: “And God said, Let there be a firmament in the midst of the waters, and let it divide the waters from the waters” (Genesis 1:6, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “He was a poor puppet dancing on its strings”: Heinrich von Kleist’s essay “On the Marionette Theatre” (1810) argues that a puppet, with no consciousness to make it self-aware, moves with more grace than any human dancer.
  • “if the dreamer was dead, how should he know”: In Lewis Carroll’s Through the Looking-Glass (1871), Tweedledum and Tweedledee tell Alice that she is only a sort of thing in the sleeping Red King’s dream, and that if he woke she would go out like a candle. See Alice in Wonderland.
  • “a golden wind harp”: See the Aeolian harp in the Chapter 55 resources. See Romantic Poets.
  • “the sky streaked with silvery mares’-tails”: Mares’ tails are wispy cirrus clouds, high ice clouds that often come before a change of weather; sailors said, “Mackerel sky and mares’ tails make lofty ships carry low sails.” See Clouds and Storms.
  • “the night-crowned heron wader”: The black-crowned night heron fishes at dusk and by night. See the night-crowned kingfisher in the Chapter 55 resources. See Birds.
  • “the mantling hawk”: In falconry, a hawk mantles when it spreads its wings and tail over its prey to hide it. See Birds.
  • “Had he not given birth to death”: In Milton’s Paradise Lost, Sin springs full-grown from the head of Satan, and she later bears his son, Death (Book 2, 1667). See Milton.
  • “The mandrakes screamed”: The mandrake root, forked like a human body, was said to shriek when pulled from the ground and to kill or madden whoever heard it: “shrieks like mandrakes torn out of the earth, / That living mortals, hearing them, run mad” (William Shakespeare, Romeo and Juliet, 4.3). See Shakespeare.
  • “He was the octopus with the seven gold crowns”: “And there appeared another wonder in heaven; and behold a great red dragon, having seven heads and ten horns, and seven crowns upon his heads” (Revelation 12:3, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “the thirteenth month of the year”: Lunar calendars need a thirteenth month every few years to keep up with the sun; the Hebrew calendar adds a second month of Adar seven times in every nineteen years.
  • “the horseshoe crabs clattering”: Horseshoe crabs are not crabs but relatives of spiders and scorpions, and their kind has lived in the sea for more than 400 million years.
  • “the great hermits, the great kings in their shells”: Hermit crabs have soft bodies and live in borrowed snail shells, moving into larger ones as they grow, as Peron put on and put off his coats. King crabs are not true crabs but descend from hermit crabs.
  • “the quivering bodies of dead luna moths”: The adult luna moth has no mouth and does not eat; it lives about a week, long enough to mate. See Butterflies and Moths.
  • “Those were pearls that were his eyes”: Ariel sings of the drowned king: “Full fathom five thy father lies; / Of his bones are coral made; / Those are pearls that were his eyes” (William Shakespeare, The Tempest, 1.2). See Shakespeare.
  • “The great barnacle birds in the clouds”: Medieval naturalists believed that barnacle geese grew from goose barnacles on driftwood, since no one had seen them nest; Gerald of Wales described it in 1188. See Birds.
  • “A garment of fog covered the earth”: “Thou coveredst it with the deep as with a garment: the waters stood above the mountains” (Psalm 104:6, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “kept time from advancing even though it had advanced, and all the hours struck at once”: Compare Miss Havisham in Charles Dickens’s Great Expectations (1861), who stopped every clock in her house at twenty minutes to nine, the moment she was jilted.
  • “with the loosest basting stitches imaginable”: Are temporary, long stitches used in sewing to hold fabric layers together or mark positions, and are easily removed later. They are commonly used to test fit, hold fabric for sewing, or transfer pattern markings. Basting stitches are typically done with a longer stitch length on a sewing machine or with longer stitches by hand. See On basting stitches and the provisional self.
  • “he heard a chimney cricket singing where there was no cricket”: In Charles Dickens’s The Cricket on the Hearth (1845), the cricket chirping on the hearth is the guardian spirit of the home.
  • “lost in the horse latitudes”: The horse latitudes, about thirty degrees north and south of the equator, are belts of calm where sailing ships could lie becalmed for weeks; the legend says sailors threw horses overboard there to save water. See Horses and Carriages.
  • “the great architect was dead”: Freemasons call God the Great Architect of the Universe.
  • “the master of the scrolls”: Young’s master of the scrolls recalls the Master of the Rolls, the English judge who first kept the rolls of the Court of Chancery and now heads its civil appeals. See Cases in Chancery in the Chapter 55 resources.
  • “the shriekings of the silken skies when they are torn in two”: “And the heaven departed as a scroll when it is rolled together” (Revelation 6:14, KJV). See The Bible.
  • The extensive list of fabric types serves multiple symbolic and thematic purposes rather than a simple inventory of clothing. This detailed enumeration of fabrics reflects the opulence and complexity of the material world surrounding the characters. The fabrics symbolize social status, the passage of time, and the layering of identity. The mention that these clothes were passed out to the poor, tramps, and ragged men throughout the years indicates a theme of decay and redistribution or how material wealth and social appearances are ultimately transient and dispersed. The passage also contrasts the elaborate, sometimes ostentatious external trappings with the internal realities of the characters, especially Mr. Spitzer, who is portrayed as a man increasingly detached from his own identity and reality. His clothes become metaphors for his fragmented self, patched over and worn thin with time and experience, much like his mental and spiritual condition. The fabrics, therefore, also evoke the themes of mortality, loss, and the illusory nature of social appearances. The list of fabric types is not merely a description of wardrobe but a symbolic representation of: the complexity and multiplicity of identities and social roles, the passage of time and inevitable decay, the contrast between external appearance and internal reality, the dispersal of wealth and status, the fragility and impermanence of human life and social constructs. This symbolic layering enriches the novel’s exploration of illusion versus reality, mortality, and the search for authentic identity.
