Chapter 41

On Chapter 41 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Christianity and Religion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 24, 2026

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

The mother's seeming miracles, making walls speak, freezing the ocean, turning mountains to song, echo the miracles of the gospels, a power over the natural world.

“with finger minarets breaking through foam and cloud, mosaic emperors”: The gold mosaics of Byzantium show emperors in glass and gold, as Justinian and Theodora in San Vitale, Ravenna (547). W. B. Yeats, in “Sailing to Byzantium” (1928), leaves the body behind: “And therefore I have sailed the seas and come / To the holy city of Byzantium,” calling on the “sages standing in God’s holy fire / As in the gold mosaic of a wall.”

The chapter's "spirits of the resurrected dead," and Mr. Spitzer's faith in his own rising, lean on the Christian promise of resurrection.

“the groundhogs who emerged on Candlemas Day”: Catherine describes the groundhog seeing its shadow like the custom in the US called Groundhog Day, yet she mentions it is like Candlemas Day also known as the Feast of the Presentation of Jesus Christ and is a Christian Holy Day. Both happen on February 2nd. German settlers in Pennsylvania brought the Candlemas lore of the badger and the hedgehog, and the groundhog took their place. An old rhyme runs, “If Candlemas Day be fair and bright, / Winter will have another flight.”

“where only the raven had spoken”: Ravens are often considered a mediator between life and death. “And he sent forth a raven, which went forth to and fro, until the waters were dried up from off the earth” (Genesis 8:7). See Genesis. Poe’s raven speaks only “Nevermore” (Edgar Allan Poe, “The Raven,” 1845). See the Chapter 26 resources. See Edgar Allan Poe. Earlier in Chapters 26 to 28.

“so many door knobs which should have been apples”: The fruit of the tree of knowledge, traditionally an apple, was “a tree to be desired to make one wise” (Genesis 3:6). See Genesis.

She believes death will make her "divinely happy," once she has found "a key to the door," or "a keyhole in a cloud," death as the passage to salvation, with Christ himself the door.

“what happens to one must happen to all”: “And I myself perceived also that one event happeneth to them all” (Ecclesiastes 2:14). See Ecclesiastes.

“were there not many roads to the crown of her head”: Catherine asks “...were there not many roads to the crown of her head?” This seems to mimic a saying often called a Hindu proverb: there are many paths up the mountain, all leading to the same place. While the exact phrasing is a modern paraphrase, the underlying concept is deeply rooted in traditional Hindu texts and philosophy. A classic parallel is found in the Siva Mahimnah Stotra (verse 7): “As the different streams having their sources in different places all mingle their water in the sea, so, O Lord, the different paths which men take through different tendencies, various though they appear, crooked or straight, all lead to Thee” (as quoted by Swami Vivekananda, address at the World’s Parliament of Religions, Chicago, September 11, 1893). Ramakrishna said, “As many faiths, so many paths” (The Gospel of Sri Ramakrishna, trans. Swami Nikhilananda, 1942). See Hinduism. In yoga the sahasrara, the thousand-petaled lotus, crowns the head, where the channels of the subtle body meet (Arthur Avalon, The Serpent Power, 1919).

“She was swifter than her own mind”: “That One, though motionless, is swifter than the mind. The senses can never overtake It, for It ever goes before.” “It moves and It moves not. It is far and also It is near. It is within and also It is without all this” (Isha Upanishad 4-5, trans. Swami Paramananda, 1919). Young: “She was far, yet she was near. She was within, yet she was outside.” See Hinduism.

"Once there were thirteen chandeliers left upon the beach" carries the number of the Last Supper and an image of light failing at the edge of the sea, fellowship and loss together.

“someone whose eyes were the moon and the sun”: “His eyes were as a flame of fire” (Revelation 1:14), and the mighty angel’s “face was as it were the sun” (Revelation 10:1). See Revelation and the Apocalypse. “The Moon was gendered from his mind, and from his eye the Sun had birth” (Rig Veda 10.90.13, trans. Ralph T. H. Griffith, 1896). See the Purusha entry below. Earlier in Chapter 29.

“an action in inaction”: “He who sees inaction in action, and action in inaction, is wise among men” (Bhagavad Gita 4.18, trans. Kashinath Trimbak Telang, 1882). See Hinduism.

“That which had never been, it was only that which was”: The Bhagavad Gita teaches that the unreal never comes into being and the real never ceases to be (2.16). See Hinduism. “All things under heaven sprang from It as existing (and named); that existence sprang from It as non-existent (and not named)” (Tao Te Ching 40, trans. James Legge, 1891). Young: “greater being in non-being.” See Taoism and Zhuangzi.

“many persons with many heads, limbs, arms”: “A thousand heads hath Purusha, a thousand eyes, a thousand feet” (Rig Veda 10.90.1, trans. Ralph T. H. Griffith, 1896). See Hinduism.
