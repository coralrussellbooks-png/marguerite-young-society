Inviting the Muses, Parts 10 and 11

← Part 9 · Inviting the Muses: A Reading, Part by Part

Parts 10 and 11 close the Reviews section and the book. They run from the end of the Indiana review through Capote, Marianne Hauser, Doris Lessing, Frederick Kiesler, two pieces on Anaïs Nin, a Sherlock Holmes roundup, and a final review on Poe, followed by the index. Where part 9 was clustered in the 1940s, these reviews spread across three decades, from 1948 to 1977.

"An Indiana Book," conclusion

The review ends with Martin's section on Debs and the Hapgoods, "men in revolt" against capitalism. Debs was the subject of Young's own unfinished biography, so it is fitting that he is the last figure mentioned in her last piece on Indiana.

"Tiger Lilies" (Kenyon Review, 1948)

Young reads Capote's Other Voices, Other Rooms as she read McCullers, as symbolic rather than realist. His characters are not "the supposed normal" but "a confluence of signs," each "the aberrant fragment of self," "a piece of the unconscious" in "a world of curved mirrors." She notes that "love is equated with death," that "everyone is doing, too, a disappearing act," and that the book happens "at the intersection between two traffic lines, the real and unreal." She closes on the marsh where tiger lilies grow "the size of a man's head," concluding that "this landscape is also psychic, a mystery."

"Comedy in a Tragic Show" (New York Herald Tribune Book Review, 1958)

Marianne Hauser's The Choir Invisible concerns a bank clerk with a blood cancer who becomes a choir director, trying to make "heavenly music on this earth." Young takes up the book's premise that when we are about to die "we begin to live," and notes his dying brings back the figures of his childhood. Her favorite pairing is a spiritualist who believes she was once a Roman officer and once a nun, "in deadly rivalry with the prosperous undertaker to whom mortality is necessity." She calls it "a book not about death but about life." The undertaker's trade recalls "The Dead Women," though here it is played for comedy.

"The Darkest Part of Africa" (Washington Post Book World, 1966)

A review of the third and fourth books in Doris Lessing's Children of Violence series. Young's reading is that "Africa is thus the true heroine" and that Lessing's white settlers miss everything by refusing to know "the African brother," cutting themselves off through "tribal taboos and totems," "curfew laws," and master-and-servant relations. She adds a sharp reversal: Lessing may doubt the Black African misses much by staying outside a white world "capable of more violence than was ever dreamed by primitive man." Her judgment on Martha, who joins a communist movement she only half understands, fits Young's own pacifist logic from 1945: the novel is "criticism not only of reality but of idealism when it begets violence."

"The 'Endless House': A Man-built Cosmos" (Architectural Forum, 1967)

On the journals of architect and sculptor Frederick Kiesler, published after his death. Young is drawn to his sense of creation as something that happens through him, as when ink drops seemed to guide his hand. She describes his sculpture of a horse a man may crawl into and emerge "born again," and repeats the etymology from "The Artist as Wanderer": the horse means "mare, water and mother." Kiesler's link-upon-link vision of a universe bound by "cosmic love" and his endless house with meadows on its roof are the kind of total work Young herself pursued. Her closing line serves as an epitaph: "It may be hoped that his end is his beginning."

"Anaïs Nin" (Voyages, 1967) and the Nin Reader review (New York Woman, 1973)

The first piece reviews the second volume of Nin's diary. Young sees her as not only lost in the labyrinth but also as its builder: "the minotaur" and "also Minos and is also Daedalus, the architect of the maze." She asks whether "a seashell know[s] its own spiralings," and answers that this diary is "the seashell writing its many rooms." The familiar creed returns: "style is thinking."

This essay also matters for what Young does with Nin's views on gender. Nin argues that the woman artist creates "in her own womb" and that man invented woman out of fear. Young quotes her at length but concludes as she did in "Feminine Sensibility": "Perhaps the problems are greater than the differences between men and women writers," rather lying between "the mediocre and true literary insight." She places Nin "with men of great gifts," with Hawthorne, Poe, and Firbank.

The second review, of The Anaïs Nin Reader and its introduction by Anna Balakian, is the piece the Publisher's Note says was unknown until Young's assistant sent it in. It praises Balakian for showing that Nin's diary and fiction "feed each other constantly," and it takes one more swipe at literalists who "seem somehow to wish to be the geniuses of the blank page and usually call themselves, for this very good reason, realists." The blank page has been her enemy from "Complex Life" in 1946 to here.

"The Great Detective" (New York Times Book Review, 1975)

A cheerful roundup of seven Holmes books. The motif Young keeps returning to is that Holmes cannot die. Doyle killed him at Reichenbach Falls, but "Sherlock could not stay dead," and the public forced his resurrection, which Doyle managed partly because "he did not believe that he himself would ever be extinguished." She treats Doyle's stories as "pre-Freudian psychodramatic confessions, just as all real fiction is." She enjoys Samuel Rosenberg's theory linking Moriarty to Nietzsche, Nicholas Meyer's The Seven-Per-Cent Solution, John Gardner's Victorian thieves' slang, and Robert L. Fish's parody The Memoirs of Schlock Homes, "a delightful crime which he is bound to survive."

"Defending an Injured Reputation" (New York Times Book Review, 1977)

The last piece in the book is a review of John Carl Miller's Building Poe Biography, on John Henry Ingram, a London post office clerk who spent his evenings rescuing Poe from Rufus Griswold's slanders. Young is unusually fierce toward Griswold, "this hack," "the mud-throwing, very unreverend Reverend," who set out to leave Poe lying "in the muck and mud until the angel of resurrection should blow his conch." She notes that nearly every figure Ingram wrote about "had left a reputation that needed redemption from slander."

The emotional center is Marie Louise Shew Houghton, the nurse at the Fordham cottage, who bought Virginia Poe's "coffin, her grave clothes, and Edgar's mourning," taught Poe to eat fish and clams for brain power, and burned the letters in which he called himself a "Lost Soul." The book's final lines are about the Poes' cat, Caterina, "so nervous and 'possessed'" after Virginia's death that the nurse feared her, and later found dead: "Mrs. Houghton was almost glad."

It is a strange, quiet ending, and a fitting one. Houghton belongs with Miss Greenwood and Dorothy Caruso, the women in this book who tend the famous man, keep his memory, and decide what to preserve and what to burn. The collection that opened with a family resurrecting a grandmother's foot closes with a man's reputation being raised from the grave.

The index

The index is a names-only list, and it works as a map of Young's mind. The longest runs belong to Riley, Alex Comfort, Marianne Moore, Nin, Patmore, Poe, and the Wandering Jew essay's painters. William James, Proust, Joyce, Sterne, Swedenborg, and Melville recur across widely separated pages, confirming them as her steady sources. Debs appears at the start of the book, in "On Teaching," and in the Indiana review. The range is part of the point: Pancho Villa, Crazy Horse, Pocahontas, Hitler, Goebbels, Innocent III, and the Abbé Sicard sit alongside Dryden and Mallarmé. Edward Young is listed, while Marguerite Young, naturally, is not, and neither is Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, which shadows almost every page.

The collection as a whole

Read end to end, Inviting the Muses shows remarkable consistency across more than fifty years. From the 1935 Poetry reviews to the 1989 teaching essay, Young holds to a few convictions: reality is stranger and more complex than common sense admits; the dream, the irrational, and the invisible are part of the real; style is thinking, and the blank page is not honesty but poverty; and what survives, whether a doll, a remembered house, a reputation, or a grandmother's foot, survives through memory and imagination. The war runs beneath much of it, and her pacifism gives the philosophy moral weight. The recurring figures, the detached hand or foot, the triangle of lovers, the woman who takes dictation from a great man, the horse, the resurrection, tie the stories, essays, and reviews into something close to a single long work, which is exactly the kind of book she spent her life defending.
