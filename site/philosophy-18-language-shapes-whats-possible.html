Chapter 18. Language Shapes What's Possible

← Previous · Philosophy of MMMD Home · Next →

See also: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Characters: Esther Longtree · Miss MacIntosh · The Stone Deaf Man · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Music, Sound, and Silence

Allusions: Deafness and Sign Language

Chapters discussed: 2, 63, 72, 82

Definition from research

Words build the world they describe. Nouns freeze things into fixed identities. And any voice that claims to speak from nowhere, in pure objectivity, is hiding where it really stands.

Miss MacIntosh, for all her faith in plain fact, distrusted words. “Words do not harness the winds,” she told Vera. “Love has shut many a woman’s mouth” (ch. 63). The realist knew that language was a construction, even if she did not know that her realism was one too.

Young’s own prose is the strongest demonstration of this principle. As Chapter 2 showed, her sentences do not state ideas and then move on. They build an idea, add to it, and let it collapse, all within the grammar. The long sentence is itself a construction that shows its own seams.

At the edges of the novel, language breaks down into something more honest. Esther Longtree was “learning the alphabet all over again because she was teaching it to the stillborn” (ch. 72). The stone deaf man speaks of words “not in the alphabet of the stars. Flowers spell them out on the earth, and grasses talk” (ch. 82). There is a language older than the alphabet, and it cannot be fixed in spelling.

The last words of the novel are a misspelled sign in a restaurant window: “Owt to luntsch. bee bak in a whale.” Then the narrator adds, “For she could not spell either.” A book of more than a thousand pages of the most elaborate English prose ends with a sentence no dictionary would accept, and it is perfectly clear. Language is a construction, and a broken construction can still carry love and a promise to return.
