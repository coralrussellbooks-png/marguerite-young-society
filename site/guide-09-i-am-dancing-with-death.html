Chapter 9: I Am Dancing with Death

On Chapter 9 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of A Guide to Reading Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

We step back from the bus now. Chapter 9 is one of the novel’s memory chapters, the kind Young writes when she wants to take you deep into the life that preceded the journey, into the texture of the years Vera spent under Miss MacIntosh’s care before everything broke apart.

If the early bus chapters showed you Miss MacIntosh through Vera’s grief and longing, Chapter 9 shows you what their daily life together actually looked like. And it is rich, strange, funny, tender, and shot through with an approaching darkness that Vera can feel but not yet name.

What actually happens in Chapter 9

Vera opens the chapter with a question she has been carrying since Miss MacIntosh disappeared: was she responsible? Did she cause it, in some way she cannot fully articulate? She does not answer this question. She turns it over and over like a stone.

Then she takes us back. She shows us the schoolroom: Miss MacIntosh at the blackboard, drilling Vera on geography, history, facts about the Chicago fire and the glaciers and the grasshoppers. She shows us the embroidery hoop, the straight basting stitches, the hemming of handkerchiefs, Miss MacIntosh’s program for turning Vera into a competent, marriageable woman, delivered with the focused intensity of someone who believes that discipline is protection.

She shows us Solomon the turtle, ancient and slow, hauling a clay flower pot with a geranium across the schoolroom floor while Miss MacIntosh eyes him with undisguised contempt. She shows us the silver dollar watch Miss MacIntosh wore, always producing it to remind Vera that time was passing, that they had work to do, that there was no room for sloth.

And she shows us Vera’s fourteenth birthday. Mr. Spitzer sends a party dress: filmy, sleeveless, embroidered with seed pearls. No guests come. Vera dances alone in the empty room, imagining a partner, and declares to Miss MacIntosh: I am dancing with Death.

Miss MacIntosh’s response is immediate and serious. Death is not a dance partner for a fourteen-year-old. Death is real, universal, coming for everyone, and not to be romanticized. Vera listens. And in the darkness afterward, she begins to think about marriage, about the man she might someday know, and whether she would ever truly know him at all.

Miss MacIntosh as teacher

The schoolroom scenes in this chapter are among the most vivid in the novel, and they deserve your full attention.

Miss MacIntosh’s education program for Vera is not gentle. It is relentless. Geography, history, domestic skills, moral instruction and all of it delivered by a woman who came from nothing, who believes that preparation is the only armor a girl has, and who is determined to give Vera the tools that her mother’s opium dreams never provided.

But notice what is underneath the curriculum. When Vera asks Miss MacIntosh about her ovaries, a reasonable question from a girl navigating adolescence, Miss MacIntosh redirects immediately to an unrelated historical lecture. When Vera asks about Miss MacIntosh’s eyebrows, she gets another lecture. Personal questions are deflected. Bodily questions are redirected. The space between them is full of things Miss MacIntosh will not say.

Young is not suggesting that Miss MacIntosh is a hypocrite. She is showing you a woman with her own private architecture, her own rooms that stay locked, teaching the best she can from the rooms that are open. Miss MacIntosh believes in honesty and reality. But there are aspects of her reality she is not prepared to share with a child. Not yet. Perhaps not ever.

Solomon the turtle, and what he represents

The turtle named Solomon has lived in the schoolroom for so long that his origins are unknown. Vera wonders if he might be the World Turtle or the mythological figure found in cultures across the globe, the great creature whose back holds the world on its shell. He carries a clay flower pot with a geranium that smells of tobacco, moving with the patience of something that has been alive longer than anyone can remember.

Miss MacIntosh despises him. She associates his slowness with sloth, with the absence of discipline, with everything she is trying to train Vera away from. He is the anti-Miss MacIntosh: ancient, unhurried, indifferent to schedules.

But Young gives Solomon a name and not just any name. Solomon was the wisest king in the Bible, famous for his wealth and his discernment. By naming this slow, contemptible-to-Miss-MacIntosh creature after wisdom itself, Young is gently undermining the idea that speed and discipline are the only forms of intelligence. Solomon the turtle does not hurry. Solomon the turtle does not care about Miss MacIntosh’s watch. Solomon the turtle has been here longer than any of them and will likely outlast them all.

The birthday party that wasn’t

Vera’s fourteenth birthday is one of those passages that Young renders with particular precision, every detail chosen to make you feel both the beauty and the sadness at once.

Mr. Spitzer sends the party dress. It is lovely: filmy, sleeveless, embroidered with seed pearls. He is not there to give it to her in person. No guests come. Miss MacIntosh is present but not celebratory. And Vera, in her beautiful dress, alone in the room, dances with an imaginary partner.

This is Vera at fourteen in miniature: imaginative, yearning, already accustomed to the absence of what she was promised. She creates what isn’t there. She dances with the invisible.

And then she says, to herself or to Miss MacIntosh: I am dancing with Death.

It might be adolescent drama. It might be genuine metaphysical awareness. Miss MacIntosh takes it as the latter because she takes everything seriously, because she is not a woman who dismisses what children say. She sits Vera down and tells her about death plainly: it is real, it comes for everyone, it is not romantic, it is not a partner you choose. You do not invite it in as a companion. You acknowledge it and you keep living.

This is the truest and most direct thing Miss MacIntosh says in the chapter. It is also, in retrospect, haunted. Because she will walk into the sea. Because the dance Vera was imagining is not far away.

What Miss MacIntosh says about marriage

In the darkness after the birthday, Vera asks or thinks about asking what marriage is really like. Miss MacIntosh’s answer is not comforting.

You do not truly know a person until after you have married them. And then it is too late.

This is not cynicism from Miss MacIntosh, it is realism. She is not saying marriage is bad. She is saying that the self people present before marriage is not the entire self, and the discovery of the self comes after the contract is already signed. She is preparing Vera for the truth that the person you marry is, in some essential way, a stranger. And that learning to live with a stranger, with their full, complicated, sometimes disappointing reality, is what marriage actually is.

Vera, at fourteen, wants something else. She wants adventure, passion, a union with someone worth knowing. She wants the story. Miss MacIntosh is trying to give her the facts.

Neither of them is wrong. That tension between the story Vera wants and the facts Miss MacIntosh insists on is the tension that runs through everything.

The guilt Vera carries

The chapter opens and returns to a question Vera cannot resolve: did she cause Miss MacIntosh’s death? Was there something she did, something she said, something she failed to do that set the ending in motion?

Young does not answer this. She will not answer it for many chapters yet. What she does instead is show you the weight of the question, the way it sits in Vera, the way she turns it over, the way she can reconstruct every detail of their life together and still not arrive at a clean cause and effect.

This is what guilt without resolution feels like. Not a verdict. Not even a clear accusation. Just the loop of memory, the same moments replayed, the same question asked again at the end that was asked at the beginning.

Notice that Vera’s guilt does not stop her from loving Miss MacIntosh. The two things exist together: grief and guilt, love and the possibility of responsibility. This is one of Young’s most honest emotional observations.

What to watch for as you keep reading

Light spoilers: This section hints at what happens later in the novel. Skip it if you want to meet the story fresh.

The party dress. Mr. Spitzer sends it, but does not attend. He is always doing this, providing for Vera at a distance, present in her life through gestures and objects but absent from the actual moments. The party dress is a generous and useless gift: beautiful, and belonging to a party that doesn’t exist. Watch how Spitzer operates throughout the novel. His presence is always slightly off.

Miss MacIntosh’s watch. She produces it constantly, checking the time, reminding Vera of the time, insisting that time is moving and there is work to do. It is her instrument of discipline. In a novel where time bends, loops, and dissolves, Miss MacIntosh’s silver dollar watch is the attempt to keep it straight. Notice what happens to time in the chapters where she is absent.

The question about ovaries. Vera asks. Miss MacIntosh redirects. This evasion matters more than it seems. Miss MacIntosh is a woman who believes in truth about everything except certain things about herself and her body. Those things are hidden. The novel will eventually show you why.

“I am dancing with Death.” Hold this phrase. It is not the last time the image of dancing appears in the novel, and it is not the last time death is present in a moment that should be celebratory.

What to hold onto

Chapter 9 is a chapter of preparation for what is coming in the novel, and for what has already come. Vera is learning, under Miss MacIntosh’s strict and loving supervision, to face facts. To hem a handkerchief straight. To know her geography. To understand that marriage is not a fairy tale and that death is not a dance partner.

She does not fully learn any of this. She is fourteen and dreaming of adventure and she dances alone in her beautiful dress with an invisible partner and says she is dancing with Death, and some part of her believes it is romantic.

But Miss MacIntosh is watching. And trying. And the watching and the trying are, in their way, as close to love as this household gets.
