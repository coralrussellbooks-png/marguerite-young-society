Research notes: Chapter 1 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 1

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The narrator Vera Cartwheel is constitutionally multiple and irresolvable. She exists simultaneously as a child in the New England house, a wanderer through harbor cities, a typist typing mortality rates, a searcher on this bus, and none of these selves supersedes the others. She tells us plainly:

"Every heart is the other heart. Every soul is the other soul. Every face is the other face. The individual is the one illusion."

This is not a philosophical position she has arrived at. It is the texture of her experience. She cannot plan a house "if there was one soul which could not live in it": her self is already too porous, too identified with the multiple, to function as a singular agent. The bus journey is itself a figure for this: she is among strangers whose inner lives she partially inhabits, the pregnant girl, the sleeping boy, the whistling driver, and she cannot stop constructing their interiorities even while knowing she is constructing them.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter does not present illusion as a veil over reality. It presents illusion as the medium in which reality occurs. The landscape outside the bus window is described not as a landscape but as the failure of landscape:

"There was now no landscape but the soul's, and that is the inexactitude, the ever shifting and the distant."

The mother's opium house is not contrasted with a real world outside it: the world outside is equally constructed, equally a "conspiracy of memories and dreams floating without purpose, without limitation." Even Miss MacIntosh, the novel's avatar of plain reality and common sense, is revealed to be, from the perspective of years, "almost vague": the very figure of anti-illusion has been consumed by the illusory process of memory. The cave does not have an outside. The chapter knows this.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Grief in this chapter is not an event. It is the atmosphere through which everything is perceived. Vera's mother is "dead among her dreams"; Miss MacIntosh has disappeared or died; the search has lasted "long years"; and the grief does not resolve; it accumulates sedimentarily into the present tense of the bus ride. Note that the chapter moves associatively, not chronologically, because grief does not observe chronology:

"There were always the dead seagulls in the whirlwind, the brown leaves falling, an empty, resonant house of broken mirrors reflecting the light of the sea, my mother dead among her dreams."

The word "always" is the key. This is not a memory being visited. This is the permanent climate. The chapter's syntax enacts this: the sentences do not conclude, they accumulate, clause upon clause, like weather systems that never fully pass.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The extended description of the pregnant girl on the bus is the chapter's purest exercise in the ethics of witnessing. The description runs for nearly two full pages and refuses to simplify her into a type. She is simultaneously pathetic and defiant, gaudy and tragic, resisting sleep "as if they were the powers of oblivion and of death". The narrator does not pity her, does not judge her, does not resolve her into a lesson. She sees the rings, the charm bracelets, the stuffed canary with the moth-eaten wing, the absence of a wedding ring, the "cold and transparent look of being not satisfied, of being not filled with the light which comes from love".

This is attention as ethical practice. The narrator stays in the room. She does not rush toward meaning. The girl is not a symbol. She is a person seen fully, which in this novel is the highest act available.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Vera's entire life as narrated here is recursive. She escapes her mother's house to find Miss MacIntosh; she searches harbor cities; she drifts from employment to employment; she arrives on this bus, and she is still building the same search, returning to the same beginning with what she has learned, which is mostly the knowledge of what she has not found:

"I had drifted from place to place, holding such little jobs as I could concentrate on and yet continue my dreams, beginning to study architecture, then giving it up."

The abandoned study of architecture is a perfect recursive figure; she cannot build a house because the building is always provisional, always incomplete, always unable to contain every soul. She returns to the search. The bus is not a destination. It is another iteration of the recursive loop, another attempt to build toward something she already knows cannot be completed. The chapter itself is structured recursively; it circles back through the same images (the harbor cities, the seagulls, the whaler's hat) each time with slightly more accumulation.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter's sentences are themselves the formal argument for this pillar. They do not end. Consider the description of the girl's clothing:

"...velvet butterflies like pansies hovering upon the moth-eaten fox fur collar of her green cloth coat which would not close and was too tight and did not cover her, coming only to her hips, and was of an old-fashioned style with pointed sleeves and pointed cuffs and scalloped edges and many velvet-covered buttons or marks where the buttons had been, small, tinkling bells moving when, in her sleep, she moved from side to side..."

This sentence does not conclude because the girl cannot be concluded. The demand for a period, for completion, would be a violence against her particularity. The novel's famous length is not excess. It is the formal consequence of this ethical position: the ongoing sentence is the honest one. Vera tells us she typed "mortality rates through a blur of tears": the irony being that mortality rates are the bureaucratic imposition of completion on lives that resist it.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Every figure in this chapter occupies a marginal position. The bus driver with his Jehovah's Witness hair and his whiskey bottle, drinking openly and calling upon God simultaneously, is a figure who cannot be categorized. The pregnant girl without a wedding ring, overdressed in a landscape of grey poverty, wearing the entire field of outdoor sports on her body while being unable to participate in any of them, is the marginal made visible. Vera herself is marginal: the daughter of an opium-dreaming mother, the searcher for a dead nursemaid, the woman who cannot finish architecture school because she is too porous to the excluded.

The marginal figures here are not exceptions to the social order. They are, as the pillar states, evidence: evidence that the categories (wife/not-wife, real/unreal, living/dead) are constructions. The pregnant girl's absent wedding ring is the chapter's most compressed symbol: the category that should organize her situation (married mother) is visibly absent, and what fills that absence is an extravagant over-compensation of other signifiers that cannot substitute for it.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This pillar is perhaps most explicitly enacted in the chapter's temporal structure. The past does not recede in Vera's narration; it presses upward:

"My life had been made up of just these disrelated, delusive images hovering only for a moment at the margin of consciousness, then passing like ships in the night, even ships manned by dead helmsmen, by ghostly crews, by one's own soul at large."

The dead helmsmen are not behind her. They are in the ship she is sailing. Miss MacIntosh's voice ("Common sense is the finest sense", "the way was very plain, quite direct") is not a memory being recalled. It is sediment that is still pressing up, shaping Vera's present journey. The mother's opium dreams are not past events; they are the ground Vera stands on, the thing she is still fleeing and still carrying. The chapter's movement is not linear but geological: layer upon layer of the same material, compressed.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter is acutely aware of its own linguistic situation. Vera's extended meditation on illusion and reality is not delivered from a neutral position; it is delivered from inside a Grey Goose bus in southern Indiana, by a woman whose head is "roaring with the memory of space", who has been shaped by an opium-dreaming mother and a common-sense nursemaid and years of harbor-city wandering. The language knows its own location.

More specifically, the chapter uses language's constructive power against itself. The description of the pregnant girl is so exhaustively particular, "pairs of stuffed red valentine hearts dangling from ribbons and faces which were painted powder puffs", that it enacts the impossibility of language ever fully capturing a person. The list keeps going because no item on the list is sufficient. Language here names its own insufficiency as it proceeds.

Contrast this with Miss MacIntosh's language, always quoted in plain declarative sentences: "nothing had happened", "the way was very plain". Her language claims to speak from nowhere, from common sense itself. The novel's implicit argument is that this is also a location, a "plain, old-fashioned" Middle Western location, and that its claim to neutrality is itself a construction, however beloved.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter lives entirely inside the ordinary and the broken. The blind man at the all-night lunch counter, groping for his coffee cup, asking "When shall the light, Peter, enter my soul again?"; this is the chapter's central religious image, and it is located in a fog-shrouded waterfront diner, not a church. The transcendent question is asked by a man whose eyes "had been withered in their sockets", with a bare light bulb glaring three inches from his face, unable to see it.

The grey bus itself becomes sacred through the quality of attention brought to it:

"the world stretching out to an unseen horizon, the world flat"

The flat, grey, featureless Middle Western landscape, which Miss MacIntosh recommended as the antidote to the opium house, turns out to be not a destination but another version of the sacred ordinary. The bus groans. The mist blurs the windows. The bus driver imitates birdsong. These are not symbols pointing outward to a transcendent beyond. They are the sacred, here, in the immanent, in the grey.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is perhaps the pillar most structurally embedded in the chapter's architecture. Vera is explicitly constituted by what was deposited before she arrived: her mother's opium dreams, Miss MacIntosh's plain ethics, the harbor cities, the dead steersman. She did not choose this constitution. It is structural:

"I had been too long half sleeping, cut off from communication with others... asking no more reasonable questions than a patient asks under the ether mask."

But she is also, on this bus, moving toward something, toward "this far place", toward the interior of America that Miss MacIntosh recommended, toward a life that might constitute ground for something not yet born. The double-headed man by the road, "a tall man with a child perched on his lean shoulders", is the chapter's most compressed image of the Crossing Point: the adult carrying the child, the past carrying the future, the two heads looking in different directions, standing at the edge of a road that "seemed to go back the way it had gone before."

Vera will never know his name. But he is "forever engraved on memory's whirling disc"; he has been deposited into her sediment, and she into his, and neither chose it. The obligation is not chosen. It is structural. The carrying continues.
