Love

Part of To All My Darlings, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated November 12, 2026

Definition from research

Chapter 12 · Love and Human Relationships

  • Miss MacIntosh’s perspective on love is pragmatic and grounded; love is about seeing and hearing another in daylight and tested by harsh experience.
  • She is portrayed as a plain and practical woman, devoid of romantic illusions or flights of fancy.

Chapter 21 · Love as Eternal and Unchanging

  • “That love is love which… does not alter with the changing circumstance” echoes Sonnet 116, as in Chapter 18.
  • Vera emphasizes that true love remains constant despite changing circumstances. Love “does not ebb, does not wane,” symbolizing an ideal of permanence in an impermanent world.
  • This eternal love contrasts with the physical and emotional struggles depicted, underscoring a spiritual or emotional steadfastness.

Chapter 21 · Love and Identity

  • Highlights Vera’s struggle to reconcile ideal love with the imperfect reality of human beings.

Chapter 22

  • “those who fall in love are always blind”: “But love is blind, and lovers cannot see” (The Merchant of Venice 2.6).
  • “her old dollar watch which was given to her by her dead love”: The silver-dollar watch of Chapter 19.

Chapter 31

  • “in love with no man on earth but with a fleeting shadow, the mere shadow of a man”: Shadows in scripture and Shakespeare.

Chapter 31 · Love and Shadows

  • Catherine’s love is portrayed as a love for a shadow, a phantom rather than a living man.

Chapter 38 · Love and Absence

  • Love is portrayed as an unfulfilled, silent, and distant force.
  • Catherine loves an absent, dead man whose love, she believes, was expressed through silence and neglect rather than conventional gestures.
  • Peron’s love is described as “his love greater than his hatred” but unexpressed, a “dead love which never was and never would be real,” paradoxically eternal.
  • Cousin Hannah is described as never having loved, or only loving the “image of the dead,” though she weeps at a snowflake or a rustling skirt.

Chapter 42 · Love and Isolation

  • Despite her militant exterior, Cousin Hannah harbors hidden loves and intimacies. The narrative hints at secret lovers, lost opportunities for human connection, and the loneliness of a life lived in opposition to societal norms.

Chapter 43 · Love and Loss

  • The suffragette expresses deep, eternal love in her delirium, though the identity of lover and beloved is unclear.
  • Love and death blur together, raising the question of whether death extinguishes love or transforms it. Her last whispered words ask, “should love be extinguished by death?”
  • The paradox of love’s endurance beyond death is a poignant motif.

Chapter 44 · Love and Loss

  • Despite being a fierce fighter for women’s rights, Cousin Hannah is shown to be deeply haunted by a lost love, which shapes much of her life and final moments.
  • Her dying cries for her “love,” “little snowdrop,” and her search for a “skirt” symbolize a yearning for something lost, possibly innocence, identity, or a personal connection.
  • The narrative suggests that her suffrage activism was motivated by personal heartbreak, intertwining public struggle with private grief.

Chapter 45 · Love and Its Ambivalence

  • Love is portrayed as an unattainable ideal.
  • Cousin Hannah’s love life is marked by loss, guilt, and a yearning for a final answer that never comes.
  • Cousin Hannah’s lamentations at her deathbed emphasize her betrayal of love (whether by herself or by fate) and her eternal search for her lost love.

Chapter 46 · Love Beyond Mortality

  • Hannah’s message is that love transcends mortal bounds, aiming for an immortal love. This idea recurs as Mr. Spitzer contemplates the power and persistence of love even after death, and the paradox that those who have loved have also died, and vice versa.

Chapter 47 · Love and Death as Intertwined Realities

  • Love is portrayed as transitory and dying, yet death is equated with a dark lover who embraces and consumes the beloved.
  • The idea that death is not an end but a transformation or continuation, possibly into another life or state.

Chapter 48

  • “an arrowsmith to make that arrow which should reach this heart”: Cupid carries two arrows, one tipped with gold to kindle love and one with lead to put it to flight (Ovid, Metamorphoses 1).
  • “all these little deaths which were like love”: La petite mort, “the little death,” is the French phrase for the swoon of love.
  • “Perhaps there was only the bridge”: “There is a land of the living and a land of the dead and the bridge is love, the only survival, the only meaning” (Thornton Wilder, The Bridge of San Luis Rey, 1927). In Zoroastrian belief the dead cross the Chinvat Bridge, wide for the righteous and narrow as a blade for the wicked.

Chapter 52 · Love and Loss

  • Central to the narrative is the motif of lost love, symbolized by the suffragette’s lost skirt and her search for love that may never be found or may be forever elusive. Love is portrayed as inseparable from death, an eternal quest that transcends time.

Chapter 64

  • “Was it a woman or a man?”: Vera asks without assuming, and Miss MacIntosh answers that there was no man, only “a beautiful dancing girl.” Her one love was a woman.

Chapter 64 · Love and Loss

  • Miss MacIntosh’s reflections on Lorena and her own life reveal deep emotional wounds.
  • The absence of romantic fulfillment and the loss of connections highlight themes of regret and yearning.

Chapter 65

  • “Love is blind”: The saying, meaning that love overlooks faults, is ancient; Plato wrote that the lover is blind about the beloved (Laws 731e). In English, Chaucer has “For love is blynd alday, and may nat see” (“The Merchant’s Tale”), and Shakespeare’s Jessica says, “But love is blind, and lovers cannot see / The pretty follies that themselves commit” (The Merchant of Venice, 2.6).

Chapter 76

  • “by Cupid, the blind god of love”: In classical mythology, Cupid is the god of desire, erotic love, attraction and affection. “And therefore is wing’d Cupid painted blind” (A Midsummer Night’s Dream, 1.1).

Chapter 82

  • “Man loves a silent woman”: In Ben Jonson’s comedy Epicoene, or The Silent Woman (1609), Morose, who cannot bear noise, marries a bride for her silence; once wed, she talks without stopping.

Chapter 82 · Love Beyond Death

  • Mr. Spitzer’s confession of being Peron and his love for Catherine after her death touches on eternal love transcending mortality.
