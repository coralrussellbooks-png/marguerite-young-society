Arc 2: The Birthday Night and Its Aftermath (Chapters 9–24)

Part of Story Arcs, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← The Bus Ride · Story Arcs Home · Mr. Spitzer →

Spoiler note: This page covers Chapters 9 to 24. If you are reading along, read it after you finish Chapter 24.

Overview

Vera returns to her childhood under Miss MacIntosh: the lessons, the empty fourteenth birthday party, the dream wedding. On the birthday night she wanders the gardens and the shore, rides Falada into the house, and finds Miss MacIntosh bald and changed. A long night and a silent pact follow, then the breakfast-table days in which nothing may be said, and Vera's survey of a household with no one to guide her.

Central figures: Vera Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Joachim Spitzer · Catherine Cartwheel · Peron Spitzer · Cousin Hannah

Chapter by chapter

Chapter 9

Vera asks whether she caused Miss MacIntosh's death simply by opening her eyes. She recalls their life together: drills in geography and history, the Chicago fire and the glaciers, embroidery hoops and hemmed handkerchiefs, Solomon the turtle dragging a geranium pot across the schoolroom, and the silver dollar watch Miss MacIntosh used to remind her that time was passing. Then comes her fourteenth birthday. Mr. Spitzer sends a party dress, but no guests come. Vera dances alone and declares she is dancing with Death, and Miss MacIntosh sternly corrects her. That night Vera lies awake wondering about the man she might marry and whether a true marriage could ever be dissolved.

Chapter 10

Unable to picture her own wedding, Vera imagines other weddings and drifts into a dream of one. Mr. Spitzer is the veiled bride and Miss MacIntosh the bridegroom. The preacher's marriage vows slide into a funeral service before he vanishes. Vera wakes troubled rather than amused. She realizes she has been thinking unkindly of Miss MacIntosh as colorless and unwanted, and she feels pity for a woman who gave her life to duty. She is hungry and wants to go to her, but she has been warned not to wander the house or the shore at night.

Chapter 11

In this very short chapter, Vera wonders how she will endure her isolation. She cannot live like her mother, and she trusts herself to know where dream ends and reality begins, as Miss MacIntosh taught her. Meanwhile Catherine works through her alphabet of phantom guests, the P's, Q's, and R's: Pericles, Admiral Peary, Penelope's web, the Pleiades, and Sir Walter Raleigh, who removes his head along with his plumed hat. Mr. Spitzer, as always, has missed it all.

Chapter 12

It is after ten at night, and Vera lies beside the Big Ben alarm clock, the one reliable clock in a house of stopped clocks. Restless, she slips out into a garden her grandfather planted for his blind wife, made to be touched and smelled rather than seen. She moves through it with closed eyes in near ecstasy and meditates on Miss MacIntosh and all she taught. The chapter closes on Catherine's suspicion that Mr. Spitzer sometimes imagines himself to be his dead brother, betting on phantom horses he could know nothing about.

Chapter 13

Vera announces in advance that on this night of her fourteenth birthday she will lose her last certainty. Miss MacIntosh will become a stranger in her eyes, though she does not yet say how. She wanders the garden of the deaf, where life-sized chessmen move on a board of light and shadow among statues and gargoyles. The chapter then turns to Catherine and Mr. Spitzer. Catherine insists that the ear and the heart are dead, that sound has fled the universe, and that this is why his music is silence.

Chapter 14

Vera comes to the grave of the black coachman, who once drove her grandfather's carriage with four white horses. Catherine is obsessed with that grave and its location. She questions Mr. Spitzer endlessly about the coachman's burial and the fate of the horses. Beneath this obsession, the story of the Spitzer twins comes out. Peron fell from a tall building, probably by suicide. Joachim has lived in the aftermath ever since, wearing his brother's cufflinks and being mistaken for him in the street. Catherine goes on talking long after Mr. Spitzer has left, asking about the grave of the other Mr. Spitzer, the one she loved, until dawn puts her to sleep.

Chapter 15

Still on her birthday night, Vera stands by a roaring sea under a skull-white moon. Exhilarated, she runs into the surf. For the first time she looks at Miss MacIntosh critically: what she does not know of undertow and the deeper things, her umbrella carried indoors, her windows thrown open in winter, her bare cold bedroom, the day's long lecture of proverbs about death, marriage, and industry. The sea briefly becomes a vision of the end of time and then goes dark again. Far off, the old blind sheep dog barks at the waves.

Chapter 16

Vera rides her old black pony, Falada, through the dark grounds, laughing and whispering secrets into his ear. She decides not to bring a real horse into her mother's dream-filled bedroom. Instead she rides him up the stairs of the great house, past mosaic emperors, a funeral gondola, and tapestries of angels healing the blind and the deaf. They reach Miss MacIntosh's door, always barred from inside, and it swings open. In the moonlight Miss MacIntosh stands by the window, greatly changed, unbelievably not herself, and yet Vera recognizes her instantly.

Chapter 17

Miss MacIntosh is bald, her skull gleaming like an ivory ball in the moonlight. Vera, shocked and half triumphant, cries out that she is a man. A struggle follows, real or partly imagined. Vera is knocked down, lifted, and laid on the bed, and drifts in and out of consciousness, wondering whether she is wrestling a dummy, a gladiator, or the angel Jacob met. In the morning her braids are neatly plaited, and the only scratches on her cheeks could have come from leafless branches.

Chapter 18

Through the rest of the night, Miss MacIntosh holds Vera and talks. She speaks in two voices: the familiar one of proverbs and common sense, and a raw, grieving one that cries about the breast she lost and the hair that was never hers. She insists nothing happened. She scolds Vera for entering without knocking and threatens to kill her if she tells. By dawn she is quizzing her on geography again. As Vera falls asleep, Miss MacIntosh whispers that she lost her hair in Vera's service, that she once had a pale gold mane, and that she is not as old as Vera thinks.

Chapter 19

Vera wakes at dawn in Miss MacIntosh's bed, half hoping the night was a dream. Before opening her eyes she reconsiders details she never questioned: the baseball cap, the evening ribbons, the veils over the whaler's hat, the umbrella indoors. Then she looks. The bald head lies on the pillow. The red wig hangs on the hat rack. Around the bare room are false teeth in a glass, a Bible chained to the table, a deuce of clubs, cards printed with Dutch windmills, and seaweed hanging on the barred door. Then she sees that one breast has been amputated.

Chapter 20

In full dawn Vera studies the sleeping woman, thinking about God, about hair, and about what the night revealed. She makes her decision: she will act as though she never saw. She will iron the shirts more eagerly, learn the genealogies and ancient measures, and keep the secret. She will protect Miss MacIntosh from the knowledge of herself. The chapter ends in a litany of biblical images for God.

Chapter 21

Vera takes a last long look at Miss MacIntosh asleep: the scar, the missing eyebrows and lashes, a green ribbon looped over a bust of Martin Luther. She falls back asleep. When she wakes, Miss MacIntosh stands fully dressed, her red wig shining, in unusually good humor, and the room is tidy as if nothing happened. She says Vera has been sick and scolds her for dancing without a partner. She holds up the birthday dress Mr. Spitzer sent, declares it cannot be mended, and sends it to the ash bin.

Chapter 22

At an unusually festive breakfast of fine china and white roses, Miss MacIntosh reads the newspaper aloud: rebellions, Wall Street, storms, strikes, the price of eggs. She fires questions at Vera, complains about dust and unmatched socks, and talks about shipwrecks off the coast. Vera is irritated by her cheerfulness, asks twice about the ruined dress, and is deflected. Almost in passing, Miss MacIntosh remarks that baldness is no cure for sorrow. Vera begins to wonder whether her nursemaid is as dreamlike as everyone else.

Chapter 23

The breakfast continues. Miss MacIntosh denounces vain man, the Emperor Constantine, and Caesar, declares that history lies, and professes her faith in common sense and the middle way. Then she settles on Benjamin Franklin, whose experiments with lightning led to electric light and the end of privacy. Darkness, she argues, once protected marriage and the poor and the ugly. Vera realizes what the tirade is for: a woman who was just seen without her wig is arguing that no one should have to be seen. The chapter ends with Miss MacIntosh quoting Job and crying that the stars have always lit the heavens.

Chapter 24

Vera's thoughts move through the household. The servants have absorbed Catherine's dreams so completely that they argue whether it is Roman in the morning and Greek in the afternoon, and assign rooms to famous phantom guests. Catherine plans her own funeral, calls Athens on a telephone wired only to the servants' quarters, and tries to reach Cousin Hannah on a number long disconnected. Mr. Spitzer will come again in the evening with his vague certainties about heaven. Vera asks herself to whom she can turn with her questions about love, marriage, birth, and death, and finds no one.
