Arc 2: The Birthday Night and Its Aftermath (Chapters 9–24)

Part of Story Arcs, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated November 10, 2026

← The Bus Ride · Story Arcs Home · Mr. Spitzer →

Spoiler note: This page covers Chapters 9 to 24. If you are reading along, read it after you finish Chapter 24.

Overview

Vera returns to her childhood under Miss MacIntosh: the lessons, the empty fourteenth birthday party, the dream wedding. On the birthday night she wanders the gardens and the shore, rides Falada into the house, and finds Miss MacIntosh bald and changed. A long night and a silent pact follow, then the breakfast-table days in which nothing may be said, and Vera's survey of a household with no one to guide her.

Central figures: Vera Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Joachim Spitzer · Catherine Cartwheel · Peron Spitzer · Cousin Hannah

Chapter by chapter

Chapter 9

Vera asks whether she caused Miss MacIntosh's death simply by opening her eyes. She recalls their life together: drills in geography and history, the Chicago fire and the glaciers, embroidery hoops and hemmed handkerchiefs, Solomon the turtle dragging a geranium pot across the schoolroom, and the silver dollar watch Miss MacIntosh used to remind her that time was passing. Then comes her fourteenth birthday. Mr. Spitzer sends a party dress, but no guests come. Vera dances alone and declares she is dancing with Death, and Miss MacIntosh sternly corrects her. That night Vera lies awake wondering about the man she might marry and whether a true marriage could ever be dissolved.

Chapter 10

Unable to picture her own wedding, Vera imagines other weddings and drifts into a dream of one. Mr. Spitzer is the veiled bride and Miss MacIntosh the bridegroom. The preacher’s marriage vows slide into a funeral service before he vanishes. Vera wakes troubled rather than amused. She realizes she has been thinking unkindly of Miss MacIntosh as colorless and unwanted, and she feels pity for a woman who gave her life to duty. She is hungry and wants to go to her, but she has been warned not to wander the house or the shore at night.

Chapter 11

In this short chapter, Vera wonders how she will bear her isolation. She cannot live on illusion as her mother does, and she trusts herself to know where the dream ends and reality begins, as Miss MacIntosh taught her. She imagines how far her mother has traveled through her opium dream, which runs by the alphabet. The P’s must be past by now. The Q’s are hard company: the Queen Charlotte Islands, and the Queen Maud Mountains, who visits as a frigid, silent house guest in a lace skirt five hundred miles long. The R’s are the charming ones, guests who never met in life: Mr. Res Tacamah, the drug bottle; a pharaoh who unwraps into Rameses I, II, and III; Roland, Rimski-Korsakov, Sir Joshua Reynolds, Rhadamanthus, and Sir Walter Raleigh, who takes off his head with his plumed hat. Mr. Spitzer has missed them all, and Catherine will tell him so.

Chapter 12

It is past ten at night. Vera lies awake beside the Big Ben alarm clock, the one reliable clock in a house of clocks stopped one by one as Catherine withdrew to her bed. Wanting human company, she sets out for Miss MacIntosh’s bedroom. On the way she crosses the garden of the blind, planted by her grandfather for his blind wife to be touched and smelled rather than seen, and she feels a joy she will not know again: this first clear vision of the world will also be her last. She looks back on the child she was at seven, who bayed at the moon, heard the dead through a wireless in her head, and believed she was Chopin reborn, and on Miss MacIntosh, who brought her to her senses with a plain creed of daylight and duty. There were signs Vera did not read: Miss MacIntosh’s short breath, her blue lips, her threats to retire. The chapter closes on Catherine’s suspicion that Mr. Spitzer sometimes imagines himself to be his dead brother, betting on dark horses.

Chapter 13

On the night of her fourteenth birthday, Vera says farewell in advance to her last certainty. After this night Miss MacIntosh, the one person who never seemed to change, will be a stranger Vera has lived with for seven years. Vera compares herself to the rider who crossed frozen Lake Constance in the dark and dropped dead when he learned where he had been. She bids farewell to the old moralities, the rote questions and answers, and What Cheer, Iowa, all of which now seem a lie. She wanders through her mother’s imagined gardens, where Galen, Harvey, Goldsmith, and Ben Jonson stroll at a perpetual garden party and life-sized chessmen move on a board of light and fog, until she reaches the garden of the deaf. There, as Catherine would tell Mr. Spitzer, the ear is dead. Catherine insists the dead ear is his, though he hears a roaring in his right ear that does not come from this world, and that this is why his music is silence.

Chapter 14

Vera comes to the grave of the black coachman, who drove her grandfather’s carriage and four white horses and outlived them all. Catherine, who never visited the grave, cannot stop asking Mr. Spitzer about it: whether the coachman was buried, as she ordered, with the carriage and horses so that he could go on driving her in death, and in his livery. She insists the arrangement was his idea, though he had no wages for years and she cannot remember his name. Her questions turn on Mr. Spitzer himself. His twin Peron, the quick, brilliant gambler, fell from a tall building, and Joachim lives on in his brother’s shadow, finding his cigarette still burning in a coat pocket and an ace dropping from his sleeve, and being taken for him in the street. Catherine counts four Mr. Spitzers, mocks his train journeys, and accuses him of losing the coachman. She talks on after he has gone and, at dawn, asks for the grave of “the slow one, the sick musician she had always loved,” reversing which twin she lost.

Chapter 15

Still on the night of her fourteenth birthday, Vera stands by a roaring sea under a moon like a white skull and runs laughing into the surf. For the first time she judges Miss MacIntosh: her proverbs, her umbrella carried indoors, her windows thrown open in winter, her bare, cold bedroom, and above all that she “could not swim a stroke” and would be dragged down by the first great wave. Vera recalls the day’s long sermon of sayings, Death as “the bridegroom,” marriage as a lottery, the warnings to keep close to the house, and laughs at her dream of a false wedding. The sea flares into colors like the end of time, then rolls back into blackness. Once the tide carried the marble Saul out to sea and he never returned. Far off, the old blind sheep dog barks at the waves.

Chapter 16

On the night of her fourteenth birthday, Vera rides her old black pony, Falada, bareback through the moonlit grounds, laughing, imagining herself a circus rider in pink tights, and whispering her secrets into his ear: that she is an orphan, that Miss MacIntosh is “an old witch who kept me enchained in a dark tower.” Mr. Spitzer gave her the pony, which had been sent to him by an admirer of his dead brother, and he named its pedigree: the horse that inspired merry-go-rounds and Queen Anne chair legs, and the speaking head of an old fairy tale. Vera rides Falada into the house, past the doge’s lions, the funeral gondola, and the tapestries of angels healing the blind and the deaf. She does not dare enter her mother’s room, for fear Catherine would know they were real. She rides on to Miss MacIntosh, who would. The door, always barred from inside, swings open. In the moonlight Miss MacIntosh stands by the window, “unbelievably not herself,” and yet Vera knows her at once.

Chapter 17

In the moonlight Miss MacIntosh stands by the window, and Vera sees that she is bald, “bald as the egg or as the rock where nothing grows,” with “only one breast.” Vera knows her only by her broken nose. Terrified and wildly triumphant at having always pictured her with a mustache and cast her as the bridegroom, Vera screams, “Miss MacIntosh, you are a man!” A struggle follows, told in the language of the boxing ring. Vera is knocked down, her mouth covered by “his hoary hand,” and laid on the bed, and she wonders whether her attacker is a fallen angel, a gladiator, the sandbag dummy come to life, or the angel Jacob wrestled. She calls it the work of someone “who should take advantage of a poor, ignorant girl, only fourteen years old.” She calls for her mother and for Mr. Spitzer, but no one comes. In the morning her braids are exactly braided, and the only scratches on her cheeks could have come from leafless branches.

Chapter 18

Through the rest of the night, Miss MacIntosh holds Vera and talks, in two voices: the familiar one of proverbs and geography lessons, and a raw, grieving one that cries for the left breast “already gone,” for her hair, and for “one kiss,” all she ever asked. A red wig hangs on the chair post. She insists nothing happened, that it was all a dream. She covers Vera’s head with a pillow, leaves “the heavy, claw-shaped mark of her hand” upon her forehead “to teach you… charity,” and swears she will kill Vera if she ever tells. By dawn she is quizzing her on Michigan’s industries and the Battle of Shiloh, and promising that all will be as it was. As Vera falls asleep, Miss MacIntosh whispers that she lost her hair in Vera’s service, that she once had a pale gold mane, and that she is not as old as Vera thinks. Vera carries the secret as her sin, and ever after tries to be charitable.

Chapter 19

“That was the false dawn, surely.” Vera wakes in Miss MacIntosh’s bed, sure that the night was a dream for which no one was responsible. Before she opens her eyes, she goes over things she never thought to question: the missing eyebrows, the face that changes color, the baseball cap in the mornings, the green ribbon in the evenings, the veils over the whaler’s hat, the black umbrella carried even indoors. Her mother calls herself “the horizontal person.” Miss MacIntosh is “perpendicular.” Then Vera looks. The bald head lies on the pillow next to hers, “cold as marble,” and the red wig is perched on the hat rack “like a rare tropical bird.” The room is bare and broken: a bust of Martin Luther, cherubim with no wings, a Bible chained to a table, false teeth in a glass, the deuce of clubs turned up, seaweed hanging on the barred door, the umbrella open on the floor. Then Vera observes “what I must surely have always known.” One breast has been amputated, and “what was left was no bigger than a baby’s.”

Chapter 20

In the full light of dawn, Vera studies the sleeping woman. It is “the light which had already killed the heart of Miss MacIntosh,” stripping her “bare as roses of their petals”: no “crowning glory of woman’s hair,” no single hair “to suggest a possibility of future growth,” and the “narrow and intolerable vacancy” where the other breast should be. Vera wonders who robbed her of her hair, and when, whether hair by hair or all at once, whether a broken heart caused it, whether she was “deserted at the altar by her bridegroom or her bride.” Then she makes her resolve. She will go on “as if I had never seen her,” iron the shirts with new alacrity, learn the Hebrew measures, coins, months, and the stations of the Exodus, and “protect her from the knowledge of herself.” The old routine is already “the elegiac past,” and she will “hold to every moment as if I lived with a dying man.” The chapter ends as Vera asks, “Was God bald, too,” in a litany of the Bible’s images for God.

Chapter 21

Vera promises herself that tomorrow she will look only at the red hair “like shining armor” and go on as before: “Daylight should cover us.” Her “good intention” is to deceive Miss MacIntosh into believing herself “but a usual being in the light.” Yet she remembers Miss MacIntosh’s own warnings, that the road to Hell “is paved with bones and good intentions,” that the Devil “does not always wear horns” and can quote Scripture. Pity wakes in her: “It was only the beginning of pity. It was not the end.” Vera takes a last long look at the sleeping body, the scar, the stalk of the cut-away breast, the wad of cloth in the brassiere, the green ribbon looped over the bust of Martin Luther. Then she falls asleep. When she wakes, Miss MacIntosh stands at the foot of the bed, fully dressed, her red hair shining, in remarkably good humor. She says Vera has been very sick, scolds her for dancing at “your first birthday party, even though no guest came,” and holds up the party dress Mr. Spitzer gave her. Vera can see no tear in it. Miss MacIntosh declares it cannot be mended: “Into the ash bin it goes.”

Chapter 22

At a breakfast table laid, for no reason anyone names, with Haviland china, silver, and a bowl of white roses, Vera sits scratched, bruised, and aching, “seethingly irritated” that Miss MacIntosh never asks how she passed the night. Miss MacIntosh reads the water-stained newspaper aloud, a Brazilian rebellion, a needle-workers’ strike, gold to be drained from the sea, a famine in China, a new star, and steers the household like a ship (“All hands to the ropes!”). She scolds Vera about dust and unmatched socks and fires the old questions at her: the days of creation, which she counts from Sunday because Constantine “got us all off on the wrong day of the week,” and the world’s islands, mountains, boxers, and stars. Twice Vera asks whether the party dress was thrown away as a punishment, and twice she is answered with more questions. Miss MacIntosh scoffs at phantoms (“Who ever saw the ghost of his father?”), says she has not looked into a mirror in years, and mutters, almost asleep, “Baldness is no cure for sorrow, my dear.” Vera thinks of the two Mr. Spitzers, of the wreckage the sea casts up, and of her mother, who mistakes everything she sees for something else. “Was she all others? I wondered.”

Chapter 23

The breakfast continues. Miss MacIntosh denounces vain man, the Emperor Constantine, and Caesar, declares that history lies, and professes her faith in common sense and the middle way. Then she settles on Benjamin Franklin, whose experiments with lightning led to electric light and the end of privacy. Darkness, she argues, once protected marriage and the poor and the ugly. Vera realizes what the tirade is for: a woman who was just seen without her wig is arguing that no one should have to be seen. She talks of igloos and ice floes, of winter’s blast, of anything but herself. The chapter ends with Miss MacIntosh quoting Job and crying that the stars have always lit the heavens.

Chapter 24

Vera’s thoughts move through the household. The servants have absorbed Catherine’s dreams so completely that they argue whether it is Roman in the morning and Greek in the afternoon, and assign rooms to famous phantom guests. Catherine plans her own funeral, calls Athens on a telephone wired only to the servants’ quarters, and tries to reach Cousin Hannah on a number long disconnected. Mr. Spitzer will come again in the evening with his vague certainties about heaven. The servants see the black coachman and the white horses, an old valet waits on Wellington, and a gardener believes he is Potiphar’s wife. Mr. Spitzer, in Catherine’s eyes, is a roundhouse for trains that run no more, a ghost ship with the captain’s table set for two. Vera asks herself to whom she can turn with her questions about love, marriage, birth, and death, and finds no one.
