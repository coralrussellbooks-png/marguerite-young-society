Unit 13, pp. 273-304: The Brotherhood and the lecture circuit

← Unit 12 · Harp Song for a Radical: A History Companion · Unit 14 →

Background for this unit: The History in Harp Song for a Radical

Debs clerks for a German grocer, joins the firemen's brotherhood, and begins filling its magazine with notices of men crushed under wheels. At the same time he brings the great orators of the age to Terre Haute: Robert Ingersoll, Wendell Phillips, and Susan B. Anthony. By 1884 he has been city clerk twice and is running for the state legislature.

Major entries

The Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen (pp. 277-283)

  • What it was: A union founded on December 1, 1873, at Port Jervis, New York, by Joshua Leach and ten other firemen of the Erie Railroad. Young tells how Leach, born near Nenagh in Tipperary, founded it after watching a fellow fireman die and helped the man's widow (pp. 277-278). It began as a mutual benefit society, paying death and disability benefits no insurer would offer.

  • Debs's role: Leach organized Vigo Lodge No. 16 in Terre Haute in February 1875, with Debs as a charter member and its secretary. Debs became associate editor of the Locomotive Firemen's Magazine in 1878 and in 1880 its editor and the brotherhood's grand secretary and treasurer.

  • The magazine: Young quotes its lodge notices of brothers killed, such as Grant Hall of the Seymour lodge, who fell between platform and train at Shoals (p. 279), and its reports of track sinking into coal mines (p. 283). These obituaries are among the best records of how dangerous railroad work was.

  • Where the record differs: Young dates the organization to 1863 (p. 278). That is the founding year of the Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers. The firemen organized in 1873.

  • Further reading: Salvatore, Eugene V. Debs (1982), chapters 2 and 3.

Robert Green Ingersoll (pp. 284-289)

  • Who he was: The son of a Congregational minister (1833-1899), a Union cavalry colonel, and attorney general of Illinois (1867-1869). He became the most famous orator in America and the "Great Agnostic," drawing huge paying crowds to hear him attack the Bible.

  • Why it matters: His lecture "Some Mistakes of Moses" (1879), which Young notes some magazine readers took for "The Mistakes of Mosaics" (p. 284), made freethought popular entertainment. He was also a Republican power, famous for his 1876 speech nominating James G. Blaine as "the Plumed Knight." Debs brought him to Terre Haute and they became friends despite Ingersoll's defense of the gold standard (p. 289).

  • Where the record differs: Young calls Ingersoll a "Republican Speaker of the House" (p. 287). He never served in Congress. He was a Republican stump speaker.

  • Further reading: Susan Jacoby, The Great Agnostic: Robert Ingersoll and American Freethought (2013).

Wendell Phillips in Terre Haute (pp. 290-292)

  • What happened: Debs brought Phillips to speak to the Occidental Literary Club, and the old abolitionist became a lasting influence. Phillips's heroes, Elijah Lovejoy, John Brown, and Toussaint Louverture (p. 292), became Debs's heroes.

  • Why it matters: After the war Phillips argued that wage labor was the next slavery to abolish. He ran for governor of Massachusetts in 1870 on a Labor Reform ticket. He is the clearest bridge between abolition and the labor movement, and Debs crossed it.

  • Faneuil Hall, 1837: Phillips's career began with an unplanned speech at Boston's Faneuil Hall on December 8, 1837, answering a speaker who had defended the mob that killed Lovejoy in Alton, Illinois.

Susan B. Anthony in Terre Haute (pp. 293-299)

  • Who she was: Born a Quaker in Adams, Massachusetts, in 1820, raised in Battenville, New York, and based in Rochester (p. 297). With Elizabeth Cady Stanton she led the National Woman Suffrage Association from 1869.

  • What happened: When she came to lecture in Terre Haute, many townspeople shunned her. Debs welcomed her publicly and escorted her. Newspapers mocked her everywhere, and Young quotes the scorn of Ambrose Bierce in the San Francisco News Letter (p. 295).

  • Why it matters: Debs supported woman suffrage from the start, well before most labor leaders. His magazine gave a woman editor her own department.

  • Further reading: Ann D. Gordon, ed., The Selected Papers of Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Susan B. Anthony (1997-2013).

Debs in local politics (pp. 300-304)

  • What happened: Debs was elected Terre Haute city clerk as a Democrat in 1879 and reelected in 1881. In 1884 he won a seat in the Indiana legislature (p. 303), where he served one term and pushed railroad safety and employer liability bills with little success.

  • Why it matters: His disappointment in the statehouse, where railroad lobbies ruled, turned him away from ordinary party politics.

  • The wider scene: Young notes that 1879 was the year of Henry George's Progress and Poverty (p. 300). George argued that land speculation caused poverty and proposed a single tax on land values. It sold millions of copies and inspired reformers worldwide. She also recalls the assassination of President James Garfield in July 1881 by Charles Guiteau, a former member of the Oneida Community (p. 300).

  • Further reading: Edward T. O'Donnell, Henry George and the Crisis of Inequality (2015).

Shorter entries

  • Herman Hulman (p. 273): A German-born Terre Haute wholesale grocer. His firm, with partner Robert S. Cox, employed Debs as a clerk from 1874. It later made Clabber Girl baking powder, and his descendant Tony Hulman bought the Indianapolis Motor Speedway in 1945.

  • Debs's reading (pp. 274-275): Young lists the books of his youth: Hugo, Sue, Dickens, Stowe, Gogol, Poe, Burns, Shelley, and Appleton's Encyclopedia, which he bought on installments.

  • The Occidental Literary Club (pp. 283-294): The Terre Haute debating club where Debs gave his early speeches and booked lecturers.

  • The Redpath Lecture Bureau (pp. 286-287): Founded in Boston in 1868 by James Redpath, a Scottish-born abolitionist journalist and biographer of John Brown. It booked Ingersoll, Twain, Phillips, and others.

  • Daniel Voorhees and the Opera House (p. 288): Terre Haute's senator introduced Ingersoll at the local Opera House.

  • Gold bugs and silver bugs (p. 289): The long quarrel over money. Gold bugs backed a gold standard. Silver bugs wanted free coinage of silver to expand the money supply and help debtors, the cause Bryan carried in 1896.

  • Myron Reed (pp. 290-291): A minister (1836-1899) in Indianapolis and later Denver, and a Christian socialist who became Debs's close friend. Young's claim that he was once a Grand Banks cod fisherman is unverified.

  • "Go West, young man" (p. 291): Young credits the slogan to John B. L. Soule in the Terre Haute Express in 1851. The story is a popular legend. No one has found the line in that paper, and it is usually linked to Horace Greeley.

  • Ida Husted Harper (pp. 293-294): Terre Haute journalist (1851-1931) who wrote the Woman's Department of the firemen's magazine. She later became Susan B. Anthony's authorized biographer.

  • Taylorsville (p. 301): Terre Haute's red-light district, which Young calls its "Tenderloin" and "Babylon."

  • "Jim Goff" (p. 302): The Irish work song "Drill, Ye Tarriers, Drill," in which a blasted driller is docked pay "for the time you were up in the sky."

Allusions

  • Oliver Wendell Holmes, "The Chambered Nautilus" (p. 275): The 1858 poem on the soul's growth, a favorite of Debs.

  • The Styx (p. 277): The river of the dead in Greek myth.

  • Pickwickian (p. 281): From Dickens's Pickwick Papers, genial and comic.

  • Galahad and the Holy Grail (p. 291): The pure knight of Arthurian legend.

  • The Mermaid Tavern (p. 290): The London tavern of Shakespeare and Ben Jonson, for a company of great talkers.

  • Vesuvius and Pompeii (p. 304): The volcano and the buried city.
