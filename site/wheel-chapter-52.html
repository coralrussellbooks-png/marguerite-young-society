Research notes: Chapter 52 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 52

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

"He was neither husband nor wife. He knew not which brother he was, whether the begotten or the unbegotten. Or perhaps they were one."

Mr. Spitzer is constitutionally multiple. He is simultaneously the lawyer and the mourner, the living and the dead, himself and his dead twin brother. The text never resolves which one is speaking or acting at any given moment. He is described as:

  • "the pawnbroker who had pawned himself away, bone by bone"
  • the "locksmith in a street of dreams where there was neither key nor lock"
  • the "doctor who had lost his patient, even himself"
  • "the architect who had designed the building which had been torn down before it

was built"

Each of these is not a metaphor for Mr. Spitzer; each is a facet of him, a simultaneous identity that cannot be collapsed into a single coherent self. The novel explicitly refuses synthesis: "he could not condemn her unless he condemned himself, one already dead in the midst of life". The self here is not fragmented in the sense of being broken; it is plural in the sense of being structurally irreducible.

The suffragette herself mirrors this. She is described as potentially "the husband and not the bride", as both "the climber and the climbed", as the Black Knight and the white witch simultaneously. Her gender, her role, her very ontological status refuses singularity. The chapter's repeated grammatical construction, "some said... and some said... and some said", is itself a formal enactment of the plural self: there is no authoritative account, only simultaneous, irresolvable versions.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

"Perhaps the unreal and the real were one — or there was only this mask of the stars, and there was no face behind the stars."

This is the chapter's most explicit philosophical statement, and it arrives almost casually, buried in a long subordinate clause. The novel does not present illusion as a problem to be overcome but as the constitutive condition of all perception and experience.

The suffragette's entire history is filtered through "dreaming newspaper reporters", through "blurred newspaper reports", through Mr. Spitzer's own admittedly unreliable reconstruction:

"even like a traveller reconstructing in his imagination a land he had never visited? For if he had gone, he would not have returned."

The cave metaphor of Pillar 2 is literalized throughout: Mr. Spitzer moves through rooms that multiply as he walks, through houses that "might have disappeared", through mirrors that return no image. The external world is confirmed as real only in the moment of its departure: "Yes, there was an external world, he knew now as it departed". Reality is only legible as it dissolves.

The wedding gowns found in the trunks (forty gowns, no bride) are perhaps the chapter's purest image of this pillar: the costume of a role, the structure of an identity, with no body inside to fill it. The cave is larger here, more carefully lit, but it remains a cave.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

"Mystery was piled over mystery as cloud over cloud, turret over turret."

The chapter's opening sentence establishes grief not as event but as climate. The clouds do not pass. They accumulate. This is the novel's dominant meteorological and psychological logic.

Mr. Spitzer's grief for the suffragette (a woman who was never his lover, never his family in any conventional sense) is grief in its purest, most immanent form: grief for what was never possessed, which means grief that can never be resolved through the consolation of having-had. He mourns:

"that which he remembered not"

"a land he had never visited"

"cities which never would be visited, doors where he would never knock, voices he would never hear, journeys he would never make"

This is grief as the permanent condition of consciousness itself, not the grief of loss but the grief of constitutive absence, the grief of the possible that was never actualized. The suffragette's own grief mirrors this: she spent her life searching for a love who went over a cliff chasing a skirt, a love she never fully possessed, in a relationship never fully named. The novel refuses to resolve this grief into meaning or acceptance. It simply continues, piling cloud on cloud.

The weather metaphor is not incidental. Snow falls throughout the chapter, through holes in roofs, over mountain peaks, over graves, over the trunks of wedding gowns. The snow does not melt. It accumulates. This is the chapter's physical embodiment of Pillar 3.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

"Scarcely had he noticed whether a person was fair or dark, spotted or unspotted, male or female, living or dead. So great was his tactfulness as a lawyer."

This passage is superficially about Mr. Spitzer's professional detachment, but it is actually a description of the ethical stance of the Witness. He does not categorize, conclude, or simplify. He stays in the room: literally, he moves through room after room of the suffragette's house, opening trunks, noting details, refusing to rush toward a verdict.

His witnessing is characterized by a specific kind of attention without resolution:

"If his patience was that oblivion permitting him to notice much which others had not noticed, his oblivion was greater than his scrutiny."

This is a precise formulation of the Witness as distinct from the analyst or the judge. The Witness notices more precisely because they do not rush to conclude. Mr. Spitzer's legal training (his habit of keeping ticket stubs, noting unimportant details "which might be important in some other case or remote contingency never counted upon on earth") is reframed here as an ethical practice rather than a professional one.

The chapter's most sustained act of witnessing is Mr. Spitzer's reading of the old newspapers about the suffragette's adventures. He does not evaluate whether her exploits were real or imaginary. He reads, he notes, he believes: "he believed everything he read and much that was not written or the print which had faded". His credulity is not naivety; it is the Witness's refusal to dismiss.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

She "had climbed old mountains as if they were new mountains, mountains which changed with every climber and every climb."

This is one of the chapter's most philosophically precise passages. The suffragette's life is explicitly described as recursive: she returns to the same mountains, the same attempts, the same journeys, but the repetition is not failure or stagnation. Each return is a new construction built on the previous one.

"Never were both the unchanging mountain and the unchanging mountain-climber."

The building is never finished because both the builder and the material change with each attempt. Mr. Spitzer himself enacts this recursively: he has closed many estates before this one, has made many inventories, has returned again and again to the same legal and existential tasks. His lists, "checking lists with lists", are themselves recursive constructions, each list generated by and modifying the previous one.

The novel's prose style is Pillar 5. The sentences spiral back on themselves, qualify their own qualifications, begin again from slightly different angles. The chapter does not progress linearly; it accretes. This is not stylistic excess; it is the formal enactment of recursive construction.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

"Perhaps there would always be at the end the box which would open to no key or no box but the dream, the dream opening to no key but the dream, and he should always have known this."

The entire chapter is structured around incompletion. The suffragette died without a will. Her trunks cannot all be opened; there is always "one lid more than there was a key, one key more than there was a lid". Her identity cannot be established. Her love was never consummated. Her suffrage work was never completed. Her airplane battle with the eagle has no reported outcome.

Mr. Spitzer's inventory, his legal duty to complete, is explicitly defeated:

his lists might "never be complete, and his counting mania might never cease"

But the novel frames this not as failure but as honesty. The demand for a complete inventory, a resolved identity, a concluded life; these are described implicitly as violences against the texture of actual experience. The suffragette "had not been committed to one destiny", and this uncommittedness is presented as her most significant quality: "how much more successful she had been in her failure than if only one door had opened to her knock".

The ethics of incompletion are most visible in Mr. Spitzer's decision to omit the wedding gowns from his official inventory:

"why should he tell the whole world her secret... Better that she should remain the lone wolf galaxy"

This is an act of ethical incompletion: he deliberately leaves the record unfinished to protect the complexity of a life from the violence of complete disclosure.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

"She was the image of the unbegotten who had realized without love that which others had achieved and who had loved without realization."

The suffragette is the chapter's primary marginal figure: a woman who dressed as a man, who loved women in an era that had no language for it, who was neither bride nor bridegroom, neither male nor female, neither living (in the conventional sense of full social participation) nor dead (she outlived her own legend ). She exists at the edges of every category the novel invokes.

But Pillar 7 insists that the marginal figure is not the exception; they are the evidence. And the chapter bears this out: the suffragette's marginality makes visible what is true of everyone. Mr. Spitzer is equally marginal: "neither husband nor wife", uncertain whether he is "himself or his dead twin", a lawyer who feels "more like a thief than a lawyer" in the houses he legally inhabits.

The chapter populates itself with marginal figures: the "men of extinct professions, of vanished trades", the "violinist without arms, the footless dancer, the blind portrait painter, the mute singer", the "dead mountain climbers" sleeping in hotels, the letters that fall from crashed planes and are never delivered. Each of these figures is marginal in a different way, but together they constitute the chapter's true population: the evidence that the categories of the living, the functional, the legible are constructions maintained at the expense of everything that falls outside them.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This pillar is perhaps most literally enacted in the chapter's Alpine imagery. The mountains are explicitly described as sedimentary: layers of snow accumulating over centuries, covering dead cities, dead lovers, lost skirts, lost loves:

She "had found a footstep left upon the snow a thousand years ago"

"the snows as old as the kingdom of Charlemagne had gathered to the snows and never would be dissolved until the end of time unearthing the lost love"

The past is not behind the suffragette; it is beneath her, pressing up. The dead cities are revealed when the sands move ; the dead mountain climbers are preserved in the snow, potentially to be uncovered when the suffragette melts the glaciers. The chapter's repeated image of things buried and potentially recoverable (letters under snow, cities under ice, bodies in trunks) is a sustained meditation on sedimentary time.

Mr. Spitzer's own temporal experience is sedimentary. He reads newspapers from different eras simultaneously, finding reports of events that happened before the reporters were born and obituaries published before the subject died. Time does not flow linearly here; it accumulates in layers that can be accessed non-sequentially, pressing up through the present moment with their full original weight.

"He had begun to believe in that past which never was as in the future which would never be."

The past that never was is still sediment. It still presses up. The ontological status of an event does not determine its weight in the sediment.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

"there were secret languages never to be translated into words"

"when, indeed, it seemed to him that no language contained truth, for the truth was that which could not be expressed, was by its very nature the inexpressible, the unutterable, the word never written, the name never told"

The chapter is obsessed with the failure and construction of language. The suffragette cried "Long live woman! Vive la femme! Viva la donna!" in multiple languages, but the multiplicity of languages does not increase access to truth; it demonstrates that each language is a construction, a different angle on something that none of them can reach.

The newspapers that document the suffragette's life are "dreaming newspaper reporters" with "blurred" reports, print that has faded, dateless papers. The documentary record, the language that is supposed to fix reality, is itself unstable, partial, located. Mr. Spitzer's legal language (inventories, lists, assessments) is explicitly defeated by the excess of reality over any possible description.

Most tellingly, the book the suffragette was reading when she died has a title Mr. Spitzer cannot quite recall:

"He was not certain of the book's title now when all writing had faded in his unretentive mind"

The last text, the final word, the book held at the moment of death, even this cannot be fixed in language. The novel names its own location here: it is a text that knows it is a construction, written from somewhere in particular (the perspective of the mournful, the marginal, the incomplete), making no claim to speak from nowhere.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

"one light in the tower window of the soul, Mr. Spitzer said."

The sacred in this chapter is never located elsewhere. There is no heaven invoked except as metaphor, no afterlife promised, no transcendence that moves beyond the immanent world. Instead, the sacred is located in the most ordinary and broken things:

  • The acorn found inside the locked safe: the entire future oak inside the most mundane object
  • The penny weights on the dead woman's eyelids, "one in memory of the sun, one in memory of the moon", the cosmic reduced to pocket change
  • The sea shell sleeping at the suffragette's feet: the ocean present in a domestic room
  • The firefly burning in Mr. Spitzer's breast: the light of consciousness as a small, biological, immanent flame

The chapter's most sustained image of immanent transcendence is Mr. Spitzer's mourning cloak:

"Perhaps his mourning cloak grew like the sea shell. His cloak sang, sighed as the wind blew."

The cloak (a piece of clothing, a material object) has a life beyond him, grows organically, participates in the world's music. The sacred is here, in the cloth, in the grief it embodies, in the wind that moves through it. It does not point outward to a beyond; it points deeper into the ordinary world of weather and cloth and loss.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

"He was neither husband nor wife. He knew not which brother he was, whether the begotten or the unbegotten. Or perhaps they were one."

Mr. Spitzer is explicitly constituted by what was deposited before he arrived. His dead twin brother is not behind him: the twin is in him, is part of what he is. The suffragette is not behind him; she is through him, the source of his sympathies, his griefs, his way of moving through the world. He is the crossing point of multiple temporalities: his own life, his brother's unlived life, the suffragette's concluded life, the lives of all the dead whose estates he has closed.

The chapter's final image enacts this most completely:

"He had closed her eyes. He had weighted down her eyelids with two pennies, one in memory of the sun, one in memory of the moon."

Mr. Spitzer is constituting the ground the not-yet-born will stand on. His act of witnessing, his careful (and deliberately incomplete) inventory, his decision to protect her secret; these are acts of transmission. He is carrying something forward. The obligation is not chosen; it is structural. He arrived at her death as her lawyer and found himself her only mourner, her only witness, the only one who would carry the knowledge of the wedding gowns forward, or choose not to.

The crossing point is also temporal: Mr. Spitzer exists at the intersection of the suffragette's past and some unspecified future. The ancestors (the suffragette, his dead twin, all the dead whose estates he has catalogued) are in the sediment beneath him. The carrying continues, even when the carrier does not know what he is carrying, even when the record is deliberately incomplete, even when the language has faded and the book's title cannot be recalled.
