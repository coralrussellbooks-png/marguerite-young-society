Named Passengers, Symbolic Identity, and the Novel's Opening Constellation

I. What the Novel Actually Says: The Passenger Roster

At the Opening: Three Passengers

Chapter 1 establishes the bus's initial population precisely. Vera is the narrator. The bus-driver is present. There are, in Vera's words, a sleeping couple, a pair of lovers, boy and girl, the only other passengers. The novel opens with four souls on a bus: Vera, the driver, and the sleeping couple. This is the foundational count.

The sleeping girl is described at extraordinary length before she is named. She is pregnant, resistant to her condition, overdressed, not wearing a wedding ring. The boy is broad-faced, innocent, wearing a football sweater with the letter C. Their names come later: Madge and Homer Capehorn.

The Four Evangelists: Not Names but Symbols

The most significant discovery from reading the novel directly is that Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John are not the actual names of four passengers. They appear in Moses Hunnecker's extended interior monologue as his symbolic identification of his cargo. The passage reads:

Only the old bus-driver was rolling in his bed, bringing these four passengers, four angels at four bed-posts, Matthew, Mark, Luke, John. He said that there was no one else.

This is Moses's vision of what he is carrying, not a census. The four bed-posts with four angels is a reference to the medieval protection prayer: Matthew, Mark, Luke, John, bless the bed that I lie on. Moses is dreaming that he is in his deathbed, guarded by the four Evangelists. His four passengers are, in his symbolic imagination, his deathbed angels. He is not driving four people named after the Gospels. He is a dying man who has turned his passengers into the witnesses of his passing.

This distinction matters architecturally. Young is not assigning Gospel names to characters. She is giving Moses a consciousness that reads his own journey as a dying man's vision, his passengers as the four witnesses every soul needs at the end. The bus becomes a deathbed. The journey becomes the last journey. The Evangelists are present not as characters but as the structure of witness that Moses's dying imagination requires.

The Fifth: One as Yet Unborn

The novel is explicit that there is a fifth presence on the bus who is not yet born. Moses himself states it twice. The first time: with these four passengers, one as yet unborn. The second time: bringing these four passengers, all four of them, all alive and one unborn.

The unborn passenger is Vera. Not literally unborn in the biological sense, but unborn as a self: she is the narrator who is in the process of becoming, the consciousness that will hold the entire novel, the self that does not yet know what she is. The novel is the process of her birth. She is the fifth passenger who cannot be counted yet because she is still being created by the journey she is narrating.

This reading is supported by Indiana's identification in the novel as the promised land: This was only the stopping ground, Indiana, the promised land. Vera is traveling toward the place where she will arrive at herself. She has not arrived. She is, for the duration of the journey, the one as yet unborn.

II. The Named Passengers and Their Names

Moses Hunnecker: The Lawgiver Who Cannot Enter

Moses names himself directly in the novel: "You know me, Moses Hunnecker, the bus-driver, my long hair streaming like a curtain over my eyes, but I can see better when I am drunk than you can see when you are sober, you old fogdog, luminous spot upon a far horizon, and I can see through the windshield, and I can see what’s coming from before, behind, having this rear mirror view." This self-naming is the most explicit symbolic identification in the opening section. Young is not being subtle. The bus-driver is Moses.

The biblical Moses led the children of Israel through the wilderness toward the promised land. He did not enter it. He brought the people to the border and died in sight of the destination. Moses Hunnecker is driving people toward Indiana, the promised land, and his every action signals that he will not arrive with them. He is drunk. He is asleep at the wheel. He believes he may already be dead. He has been driving this route so long he does not know if the journey has ended or is still ongoing.

Moses's hair is his most sustained symbolic attribute. He has taken a vow not to cut it until the Democrats leave the White House, but the novel gives this vow the weight of a Nazarite oath. The Nazarite vow in the Bible, taken by figures including Samson, involved not cutting the hair as a sign of consecration and power. When Moses says "When you cut my hair, I said to him, I will be dead and not the man I was", he is speaking in Samson's register. His uncut hair is his life, his power, his identity. The barber who would cut it is the Delilah figure, the doctor who keeps trying to tell him he is already dead.

The infant Moses sleeping in his little boat among the reeds appears later in the novel in a completely separate context, describing a scene in Cousin Hannah's house. Young uses the Moses image twice: the bus-driver who names himself Moses, and the hidden infant in the bulrushes. Both are Moses in different moments of the same story: the hidden, protected child and the old man who has led the people to the edge of what he cannot enter.

Moses's destination is also significant. He is going home. Moses Hunnecker's "old woman waiting" is, in fact, his mother. He lives with her in what she perceives as a "bridal chamber," where she considers him her bridegroom, despite her being "old and sick and dying". Moses Hunnecker himself refers to her as "my old mother" and notes her constant "creating again" in this context. The lawgiver who cannot enter the promised land is going home to his wife. His destination and Vera's destination are not the same destination. He is circling back. She is going forward for the first time.

Dr. Justice O'Leary: The Name That Judges Itself

The old doctor introduces himself with his full name in the novel: Dr. Justice O'Leary, for so he called himself, announcing frequently not only his name but the fact that he was the best medical practitioner this country ever saw. The name Justice is not incidental. It is his self-proclaimed title and it is the name he announces frequently, as if repeating it will make it true.

Justice O'Leary practices medicine only in his crazy dreams. He is not practicing on real patients. He is practicing on hallucinated women in labor, on imaginary births, on the dead who need someone to confirm they are dead. He has lost his actual practice. What he has retained is his name, his claim to be Justice, and his conviction that he is still doing the work of justice: delivering the living into the world, releasing the dead into death.

The irony Young has built into this name is layered. Justice is both a title and a concept. Dr. Justice O'Leary administers no justice that the world recognizes. He delivers no babies that anyone else can see. He practices in a world of his own imagination, and in that world he is the most excellent doctor this country ever saw. Justice in the novel is always this: the standard one holds oneself to in the interior world, which has no necessary relation to what the exterior world recognizes as justice.

O'Leary is an Irish name: son of the descendant of the one who is cheerful. He is depicted as suffering from dementia, believing he is still delivering babies though he is retired. He is the cheerful doctor who will not stop delivering what he believes is new life into a world that has stopped needing his deliveries. He is justice without a court. He is medicine without patients. He is cheerfulness in a dead country.

Madge: The Pearl in the Chaos

Madge is the short form of Margaret, which derives from the Greek margarites, meaning pearl. Madge is dressed in pearls at the novel's opening: cascades of oblong ten cent store pearls were dripping from her coral-colored ears. She is wearing cheap pearls, costume jewelry pearls, the imitation of the precious thing. She is named for the pearl but wearing the copy.

Madge is pregnant with a child that is not her husband's. She is returning to a small town to live with her mother-in-law. She did not marry the man she wanted. Every element of her situation is the opposite of what her name promises: the pearl is cast before swine, the precious thing is in the wrong setting, the genuine value is hidden under layers of cheap imitation. She sees clearly what Homer cannot or will not see. She is the one who knows Jacqueline is dying, who knows what Homer feels, who knows her own situation with a cold precision that the novel respects even as it chronicles her suffering.

The pearl has additional resonance in the novel's broader symbolic vocabulary. Pearls appear throughout the novel associated with wisdom acquired through suffering, with the interior life that the exterior world does not recognize. Catherine Cartwheel's lovers, it is said, could not diminish her pearls in their number. The pearl is what the interior life produces through the pressure of its circumstances. Madge, named for the pearl, is producing her wisdom through exactly this pressure.

Homer Capehorn: The Epic Poet at the Edge of the World

Homer Capehorn carries two names, each significant. Homer is the blind poet of the Iliad and the Odyssey, the epic tradition's founding voice, the teller of stories about war and homecoming. Cape Horn is the southernmost tip of South America, the most treacherous navigational passage in the world, the point beyond which ships either make it or go down.

Homer Capehorn is sleeping through the novel's opening miles, his cheek against the cold bus-window, his lips placidly smiling. He is the blind poet who does not see what is in front of him: Madge's suffering, Jacqueline's death, his own marriage's emptiness. He is the narrator of epics who cannot narrate his own situation. He is the dangerous passage that wrecks ships, but he is asleep at it, unaware of the danger he represents or embodies.

Homer's blindness is not physical but moral and emotional. He is described as all blissful stolidity and broad-faced innocence. His football sweater has the letter C on its front: C for Capehorn, C for the Cape that is the dangerous passage, C for the obliviousness that makes him the most ordinary and in some ways the most devastating figure in the opening section. He is the epic poet who has nothing to say about his own life.

Vera: The True One, Unborn

Vera means true in Latin. She is the novel's narrator, the consciousness that contains all the others, the one as yet unborn who will spend 1,198 pages being born. Her name is the novel's central claim: that the truth is what Vera carries, and that Vera herself is the truth that is trying to emerge.

Vera is not named in the bus sections in the way that Moses and the others are named. She names herself gradually, as a narrator identifies herself to the reader. She is the unborn fifth, the truth that has not yet taken form. The novel is her birth process. She arrives in Indiana, the promised land, without having reached What Cheer, Iowa. She does not find Miss MacIntosh. She finds something else: herself, in the act of loving the stone deaf man, in the recognition of Esther Longtree as the mother of us all. That recognition is Vera's birth. The truth has arrived. The unborn has been born.

III. The Conversation the Names Are Having

Young named these characters with intention. The bus is not a realistic setting populated with realistic strangers. It is a symbolic assembly: a lawgiver who cannot enter the promised land, a justice who administers justice only in his imagination, a pearl in the wrong setting, a blind epic poet, and an unborn truth. These five are in conversation with each other, and the conversation is about what it means to carry something you cannot deliver.

Moses carries the people to the promised land and cannot enter. Justice O'Leary carries the practice of medicine into a world with no patients. Madge carries a child that is not her husband's toward a life she did not choose. Homer carries the epic tradition and cannot see what is in front of him. Vera carries the truth of all their stories and has not yet been born into the capacity to tell it.

The bus is a vehicle for this carrying. It goes in circles, or seems to: Moses is always on the same road, passing the same landmarks. The journey is endless because the delivery cannot be completed until Vera is born, and Vera is born only at the novel's end. Moses drives until the novel ends. He does not stop because the truth has not yet arrived at itself.

The Grey Goose bus: "The old grey goose is dead. Died in the morning," he sang, "died in the morning, died in the morning with a pillow under her head. He mumbled to himself, his head drooping, 'Oh, let us not marry her. Oh, let us bury her.'" The vehicle carrying these symbolic figures is itself a dead thing, a grey goose that has died in the morning, still flying.
