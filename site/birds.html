Definition from research

Chapter 2 · The Canary

  • Life and hope in a cage; fragile and drugged, paralleling Catherine’s own condition.
  • Symbolic of the precariousness of existence and the illusion of continuity.

Chapter 2 · Two Sister Ravens

  • The ravens appear symbolically and thematically as mysterious, almost otherworldly figures that contribute to the surreal, dreamlike atmosphere surrounding Catherine and her suspended existence between life and death.
  • The ravens are described as "two sister ravens who had created the universe" This description situates the ravens not merely as birds but as powerful, almost divine entities involved in cosmic creation and the shaping of reality itself.
  • Their role emphasizes Catherine’s immersion in a realm where boundaries between reality and illusion, life and death, are blurred.
  • The ravens, linked with creation, suggest a world that is constantly in flux, shaped by mysterious forces beyond ordinary human perception.
  • The ravens contribute to Catherine’s perception of her own life as “more monstrous than hers and terribly extra-human,” reflecting her alienation from the normal flow of time and human relations.
  • Their presence as mythic creators contrasts with Catherine’s physical stasis and withdrawal, underlining the tension between dynamic cosmic forces and her horizontal, dream-bound existence.
  • Ravens traditionally symbolize mystery, death, and prophecy in literature and myth, fitting well with Catherine’s liminal state caught between life and death, and her visions of phantoms, spirits, and the dead.
  • Their “sister” relationship may also suggest duality and companionship in a lonely, spectral world where Catherine resides, further personifying the enigmatic and haunting nature of her dreams.

Chapter 3

  • “all these fine-feathered birds of paradise”: The birds of paradise of New Guinea reached Europe in the 1500s as trade skins with their feet removed, and naturalists concluded that they had no feet and lived their whole lives in the air, feeding on dew, until they fell to earth at death. Linnaeus named one species Paradisaea apoda, “footless.”
  • “an Arctic tern stopping upon the rocks”: The Arctic tern makes the longest migration of any animal, flying each year from its Arctic breeding grounds to the Antarctic and back, and so sees two summers and more daylight than any other creature.

Chapter 4 · Red Hair and Red Rooster’s Wing

  • Symbolizes vitality, passion, and the vividness of Miss MacIntosh’s presence.

Chapter 5 · Stormy Petrels, Woodpeckers, Red-winged Blackbirds

  • Represent chaos, disturbance, and the persistence of nature amid decay.

Chapter 6 · The Stuffed Bird on Madge’s Hat

  • Symbolizes fragile consciousness, the artificiality of appearance, and the precariousness of Madge’s situation.

Chapter 6

  • “little red-winged blackbirds where the snow was falling”: Blackbirds can symbolize different things depending on the culture: death, change, magic, and mystery. Specifically in Christianity it is a symbol of temptation and carnal pleasure. Blackbirds in the chapter are multifaceted symbols embodying death, the haunting past, sorrow, and the thin veil separating life and death, reinforcing the novel’s pervasive atmosphere of melancholy and existential uncertainty.

Chapter 7 · Feathers and Wings

  • The fragile boundary between life and death, the ephemeral nature of existence, spiritual transition, decay, and the vulnerability of identity and innocence. They create an atmosphere of haunting delicacy and the inevitability of loss and change.

Chapter 8 · Birds (Woodpeckers, Bluejays, Mockingbirds)

  • Birds appear repeatedly, symbolizing nature, communication, and, perhaps, the persistence of life amid decay

Chapter 9 · Bird Imagery, Especially Seagulls

  • Seagulls appear as recurring natural motifs connected with care and injury. They may symbolize vulnerability and healing, but also freedom and the natural world.
  • The death of the seagull “when its wound was healed” suggests a paradox of survival and loss, or the cost of recovery.

Chapter 11

  • “Queen Charlotte Islands”: The Queen Charlotte Islands, off the Pacific coast of Canada, were named in 1787 for the ship Queen Charlotte, which was named for George III’s queen. They are the home of the Haida and are now called Haida Gwaii.
  • “a wild swan absent, one wild swan more”: In Andersen’s “The Wild Swans” (1838), eleven brothers are turned into swans, and their sister weaves nettle shirts to free them. The last shirt is unfinished, and the youngest is left with a swan’s wing.
  • “the sister ravens, the brother doves”: The Grimms’ “The Seven Ravens” and Giambattista Basile’s “The Seven Doves” (1634-36) both tell of brothers changed into birds and a sister who saves them. Young makes the ravens sisters.

Chapter 12

  • “the spot on a peacock’s tail”: Juno set the hundred eyes of the slain watchman Argus in the peacock’s tail (Ovid, Metamorphoses 1).
  • “Mr. Chandelier or Mr. Chanticleer”: Chanticleer is the proud rooster of Chaucer’s “The Nun’s Priest’s Tale” and the fables of Reynard the Fox. Mr. Chandelier is Catherine’s companion from Chapter 2.
  • “a rooster crowing when there was no rooster”: “Before the cock crow, thou shalt deny me thrice” (Matthew 26:34).
  • “twelve white swans like brothers or suitors bewitched”: Andersen’s eleven brothers turned into swans, and the bewitched swan maidens of Tchaikovsky’s Swan Lake (1877).

Chapter 13

  • “One should see the bird and the shadow of the bird, but one should not hear the song”: Wallace Stevens, “Thirteen Ways of Looking at a Blackbird” (1917): “The blackbird whistling / Or just after.”

Chapter 14

  • “the golden cock perched at the foot of her bed, that it was really Pythagoras shedding his golden feathers”: In Lucian’s dialogue “The Dream, or the Cock,” a cobbler’s rooster reveals that it is Pythagoras reborn. Pythagoras was said to have a golden thigh. Rimsky-Korsakov’s opera The Golden Cockerel (1909), from Pushkin’s tale (1834), has a golden cock that warns a tsar.
  • “her peacocks”: Aristotle referred to the peacock’s vain reputation. The Roman poet Ennius, following the Pythagorean teaching of rebirth, dreamed that Homer’s soul had passed into a peacock and then into himself. The peacock symbolizes the dazzling but ephemeral nature of beauty and social display, the multiplicity and fragmentation of perception, and the tension between illusion and reality that the characters navigate.

Chapter 15

  • “as crazy as loons”: The loon’s wild, laughing cry gave the phrase.

Chapter 16

  • “the phoenix rising from those cold ashes of the dream”: The phoenix, which dies in fire and rises from its own ashes.
  • “the stuffed lute birds”: The lyrebird of Australia, named for its lyre-shaped tail.

Chapter 18 · Feather Pillow

  • Used by Miss MacIntosh to cover Vera’s head, symbolizing an attempt to induce forgetfulness, sleep, or escape from harsh realities.

Chapter 27

  • “for the tongue-less nightingales”: Philomela, whose tongue Tereus cut out, became the nightingale (Ovid, Metamorphoses 6).
  • “storks’ nests on the roof-tops”: In northern Europe a stork’s nest on the roof brought luck, and storks brought babies. Hans Christian Andersen’s “The Storks” (1838).

Chapter 28

  • “a booming sound like that of the loud sea bittern”: The bittern, a marsh heron, is known for its deep, booming call.
  • “more precious than the cuckoo singing as it flies”: Wordsworth’s “To the Cuckoo” (1807): “No bird, but an invisible thing, / A voice, a mystery.”

Chapter 29

  • “butterflies as big as the wailing lapwing”: Lapwing is a ground nesting bird found in Europe.

Chapter 32

  • “the stately mating dance of the whooping cranes”: Whooping cranes court with leaps, bows, and wing-flapping. By 1941 only about fifteen wild birds remained.

Chapter 33

  • “Owls, great horned owls hooting above the lonely church steeples”: “From yonder ivy-mantled tow’r / The moping owl does to the moon complain” (Thomas Gray, “Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard,” 1751). Young named Gray’s Elegy as a model for Angel in the Forest (Marguerite Young, interview with Charles Ruas and Joan Schwartz, WBAI radio, New York).

Chapter 33 · The Blackbird and the Door

  • She tells someone to open the door, and the voice answers that it is already open, though the door is closed. She is talking to a human-sized blackbird.
  • These images portray the overlap of the real and the imaginary.

Chapter 34 · The Cock Crowing

  • Symbolizes awakening, sovereignty, and perhaps the harsh reality that disrupts illusions.

Chapter 35

  • “great gold-eyed white ptarmigans”: Ptarmigans are medium-sized game birds in the grouse family.

Chapter 36 · Animals and Nature

  • Birds (blackbirds, seagulls), horses, and natural phenomena (fog, wind, fireflies) evoke transformation, freedom, and the boundary between worlds.
  • The black coachman lies where “the white manes of the running horses” streak the waves.

Chapter 37

  • “the footless messengers”: Birds of paradise were believed to have no feet and never to land, because their skins reached Europe with the feet cut off. Linnaeus named the greater bird of paradise Paradisaea apoda, “footless,” in 1758.

Chapter 37 · The Hawk and Birds

  • Represent death, predation, and perhaps the inevitability of mortality. “Her head was eaten by great birds. Great birds had plucked out her eyes.”

Chapter 39

  • “the stormy petrels flying”: Petrels are said to be named for St. Peter, because they seem to walk on the water (Matthew 14:29).

Chapter 40

  • “screaming contests of Platan rails like the human voice exerted to its utmost pitch”: Young’s source is W. H. Hudson, who describes the giant wood rails, the ypecaha, of La Plata: “This is a tremendous screaming concert,” and “The screams they utter have a certain resemblance to the human voice, exerted to its utmost pitch” (W. H. Hudson, The Naturalist in La Plata, 1892, chapter 19, “Music and Dancing in Nature”).
  • “Would the sheathbills have told”: Sheathbills are Antarctic shore scavengers, ruddy turnstones are wading shorebirds, and stormy petrels are seabirds of the open ocean. See the petrel entry in the Chapter 39 resources and Birds.

Chapter 41

  • “One lost one’s magnetic sense, such as migratory birds are perhaps guided by”: Young’s “perhaps” came just before the proof. In 1965 Friedrich Merkel and Wolfgang Wiltschko showed that caged European robins orient by the earth’s magnetic field (“Magnetismus und Richtungsfinden zugunruhiger Rotkehlchen,” Die Vogelwarte 23, 1965, 71-77).
  • “the snow-white whetsaw owls”: Whetsaw owls may refer to the Saw-whet owl found in North America and one of the smallest owl species. Its name comes from its call, said to sound like a saw being whetted on a stone.

Chapter 42

  • “Ptarmigan, swallowed”: Ptarmigan is a bird in the grouse subfamily.
  • “never by robin redbreast covering him with the autumn leaf”: “Call for the robin-red-breast and the wren, / Since o’er shady groves they hover, / And with leaves and flowers do cover / The friendless bodies of unburied men” (John Webster, The White Devil 5.4, 1612). In the ballad of the Babes in the Wood, robins cover the dead children with leaves.

Chapter 45

  • “the great snowy ptarmigan”: The ptarmigan turns white in winter to vanish against the snow.
  • “Pigeons had stopped an old town clock.”: On August 12, 1945, a flock of starlings settled on the minute hand of Big Ben and slowed it by several minutes.
  • “the pigeon with the Red Cross upon its snow-white breast”: Carrier pigeons served in both world wars. Cher Ami carried the message that saved the “Lost Battalion” in the Argonne in 1918, though shot through the breast, and France awarded the bird the Croix de Guerre.
  • “the Bleeding Heart”: The bleeding-heart dove of the Philippines has a white breast with a blood-red patch at its center, as if it had been wounded.
  • “passenger pigeons now extinct”: Audubon watched a flight of passenger pigeons pass for three days in 1813, “the light of noon-day” obscured “as by an eclipse” (John James Audubon, Ornithological Biography, 1831). The last, Martha, died at the Cincinnati Zoo on September 1, 1914. See Martha in the Chapter 49 resources.
  • “no trumpeter swan could accompany her”: The trumpeter swan was nearly gone but not extinct. In 1932 only 69 were counted in the United States outside Alaska, and the Red Rock Lakes refuge was set aside for them in 1935.

Chapter 46

  • “like the light-winged cranes”: Pliny wrote that cranes “ballast themselves with coarse sand” before crossing the Black Sea, choose a leader, and post sentinels that each hold “a little stone in its claw” (Natural History 10.30, trans. John Bostock and H. T. Riley, 1855).

Chapter 48

  • “goose barnacles”: Goose barnacles were long believed to hatch into barnacle geese, since no one had seen the geese nest; Gerald of Wales reported it in his Topographia Hibernica (about 1188), and the barnacle goose is named for the belief.
  • “having mistaken the lighthouse for the star”: Birds migrating at night are drawn to lighthouse beams and strike the glass, and keepers long recorded the dead birds found at the foot of the tower in the morning.

Chapter 49

  • “the last passenger pigeon known to earth”: Martha died at the Cincinnati Zoo on the afternoon of September 1, 1914, a month after the First World War began, alone since her last companions died in 1910. Young gives the date as September 14, 1918, the last year of the war, at one o’clock. Mr. Spitzer mourns her as the last of her kind: once something is lost, whether a species, a person, or a moment, it cannot be recovered, and mourning for her is mourning for all that is irretrievably gone. Aldo Leopold wrote for the monument to the pigeon at Wyalusing State Park in Wisconsin: “We have erected a monument to commemorate the funeral of a species. It symbolizes our sorrow” (“On a Monument to the Pigeon,” 1947).
  • “trapped by nets like the fishes of the sea”: Netters lured whole flocks with a captive pigeon tied to a perch or stool, the origin, it is said, of “stool pigeon.” At the last great nesting near Petoskey, Michigan, in 1878, hunters killed an estimated 50,000 birds a day for nearly five months, netting, shooting, and smothering them with burning sulfur, and shipped them to city markets.
  • “If there were no pigeons, could there be people”: The passenger pigeon’s story is a powerful symbol of the consequences of human actions, overexploitation, and habitat destruction. Here pigeons stand for life, presence, and continuity, bound up with dawn and twilight, the passage between night and day, life and death. The mottled “pigeon-colored dawn” is complex and uncertain rather than a clear moment, and “He had lost all sense of the hour”: his time is fractured like his identity, and even the dawn is indistinct to him.
  • “had no soul, the old caretaker said”: John James Audubon painted a pair of passenger pigeons for The Birds of America (plate 62, 1827-1838), and Mark Catesby before him in The Natural History of Carolina, Florida and the Bahama Islands (1731-1743).
  • “whistle all the way from here to Labrador”: The Labrador duck, last recorded in 1878, was the first North American bird known to go extinct after European settlement.
  • “the whooping crane and the lute bird”: By 1941 only about twenty whooping cranes were left in the world, the wild flock wintering at Aransas on the Texas coast. The lute bird may be the lyrebird of Australia, named for the lyre shape of the male’s tail and famous for imitating any sound.
  • “He was this old fan-tail fanning his tail”: Darwin bred fancy pigeons and opened On the Origin of Species (1859) with them: the fantail with thirty or forty tail feathers spread upright and the pouter with its swollen crop. Mr. Spitzer is “pouter-breasted” too.

Chapter 49 · Birds (Pigeons, Blackbirds, Fireflies)

  • Birds symbolize life, freedom, and the passage of souls.
  • The extinction of the passenger pigeon is a metaphor for loss and the fragility of existence.
  • Fireflies and moths symbolize faint, fragile light, hope, memory, or the soul’s flicker.

Chapter 52 · Birds and Butterflies

  • Symbols of transformation, fleeting beauty, and the soul’s journey. The butterfly sailing out of the ear (a door) suggests escape or transcendence.

Chapter 56

  • “the night-crowned heron wader”: The black-crowned night heron fishes at dusk and by night. See the night-crowned kingfisher in the Chapter 55 resources.
  • “the mantling hawk”: In falconry, a hawk mantles when it spreads its wings and tail over its prey to hide it.
  • “The great barnacle birds in the clouds”: Medieval naturalists believed that barnacle geese grew from goose barnacles on driftwood, since no one had seen them nest; Gerald of Wales described it in 1188.

Chapter 57

  • “how many black swans following the white midnight”: Europeans long used the black swan as a byword for the impossible, after the Roman poet Juvenal’s “rara avis in terris nigroque simillima cygno,” a bird as rare on earth as a black swan (Satires 6.165), until the Dutch explorer Willem de Vlamingh found black swans in western Australia in 1697.

Chapter 59

  • “the barkings of red-eyed dogs accompanying dead souls to Hades”: In northern English folklore the Gabriel Hounds, or Gabble Ratchets, were a spectral pack heard yelping overhead at night, and their cry foretold a death; the sound was probably wild geese in passage.
  • “hearing the kingfisher’s cries”: In Ovid, Ceyx drowns at sea and his wife Alcyone finds his body washed ashore; the gods turn both into kingfishers, and the calm “halcyon days” are the days when the sea is still for their nesting (Metamorphoses, Book 11).
  • “ossifrage, the breaking of his bones”: Ossifrage means bone-breaker, from the Latin os, bone, and frangere, to break. It is the old name of the bearded vulture, which drops bones on rocks to split them for the marrow; the King James Bible lists “the ossifrage” among the unclean birds (Leviticus 11:13).

Chapter 59 · Birds and Migratory Patterns

  • Migratory birds symbolize movement between worlds, the passage of souls, and the patterns of life and death.
  • The cries of birds are likened to music and the fleeting nature of human emotion.

Chapter 60

  • “the great gold-eyed snow owl”: The snowy owl of the Arctic has yellow eyes; in some winters snowy owls come south in numbers to fields, shores, and airports.
  • “calling the wild ducks out of the clouds”: The different references to ducks are multifaceted symbols that evoke loss, innocence, mourning, and the liminal state between life and death. They enrich the emotional landscape, highlighting suffering and the fragile nature of existence amidst the novel’s broader themes of decay, memory, and transcendence.
  • “the polysyllabic horns of the wild ducks”: Young’s “polysyllabic horns” are the ducks’ calls of many syllables, which hunters imitate with wooden duck calls.
  • “a flight of mandarins driven by non-adventitious winds far off their usual course”: The mandarin duck of East Asia is the most brilliantly colored of ducks, the drake crested and with orange “sails” on his back. In China a pair of mandarin ducks stands for faithful married love.
  • “those poor birds of passage who, with their eyes blinded by the light”: Lighthouse keepers reported hundreds of migrating birds killed against the lantern in a single foggy night; the light draws night migrants, above all in fog.

Chapter 60 · Wild Ducks

  • Represent migration, passage, and the ephemeral beauty of life.
  • Their “crested heads and veiled gold eyes” suggest exoticism and a mystical presence.
  • Their flight and honking are likened to a “wild sonata,” connecting nature with music.

Chapter 61

  • “the great bitterns booming in the clouds”: The American bittern’s booming spring call carries far across the marsh; country people called the bird the thunder-pumper and the stake-driver.
  • “the black-crowned night heron splashed”: The black-crowned night heron feeds at dusk and by night; its call is a flat “quok.”
  • “the shadow of the mantling hawk”: In falconry a hawk mantles when it spreads its wings over its prey, or stretches a wing and a leg down one side.
  • “in cloud cuckooland”: In Aristophanes’s The Birds (414 BC), the birds build Cloudcuckooland, a city in the air between gods and men.
  • “the hermit thrush’s speckled breast”: The hermit thrush sings the elegy in Walt Whitman’s “When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom’d” (1865), his poem on the death of Lincoln.
  • “sound of the water ouzel”: A water ouzel is the traditional name for the American Dipper, a medium-sized, gray songbird known for its aquatic lifestyle, as it dives into cold, fast-moving streams to hunt for food. It is the only North American songbird that forages underwater, where it walks along the stream bed to catch aquatic insects and other prey. John Muir wrote it a hymn of praise, “The Water-Ouzel” (The Mountains of California, 1894).
  • “the great Arctic birds who round this globe each year, going from pole to pole”: The Arctic tern flies each year from its Arctic nesting grounds to the Antarctic and back, the longest migration of any bird.

Chapter 62

  • “for this was his swan song, his silent song, the swan being mute”: The mute swan is not truly mute; it hisses, snorts, and grunts. See the swan song in the Chapter 61 resources.
  • “many golden cockerels flapping their wings and crowing on singing weathervanes”: Compare Rimsky-Korsakov’s opera The Golden Cockerel (first performed 1909), whose golden bird crows warnings from its perch.
  • “the secretive egg of some great bird like the sea gannet”: The sea gannet is a large seabird, most commonly referring to the Northern Gannet, known for its spectacular plunge-diving to catch fish. These birds are white with black and gold markings and spend their lives at sea, only coming to land to nest.
  • “dance of the golden cock-of-the-rock”: The cock-of-the-rock is a real bird of South American cloud forests; the brilliant orange males gather at leks, where they dance and display to win the females.
  • “screaming coradicorns”: Young’s “coradicorns” are likely the Coraciiformes, the order of rollers, kingfishers, bee-eaters, and motmots, in which hornbills and hoopoes were also long classed.
  • “hornbills, hoopoes, motmots”: The hoopoe leads the birds on their pilgrimage in Attar’s Conference of the Birds (1177), and in Aristophanes’s The Birds it is the transformed king Tereus. See Cloudcuckooland in the Chapter 61 resources.
  • “wild screamings of stormy petrels”: Sailors named the storm petrel for Saint Peter, because it seems to walk on the water as it feeds (Matthew 14:29).
  • “loon soul crying as the light faded”: The common loon’s wailing night call carries for miles across northern lakes.
  • “apivorous birds”: Apivorous is an adjective that means “feeding on bees.” It describes animals, particularly birds, that eat bees as a food source. Bee-eaters and the honey buzzard are among them.

Chapter 63

  • “A screech owl flew by just then”: The screech owl’s cry was long taken for an omen of death.

Chapter 69 · Turkey Buzzards/Canaries

  • Represent death and the grotesque in the everyday world.

Chapter 74 · Birds (Blackbirds, Sobbing Bird)

  • Represent sorrow, the passage of time, and the fragility of life.

Chapter 77 · Birds (Robins, Blackbirds, Moths)

  • Represent life, death, innocence, and societal neglect.
  • Blackbirds especially tie to racial themes and death ignored by authorities.

Chapter 78 · Birds and Nests

  • Birds (woodpeckers, bluejays, blackbirds) symbolize freedom, nature, and sometimes fragility. The blackbirds’ nest with pale blue eggs and gold hair suggests innocence and the preciousness of life, as well as the shadow of loss.
