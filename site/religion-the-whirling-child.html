Definition from research

Marguerite Young was raised in large part by her maternal grandmother, a deeply religious woman whose faith ran through the household like weather. When the grandmother suffered a stroke late in life, her delusions filled with angels and other such creatures, and the child at that bedside took away a lasting sense that the unearthly was not far off but close at hand, folded into the ordinary furniture of a life. Young later recalled a small ritual from those years. When someone said the old admonition, Satan, get behind me, the child would whirl around at once, hoping to catch a glimpse of him before he could hide.

The gesture is worth pausing on. The child does not look up toward a distant hell or forward to a day of judgment. She looks behind her, into this world, at close range, certain the figure is standing right there. That instinct, that the sacred and the terrible are immediately present rather than safely elsewhere, is the seed of everything the novel would later become. By the time Young described her creed to the Paris Review, she had a phrase for it. Her religion, she said, was poetic and cosmic, not theological. She could not believe in a God cut to the measure of a single denomination. Her God was more imaginative, and always on the move. The remark reads less like a rejection of religion than a relocation of it. The holy is not evicted from Young's world. It is brought down into it, scattered across birds and beasts and weather and the faces of the dead, made native to the here.
