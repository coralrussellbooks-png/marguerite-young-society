The Style

Characters: Mr. Chandelier · Mr. Res Tacamah · Vera Cartwheel

Most descriptions of Young's prose reach quickly for the same words: lyrical, dense, poetic, hypnotic, difficult. All of that is accurate, and none of it distinguishes her from a dozen other ambitious stylists. What makes the style hers is not that it is beautiful but that it is doing the novel's philosophical work at the level of the sentence. The prose does not describe a world where reality and illusion will not separate, where the self is plural, where nothing resolves. It enacts that world in its grammar, its shape, and its smallest connective choices. To read Young's sentences carefully is to watch the book's argument happen in the syntax before it ever happens in the plot. Four features carry most of that work.

The grammar undermines what it states

This is the most important and least obvious feature of the style, and it is easy to miss because it hides inside sentences that seem merely descriptive. Young repeatedly builds a clause that asserts something and then, within the same breath, takes the assertion back, so that the sentence arrives nowhere it can be pinned. A character is "the illusion of the illusion and the spectre of the spectre in a mirrorless world." Read that slowly. Each noun is qualified by its own double, and the world that might have reflected and confirmed either one is removed at the end. The sentence offers a thing, then the copy of the thing, then denies the mirror that would let you check which was real. It is a grammar of retraction, and it is everywhere.

The effect is that her sentences will not let the reader stand on them. A statement is made and then hedged, doubled, or reversed before the period, so that certainty never quite forms. This is not vagueness. It is precise. Young is refusing, at the level of the clause, to do the thing prose normally does, which is to fix a fact and move on. Her prose describes a world that cannot be fixed, and it describes it in sentences that cannot be fixed either. The reader who wants a solid handhold is denied one on purpose, sentence after sentence, because a solid handhold would be a lie about the reality the book is rendering. The style is honest by being unstable.

"And" where another writer would put "but"

The smallest instance of that same refusal is a single word, and it is the most transferable thing a writer can take from her. Young overwhelmingly joins her clauses with "and" rather than "but." Where ordinary prose sets one thing against another, this happened, but that undercut it, Young lays the two side by side and lets them both stand, joined by the flat, non-hierarchical "and." The living and the dead, the real and the illusory, the beautiful and the ruined are strung together on "and," never sorted by "but" into the true one and the false one.

This is grammar as philosophy. "But" is the conjunction of resolution; it tells the reader which clause wins. "And" is the conjunction of coexistence; it refuses to rank. A novel whose whole argument is that opposites do not resolve into synthesis, that the dialectic is denied its arrival, could hardly choose otherwise. Every "and" where the ear expects "but" is a tiny enactment of the book's central refusal. The prose does not argue that contradictions coexist. It coordinates them with "and" and lets them lie there together, unresolved, which is a stronger claim than any argument because it is built into the sentence's spine rather than stated as a theme.

The recursive structure

Young's sentences do not advance so much as circle. A phrase generates a variation of itself, which generates another, so that the sentence turns around its subject rather than moving past it, "fading ripples begetting fading ripples," "the music becoming the unconscious music... So the conscious music became the unconscious." Clauses fold back on their own words; the end of one phrase becomes the beginning of the next; images recur slightly altered a few lines on. The unit of her prose is not the line that gets somewhere but the loop that deepens where it already is.

This recursion is the sentence-level version of the novel's whole architecture. The book does not build toward a climax; it accumulates revolutions, a spoke rotating rather than a line traveling. The prose is built the same way. A recursive sentence is a wheel turning in miniature, and a paragraph of them is the novel's structure rendered small enough to hold in one breath. This is also why the book can be so long without going anywhere in the ordinary sense: the sentences are not designed to progress, so the reader who reads them expecting progress fights the prose the whole way. Read for the turning instead, and the difficulty resolves into rhythm. The style teaches the reader how to read the book, if the reader will let the sentence circle instead of demanding it arrive.

Language as construction

The fourth feature connects the style directly to the novel's belief that reality is made rather than found. Young's prose is visibly building its world out of words, and it lets the seams show. Her characters are conjured by naming, a chandelier becomes Mr. Chandelier, a drug bottle becomes Mr. Res Tacamah, "the author of so many fantasies," the instant they are named. The prose does the same thing openly, assembling a person or a place out of accumulated clauses and never pretending the accumulation is anything but made. The famous cumulative sentences, the ones that pile detail on detail through a character's attire or a room's contents until the thing stands fully built, are demonstrations of construction. You watch the sentence make the object. It does not report a dress that exists; it builds a dress out of clauses, and the building is on display.

This is why the density is not decoration and cannot be trimmed without loss. In a novel arguing that the world is constructed by the people who need it, prose that constructs its world in plain sight is the argument in action. The reader is made complicit in the making, assembling each phantasmal figure and shifting house out of the words Young lays down, which is exactly what Vera does on the bus and the mother does in her bed. To read the style is to perform the novel's thesis about reality. The sentence is a small machine for building something that was not there, and letting you see it built.

The surface features, in service of the four

The qualities usually named as Young's style are real, and they all serve the four functions above rather than standing on their own. The sophisticated, surreal vocabulary, "phantasmagoric," "miasmic," "Stygian," "empearled," works by blending the tangible with the ethereal, a "gold chain" beside "ghostly tides," so that diction itself refuses to separate the solid from the dreamed. The pervasive metaphors and personification, "the rain falling like blows," "the moon shining at noon as a distant street lamp," give agency to the inanimate and blur the line between animate and not, which is the reality-illusion collapse worked in imagery. The sound devices, the consonant and vowel repetition that make the prose "billowing before her eyes and blotting out visibility," produce the hypnotic quality that carries a reader through recursion without resistance. The recurring, slightly varied images that make the novel "a canto to obsession" are recursion operating across the whole book rather than within a sentence. And Vera's narrating voice, by turns intimate and cosmic, compassionate and melancholy, is the instrument that holds all of this in one register, close enough to enter a character and distant enough to witness the whole turning world at once.

None of these is the point by itself. Each is a means to the four ends: a grammar that will not let a statement stand, an "and" that refuses to rank, a sentence that circles instead of arriving, and a prose that builds its world in the open. Together they make a style that does not talk about Young's philosophy but performs it, so that a reader who understands how her sentences work has already understood the novel, whether or not a single theme has been named.

The lesson for a writer is the largest the book offers about craft: style is not the decoration laid over meaning but the place where meaning is enacted. Young could have written about a world where reality and illusion will not part, in clear declarative prose, and the book would have been a lesser thing, a description of a philosophy rather than an experience of one. Instead she built the philosophy into the grammar, so that the refusal to resolve, the coexistence of opposites, the turning rather than arriving, the making of reality out of language, all happen to the reader in the act of reading rather than being reported to the reader after the fact. A writer who wants a theme to be felt rather than stated should study how Young makes the sentence itself carry the argument, down to the choice of a single conjunction, until there is no gap left between what the prose says and what the prose does.
