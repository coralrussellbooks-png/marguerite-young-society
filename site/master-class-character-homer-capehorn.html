Homer Capehorn: The Champion of a Finished Season

Part of Teaching a Writing Masterclass, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Madge Capehorn Edwards · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Jacqueline / Jackie White →

Spoiler note: This portrait is written for readers who have finished the novel. It discusses the character’s whole story, including how it ends.

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Homer Capehorn · Jacqueline White · Justice O’Leary · Madge Capehorn

He is asleep the whole time. He never wakes, never speaks a waking word, and everything known about him is either dreamed by him or dreamed onto him because he is a man whose whole life has already happened, who is present now only as deep sleep and old glory.

Homer Capehorn, once the captain of the football team, the all-star athlete, the champion carried off the field on his teammates' shoulders, and now a faded figure sleeping his deep sleep on a bus beside a pregnant wife he does not notice, in a football sweater gone to rags with a red-stained letter across the front. Madge, awake and bitter beside him, calls him a human football, always out kicking an old ball on the practice fields of his dreams. The crowds that once roared for him have long gone home. The teammates who carried him are the dead crew now, a team of which he is the only living member, a season that exists only inside his sleep. And in that sleep he calls a name that is not his wife's. Jacqueline. Jacqueline White, the girl cheerleader who cheered when he was captain, the best girl athlete he ever knew, the ice-skater who could skate like a comet, and who at this very hour is breathing her last, or is already being carried up the hill by her pallbearers to her grave.

Radical Immanence holds that the past does not go away, that the dead go on living inside the present and shaping it. Homer is that idea worn as a whole man. The question the book asks through him is not why he sleeps, but what becomes of a person whose life is entirely behind him, whose only real season is a finished one. The answer is that he does not live in the present at all. He lives in the game that ended and the love that never started, and the two together have swallowed everything a present might have held.

His glory is complete and gone, and he cannot leave it. He is the all-star athlete of the past, and the word past has eaten the rest of him. He kicked the ball clear over the stars once, because Jacqueline was cheering, and he has been kicking at that same vanished ball ever since, on practice fields that exist only in his dreams, always the wrong ball, never any goal. A man can be finished while his heart still beats, and Homer is the plainest case in the book: carried forever on the shoulders of a dead crew, captain of a team with no other living member, replaying a season the world stopped watching years ago.

The reason the past will not release him is that the one thing at the center of it was never spoken. He loved Jacqueline and was too shy to say it, was helpless in his love and never once put it into words. No one ever read Jacqueline's mind, and he never let her read his. An unspoken love is the one kind that cannot end, because it had never begun, never answered, never refused, never lived out and used up. It stays open forever, a door no one walked through, and it surfaces now only the way such things surface, as a name cried out in sleep to a woman who is dying and who scarcely remembers him. What Homer never said is exactly what will not pass. The silence that failed him at the one moment that mattered fixed the love in amber, and a love in amber does not age or die. It only waits, in the dark under the deep sleep, and speaks the one word it knows.

He keeps Jacqueline alive by refusing to believe she can truly die. He believes in the best and not the worst, believes in immortality, insists that what he loved was never the body but something the grave cannot reach. Where Madge, bitter at his side, says the bridegroom is the skeleton, that death is the plain end of the flesh, Homer holds the opposite, that the flesh is nothing and the essence goes on. His faith is not wisdom. It is the same refusal to let the dead be gone that runs through the whole book, through Spitzer's cheroots and Catherine's imaginary guests and O'Leary's ghost sister, and Homer performs it in the simplest, most trusting form of all, a boy's belief that the girl he never won is somewhere still skating, that no one he loved is ever really lost. The book neither mocks the belief nor confirms it. It lays the belief down beside Madge's bitterness and lets the reader feel that both are ways of standing the same unbearable fact, that the ones a person loves go into the ground.

Homer and Madge are another example of exact opposites, like Spitzer and Catherine. Madge is all bitter surface and body and the single lurching present moment, unable to escape her own flesh. Homer is all faith and dream and a finished past, never once inside his present. She is awake and raging; he is asleep and serene. Between them they chart the two ways of missing the here-and-now that the whole long bus journey is trying to reach, the one trapped in the body's instant with no future ahead of it, the other gone off into a season that is over. The sleeping couple on the bus, built out of two strangers' faces, turn out to be a small double portrait of the very split the novel is built on.

The lesson for a writer is in how much can be carried by a character who never wakes. Homer speaks not one waking word and performs not one waking act, and he is a complete and moving man all the same, because a person can be built entirely out of what lies behind him and what he could not bring himself to say. Grief for a dead love, glory that has ended, a sentence never spoken: these are not the leftovers of a life. They are a life, and a writer who understands that can make a whole human being out of a man asleep in a bus seat with a name on his lips. Homer is finished before the book begins, and the book loses nothing by it, because here the finished and the unspoken are not the absence of a story. They are the story.
