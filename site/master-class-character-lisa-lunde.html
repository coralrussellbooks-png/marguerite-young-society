Lisa Lunde: All A's Are C's

← Mrs. Hogden · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Esther Longtree →

Spoiler note: This portrait is written for readers who have finished the novel. It discusses the character’s whole story, including how it ends.

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Lisa Lunde · Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Res Tacamah · Mr. Weed · Mrs. Hogden · Vera Cartwheel

The young woman who takes Vera's place, once Vera has packed a suitcase and gone, is Lisa Lunde, "my mother's young companion who took my place when, carrying a large suitcase, I escaped from my mother's house." She is the replacement, the next daughter hired to sit beside the mad mother and hold the household to some measure of sense. She is also a would-be physicist, an amateur scientist working for a living, and she keeps her own mad mother fifteen miles away in a telescope tenement. All through the Mrs. Hogden episode she stands exactly where the book likes to put people, between two versions of the real, and her whole character is a study of what a person does with that position when she has decided in advance what is true.

She is Vera's mirror, and the mirror is exact. Lisa Lunde works "to help support her mother who was poor and mad and dreamed that she was rich, even as my mother dreamed that she was poor." Two daughters, two lost mothers, one dreaming up and one dreaming down, and the sentence hinges them together on the words so that you cannot pull them apart. The dreamer's daughter and the debunker's daughter are the same daughter twice. This is why the confusions gather around her. Mrs. Hogden takes Lisa Lunde "to be the daughter of the house," and at the end, when Vera stands in the deserted playroom looking for whoever passed through, the message left behind was meant for her because Mrs. Hogden "mistakenly supposed" she "was Lisa Lunde." The replacement and the replaced trade names without anyone deciding to.

What she is proud of is seeing through things. She "prided herself on seeing immediately through Mrs. Hogden's pretensions." She is the one in the house who names the drug bottle a drug bottle. When Mrs. Hogden asks after Mr. Res Tacamah, the shy gentleman who will share the arithmetic lessons, Lisa Lunde shouts, "with cruel distinctness, determined to shatter all illusions at one blow," that Res Tacamah "is nothing but a drug bottle." She has the reductionist's good ear for the deflating line. To the mother's soft "a rose by any other name would smell as sweet," she answers, "Would smell as strong." She corrects, she classifies, she debunks. She is the consensus-reality officer of a house that has stopped keeping consensus reality, and she does the job with relish.

But the book puts her in the one job where seeing through is exactly the wrong skill, and watches her fail at it. Her real work in the episode is messenger. She carries the argument back and forth between the mother, who will not come out, and Mrs. Hogden, who will not go home, and she carries it badly on purpose. She "ran back and forth with meaningless messages between the two, and she would do nothing to interpret either character to the other." Handed the exact position of the witness, the go-between who could translate one soul to another, she empties it. She grows "revengeful," and she "began to enjoy the embarrassing situation." Where Mr. Weed is the anti-witness who never once meets the person he kills, Lisa Lunde is the anti-witness who stands directly between two people and refuses to carry either one across. Weed arranged never to look. Lisa Lunde looks, and declines to say what she sees.

Here the book turns her own pride against her. The mother's verdict on Lisa Lunde, relayed through Mrs. Hogden, is that she is "a day-dreamer," that "you never notice anything." The would-be physicist, the one who prides herself on seeing through everyone, is told she is the one who does not see, and the book means it. To see through Mrs. Hogden, to file her as a shoplifter and name her drug bottle, is to not see Mrs. Hogden at all, and Mrs. Hogden is precisely the figure who cannot be filed. She was "hard to classify," "something that evaded definition," a woman it would "take a life time" to pick up off the floor. Seeing through a person is not seeing her. Naming the thing "at one blow" is the opposite of staying in the room with it. Even Lisa Lunde's facts are guesses wearing lab coats: told that James went to Alaska, she shouts a date, "merely guessing at the time," and calls it knowledge.

Then the blackboard. Substitution is the law Lisa Lunde lives by, the physicist's clean confidence that "you could substitute one thing for another and get the same result. Apples, buttons, eggs." Mrs. Hogden believes the same thing about arithmetic, and when she leaves she chalks the law itself onto the little three-legged blackboard, a syllogism: "All a's are b's … all b's are c's … and therefore, dear heart, all a's are c's. You know me." In a physics notebook that is valid and tidy and true. In this house it is the horror. The transitive law that lets a stand for c is the same law by which Lisa Lunde stands for Vera, Mrs. Hogden stands for Miss MacIntosh and for a dead Cousin Hannah, and one Mr. Spitzer stands for another. The debunker's most trusted tool is the very thing dissolving the outlines of everyone, her own included. She thinks substitution is how you reason your way to the truth. The novel shows it is how a self loses its edges. "You know me," the blackboard says, and it is right. The syllogism knows Lisa Lunde better than she knows it, because she is one of its terms.

This is where she matters most against the philosophy the book keeps testing. Its failures are never the ones who suffer. They are the ones who "stop paying honest attention," who "replace real complexity with a simpler story they can manage." That is Lisa Lunde's whole method, raised to a virtue and called science. She meets the least sortable person in the house and spends her strength trying to sort her at one blow. She is handed the crossing point, the place where one life could be carried across to another, and she runs meaningless messages through it and enjoys the gap she keeps open.

The turn for a writer is the sharpest one in the set, because Lisa Lunde is not the writer's enemy but the writer's easiest self. She is the debunker at the desk, the one who reduces the strange character to "nothing but a drug bottle" and calls the reduction clarity, who guesses at a fact and calls it research, who stands between two invented people and carries messages without ever translating either into the other. It is the tidy way to work, and it feels like intelligence, and it is the exact refusal the book keeps naming. Set it beside the other kind of making, the kind that stays with the person who evades definition and does not shatter her at one blow. The novel lets Lisa Lunde have her sharp lines and her clean law, and then it lets her fade out of the playroom under someone else's name, taken for the woman she replaced, while the message on the blackboard spells out, in red chalk, the law she trusted and never once turned on herself. All a's are c's. She knew everyone. She never noticed she was one of them.
