Rosemary Longtree: The Perfect Child Who Was Never Born

← Esther Longtree · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Walter →

Characters: Esther Longtree · Rosemary

She is the most loved person in the Longtree house, and she was never alive for a single minute. Rosemary was Esther Longtree's twin, "stillborn," while "Esther lived, a reproach to her mother." From that first morning, when the mother "had not known there were two until the first one came and was laid out upon a tiny lace pillow," Rosemary became the daughter the mother wanted and Esther became the one she got. Rosemary is built entirely out of the mother's speech. She exists as the one "of whom her mother had spoken ever since Esther Longtree's earliest memory," and everything about her is perfect because nothing about her was ever tested by being real. "Rosemary's hair was golden, soft as moth's silk, and Rosemary's little feet were perfectly formed." She "would have grown to perfection and would have been a daughter to lean upon." She is the one "who had grown not but who would have been perfect."

Grief is the engine here, and the philosophy's words for it are exact: grief does not resolve, "it accumulates," "you carry it." But the mother carries it in the worst possible way, by turning it into a weapon against the child who lived. She has decided that Esther killed her twin in the womb, that she "kicked Rosemary out into the cold winds, the snows, the starlight, straight out of the womb into the grave," and she says so, over and over, all of Esther's life. The stillborn daughter becomes the standard the living daughter can never meet, because the dead do not track mud on the parlor floor or break the furniture or "smell to high heaven." Rosemary would have been considerate, would have learned her lessons "quick as a flash," would never have talked back. She is perfect by the simple mechanism of never having had to be anything at all.

The cruelest turn is that no one can say for certain which twin was the shadow. The mother tells it so that Esther "had seemed the shadow herself until she was born dead and killed by Esther," and the words fold back on themselves until the living daughter and the dead one trade places, "one seeming a shadow or a reflection." Esther grows up believing her "soul was the soul of Rosemary," half convinced that the real child died and the wrong one lived. She digs in the ground "for Rosemary with an old, earth-colored spoon," under one pear tree and another, because the mother will never say which pear tree holds the grave, "there being so many now, so many little pear trees." She turns up thimbles and wooden beads and a teething ring and "a long gold hair," the buried evidence of a child who may be her sister or may be her own first stillborn, and she cannot tell them apart. The alphabet book she digs out of the ground spells the whole thing: "M for murder, murder, murder, M for mother."

This is where Rosemary explains Esther, and the book knows it. A woman raised to believe she murdered the perfect child in the womb, and that the perfect child is buried somewhere in the ground waiting to be found, will spend her life trying to give birth to her, to undo the first death, to at last deliver the golden daughter who lived. Esther's everlasting pregnancy is the attempt to produce Rosemary. Every stillborn is another failure to bring the first one back. The mother built a flawless dead child out of words and hung her over the living one, and the living one has been trying ever since to make the words come true.

The turn for a writer is the one that stings most, because every writer keeps a Rosemary. She is the perfect version, the flawless thing that exists only in the telling, the book that "would have been perfect" and shames every actual page. She costs nothing and she ruins everything, because she never had to survive contact with the world, never tracked mud, never broke the furniture, never smelled to high heaven, and so she wins every comparison with the real, mud-tracking, furniture-breaking work actually made. She is built entirely of speech, of would have, and she is more beloved than anything finished. Set her beside Esther, the living daughter, big and awkward and bruised and alive, and the book's whole moral is in which one the mother chose. To love Rosemary is to love the thing that was never born over the thing that is. It is the writer's oldest cruelty, turned inward, and the book lets Esther dig for the golden child under all the little pear trees, forever, spoon in hand, looking for the sister she is accused of killing and may only have dreamed.
