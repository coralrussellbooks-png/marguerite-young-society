Definition from research

Chapter 5

  • Jezebel is in the Book of Kings in the Hebrew Bible. She is known for violently purging the prophets of God from Israel and instituting the worship of Baal and Asherah. She was later put to death by defenestration or being thrown from a high place or window. In the Book of Revelations she is symbolically associated with false prophets. (Wikipedia) "Jezebel" in this context represents a symbol of moral judgment and inner conflict within the bus-driver's troubled psyche during a moment of dark reflection and suffering.

Chapter 5

  • Gabriel is an archangel first described in the Hebrew bible. The reference to Gabriel serves to mark the bus journey as not merely physical but spiritual, underscoring the novel's exploration of mortality, transcendence, and the search for meaning beyond the material world.

Chapter 7

  • “old angel Gabriel blowing his tin horn”: Gabriel is an archangel in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. Gabriel announces the births of John the Baptist and of Jesus (Luke 1:19, 26), fit company for a doctor of births. The Bible does not name the angel of the last trumpet; the spiritual “Blow Your Trumpet, Gabriel,” printed in Slave Songs of the United States (1867), gives it to Gabriel.

Chapter 7

  • Jahaziel is the name of five characters in the Hebrew Bible.

Chapter 7

  • Mediator is someone who acts as an intermediary, a go-between, or a peacemaker between two or more parties, particularly between God and humanity. Jesus Christ is the ultimate mediator, reconciling humanity with God through his sacrifice and subsequent exaltation. Easton’s Bible Dictionary (1897) defines a mediator as “one who intervenes between two persons who are at variance, with a view to reconcile them,” and 1 Timothy 2:5 reads, “For there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus.” (Galatians 3:19; 1 Timothy 2:5; Hebrews 8:6, 9:15, 12:24).

Chapter 10

  • “the rites for the burial of the dead”: Funeral rites originated from Jewish and Roman customs. The Order for the Burial of the Dead in the Book of Common Prayer is the service the preacher slides into.

Chapter 10

  • “the dreadful Day of Judgment”: The Day of Judgment belongs to eschatology, the part of theology that deals with the ultimate destiny of humans. It is found in Judaism, Christianity, Islam, and Zoroastrianism. The golden thrones and “the sea shall turn to glass” come from Revelation 4:4-6: “a sea of glass like unto crystal.”

Chapter 20

  • “the digit or fingerbreadth, the tophach or handbreadth, the cubit, the fathom, the reed, the foot”: Miss MacIntosh’s lessons follow the tables of measures, weights, months, and Exodus stations printed in the back of many old Bibles.
  • “the value of the Attic drachma, the gerah, the beka, the maneh, the talent”: The coins and weights of the Greeks and Hebrews, part of humanity’s effort to impose order and meaning on life.
  • “I would learn the Jewish calendar”: The Jewish calendar is a lunisolar calendar, which means it’s based on both the moon and the sun. It has 12 months, with each month containing either 29 or 30 days. To keep it in step with the solar year, a leap month is added seven times in every 19 years, the Metonic cycle. The months are Nisan, Iyar, Sivan, Tammuz, Av, Elul, Tishrei, Heshvan, Kislev, Tevet, Shevat, and Adar. In leap years, Adar becomes Adar II and another month, Adar I, is added before it. (Wikipedia) The civil year begins on Rosh Hashanah, but Nisan is the first month of the religious year: “This month shall be unto you the beginning of months” (Exodus 12:2). “Jyar” is an old spelling of Iyar. The calendar’s structure ensures that Jewish holidays take place in the correct season. It is significant as a motif of disciplined learning, historical consciousness, and the search for order in a world otherwise marked by instability and dreamlike disorientation.

Chapter 22

  • “What cared she for Issachar, Aaron, Absalom, Togarmah?”: The significance of these names is to situate the novel’s meditation on mortality, loss, and the passing of time within a biblical and mythic framework, underscoring the themes of decay and the search for meaning in the face of inevitable death.

Chapter 29

  • “the Happy Isles, the Fortunate Isles, the Isles of the Blessed”: The extensive listing of names and ideas about heaven serves several significant purposes: 1) the list reflects the vast and multifaceted nature of heaven as imagined through various cultural, religious, and mythical lenses. By naming places like the Happy Isles, the Fortunate Isles, Valhalla, Nirvana, and others, the text emphasizes the multiplicity of human beliefs about the afterlife and the unknowability of it. This underscores the novel’s ongoing exploration of the ambiguity between life and death, reality and illusion. 2) Vera, through Mr. Spitzer’s reflections, expresses a profound philosophical quest for understanding what lies beyond this life. The enumeration symbolizes the human desire to categorize and comprehend the afterlife, yet simultaneously reveals the inadequacy of all such attempts since “God was not God in Himself” but only a series of images or ideas. This points to the futility and mystery in trying to grasp the ultimate reality through human concepts. 3) it suggests that what people conceive as heaven might be no more than “pitiful images” or “phantoms fading on the air,” reinforcing the theme that both life and what lies beyond are interwoven with illusion and uncertainty. It reflects the novel’s concern with the elusive nature of truth and the limits of human knowledge. 4) the list also serves to reveal Mr. Spitzer’s mindset as a character deeply engaged in intellectual and spiritual inquiry but who remains uncertain and somewhat resigned about the nature of existence and the afterlife. His reflections convey both his yearning for certainty and his acceptance of ambiguity, thus enriching his role as the contemplative witness to the novel’s events. It is significant because it encapsulates the novel’s central themes of mystery, the human quest for meaning, and the interplay of illusion and reality, while deepening the characterization of Mr. Spitzer and setting a contemplative tone about the afterlife and existence beyond death.
  • “God, it had been said, was the True Vine”: The list of ways that refer to God and/or Jesus holds deep symbolic and thematic significance. It encapsulates the multifaceted nature of the divine as understood within Christian theology and mysticism. The significance of this enumeration lies in its portrayal of God/Jesus as an entity with many aspects and attributes, each reflecting different dimensions of spiritual truth and human experience. It emphasizes the complexity and paradox inherent in the divine nature: simultaneously merciful and just, immanent and transcendent, suffering and triumphant. This multiplicity mirrors the novel’s broader themes of ambiguity, multiplicity of selves, and the intertwining of life and death. The list serves to underscore the novel’s exploration of spiritual reality versus human perception. The divine is depicted not as a singular, easily definable being but as a presence that manifests in diverse and sometimes contradictory ways, much like the fragmented identities and overlapping realities experienced by the characters such as Mr. Spitzer and Vera’s mother, Catherine. This reflects the novel’s preoccupation with the elusive nature of truth, the complexity of existence, and the blurred boundaries between reality and illusion. By invoking these various titles, the text taps into a rich tradition of Christian symbolism, inviting readers to contemplate the depth of spiritual meaning behind everyday experiences and the mysterious workings of grace, suffering, and redemption. It situates the characters’ personal struggles within a cosmic framework, suggesting that their lives are part of a larger divine narrative that encompasses both the mortal and the eternal. This list also contrasts with the practical, grounded character of Miss MacIntosh, who embodies common sense and reality, as opposed to the more mystical and dreamlike experiences of other characters. The divine names remind us of the novel’s tension between the earthly and the spiritual, the seen and the unseen, the real and the illusory. The list is a poetic and theological meditation on the nature of divinity, serving as a thematic focal point that enriches the novel’s exploration of identity, faith, and the search for meaning amidst uncertainty and paradox.

Chapter 31

  • “draped by veils of black chiffon in memory of some earlier death”: Mirrors were covered in houses of mourning, in Victorian custom and in the Jewish shiva.

Chapter 33

  • “except that the door was closed”: The Parable of the Ten Virgins (Matthew 25:1-13). It symbolizes spiritual readiness, and those that are able to enter the wedding feast. This imagery is rooted in Jewish wedding customs, where the bridegroom’s arrival was a central event, and the bride and her attendants would go out to meet him. This parable underscores the tension between those who are prepared to meet reality and those who remain caught in illusions or delay facing their fate. It reflects the urgency of spiritual and moral preparedness amid the uncertainty of life and the inevitability of death. The waiting virgins symbolize individuals poised between hope and despair, life and death, dream and awakening. It highlights the novel’s exploration of human mortality, the fleeting nature of life, and the necessity of readiness for the “bridegroom” or the final reckoning, a theme echoed throughout Vera’s journey and the experiences of the other characters. The bridegroom came, “and the door was shut” (Matthew 25:10).

Chapter 34

  • “a seven-branched crystal candelabra”: The menorah of the Tabernacle had seven branches (Exodus 25:31-37).

Chapter 36

  • “Had she not ridden from Minsk to Pinsk”: “From Minsk to Pinsk” is a common trope in classic Jewish humor, used to evoke a long, arduous, or circular journey within the Russian Empire. It is less a single specific joke between two businessmen than a setting for numerous different stories, often featuring Jewish peddlers or paupers and other travelers. It refers to a journey or passage between two places that symbolize a vast and difficult journey through strange or foreign lands. Minsk and Pinsk are actual cities in Eastern Europe, located in Belarus, and the phrase evokes a sense of traveling through a remote, challenging, or even mysterious landscape.
  • Seven heavens are named in the Talmud (Chagigah 12b), and in Islamic tradition Muhammad climbed through the seven heavens on the Night Journey, the Mi’raj, a word that means ladder.

Chapter 38

  • “seven deathless eternities”: “Seven deathless eternities” may recall Dante’s Divine Comedy, where the seven terraces of Purgatory purge the seven deadly sins. Seven heavens are found in Judaism and Islam, and Hindu cosmology has seven upper worlds, the lokas. The seven virtues join the four cardinal virtues of Greek philosophy to the three theological virtues of Paul (1 Corinthians 13:13). It can be understood as representing the eternal, unchanging spiritual realms or dimensions, where the soul and spirit exist beyond physical mortality and temporal constraints. It highlights the novel’s recurring theme of the interplay between mortal life, death, and the possibility of immortal spiritual existence.

Chapter 39

  • “wandering Jews”: Wandering Jew is a mythical, immortal man whose legend began to spread in Europe in the 13th century. Young explained her interpretation in the essay “The Artist as Wanderer” (Marguerite Young, Inviting the Muses: Stories, Essays, Reviews, Dalkey Archive Press, 1994), where to her this represents the external and internal wandering of an artist.

Chapter 40

  • “that she listened for hours to Ahaseuris, the Wandering Jew”: Ahaseuris or Ahasuerus is the name of a king, the husband of Esther, in the Book of Esther from the Bible. A German pamphlet of 1602 gave the same name to the Wandering Jew, and Young follows it.
  • “as she preferred to think of him, the Everlasting Jew”: Der ewige Jude, the Everlasting Jew, is the German name of the Wandering Jew. The Everlasting Jew is also the title of a modern collection of the writings of Rabbi Isaac Lichtenstein (1825-1908), ed. D. Thomas Lancaster (First Fruits of Zion, 2013). Lichtenstein who after an encounter with the Gospel became an outspoken disciple of Yeshua (Jesus) of Nazareth, but he remained in his post as district rabbi for several years. Despite pressure from rabbinic authorities, Jewish believers, and Christian leaders, he could not be compelled to renounce Judaism or his devotion to the Torah. He is regarded as a pioneer of the Messianic Jewish movement. The significance lies in the metaphorical portrayal of a timeless, wandering consciousness that parallels the novel’s motifs of loss, memory, and the search for self amid shifting, ambiguous realities.

Chapter 44

  • “Judas cock”: The Judas cock may refer to the Book of the Cock/Rooster which was probably written in the 400s or 500s in Arabic or Greek and used by Ethiopian Christians who have and use the text during Holy Week activities. It describes the final three days of Jesus’s life, including a sequence where he reanimated a previously roasted rooster that He sent to spy on Judas Iscariot when he (Judas) was plotting to betray Jesus. The rooster informed Jesus and his disciples of the plan. Among other events, the Book of the Rooster describes Saul of Tarsus’ participation in the arrest, abuse and crucifixion of Jesus, the betrayal of Jesus by a female relative of Judas, a debate between Pilate and Herod about the culpability of Jesus, Mary’s grief, John the Evangelist witnessing the crucifixion of Jesus, the conversations Jesus had with the two thieves he was crucified alongside Gestas (Awsēmobyā) and Demas (Salikonilidākki) and references to other miracles that Jesus performed during his life. The rooster may be based on the Ziz of Jewish mythology, a giant bird. Like the Gospel of Judas from the second century, the Book of the Rooster attempts to explain the betrayal of Judas before the Last Supper, an act which is traditionally seen as abrupt or lacking reason (Wikipedia).

Chapter 46

  • “the mirrors were rheumed with frost”: In Jewish mourning, and in many Victorian houses, the mirrors were covered after a death.

Chapter 47

  • “some small Moses in his mollusk boat”: “And when she could not longer hide him, she took for him an ark of bulrushes… and she laid it in the flags by the river’s brink” (Exodus 2:3). Moses is an important prophet in Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Druze faith, Baha’i faith and other Abrahamic religions. He is significant as a liminal figure embodying the journey through mortality and the blurred boundaries between life and death, guiding both the living and the reader through the novel’s complex interplay of reality, memory, and dream.

Chapter 49

  • “But why should any man weep if the heavens wept for him?”: In the Bible God grieves and Jesus weeps: “it grieved him at his heart” (Genesis 6:6); “Oh that my head were waters, and mine eyes a fountain of tears” (Jeremiah 9:1); “Jesus wept” (John 11:35). In the Book of Moses of the Latter-day Saints, “the God of heaven looked upon the residue of the people, and he wept” (Moses 7:28). The Quran says of Pharaoh’s drowned people, “And the heaven and the earth wept not for them” (44:29, trans. Marmaduke Pickthall, 1930), and the Egyptians said the Nile rose with the tears of Isis for Osiris (Pausanias, Description of Greece 10.32.18). If the heavens themselves mourn a person’s fate, the individual weeping might seem redundant or insignificant in comparison: cosmic forces acknowledge and encompass human sorrow on a scale greater than any one person could express. Mourning rises beyond the personal, and personal sorrow is enveloped in the universal cycles of life and death.

Chapter 51

  • “Perhaps he was twelve cloaks”: Mr. Spitzer cannot define himself; he shifts between roles and images. Twelve cloaks suggest concealment, disguise, and many layers of identity, the number perhaps recalling the twelve apostles or the twelve signs of the zodiac. The islands of flowers in the flood are beauty and fragility amid chaos, isolated havens like the pockets of meaning he makes in a world of flux. The stalled sail is stagnation, the sail breaking into flame sudden and violent change. The ram’s horn, the shofar, is a call to awakening, remembrance, and the divine, and “metamorphosed” makes him not a fixed entity but a being always changing.
  • “A flaw in the creation had permitted this creation”: In the Kabbalah of Isaac Luria (sixteenth century), the vessels that held God’s light shattered at the creation, the shevirat ha-kelim, the breaking of the vessels, so that the world was made through a break. Mr. Spitzer’s errors “had never evolved from an original sin,” Augustine’s doctrine of the sin inherited from Adam.

Chapter 53

  • This may refer to Genesis 32 that describes Jacob wrestling with a “man” throughout the night. In the Book of Hosea, this figure is explicitly called an “angel.” During the struggle, Jacob is injured, specifically having the socket of his hip put out of joint. This injury left him with a limp. Jacob is considered a foundational figure in Judaism and the patriarch from whom the nation of Israel descends. “Yea, he had power over the angel, and prevailed: he wept, and made supplication unto him” (Hosea 12:4, KJV).

Chapter 57

  • “for the table was always set for the wanderer”: Compare the old custom of setting an extra place at the table for the unexpected guest or wanderer, kept in Poland on Christmas Eve, and the cup poured for the prophet Elijah at the Passover seder.

Chapter 61

  • “like a cabalistic language few could understand”: The Kabbalah is the Jewish mystical tradition that reads hidden meanings in the letters and numbers of Scripture; “cabalistic” came to mean secret and hard to understand.

Chapter 65

  • “the star of the dirty Jew”: “Dirty Jew” is a widely recognized and offensive antisemitic slur with historical roots in hate speech and discrimination. The term is a standard antisemitic taunt used in real-world attacks and verbal abuse against Jewish people. During the Holocaust, the yellow Star of David identification marker that Jews were forced to wear encouraged people to use slurs like “dirty Jew” and to harass them. The phrase draws on ancient anti-Jewish themes that falsely associated Jews with filth or spreading disease. Miss MacIntosh objects, “quite flatly,” that the Jews should not be singled out.
  • In Chicago the Jewish neighborhood around Maxwell Street on the Near West Side was known from the 1880s as the ghetto.

Chapter 66

  • “For otherwise, where would they go?”: At the Évian Conference of July 1938, thirty-two nations met over the Jewish refugees fleeing Nazi Germany, and nearly all refused to take more. In 1939 the ship St. Louis, carrying 937 passengers, most of them Jewish refugees, was turned away by Cuba, the United States, and Canada.
  • Peter Stuyvesant, the Dutch director-general of New Amsterdam, is buried there (1672). In 1654 he tried to turn away twenty-three Jewish refugees from Brazil, and the Dutch West India Company overruled him; he also held enslaved Africans.

Chapter 70

  • “one of those European refugees who had come from a town filled with storks”: There are “Stork Villages” such as: Ruhstadt, Germany, Zywkowo, Poland, Pentowo, Poland, Cigoc, Croatia, Alsace France, and Trujillo, Spain. These villages may also refer to the nearly 1000 Jewish Holocaust survivors brought to Fort Ontario in Oswego, New York by President Roosevelt in 1944 as America’s only World War II refugee shelter, known as “guests” offering a temporary sanctuary from Nazi Europe before World War II ended. They lived behind barbed wire, couldn’t work, and had to promise to return to Europe after the war, though most eventually stayed in the US.
  • “It would admit both Jews and Negroes”: Catherine’s hotel “would admit both Jews and Negroes.” Jews and African Americans could not freely stay in hotels in the US until the passage of federal and state laws that banned discrimination in public accommodations, which was enforced with the Civil Rights Act of 1964. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 was a landmark piece of legislation that explicitly prohibited racial discrimination in public places, such as hotels, and this was the first time that Jewish people were also protected from discrimination in public places. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibited discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin in public accommodations like hotels, restaurants, and theaters. In the pre-1964 era, many hotels openly discriminated against both African Americans and Jews, often barring them from the premises entirely or requiring them to stay in separate, inferior accommodations. This was particularly true in the South, where discriminatory practices were widespread, even after the Civil War. In 1877 the banker Joseph Seligman was turned away from the Grand Union Hotel at Saratoga Springs because he was a Jew, and resorts advertised themselves as “restricted.” From 1936 Black travelers carried Victor Hugo Green’s Negro Motorist Green Book to find where they could sleep.

Chapter 71

  • “The other doctor, Simeon”: Simeon is derived from Simeon, son of Jacob and Leah, patriarch of the Tribe of Simeon. The text of Genesis 29:33 argues that the name refers to Leah’s belief that God had heard that she was hated by Jacob, in the sense of not being as favored as Rachel. “Because the LORD hath heard that I was hated, he hath therefore given me this son also: and she called his name Simeon” (Genesis 29:33, KJV). Thousands of refugee doctors fled Nazi Europe for the United States, and many were kept from practice by state licensing rules and by antisemitism.
  • “Hitler ran him out of Germany”: Adolf Hitler was an Austrian-born German politician who was the dictator of Germany from 1933 until his death in 1945. He rose to power as the leader of the Nazi Party. During his dictatorship, he initiated World War II in Europe by invading Poland on 1 September 1939. He was closely involved in military operations throughout the war and was central to the perpetration of the Holocaust, the genocide of about six million Jews and millions of other victims. (Wikipedia)

Chapter 72

  • In his last years Luther turned on the Jews in On the Jews and Their Lies (1543), a tract the Nazis reprinted.

Chapter 73

  • “worse than the Chaldeans”: In the Bible the Chaldeans are the Babylonians of Nebuchadnezzar, who burned the Temple and carried the people of Judah into captivity (2 Kings 25). Habakkuk calls them “that bitter and hasty nation” (Habakkuk 1:6, KJV), and in Daniel they are the king’s astrologers: “the magicians, and the astrologers, and the sorcerers, and the Chaldeans” (Daniel 2:2, KJV). For a Jewish fighter, no people could be worse.
  • “the brave little Jew, hard as rock”: Jewish fighters ruled American boxing in the first half of the twentieth century: twenty-six Jewish world champions between 1910 and 1940, most of them wearing the Star of David on their trunks. Abe Attell held the featherweight title from 1906 to 1912 (he first claimed it in 1901) and was later tied to the fixing of the 1919 World Series. Benny Leonard was lightweight champion from 1917 to 1925, and Barney Ross, raised in Chicago, held titles in the 1930s.
  • Doctors sent consumptives west for the dry, high air, and Denver filled with them. National Jewish Hospital for Consumptives opened there in 1899 under the motto “None who enter shall pay; none who can pay shall enter,” and the Jewish Consumptives’ Relief Society opened at Lakewood in 1904.
  • “a fighter in the Jewish family, beginning with those who had fought the angels”: “And Jacob was left alone; and there wrestled a man with him until the breaking of the day” (Genesis 32:24, KJV). The man renamed him Israel, “for as a prince hast thou power with God and with men, and hast prevailed” (Genesis 32:28, KJV); Hosea says “he had power over the angel, and prevailed” (Hosea 12:4, KJV). Joe’s last bout, with God on a peak above the clouds, is Jacob’s, and he is losing it.

Chapter 74

  • “to be let alone outside the pale of law”: The Pale was the English-ruled ground around Dublin; to be beyond the pale was to be outside the law and its protection. The Pale of Settlement (1791 to 1917) was the land at the western edge of the Russian Empire where Jews were forced to live.
  • “a shroud with many, many pockets”: “Shrouds have no pockets”: what is hidden in life cannot be carried into death. An Italian proverb in English print since 1854, it is also a Yiddish saying.

Chapter 77

  • “the Song of Songs of the sterility which is the creation”: The Song of Songs is an erotic poem. It is unique within the Hebrew Bible in that it celebrates sexual love. The two are in harmony, each desiring the other and rejoicing in sexual intimacy. Jewish tradition reads it as an allegory of the relationship between God and Israel In Christianity, it is treated as an allegory celebrating the love between God and Church or Christ and his bride, the Church. The Christian church’s interpretation of the Song is evidence of God’s love for his people, both collectively and individually. Over time a moral element and the bride as Virgin Mary were added. It is unique in that these conclusions are not found in the overtly theological books of the Bible. Those books reveal an imbalance in the relationship between God and man, but Song of Songs is a theological metaphor in which the two partners are equals, bound in a committed relationship. In modern times the poem has attracted the attention of feminist biblical critics, with Phyllis Trible’s “Depatriarchalizing in Biblical Interpretation” (1973) and the Feminist Companion to the Bible series edited by Athalya Brenner and Carole Fontaine.

Chapter 79

  • “This third person, this dark woman”: A “dark woman” who assisted with births may refer to the following: 1) the Biblical midwife Shiphrah, one of the “Hebrew midwives” of Exodus 1:15, though some readers have taken her and Puah for Egyptians. Shiphrah, along with her colleague Puah, defied the Pharaoh’s order to kill newborn Hebrew males. 2) Biddy Mason, who was born into slavery, was a midwife and advocate for other enslaved people in 19th-century California. After gaining her freedom, she became a successful entrepreneur and philanthropist. 3) Margaret Charles Smith was an African American midwife in Alabama who delivered over 3,000 babies during a career spanning over 30 years, often in rural areas with limited healthcare access. 4) Mary Francis Hill Coley was a midwife from Georgia who delivered over 3,000 babies during her career. Her work was featured in the 1952 documentary, All My Babies. 5) Susie Carey was a third-generation midwife featured in a photograph at the National Museum of African American History and Culture. 6) Sarah Hudson Grant was a Black Southern midwife whose work is remembered today.

Chapter 81

  • The whole human race “turned into smoke” may also call up the crematoria of the death camps, where six million Jews were murdered.
  • The concept of a “Jewish conspiracy” is an antisemitic myth with roots in medieval Europe that became the foundation for modern political antisemitism. These conspiracy theories falsely allege that Jewish people secretly manipulate institutions, banks, the media, and governments for malevolent purposes, such as achieving world domination. It is a dangerous and debunked fiction used throughout history to justify discrimination, persecution, and violence against Jews. In the Middle Ages, hostility toward Jews was often rooted in religious prejudice and false accusations, which served as a basis for later conspiracy myths. The antisemitic myth of blood libel emerged in the 12th century with the story of William of Norwich. It falsely claimed that Jews ritually murdered Christian children to use their blood for religious rituals, particularly for Passover matzah. This accusation frequently led to violent massacres and expulsions of Jewish communities. During the Black Death in the mid-14th century, Jews were used as scapegoats and falsely accused of poisoning wells to cause the plague. This led to the destruction of hundreds of Jewish communities across Europe. Due to restrictions that prevented Jews from owning land or joining certain trades, many worked as moneylenders. This created resentment and gave rise to stereotypes of Jews as greedy and obsessed with money, which later fueled secular conspiracy theories about Jewish wealth and control. In the 19th century, antisemitic ideas evolved from religious prejudice into modern political and racial theories of antisemitism, giving rise to more elaborate conspiracy narratives. German novelist Hermann Goedsche published Biarritz, which featured a fictional scene of a secret rabbinical cabal meeting in a Jewish cemetery to discuss a plot for world domination. This work was a commercial success and was later plagiarized to create The Protocols of the Elders of Zion (circa 1903), this fabricated document is the most infamous antisemitic text. It was a forgery created by the Russian secret police to stir up antisemitism and deflect from public discontent with the Tsarist regime. The Protocols claim to be the minutes of secret meetings held by Jewish leaders outlining a plan to take over the world. Though repeatedly exposed as a hoax, it continues to circulate and influence conspiracy theories to this day. Antisemitic conspiracy myths played a central role in the rise of Nazism and the Holocaust. Adolf Hitler and the Nazi Party adopted the Protocols as proof of a “Jewish world conspiracy.” They used it to justify their persecution and extermination of six million Jews. They falsely claimed that Jews were behind communism, capitalism, and Germany’s loss in World War I. The American industrialist Henry Ford distributed massive quantities of antisemitic material, including articles based on the Protocols, through his newspaper, The Dearborn Independent. He later retracted and apologized for his publications following a lawsuit, but the damage was already done. Following the Russian Revolution, an antisemitic conspiracy theory known as “Jewish Bolshevism” or Żydokomuna claimed that Jews controlled the Soviet Union and international communist movements. This false notion was promoted by anti-communist factions and heavily used by Nazi propaganda. In the modern era, antisemitic conspiracy theories have adapted to contemporary fears and platforms, continuing to spread hatred. “ZOG” theory (Zionist Occupation Government) theory, promoted by some white nationalists, claims that Jews secretly control Western governments. “Great Replacement” theory is a white nationalist conspiracy theory, which has influenced mass shootings, and claims that Jews are orchestrating a demographic “replacement” of white people through immigration. At the 2017 Charlottesville rally, participants chanted “Jews will not replace us.” The internet and social media have facilitated the widespread dissemination of antisemitic conspiracy theories, with groups like QAnon promoting versions of the myth. These online forums and platforms amplify old lies and connect them to new global events, continuing the cycle of prejudice and misinformation. Antisemitism remains part of American life. On October 27, 2018, a gunman who blamed Jews for immigration killed eleven worshipers at the Tree of Life synagogue in Pittsburgh, the deadliest attack on Jews in American history; another killed a worshiper at the Chabad of Poway synagogue in California in 2019, and in 2022 a gunman held a rabbi and three congregants hostage in Colleyville, Texas. The Anti-Defamation League, which has tracked incidents since 1979, counted a record 9,354 antisemitic incidents in 2024 and 6,274 in 2025, when assaults rose to 203. That year two staff members of the Israeli embassy were shot dead outside the Capital Jewish Museum in Washington, D.C. (May 21, 2025); a firebombing in Boulder, Colorado, killed an 82-year-old woman; and an arsonist set fire to Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro’s residence on the first night of Passover. The FBI counted 1,528 anti-Jewish hate crimes in 2025, 63 percent of all religion-based hate crimes, though Jews are about two percent of the population. Like many forms of prejudice and bigotry in the United States, antisemitism is still being reckoned with.
