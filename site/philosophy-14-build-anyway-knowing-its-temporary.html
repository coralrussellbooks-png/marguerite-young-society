Chapter 14. Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

← Previous · Philosophy of MMMD Home · Next →

See also: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Chapters discussed: 22, 59

Definition from research

Since nothing is ever truly final, the honest move is to build anyway, provisionally, and keep revising. The frozen, finished blueprint is the thing to distrust.

The image for this principle comes from the beach where Miss MacIntosh raised Vera. Because God stopped too soon, they refused to rest. “We worked on both Sunday and Saturday as well as all the other days, observing no period of rest” (ch. 22). And their work was strange. On Saturdays they “nailed old boards or unnailed old boards.” Building and unbuilding are the same activity. Nothing on that beach is finished, and nothing is meant to be.

Mr. Spitzer’s music follows the same law. He tries to compose from the movements of billiard balls and discovers that “no game could doubtless ever exactly repeat itself” (ch. 59). The music can never be fixed in a score. It must be made again each time, differently.

Young’s own practice embodies the principle. She expected to finish the novel in two years and kept building for twenty. The Debs biography never reached its planned end. The construction was left open because the life it described was open.

The opposite of this principle is the utopia. Rapp’s New Harmony and Owen’s New Harmony were both finished blueprints, complete plans for human life imposed on real people. Young watched both fail before she began her novel. Keeping the construction open is her answer to them.
