Research notes: Chapter 2 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 2

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Mr. Chandelier

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The mother is perhaps the most radical embodiment of the plural self in American fiction. She is simultaneously:

  • A living woman in a bed
  • A beautiful corpse on a marble sarcophagus
  • A rider on a white horse through endless surf
  • A dead woman who is walking
  • A hostess receiving distinguished guests
  • A patient in a charity ward

"she believed that she had died in her youth and was yet alive, that there were two selves, the dead, the living"

But the text refuses to resolve which self is primary. The living self is horizontal and still; the dead self is the one walking. This inversion is not metaphor; it is ontological insistence. The self is not one thing that appears multiple; it is genuinely, irresolvably multiple.

Mr. Spitzer enacts the plural self differently. He is haunted by his dead twin brother to such a degree that others, including the Egyptian, cannot distinguish between them. He is himself and the ghost of his brother simultaneously:

"he was not the man the Egyptian was looking for... he was his living brother, his face covered with apologies, that his brother was dead... that the brother he now beheld and thought he recognized was a perfect stranger to him"

The Egyptian's inability to tell the living from the dead twin is not comic confusion. It is Young's argument: the self is not singular enough to be clearly distinguished even from another self. The plural self bleeds across the boundary of death.

Even the narrator, the child, is constituted by these plural, unstable selves. The chapter begins "My mother" but the mother never stabilizes into a single person the narrator can reliably know. The child is formed by a self that cannot be known because it is not one.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

This pillar is the chapter's structural spine. Young does not present illusion as the mother's problem: a symptom of opium addiction or madness to be corrected by reality. She presents it as the universal condition, with the mother simply more honest about it than others.

"she could not tell where the divide was between what was real and what was unreal, for the unreal things were real to her... and the real were unreal. The dreams begot the only realities"

The crucial move Young makes is to validate this epistemology rather than pathologize it. The mother's visions are described with the same lush, precise attention as any "real" event in the chapter. The Egyptian sailor who arrives from Alexandria turns out to be actually there: Mr. Spitzer finds him:

he "was greatly surprised, therefore, that there was, indeed, just as she had said, an Egyptian, a gold-headed fellow sitting in the morning room in the evening light"

This is Young's most elegant structural argument: the moment the reader has accepted that the mother's visions are hallucinations, one of them is confirmed as real. The text will not let the reader settle into the comfortable position of knowing which is which. The larger cave is more carefully lit, but it is still a cave.

The mother herself articulates Pillar 2 directly:

"The world was this deception played upon her, and it included Mr. Spitzer with his high silk hat... How did she know that they were not both dead"

The "deception" is not the mother's illness. It is existence itself. Reality is not found; it is made. And once made, it cannot be distinguished from illusion with any certainty.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The chapter is saturated with grief that does not move, does not resolve, does not arrive anywhere. It accumulates.

The mother grieves:

  • Her husband's disappearance in the Alps
  • The death of Mr. Spitzer's brother (whom she loved)
  • Her father's domination and death
  • Her own abandoned social life
  • The child she has forgotten she bore
  • Her own beauty, which she preserves precisely because it cannot be used

None of these griefs are processed. They are all simultaneously present, layered in her opium dreams like geological strata. The husband who fell into a snowbank on the Jungfrau or the Matterhorn or the Dolomites, she cannot remember which, is not mourned and moved past. He is still falling:

"he had fallen head first, disappearing under a bank of snow and granite, and was never found... and had been wearing a white rose on his coat lapel when last seen, so should be easily recognized"

The white rose on the lapel, the detail she retains, is grief's specific, irrational texture. Not the fact of his death but the white rose. This is how grief actually works: not as narrative arc but as the persistence of specific, sensory fragments that refuse to recede.

Mr. Spitzer's grief is equally permanent. His entire life was broken by his brother's death:

"his life had been broken by his brother's sudden and unexpected death, and he had thus practiced law in only the most desultory fashion... and he had written only silent music, that which, for many years now, no one else had ever heard"

The silent music is grief made structural. He composes; no one hears it. The grief does not end. It becomes the form of his existence: the shape his life takes around the absence.

And the climate of the chapter itself (the sea road, the shrouded bedroom, the wave-washed garden) is the weather of permanent grief. The house is not a home. It is a monument to everything that did not happen.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator of this chapter is perhaps the most important figure in it, not because the narrator acts, but because the narrator stays in the room. The child who begins "My mother" and then proceeds to describe, with extraordinary precision and without flinching, the full reality of a mother who has forgotten the child exists.

This is the ethical stance of the Witness: to see fully, without rushing toward resolution. The narrator does not explain the mother. Does not excuse her. Does not condemn her. Does not simplify her. The narrator attends.

The prose itself enacts witnessing. Look at the density of observation:

"Heavily laden with jewels as a Greek corpse, my mother, she who had retired from the brutal world, whose eyes were shielded against the vulgar sunlight, slept for tideless years which were her vast excitement, surrounding herself with a world of dreams, visions, phantoms, her bedroom as filled with visitors as the Grand Central Station"

The comparison, "as a Greek corpse," "as filled with visitors as the Grand Central Station", is not judgment. It is seeing. The narrator holds two incompatible images (the ancient, jeweled dead; the modern, crowded transit hub) simultaneously, refusing to collapse them into a single stable meaning.

Mr. Spitzer also practices a form of witnessing, though imperfect. He comes every evening, uninvited, unwelcome, and stays:

"he came in the evenings down the ribboned sea road for that unwelcomed visit which his own sense of honor or habit had imposed"

He does not fix the mother. He does not cure her. He does not leave. He witnesses. The chapter presents this faithfulness, staying in the room when nothing can be resolved, as the central ethical act.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The mother's entire existence is recursive construction. She builds her world of dreams, knowing it is provisional, knowing it depends on the canary living, knowing the drugs will wear thin. And she builds it again every day.

"Her own mind had been all that was left to provide for her a better world, cities, mountains, dead faces, vague, clouded longings"

She does not build despite knowing the building is provisional. She builds because there is nothing else. The recursion is structural: the dreams create her, and she creates the dreams:

"She had not created the dreams, for they had created her, making her all she was and more than even she could realize"

This is recursive construction at its most radical: the builder and the building are mutually constitutive. There is no original self who then began to dream. The dreaming and the self arose together.

The canary system is perhaps the chapter's most elegant image of recursive construction. Mr. Spitzer arranges for a continuous substitution of dead canaries with living ones:

"It was Mr. Spitzer who had arranged, in fact, that there should always be this secret change, this substitution of the living for the dead"

He builds the illusion of continuity, knowing it is illusion, because the building is necessary. The structure is provisional (one bird dies, another takes its place) but the structure works, and the mother lives. The building is never finished (there will always be another canary to replace) and that is precisely how it functions.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter's prose form is itself an argument for this pillar. Young's sentences do not conclude. They accumulate, spiral, return, extend. The paragraph that begins "Heavily laden with jewels as a Greek corpse" does not arrive at a destination. It circles the mother, adding detail, qualifying, extending, never summarizing.

This is not stylistic excess. It is ethical insistence: the mother cannot be concluded. To write a sentence that ends cleanly about her would be a violence against her reality.

The mother's own relationship to incompletion is explicit. Her letter, never finished:

"an ebony writing desk inlaid with mother-of-pearl where she had started to write a letter yesterday or years before"

The letter is not a failure. It is honest. To finish the letter would be to pretend that something had been resolved. The ongoing sentence, the letter started years ago and never completed, is the form that integrity takes.

The mother's reasons for going to bed are also structurally incomplete. Young gives us a long list: the birthmark, the coldness of the snow, the wealth of her inheritance, the advice of physicians, the abandonment by those she loved, her social humiliations in Boston, being refused admission to a hotel, her husband's departure, and then:

"She had gone to bed for no reason at all, only that she had found the so-called real world unsatisfactory"

The list is abandoned. The "reason" dissolves. This is the ethics of incompletion: the demand for a single, conclusive explanation is refused. The honest answer is the ongoing, unresolved accumulation.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Every significant figure in this chapter exists at the edges of categories:

The mother is neither living nor dead, neither sane nor insane, neither present nor absent, neither hostess nor recluse. She is the marginal person par excellence, and through her marginality, the constructed nature of all these categories becomes visible.

"she believed that she had died in her youth and was yet alive... this beautiful corpse or effigy upon a marble sarcophagus... this was the living being, this with the bright, luminous eyes"

The category "living" is revealed as a construction precisely because she inhabits both sides of it simultaneously.

Mr. Spitzer exists at the margin between presence and absence, between himself and his dead brother, between the legal world and the world of lost causes and silent music. He is described as practicing law "in only the most desultory fashion, often without fee, preferring the lost causes". The lost cause is the marginal case: the case that falls outside what the law can resolve.

James the chauffeur is marginal by legal history (ex-convict, falsely accused) and by his position in the household; neither servant nor free, present only in his absence, still at the wheel of a car that has long since gone to the junk pile.

The Egyptian is the most explicit figure of marginality: a man from another civilization, another time, who arrives at the wrong port, looking for a dead man, and is found to be real precisely when everyone has decided he must be imaginary. His existence at the edge of the possible, neither fully real nor fully phantom, is where the constructed nature of "the real" becomes most visible.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The chapter's time is not linear. It is geological. The past does not recede; it accumulates beneath the present, pressing upward.

The mother's bedroom is a site of sedimentary time. Every object carries multiple temporal layers:

"the curious bed carved with fantastic figures seeming mobile, lutes, angels, arabesques"

"the latest magazine which he might care to read, L'Illustration of a decade ago, for example, opened at the seventh page and covered with dust. The parasols were open."

The magazine from a decade ago, still open, still dusty; this is not neglect. It is the visible form of sedimentary time. The decade has not passed. It is here, pressing up through the dust.

The mother's father is the most powerful example. He died before her marriage, yet:

"she had gone to bed to escape from the domination of her father who had died before her marriage"

The dead father's domination continues. His rituals persist: the table still laid for the dead, the place set for the wandering angel, the twelve goblets for the twelve hours. The father is not behind her. He is beneath her, still pressing up through the structure of her life.

The horses on the Egyptian's race dope sheet, "those Arabian horses no longer running, those he declared were running again, their golden hooves beating the turf", are perhaps the chapter's most precise image of sedimentary time. The dead horses run again. What was lost is not behind us. It is beneath us, still pressing.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Young's prose is the most self-aware in this chapter about language as construction. The mother's instructions to the Egyptian are a masterclass in language that makes rather than describes:

"she had described a landscape shifting like water, the schools of faceless dolphins which should follow in his wake, the great birds with teeth, the stars not in their places but in another place"

She is not describing the house. She is constructing it through language, and the construction shifts even as she speaks: "for now there were rooms between the rooms, and now the doors had changed their places".

The mother's complaints about arithmetic are also, at their core, complaints about language:

"did she not suffer endlessly from her lack of any knowledge of arithmetic... All she could do was multiply, divide"

Arithmetic is a language system. Her inability to "add and subtract" in that system is her inability to operate within the linguistic construction of ordinary reality. She can only multiply (proliferate images, visions, selves) and divide (split the real from the unreal, the living from the dead). She cannot perform the operations that would make her world cohere according to conventional construction.

Mr. Spitzer's "silent music" is language's limit: music that no one else has ever heard. It exists; it is composed; it is written. But it cannot be transmitted. Language (musical language) has failed to carry meaning across the gap between self and other. The honest response to this failure is not to stop composing. It is to compose silently, acknowledging the limitation.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter is here, in the ordinary-extraordinary details of the bedroom, the sea road, the canary cage. It does not point outward to a beyond. It points deeper into the immanent.

The mother's father set a place at his table for the wandering angel. But the mother herself is surrounded by angels in her opium paradise:

"the angels surrounded her in her opium paradise, their fleecy wings outspreading, shadowing her cheek, and the harpers harped, and the seas were turned to glass"

This is not transcendence away from the world. It is transcendence in the bed, in the bedroom, in the body weighted with jewels and pearls. The sacred is immanent in the horizontal person, in the drugged canary, in Mr. Chandelier:

"She would imagine, in some slow moment of awakening, that the chandelier was Mr. Chandelier, her companion"

The chandelier becomes a companion, a sacred presence, not by pointing toward heaven but by being fully itself, fully here, fully attended to. The sacred lives in the ordinary object given full attention.

The most powerful instance of immanent transcendence is the canary:

"This little yellow canary, sleeping in a gilded cage at her elbow, delicate as a puff of opium-colored smoke... was all that had ever stood between her and tides of death"

A small, drugged, frequently dead-and-replaced bird is the thing that stands between a human being and death. Not God. Not love. Not medicine. A canary. The sacred is here, in this specific, fragile, ordinary creature. And it is enough, or it is all there is, which amounts to the same thing.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The narrator of this chapter, the child, is the crossing point made visible. The child is constituted by:

  • The mother's forgotten childbirth
  • The father who disappeared in the Alps
  • The grandfather's rituals and blindness
  • The grandmother's blindness and extraordinary compensation
  • Mr. Spitzer's faithfulness
  • James's violence and absence
  • The canaries, each replacing the last

"She simply had forgotten that she had ever given birth to a child."

The child exists at the crossing point of all these forces and none of them chose the child. The child did not choose to be constituted this way. It is structural. The ancestors: the grandfather with his star-lighted eyes, the grandmother who fooled the couturier about colors she could not see, the dead twin brother whose ring Mr. Spitzer still wears: are in the sediment.

The grandmother's story is the chapter's most explicit statement of the crossing point:

"He had felt that his eyes were gone, that his body was turning into starlight. When he was blind, he had gotten a Seeing Eye dog who had led him, old and stooped, through the garden. For a long while before anyone recognized this, and he himself had never known it, the old Seeing Eye dog was blind, yet never lost his path"

The blind leading the blind through a garden planted for a blind wife; this is the crossing point in its fullest form. Each generation deposits something into the ground. The grandfather walks paths laid out for his dead wife. The dog navigates by something other than sight. The carrying continues without anyone fully understanding what is being carried or how.

The child narrating this chapter is walking those same paths. The obligation is not chosen. It is structural. And the chapter (this enormous, unfinished, spiraling sentence about a mother who forgot the child existed) is the carrying.
