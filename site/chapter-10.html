Chapter 10

Summary

Unable to picture her own wedding, Vera imagines other weddings and drifts into a dream of one. Mr. Spitzer is the veiled bride and Miss MacIntosh the bridegroom. The preacher’s marriage vows slide into a funeral service before he vanishes. Vera wakes troubled rather than amused. She realizes she has been thinking unkindly of Miss MacIntosh as colorless and unwanted, and she feels pity for a woman who gave her life to duty. She is hungry and wants to go to her, but she has been warned not to wander the house or the shore at night.

Key passages

From the research

  • Vera dreams or hallucinates a surreal wedding ceremony in which Mr. Spitzer and Miss MacIntosh are paired, but the roles and identities are confused and inverted. The preacher’s words blur distinctions between man and woman, human and beast, and even marriage rites and funeral rites become intertwined. This dream culminates in an eerie meditation on death and the fleeting nature of life. The preacher’s speech shifts seamlessly from wedding vows to funeral liturgy, emphasizing the inseparability of marriage and death, union and dissolution. The dream ends with the unsettling image of Mr. Spitzer as the bride and Miss MacIntosh as the bridegroom, highlighting ambiguous identities and roles.
  • Following the dream, Vera reflects on Miss MacIntosh’s situation, age, and prospects for marriage. She is portrayed as a somewhat colorless, ordinary woman, past the prime of youth, who has resigned herself to loneliness. Yet Vera also feels a budding pity and empathy for her, contemplating the pain of being old and unloved and recognizing her human frailty.
  • Throughout the chapter, there is a strong undercurrent of existential melancholy. The preacher’s speech invokes biblical imagery about the brevity of life, decay, and the inevitability of death. This generalizes human fate and questions the permanence of social institutions like marriage.
  • Vera’s hunger, both literal and metaphorical, is evident as she contemplates the absence of celebration and the lack of meaningful connection. She regrets not visiting Miss MacIntosh, wishing to reconcile and share intimacy, yet respects her privacy and limitations, underscoring themes of isolation and the human need for companionship.
  • The chapter is rich in symbolism. The wedding and funeral imagery combined suggests the cyclical nature of life and death. Mr. Spitzer’s twin iron bedsteads symbolize duality or fractured identity; the snow and ice evoke coldness and isolation; the locked bedroom door represents the boundaries of privacy and emotional distance. The preacher’s litany names biblical and mythic figures (Lucifer, Beelzebub, Gorgons, Hydras, Chimeras) and cosmic events, creating a vision of a chaotic universe where human rituals struggle for meaning.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • The preacher does not seem “to know who was woman and who was man and who was beast and who was bird.” What does the dream undo?
  • Mr. Spitzer nods “negatively as he replied affirmatively.” What is he really saying?
  • When the stars light the scene, “this error was the error of the dream which had begotten nothing.” Whose error is it?

Characters

  • Vera gives Miss MacIntosh a waxed mustache and a silk hat, as she did in Chapter 9. What does Vera keep seeing in her?
  • Mr. Spitzer would marry “no one now but his brother worm.” What holds him to his dead brother?
  • Vera calls Miss MacIntosh “the most colorless” person in the world, then feels “the first embryonic emotion of pity.” What changes in one night?

Connections

  • Madge’s wedding was a funeral in Chapter 6, and here the preacher slides from one service to the other. Why does the novel keep joining weddings and funerals?
  • Miss MacIntosh’s locked bedroom door returns to the locked doors of Chapters 7 and 8. What is behind it?
  • The preacher recites the tribes of Israel as Miss MacIntosh made Vera recite them in Chapter 9. Why does the dream use her lessons?

Reflection

  • “One who could know us and yet love us was undoubtedly the truest love this world has ever seen.” Is it possible to be fully known and still loved?
  • “What would it be like to be old and unloved?” Is it harder to be young and unloved, or old?
  • Vera laughs at the dream, then feels ashamed. When does laughing at someone become wronging them?

Research: Characters

Vera Cartwheel

  • She is the central consciousness reflecting on marriage, dreams, and the strange events unfolding.
  • She is introspective, sensitive, and somewhat confused by the surreal experiences.

Mr. Joachim Spitzer

  • An older man, melancholic and resigned to his fate.
  • He is described with an old-fashioned elegance, symbolized by his clothes and the twin beds he shares (in memory) with his deceased brother.
  • His identity is ambiguous, shifting between bride and groom in the dream.

Miss MacIntosh

  • An older woman, practical and resigned to a life without love or marriage.
  • She is portrayed as colorless, ordinary in appearance, yet capable and domestically inclined.
  • In the dream, she assumes the role of bridegroom, further blurring gender and identity.

The Preacher

  • A mysterious figure who recites ritualistic and apocalyptic language blending wedding rites with funeral orations.
  • His speech is symbolic and confusing, reflecting the instability of the event.
  • His words come almost entirely from the Book of Common Prayer, the marriage service sliding into the Order for the Burial of the Dead, with verses from Job, Psalms, Corinthians, Jude, and Revelation spliced in.

The turtle Solomon

  • He can be interpreted as a symbol of longevity, endurance, and ancient wisdom.
  • Turtles traditionally embody qualities such as patience, stability, and a slow, deliberate approach to life.
  • In the context of this chapter, where Vera confronts themes of mortality, the fleeting nature of existence, and the confusion between life’s ceremonies (weddings) and rites of passage (funerals), Solomon may represent a counterpoint to human transience and folly.
  • The turtle’s slow, deliberate presence contrasts with the chaotic, disordered, and surreal wedding-funeral rites, emphasizing the tension between the ephemeral and the enduring.
  • The turtle’s name, “Solomon,” evokes the biblical King Solomon, renowned for his wisdom and judgment. This further amplifies the symbolic weight of the turtle as a figure of insight and contemplation amidst the chaos and absurdity Vera perceives around her.
  • Solomon’s silent witness role amidst this disarray may suggest the presence of a wise, patient observer within Vera’s subconscious, hinting at a deeper understanding or acceptance of life’s contradictions and mysteries.

Marriage and Identity

  • Marriage is portrayed ambiguously, blending union and separation, life and death.

See: Death and Mortality

  • The wedding turns into a funeral, reflecting the inseparability of life and death.
  • Biblical and apocalyptic references underscore the transient nature of human life.
  • The preacher’s speech highlights human frailty and the inevitability of death.

Loneliness and Human Connection

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Miss MacIntosh’s character symbolizes the loneliness of aging and societal neglect.
  • Vera feels pity and empathy, recognizing the shared human condition.
  • The dream’s isolation (few guests, symbolic objects) stresses the solitude underlying social ceremonies.

Dream versus Reality

See: Illusion and Reality · Dreams and Imagination

  • Vera’s hallucinatory experience reflects inner turmoil and existential questioning.

Social Roles and Expectations

  • Miss MacIntosh’s rejection of marriage and Vera’s reflections reveal societal pressures on women.
  • The notion that “none would have her” contrasts with Vera’s hope for unexpected companionship.

Research: Resources

  • “some other bride blushing like a red, red rose”: Robert Burns, “O my Luve’s like a red, red rose” (1794). See Romantic Poets.
  • “live happily ever after as in an old fairy tale”: The fairy-tale ending, “and they lived happily ever after.” See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “his brother worm who was under the ground”: Job says “to the worm, Thou art my mother, and my sister” (Job 17:14), and Hamlet says, “Your worm is your only emperor for diet” (Hamlet 4.3). See The Book of Job and Shakespeare.
  • “pots and pans jangling in my ears”: A shivaree, or charivari, was the old custom of banging pots and pans outside newlyweds’ windows on their wedding night. See Weddings and Funerals.
  • “it was a funeral march which was played at a perfect wedding”: Chopin’s Funeral March (Piano Sonata No. 2, 1839) in place of Mendelssohn’s Wedding March (1842) or Wagner’s “Here Comes the Bride” (Lohengrin, 1850). See Music and Composers.
  • “we are gathered here in the sight of God and of this company to join man and woman together”: The Book of Common Prayer opens the marriage service, “Dearly beloved, we are gathered together here in the sight of God, and in the face of this congregation, to join together this man and this woman in holy matrimony.” See Weddings and Funerals.
    • Priestly blessing vows came from the Sarum rite (missal) in medieval England.
    • Council of Carthage 398 AD, also known as the Fourth Council of Carthage, did not explicitly define marriage in a single canon, but it addressed aspects related to marriage and divorce through various decrees. One notable aspect was the reinforcement of the canon against clerics marrying, and the enforcement of celibacy for those ordained. Additionally, the council addressed the issue of remarriage after consecration, deeming it similar to adultery and punishable by excommunication, while also stating that such marriages were valid. One canon of the council’s rulings had the bride and groom presented by their parents to the priest for a blessing.
  • “for the end of the world draweth nigh”: This echoes “The end of all things is at hand” (1 Peter 4:7) and “your redemption draweth nigh” (Luke 21:28). “Lightnings and thunderings and voices” come from Revelation 4:5, and “in the twinkling of an eye” from 1 Corinthians 15:52: “we shall all be changed, in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye.” The phrase echoes the novel’s recurring themes of mortality, judgment, and the struggle between hope and despair in the face of inevitable endings. See The Bible.
  • “when there shall be many gnashings of teeth, many wailings”: Matthew 8:12 reads, “there shall be weeping and gnashing of teeth.” This phrase underscores themes of mortality, judgment, and the sorrow that accompanies human existence, blending the sacred with the tragic in Vera’s experience of the marriage ritual. See The Bible.
  • “a mighty hurricane is blowing in from the Canary Islands or Hebrides”: Atlantic hurricanes are born off West Africa, near the Canary and Cape Verde Islands. The Hebrides lie in the cold north of Scotland, where no hurricane begins.
    • The Canary Islands are a Spanish archipelago off the coast of northwestern Africa.
    • Hebrides is an archipelago comprising hundreds of islands off the northwest coast of Scotland.
  • “this life is very short and full of mortal sorrow”: Job 14:1 reads, “Man that is born of a woman is of few days, and full of trouble.” This phrase serves as a sobering meditation on human finitude, the inevitability of death, and the sorrow that colors our brief time alive. See The Book of Job.
  • “Do either of ye know of an impediment to this marriage”: The phrase is from the Book of Common Prayer (Sarum Rite/Missal). The prayer book reads, “I require and charge you both, as ye will answer at the dreadful day of judgment when the secrets of all hearts shall be disclosed, that if either of you know any impediment . . . ye do now confess it,” and calls on anyone who knows a cause to “speak, or else hereafter for ever hold his peace.” See Weddings and Funerals.
  • “St. Michael and of all the holy angels”: The list comes out of Milton’s Paradise Lost, which gives Hell “Gorgons and Hydras, and Chimaeras dire” (2.628) and seats Chaos as umpire with “high arbiter Chance” who “governs all” (2.907-910). See Milton and Angels and Archangels.
    • St. Michael is an archangel, a spiritual warrior in the battle of good versus evil.
    • Lucifer “who was the brightest and is fallen farthest” is the name of different figures in folklore associated with the planet Venus. “How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!” (Isaiah 14:12). See the Chapter 7 resources.
    • Beelzebub is Baal-zebub, “lord of the flies,” god of the Philistine city of Ekron (2 Kings 1:2), and in the Gospels the prince of devils (Matthew 12:24).
    • Chaos is the void before the forming of the universe in Greek mythology.
    • Gorgons are creatures in Greek mythology. Medusa and her sisters had a gaze that turned men to stone.
    • Hydras are serpentine water monsters in Greek and Roman mythology. Heracles killed the many-headed Hydra as his second labor.
    • Chimeras are monsters made of different parts of animals in mythology. Bellerophon killed the Chimera, part lion, part goat, part serpent.
    • In Aristotle’s Physics, chance (automaton) works in nature and luck (tyche) in human affairs.
  • “the dreadful Day of Judgment”: Day of Judgment is the part of theology that deals with the ultimate destiny of humans. It is found in Judaism, Christianity, Islam and Zoroastrianism. The golden thrones and “the sea shall turn to glass” come from Revelation 4:4-6: “a sea of glass like unto crystal.” See The Bible.
  • “Unadvisedly or lightly, marriage should not be entered in”: The prayer book says marriage is “not by any to be enterprised, nor taken in hand, unadvisedly, lightly . . . but reverently, discreetly, advisedly, soberly.” See Weddings and Funerals.
  • “none shall take a wife of whoredoms”: God tells the prophet Hosea, “Go, take unto thee a wife of whoredoms” (Hosea 1:2). See The Bible.
  • “to have and to hold from this day forward”: The prayer book vows: “to have and to hold from this day forward, for better for worse . . . in sickness and in health, to love, cherish, and to obey.” Mr. Spitzer nods “negatively as he replied affirmatively.” See the Chapter 6 resources on “love, honor, and obey.”
  • “all corporeal and incorporeal hereditaments”: Appurtenances are an accessory or other item associated with a particular activity or style of living. The preacher slips into the language of property deeds, which pass “hereditaments and appurtenances,” everything that can be inherited or that goes with an estate.
  • “Man that is born of woman”: The burial service opens, “Man, that is born of a woman, hath but a short time to live, and is full of misery.” The preacher adds the swollen stomach, from the trial of the jealous husband’s wife in Numbers 5:27. See The Book of Job.
    • Numbers 5:27 This phrase encapsulates the complex interplay of physical condition and the spiritual or emotional state likely reflecting on the human condition’s heaviness and vulnerability as portrayed throughout the novel.
  • “the rites for the burial of the dead”: Funeral rites originated from Jewish and Roman customs. The Order for the Burial of the Dead in the Book of Common Prayer is the service the preacher slides into. See Weddings and Funerals.
  • “He cometh up, and he is cut down”: Job 14:2 This phrase serves as a poignant reminder within the narrative of life’s impermanence and the inescapable presence of death even at moments of joy and union. The preacher quotes the burial service word for word: “He cometh up, and is cut down, like a flower; he fleeth as it were a shadow, and never continueth in one stay.” See The Book of Job.
  • “He has gone from Newport to Mystic, from Wellfleet to Buzzards Bay”: The preacher lists the coast of Rhode Island, Connecticut, and Cape Cod, the New England shore where Vera grows up.
  • “May God forgive us, for we know not who or where we are”: “Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do” (Luke 23:34). See The Bible.
  • “Spare us, Lord most holy, God most mighty, holy and merciful Savior”: The burial service prays, “spare us, Lord most holy, O God most mighty, O holy and merciful Saviour.”
  • “the archangel trumpeting the last trump”: “For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God” (1 Thessalonians 4:16). See Angels and Archangels.
  • “Why stand we in jeopardy every hour?”: 1 Corinthians 15:30 The phrase conveys a broader theme in the novel of the tension between life and death, the known and the unknown, and the profound uncertainties that define human experience. It is a rhetorical question that underscores the fragile, often perilous journey of life that the characters navigate throughout the narrative. 1 Corinthians 15 is the lesson read at the burial service. See The Bible.
  • “Nothing thou hast brought into this world”: “For we brought nothing into this world, and it is certain we can carry nothing out” (1 Timothy 6:7), one of the burial service’s opening sentences. See The Bible.
  • “Gold and silver rust”: “Your gold and silver is cankered; and the rust of them shall be a witness against you” (James 5:3). See The Bible.
  • “What is man but a poor, two-legged animal?”: Plato defined man as a featherless biped, and Diogenes answered with a plucked chicken. Lear calls man “such a poor, bare, forked animal” (King Lear 3.4). See Plato and Platonism and Shakespeare.
  • “His beauty consumeth away, like as if it were a moth fretting a woolen garment”: “Thou makest his beauty to consume away, like as it were a moth fretting a garment” (Psalm 39:12, as the burial service gives it). See The Bible.
  • “In the morning it is green and groweth up, but in the evening it is cut down, withered”: “In the morning it is green, and groweth up; but in the evening it is cut down, dried up, and withered” (Psalm 90:6, in the burial service). See The Bible.
  • “All hearts shall melt to water”: “The hearts of the people melted, and became as water” (Joshua 7:5). See The Bible.
  • “Selah, selah, selah”: In the Bible, the word “selah” typically signifies a pause, a time for reflection, or a musical interlude. It appears most often in the Psalms and Habakkuk, suggesting a moment to stop and consider what has just been said or sung.
  • “Where is Nepthalin? Where is Reuben who begot a horde? Where is Manasses?”: Nephthalim and Manasses are the King James spellings of Naphtali and Manasseh in Revelation 7:6, where twelve thousand of each tribe are sealed. See the Chapter 9 resources on the genealogies.
  • “Oh, death, where is thy sting? Oh, grave, where is thy victory?”: 1 Corinthians 15:55-57 The phrase encapsulates the struggle to find meaning beyond death and the hope or belief that death may not be the ultimate end. It reflects a yearning for transcendence, where death’s “sting” (its pain and finality) and the “victory” of the grave (the end of life) are questioned or denied. See The Bible.
  • “thy womb of morning”: “From the womb of the morning” (Psalm 110:3). See The Bible.
  • “Can these bones live?”: God asks Ezekiel in the valley of dry bones (Ezekiel 37:3). See The Bible.
  • “earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust”: The burial service reads, “Forasmuch as it hath pleased Almighty God . . . to take unto himself the soul of our dear brother . . . we therefore commit his body to the ground; earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust.” The preacher’s “brother to brother, deep calling to deep” adds Psalm 42:7. See the Chapter 1 resources.
  • “As it was in the beginning, is now, and ever shall be, world without end”: The Gloria Patri, the doxology sung at the end of the psalms.
  • “Where is Philemon?”: Philemon was a Christian of Colossae to whom Paul wrote his letter about Onesimus, a runaway slave, asking forgiveness for him. In Ovid, Philemon and Baucis are the old married couple who asked to die at the same hour and were turned into two trees, an oak and a linden. See The Bible and Greek Mythology.
  • “Where is Zipporah, a little bird?”: Zipporah is the wife of Moses. Her name means “bird” in Hebrew. See The Bible.
  • “Where are those wandering stars to whom is reserved the blackness of darkness forever?”: The preacher recites Jude 1:12-13: “clouds they are without water, carried about of winds; trees whose fruit withereth, without fruit, twice dead, plucked up by the roots . . . wandering stars, to whom is reserved the blackness of darkness for ever.” See The Bible.
  • “Strike up the music! On with the dance”: Byron’s “On with the dance! let joy be unconfined,” from the ball on the eve of Waterloo (Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage, Canto III). See Romantic Poets.
  • “the turtle Solomon and the red geranium and an old, empty shoe”: An old shoe was thrown after the bride or tied to the carriage for luck. See Weddings and Funerals.
  • “Her face was certainly not her fortune”: The nursery rhyme’s milkmaid answers, “My face is my fortune, sir, she said.” See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “She had stopped it only once, and that was in Spokane”: Miss MacIntosh’s broken nose from a bus in Spokane, from Chapter 9. See the Chapter 9 resources.
  • “the oldest fool was the greatest fool”: “There is no fool like an old fool” (John Heywood, 1546).
  • “she could be the helpmeet”: “I will make him an help meet for him” (Genesis 2:18). See The Bible.
  • “their numbers were legion”: “My name is Legion: for we are many” (Mark 5:9). See The Bible.
  • “Familiarity breeds contempt”: Chaucer’s Tale of Melibee is the most widely cited source for the phrase in English. Chaucer used the phrase “hoomlynesse” (familiarity) and “dispresisynge” (contempt). In St. Augustine’s Latin a similar idea, expressed as “vulgare proverbium est, quod nimia familiaritas parit contemptum” (meaning “it is a common proverb, that too much familiarity breeds contempt”), appears in St. Augustine’s writings from the 5th century AD. Some point to Srimad Bhagavatam as the ultimate origin, with the phrase being expressed in Sanskrit by the sage Narada Muni. This interpretation suggests the idea has been around for a much longer period.
  • “One who could know us and yet love us was undoubtedly the truest love this world has ever seen”: Psalm 139 It suggests that the deepest and most genuine form of love is not blind or naive, but rather one that fully comprehends all aspects of a person (their strengths, weaknesses, flaws, and complexities) and loves them in spite of, or even because of, that complete knowledge. This statement highlights the theme of authentic love as acceptance and compassion for the whole person, including their imperfections and vulnerabilities. It contrasts with superficial or idealized love, which might only appreciate a partial or idealized image. The “truest love” is grounded in reality, acknowledging the full truth of the beloved. In the context of the chapter, Vera (the narrator) reflects on the nature of love and marriage, and the idea that true love involves a deep understanding and acceptance of the other person. Miss MacIntosh, who values common sense and reality, would likely agree with this view, emphasizing love based on truth rather than illusion or fantasy. The phrase captures a central insight into the nature of love as something that embraces the whole self (the light and the dark, the certain and the uncertain) and that this kind of love is the most meaningful and enduring.
  • “to waste one’s sweetness in the desert air”: Thomas Gray’s Elegy: “Full many a flower is born to blush unseen, / And waste its sweetness on the desert air.” See the Chapter 5 resources.
  • “The sands of time were always running low”: The hourglass, and the hymn “The sands of time are sinking” (Anne Ross Cousin, 1857).
  • “claws upon her forehead like the claws of a lobster”: The pain from shoulder to hand and the crab’s claws may foreshadow what Vera learns of Miss MacIntosh’s body. Cancer takes its name from the crab, the Greek karkinos.
  • “whirling dervishes”: The Mevlevi Sufis, followers of the poet Rumi, worship by whirling.
  • “good, true, beautiful”: Plato’s three highest ideas, the Good, the True, and the Beautiful. See Plato and Platonism.
