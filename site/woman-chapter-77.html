Chapter 77

On Chapter 77 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Woman in Young's Magnum Opus.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Characters: Esther Longtree

The chapter opens on a cosmic scale of blame, "who killed the heavens," the "green earth," "love," and the diffusion of agency the book keeps stating is raised here to the size of the sky. Esther's private tragedy, the deaths of her children and her unheard cries, is written across the whole of creation, and "everybody else" who "had done wrong" bringing unwanted children into the world is set against her, who "had done no wrong" because she "wanted children," the interrupted birth reframed as the one honest desire in a world of forced and resented ones. The "great, overhanging veil" the doctor names is the social and existential weight pressing on a woman's reproductive life, and Esther's spine "curved by the weight of memory" carries it in her body.

The flour miller's story of the "thumb print" that cannot be inherited on the flour is the failure of the record to hold a life, an identity that leaves no lasting mark, and the "little book" Esther finds rain-stained and buried is the lost knowledge and silenced testimony the book keeps unearthing, a message from the dead or the unborn. Its alphabet becomes "exhibits" that accuse her, "Mother, why did you kill me?", the letters A through U arraigning her, and the reduction of a woman's reproductive losses to evidence in a trial is the legal machinery the book keeps exposing, motherhood surveilled and prosecuted rather than witnessed.

The detective investigates only "little white children" and ignores the deaths of "little black children" because he "hated the black," and the racial line drawn through justice is the intersecting erasure the book has carried since Martha the passenger pigeon, whose loss the old black porter alone mourned. That the detective finds both a white and a black child and retreats is society's selective empathy made stark, the black child's death unrecorded and unavenged, and Esther's own child by a black father places her inside that erasure, a woman whose motherhood crosses the line the law refuses to cross.

The story of the "mass murderess" abortionist who killed poor unmarried mothers and their babies, who murdered even a woman "pregnant in her dreams," is the danger women face in a world that condemns and criminalizes their reproductive lives, the interrupted birth turned to slaughter. Esther's reflection that "parts of us must die even as we are conceived" folds the boundary between life and death into the very act of creation, and her question "Should a woman ever answer a man's questions?" closes the chapter on a refusal of the whole interrogation, a woman keeping her mystery against a surveillance that has no page for her and no justice for the children it decides do not count.
