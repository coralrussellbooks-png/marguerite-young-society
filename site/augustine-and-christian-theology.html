Chapter 3

  • The Protestant Work Ethic, the Calvinist Work Ethic, or the Puritan Work Ethic is a work ethic concept in theology, sociology, economics and history that emphasizes that hard work, discipline, and frugality are a result of a person’s subscription to the values espoused by the Protestant faith, particularly Calvinism. The phrase was initially coined in 1904-1905 by Max Weber in his book The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism. Weber asserted that Protestant ethics and values along with the Calvinist doctrine of asceticism and predestination gave birth to capitalism. The novel is supposed to examine the culture of the Middle Westerner in the USA. It is defined by the Census Bureau as Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Michigan, Minnesota, Missouri, Nebraska, North Dakota, Ohio, South Dakota, and Wisconsin.

Chapter 3

  • “Time was not given to be wasted in vain pursuits, such as that of false knowledge, the argument as to how many angels might stand on a needle’s point” - The question of how many angels can stand on a needle's point is a philosophical and theological one, often used to illustrate the difference between physical and spiritual beings. If angels are understood as immaterial beings, an infinite number could potentially occupy the space of a needle's point. However, if angels are considered to have physical bodies, then the number would be limited by the physical space available. It is also used as a metaphor for wasting time, debating topics of no practical value, or questions whose answers hold no intellectual consequence, while more urgent concerns accumulate.

Chapter 3

  • St. Augustine in the City of God is a book of Christian philosophy written in Latin by Augustine of Hippo. The mention of St. Augustine's "City of God" serves as a profound symbol of the contrast between earthly decay and spiritual aspiration, reflecting the characters' inward struggles with faith, reality, and the hope for salvation or ultimate truth beyond the ephemeral world

Chapter 5

  • Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John are the authors of the first four books of the New Testament, collectively known as the Gospels. These books provide accounts of the life, teachings, death, and resurrection of Jesus Christ. They are not simply historical accounts but are also theological interpretations of Jesus' life. Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John in the chapter symbolize the four passengers as spiritual or angelic figures, evoking the Four Evangelists and thus linking the narrative to themes of spiritual journey, life, death, and cosmic judgment.

Chapter 8

  • “that same old one-eyed Beatrice”: Beatrice is a name derived from the French name Béatrice, which came from the Latin Beatrix, which means “she who makes happy.” The popularity of the name spread because of Dante Alighieri’s poetry about the Florentine woman Beatrice Portinari. Dante presents Beatrice as being worthy of speaking for God, making her a holy individual. Beatrice “Bice” di Folco Portinari was an Italian woman who has been commonly identified as the principal inspiration for Dante Alighieri’s Vita Nuova, and is also identified with the Beatrice who acts as his guide in the last book of his narrative poem the Divine Comedy, Paradiso, and during the conclusion of the preceding Purgatorio. Within the Comedy, Beatrice is symbolic of divine grace and theology. Young’s Beatrice, a one-eyed washerwoman called a whore, is the opposite of Dante’s guide to heaven. Yet she too lives where the road divides, the graveyard on one side and the village of “promise through eternity” on the other.

Chapter 10

  • “Familiarity breeds contempt”: Chaucer’s Tale of Melibee is the most widely cited source for the phrase in English. Chaucer used the phrase “hoomlynesse” (familiarity) and “dispresisynge” (contempt). In St. Augustine’s Latin a similar idea, expressed as “vulgare proverbium est, quod nimia familiaritas parit contemptum” (meaning “it is a common proverb, that too much familiarity breeds contempt”), appears in St. Augustine’s writings from the fifth century. Some point to Srimad Bhagavatam as the ultimate origin, with the phrase being expressed in Sanskrit by the sage Narada Muni. This interpretation suggests the idea has been around for a much longer period.

Chapter 10

  • “the dreadful Day of Judgment”: The Day of Judgment belongs to eschatology, the part of theology that deals with the ultimate destiny of humans. It is found in Judaism, Christianity, Islam, and Zoroastrianism. The golden thrones and “the sea shall turn to glass” come from Revelation 4:4-6: “a sea of glass like unto crystal.”

Chapter 14

  • “the music of the spheres, the silent music”: Boethius was a Roman senator, consul, magister officiorum, polymath, historian, and philosopher of the Early Middle Ages. He sought to reconcile the teachings of Plato and Aristotle with Christian theology. He broke logic into three parts: that which defines, that which divides, and that which deduces. There are three types of arguments: those of necessity, of ready believability, and sophistry. In De Musica there are three classifications: Musica mundana, Musica humana, Musica instrumentalis. In Dante’s Divine Comedy, the spirit of Boethius is pointed out by Saint Thomas Aquinas and is mentioned further in the poem. In his book De Institutione Musica (Fundamentals of Music) his drawings appear striking and may be an inspiration for the organization of Young’s novel. Musica universalis or music of the spheres or harmony of the spheres is a Greek philosophical concept founded by Pythagoras. This metaphysical principle stated that mathematical relationships could produce ‘tones’ of energy that could produce, among other things, sounds. Pythagoras proposed that the celestial bodies all have their own unique hum based on their orbital revolution. This idea influenced many scholars until the end of the Renaissance. Johannes Kepler, a German astronomer from the 1600s wrote extensively on the subject. Later astronomy set it aside. Boethius wrote The Consolation of Philosophy in prison awaiting execution, with its Wheel of Fortune.

Chapter 15

  • “The old sheep dog barking in the night had not barked for nothing”: The proverb is “An old dog barks not in vain,” from George Herbert’s Outlandish Proverbs (1640).
  • “Men are fallen”: The Fall of man is the Christian term for the passage of the first man and woman from innocent obedience to God to guilty disobedience.

Chapter 19

  • “the bust of Martin Luther”: Martin Luther (1483-1546) was a German professor of theology, priest, Augustinian monk, and hymn writer, and the central figure of the Reformation. His “A Mighty Fortress Is Our God” (c. 1529) is still sung in Protestant churches. Luther himself opposed the image-breakers of 1522, but the Reformation he began stripped many churches bare, as Miss MacIntosh strips her room. Miss MacIntosh quoted a line attributed to him at dawn in Chapter 18.

Chapter 20

  • “Calvinistic rigors and denials and aggressions”: John Calvin (1509-1564) taught predestination, that God has chosen from eternity who will be saved. Calvinism shaped the Puritans and the Presbyterian and Reformed churches. Young sets its rigor against a woman’s “vague evasions and whimsicalities.”

Chapter 21

  • “The Devil, she had said when opening her black umbrella in the sunlight, is God’s ape”: The devil as God’s ape, simia Dei, who mocks God by imitating Him, is an old Christian saying, often traced to the Church Fathers and used by Luther.

Chapter 22

  • “the Emperor Constantine’s having changed the calendar”: On March 7, 321, Constantine ordered that “on the venerable day of the Sun” magistrates and city people should rest. The Seventh-day Adventists and Seventh Day Baptists teach that Rome changed the Sabbath to Sunday, and Miss MacIntosh’s quarrel with Constantine is theirs.

Chapter 24

  • “She had called the Bishop of Hippo”: Bishop of Hippo is Saint Augustine who was also a theologian and philosopher.

Chapter 29

  • “God, it had been said, was the True Vine”: The list of ways that refer to God and/or Jesus holds deep symbolic and thematic significance. It encapsulates the multifaceted nature of the divine as understood within Christian theology and mysticism. The significance of this enumeration lies in its portrayal of God/Jesus as an entity with many aspects and attributes, each reflecting different dimensions of spiritual truth and human experience. It emphasizes the complexity and paradox inherent in the divine nature: simultaneously merciful and just, immanent and transcendent, suffering and triumphant. This multiplicity mirrors the novel’s broader themes of ambiguity, multiplicity of selves, and the intertwining of life and death. The list serves to underscore the novel’s exploration of spiritual reality versus human perception. The divine is depicted not as a singular, easily definable being but as a presence that manifests in diverse and sometimes contradictory ways, much like the fragmented identities and overlapping realities experienced by the characters such as Mr. Spitzer and Vera’s mother, Catherine. This reflects the novel’s preoccupation with the elusive nature of truth, the complexity of existence, and the blurred boundaries between reality and illusion. By invoking these various titles, the text taps into a rich tradition of Christian symbolism, inviting readers to contemplate the depth of spiritual meaning behind everyday experiences and the mysterious workings of grace, suffering, and redemption. It situates the characters’ personal struggles within a cosmic framework, suggesting that their lives are part of a larger divine narrative that encompasses both the mortal and the eternal. This list also contrasts with the practical, grounded character of Miss MacIntosh, who embodies common sense and reality, as opposed to the more mystical and dreamlike experiences of other characters. The divine names remind us of the novel’s tension between the earthly and the spiritual, the seen and the unseen, the real and the illusory. The list is a poetic and theological meditation on the nature of divinity, serving as a thematic focal point that enriches the novel’s exploration of identity, faith, and the search for meaning amidst uncertainty and paradox.
  • “even in a dark night of the human soul”: Dark Night of the Soul is a poem written by a Spanish Christian mystic St. John of the Cross (c. 1578). Dark night in Roman Catholic spirituality describes a spiritual crisis in the journey toward union with God. In the chapter it portrays the soul’s passage through suffering, doubt, and isolation toward a higher spiritual reality.

Chapter 29 · The Role of God and the Divine

  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates the nature of God, presenting a multifaceted and paradoxical image of divinity.
  • God is the King of Light, “opposed… by the necessary King of Darkness,” who must “create… bless, absolve, curse, forgive,” and is elusive beyond human comprehension.
  • The divine is intertwined with the symbolism of butterflies and the cosmic order.

Chapter 30

  • “Cousin Hannah had known the politics of all those nations”: Most of the main characters recite or list facts, real or imaginary. This may be a critique of school learning or rote memorization or The three Rs. The three Rs are three basic skills taught in schools: reading, writing and arithmetic. The phrase appears to have been coined at the beginning of the 19th century. The skills themselves are alluded to in St. Augustine’s Confessions where students learn to read, and write, and do arithmetic.

Chapter 38

  • Augustine defended the freedom of the will against predestination’s critics in On Free Choice of the Will (388-395).

Chapter 39

  • “what was her personal identity among so many transshifting objects”: The chapter explores the complex and ambiguous nature of identity. It suggests that identity is not fixed or unitary but rather elusive, multifaceted, and constantly shifting. The chapter highlights the difficulty of defining a person, noting that people can appear as different selves or reflections and may be surrounded by illusions, dreams, and multiple selves that complicate any clear understanding of who they truly are. Catherine, reflects on the fragmented, transient, and sometimes contradictory aspects of identity, suggesting that a person is composed of many transient selves, some of which may be hidden or unknown even to the individual. It is said that the self is often divided, with inner conflicts and shifting moods, and that external appearances can be deceiving or insufficient to capture the true nature of a person. The novel emphasizes that identity can be like a mask, a dream, or a reflection in a mirror, all subject to change and interpretation. It touches on the idea that a person may be different at various times or in different contexts, and that the boundaries between life and death, self and other, reality and illusion are blurred. The chapter raises questions about the permanence of identity and whether it is possible to truly know oneself or others, given these complexities. Identity is portrayed as a fluid, dynamic phenomenon that resists simple categorization, shaped by internal contradictions, external perceptions, and the interplay of reality and illusion. Young spoke of “the profound influence upon me of William James and Saint Augustine in ‘The City of God’” (Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs, “A Conversation with Marguerite Young,” Review of Contemporary Fiction 23, no. 1, Spring 2003 (1989 original)). She told Studs Terkel, “William James made a beautiful statement, man is body, soul, and clothing” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). William James wrote The Varieties of Religious Experience with twenty lectures. William James also supported Pragmatism that considers words and thought as tools and instruments for prediction, problem solving, and action, and rejects the idea that the function of thought is to describe, represent, or mirror reality.
  • “my mother’s soul was like a many-spiraling house with many rooms”: “In my Father’s house are many mansions” (John 14:2). Teresa of Ávila pictured the soul as a castle of diamond or clear crystal with many rooms, as in heaven there are many mansions (The Interior Castle, 1577).

Chapter 42

  • “Forever would a lost psyche wander down the wind”: While death is inevitable there are different forms of immortality, covered above, and St. Augustine’s perspective where he emphasized the eternal significance of the “hour of death” and the importance of being prepared for it since one has been promised forgiveness but not necessarily the time: “No one has promised you that you are going to be alive tomorrow” (Augustine, Sermon 40.5, trans. Edmund Hill, 1990). This line encapsulates the novel’s meditation on the soul’s vulnerability to disorientation within the vast, often unfathomable realms of life, death, and memory.

Chapter 43

  • “the eighth day of the week”: Eighth day of the week may refer to Burmese Theravada Buddhism where the week has eight days. Wednesday is divided into two parts. Early Christian writers also called the resurrection the “eighth day,” the day after the seven days of creation, and Augustine ends The City of God with the seventh day of rest leading into “the eighth and eternal day.” It is a time outside ordinary time.

Chapter 46

  • “For who had ever known the present moment?”: Augustine found that the present has no extent: “the present hath no space” (Confessions 11.15, trans. E. B. Pusey, 1838).

Chapter 47

  • seems to mirror mystical interpretations from Sufism and Christian Theology and signifies the ultimate unity and interconnectedness of love, the person experiencing love, and the object of that love. In Sufism, the phrase highlights the journey of the soul towards union with the divine. The lover seeks to merge with the Beloved and in that ultimate union the distinction between the two dissolves. In Christian Theology the Father can be seen as the Lover, the Son as the Beloved, and the Holy Spirit as the love that binds them: “Behold, then, there are three things: he that loves, and that which is loved, and love” (Augustine, On the Trinity 8.10.14, trans. Arthur West Haddan, 1873). In philosophy the phrase emphasizes the interconnectedness of all things and the illusion of separation. This reflects the novel’s broader meditation on the illusory nature of individuality and the interconnectedness of all beings, suggesting that what appear to be separate states or identities are part of a single, fluid existence.

Chapter 49

  • “three trinitarian minutes”: The old caretaker times Martha’s death to “three minutes after one… three trinitarian minutes. Father, Mother, Son.” The Christian Trinity is the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit; Young puts Mother in the Spirit’s place, joining the sacred triad to family, birth, death, and spiritual passage. The precise minutes make her passing not only a physical event but a metaphysical one, framed within the religious and cosmic order the novel reflects upon.
  • “the dove of God like that which flew up through Mary’s skirts”: The Holy Spirit descends “in a bodily shape like a dove” (Luke 3:22), and in paintings of the Annunciation a dove brings the conception to Mary. See the three trinitarian minutes, above.

Chapter 51

  • “A flaw in the creation had permitted this creation”: In the Kabbalah of Isaac Luria (sixteenth century), the vessels that held God’s light shattered at the creation, the shevirat ha-kelim, the breaking of the vessels, so that the world was made through a break. Mr. Spitzer’s errors “had never evolved from an original sin,” Augustine’s doctrine of the sin inherited from Adam.

Chapter 54

  • “old, dour Mather” may refer to Cotton Mather who was a New England Puritan clergyman and writer.

Chapter 54

  • Paradiso is the third and final part of Dante's Divine Comedy, following the Inferno and the Purgatorio. It is an allegory telling of Dante's journey through Heaven, guided by Beatrice, who symbolizes theology. In the poem, Paradise is depicted as a series of concentric spheres surrounding the Earth, consisting of the Moon, Mercury, Venus, the Sun, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, the Fixed Stars, the Primum Mobile and finally, the Empyrean. It was written in the early 14th century. Allegorically, the poem represents the soul's ascent to God.

Chapter 55

  • “Will of the Dead” may refer to a legal will and testament, a legally binding document that specifies how a person's property should be distributed after their death. In a spiritual or metaphorical context, it could refer to the concept of the deceased having continued influence or wishes, such as in the phrase "Let the dead bury the dead," which encourages focusing on the living rather than the past or those who are spiritually dead. The phrase can also refer to the theological concept of the New Covenant in the Bible, where Jesus's death is required for the will to take effect.

Chapter 55

  • Semper idem is Latin for always the same and was used by English Puritans in reference to Jesus Christ.

Chapter 63

  • "Heaven preserve you from a beardless man. Heaven preserve you from a hairy woman." This opening line is a warning against unnatural or incomplete identities, possibly alluding to the disruption of traditional gender roles or the loss of conventional human distinctions. The "beardless man" might symbolize immaturity or failure to fulfill societal or spiritual masculinity, while the "hairy woman" could indicate a departure from expected femininity or an overwhelming, chaotic force. The invocation of "Heaven preserve you" is a religious appeal for divine protection from these unsettling or undesirable conditions. "Consider well, my dear, if you mean what you are saying, if this is your infinite desire for nothing." Miss MacIntosh challenges the listener (Vera) to reflect carefully on whether their longing for "nothing", a void, emptiness, or perhaps death or nihilism is truly what they want. It suggests a deep questioning of desire, existence, and meaning. “She clapped the air with one hand. ‘A screech owl flew by just then. Did you not see it, hear it?’” The screech owl is a traditional symbol of death, omens, or the supernatural. Its sudden appearance emphasizes the gravity and ominous tone of the warning. The rhetorical question underscores awareness and perception, suggesting that profound or unsettling truths may go unnoticed. "The sun and the moon will shine in the same sky, and yet the earth will not be lighted but will be wrapped in darkness like the night." This line juxtaposes the presence of celestial bodies (sun and moon), which symbolize light, guidance, and life, with the paradoxical image of the earth being wrapped in darkness. It could imply a world where natural order and illumination exist but fail to reach or enlighten humanity, reflecting spiritual darkness despite physical light. "All earth will be made bare like this. No birds will sing in the tree-tops ever again. There shall be bare and ruined choirs." The "bare and ruined choirs" is a direct allusion to John Milton's Sonnet 7 ("...the bare and level plains, / The ruin of the ancient cities..."), evoking desolation, decay, and the loss of harmony or divine order. The silence of birds, which often symbolize the soul or spiritual joy, suggests a profound loss of life or spirit. "The flower will perish while it is still in the seed. The tides will not return what the tides take out." These images symbolize premature death or failure to reach potential ("flower perishing in the seed") and irreversible loss ("tides will not return what the tides take out"). They evoke the idea that some losses are final and natural cycles of renewal are broken. "There will be only, my dear, the dream." The closing line suggests that after all the destruction and loss, only the "dream" remains, possibly illusion, memory, or an insubstantial reality. It can be interpreted as a reflection on the ephemeral nature of life and the dominance of subjective perception or fantasy when the physical world fails. The invocation of "Heaven" and "Heaven preserve you" draws on Judeo-Christian imagery. The allusion to "bare and ruined choirs" connects to Milton, whose work is steeped in Christian theology and themes of the Fall and apocalypse. The presence of the sun, moon, and the view of the earth wrapped in darkness resonates with biblical apocalyptic imagery, such as the darkening of the sun and moon in the Book of Revelation signaling the end times. The repeated themes of loss, desolation, and the finality of death align with Christian meditation on mortality, sin, and the hope or despair beyond life. This passage is a somber meditation on loss, identity, and the tenuous hold on reality, filled with symbolic and religious imagery that suggests a spiritual or existential crisis. Miss MacIntosh’s words warn of the dangers of embracing "nothingness" or illusion without acknowledging the profound reality of life and death.

Chapter 63

  • Noah is the last of the pre-flood patriarchs of the Book of Genesis. He builds an ark and takes pairs of animals in order to survive the great flood. The biblical story of Noah's Ark signifies a profound message about divine judgment, mercy, and salvation. It illustrates that God judges wickedness and sin but also provides a way for salvation for those who are righteous, like Noah, and those who have faith. The story highlights themes of God's faithfulness through the covenant of the rainbow, and in Christian theology, the ark is often seen as a foreshadowing of Christ as the ultimate source of salvation.

Chapter 65

  • John Knox was a Scottish minister, Reformed theologian, and writer who was a leader of the country's Reformation. He was the founder of the Church of Scotland.

Chapter 65

  • Eloise and Abelard refers to Heloise who was a French nun, philosopher, writer, scholar, and abbess. She is famous in history and popular culture for her love affair and correspondence with the leading medieval logician and theologian Peter Abelard, who became her colleague, collaborator and husband.

Chapter 67

  • Moses recites a line from the poem The Wonderful One-Hoss-Shay by Oliver Wendell Holmes. In the poem, a deacon laments the fact that carriages break down due to weakness at one place or another. The carriages were usually built locally by enterprising villagers. The deacon deduces that if he were to build a one hoss shay using the finest materials from top to bottom, it would have no weaknesses and would therefore endure forever. So the deacon decides to build the most reliable one hoss shay there ever could logically be, in accordance with the Puritan principles that critics of the era believed Oliver Wendell Holmes to be satirizing. Every part of it is equally as strong as every other part. This marvel of logical construction lasts and lasts through a parade of decades and deacons, providing perfect service all the while. But as Holmes says, logic is logic, and eventually it catches up to the shay. Exactly one hundred years to the day that the deacon finished it, the whole thing collapses in a heap of particulates, causing the current deacon embarrassment and a sore behind.

Chapter 68

  • "death of Christian civilization" often point to the rise of secularism, "dechurching," and declining Christian influence in the West, viewing it as the end of Christendom, a historical era where Christianity dominated public life, leading to new worldviews like postchristianity; this perspective highlights major societal shifts, but Christianity remains a significant global faith, though its role in Western culture is changing dramatically. Decolonizing Christianity is a movement challenging Eurocentric, white-dominated theology and practices, aiming to dismantle systems of oppression (like racism, capitalism, patriarchy) that became intertwined with the faith during colonialism, and instead centering marginalized voices, indigenous knowledge, and a pursuit of justice, often through practices like reading the Bible from the margins and reclaiming faith from a non-white, non-Western perspective. It involves critically examining Christianity's historical complicity in colonial violence, repenting for injustices, and fostering a faith rooted in solidarity with the oppressed for true liberation, moving beyond "white" or nationalist versions of Christianity.

Chapter 72

  • Martin Luther was a German priest, theologian, author and hymn writer. A former Augustinian friar, he is best known among Christians as the seminal figure in the Protestant Reformation and as the namesake of Lutheranism.

Chapter 77

  • The Song of Songs is an erotic poem. It is unique within the Hebrew Bible in that it celebrates sexual love. The two are in harmony, each desiring the other and rejoicing in sexual intimacy. Jewish tradition reads it as an allegory of the relationship between God and Israel In Christianity, it is treated as an allegory celebrating the love between God and Church or Christ and his bride, the Church. The Christian church's interpretation of the Song is evidence of God's love for his people, both collectively and individually. Over time a moral element and the bride as Virgin Mary were added. It is unique in that these conclusions are not found in the overtly theological books of the Bible. Those books reveal an imbalance in the relationship between God and man, but Song of Songs is a theological metaphor in which the two partners are equals, bound in a committed relationship. In modern times the poem has attracted the attention of feminist biblical critics, with Phyllis Trible's Depatriarchalizing in Biblical Interpretation and the Feminist Companion to the Bible series edited by Athalya Brenner and Carole Fontaine.

Chapter 78

  • Esther says her mother is a “pallid descendant of George Washington’s sister.” This may refer to Betty Washington Lewis who was the younger sister of George Washington and the only sister that survived childhood. Betty Washington was born into the Washington Family as the first daughter of Augustine Washington and Mary Ball Washington.

Chapter 79

  • The Immaculate Conception is the belief that the Virgin Mary was free of original sin from the moment of her conception. Original sin is the Christian doctrine that holds that humans, through the fact of birth, inherit a tainted nature in need of regeneration and a proclivity to sinful conduct. According to Christian apocryphal and Islamic tradition, Saint Anne was the mother of Mary and the maternal grandmother of Jesus. Saint Anne appears as the mother of Mary in the late 2nd-century Gospel of James. Saint Anne and her husband, Joachim, are infertile, but God hears their prayers and Mary is conceived. The conception is claimed to occur without sexual intercourse between Saint Anne and Joachim, which fits well with the Gospel of James' persistent emphasis on Mary's sacred purity, but the story does not advance the idea of an immaculate conception. The Orthodox Church claims that Mary was conceived by her parents in the same way everyone is conceived. Anne (Hannah in Arabic) is recognized as a highly spiritual woman and as the mother of Mary. In the Quran she is described as childless until her old age. One day, Hannah saw a bird feeding its young while sitting in the shade of a tree, which awakened her desire to have children of her own. She prayed for a child and eventually conceived. Joachim (Imran in Arabic) died before the child was born. Expecting the child to be male, Hannah vowed to dedicate him to isolation and service in the Second Temple. However, Hannah bore a daughter instead, and named her Mary. Her words upon delivering Mary reflect her status as a great mystic, realizing that while she had wanted a son, this daughter was God's gift to her. She said - My Lord! Truly, I brought her forth, a female. And God is greater in knowledge of what she brought forth. And the male is not like the female. So her Lord received her with the very best acceptance. And her bringing forth caused the very best to develop in her. Joachim was a rich and pious man, who regularly gave to the poor. At the temple, Joachim's sacrifice was rejected, so the couple's childlessness was interpreted as a sign of divine displeasure. Joachim consequently withdrew to the desert, where he fasted and did penance for 40 days. Angels then appeared to both Joachim and Anne to promise them a child. Joachim later returned to Jerusalem and embraced Anne at the city gate, located in the Walls of Jerusalem. An ancient belief held that a child born of an elderly mother who had given up hope of having offspring was destined for great things. Joachim is named as the patron saint of fathers, grandfathers, grandparents, married couples, cabinet makers, and linen traders.

Chapter 79

  • According to Christian theology, Mary was not impregnated by a literal bird like a dove, but rather conceived Jesus through the power of the Holy Spirit. While artistic depictions sometimes show a dove (a symbol of the Holy Spirit) during the Annunciation, this is a symbolic representation.
