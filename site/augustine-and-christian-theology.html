Chapter 3

  • The Protestant Work Ethic, the Calvinist Work Ethic, or the Puritan Work Ethic is a work ethic concept in theology, sociology, economics and history that emphasizes that hard work, discipline, and frugality are a result of a person’s subscription to the values espoused by the Protestant faith, particularly Calvinism. The phrase was initially coined in 1904-1905 by Max Weber in his book The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism. Weber asserted that Protestant ethics and values along with the Calvinist doctrine of asceticism and predestination gave birth to capitalism. (Wikipedia) The novel is supposed to examine the culture of the Middle Westerner in the USA. It is defined by the Census Bureau as Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Michigan, Minnesota, Missouri, Nebraska, North Dakota, Ohio, South Dakota, and Wisconsin.

Chapter 3

  • “Time was not given to be wasted in vain pursuits, such as that of false knowledge, the argument as to how many angels might stand on a needle’s point” - The question of how many angels can stand on a needle's point is a philosophical and theological one, often used to illustrate the difference between physical and spiritual beings. If angels are understood as immaterial beings, an infinite number could potentially occupy the space of a needle's point. However, if angels are considered to have physical bodies, then the number would be limited by the physical space available. It is also used as a metaphor for wasting time, debating topics of no practical value, or questions whose answers hold no intellectual consequence, while more urgent concerns accumulate.

Chapter 3

  • St. Augustine in the City of God is a book of Christian philosophy written in Latin by Augustine of Hippo. The mention of St. Augustine's "City of God" serves as a profound symbol of the contrast between earthly decay and spiritual aspiration, reflecting the characters' inward struggles with faith, reality, and the hope for salvation or ultimate truth beyond the ephemeral world

Chapter 5

  • Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John are the authors of the first four books of the New Testament, collectively known as the Gospels. These books provide accounts of the life, teachings, death, and resurrection of Jesus Christ. They are not simply historical accounts but are also theological interpretations of Jesus' life. Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John in the chapter symbolize the four passengers as spiritual or angelic figures, evoking the Four Evangelists and thus linking the narrative to themes of spiritual journey, life, death, and cosmic judgment.

Chapter 8

  • “that same old one-eyed Beatrice”: Beatrice is a name derived from the French name Béatrice, which came from the Latin Beatrix, which means “she who makes happy.” The popularity of the name spread because of Dante Alighieri’s poetry about the Florentine woman Beatrice Portinari. Dante presents Beatrice as being worthy of speaking for God, making her a holy individual. Beatrice “Bice” di Folco Portinari was an Italian woman who has been commonly identified as the principal inspiration for Dante Alighieri’s Vita Nuova, and is also identified with the Beatrice who acts as his guide in the last book of his narrative poem the Divine Comedy, Paradiso, and during the conclusion of the preceding Purgatorio. Within the Comedy, Beatrice is symbolic of divine grace and theology. (Wikipedia) Young’s Beatrice, a one-eyed washerwoman called a whore, is the opposite of Dante’s guide to heaven. Yet she too lives where the road divides, the graveyard on one side and the village of “promise through eternity” on the other.

Chapter 10

  • “Familiarity breeds contempt”: Chaucer’s Tale of Melibee has “over-greet hoomlynesse engendreth dispreisynge,” too much familiarity breeds contempt. The Latin proverb “Nimia familiaritas parit contemptum” is credited to Publilius Syrus and repeated by Thomas Aquinas (Hoyt’s New Cyclopedia of Practical Quotations, 1922), and the English wording first appears in Richard Taverner’s Garden of Wysdome (1539). In Hindu tradition the Srimad Bhagavatam (Bhagavata Purana) teaches the same lesson in Canto 6, Chapter 7: Indra, king of the demigods, grows so used to the daily presence of his spiritual master, Bṛhaspati, that he takes him for granted and fails to rise when Bṛhaspati enters the assembly. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada draws the moral in his purport: “Familiarity sometimes breeds contempt, but one should be very careful in one’s dealings with the acarya” (Srimad-Bhagavatam 6.7.15, purport).

Chapter 10

  • “the dreadful Day of Judgment”: The Day of Judgment belongs to eschatology, the part of theology that deals with the ultimate destiny of humans. It is found in Judaism, Christianity, Islam, and Zoroastrianism. The golden thrones and “the sea shall turn to glass” come from Revelation 4:4-6: “a sea of glass like unto crystal.”

Chapter 14

  • “the music of the spheres, the silent music”: Boethius was a Roman senator, consul, magister officiorum, polymath, historian, and philosopher of the Early Middle Ages. He sought to reconcile the teachings of Plato and Aristotle with Christian theology. He broke logic into three parts: that which defines, that which divides, and that which deduces. There are three types of arguments: those of necessity, of ready believability, and sophistry. In De Musica there are three classifications: Musica mundana, Musica humana, Musica instrumentalis. In Dante’s Divine Comedy, the spirit of Boethius is pointed out by Saint Thomas Aquinas and is mentioned further in the poem. In his book De Institutione Musica (Fundamentals of Music) his drawings appear striking and may be an inspiration for the organization of Young’s novel. Musica universalis or music of the spheres or harmony of the spheres is a Greek philosophical concept founded by Pythagoras. This metaphysical principle stated that mathematical relationships could produce ‘tones’ of energy that could produce, among other things, sounds. Pythagoras proposed that the celestial bodies all have their own unique hum based on their orbital revolution. This idea influenced many scholars until the end of the Renaissance. Johannes Kepler, a German astronomer from the 1600s wrote extensively on the subject. (Wikipedia) Later astronomy set it aside. Boethius wrote The Consolation of Philosophy in prison awaiting execution, with its Wheel of Fortune.

Chapter 15

  • “The old sheep dog barking in the night had not barked for nothing”: The proverb is “An old dog barks not in vain,” from George Herbert’s Outlandish Proverbs (1640).
  • “Men are fallen”: The Fall of man is the Christian term for the passage of the first man and woman from innocent obedience to God to guilty disobedience.

Chapter 19

  • “the bust of Martin Luther”: Martin Luther (1483-1546) was a German professor of theology, priest, Augustinian monk, and hymn writer, and the central figure of the Reformation. (Wikipedia) His “A Mighty Fortress Is Our God” (c. 1529) is still sung in Protestant churches. Luther himself opposed the image-breakers of 1522, but the Reformation he began stripped many churches bare, as Miss MacIntosh strips her room. Miss MacIntosh quoted a line attributed to him at dawn in Chapter 18.

Chapter 20

  • “Calvinistic rigors and denials and aggressions”: John Calvin (1509-1564) taught predestination, that God has chosen from eternity who will be saved. Calvinism shaped the Puritans and the Presbyterian and Reformed churches. Young sets its rigor against a woman’s “vague evasions and whimsicalities.”

Chapter 21

  • “The Devil, she had said when opening her black umbrella in the sunlight, is God’s ape”: The devil as God’s ape, simia Dei, who mocks God by imitating Him, is an old Christian saying, often traced to the Church Fathers and used by Luther. Medieval bestiaries made the ape the devil’s creature: “As the devil poorly imitates the Lord, so do apes poorly imitate us” (Samiksha Chopra, “Book of Beasts,” Getty Iris).

Chapter 22

  • “the Emperor Constantine’s having changed the calendar”: On March 7, 321, Constantine ordered that “on the venerable day of the Sun” magistrates and city people should rest. The Seventh-day Adventists and Seventh Day Baptists teach that Rome changed the Sabbath to Sunday, and Miss MacIntosh’s quarrel with Constantine is theirs.

Chapter 24

  • “She had called the Bishop of Hippo”: Bishop of Hippo is Saint Augustine who was also a theologian and philosopher.

Chapter 29

  • “God, it had been said, was the True Vine”: The list mixes titles of Christ from the Bible and Christian devotion (the True Vine, the Bread of Life, the Door, the Good Shepherd, the Light of the World, the Man of Sorrows, the Prince of Peace) with the Great Spirit of many Native American traditions, and Young sums it up: “God was many things to many people.”
  • “even in a dark night of the human soul”: Dark Night of the Soul is a poem written by a Spanish Christian mystic St. John of the Cross (c. 1578). Dark night in Roman Catholic spirituality describes a spiritual crisis in the journey toward union with God.

Chapter 29 · The Role of God and the Divine

  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates the nature of God, presenting a multifaceted and paradoxical image of divinity.
  • God is the King of Light, “opposed… by the necessary King of Darkness,” who must “create… bless, absolve, curse, forgive,” and is elusive beyond human comprehension.
  • The divine is intertwined with the symbolism of butterflies and the cosmic order.

Chapter 30

  • “Cousin Hannah had known the politics of all those nations”: Most of the main characters recite or list facts, real or imaginary. This may be a critique of school learning or rote memorization or The three Rs. The three Rs are three basic skills taught in schools: reading, writing and arithmetic. The phrase appears to have been coined at the beginning of the 19th century. The skills themselves are alluded to in St. Augustine’s Confessions where students learn to read, and write, and do arithmetic. (Wikipedia)

Chapter 38

  • Augustine defended the freedom of the will against predestination’s critics in On Free Choice of the Will (388-395).

Chapter 39

  • “what was her personal identity among so many transshifting objects”: The chapter explores the complex and ambiguous nature of identity. It suggests that identity is not fixed or unitary but rather elusive, multifaceted, and constantly shifting. The chapter highlights the difficulty of defining a person, noting that people can appear as different selves or reflections and may be surrounded by illusions, dreams, and multiple selves that complicate any clear understanding of who they truly are. Catherine, reflects on the fragmented, transient, and sometimes contradictory aspects of identity, suggesting that a person is composed of many transient selves, some of which may be hidden or unknown even to the individual. It is said that the self is often divided, with inner conflicts and shifting moods, and that external appearances can be deceiving or insufficient to capture the true nature of a person. The novel emphasizes that identity can be like a mask, a dream, or a reflection in a mirror, all subject to change and interpretation. It touches on the idea that a person may be different at various times or in different contexts, and that the boundaries between life and death, self and other, reality and illusion are blurred. The chapter raises questions about the permanence of identity and whether it is possible to truly know oneself or others, given these complexities. Identity is portrayed as a fluid, dynamic phenomenon that resists simple categorization, shaped by internal contradictions, external perceptions, and the interplay of reality and illusion. Young spoke of “the profound influence upon me of William James and Saint Augustine in ‘The City of God’” (Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs, “A Conversation with Marguerite Young,” Review of Contemporary Fiction 23, no. 1, Spring 2003 (1989 original)). She told Studs Terkel, “William James made a beautiful statement, man is body, soul, and clothing” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). William James wrote The Varieties of Religious Experience with twenty lectures. William James also supported Pragmatism that considers words and thought as tools and instruments for prediction, problem solving, and action, and rejects the idea that the function of thought is to describe, represent, or mirror reality.
  • “my mother’s soul was like a many-spiraling house with many rooms”: “In my Father’s house are many mansions” (John 14:2). Teresa of Ávila pictured the soul as a castle of diamond or clear crystal with many rooms, as in heaven there are many mansions (The Interior Castle, 1577).

Chapter 42

  • “Forever would a lost psyche wander down the wind”: While death is inevitable there are different forms of immortality, covered above, and St. Augustine’s perspective where he emphasized the eternal significance of the “hour of death” and the importance of being prepared for it since one has been promised forgiveness but not necessarily the time: “No one has promised you that you are going to be alive tomorrow” (Augustine, Sermon 40.5, trans. Edmund Hill, 1990). This line encapsulates the novel’s meditation on the soul’s vulnerability to disorientation within the vast, often unfathomable realms of life, death, and memory.

Chapter 43

  • “the eighth day of the week”: Eighth day of the week may refer to Burmese Theravada Buddhism where the week has eight days. Wednesday is divided into two parts. Early Christian writers also called the resurrection the “eighth day,” the day after the seven days of creation, and Augustine ends The City of God with the seventh day of rest leading into “the eighth and eternal day.” It is a time outside ordinary time.

Chapter 46

  • “For who had ever known the present moment?”: Augustine found that the present has no extent: “the present hath no space” (Confessions 11.15, trans. E. B. Pusey, 1838).

Chapter 47

  • seems to mirror mystical interpretations from Sufism and Christian Theology and signifies the ultimate unity and interconnectedness of love, the person experiencing love, and the object of that love. In Sufism, the phrase highlights the journey of the soul towards union with the divine. The lover seeks to merge with the Beloved and in that ultimate union the distinction between the two dissolves. In Christian Theology the Father can be seen as the Lover, the Son as the Beloved, and the Holy Spirit as the love that binds them: “Behold, then, there are three things: he that loves, and that which is loved, and love” (Augustine, On the Trinity 8.10.14, trans. Arthur West Haddan, 1873). In philosophy the phrase emphasizes the interconnectedness of all things and the illusion of separation. This reflects the novel’s broader meditation on the illusory nature of individuality and the interconnectedness of all beings, suggesting that what appear to be separate states or identities are part of a single, fluid existence.

Chapter 49

  • “three trinitarian minutes”: The old caretaker times Martha’s death to “three minutes after one… three trinitarian minutes. Father, Mother, Son.” The Christian Trinity is the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit; Young puts Mother in the Spirit’s place, joining the sacred triad to family, birth, death, and spiritual passage. The precise minutes make her passing not only a physical event but a metaphysical one, framed within the religious and cosmic order the novel reflects upon.
  • “the dove of God like that which flew up through Mary’s skirts”: The Holy Spirit descends “in a bodily shape like a dove” (Luke 3:22), and in paintings of the Annunciation a dove brings the conception to Mary. See the three trinitarian minutes, above.

Chapter 51

  • “A flaw in the creation had permitted this creation”: In the Kabbalah of Isaac Luria (sixteenth century), the vessels that held God’s light shattered at the creation, the shevirat ha-kelim, the breaking of the vessels, so that the world was made through a break. Mr. Spitzer’s errors “had never evolved from an original sin,” Augustine’s doctrine of the sin inherited from Adam.

Chapter 54

  • “that old, dour Mather”: “old, dour Mather” may refer to Cotton Mather who was a New England Puritan clergyman and writer. Increase, Cotton, and Samuel Mather lie in the Mather family tomb on Copp’s Hill.

Chapter 55

  • “For what was time?”: Augustine asked the same question: “What then is time? If no one asks me, I know: if I wish to explain it to one that asketh, I know not” (Augustine, Confessions 11.14, trans. E. B. Pusey, 1838).

Chapter 58

  • “loud Te Deums”: “Te Deums” may refer to Te Deum laudamus which means Thee, O God, we praise. It is a Latin Christian hymn. Legend says Ambrose and Augustine sang it in alternate verses at Augustine’s baptism in 387.

Chapter 63

  • “the Emperor Constantine who had changed the calendar”: Emperor Constantine or Constantine the Great ruled Rome and was the first Roman emperor to convert to Christianity. Miss MacIntosh frequently expresses her disapproval of Emperor Constantine because he changed the calendar and converted to Christianity, which she views as a corruption of truth and nature. Constantine is historically known for legalizing Christianity and changing the Roman calendar, including shifting the day of rest to Sunday, the first day of the week. Miss MacIntosh’s disdain for him symbolizes her rejection of these imposed changes to natural cycles and traditional ways of life. She objects to Constantine’s alteration of the calendar, which she sees as confusing and disrupting the natural order established by God. She emphasizes the importance of simple, earnest living connected to nature and common sense, contrasting this with the artificial imposition of religious and societal structures that Constantine represents. Her scolding of him is thus a symbolic condemnation of how Christianity, as institutionalized by Constantine, had altered timekeeping, worship practices, and human experience, creating confusion and distancing people from an authentic connection to the rhythms of life and nature.

Chapter 77

  • “the Song of Songs of the sterility which is the creation”: The Song of Songs is an erotic poem. It is unique within the Hebrew Bible in that it celebrates sexual love. The two are in harmony, each desiring the other and rejoicing in sexual intimacy. Jewish tradition reads it as an allegory of the relationship between God and Israel In Christianity, it is treated as an allegory celebrating the love between God and Church or Christ and his bride, the Church. The Christian church’s interpretation of the Song is evidence of God’s love for his people, both collectively and individually. Over time a moral element and the bride as Virgin Mary were added. It is unique in that these conclusions are not found in the overtly theological books of the Bible. Those books reveal an imbalance in the relationship between God and man, but Song of Songs is a theological metaphor in which the two partners are equals, bound in a committed relationship. In modern times the poem has attracted the attention of feminist biblical critics, with Phyllis Trible’s “Depatriarchalizing in Biblical Interpretation” (1973) and the Feminist Companion to the Bible series edited by Athalya Brenner and Carole Fontaine.

Chapter 78

  • “She was not born innocent and stainless”: “Behold, I was shapen in iniquity; and in sin did my mother conceive me” (Psalm 51:5, KJV). Augustine, in the first book of his Confessions, saw sin even in the jealous infant at the breast; the doctrine of original sin holds that every child is born guilty.

Chapter 79

  • “After all, her own was the virgin birth”: The Immaculate Conception is the belief that the Virgin Mary was free of original sin from the moment of her conception. Original sin is the Christian doctrine that holds that humans, through the fact of birth, inherit a tainted nature in need of regeneration and a proclivity to sinful conduct. According to Christian apocryphal and Islamic tradition, Saint Anne was the mother of Mary and the maternal grandmother of Jesus. Saint Anne appears as the mother of Mary in the late 2nd-century Gospel of James. Saint Anne and her husband, Joachim, are infertile, but God hears their prayers and Mary is conceived. The conception is claimed to occur without sexual intercourse between Saint Anne and Joachim, which fits well with the Gospel of James’ persistent emphasis on Mary’s sacred purity, but the story does not advance the idea of an immaculate conception. The Orthodox Church claims that Mary was conceived by her parents in the same way everyone is conceived. Anne (Hannah in Arabic) is recognized as a highly spiritual woman and as the mother of Mary. In the Quran she is described as childless until her old age. One day, Hannah saw a bird feeding its young while sitting in the shade of a tree, which awakened her desire to have children of her own. She prayed for a child and eventually conceived. Joachim (Imran in Arabic) died before the child was born. Expecting the child to be male, Hannah vowed to dedicate him to isolation and service in the Second Temple. However, Hannah bore a daughter instead, and named her Mary. Her words upon delivering Mary reflect her status as a great mystic, realizing that while she had wanted a son, this daughter was God’s gift to her. She said: “My Lord! Truly, I brought her forth, a female. And God is greater in knowledge of what she brought forth. And the male is not like the female” (Quran 3:36). So her Lord received her with the very best acceptance. And her bringing forth caused the very best to develop in her. Joachim was a rich and pious man, who regularly gave to the poor. At the temple, Joachim’s sacrifice was rejected, so the couple’s childlessness was interpreted as a sign of divine displeasure. Joachim consequently withdrew to the desert, where he fasted and did penance for 40 days. Angels then appeared to both Joachim and Anne to promise them a child. Joachim later returned to Jerusalem and embraced Anne at the city gate, located in the Walls of Jerusalem. An ancient belief held that a child born of an elderly mother who had given up hope of having offspring was destined for great things. Joachim is named as the patron saint of fathers, grandfathers, grandparents, married couples, cabinet makers, and linen traders. “The Holy Ghost shall come upon thee, and the power of the Highest shall overshadow thee” (Luke 1:35, KJV).
  • “the other woman carrying candle, book, bell, funeral wreath”: “woman carrying candle, book, bell, funeral wreath” The phrase “bell, book, and candle” refers to a Latin Christian method of excommunication by anathema, imposed on a person who had committed an exceptionally grievous sin. Evidently introduced by Pope Zachary around the middle of the 8th century, the rite was once used by the Latin Church. This form of excommunication was inflicted on Robert II of France by Pope Gregory V for his marriage to Bertha of Burgundy in the year 996, because Bertha was his second cousin. He was later reconciled with the Church after negotiations with Gregory’s successor Pope Silvester II. (Wikipedia)
