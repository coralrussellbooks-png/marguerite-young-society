Intertextuality in MMMD

6. The Canon Through Its Wounds

← Previous · Intertextuality in MMMD Home · Next →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh

Allusions: Milton · Romantic Poets

Definition from research

When the great figures of the past enter the novel, they tend to arrive injured, humiliated, or pitied.

Catherine's bedroom receives "crippled Mr. Alexander Pope, for example, he who had been scorned by Lady Mary Wortley Montagu." Then come "Lord Byron with his club foot" and "blind Milton dictating Paradise Lost to those indifferent daughters" (chapter 2). The house is haunted by "the bloody head of Alcibiades staring through an upper window" (chapter 2). Years later the head of Alcibiades is "in the drainpipe, literally" (chapter 70). Among Catherine's languages is "the language of Volpone who died an imaginary death" (chapter 40).

Miss MacIntosh's adversaries go the same way. For most of the book she scolds Constantine, Franklin, and Edison as "vain" men. Constantine's conversion is remembered through his tears, "shedding his tears of repentance which fell around his feet as pearls" (chapter 22). In chapter 63, undressing at the edge of her own death, her scorn turns to pity: "Oh, my poor Canute who could not stay the tide although he bid the tide stand still! Constantine who changed the calendar but could not change the time."

Canute is the key figure here. The legend of the king who commanded the tide shows the limit of power, and Miss MacIntosh finally hears it that way. The great men become fellow sufferers who also failed at something too large for them.

This strategy explains why Young's allusions rarely feel reverent even when they are learned. She is not interested in the canon as a hall of monuments. She keeps the writers and emperors, but she brings them in through the body and through failure: the club foot, the blind eyes, the tears, the severed head, the tide that would not stop. This fits the principle the notes call "The Margins Reveal the Truth." The greats are seen from the margins of their own lives, where they look most human.
