Glossary

Part of Start Here, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Start Here · Read-Along 2028–2030

Young’s vocabulary is wide and old-fashioned, and she scatters Latin through the novel. Every entry below appears in the novel itself. Entries are arranged by the chapter where each first comes up, so readers can find the words for the chapter they are reading. Each chapter heading links to its Chapter Hub.

Latin and other languages

Chapter 14

alter ego
A second self: what Catherine’s confusion gives Mr. Spitzer.

Chapter 18

O tempora! O mores!
Oh, the times! Oh, the customs!: Cicero against Catiline. · More
tempus fugit
Time flies: Miss MacIntosh’s last words of the long night. · More

Chapter 22

homo homini lupus
Man is a wolf to man: from Plautus, made famous by Hobbes.

Chapter 23

Rubicon
The river Caesar crossed into Italy in 49 BC, so a step that cannot be taken back.

Chapter 29

nirvana
In Buddhism, the blowing out of desire and the end of rebirth; here one of the heavens on Mr. Spitzer’s list.
psyche
The Greek word for both soul and butterfly.

Chapter 31

coup de grâce
The finishing stroke, the death-blow (French); Young gives all three phrases side by side.

Chapter 32

femme fatale
A fatal woman, a seductress (French): the role Vera’s mother imagines for herself.
requiem
Rest: a Mass for the dead, named for its first word.

Chapter 35

terra incognita, terra firma
Unknown land; solid ground.

Chapter 38

idée fixe
A fixed idea, an obsession (French).

Chapter 39

memento mori
Remember that you must die: a reminder, often an object, kept to call death to mind.

Chapter 40

beau monde
Fashionable society (French).
ignotum per ignotius
The unknown by the still more unknown: explaining one mystery by a greater one. Young translates it in the text.

Chapter 41

sunt lacrimae rerum
There are tears in things: Aeneas weeping in Carthage, Book One of the Aeneid. · More

Chapter 47

corpo santo
Holy body: the glow of St. Elmo’s fire on a ship’s masts in a storm.

Chapter 54

januis clausis
Behind closed doors.
mare clausum
A closed sea, one nation’s waters, the opposite of mare liberum, a sea free to all.
per diem
By the day: Mr. Spitzer’s one cigarette a day.
prima donna
First lady: the leading woman singer of an opera company (Italian).

Chapter 55

carpe diem
Seize the day: Peron’s motto.
hoc opus, hic labor est
This is the work, this is the labor: Joachim’s motto, from the Sibyl in Book Six of the Aeneid. · More
morceau
A short piece of music or writing (French).
semper fidelis, semper idem, semper paratus
Always faithful, always the same, always ready.
siste viator
Stop, traveler: an inscription on Roman roadside tombs.
sub specie aeternitatis
Under the aspect of eternity: Spinoza’s phrase, translated by Young in the text.

Chapter 58

Te Deum
We praise thee, O God: an early Christian hymn of thanksgiving.

Chapter 59

de profundis
From the depths: the opening of Psalm 130, a cry to God from the depths. Young calls Mr. Spitzer’s unheard music “silent music de profundis”. · More

Chapter 60

basso profundo
The deepest bass voice (Italian).

Chapter 61

Carthago est delenda
Carthage must be destroyed: Cato the Elder’s refrain at the end of every speech, which Mr. Spitzer hears from the world beyond as the mute leads him to the burial ground. · More
Pax vobiscum
Peace be with you: a blessing from the Latin Mass, which Mr. Spitzer cannot speak beside the mute’s grave.
respice finem
Look to the end: a reminder that one will die. · More

Chapter 62

fin de siècle
The end of the century, the style and mood of the 1890s (French).

Chapter 68

corpus delicti
The body of the crime, the proof that a crime took place. Mr. Spitzer argues that without it Miss MacIntosh may not be dead.

Chapter 75

corpus derelicti
Young’s twist on corpus delicti: abandoned bodies, the corpses the Chicago detective cannot produce.

Young’s words

Chapter 1

languorous
Pleasantly heavy and still.

Chapter 2

great auk
A flightless seabird hunted to extinction in the 1840s.
lorgnette
Eyeglasses held up by a handle.
sarcophagus
A stone coffin.
solipsism
The belief that only one’s own mind is sure to exist.
turret
A small tower.

Chapter 3

antiphon
A short chant sung as a response or refrain in church.
satyr
A woodland spirit of Greek myth, part man and part beast.
soporific
Sleep-inducing.

Chapter 4

quinine
A bitter medicine from cinchona bark, used against malaria.

Chapter 5

distaff side
The female side of a family.

Chapter 8

bend sinister
A diagonal band on a coat of arms, popularly called the bar sinister, said to mark illegitimacy.

Chapter 10

chimera
A monster made of parts of different animals.

Chapter 11

rubric
A heading or instruction, originally written in red ink.
runes
The letters of the old Germanic alphabets.

Chapter 12

chanticleer
The proud rooster of medieval fable, as in Chaucer.

Chapter 13

somnambulist
A sleepwalker.
supernumerary
Extra, beyond the usual number.

Chapter 14

antinomy
A contradiction between two conclusions that both seem justified.
codicil
An addition or amendment to a will.
porte-cochere
A covered entrance where carriages stop.
winding sheet
A burial shroud.

Chapter 18

ephemeral
Short-lived.

Chapter 19

acanthus
A plant whose leaves are a classic carved ornament, as on Corinthian columns.

Chapter 20

antediluvian patriarchs
The long-lived men of Genesis 5 who lived before the Flood, from Adam to Noah.
aureole
A ring of light around a holy figure in art.
handbreadth
An ancient measure, the width of a hand.
maneh
An ancient Hebrew weight.

Chapter 22

mackerel sky
High, rippled clouds that look like fish scales.

Chapter 25

fecundity
Fruitfulness, the power to produce many offspring.

Chapter 29

quixotic
Idealistic to the point of impracticality, after Don Quixote.
red admiral, painted lady
Two common butterflies in Mr. Spitzer’s long list of species.
somnolent
Drowsy.
transfiguration
A change into a glorified form, as Christ’s on the mountain.

Chapter 30

argosy
A large merchant ship.
lazar
A poor, diseased person, especially a leper, after Lazarus.

Chapter 32

consanguineous
Related by blood.

Chapter 37

mountebank
A charlatan, originally a quack selling remedies from a platform.

Chapter 40

imbroglio
A confused, embarrassing tangle.

Chapter 41

crinoline
A stiff petticoat or hooped frame that held skirts out.
peruke
A long wig of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

Chapter 43

bloomers
Loose trousers for women, adopted by nineteenth-century dress reformers.

Chapter 45

philatelist
A stamp collector.

Chapter 46

aubade
A dawn song, often of lovers parting.

Chapter 47

lapidarian
A cutter or engraver of stones.
mosaicist
A maker of mosaics.

Chapter 48

nacreous
Shimmering like mother-of-pearl.

Chapter 49

porphyry
A hard purple stone prized by emperors.
reveille
The bugle call that wakes soldiers.
surrey
A light four-wheeled carriage.

Chapter 50

nadir
The lowest point; in astronomy, the point directly below.
nocturne
A dreamy piece of music suggesting night.
sensorium
The whole apparatus of the senses.
sidereal
Measured by the stars.
zenith
The highest point; in astronomy, the point directly overhead.

Chapter 51

nimrod
A mighty hunter, after the Nimrod of Genesis.
semaphore
Signaling with flags or arms.

Chapter 53

contrapuntal
Having independent melodies sounding together.
skijoring
Skiing while pulled by a horse or dogs.

Chapter 54

carbuncle
A deep red garnet, cut smooth and rounded.
casbah
The walled citadel of a North African city.
cuckold
A man whose wife is unfaithful.
droshky
A light, open Russian carriage.
equerry
An officer who attends members of a royal household.
haberdasher
A seller of men’s clothing; in Britain, of sewing goods.
lodestar
A guiding star, especially the North Star.
majolica
Tin-glazed, brightly painted pottery.
opera hat
A collapsible top hat.
sextant
A navigator’s instrument for measuring the angle of the sun or stars.
stirrup cup
A parting drink for a rider already in the saddle.

Chapter 55

avant-courier
A forerunner sent ahead to announce an arrival.
croupier
The attendant who runs a gaming table.
parimutuel
Betting in which all wagers are pooled and shared among the winners.
schottische
A slow round dance like a polka.

Chapter 56

integument
A tough outer covering, like skin or rind.
plus fours
Baggy golf trousers ending four inches below the knee.

Chapter 57

landau
A four-wheeled carriage with a folding top.
shay
A light carriage, as in Holmes’s poem of the one-hoss shay.

Chapter 58

neap tide
A tide with the least difference between high and low water.

Chapter 61

conchologist
A collector and student of shells.
water ouzel
The American dipper, a songbird that walks underwater in streams.

Chapter 63

ironmonger
A seller of hardware.

Chapter 64

phrenologist
Someone who claimed to read character from the bumps of the skull.

Chapter 66

anthracite
Hard, slow-burning coal.

Chapter 72

ergot
A grain fungus once given to bring on labor, and used to end pregnancies.
layette
The clothes and supplies prepared for a newborn.

Chapter 75

maypole
A decorated pole for spring dances.

Chapter 77

baby farming
Taking in infants for payment, a practice often linked to neglect and death.

Chapter 78

icebox
A cabinet cooled by a block of ice, before electric refrigerators.

Chapter 79

spiritualism
The belief, popular from the 1840s, that the living can speak with the dead through mediums.

Chapter 82

jackdaw
A small crow.
