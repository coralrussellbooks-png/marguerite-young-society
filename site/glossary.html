Glossary

Start Here · Read-Along 2028–2030

Young’s vocabulary is wide and old-fashioned, and she scatters Latin through the novel. Every entry below appears in the novel itself, with the chapter where it first comes up. The chapter number links to its Chapter Hub.

Latin and other languages

alter ego
A second self: what Catherine’s confusion gives Mr. Spitzer. Chapter 14
basso profundo
The deepest bass voice (Italian). Chapter 60
beau monde
Fashionable society (French). Chapter 40
carpe diem
Seize the day: Peron’s motto. Chapter 55
Carthago est delenda
Carthage must be destroyed: Cato the Elder’s refrain at the end of every speech, which Mr. Spitzer hears from the world beyond as the mute leads him to the burial ground. Chapter 61 · More
corpo santo
Holy body: the glow of St. Elmo’s fire on a ship’s masts in a storm. Chapter 47
corpus delicti
The body of the crime, the proof that a crime took place. Mr. Spitzer argues that without it Miss MacIntosh may not be dead. Chapter 68
corpus derelicti
Young’s twist on corpus delicti: abandoned bodies, the corpses the Chicago detective cannot produce. Chapter 75
coup de grâce
The finishing stroke, the death-blow (French); Young gives all three phrases side by side. Chapter 31
de profundis
From the depths: the opening of Psalm 130, a cry to God from the depths. Young calls Mr. Spitzer’s unheard music “silent music de profundis”. Chapter 59 · More
femme fatale
A fatal woman, a seductress (French): the role Vera’s mother imagines for herself. Chapter 32
fin de siècle
The end of the century, the style and mood of the 1890s (French). Chapter 62
hoc opus, hic labor est
This is the work, this is the labor: Joachim’s motto, from the Sibyl in Book Six of the Aeneid. Chapter 55 · More
homo homini lupus
Man is a wolf to man: from Plautus, made famous by Hobbes. Chapter 22
idée fixe
A fixed idea, an obsession (French). Chapter 38
ignotum per ignotius
The unknown by the still more unknown: explaining one mystery by a greater one. Young translates it in the text. Chapter 40
januis clausis
Behind closed doors. Chapter 54
mare clausum
A closed sea, one nation’s waters, the opposite of mare liberum, a sea free to all. Chapter 54
memento mori
Remember that you must die: a reminder, often an object, kept to call death to mind. Chapter 39
morceau
A short piece of music or writing (French). Chapter 55
nirvana
In Buddhism, the blowing out of desire and the end of rebirth; here one of the heavens on Mr. Spitzer’s list. Chapter 29
O tempora! O mores!
Oh, the times! Oh, the customs!: Cicero against Catiline. Chapter 18 · More
Pax vobiscum
Peace be with you: a blessing from the Latin Mass, which Mr. Spitzer cannot speak beside the mute’s grave. Chapter 61
per diem
By the day: Mr. Spitzer’s one cigarette a day. Chapter 54
prima donna
First lady: the leading woman singer of an opera company (Italian). Chapter 54
psyche
The Greek word for both soul and butterfly. Chapter 29
requiem
Rest: a Mass for the dead, named for its first word. Chapter 32
respice finem
Look to the end: a reminder that one will die. Chapter 61 · More
Rubicon
The river Caesar crossed into Italy in 49 BC, so a step that cannot be taken back. Chapter 23
semper fidelis, semper idem, semper paratus
Always faithful, always the same, always ready. Chapter 55
siste viator
Stop, traveler: an inscription on Roman roadside tombs. Chapter 55
sub specie aeternitatis
Under the aspect of eternity: Spinoza’s phrase, translated by Young in the text. Chapter 55
sunt lacrimae rerum
There are tears in things: Aeneas weeping in Carthage, Book One of the Aeneid. Chapter 41 · More
Te Deum
We praise thee, O God: an early Christian hymn of thanksgiving. Chapter 58
tempus fugit
Time flies: Miss MacIntosh’s last words of the long night. Chapter 18 · More
terra incognita, terra firma
Unknown land; solid ground. Chapter 35

Young’s words

acanthus
A plant whose leaves are a classic carved ornament, as on Corinthian columns. Chapter 19
antediluvian patriarchs
The long-lived men of Genesis 5 who lived before the Flood, from Adam to Noah. Chapter 20
anthracite
Hard, slow-burning coal. Chapter 66
antinomy
A contradiction between two conclusions that both seem justified. Chapter 14
antiphon
A short chant sung as a response or refrain in church. Chapter 3
argosy
A large merchant ship. Chapter 30
aubade
A dawn song, often of lovers parting. Chapter 46
aureole
A ring of light around a holy figure in art. Chapter 20
avant-courier
A forerunner sent ahead to announce an arrival. Chapter 55
baby farming
Taking in infants for payment, a practice often linked to neglect and death. Chapter 77
bend sinister
A diagonal band on a coat of arms, popularly called the bar sinister, said to mark illegitimacy. Chapter 8
bloomers
Loose trousers for women, adopted by nineteenth-century dress reformers. Chapter 43
carbuncle
A deep red garnet, cut smooth and rounded. Chapter 54
casbah
The walled citadel of a North African city. Chapter 54
chanticleer
The proud rooster of medieval fable, as in Chaucer. Chapter 12
chimera
A monster made of parts of different animals. Chapter 10
codicil
An addition or amendment to a will. Chapter 14
conchologist
A collector and student of shells. Chapter 61
consanguineous
Related by blood. Chapter 32
contrapuntal
Having independent melodies sounding together. Chapter 53
crinoline
A stiff petticoat or hooped frame that held skirts out. Chapter 41
croupier
The attendant who runs a gaming table. Chapter 55
cuckold
A man whose wife is unfaithful. Chapter 54
distaff side
The female side of a family. Chapter 5
droshky
A light, open Russian carriage. Chapter 54
ephemeral
Short-lived. Chapter 18
equerry
An officer who attends members of a royal household. Chapter 54
ergot
A grain fungus once given to bring on labor, and used to end pregnancies. Chapter 72
fecundity
Fruitfulness, the power to produce many offspring. Chapter 25
great auk
A flightless seabird hunted to extinction in the 1840s. Chapter 2
haberdasher
A seller of men’s clothing; in Britain, of sewing goods. Chapter 54
handbreadth
An ancient measure, the width of a hand. Chapter 20
icebox
A cabinet cooled by a block of ice, before electric refrigerators. Chapter 78
imbroglio
A confused, embarrassing tangle. Chapter 40
integument
A tough outer covering, like skin or rind. Chapter 56
ironmonger
A seller of hardware. Chapter 63
jackdaw
A small crow. Chapter 82
landau
A four-wheeled carriage with a folding top. Chapter 57
languorous
Pleasantly heavy and still. Chapter 1
lapidarian
A cutter or engraver of stones. Chapter 47
layette
The clothes and supplies prepared for a newborn. Chapter 72
lazar
A poor, diseased person, especially a leper, after Lazarus. Chapter 30
lodestar
A guiding star, especially the North Star. Chapter 54
lorgnette
Eyeglasses held up by a handle. Chapter 2
mackerel sky
High, rippled clouds that look like fish scales. Chapter 22
majolica
Tin-glazed, brightly painted pottery. Chapter 54
maneh
An ancient Hebrew weight. Chapter 20
maypole
A decorated pole for spring dances. Chapter 75
mosaicist
A maker of mosaics. Chapter 47
mountebank
A charlatan, originally a quack selling remedies from a platform. Chapter 37
nacreous
Shimmering like mother-of-pearl. Chapter 48
nadir
The lowest point; in astronomy, the point directly below. Chapter 50
neap tide
A tide with the least difference between high and low water. Chapter 58
nimrod
A mighty hunter, after the Nimrod of Genesis. Chapter 51
nocturne
A dreamy piece of music suggesting night. Chapter 50
opera hat
A collapsible top hat. Chapter 54
parimutuel
Betting in which all wagers are pooled and shared among the winners. Chapter 55
peruke
A long wig of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Chapter 41
philatelist
A stamp collector. Chapter 45
phrenologist
Someone who claimed to read character from the bumps of the skull. Chapter 64
plus fours
Baggy golf trousers ending four inches below the knee. Chapter 56
porphyry
A hard purple stone prized by emperors. Chapter 49
porte-cochere
A covered entrance where carriages stop. Chapter 14
quinine
A bitter medicine from cinchona bark, used against malaria. Chapter 4
quixotic
Idealistic to the point of impracticality, after Don Quixote. Chapter 29
red admiral, painted lady
Two common butterflies in Mr. Spitzer’s long list of species. Chapter 29
reveille
The bugle call that wakes soldiers. Chapter 49
rubric
A heading or instruction, originally written in red ink. Chapter 11
runes
The letters of the old Germanic alphabets. Chapter 11
sarcophagus
A stone coffin. Chapter 2
satyr
A woodland spirit of Greek myth, part man and part beast. Chapter 3
schottische
A slow round dance like a polka. Chapter 55
semaphore
Signaling with flags or arms. Chapter 51
sensorium
The whole apparatus of the senses. Chapter 50
sextant
A navigator’s instrument for measuring the angle of the sun or stars. Chapter 54
shay
A light carriage, as in Holmes’s poem of the one-hoss shay. Chapter 57
sidereal
Measured by the stars. Chapter 50
skijoring
Skiing while pulled by a horse or dogs. Chapter 53
solipsism
The belief that only one’s own mind is sure to exist. Chapter 2
somnambulist
A sleepwalker. Chapter 13
somnolent
Drowsy. Chapter 29
soporific
Sleep-inducing. Chapter 3
spiritualism
The belief, popular from the 1840s, that the living can speak with the dead through mediums. Chapter 79
stirrup cup
A parting drink for a rider already in the saddle. Chapter 54
supernumerary
Extra, beyond the usual number. Chapter 13
surrey
A light four-wheeled carriage. Chapter 49
transfiguration
A change into a glorified form, as Christ’s on the mountain. Chapter 29
turret
A small tower. Chapter 2
water ouzel
The American dipper, a songbird that walks underwater in streams. Chapter 61
winding sheet
A burial shroud. Chapter 14
zenith
The highest point; in astronomy, the point directly overhead. Chapter 50
