Summary

After Catherine took to her bed, Mr. Spitzer hired Miss Georgia MacIntosh of What Cheer, Iowa, to raise Vera. Vera paints her in full: marcelled red hair, a broken nose, a black umbrella, a tight corset, sensible shoes, long walks along the shore in any weather, strict schedules, and a deep suspicion of anything imaginary. She is everything Catherine is not, the practical Middle West in one person. Vera recalls how Miss MacIntosh dealt with an Australian bushman, a head-hunter who visited Catherine’s bedside and was obsessed with the nursemaid’s head, and she mentions Miss MacIntosh’s brother, a missionary bishop lost among the islands. Then Vera returns to the loss. Miss MacIntosh’s clothes were found on the beach and her purse held two pennies. Her body was never found, and Vera blames herself. Mr. Spitzer always insists that she was a good and dependable woman who never wavered.

Key passages

From the research

  • Miss MacIntosh is described as a “truer than the North star” guide to the bewildered child, Vera, someone who is steadfast in her moral principles and practical in her approach to life. She rejects fantasies, illusions, and the “phantoms thickening in the air” around others, especially Vera’s mother, Catherine’s hallucinatory environment. Her quick temper and no-nonsense attitude symbolize a force that dispels the fanciful and unreal.
  • The detailed imagery of Miss MacIntosh’s red hair shining like fire or sparks, her robust health sustained by sunlight and physical exercise, and her preference for outdoor life even in inclement weather emphasize her grounded, vigorous nature. She uses physical activity and connection with natural elements to keep herself strong and unclouded by fantasy or despair. This connection also reflects her philosophy that life is about facing realities, not escaping them.
  • Miss MacIntosh disdains fanciful dreams, luxury, and illusions such as the “golden apples” or “pearls” that Catherine dreams of. She insists on simplicity, honesty, and the acceptance of life’s hardships: “Life was made up, in short, of the necessary deprivations.” She believes in fulfilling one’s duties, avoiding self-pity, and not chasing impossible ideals. Her practical mindset is embodied in her tasks like mending clothes, preparing meals, and maintaining order.
  • Miss MacIntosh holds a stark, unromantic view of death as a final, dreamless sleep without illusions of heaven or afterlife grandeur. She rejects “six-winged seraphim” and “golden towers” as fanciful nonsense, insisting that if there is a heaven, it should be plain and unfurnished. This reflects her consistent rejection of fantasy and her focus on the tangible realities of mortal life.
  • Miss MacIntosh is brusque, solitary, and does not encourage intimacy or pity. Her past is largely unknown, and she has no friends, embodying a figure who compensates for others’ losses in a practical way. She has little patience for mental or emotional sorrows that lack physical cause, emphasizing courage in the face of actual trials. Her interactions with the Australian head-hunter highlight her disdain for affectation and false pretenses.
  • The Australian head-hunter serves as a foil to Miss MacIntosh’s grounded realism. She despises his semi-civilized, erratic behavior, his ambitions, and his inappropriate intimacy, seeing him as a symbol of confused and corrupt aspirations that contrast with her straightforward, honest life. Her reactions to him underscore her rejection of fantasy, deceit, and social pretense.
  • The chapter ends with the mystery of Miss MacIntosh’s disappearance, leaving behind her clothes and personal effects on the beach as if she had walked into the sea. Mr. Spitzer’s reflections underscore her enigmatic presence and the lasting impact she had on those around her. Despite her ordinariness, she was a “pearl of light” in a world of shadows and illusions, and her absence leaves an unresolved question about her fate.
  • Miss MacIntosh believes in honest work, self-discipline, and the acceptance of life’s hardships. She foresees a necessary revolution to overthrow the “powers of darkness” and strip away illusions and luxury. Her philosophy is one of immediate action and responsibility to others, emphasizing that life is short and should not be wasted in “the vain expectation of that which might never happen” or on “graven images.” This reflects a deep existential and moral seriousness.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • The chapter opens with adults “like the shadows of grotesque puppets dancing on a wall,” and “The puppeteer they dreamed of did not exist although he moved the strings.” How does this image prepare for a nursemaid who believes only in what she can see?
  • Miss MacIntosh “could endure nothing that took its time,” and her days are filled with mending, hammering, sweeping, and running. What is she outrunning?
  • She and Vera “swept our footprints away from the sands before the waters crept over” them. Why might someone so committed to the present want to leave no trace?
  • Miss MacIntosh makes a lamp of fireflies when they are lost in the dark, “carrying the lamp up high like a lighthouse.” How does this moment complicate her refusal of anything fanciful?

Characters

  • Miss MacIntosh is “so usual” that her ordinariness becomes strange in Catherine’s house. Yet she has a broken nose, a receding brow, a past no one knows, and a cheek that throbs when her hand is touched. What clues does the chapter plant that she is not what she seems?
  • Mr. Spitzer remembers hiring “the shawled figure” he took for a brunette, whose “eyebrows were dark and beetling cliffs.” Why might the novel let him misremember the one person he chose so quickly?
  • The head-hunter tells Miss MacIntosh that she “had a greater imagination than anyone who lived.” Why does this compliment offend her more than any insult?
  • Miss MacIntosh preaches common sense, but her own sayings are full of contradictions: there is no heaven, yet God is a harness-maker; the sins committed are worse than those of omission, yet God has forgotten things. How does she hold her faith together?

Connections

  • In Chapter 2 Catherine lives only in dreams. In Chapter 3 Miss MacIntosh insists “If she dreamed, she would dream that she was running.” What does Vera inherit from each of these two mothers?
  • Miss MacIntosh believes in revolution, “the whole bird,” not the left wing or the right. How do her politics grow out of her years in “the dregs of the city”?
  • The chapter ends with clothes on the beach, two pennies under a rock, and a body never found. How does this loss reshape everything Vera has said about Miss MacIntosh up to that point? See The Search for Life, for Love, for Truth That Does Not Fail.

Reflection

  • “Who but I had killed her?” Vera asks. Why do children so often blame themselves for the losses of the adults around them?
  • Miss MacIntosh says “we knew now all we would ever know . . . of misery or happiness.” Is living wholly in the present a kind of courage, a kind of hiding, or both?

Research: Characters

Miss (Georgia) MacIntosh

  • A nursemaid who embodies practicality, common sense, and moral certainty.
  • Described physically with “marcelled red hair,” “greyish, greenish eyes,” and a robust constitution.
  • She is a symbol of stability, reality, and groundedness amidst chaos and confusion.
  • Rejects illusions, fantasies, and idle dreams; values labor, discipline, and the present moment.
  • Wears simple, durable clothing; disdains luxury and extravagance.
  • Has a brusque, rough demeanor, often compared to an old sailor.
  • She is industrious, always busy, and advocates physical exercise and self-forgetfulness.
  • Holds a strict moral code, emphasizing honesty, self-control, and acceptance of life’s hardships.
  • Originates from What Cheer, Iowa, a simple town, and contrasts sharply with the opulent, dreamlike environment of the household.
  • Maintains distance from others, avoids intimacy, and does not indulge in self-pity.
  • Ultimately disappears under mysterious circumstances, leaving behind only her clothes and belongings on the beach.
  • Mentions her brother, who went out as a missionary bishop to the Easter Islands and never returned.

Vera Cartwheel

  • A child who has escaped from her mother’s “opium paradise.”
  • Looks up to Miss MacIntosh as a guide and protector.
  • Observes and reflects on the strange atmosphere and characters around her.

Mr. Spitzer

  • Employer who hired Miss MacIntosh without much inquiry.
  • Holds a fondness and respect for her practical nature.
  • Believes Miss MacIntosh might have returned to the city’s “dregs” after disappearance.
  • Is uncertain about her fate but respects her memory and legacy.

The Head-Hunter (Australian Bushman)

  • An unwelcome visitor who comes each afternoon in a black-curtained cab to sit at Catherine’s bedside.
  • Seen by Miss MacIntosh as deceitful, semi-civilized, and culturally alien.
  • His presence and behavior clash sharply with Miss MacIntosh’s values.
  • Fascinated by Miss MacIntosh’s head, which he wants to sculpt, but she rejects his advances.

Catherine Cartwheel

  • Living in a surreal, opium-induced environment with grand illusions and fantasies.
  • Contrasted with Miss MacIntosh’s grounded reality.

Research: Themes

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Miss MacIntosh represents the harsh reality of life, while Catherine’s world is filled with dreams, illusions, and hallucinations.
  • The nursemaid’s disdain for luxury, fantasy, and self-pity underscores the value of facing life’s truths directly.

  • Life is portrayed as a testing ground requiring discipline, hard work, and acceptance of necessary deprivations.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s emphasis on practical tasks (mending clothes, building fires) and physical exercise reflect this ethos.

  • Frequent imagery of time running low, life’s brevity, and the inevitability of death.

Nature and the Elements

  • She prefers exposure to natural elements over sheltered luxury or confinement.

Social Order and Class

Research: Resources

  • “like the shadows of grotesque puppets dancing on a wall”: In the allegory of the cave in Plato’s Republic, prisoners chained in the dark see only shadows thrown on a wall by figures carried past a fire, like a puppet show, and take the shadows for the whole of reality. Vera’s adults are the shadows, and “The puppeteer they dreamed of did not exist although he moved the strings.” See Plato and Platonism.
  • “Miss MacIntosh, my darling, truer than the North star”: Polaris, the North Star, stays nearly fixed above the North Pole while the other stars wheel around it, so sailors steered by it. Shakespeare’s Julius Caesar boasts, “I am constant as the northern star.” In Chapter 1 she was “the dead steersman.” See Moon and Stars.
  • “not even a game of chess as chess was the game of kings”: Chess has long been called “the royal game” and “the game of kings.” Its pieces are a feudal court, with the king who must be protected at all costs and the pawns who are sacrificed, which is why Miss MacIntosh finds the game undemocratic.
  • “her marcelled red hair fitting close like a helmet”: Marcelled hair is set in deep, regular waves with heated tongs, a method introduced by the Paris hairdresser Marcel Grateau in 1872 and most fashionable in the 1920s. See Marcelled hair (Ch 3).
  • “imaginary nightingales or turrets of ivory”: The Song of Solomon says, “Thy neck is as a tower of ivory” (7:4), and “Tower of Ivory” became a title of the Virgin Mary. By the nineteenth century an ivory tower meant a place of dreamy retreat from the world, which Miss MacIntosh will not allow. The nightingale is the bird of the Romantic poets, as in Keats’s “Ode to a Nightingale.”
  • “flower-crowned satyrs and moon-eyed minotaurs”: A satyr is a male nature spirit of Greek myth with the ears and tail of a horse, a follower of Dionysus given to wine, music, and dancing. Later Roman art gave satyrs the legs of goats, like Pan.
  • “perhaps not even by God Who had God’s limitations, Who had not created everything”: Christian theology has long described God as omniscient (knowing everything, past, present, and future), omnipotent (able to do anything), omnipresent (present everywhere), and omnibenevolent (perfectly good). Philosophers have pointed out that these attributes are hard to hold together. The problem of evil, an argument traditionally credited to Epicurus and reported by the Christian writer Lactantius, asks: if God is willing to prevent evil but not able, he is not all-powerful; if able but not willing, he is not all-good; if both able and willing, why is there evil? The paradox of the stone asks whether God could make a rock so heavy he could not lift it. And if God knows every choice in advance, some argue, free will is an illusion. Theologians have answered each argument in turn, and the debate continues. Miss MacIntosh settles it in her own way: God “had left many things uncreated.” See Faith, God, and the Supernatural.
  • “God had been her harness-maker, that He had hung her harness with tinkling bells so that it might always be known where she was”: The robe of the Hebrew high priest was hung at its hem with golden bells and pomegranates, “and his sound shall be heard when he goeth in unto the holy place” (Exodus 28:33-35). Miss MacIntosh’s bells ring on through the chapter: “her bells ringing so that I might know, through fog and mist and darkness, where she was.”
  • “labor was man’s lot upon this earth”: The Protestant work ethic, the Calvinist Work Ethic, or the Puritan Work Ethic is a work ethic concept in theology, sociology, economics and history that emphasizes that hard work, discipline, and frugality are a result of a person’s subscription to the values espoused by the Protestant faith, particularly Calvinism. The phrase was coined by the sociologist Max Weber in his book The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1904 to 1905). Weber asserted that Protestant ethics and values along with the Calvinist doctrine of asceticism and predestination gave birth to capitalism. See The Protestant Work Ethic (Ch 3) and The Protestant Ethic.
  • “almost Tyrian purple in certain lights”: Tyrian purple is a reddish-purple dye made from the glands of murex sea snails, first in the Phoenician city of Tyre. Thousands of snails were needed for a single garment, so the color was worth more than gold, and in Rome it was reserved for emperors.
  • “knocking down ninepins and the old kingpin who should be leveled like the old king of Bulgaria”: Ninepins, the old form of bowling, is the game the ghostly crew plays in the mountains in Washington Irving’s “Rip Van Winkle.” The kingpin stands at the center. Bulgaria’s kings fell one by one: Tsar Ferdinand abdicated after defeat in the First World War in 1918, his son Boris III died suddenly in 1943, and in 1946 the monarchy was abolished and the boy king Simeon II sent into exile.
  • “no hawk’s-wing sea shell with a broken wing or broken great keyhole limpet or star sea shell dragging its broken foot”: Limpets are sea snails with simple cone-shaped shells that cling to rocks. The keyhole limpet has a small opening at the top of its shell, and the hawk-wing conch and star shells are other sea snails of warm waters. Miss MacIntosh “could endure nothing that took its time.”
  • “not venturing to do what the wisest man had failed to do”: When the oracle at Delphi said that no one was wiser than Socrates, Socrates concluded that his only wisdom was knowing that he knew nothing (Plato, Apology). The wisest man in the Bible, Solomon, ends Ecclesiastes with the same humility: “of making many books there is no end.”
  • “I should make a useful contribution to human society, which was composed of simple people”: The Greek word aretē means excellence or virtue, the fulfillment of something’s purpose, and for people the act of living up to one’s full potential.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s philosophy could be compared to the Amish, Quakers, or Shakers. The Amish have a significant population in the Middle West and are known for simple living, plain dress, and reluctance to adopt many conveniences of modern technology. They educate their children up to the eighth grade and then they are apprenticed to work.
  • “these unusual luxuries which tempted no honest face”: The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899) is a treatise on economics and a detailed, social critique of conspicuous consumption. Its author, Thorstein Veblen, coined the phrase “conspicuous consumption” for the spending of money on and acquiring of luxury goods and services to publicly display economic power in order to attain or maintain social status.
  • “as if she were trying to placate it”: An old story told by the chronicler Henry of Huntingdon has King Canute set his throne on the shore and command the tide to stop, to show his flattering courtiers that even a king cannot rule the sea.
  • “rolling pea-souper”: A pea-souper was a thick, yellowish fog, named for its likeness to pea soup, especially the coal-smoke fogs of London. See Fog and Mist.
  • “wearing her salt-streaked mackintosh of almost faded plaids”: The mackintosh, the rubberized raincoat, is named for the Scottish chemist Charles Macintosh, who patented his waterproofing process in 1823, and Miss MacIntosh wears her name. See Charles MacIntosh (Ch 3).
  • “useful work for the benefit of others and strenuous physical exercise and self-forgetfulness prolonged one’s useful life”: In his speech “The Strenuous Life,” given in Chicago on April 10, 1899, Theodore Roosevelt said, “I wish to preach, not the doctrine of ignoble ease, but the doctrine of the strenuous life.” He advocated for a life of effort, hardship, and struggle over ease and idleness, arguing that it was essential for both individual character and national greatness. He believed that facing challenges, whether physical or moral, was the only way to achieve true success and national power in the coming century. The speech also reflected his views on American expansionism and the “strenuous” duty of the nation to take an active role in the world. Miss MacIntosh warns against “slothful ease.”
  • “on this old terra firma which was certainly no cloud”: Terra firma is Latin for “firm land,” dry ground as opposed to the sea.
  • “there was not much room for the individual, for those who should lift up their heads”: A Japanese proverb says, “the nail that sticks out shall get hammered down.”
  • “the mischief caused by those who did not work and yet were restless”: The saying that the devil finds work for idle hands is often thought to be biblical, but it comes from Isaac Watts’s hymn for children “Against Idleness and Mischief” (1715): “For Satan finds some mischief still / For idle hands to do.” Proverbs 16:27 has a similar thought: “An ungodly man diggeth up evil.”
  • “no time for the luxury of idle tears”: The phrase recalls Tennyson’s song from The Princess (1847): “Tears, idle tears, I know not what they mean, / Tears from the depth of some divine despair.”
  • “the dark prince charming who should carry me away and rescue me from this dark tower”: The dark prince is a symbolic figure representing an imaginary or idealized rescuer, a figure of hope and escape for Vera.
  • “this dark tower”: The phrase recalls Robert Browning’s “Childe Roland to the Dark Tower Came” (1855), whose title comes from a line in King Lear. In Chapter 30 Catherine’s search for her lost husband ends where “Childe Roland had blown his horn before a dark tower.” See Childe Roland to the Dark Tower Came.
  • “Like the princess, I would let down my hair, and he should climb it as if it were a rope”: The image is Rapunzel’s, from the Grimm brothers’ tale of 1812: “Rapunzel, Rapunzel, let down your hair.” The Grimms took the story from Charlotte-Rose de La Force’s French tale “Persinette” (1698). Many scholars have interpreted “Maiden in the Tower” stories, which Rapunzel is a part of, as a metaphor for the protection of young women from premarital relationships by overzealous guardians. Scholars have drawn comparisons of the confinement of Rapunzel in her tower to that of a convent, where women’s lives were highly controlled and they lived in exclusion from outsiders. Scholars have also noted the strong theme of love conquering all in the story, as the lovers are united after years of searching in all versions after Persinette and are ultimately happily reunited as a family. The “Maiden in the Tower” archetype has drawn comparisons to a possible lost matriarchal myth connected to the sacred marriage between the prince and the maiden and the rivalry between the maiden, representing life and spring, and the crone, representing death and winter. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “We should not nurture our grief at the breast of sorrow”: Miss MacIntosh allows no time for grief. Elisabeth Kübler-Ross later described five stages of grief in On Death and Dying (1969): denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance. See Grief, Loss, and Mourning.
  • “Nature was grand, and life was short”: Spiritual ecology is an emerging field in religion, conservation, and academia recognizing that there is a spiritual facet to all issues related to conservation, environmentalism, and earth stewardship.
  • “God, moving in His mysterious ways”: “God Moves in a Mysterious Way” is a Christian hymn written in 1773 by the English poet William Cowper, first titled “Light Shining out of Darkness.” The poem is the likely source for the phrase “God moves in mysterious ways”, although the first line of the poem actually runs “God moves in a mysterious way.” Cowper wrote it at Olney, and it is often said to have been his last hymn, written as he felt a period of deep depression coming on.
  • “Apples fell downward, not upward”: The best-known falling apple is Isaac Newton’s. Newton told his friend William Stukeley that the fall of an apple in his mother’s orchard at Woolsthorpe set him thinking about gravity, the force that keeps the moon in its orbit. Miss MacIntosh’s law of gravity is moral as well as physical: “All that fell upward were bound to fall downward.”
  • “not even should there be those golden apples of which my mother dreamed”: Catherine dreamed of the golden apples of the Hesperides in Chapter 2. The golden apple serves as a metaphor for the tension between illusion and reality, dreams and practicality, with Miss MacIntosh advocating for reality and practicality.
  • “the macintosh apple and the crab apple and the pippin and the russet”: The McIntosh apple grew from a seedling that John McIntosh found on his farm in Ontario in 1811. Pippins and russets are old English apple varieties; a russet has rough, brownish skin. The nursemaid named MacIntosh lists her own apple first.
  • “the path of the brave was narrow and girt about by thorns, and it was lonely, there being great solitude, for man was seldom good”: The passage turns on Matthew 7:13-14: “wide is the gate, and broad is the way, that leadeth to destruction, and many there be which go in thereat: Because strait is the gate, and narrow is the way, which leadeth unto life, and few there be that find it.”
  • “of fleshly pleasures and fleshpots of luxury, of dancing girls and headless men”: The fleshpots come from Exodus 16:3, where the Israelites in the wilderness long for Egypt, “when we sat by the flesh pots, and when we did eat bread to the full.” The dancing girl and the headless man recall Salome and John the Baptist from Chapter 2.
  • “none finding a celestial city”: The Celestial City is the goal of the pilgrim Christian in Bunyan’s Pilgrim’s Progress, the heavenly Jerusalem at the end of his road. Miss MacIntosh’s travelers on the path of virtue never reach it.
  • “chasing a will-o’-the-wisp, a false light”: The will-o’-the-wisp, or ignis fatuus, “foolish fire,” is a faint light seen at night over marshes, probably caused by burning marsh gas. In folklore it lures travelers off the path and into the bog.
  • “no slug with its shell rudimentary and often buried in the mantle or wanting entirely”: The phrase reads like an entry from an old natural history or dictionary, which defined slugs as mollusks whose shell is small and hidden in the mantle or wanting altogether. Miss MacIntosh wants no child without a shell to carry.
  • “my house would be well kept, and I should make my husband happy”: There has been important feminist criticism of marriage including: the effect that marriage has on individual liberty, equality between the sexes, the relation between marriage and violence, philosophical questions about how much control can a government have over its population, the amount of control a person has over another, the financial risk when measured against divorce/separation, and the questioning of the necessity to have a relationship sanctioned by government or religious authorities. There is also the emotional labor and management of the house that has been and is the unpaid work of mostly women.
  • “the numbers of tons of coal mined by coal-miners in distant mines”: Coal mining labor history in the United States is marked by dangerous work, exploitation, and the struggle for workers’ rights, driven by waves of immigration, the Civil War, and industrialization. Early laborers were diverse, including enslaved and free African Americans, and later, large numbers of immigrants from Great Britain, Ireland, and Eastern and Southern Europe were drawn to the industry by recruiters. The United Mine Workers of America (UMWA) was founded in 1890 to fight for better wages, safer conditions, and benefits, leading to significant labor conflicts and hard-won reforms like the eight-hour workday and pensions. The coal industry was marked by a series of violent conflicts and massacres, often stemming from labor disputes between striking workers (many of whom were immigrants) and mine owners who hired private security forces or leveraged local law enforcement to suppress union organizing efforts. Lattimer Massacre (September 10, 1897): In Lattimer, Pennsylvania, a sheriff’s posse opened fire on a march of unarmed striking immigrant anthracite miners, mostly of Polish, Slovak, Lithuanian, and German descent. At least 19 miners were killed, and many more were wounded. The sheriff and his deputies were later tried but acquitted. Battle of Virden (October 12, 1898): In Virden, Illinois, a gun battle erupted when the Chicago-Virden Coal Company attempted to bring in African-American strikebreakers with armed guards. Eight striking miners and at least four guards were killed. Ludlow Massacre (April 20, 1914): During a major strike in Colorado, the Colorado National Guard and guards from the Colorado Fuel & Iron Company attacked a tent colony of 1,200 striking miners and their families. The assault resulted in the deaths of approximately 19 people, including women and children, many of whom were burned to death when the camp was set on fire. Matewan Massacre (May 19, 1920): A deadly shootout occurred in Matewan, West Virginia, between Baldwin-Felts detective agency guards (hired by mine owners to evict striking workers from company-owned housing) and local residents, including the mayor and police chief, who supported the miners. Ten people died in the clash (seven detectives, two miners, and the mayor). Herrin Massacre (June 21-22, 1922): In Herrin, Illinois, a violent confrontation occurred between union miners and strikebreakers at a strip mine. After a gunfight and a surrender under the promise of safe passage, union miners killed the mine superintendent and 19 strikebreakers and guards, and three union miners also died. The incident is considered one of the most violent labor disputes in US history. Battle of Blair Mountain (August/September 1921): The culmination of the West Virginia Mine Wars, this was the largest armed insurrection in U.S. history since the Civil War. Thousands of union miners fought against local law enforcement and company-hired forces. Federal troops were eventually sent in by President Harding to quell the uprising, which involved the use of airplanes to drop bombs. Estimates of the dead vary widely. Columbine Mine Massacre (November 21, 1927): In Serene, Colorado, striking miners were met with machine-gun fire from the Colorado Rangers and guards from the Rocky Mountain Fuel Company during a strike, resulting in the deaths of six miners. These events, part of the broader “Coal Wars” (roughly 1890-1930), were pivotal in the struggle for better wages, safer working conditions, and the right to unionize in the U.S. coal industry.
  • “We dealt with the unjust circumstances under which Negroes were employed”: Miss MacIntosh’s lessons show her concern with social movements, the working class, and inequality, and she is repeatedly described as left-leaning. “Negro” was the standard term of the time. See Society, Class, and Race.
  • Ta-Nehisi Coates’ “The Case for Reparations” is a 2014 essay arguing that the U.S. owes African Americans compensation for centuries of systemic injustices, from slavery and Jim Crow laws to modern discrimination like redlining. The essay uses historical examples and personal accounts, like that of Clyde Ross, to trace how a legacy of stolen labor, land, and wealth created an enduring racial wealth gap. Coates argues that acknowledging and addressing this “moral debt” is necessary for both African Americans and the nation’s spiritual and economic healing.
  • The 1619 Project (2019) reframes American history by centering the consequences of slavery and the contributions of Black Americans, arguing that the country’s founding can be understood as beginning with the arrival of enslaved people in 1619. It explores how slavery has shaped modern America, including its economy, healthcare, and music, and challenges traditional narratives of American exceptionalism. The project originated as a New York Times Magazine special issue and has since expanded into a book and podcast series. Some of its claims, especially about the American Revolution, were disputed by a number of historians.

The interplay of light and darkness captures the novel’s thematic exploration of the fragility of human perception and the elusiveness of truth.   • “What is the best time to plant corn? Which part of the ship is abaft the main-mast?”: Old farm lore says to plant corn when the oak leaves are as big as a squirrel’s ear, when the soil has warmed. Abaft means toward the stern, and starboard is the right-hand side of a ship facing forward.
  • “two pennies with which, she used to say, her eyes would be closed when she was dead”: Charon’s obol is the term for the coin placed in or on the mouth of a dead person before burial. Greek and Latin literary sources specify the coin as an obol, and explain it was used as a payment or bribe for Charon, the ferryman who conveyed souls across the river that divided the world of the living from the world of the dead. In later custom, coins were laid on the eyelids of the dead to keep them closed. It is a powerful symbol of passage, marking the threshold between the known world and the mysterious beyond, and reflects the novel’s preoccupation with mortality and the metaphysical journey that follows.
  • “better than all the principalities, all the powers of darkness”: Ephesians 6:12 reads, “For we wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against the rulers of the darkness of this world.”
  • “What Cheer, Iowa, a small town like any other”: What Cheer is a real town in Keokuk County, Iowa, of about six hundred people. Its name is an old English greeting; the Narragansett people are said to have greeted Roger Williams in 1636 with “What cheer, netop?” (“What cheer, friend?”). In an interview Young said she had no idea there was a real town by the name of What Cheer, Iowa. She thought she had made it up. See What Cheer (Ch 3) and Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest.
  • “Few ever saw Miss MacIntosh”: Social invisibility here signifies the overlooked yet vital presence of Miss MacIntosh, whose plainness and refusal to engage in illusion cause her to be unseen or ignored, representing those who labor quietly and faithfully without acknowledgment in a world filled with illusions and distractions. It also emphasizes themes of isolation, unrecognized labor, and the contrast between appearance and reality. Social invisibility occurs when a group or individual is consistently overlooked or excluded from social, political, or economic participation. Marginalized groups like the homeless, certain ethnic groups, or individuals with disabilities can experience social invisibility. Social invisibility can lead to a lack of recognition, limited opportunities, and a feeling of being unseen or unheard.
  • “her admirer was a head-hunter. He was an Australian bushman”: In Australian English a bushman is someone at home in the outback, usually a white settler skilled in bush life, and the Bushmen contingents were Australian volunteers who fought in the Boer War in 1900. The word did not usually mean Aboriginal people. There is little evidence of head-hunting on the Australian mainland. He is a wild, primitive figure with a surprising touch of grace and aspiration. His obsessive admiration for Miss MacIntosh, coupled with his cultural and personal dislocation, make him a symbol of alienation and the tensions between civilization and savagery. Miss MacIntosh’s disdain and rejection of him underline her role as a practical, grounded counterpoint to the novel’s dreamlike and chaotic elements. This character enriches the novel’s exploration of identity, otherness, and the boundaries of social acceptance. The Australian Bushman symbolizes a primitive, untamed force, a disruption within the otherwise orderly and practical world that Miss MacIntosh represents. His “wild mien” and “calculated, incalculable eloquence” reflect a tension between civilization and savagery, rationality and irrationality. The chapter suggests that he is out of place in the refined and wealthy environment of the invalid’s house, likening his presence to a scandal or an intrusion on social norms. The bushman serves as a foil to Miss MacIntosh. While she embodies sobriety, practicality, and a clear-eyed acceptance of reality, he represents fantasy, confusion, and the disruptive power of illusion and unfulfilled ambition. His presence heightens the contrast between the grounded nursemaid and the fantastical, opium-induced world of the invalid and her acquaintances.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s head-hunter is a mixture. He is called an Australian bushman, a “wild, half-naked man of Borneo,” and a recent arrival from New Mexico, and her brother was lost in the Easter Islands. A bushman is easily confused with a bushranger, an outlaw of the bush; the most famous, Ned Kelly, was hanged in 1880, and some saw him as a champion of poor farmers against large landowners.
  • Head-hunting was practiced in the Torres Strait Islands, between Australia and New Guinea, including on Badu, where taking heads was tied to warfare and ceremonies honoring the dead. It was also practiced by the Dayak peoples of Borneo and in New Guinea, and the Māori of New Zealand preserved the tattooed heads of ancestors and enemies.
  • “his dreams . . . were that he might go over to Rome”: To “go over to Rome” was the English phrase for converting to Roman Catholicism, used of John Henry Newman and others in the Oxford Movement of the 1840s.
  • “nor was he even a good Presbyterian or Free Methodist or Shouting Baptist or Jehovah’s Witness”: Miss MacIntosh lists the plain Protestant churches of the Middle West. The Free Methodist Church was founded in New York in 1860 in protest against pew rents and slavery, and “shouting” describes the loud, ecstatic worship of revival meetings. See American Religious Movements.
  • “nor did he believe in total immersion, for he believed in sprinkling”: Churches have long divided over baptism. Baptists and many revival churches baptize adults by total immersion, while Catholics, Presbyterians, and Methodists usually baptize by sprinkling or pouring. Anabaptist groups are established in Iowa, including the Amish, the Mennonites, and the Old Order River Brethren, who baptize by immersion.
  • Baptism for especially early Christians was like being buried so that a person could rise to a new life. According to Paul, full immersion under water represents the believer’s death and burial in a watery grave with Christ, and the rising out of the water represents resurrection to a new life (Romans 6:3-4). Drownings during baptism have occasionally happened, and in the 1500s drowning was used as a punishment against the Anabaptists themselves: Felix Manz was drowned in Zurich in 1527, a mocking “third baptism.”
  • “as if he might even some day become the Pope wearing turrets of gold”: The papal tiara was a beehive-shaped crown of three tiers. Pope Paul VI was crowned with one in 1963 and then gave it away, and no pope since has worn it.
  • “walls of bones in beautiful designs like lace”: In the Capuchin Crypt beneath the church of Santa Maria della Concezione in Rome, the bones of some 3,700 friars are arranged in arches, rosettes, and chandeliers.
  • “the dead burying the dead until there were no more to bury”: Jesus tells a would-be follower who first wants to bury his father, “Follow me; and let the dead bury their dead” (Matthew 8:22).
  • “the primitive Christians of great works and little faith, not of great faith and little works”: The old quarrel between faith and works runs through Christianity. James 2:17 says, “faith, if it hath not works, is dead,” and Martin Luther, who taught salvation by faith alone, called the Epistle of James “an epistle of straw.”
  • “stopping to admire a broken Apollo”: Apollo is an Olympian deity in classical Greek and Roman religion and mythology. He is a metaphorical emblem of beauty, transformation, and the dualities inherent in life, drawing from classical mythology and natural imagery to deepen the novel’s exploration of human experience and perception
  • “a dreaming Psyche”: The story of Psyche, told by Apuleius in The Golden Ass, is about the overcoming of obstacles to the love between Psyche and Cupid/Amor. Psyche in this context embodies the spiritual and psychological dimensions of human life, the inner reality behind outward appearances, and the continual transformation inherent in the human condition.
  • “when it had fallen from a Roman roof”: Roman roofs were made of overlapping clay tiles, flat tegulae with curved imbrices covering the joints, a durable design still used across the Mediterranean.
  • “the wooden huntresses in ruined gardens”: Diana is a goddess in Roman and Hellenistic religion primarily considered a patroness of the countryside, hunters, crossroads, and the Moon. She is equated with the Greek goddess Artemis. The goddess Diana in this chapter serves as an archetype of feminine power, purity, and the sacred mysteries of birth and renewal amidst the novel’s broader themes of illusion, mortality, and transformation.
  • “born in his native bush among bush-whackers”: In Australia a bushwhacker is someone who lives in the bush or clears it. In the United States the word meant a guerrilla fighter, especially in the Civil War. See The Civil War.
  • Christian missionaries go out to spread the faith and win new converts. In Australia, governments and church missions removed tens of thousands of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children from their families between about 1910 and 1970, the Stolen Generations. The 1997 report Bringing Them Home documented the removals, and Prime Minister Kevin Rudd made a national apology in 2008. Canada’s prime minister apologized for its residential schools in 2008 and Pope Francis did so in 2022, and President Biden apologized for the American Indian boarding schools in 2024.
  • “the love songs of a lyre bird who had fallen in love with an Australian spinster and had built a tower of earth to her window”: The lyrebird is a ground-dwelling Australian bird famous for mimicking natural and artificial sounds, even chainsaws and camera shutters. The male builds mounds of earth on which he dances and sings to court the female. Rural Australia also has its Bachelors and Spinsters Balls, country dances where single men and women meet.
  • “an old Ritz menu . . . an Etruscan mirror and an ivory comb”: César Ritz opened the Hôtel Ritz in Paris in 1898 with the chef Auguste Escoffier, whose French menus set the standard of luxury dining. Etruscan mirrors were polished bronze hand mirrors, engraved on the back with scenes from myth.
  • It is interesting that the Head-hunter focuses on her head given what follows in later chapters. This also highlights a man’s feeling that he has the right to interrupt, surprise, and corner a woman when he is interested in her no matter how the woman is feeling. In feminist literature this behavior can be identified as a man who feels he is entitled, deserving, and owed a woman’s attention regardless of her feelings. He is the “hunter,” and she is his “prey.”
  • “he might still revert to the noble savage”: The phrase “noble savage” first appears in English in John Dryden’s play The Conquest of Granada (1672). It is often credited to Jean-Jacques Rousseau, who never used it, though he praised humanity in a natural state.
  • “the wild, half-naked man of Borneo”: “The Wild Man of Borneo” was a stock attraction of nineteenth-century freak shows, and P. T. Barnum exhibited men under that name.
  • “memories of Eskimos”: Eskimos are the Indigenous peoples who have traditionally inhabited the northern region from eastern Siberia to Alaska, Canada, and Greenland. Many now prefer their own names, such as Inuit and Yupik.
  • “cherubim, many-faced angels with plumes of gold”: In Ezekiel’s vision the cherubim each have four faces, of a man, a lion, an ox, and an eagle (Ezekiel 1:10).
  • In Greek and Roman mythology Charon is the ferryman of Hades who carries souls of the newly deceased across the river Styx and Acheron that divided the world of the living from the world of the dead.
  • Cheops is the Greek name of the Egyptian pharaoh Khufu, who built the Great Pyramid of Giza around 2560 B.C. See Ancient Egypt.
  • St. Simeon the Stylite was a Syriac ascetic saint who achieved notability for living thirty-seven years on a small platform on top of a pillar near Aleppo. The significance of St. Simeon the Stylite lies in his representation of spiritual isolation and relentless pursuit of higher realities, contributing to the novel’s exploration of human suffering, transcendence, and the elusive nature of truth.
  • “Daniel encompassed by sleeping lions”: Daniel 6:22 tells how Daniel is saved from lions by the God of Israel. Symbolically, this reference typically evokes themes of courage, faith, endurance, and survival in the face of danger or adversity.
  • The Appian Way, begun in 312 B.C., is one of the earliest and most important roads of the Roman Republic. Its first miles outside Rome are lined with tombs, the “houses of the dead.”
  • The City of God is a book of Christian philosophy written in Latin by Augustine of Hippo after the sack of Rome in 410. The mention of St. Augustine’s “City of God” serves as a profound symbol of the contrast between earthly decay and spiritual aspiration, reflecting the characters’ inward struggles with faith, reality, and the hope for salvation or ultimate truth beyond the ephemeral world See Augustine and Christian Theology.
  • Georgian architecture is the name given in most English-speaking countries to the set of architectural styles between 1714 and 1830, the reigns of the first four King Georges.
  • Spode is the English pottery founded by Josiah Spode in Stoke-on-Trent in 1770, famous for its blue-and-white transfer-printed ware.
  • “of Delft, of silver urns”: Delftware is the blue-and-white tin-glazed pottery made in the Dutch city of Delft from the seventeenth century, in imitation of Chinese porcelain.
  • Thomas Baker was a Methodist missionary in Fiji and is the only missionary in that country to be killed and eaten.
  • “Her own dear brother had gone out as a primitive Christian bishop from Iowa”: A bishop is an ordained member of the Christian clergy who oversees other clergy. See Richard MacIntosh.
  • Easter Island is an island and special territory of Chile in the southeastern Pacific Ocean. It is famous for its nearly 1,000 statues called moai. Its people are the Rapa Nui. Recent research has challenged the old story that an ecological collapse drove the islanders to war and cannibalism.
  • “the ladies mentioned by Plutarch”: Plutarch was a Greek Middle Platonist philosopher, biographer, and essayist, and a priest at the Temple of Apollo at Delphi, best known for his Parallel Lives of famous Greeks and Romans. Plutarch functions as a symbol of the mother’s intellectual and cultural anchoring amidst her drifting consciousness, representing a link to enduring human wisdom and the complexities of love, fate, and history that permeate her visionary world.
  • “the Protestant graveyard where Keats and Shelley slept and who after only one week in Rome had died of the marsh fever”: Keats and Shelley are buried in Rome’s Non-Catholic Cemetery near the Pyramid of Cestius. Marsh fever, or Roman fever, was malaria, whose name comes from the Italian mal aria, “bad air.” In Henry James’s “Daisy Miller” (1878), an American girl dies of Roman fever after a night visit to the Colosseum. See Romantic Poets.
  • “the best hotel would be the Quirinale”: The Quirinal Palace in Rome was a papal palace, then the royal palace, and is now the home of the president of Italy. Rome also has a Hotel Quirinale, on the Via Nazionale. It has housed thirty Popes, four Kings of Italy and twelve presidents of the Italian Republic.
  • “if he should not be admitted there, then he should try the Pantheon”: The Pantheon, completed about 125 A.D., was a temple to all the gods. Since 609 it has been a Catholic church, Santa Maria ad Martyres, and it holds the tombs of the painter Raphael and two kings of Italy, a strange hotel.
  • “mummery and flummery and junk”: Mummery is empty ritual or play-acting, from the masked mummers of folk plays. Flummery is a soft pudding, and so empty flattery or nonsense.
  • “For herself, she asked nothing”: Saint Teresa Margaret of the Sacred Heart, an eighteenth-century Carmelite nun of Florence, gave this counsel: “Always receive with equal contentment from God’s hand either consolations or sufferings, peace or distress, health or illness. Ask nothing, refuse nothing, but always be ready to do and to suffer anything that comes from His Providence.”
  • “even as the canker eats the rose, as gold and silver rust”: Shakespeare’s Sonnet 35 says “loathsome canker lives in sweetest bud,” the canker being a worm or blight. James 5:3 warns the rich, “Your gold and silver is cankered; and the rust of them shall be a witness against you,” and Matthew 6:19 speaks of treasures on earth “where moth and rust doth corrupt.”
  • “a dead lobster or broken hermit shell or lion’s mane”: The lion’s mane is the largest known jellyfish, with a reddish bell and masses of long, stinging tentacles like a mane.
  • “There must be, in fact, a revolution”: In political science, a revolution is a fundamental and relatively sudden change in political power and political organization which occurs when the population revolts against the government, typically due to perceived oppression or political incompetence.
  • Eco-socialism is an ideology that generally believes the expansion of the capitalist system is the cause of social exclusion, poverty, war and environmental degradation through globalization and imperialism, under the supervision of repressive states and transnational structures.
  • Darwinian evolution, set out in Charles Darwin’s On the Origin of Species (1859), is a theory of biological evolution stating that all species of organisms arise and develop through the natural selection of small, inherited variations that increase the individual’s ability to compete, survive, and reproduce.
  • “God’s was the strength of the unicorn”: Numbers 23:22 and 24:8 say of God that “he hath as it were the strength of an unicorn.” The King James translators used “unicorn” for the Hebrew re’em, probably the wild ox. The phrase may be interpreted as a poetic way of emphasizing the ineffable, incorruptible power of the divine, which stands apart from the fragile, chaotic human condition depicted throughout the novel.
  • “were we not still in Egypt, the waste-howling wilderness”: Miss MacIntosh’s speech is a weave of scripture. God led Israel out of Egypt, and Deuteronomy 32:10 says he found Jacob “in the waste howling wilderness.” “I was shapen in iniquity” is Psalm 51:5, “the mystery of iniquity” is 2 Thessalonians 2:7, “the joy of the hypocrite but for a moment” is Job 20:5, and “In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread” is Genesis 3:19. See The Bible.
  • “Honesty was surely, and it had always been, the best policy”: The proverb has been attributed to Sir Edwin Sandys, who used it in 1599, and it was later quoted by Benjamin Franklin and Thomas Jefferson.
  • “never to hide one’s head in the desert sands like the poor ostrich”: The belief that the ostrich hides its head in the sand goes back to Pliny the Elder. Ostriches actually lie low with their necks along the ground, or turn their eggs in the nest with their beaks.
  • “our good works should not live after us”: Miss MacIntosh reverses Mark Antony in Shakespeare’s Julius Caesar: “The evil that men do lives after them; / The good is oft interred with their bones.” See Shakespeare.
  • “not the left wing . . . not the right wing . . . but something more immense, an honest point of view, the whole bird”: Centrism is a political outlook or specific position that involves acceptance or support of a balance of a degree of social equality and a degree of social hierarchy, while opposing political changes which would result in a significant shift of society strongly to either the left or the right. Centrism has a specific meaning within the Marxist movement, referring to a position between revolution and reformism. Miss MacIntosh’s centrist position is a firm, pragmatic, and sober approach to life, emphasizing common sense, self-discipline, and the rejection of illusions as the best way to navigate a harsh and uncertain world. Miss MacIntosh’s centrism is not merely a political or social stance but a fundamental worldview that privileges order, clarity, and practical wisdom in a world otherwise engulfed by confusion and chaos. Catherine’s list in Chapter 2 included “the left-wing political movement, the right wing of the dove.”
  • “another sea-swept spar which, clothed with feathery goose barnacles”: Goose barnacles hang from driftwood on fleshy stalks, their feathery feeding legs looking like plumes. Medieval writers such as Gerald of Wales believed that barnacle geese hatched from them, since no one had seen the geese nest.
  • “without the dead body, there could be no proof of death in a legal sense”: In the common law, a person who has been missing without word for seven years may be presumed dead, but without a body or that long wait, a death cannot be proved.
  • “or even a lamp-lighter”: A lamplighter lit and tended candle or gas street lamps at dusk. Maria Susanna Cummins’s novel The Lamplighter (1854) was one of the great bestsellers of its century, and the lamplighter on his tiny planet in Antoine de Saint-Exupéry’s The Little Prince (1943) lights and puts out his lamp every minute. The lamplighter stands as a symbol of hope, constancy, and the pursuit of clarity in a world engulfed by darkness, illusion, and uncertainty.
  • “The wait-and-see policy had always been his”: “Wait and see” became a political catchphrase in 1910, when the British prime minister H. H. Asquith used it repeatedly in Parliament, and his critics made it his nickname.
  • “no man knew, unless it was Mr. Spitzer, the hour of his death”: “Watch therefore, for ye know neither the day nor the hour wherein the Son of man cometh” (Matthew 25:13).
  • “He had always preferred a dark lady”: The phrase recalls the Dark Lady of Shakespeare’s Sonnets, the dark-haired, dark-eyed woman of Sonnets 127 to 152, so unlike the fair beloved of convention. Miss MacIntosh, the redhead Mr. Spitzer remembers as a brunette, is the novel’s own dark lady.
  • “under the sign of three golden balls of a pawn shop like golden apples”: The pawnbroker’s sign of three golden balls is often traced to the Medici of Florence, whose coat of arms bore balls, and to Saint Nicholas, patron of pawnbrokers, who gave three bags of gold to save three poor girls.
