Summary

After Catherine took to her bed, Mr. Spitzer hired Miss Georgia MacIntosh of What Cheer, Iowa, to raise Vera. Vera paints her in full: marcelled red hair, a broken nose, a black umbrella, a tight corset, sensible shoes, morning swims in any weather, strict schedules, and a deep suspicion of anything imaginary. She is everything Catherine is not, the practical Middle West in one person. Vera recalls how Miss MacIntosh dealt with an Australian bushman living in the household who was obsessed with her head, and she mentions the nursemaid's brother. Then Vera returns to the loss. Miss MacIntosh's clothes were found on the beach and her purse held two pennies. Her body was never found, and Vera blames herself. Mr. Spitzer always insists that she was a good and dependable woman who never wavered.

Key passages

From the research

  • Miss MacIntosh is described as a "truer than the North star" guide to the bewildered child, Vera, someone who is steadfast in her moral principles and practical in her approach to life. She rejects fantasies, illusions, and the "phantoms thickening in the air" around others, especially Vera’s mother, Catherine’s hallucinatory environment. Her quick temper and no-nonsense attitude symbolize a force that dispels the fanciful and unreal.
  • The detailed imagery of Miss MacIntosh’s red hair shining like fire or sparks, her robust health sustained by sunlight and physical exercise, and her preference for outdoor life even in inclement weather emphasize her grounded, vigorous nature. She uses physical activity and connection with natural elements to keep herself strong and unclouded by fantasy or despair. This connection also reflects her philosophy that life is about facing realities, not escaping them.
  • Miss MacIntosh disdains fanciful dreams, luxury, and illusions such as the "golden apples" or "pearls" that Catherine dreams of. She insists on simplicity, honesty, and the acceptance of life’s hardships: "life was made up... of necessary deprivations." She believes in fulfilling one's duties, avoiding self-pity, and not chasing impossible ideals. Her practical mindset is embodied in her tasks like mending clothes, preparing meals, and maintaining order.
  • Miss MacIntosh holds a stark, unromantic view of death as a final, dreamless sleep without illusions of heaven or afterlife grandeur. She rejects "six-winged seraphim" and "golden towers" as fanciful nonsense, insisting that if there is a heaven, it should be plain and unfurnished. This reflects her consistent rejection of fantasy and her focus on the tangible realities of mortal life.
  • Miss MacIntosh is brusque, solitary, and does not encourage intimacy or pity. Her past is largely unknown, and she has no friends, embodying a figure who compensates for others' losses in a practical way. She has little patience for mental or emotional sorrows that lack physical cause, emphasizing courage in the face of actual trials. Her interactions with the Australian head-hunter highlight her disdain for affectation and false pretenses.
  • The Australian head-hunter serves as a foil to Miss MacIntosh’s grounded realism. She despises his semi-civilized, erratic behavior, his ambitions, and his inappropriate intimacy, seeing him as a symbol of confused and corrupt aspirations that contrast with her straightforward, honest life. Her reactions to him underscore her rejection of fantasy, deceit, and social pretense.
  • The chapter ends with the mystery of Miss MacIntosh’s disappearance, leaving behind her clothes and personal effects on the beach as if she had walked into the sea. Mr. Spitzer’s reflections underscore her enigmatic presence and the lasting impact she had on those around her. Despite her ordinariness, she was a "pearl of light" in a world of shadows and illusions, and her absence leaves an unresolved question about her fate.
  • Miss MacIntosh believes in honest work, self-discipline, and the acceptance of life’s hardships. She foresees a necessary revolution to overthrow the "powers of darkness" and strip away illusions and luxury. Her philosophy is one of immediate action and responsibility to others, emphasizing that life is short and should not be wasted on "vain expectations" or "idols." This reflects a deep existential and moral seriousness.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Miss (Georgia) MacIntosh

  • A nursemaid who embodies practicality, common sense, and moral certainty.
  • Described physically with "marcelled red hair," "greyish, greenish eyes," and a robust constitution.
  • She is a symbol of stability, reality, and groundedness amidst chaos and confusion.
  • Rejects illusions, fantasies, and idle dreams; values labor, discipline, and the present moment.
  • Wears simple, durable clothing; disdains luxury and extravagance.
  • Has a brusque, rough demeanor, often compared to an old sailor.
  • She is industrious, always busy, and advocates physical exercise and self-forgetfulness.
  • Holds a strict moral code, emphasizing honesty, self-control, and acceptance of life's hardships.
  • Originates from What Cheer, Iowa, a simple town, and contrasts sharply with the opulent, dreamlike environment of the household.
  • Maintains distance from others, avoids intimacy, and does not indulge in self-pity.
  • Ultimately disappears under mysterious circumstances, leaving behind only her clothes and belongings on the beach.
  • Mentions she has a brother Richard, who disappeared while being a missionary.

Vera Cartwheel

  • A child who has escaped from her mother’s “opium paradise.”
  • Looks up to Miss MacIntosh as a guide and protector.
  • Observes and reflects on the strange atmosphere and characters around her.

Mr. Spitzer

  • Employer who hired Miss MacIntosh without much inquiry.
  • Holds a fondness and respect for her practical nature.
  • Believes Miss MacIntosh might have returned to the city’s “dregs” after disappearance.
  • Is uncertain about her fate but respects her memory and legacy.

The Head-Hunter (Australian Bushman)

  • An unwelcome figure in the household.
  • Seen by Miss MacIntosh as deceitful, semi-civilized, and culturally alien.
  • His presence and behavior clash sharply with Miss MacIntosh’s values.
  • Fascinated by Miss MacIntosh’s head, which he wants to sculpt, but she rejects his advances.

Catherine Cartwheel

  • Living in a surreal, opium-induced environment with grand illusions and fantasies.
  • Contrasted with Miss MacIntosh’s grounded reality.

  • Coal mining labor history in the United States is marked by dangerous work, exploitation, and the struggle for workers' rights, driven by waves of immigration, the Civil War, and industrialization. Early laborers were diverse, including enslaved and free African Americans, and later, large numbers of immigrants from Great Britain, Ireland, and Eastern and Southern Europe were drawn to the industry by recruiters. The United Mine Workers of America (UMWA) was founded in 1890 to fight for better wages, safer conditions, and benefits, leading to significant labor conflicts and hard-won reforms like the eight-hour workday and pensions. The coal industry was marked by a series of violent conflicts and massacres, often stemming from labor disputes between striking workers (many of whom were immigrants) and mine owners who hired private security forces or leveraged local law enforcement to suppress union organizing efforts. Lattimer Massacre (September 10, 1897): In Lattimer, Pennsylvania, a sheriff's posse opened fire on a march of unarmed striking immigrant anthracite miners, mostly of Polish, Slovak, Lithuanian, and German descent. At least 19 miners were killed, and many more were wounded. The sheriff and his deputies were later tried but acquitted. Battle of Virden (October 1898): In Virden, Illinois, a gun battle erupted when the Chicago-Virden Coal Company attempted to bring in African-American strikebreakers with armed guards. Eight strikers and six guards/strikebreakers were killed. Ludlow Massacre (April 20, 1914): During a major strike in Colorado, the Colorado National Guard and guards from the Colorado Fuel & Iron Company attacked a tent colony of 1,200 striking miners and their families. The assault resulted in the deaths of approximately 19 people, including women and children, many of whom were burned to death when the camp was set on fire. Matewan Massacre (May 19, 1920): A deadly shootout occurred in Matewan, West Virginia, between Baldwin-Felts detective agency guards (hired by mine owners to evict striking workers from company-owned housing) and local residents, including the mayor and police chief, who supported the miners. Ten people died in the clash (seven detectives, two miners, and the mayor). Herrin Massacre (June 21-22, 1922): In Herrin, Illinois, a violent confrontation occurred between union miners and strikebreakers at a strip mine. After a gunfight and a subsequent surrender under the promise of safe passage, union miners murdered 20 strikebreakers and mine guards, and three union men also died. The incident is considered one of the most violent labor disputes in US history. Battle of Blair Mountain (August/September 1921): The culmination of the West Virginia Mine Wars, this was the largest armed insurrection in U.S. history since the Civil War. Thousands of union miners fought against local law enforcement and company-hired forces. Federal troops were eventually sent in by President Harding to quell the uprising, which involved the use of airplanes to drop bombs. At least 10 to 33 people were killed in the fighting. Columbine Mine Massacre (November 21, 1927): In Serene, Colorado, striking miners were met with machine-gun fire from the Colorado Rangers and guards from the Rocky Mountain Fuel Company during a strike, resulting in the deaths of six miners. These events, part of the broader "Coal Wars" (roughly 1890-1930), were pivotal in the struggle for better wages, safer working conditions, and the right to unionize in the U.S. coal industry.

  • Light and darkness carry profound symbolic significance, reflecting the tension between reality and illusion, knowledge and ignorance, life and death. For Miss MacIntosh, the practical and no-nonsense nursemaid, embodies a clear preference for reality and common sense or the "light" and is skeptical of illusions, dreams, and the "darkness." She insists on facing life’s facts plainly, avoiding the "soporific luxury" of dreaming or indulging in fanciful visions. For her, the light symbolizes clarity, truth, and the tangible aspects of existence that must be accepted and dealt with. She values labor, self-forgetfulness, and living in the present moment, and she warns against chasing illusions or "things which the conditions of this mortal frame could not simply provide". Conversely, darkness in this chapter is associated with illusion, uncertainty, and the unknown. It represents the realm of dreams, memories, and the ethereal, all of which are unstable and elusive. The darkness is also linked to death and the unconscious, a place where boundaries dissolve and individual identity blurs into a collective or universal soul. Miss MacIntosh’s mother, for example, lives in a twilight state between life and death, surrounded by shadows, dreams, and hallucinations, unable to firmly grasp reality. The interplay of light and darkness captures the novel’s thematic exploration of the fragility of human perception and the elusiveness of truth. The light is never fully stable or pure, there is always shadow and ambiguity and the darkness is not merely absence but a realm charged with mystery and the possibility of other forms of knowledge or existence. Miss MacIntosh’s pragmatic insistence on the light and rejection of the darkness is thus a stance against the chaos and uncertainty that the darkness represents, but the novel complicates this by showing how darkness is also an inescapable part of life and consciousness. Light and darkness symbolize the conflict between the tangible, knowable world and the intangible, mysterious realms of dreams and death. Miss MacIntosh’s character aligns with the light, representing order and reason, while the darkness embodies the dreamlike, uncertain, and often threatening aspects of existence. This duality frames much of the novel’s meditation on human experience.
