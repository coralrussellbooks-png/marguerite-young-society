Summary

In Hannah's house of whispering sounds and keys that fit no lock, Mr. Spitzer performs his duties as a messenger of the dead. He contrasts Peron, who burned brightly and lit matches, with himself, who always feared catching fire. He imagines himself a lamplighter of the stars and is jealous of anyone who might take his place. He sees life as a long rehearsal for the silence.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, Mr. Spitzer is depicted as possibly the last or the first messenger in the realm beyond life. He performs duties in an old house filled with mysterious sounds and shadows, symbolizing the liminal space between worlds. This passage introduces the central figure and mood of the chapter, emphasizing themes of unfinished business, lost meaning, and the persistence of music and memory beyond death. The imagery of musical litigation, black notes missing from the score, and echoes fading but haunting him evokes the elusive nature of meaning after death.
  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates many trunks and boxes, keys jangled around a suffragette's bed, and the absence of a master key to unlock all secrets. This passage symbolizes the mysteries of death, memory, and identity as locked away, fragmented, and inaccessible. Mr. Spitzer’s fumbling with keys and longing for an “arrowsmith” to pierce the dead heart reflects his desire to unlock what is hidden or to find a way through the barrier of death.
  • Spitzer is cautious, afraid of burning or sudden incandescence, while his brother is described as burning brightly, lighting matches, and tempting fate, even symbolizing a reckless embrace of life and death. This contrast explores different attitudes towards mortality: the careful, deliberate acceptance versus the flamboyant, perhaps accidental, surrender to death. It also reflects on consciousness before and after death, and the idea that death may happen twice or that love might transcend death.
  • Mr. Spitzer reflects on the idea that all things happen twice to him, including death and love. He ponders lost possibilities, the ephemeral nature of consciousness, and the fading of meaning, suggesting a cyclical or layered experience of time, where past and future blur and overlap. This philosophical meditation highlights the elusive nature of existence and memory.
  • The chapter delves into the notion that in long relationships, one might live with a "dead love" or a "stranger" better than the living love itself. The passage contemplates betrayal, death within life, and the persistence of ghosts as the memories and shadows of past emotions and identities. This complex emotional insight into love and loss deepens the psychological dimension of the chapter.
  • The old, high-shouldered house full of windows and doors that are barred or locked becomes a metaphor for the afterlife or the unknown beyond death. His inability to find the exit despite knowing the entrance symbolizes the difficulty of crossing from one state of being to another, the uncertainty of what lies beyond life.
  • Throughout the chapter, there are recurring motifs of stars fading, moonlight, dawn, and darkness. Mr. Spitzer reflects on the egocentric nature of stars, the creation of space, and the vast abyss swallowing moons and suns. These cosmic images underscore the insignificance and mystery of life and death in the grand scale of the universe, and the paradox of light and darkness existing together..
  • Mr. Spitzer muses on whether consciousness can record its own fading, the nature of the bridge between life and death, and the unknowability of one’s own end. He contemplates if life and death are two points with no journey between, or if time itself is a construct. This deep inquiry is central to the chapter's exploration of mortality and existence.
  • Mr. Spitzer imagines himself as the lamplighter who lights and extinguishes stars, feeling angry that someone else might take his place in this cosmic duty. This passage illustrates his struggle with identity, purpose, and the loneliness of his role in the cosmos, as well as the fading of stars as a metaphor for fading life.
  • Mr. Spitzer doubts his own identity and profession, feeling like an old gambler or a musician, uncertain if he is truly the person he believes himself to be. He is haunted by the idea of being a stowaway, a hidden passenger who has sunk without awareness. This passage captures the existential uncertainty and fragmented self that permeate the chapter.
  • Towards the end, Mr. Spitzer notices the loss of silver buttons and the threadbare state of his coat, symbolizing his gradual loss of wholeness, identity, and connection to the living world. The imagery of his torn cape and star shining through holes evokes vulnerability and the fragile boundary between presence and absence.
  • The chapter closes with Mr. Spitzer’s questioning of the fixed boundaries between life and death, wondering if he can move forward or backward across these lines, and whether standing still might mean living by dying or dying by living. This passage encapsulates the chapter’s overarching theme of the fluidity and mystery surrounding existence and mortality.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer (Joachim)

  • The central figure, a messenger or lawyer in the world of the dead, who reflects deeply on existence, identity, and the transition between life and death. He embodies uncertainty, introspection, and the search for meaning.

Mr. Spitzer’s Brother (Peron)

  • Contrasts with Mr. Spitzer by his flamboyance and willingness to "burn brightly," symbolizing a different approach to life and death. His early death and vibrant life are a foil to Mr. Spitzer’s cautious and contemplative nature.

Cousin Hannah, The Old Suffragette

  • A figure who carried keys from suffrage journeys, symbolic of struggles for freedom and the unlocking of mysteries.

Research: Themes

Life and Death as Continuum

See: Death and Mortality

  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates the blurred boundaries between life and death, suggesting that consciousness may exist before and after death, and that these states may not be as distinct as commonly believed.
  • The concept that death is not a definitive end but part of a continuous experience, where events happen twice or existence folds back on itself.
  • The metaphor of the "bridge" between life and death that may not physically exist but is experienced as passage or transition.

Memory, Lost Meaning, and the Music of Existence

See: Memory and the Past · Music, Sound, and Silence · The Search for Meaning

  • The chapter frequently references music as a metaphor for life’s meaning and its transient, often elusive nature. Missing notes, echoes, and lost music symbolize forgotten memories or lost meaning in life and death.
  • The idea that the greatest music might be that which has no clear meaning, symbolizing the ineffability of existence.

Identity and Self-Consciousness

See: Identity and the Self

  • Mr. Spitzer questions the nature of identity, whether the self remains unchanged after death, and how one can recognize oneself or others beyond life.
  • The possibility that he and his brother might be one and the same or that identity is fragmented and uncertain.

Fate, Chance, and the Gambler’s Paradox

See: Fate and Chance

  • The motif of gambling and chance recurs, reflecting life's unpredictability and the paradox of winning through losing or losing through never winning.
  • Mr. Spitzer sees himself as an inadvertent gambler who may have won greater stakes beyond earthly understanding.

The Role of the Messenger or Guide

  • Mr. Spitzer appears as a liminal figure, a messenger "in the world of the dead," akin to a lamplighter, lamp-lighter, or ferryman guiding souls between realms.
  • His role is both active and passive, involving opening and closing doors, unlocking mysteries, and confronting the unknown.

Light, Darkness, and the Celestial Imagery

See: Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos · Light and Darkness

  • Extensive use of celestial imagery: stars, moonlight, dawn, dusk, the morning and evening star.
  • Light often symbolizes consciousness, life, and hope, while darkness suggests death, mystery, and the unknown.
  • The fading stars symbolize the impermanence of life and the universe.

Research: Resources

  • In Hegel's philosophy, negation is not simply a denial or opposition, but a dynamic and constructive force driving the development of concepts and reality. It's a key aspect of his dialectical method, where a concept (thesis) generates its opposite (antithesis), and the resolution of this opposition (synthesis) leads to a more advanced understanding. This process of negation, or "negativity," is essential for overcoming limitations and contradictions in thought and experience. Negation in the chapter functions to unsettle any notion of a stable, knowable reality or self. It reflects the novel's preoccupation with the interplay between presence and absence, being and non-being, and the limits of human understanding in the face of cosmic and existential mysteries. This persistent negation challenges the reader to confront the impermanence and ambiguity at the heart of the human condition as portrayed in the narrative.
  • “Man could not see his own end, for he passed beyond his end.” It conveys a profound meditation on human mortality and the nature of death. This means that a person cannot truly witness or comprehend the moment or reality of their own death because, by the time death occurs, the individual consciousness has already gone beyond that final point. In other words, one cannot experience or observe their own death directly from within their living perspective, as the self "passes beyond" or transcends the end of life at the moment it happens. This reflects the novel’s recurring themes about the elusive boundary between life and death, the mysterious transition, and the limitations of human understanding about these ultimate events. It suggests that death is a passage that is inherently unknowable and outside the scope of individual perception. This idea is connected to the broader exploration in the book of how life, death, memory, and identity intertwine and dissolve into one another, emphasizing the impermanence and mystery of existence
  • “Consciousness was ever that which was sporadic, fluttering, not constant even in this life, just as sustained attention was impossible” It reflects a philosophical observation on the nature of human awareness and perception. This means that consciousness is inherently intermittent and unstable; it does not flow as a continuous, unbroken stream. Even in waking life, our awareness flickers and shifts rather than remaining fixed or steady. Just as it is impossible for a person to maintain sustained, unbroken attention indefinitely, consciousness too is characterized by fluctuations, sudden shifts, and moments of absence or distraction. It emphasizes a theme throughout the novel: the elusive, fragmented quality of reality and experience. Human consciousness is not a constant, clear illumination but a series of fleeting moments: moments of insight, of awareness, but also of confusion and forgetfulness. This aligns with the novel’s broader meditation on the instability of identity, memory, and perception, where characters struggle to grasp a coherent or continuous self amid illusions, dreams, and shifting realities. This passage suggests that our experience of life is always partial and fragmented, and complete, sustained clarity or consciousness may be an unattainable ideal.
  • “much as the evening star and the morning star” Both “stars” most commonly refer to the planet Venus when it appears in the eastern sky in the morning and evening sky in the west. This metaphor captures the novel’s exploration of paradox and ambiguity: just as the evening and morning stars are one, so too might two seemingly different lives, moments, or identities be one, intertwined or overlapping in ways that defy simple distinction. It is a poetic expression of the novel’s recurring motif that boundaries between opposites often dissolve, revealing unity beneath apparent duality.
  • “for this life was a long rehearsal of music for the silence” It conveys a profound metaphor about life and death. It suggests that life is essentially a preparation or practice ("a long rehearsal") for the ultimate silence of death. The "music" symbolizes the experiences, emotions, and events of life, which, despite their variety and intensity, are temporary and transient. The "silence" represents death, the final state where all worldly noise and activity cease. This metaphor captures the novel's recurring themes of the ephemeral nature of life, the inescapable approach of death, and the idea that our earthly existence is a fleeting prelude to an unknown, silent realm beyond. The rehearsal implies that life, with all its complexities and illusions, is not the final reality but a stage leading up to the profound stillness that follows. The phrase reflects the novel's broader meditation on mortality and the illusory quality of life, emphasizing that all the "music" of human experience ultimately fades into silence.
  • “he who knew rhombs, circles, semicircles, boxes, boxes within boxes, parallelograms, octagons, octahedrons, ellipses, eclipses of light by darkness, darkness by light, darkness by darkness” It refers to Mr. Spitzer’s deep familiarity with complex, intricate patterns and forms: both geometric and metaphorical. This passage symbolizes Mr. Spitzer’s awareness and understanding of the complicated, layered, and often paradoxical nature of reality, life, and experience. The various shapes represent different dimensions and facets of existence, including the interplay and overlap of opposites such as light and darkness, presence and absence, life and death. The mention of “boxes within boxes” and “eclipses of light by darkness” suggests an awareness of nested complexities and the constant intermingling of contrasting elements. This imagery highlights Mr. Spitzer’s contemplation of the intricate, often contradictory structures that make up the world and the human condition: how clarity and obscurity, certainty and uncertainty, coexist and shape our perception and understanding. It reflects his struggle to comprehend a reality that is both ordered and chaotic, visible and hidden, tangible and elusive.
  • “It seemed better not to know that which was unknowable.” It reflects a recurring theme in the novel about the limits of human understanding and the acceptance of mystery. Mr. Spitzer, who is a contemplative and somewhat melancholic character, grapples throughout the book with existential uncertainties, the boundaries between life and death, reality and illusion, and the unknowability of ultimate truths. This specific phrase suggests a sense of cautious humility and resignation. It implies that attempting to grasp or confront certain profound or cosmic mysteries (such as the nature of existence, death, or the afterlife) may be futile or even harmful. Instead, it is better to accept that some things are beyond human comprehension and to live without the burden of trying to fully understand or control these unknowable aspects of life. The idea fits with Mr. Spitzer’s larger struggle with his identity, memory, and the elusive nature of reality, as well as the novel’s broader exploration of illusion versus reality and the limits of knowledge. Throughout the story, characters face shifting perceptions and elusive truths, and this phrase encapsulates the wisdom of accepting mystery rather than being tormented by the desire for complete understanding
  • A pigeoncote or dovecote is housing for doves or pigeons either free standing or attached to a house. They used to be an important food source.
  • Nacreous means iridescent.
  • Sir Henry Morton Stanley was a Welsh-American famous for his exploration of Central Africa and his search for David Livingstone. He claimed to have found and greeted him, “Dr Livingstone, I presume?” David Livingstone was an explorer of Africa and one of the most popular British heroes of the Victorian Era.
  • The law firm Mr. Spitzer works at is named Blackstone which may refer to Sir William Blackstone who was an English jurist, judge and Tory politician of the eighteenth century. He is most noted for writing the Commentaries on the Laws of England. The Commentaries are an influential 18th-century treatise on the common law of England. It consists of four volumes covering the rights of persons, the rights of things, of private wrongs and of public wrongs. The Commentaries was the leading work on the development of English law and played a role in the development of the American legal system. Blackstone’s Ratio is his own statement of the principle that it - is better that ten guilty persons escape than that one innocent suffer. Blackstone's analysis was picked up by Benjamin Franklin and others. John Adams, having studied Blackstone, thought it was more important to protect innocence than guilt to be punished because crimes were so frequent that it was impossible to punish all of them. If an innocent person was convicted then virtue would not matter or provide any security. If that happened then it would end all security.
  • Lucifer is also known as “the light-bringer.” In Roman mythology he was associated with the planet Venus and personified the dawn. When he was absorbed into Christian mythology he became a devil in hell and Satan was his vassal on earth.
  • Portmanteau is a large trunk or suitcase or a word blending the sounds and combining the meanings of two others.
  • The soul in the chapter signifies the profound and unresolved mystery of human existence, the interplay of life and death, and the search for meaning and identity beyond the tangible world. It embodies the novel’s themes of fragmentation, transformation, and the elusive nature of selfhood, serving as a crucial element in the narrative’s exploration of what it means to be human.
  • The Dog star is Sirius in the constellation, Canis Major, the Greater Dog. It functions as a symbol of illumination within darkness, a beacon in the uncertain realms between life and death, and a representation of the profound, often incomprehensible mysteries of human existence that Mr. Spitzer grapples with throughout the narrative.
