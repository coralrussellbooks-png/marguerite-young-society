To All My Darlings

Chapter 48

On Chapter 48 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 11, 2026

← Chapter 47 · Home · Chapter 49 →

Content note: This chapter mentions the suicide of Mr. Spitzer’s twin brother, Peron.

Summary

Alone in Cousin Hannah’s empty house before dawn, Mr. Spitzer, a messenger in the world of the dead acting in his self-appointed legal office, sorts her papers and boxes and fumbles with the forty keys she kept in her bed, among which there is no master key. The lights have gone out, and he lights matches, though he has always been afraid of catching fire. He remembers Peron, who burned like the brightest star and lit match after match, tempting fate long before his apparent suicide. All things happen twice to Mr. Spitzer, perhaps even his death and his love, and he wonders whether one may live for years with a love who died long ago, as with a stranger. He cannot find the way out of the house of windows and doors, and he hopes the watchman will not take him for a thief. He wonders whether the lamplighter is also the one who puts out the light, as the evening star and the morning star are one, and is angry that anyone else might take his place. He doubts that he is a lawyer or a musician, imagines himself a stowaway asleep in a ship that went down long ago, and decides it is better not to know the unknowable. Noticing a lost silver button and his threadbare cape, he asks whether the boundary lines between life and death are fixed, or whether he might cross them backward as well as forward.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, Mr. Spitzer is depicted as possibly the last or the first messenger in the realm beyond life. He performs duties in an old house filled with mysterious sounds and shadows, symbolizing the liminal space between worlds. This passage introduces the central figure and mood of the chapter, emphasizing themes of unfinished business, lost meaning, and the persistence of music and memory beyond death. The imagery of musical litigation, black notes missing from the score, and echoes fading but haunting him evokes the elusive nature of meaning after death.
  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates many trunks and boxes, keys jangled around a suffragette’s bed, and the absence of a master key to unlock all secrets. This passage symbolizes the mysteries of death, memory, and identity as locked away, fragmented, and inaccessible. Mr. Spitzer’s fumbling with keys and longing for an “arrowsmith” to pierce the dead heart reflects his desire to unlock what is hidden or to find a way through the barrier of death.
  • Mr. Spitzer is cautious, afraid of burning or sudden incandescence, while his brother is described as burning brightly, lighting matches, and tempting fate, even symbolizing a reckless embrace of life and death. This contrast explores different attitudes towards mortality: the careful, deliberate acceptance versus the flamboyant, perhaps accidental, surrender to death. It also reflects on consciousness before and after death, and the idea that death may happen twice or that love might transcend death.
  • Mr. Spitzer reflects on the idea that all things happen twice to him, including death and love. He ponders lost possibilities, the ephemeral nature of consciousness, and the fading of meaning, suggesting a cyclical or layered experience of time, where past and future blur and overlap. This philosophical meditation highlights the elusive nature of existence and memory.
  • The chapter delves into the notion that in long relationships, one might live with a “dead love” or a “stranger” better than the living love itself. The passage contemplates betrayal, death within life, and the persistence of ghosts as the memories and shadows of past emotions and identities. This complex emotional insight into love and loss deepens the psychological dimension of the chapter.
  • The old, high-shouldered house full of windows and doors that are barred or locked becomes a metaphor for the afterlife or the unknown beyond death. His inability to find the exit despite knowing the entrance symbolizes the difficulty of crossing from one state of being to another, the uncertainty of what lies beyond life.
  • Throughout the chapter, there are recurring motifs of stars fading, moonlight, dawn, and darkness. Mr. Spitzer reflects on the egocentric nature of stars, the creation of space, and the vast abyss swallowing moons and suns. These cosmic images underscore the insignificance and mystery of life and death in the grand scale of the universe, and the paradox of light and darkness existing together.
  • Mr. Spitzer muses on whether consciousness can record its own fading, the nature of the bridge between life and death, and the unknowability of one’s own end. He contemplates if life and death are two points with no journey between, or if time itself is a construct. This deep inquiry is central to the chapter’s exploration of mortality and existence.
  • Mr. Spitzer imagines himself as the lamplighter who lights and extinguishes stars, feeling angry that someone else might take his place in this cosmic duty. This passage illustrates his struggle with identity, purpose, and the loneliness of his role in the cosmos, as well as the fading of stars as a metaphor for fading life.
  • Mr. Spitzer doubts his own identity and profession, feeling like an old gambler or a musician, uncertain if he is truly the person he believes himself to be. He is haunted by the idea of being a stowaway, a hidden passenger who has sunk without awareness. This passage captures the existential uncertainty and fragmented self that permeate the chapter.
  • Towards the end, Mr. Spitzer notices the loss of silver buttons and the threadbare state of his coat, symbolizing his gradual loss of wholeness, identity, and connection to the living world. The imagery of his torn cape and star shining through holes evokes vulnerability and the fragile boundary between presence and absence.
  • The chapter closes with Mr. Spitzer’s questioning of the fixed boundaries between life and death, wondering if he can move forward or backward across these lines, and whether standing still might mean living by dying or dying by living. This passage encapsulates the chapter’s overarching theme of the fluidity and mystery surrounding existence and mortality.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “Perhaps the greatest music was that which had no meaning.” What would music without meaning be?
  • “The exit could not be the entrance, surely.” Why can Mr. Spitzer find his way into the house but not out?
  • “The creation was not the stars.” What does Mr. Spitzer mean when he says the abyss was the creation?

Characters

  • Peron “had burned like the brightest star,” and Mr. Spitzer “had always been afraid to light matches.” What does each brother’s relation to fire say about him?
  • Peron was never “so flamboyant as when his horse had lost,” and “He sorrowed when he won.” Why would a man rejoice in losing?
  • Cousin Hannah kept forty keys in bed with her, and “among them all, no master key.” What was she guarding?

Connections

  • In Chapter 47 Mr. Spitzer said “good-night, sweet prince.” Here he practices saying good-morning. What has changed?
  • Mr. Spitzer wonders whether he and his brother are “one and the same.” Where else in the novel do twins or doubles merge?
  • Mr. Spitzer doubts he practices law, and his music is never played. What is his true profession?

Reflection

  • “Man could not see his own end, for he passed beyond his end.” Can anyone know his own death?
  • “It seemed better not to know that which was unknowable.” Is it?
  • “Though one had lived with one’s love for years, it was many years before one recognized that long ago his love had died.” Can a person live with a stranger without knowing it?

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer (Joachim)

  • The central figure, a messenger or lawyer in the world of the dead, who reflects deeply on existence, identity, and the transition between life and death. He embodies uncertainty, introspection, and the search for meaning.
  • He is “but this unemployed lawyer who had come to make a last account tentative as all things must be, to sum up all which had been missing, all which had never been.”

Peron Spitzer

  • Contrasts with Mr. Spitzer by his flamboyance and willingness to burn, for he “had burned like the brightest star burning in the wind,” symbolizing a different approach to life and death. His early death and vibrant life are a foil to Mr. Spitzer’s cautious and contemplative nature.
  • “Long before his apparent suicide, he had tempted fate in many ways of which he was unconscious even at his brightest moments.”
  • “Never was he so flamboyant as when his horse had lost… He sorrowed when he won.”

Cousin Hannah, the Old Suffragette

  • A figure who carried keys from suffrage journeys, symbolic of struggles for freedom and the unlocking of mysteries.
  • She kept the forty keys “in bed with her that none might find her hiding place, nesting place—for even the wounded must seek a nesting place.”

Research: Themes

Life and Death as Continuum

See: Death and Mortality

  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates the blurred boundaries between life and death, suggesting that consciousness may exist before and after death, and that these states may not be as distinct as commonly believed.
  • The concept that death is not a definitive end but part of a continuous experience, where events happen twice or existence folds back on itself.
  • The metaphor of the “bridge” between life and death that may not physically exist but is experienced as passage or transition.

Memory, Lost Meaning, and the Music of Existence

See: Memory and the Past · Music, Sound, and Silence · The Search for Meaning

  • The chapter frequently references music as a metaphor for life’s meaning and its transient, often elusive nature. Missing notes, echoes, and lost music symbolize forgotten memories or lost meaning in life and death.
  • The idea that the greatest music might be that which has no clear meaning, symbolizing the ineffability of existence.

Identity and Self-Consciousness

See: Identity and the Self

  • Mr. Spitzer questions the nature of identity, whether the self remains unchanged after death, and how one can recognize oneself or others beyond life.
  • The possibility that he and his brother might be one and the same or that identity is fragmented and uncertain.

Fate, Chance, and the Gambler’s Paradox

See: Fate and Chance

  • The motif of gambling and chance recurs, reflecting life’s unpredictability and the paradox of winning through losing or losing through never winning.
  • Mr. Spitzer sees himself as an inadvertent gambler who may have won greater stakes beyond earthly understanding.

The Role of the Messenger or Guide

  • Mr. Spitzer appears as a liminal figure, a messenger “in the world of the dead,” akin to a lamplighter or to the ferryman guiding souls between realms.
  • His role is both active and passive, involving opening and closing doors, unlocking mysteries, and confronting the unknown.

Light, Darkness, and the Celestial Imagery

See: Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos · Light and Darkness

  • Extensive use of celestial imagery: stars, moonlight, dawn, dusk, the morning and evening star.
  • Light often symbolizes consciousness, life, and hope, while darkness suggests death, mystery, and the unknown.
  • The fading stars symbolize the impermanence of life and the universe.

Keys and Locks

Matches and Fire

See: Fire

  • Fire represents life, energy, and destruction. Mr. Spitzer’s fear of lighting matches contrasts with his brother’s flamboyant use of fire, symbolizing different attitudes toward life and death.

Stars and Celestial Bodies

See: Moon and Stars

  • Represent identity, fate, and the cosmic order; their fading suggests mortality and the ephemeral nature of existence.

Mirrors and Reflections

See: Mirrors and Reflections

  • Question identity and self-recognition, blurring the line between reality and illusion.

The House

See: The House

  • A metaphor for the realm between life and death or consciousness; its doors and windows are mysterious passageways.

Research: Resources

  • “Perhaps the greatest music was that which had no meaning”: “All art constantly aspires towards the condition of music” (Walter Pater, “The School of Giorgione,” The Renaissance, 1873), the art in which form and meaning cannot be told apart. See Music and Composers.
  • “left by the long-whiskered pack rat”: The pack rat, or trade rat, is said to drop what it carries when it finds something shinier to take, leaving the one in place of the other: “for certainly he had taken something away.” See the pack rat in Chapter 62. See The Pack Rat.
  • “forty keys with which to open forty trunks”: In Perrault’s “Bluebeard” (1697), a husband gives his wife the keys to every room, forbids one small key, and keeps the bodies of his former wives behind its door. Here the keys belong to the wife, who fought for those “imprisoned by their cruel husbands and lords.” See Fairy Tales and Folklore and Keys, Doors, and Locks.
  • “an arrowsmith to make that arrow which should reach this heart”: Cupid carries two arrows, one tipped with gold to kindle love and one with lead to put it to flight (Ovid, Metamorphoses 1). See Greek Mythology and Love.
  • “had gone through the door of the sun”: In the Upanishads the wise “depart free from passion through the sun to where that immortal Person dwells whose nature is imperishable” (Mundaka Upanishad 1.2.11, trans. F. Max Müller, 1884). The Sanskrit is sūrya-dvāreṇa, “by the door of the sun.” Those who return to rebirth go by the path of the moon (Chandogya Upanishad 5.10). Peron goes by the sun, Mr. Spitzer by the moon. See Hinduism.
  • “bright Peron who had lighted match after match”: John Walker of Stockton-on-Tees sold the first friction matches in 1827, and Samuel Jones of London sold copies from 1829 as “Lucifers,” light-bearers, which gave off a sulphurous smoke when struck. See Lamps, Candles, and Light.
  • “after he had consulted his broken watch”: See “his broken watch… a watch with no numbers to the hours” in the Chapter 47 resources. See Clocks and Watches.
  • “He was always posting, trotting, gaiting”: These are the paces of a riding horse. Posting is rising and falling with the trot; the rack and the slow single-foot are the extra gaits of the five-gaited American Saddlebred. See Horses and Carriages.
  • “much like the effect preceding the cause”: The White Queen lives backward: “There’s the King’s Messenger. He’s in prison now, being punished: and the trial doesn’t even begin till next Wednesday: and of course the crime comes last of all,” and “It’s a poor sort of memory that only works backwards” (Lewis Carroll, Through the Looking-Glass, 1871, chapter 5). See Alice in Wonderland.
  • “when bridge music quivered like the harp”: “O harp and altar, of the fury fused, / (How could mere toil align thy choiring strings!)” (Hart Crane, “To Brooklyn Bridge,” The Bridge, 1930). The bridge returns as a harp later in the chapter: “no harp, no bridge, no wind plucking the strings.” See American Writers.
  • “all these little deaths which were like love”: La petite mort, “the little death,” is the French phrase for the swoon of love. See Love.
  • “out, out of this life”: “Out, out, brief candle! / Life’s but a walking shadow” (Macbeth 5.5). Mr. Spitzer speaks it with a match burning in his hand. See Shakespeare and Lamps, Candles, and Light.
  • “like Stanley, find his Livingston”: Sir Henry Morton Stanley was a Welsh-American journalist and explorer of Central Africa. Sent by the New York Herald to find the missionary David Livingstone, he found him at Ujiji on Lake Tanganyika in November 1871 and reported greeting him, “Dr. Livingstone, I presume?” (How I Found Livingstone, 1872). The pages of Stanley’s diary for that meeting were torn out, and his biographer Tim Jeal doubts the words were said (Stanley: The Impossible Life of Africa’s Greatest Explorer, 2007). David Livingstone was an explorer of Africa and one of the most popular British heroes of the Victorian Era. See Exploration and the Poles.
  • “Did the house expand”: “What a curious feeling!” said Alice; “I must be shutting up like a telescope” (Lewis Carroll, Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland, 1865, chapter 1). Like Alice, Mr. Spitzer is in a house of doors and keyholes, and later “Perhaps he had passed through a keyhole.” See Alice in Wonderland.
  • “Must the poles be reversed?”: See the reversals of the earth’s magnetic poles in the Chapter 42 resources.
  • “infinitely small, infinitely divisible”: Zeno’s paradoxes turn on the infinitely divisible. See the Zeno entry in the Chapter 38 resources.
  • “through clouds of eye witnesses”: “Wherefore seeing we also are compassed about with so great a cloud of witnesses, let us lay aside every weight” (Hebrews 12:1). See The Bible.
  • “goose barnacles”: Goose barnacles were long believed to hatch into barnacle geese, since no one had seen the geese nest; Gerald of Wales reported it in his Topographia Hibernica (about 1188), and the barnacle goose is named for the belief. See Birds.
  • “the wingless female glow-worm”: The female glow-worm of Europe (Lampyris noctiluca) cannot fly; she climbs a stem at night and glows to call the winged male, who barely glows at all. See Fireflies.
  • “perhaps the pilot star when there was no pilot”: “For tho’ from out our bourne of Time and Place / The flood may bear me far, / I hope to see my Pilot face to face / When I have crost the bar” (Alfred Tennyson, “Crossing the Bar,” 1889). See Romantic Poets.
  • “tents like the tents of Arabs”: “And the cares, that infest the day, / Shall fold their tents, like the Arabs, / And as silently steal away” (Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, “The Day Is Done,” 1844). See American Writers.
  • “a customs officer at an empty port”: Hawthorne was surveyor of the Salem Custom House from 1846 to 1849 and wrote of its idle wharves in “The Custom-House,” his introduction to The Scarlet Letter (1850). Melville was a customs inspector in New York from 1866 to 1885. See American Writers.
  • “Man could not see his own end, for he passed beyond his end”: No one can witness his own death, because by the time death comes the self has already passed beyond it. Epicurus: “when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not” (Letter to Menoeceus, in Diogenes Laertius 10.125, trans. R. D. Hicks, 1925). Wittgenstein: “Death is not an event of life. Death is not lived through” (Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus 6.4311, trans. C. K. Ogden, 1922). See Epicureanism and Wittgenstein.
  • “with his mind forever divided among possibilities”: In Hegel’s philosophy, negation is not simply a denial or opposition, but a dynamic and constructive force driving the development of concepts and reality. It is a key aspect of his dialectical method, where a concept (thesis) generates its opposite (antithesis), and the resolution of this opposition (synthesis) leads to a more advanced understanding. The triad of thesis, antithesis, and synthesis is closer to Fichte’s language than to Hegel’s, who seldom used it; it was popularized by Heinrich Moritz Chalybäus (Gustav E. Mueller, “The Hegel Legend of ‘Thesis-Antithesis-Synthesis,’” Journal of the History of Ideas 19, no. 3, 1958). This process of negation, or “negativity,” is essential for overcoming limitations and contradictions in thought and experience. See Kant and Hegel.
  • “Consciousness was ever that which was sporadic, fluttering, not constant even in this life, just as sustained attention was impossible”: Consciousness here is intermittent, flickering rather than flowing, never a steady light even in waking life. William James described it instead as a stream: “It is nothing jointed; it flows” (The Principles of Psychology, 1890, chapter 9). Yet he agreed about attention: “There is no such thing as voluntary attention sustained for more than a few seconds at a time” (chapter 11). For Mr. Spitzer one note held too long becomes two notes, a note and its echo. Experience is always partial, and complete, sustained clarity may belong only to the dead, “perhaps not even then.” See William James and Pragmatism.
  • “Perhaps there was only the bridge”: “There is a land of the living and a land of the dead and the bridge is love, the only survival, the only meaning” (Thornton Wilder, The Bridge of San Luis Rey, 1927). In Zoroastrian belief the dead cross the Chinvat Bridge, wide for the righteous and narrow as a blade for the wicked. See Love.
  • “drawing all things unto themselves”: “And I, if I be lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto me” (John 12:32). See The Bible.
  • “holes in ice swallowing the sled”: Sir John Franklin’s expedition of 1845 in search of the Northwest Passage was lost when its ships, Erebus and Terror, were locked in the ice; all 129 men died. See Exploration and the Poles.
  • “The mother of creation was this great abyss”: “Verily at the first Chaos came to be” (Hesiod, Theogony 116, trans. Hugh G. Evelyn-White, 1914), and “darkness was upon the face of the deep” (Genesis 1:2). See Hesiod’s Night in the Chapter 47 resources. See Greek Mythology and Genesis.
  • “Orion flying as if it were a bird”: The Pleiades flee the hunter Orion across the sky: “when the Pleiades plunge into the misty sea to escape Orion’s rude strength” (Hesiod, Works and Days 619-620, trans. Hugh G. Evelyn-White, 1914). The Greeks heard in their name peleiades, doves, and told that they were turned into doves to escape him. See Greek Mythology and Astronomy.
  • “certainly not by those who were the living, for they were dead”: “Follow me; and let the dead bury their dead” (Matthew 8:22). See The Bible.
  • “a golden-footed honey bee buzzing inside him”: The Greeks pictured souls as bees; Porphyry wrote that the ancients called souls bees (On the Cave of the Nymphs, trans. Thomas Taylor, 1823). See Greek Mythology.
  • “Was he who was the lamp-lighter also he who put out the light”: Robert Louis Stevenson’s child watches Leerie: “For we are very lucky, with a lamp before the door, / And Leerie stops to light it as he lights so many more” (“The Lamplighter,” A Child’s Garden of Verses, 1885). In The Little Prince (1943) Antoine de Saint-Exupéry’s lamplighter lights his lamp and puts it out once a minute on a planet that turns once a minute, saying good morning and good evening (chapter 14). See Lamps, Candles, and Light.
  • “much as the evening star and the morning star”: Both “stars” are the planet Venus, seen in the east before sunrise and in the west after sunset. The Greeks called the morning star Phosphorus and the evening star Hesperus; Pythagoras or Parmenides is credited with seeing that they are one (Diogenes Laertius 8.14, 9.23), and the philosopher Gottlob Frege used the pair to show that two names may name one thing (“On Sense and Reference,” 1892). See Astronomy.
  • “good-night, sweet prince and lady”: See “good-night, sweet prince” and “good-night, my lady” in the Chapter 47 resources. See Shakespeare.
  • “He had gone down in darkness and in whirlwind”: The reverse of Elijah’s ascent: “and Elijah went up by a whirlwind into heaven” (2 Kings 2:11). See The Bible.
  • “His star had fallen, never again to rise”: “How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!” (Isaiah 14:12). Lucifer, Latin for “light-bearer,” is the morning star, the planet Venus, which the Greeks called Phosphorus and which heralds the dawn. The verse was addressed to the king of Babylon, but Christian tradition, reading it in Jerome’s Latin, made Lucifer a name for Satan, the angel who fell from heaven. See The Bible and Angels, Devils, and Myth.
  • “for this life was a long rehearsal of music for the silence”: Life is a rehearsal, a practice, for the silence of death. The “music” is the experience of a life, passing and temporary; the “silence” is death, where all of it ceases, and which “could surely not answer or reproach.” Socrates says that “the true philosophers, Simmias, are always occupied in the practice of dying” (Plato, Phaedo 67e, trans. Benjamin Jowett, 1871), and Montaigne titled an essay “That to philosophize is to learn to die” (Essays 1.20, 1580). Hamlet’s last words are “the rest is silence” (5.2). See Plato and Platonism and Shakespeare.
  • “the municipal watchman who passed down the long street of the lights going out”: On the eve of the First World War the British foreign secretary Edward Grey watched the lamps being lit in St. James’s Park and said, “The lamps are going out all over Europe; we shall not see them lit again in our life-time” (Grey, Twenty-Five Years, 1892-1916, 1925). See Lamps, Candles, and Light.
  • “the old lamplighter with his trembling ladder of dreams”: “And he dreamed, and behold a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven: and behold the angels of God ascending and descending on it” (Genesis 28:12). See Genesis.
  • “the ferryman who ferried the souls of the dead”: See Charon in the Chapter 46 resources. See Greek Mythology.
  • “he who knew rhombs, circles, semicircles”: Mr. Spitzer knows the geometry of things, rhombs, circles, boxes within boxes, octahedrons, and the eclipses of light by darkness and darkness by light: the nested, overlapping forms of a reality both ordered and hidden. He calls them “the forms of a divine nature.” In Plato’s Timaeus the elements are built of regular solids, air of the octahedron (55-56), and Galileo wrote that the book of the universe “is written in the language of mathematics, and its characters are triangles, circles, and other geometric figures” (The Assayer, 1623, trans. Stillman Drake, 1957). See Plato and Platonism.
  • “a small pharaoh in his tomb or boat”: The pharaoh was buried with a boat to sail with the sun through the underworld. In 1954 the cedar ship of Khufu, 143 feet long, was found dismantled in a sealed pit beside the Great Pyramid. See Ancient Egypt.
  • “under the lighthouse it is darkest”: The Japanese proverb tōdai moto kurashi, “it is dark at the foot of the lighthouse,” means that what is nearest is hardest to see; the tōdai was first an oil-lamp stand.
  • “having mistaken the lighthouse for the star”: Birds migrating at night are drawn to lighthouse beams and strike the glass, and keepers long recorded the dead birds found at the foot of the tower in the morning. See Birds.
  • “he was not the practitioner of Blackstone”: A practitioner of Blackstone is a lawyer. Sir William Blackstone was an English jurist, judge and Tory politician of the eighteenth century, most noted for his Commentaries on the Laws of England (1765-1769), an influential treatise on the common law in four volumes covering the rights of persons, the rights of things, private wrongs and public wrongs. The Commentaries were the leading work on English law and shaped the American legal system. Blackstone’s ratio is his statement that “it is better that ten guilty persons escape, than that one innocent suffer” (Commentaries, Book 4, chapter 27). Benjamin Franklin raised it to “a hundred guilty Persons” (letter to Benjamin Vaughan, March 14, 1785), and John Adams, who had studied Blackstone, argued that “it’s of more importance to community, that innocence should be protected, than it is, that guilt should be punished; for guilt and crimes are so frequent in the world, that all of them cannot be punished,” and that if innocence itself is condemned, “virtue itself, is no security” (closing argument, Boston Massacre trials, December 1770). Blackstone appears again in Chapter 55, where Mr. Spitzer might “never have practiced law, never have consulted Blackstone.” See the Chapter 55 resources. See Guilt, Innocence, and Justice.
  • “cases in chancery”: The suit of Jarndyce and Jarndyce in the Court of Chancery runs for generations in Charles Dickens’s Bleak House (1853) until the costs have eaten the whole estate.
  • “laws of undiscovered countries”: “The undiscover’d country from whose bourn / No traveller returns” (Hamlet 3.1) is death. See Shakespeare.
  • “the unheard music of this waning planet”: The music of the spheres, the harmony Pythagoras heard in the turning planets, is music no living ear can hear. “Heard melodies are sweet, but those unheard / Are sweeter” (John Keats, “Ode on a Grecian Urn,” 1819). See Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres and Romantic Poets.
  • “the phoenix stars”: See the burning phoenix in the Chapter 43 resources. See Greek Mythology.
  • “casting their lights like diamond horseshoes”: The Diamond Horseshoe was the ring of boxes at the Metropolitan Opera House, opened in 1883, named for the jewels of the wealthy families who held them.
  • “island universes”: In 1755 Immanuel Kant proposed that the nebulae were whole systems of stars like the Milky Way (Universal Natural History and Theory of the Heavens). The phrase “island universe” came later, from Ormsby MacKnight Mitchel’s 1846 translation of Johann Heinrich von Mädler’s Weltinsel in The Sidereal Messenger. See Kant and Hegel and Astronomy.
  • “neither spade nor heart nor diamond nor club”: The four French suits date from fifteenth-century France. See the ace of spades and the ace of hearts in the Chapter 42 resources. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “this dread henbane of human life”: The ghost of Hamlet’s father was poisoned “With juice of cursed hebenon in a vial, / And in the porches of mine ears did pour / The leperous distilment” (Hamlet 1.5); hebenon is often read as henbane. See Shakespeare.
  • “Perhaps he was the stowaway”: “But Jonah was gone down into the sides of the ship; and he lay, and was fast asleep” (Jonah 1:5). See The Bible.
  • “the portmanteau personality carrying another soul within his soul”: Portmanteau is a large trunk or suitcase opening into two halves, or a word blending the sounds and combining the meanings of two others, a sense Lewis Carroll gave it: “You see it’s like a portmanteau—there are two meanings packed up into one word” (Through the Looking-Glass, 1871, chapter 6). See Alice in Wonderland and Twins and Doubles.
  • “had he lost his soul, his little man, his miniature”: Medieval art often shows the soul leaving the dying body as a small naked figure, a little man, from the mouth. See the homunculus in the Chapter 35 resources.
  • “in the midst of raging war, a small neutral country”: Switzerland’s permanent neutrality was recognized by the powers at Paris on November 20, 1815, and it stayed neutral through both world wars.
  • “It seemed better not to know that which was unknowable”: Mr. Spitzer chooses humility before mystery: to try to grasp the ultimate things, death and what follows it, may be futile or even harmful, and it is better to live without the burden of complete understanding. Keats called this negative capability, “when man is capable of being in uncertainties, Mysteries, doubts, without any irritable reaching after fact & reason” (letter to George and Tom Keats, December 1817). “For in much wisdom is much grief: and he that increaseth knowledge increaseth sorrow” (Ecclesiastes 1:18). See Romantic Poets and Ecclesiastes.
  • “only by losing life did one win life”: “For whosoever will save his life shall lose it: and whosoever will lose his life for my sake shall find it” (Matthew 16:25). See The Bible.
  • “heart shells and bishop’s mitres”: The bishop’s mitre (Mitra mitra) is a tall, red-spotted cone of a sea shell shaped like a bishop’s hat; heart shells are cockles shaped like a heart.
  • “like the undertaker’s suit which has no back”: Burial clothes are often cut open down the back so that they can be fitted on the body lying down.
  • “like the moon which turns but one side to earth”: The moon turns once on its axis in the same time it takes to circle the earth, so the same face always looks toward the earth; its far side was first photographed by the Soviet Luna 3 in 1959. See Astronomy.
  • “circling earth with passenger birds”: Passenger pigeons once flew over North America in flocks of billions; the last, Martha, died at the Cincinnati Zoo on September 1, 1914. See Martha the Passenger Pigeon.
  • “Perhaps he was translated”: “By faith Enoch was translated that he should not see death” (Hebrews 11:5); to be translated is to be carried to heaven without dying. “Bless thee, Bottom, bless thee! thou art translated” (A Midsummer Night’s Dream 3.1). See The Bible and Shakespeare.
  • “He was the dog barking at the Dog star in the cloud”: The Dog star is Sirius in the constellation, Canis Major, the Greater Dog. Homer calls Sirius the dog of Orion, the brightest of stars but a sign of evil (Iliad 22.26-31). See Homer’s Epics and Dogs.
  • “like the lute in the whirlpool”: Poe’s “The Fall of the House of Usher” (1839) opens with lines from Béranger: “Son cœur est un luth suspendu; / Sitôt qu’on le touche il résonne,” his heart is a hanging lute; as soon as it is touched, it sounds. See Edgar Allan Poe.

Vocabulary

Abyss
A bottomless pit or chasm.
Amulet
A charm worn against evil.
Apparition
A ghost, or a ghostlike image of a person.
Barnacled
Crusted with barnacles, small shelled sea creatures that cling to rocks, pilings, and the hulls of ships.
Bellrope
A rope pulled to ring a bell, such as one that summons a servant.
Bindlestiff
A hobo who carries his belongings in a bundle.
Brigand
A bandit of the hills.
Canter
A horse’s easy three-beat gait, between a trot and a gallop.
Capacious
Roomy; able to hold much.
Carousel
A merry-go-round. Earlier, a tournament in which horsemen performed exercises and displays.
Casket
A small, often ornate box for jewels or valuables. In American usage, also a coffin, so Young’s “casket containing no jewel” carries both meanings.
Cavalier
A horseman or gallant gentleman; also offhand and disdainful.
Chancery
A court of equity, as distinct from a court of common law; in England, the Lord Chancellor’s court.
Conformation
The shape and proportions of an animal’s body, especially a horse’s.
Contraband
Forbidden; smuggled.
Derelict
A person or thing abandoned; a homeless outcast.
Diffused
Spread out widely.
Divan
A long, low couch without a back or arms.
Domino
A loose hooded cloak worn with a half mask at a masquerade.
Dominoes
A game played with small oblong tiles marked with spots.
Dubiety
Doubt; a feeling of uncertainty.
Eaves
The edges of a roof that overhang the walls.
Empearled
Adorned with pearls, or having a pearly sheen.
Euchre
A card game for four players, for which the joker was first added to the deck.
Fanlight
A half-round window, often ribbed like a fan, over a door.
Flagstone
A flat paving stone.
Glow-worm
A beetle whose wingless female and larvae give off a greenish light.
Gossamer
A fine film of cobweb; anything light and delicate.
Halo
A ring of light around the sun or moon, caused by ice crystals high in thin clouds. In art, a ring of light around the head of a saint.
Henbane
A foul-smelling, poisonous plant of the nightshade family.
Hermetical
Sealed airtight; also secret or occult, from Hermes Trismegistus, the legendary father of alchemy.
Incandescence
The giving off of light by something heated until it glows.
Ineffable
Too great or wonderful to be put into words.
Inscrutable
Impossible to understand or interpret.
Inscrutably
In a way that cannot be understood.
Keel and list
To keel over is to capsize, with the keel, the ship’s backbone along the bottom, turning upward. To list is to lean to one side, usually from water taken on.
Leeward
On the side sheltered from the wind.
Lute
A plucked string instrument with a rounded back shaped like half a pear, popular in Europe from the Middle Ages through the 1700s.
Marsh light
A will-o’-the-wisp, a flickering light over marshes from burning marsh gas.
Mitre
The tall pointed hat of a bishop.
Mollusk
A soft-bodied creature, such as a snail or clam, usually with a shell.
Moonstone
A milky, pearly-blue gemstone.
Nacreous
Like mother-of-pearl; iridescent.
Nebula
A cloud of gas and dust in space; once any hazy patch of light among the stars, including galaxies.
Nocturnal
Of or happening in the night.
Octahedron
A solid figure with eight faces.
Opalescent
Shimmering with changing colors, like an opal.
Palsy
Paralysis or uncontrollable trembling.
Phaeton
A light, open, four-wheeled carriage; also a name for some seashells.
Pigeoncote
A house for doves or pigeons, standing alone or built into a house; pigeons kept there were once an important source of food.
Plaintive
Sad and mournful in sound.
Precarious
Unstable and likely to fall or fail.
Preternatural
Beyond what is natural.
Problematical
Doubtful; hard to settle.
Rhomb
A rhombus, a parallelogram with four equal sides.
Sarcophagus
A stone coffin, often carved, placed above ground. The plural is sarcophagi.
Sentinel
A guard or watchman.
Sloop
A sailboat with one mast.
Sporadic
Occurring now and then.
Spruce
An evergreen tree of northern forests.
Starboard
The right-hand side of a ship, facing forward.
Suffragette
A woman who campaigned for women’s right to vote. The word was coined by a London newspaper in 1906 for militant British activists. Most American activists called themselves suffragists. See Women’s Suffrage.
Sulphurous
Of sulphur; hellish.
Tapestry
A heavy cloth woven with pictures, hung on walls.
Tentative
Hesitant; not certain.
Threadbare
Worn so thin the threads show.
Torpid
Sluggish; inactive.
Tort
A wrongful act, other than a breach of contract, for which one may sue.
Transmutation
A change from one form or substance into another, especially the alchemists’ dream of turning base metal into gold.
Transom
A small window above a door, often hinged to open.
Treble
The highest part in music.
Vainglorious
Excessively proud and boastful.
Vicissitude
A change of fortune, usually for the worse.
Wayfarer
A traveler, especially on foot.
Wentletrap
A sea snail whose shell winds like a spiral staircase.
Widow walk
A railed platform on a coastal roof, from which to watch for ships.
Widow’s walk
A railed rooftop platform on a coastal house, said to be where wives watched for ships.
Wrack
Wreck or ruin, as in “wrack and ruin.”
