Research notes: Chapter 70 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 70

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Lisa Lunde · Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Res Tacamah · Mrs. Hogden

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The narrator is never singular. She is simultaneously the child raised by Miss MacIntosh, the absent adult on a bus miles away, the dreamer reconstructing Mrs. Hogden from secondhand reports, and the consciousness that confesses it cannot separate these selves. She openly admits the substitution:

"It was a trick of my imagination, this substituting of the one for the other."

But the plural self extends beyond the narrator. Mrs. Hogden herself refuses singular identity (she is Mrs. Hogden, Mrs. Logden, Mrs. Fogden, Mrs. Hogden-Fogden, Mrs. Logden-Hogden-Dogden) the text proliferates her name as if to demonstrate that naming is itself unstable. She is simultaneously:

  • An arithmetic teacher
  • A possible shoplifter
  • A descendant of Daniel Boone, William the Conqueror, and Alexander Graham Bell
  • A ghost of Miss MacIntosh
  • A gargoyle in progress
  • The mistress of the house she does not own

"Mrs. Hogden or Mrs. Logden? Nobody knew which it was, as nobody thought to ask."

The mother is equally plural; she is present and absent, dead and alive, the person who wrote the letter and the person who denies writing it. Even Mr. Spitzer is doubled: Mrs. Hogden entertains two Mr. Spitzers, alike as peas in a pod. The self in this chapter is not a stable unit but a field of overlapping projections, each claiming primacy, none achieving it.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The entire architecture of this chapter is built on the impossibility of distinguishing the real from the constructed. The central question (did the mother write the letter inviting Mrs. Hogden?) is never resolved. It cannot be resolved, because:

"From nobody else could Mrs. Hogden have learned so much of my mother's most private life."

And yet the mother denies it absolutely. Both things are simultaneously true within the text's logic. The letter exists and does not exist. Mrs. Hogden was invited and was not invited.

The mother's bedroom is protected by a door that plays music when opened: reality is mediated by an aesthetic threshold. Mr. Res Tacamah, believed by Mrs. Hogden to be a shy gentleman caller, is a drug bottle. James the chauffeur, promised to Mrs. Hogden as help, has been in Alaska for a quarter century. The house itself is described as a place where:

"Shadowy figures stepped out of the walls. There were moths with human faces."

This is not presented as pathology. It is presented as the operating condition of the household, and by extension, of consciousness itself. The cave is not outside; it is the house. The house is not outside; it is the mind. The text offers no sunlit exterior.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The chapter is saturated with a grief that never announces itself as grief, which is precisely how the pillar describes it, not as event but as climate.

Miss MacIntosh's absence haunts every sentence about Mrs. Hogden. The narrator is not mourning Miss MacIntosh in this chapter; she has been mourning her continuously, and Mrs. Hogden is the latest weather formation produced by that permanent grief:

"Miss MacIntosh, with a firm hand and a resolute mind and the unswerving ideal of common sense, brought me up to understand..."

"and then it was of her I dreamed the most when she was dead, in fact, of her only."

The grief does not resolve. It recruits new objects. Mrs. Hogden becomes a vessel for the grief about Miss MacIntosh, who was herself perhaps a vessel for something prior. The narrator confesses this directly:

"Perhaps it is wrong to imply companionship where it does not exist or to be betrayed by the apparent similarity of persons who differed so much from each other."

But she cannot stop the substitution. Grief is the mechanism of substitution. The playroom (the physical site of the lost childhood, of Miss MacIntosh) becomes Mrs. Hogden's room, and then becomes empty again, and the narrator stands in it at the end asking:

"Who came here during my absence?"

The question is not really about Mrs. Hogden. It is about Miss MacIntosh. It is about the self that existed before the loss. Grief, here, is the permanent weather of the house, of the narrative, of the prose itself, which never stops circling.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator never meets Mrs. Hogden. This is the chapter's most radical formal choice, and it is a direct enactment of the Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act pillar, but inverted and complicated. The narrator cannot be in the room, and yet she witnesses anyway, through Lisa Lunde, through inference, through imagination, through the evidence left behind. She constructs a complete portrait of a woman she never saw:

"Yes, just by closing my eyes, by concentrating, I could see her now."

This is witnessing as an act of sustained attention across absence. The narrator refuses the easy conclusion that because she did not see Mrs. Hogden, Mrs. Hogden does not matter:

"After all, however, how can we confine our life to our actual life? Mrs. Hogden is important to me for her own sake."

This sentence is a manifesto of the Witness stance. The ethical obligation to see fully is not contingent on physical presence. It is an obligation of consciousness. The narrator stays in the room, the imaginative room, without flinching from Mrs. Hogden's grotesquerie, her smell, her drinking, her delusion, her loneliness, her dignity, her absurdity. She does not simplify her into villain, victim, or comic figure. She holds all of it.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The chapter is itself a recursive structure. The narrator begins by describing a dream-state in a hotel room, moves into memory of Miss MacIntosh, slides into the story of Mrs. Hogden told to her by Lisa Lunde, circles back to Miss MacIntosh, returns to Mrs. Hogden, and ends in the playroom, which is both the physical site of the story and the mental site of the narrator's construction of it.

The blackboard Mrs. Hogden leaves behind is the emblem of recursive construction:

"She would write with a piece of red or yellow chalk on the child's blackboard, four marks crossed by a fifth — as if she were counting off the days, however, not as if she were solving a problem."

She is not solving. She is marking time, building a record of her own presence, knowing it will be chalked over. And indeed the final message on the blackboard is:

"All a’s are b’s, it read—all b’s are c’s—and therefore, dear heart, all a’s are c’s. You know me."

This is a syllogism, the form of logical construction, but it is also a declaration that all substitutions are valid, that the building is made of replaceable parts, that the construction continues regardless. Mrs. Hogden builds her life in the playroom, her island of order amid boxes she never opens, knowing it is temporary, knowing she is unwanted, building anyway.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter refuses every form of conclusion. Mrs. Hogden never teaches arithmetic. The mother never learns it. The narrator never meets Mrs. Hogden. The letter's authorship is never established. Mrs. Hogden's real name is never confirmed. Her past is never disclosed:

"Talking out loud to herself, yet Mrs. Hogden never betrayed who she was or exactly why she had come to my mother's house."

"She was one of those persons who, disappearing from the earth, would have been missed by nobody at all."

Even her departure is shrouded:

"Mrs. Hogden rode away in the mist one day and was never seen again in that district."

The text does not apologize for these non-resolutions. It accumulates them as a form of structural honesty. The demand that Mrs. Hogden be explained, classified, concluded, represented by Lisa Lunde's attempts to fix her as shoplifter or madwoman, is shown to be a failure of perception:

"She was hard to classify. It would be like trying to pick up all her packages from the floor. It would take a life time."

The incompletion is not laziness. It is the text's refusal to commit the violence of false closure against a human being who exceeds every category offered to contain her.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Mrs. Hogden is a catalog of marginality. She is:

  • Too old to be employable, too present to be dismissed
  • Too poor to be respectable, too confident to be pitied
  • Too real to be a ghost, too strange to be ordinary
  • A teacher who never teaches, a companion who is never companioned
  • A woman wearing four coats, carrying an empty birdcage and birdseed for a bird she does not have

She lives at the edge of every category. The text uses her marginality not to pathologize her but to reveal the constructed nature of the categories themselves. When the servants cannot classify her:

"She was hard to classify... there was something so evasive about her, something that evaded definition."

This is not Mrs. Hogden's failure. It is the failure of classification. She is the evidence that the categories are insufficient. Similarly, Miss MacIntosh, described at the chapter's end as "neither man nor woman, neither dark nor fair", occupies the same ontological position. Both women live at the margins, and both are where the text's deepest meaning accumulates.

The old man who stole egg-beaters because they brought him closer to his dead wife belongs to the same ontology. His behavior is irrational by any utilitarian standard, and yet the narrator treats it with complete seriousness. The marginal act reveals the marginal truth: that grief and love operate outside the logic of use.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The playroom is the chapter's central spatial emblem of sedimentary time. It is described as:

"a catch-all for useless or forgotten objects"

containing "my father's most useless collection, the dolls exhumed from children's graves". The room holds the layered past of the household: Miss MacIntosh's physical exercises, the narrator's childhood basting stitches "to hold together surf and shore", the father's European travels, the mother's dreams. Mrs. Hogden moves into this sediment and adds her own layer.

When the narrator stands in the empty playroom at the end:

"The white dust was streaked with wheeling footprints as if someone had walked around and around, slapping her hands — Miss MacIntosh, doing her physical exercise."

Miss MacIntosh's footprints are still in the dust. She left years ago. She is still pressing up through the ground. The sediment is literal (dust, footprints, whiskey bottles, apple cores, broken egg shells, the artificial eyelash) each layer deposited by a different inhabitant, none of them gone.

Time in this chapter does not move forward. It accumulates downward. The mother's dreams contain the past; the playroom contains the past; the narrator's consciousness contains the past. The present moment is always already composed of everything that preceded it.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter's language is relentlessly self-aware about its own constructedness. The narrator constantly marks the limits of her knowledge:

"Perhaps I slept for a while, or perhaps I dreamed. Perhaps I was awake and dreaming of the past..."

"Who could tell?"

"Is it possible that any life can be the surface impression only, just no more than it seems to others?"

The proliferation of Mrs. Hogden's names (Hogden, Logden, Fogden, Bogden, Dogden) is a linguistic enactment of the pillar's claim. The name is not a stable referent. It is a sound that shifts. The text uses this not as error but as philosophical demonstration: if the name is unstable, the person named is constructed, and if the person is constructed, so is the reality in which she lives.

The final blackboard message is the chapter's most explicit statement about language as construction:

"All a’s are b’s, it read—all b’s are c’s—and therefore, dear heart, all a’s are c’s. You know me."

This is a logical syllogism, the most formal structure of language, used to say something that exceeds logic: that substitution is valid, that one person can stand for another, that the narrator knows Mrs. Hogden despite never having seen her. Language here names its own location: it speaks from the position of radical uncertainty and calls that uncertainty knowledge.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The chapter's most striking formal feature is its treatment of Mrs. Hogden's grotesque body and chaotic possessions as sacred material. The description of her arrival (four coats, Javanese temple bells, empty birdcage, tape measure around her neck, lemon-colored gloves with twisted strings) is rendered with the same reverence and attention as a liturgical procession.

The text explicitly reaches toward transcendence through the ordinary:

"God, if there is a God, cannot know everything, any more than Mrs. Hogden-Fogden could, poor soul. There must be at least one small, warped flower growing, I believe, contrary to the ideal of unity, outside the consciousness of God, outside His field — and that would be the crucial flower, of course, the important one."

The sacred is not in the transcendent unity. It is in the warped flower outside the field: the exception, the marginal, the overlooked. Mrs. Hogden herself is that warped flower. Her temple bells whisper as she speaks. Her radio, running down, produces voices that she hears as company. Her chess games, played alone, produce genuine tears. These are not parodies of the sacred. They are the sacred in its immanent form: found in the creaturely, the lonely, the absurd.

The Arctic tern that visited her every year at the Dutch windmill, "compelled by his own nature to circle the earth from pole to pole as if he were some kind of minor planet on its course in the starry endless heavens", is another figure of immanent transcendence. The cosmic and the domestic are not separate registers. The tern is both a bird and a cosmological force, and Mrs. Hogden is both a fat old woman and the person the cosmos singles out for its annual attention.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The chapter's final movement enacts the Crossing Point with extraordinary precision. The narrator stands in the playroom (physically absent from the house during the events described, now returned) and finds herself at the intersection of multiple temporal streams:

  • Miss MacIntosh's footprints in the dust
  • Mrs. Hogden's derelict whiskey bottles and apple cores
  • The narrator's own childhood, embedded in the room's objects
  • The blackboard message addressed to someone who is not quite her

"As if Mrs. Hogden, whoever she was, had guessed who it was I would be looking for — but I was Lisa Lunde, she had mistakenly supposed — she had written a message on the little three-legged blackboard..."

The message was meant for Lisa Lunde. It reaches the narrator. The substitution, the very substitution the chapter has been about, is enacted one final time. The narrator receives a message not addressed to her, from a woman she never met, about a woman (Miss MacIntosh) who left before Mrs. Hogden arrived. She is the crossing point of these temporal and personal streams.

"All a’s are b’s, it read—all b’s are c’s—and therefore, dear heart, all a’s are c’s. You know me."

This is the Crossing Point articulated as logic. The narrator is constituted by what was deposited before she arrived: Miss MacIntosh's lessons, her mother's dreams, Mrs. Hogden's brief habitation of the room. She is also constituting the ground for whoever reads this text. The "dear heart" addressed on the blackboard is not Lisa Lunde. It is the reader. The carrying continues.
