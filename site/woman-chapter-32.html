Chapter 32

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah

Two women stand for two ways of refusing, and their doubling is the pillar. Cousin Hannah brandishes "the strident clash of arms," fighting overwhelming battles for suffrage and emancipation, refusing to yield or to "simulate indifference as Catherine does"; Catherine keeps to her frail, dreamlike existence, her opium and her illusions. Militant action on one side and retreat into dream on the other are not opposites so much as two faces of a single unwillingness to accept the world as given, and Young holds both without ranking them into simple hero and failure.

Hannah is "the high-souled lover of woman," not a hater of men but a rescuer of women from subjugation, and her program to turn parasols and veils, the ornamental trappings of flirtation and fragility, into "weapons of offense or defense" is the reclaiming of female agency from decorative passivity. She is a "great courier of every storm," a global and historical force, and feminism in her is a worldwide siege, "storming castles" and corroding shores, persistent through repeated failure. The cost is named too: loneliness, the absence of romantic love, an "unsullied honor" preserved at the price of a solitary life. Feminist militancy exacts its price and the chapter does not hide it.

Catherine's surreal marriages, "sometimes to phantoms or natural elements," refuse conventional heterosexual roles from the other direction, elusiveness where Hannah offers confrontation. The chapter ends on the paradox of visibility and invisibility that dogs feminist struggle: Hannah dies "in bed and not upon the lonely battlefield," her public battles resolving into a private death, while Catherine longs to "die while travelling," to be transformed out of stasis. The two women's fates rhyme rather than contrast, and the doubling of action and dream, sacrifice and escape, is the chapter's finished figure for how women refuse a world that will not have them.
