What the Economy Is For and How to Build One That Serves Everyone

A Companion Document to the Constitution

The Philosophical Foundation

Capitalism presents itself as the natural expression of human nature. People are self-interested. Markets aggregate those interests efficiently. The outcomes are therefore the natural result of who people are and what they deserve. This is the most consequential construction of the second pillar: an economic system built at a specific historical moment by specific people with specific interests, producing specific outcomes that serve those interests, presenting itself as the inevitable expression of human nature and economic law. It is not. It is a choice. It was built. It can be rebuilt.

The assumptions underneath the construction are worth naming. Human beings are primarily self-interested individuals rather than relational, communal, plural selves: this is the first pillar's singular acceptable self applied to economic theory. Value is what the market will pay rather than what sustains life and enables flourishing: this is the second pillar's consensus reality applied to the accounting of what matters. The care economy, the work of raising children and tending the sick and maintaining the relationships on which all other economic activity depends, is not counted because the market does not pay for it: this is the third pillar's permanent grief, the accumulated loss of the work that sustains everything and is recognized by nothing.

The economic floor, which the constitution establishes as a right that can be enforced in court, is not charity. It is the philosophical prerequisite for everything else in the document. The person whose entire cognitive and emotional bandwidth is consumed by not having enough to eat or nowhere to sleep cannot develop the plural self, cannot grieve honestly, cannot witness, cannot build recursively. Research on trauma and the nervous system is clear: a person in survival mode cannot access the psychological bandwidth needed to act from their values, reflect on who they are, or build toward a different future. The floor is not a supplement to the philosophy. It is the material condition without which the philosophy cannot be lived.

What the Current System Is Actually Doing

The Distribution of Wealth and Power

The United States has among the highest levels of wealth inequality of any wealthy democracy. The top one percent of households holds nearly as much wealth as the bottom ninety percent combined. This concentration has increased consistently over the past fifty years, accelerating since the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic. The Gini coefficient, a numerical scale economists use to measure how unequally income is distributed across a population, has increased consistently since the 1970s and is now higher than at any point since the Great Depression.

This concentration of wealth is not the outcome of a neutral market that rewards superior contribution. It is the outcome of a series of deliberate policy choices: the reduction of top marginal tax rates, the deregulation of financial markets, the weakening of labor law and union rights, the treatment of capital income more favorably than labor income, the provision of public subsidies to industries that then used their profits to further influence the policies affecting them. Each of these choices was made by people with the political power to make them, for the benefit of people with the economic power to fund the political power that made them. The construction is self-reinforcing. Concentrated wealth produces concentrated political power which produces policies that further concentrate wealth.

The political consequences of wealth concentration are documented. A landmark study by political scientists Martin Gilens and Benjamin Page examined twenty years of policy outcomes and found that the preferences of economic elites and organized business interests have a substantial independent impact on policy, while average citizens and mass-based interest groups have little or no independent influence. In plain terms: the policies this country adopts reflect what wealthy people and corporations want, not what most people want. The democratic system described by the constitution, in which the people are the government, does not exist in the presence of the level of wealth concentration the current economic system produces. The economic system and the democratic system are in fundamental conflict. One of them has to change.

The Poverty Trap

The American poverty trap is the most thoroughly documented feature of the economic system and the most thoroughly ignored in the design of the policies that claim to address it. The poor are poor not because they have failed to make the choices that would lift them out of poverty but because the system is designed to keep them in it. The loss of benefits at income thresholds that make marginal increases in income economically irrational. The cost of childcare that exceeds the income from the job that would pay for it. The lack of transportation that makes the job inaccessible. The criminal record from the drug conviction that forecloses the employment that would have provided the income. The medical debt from the untreated condition that became an emergency that became a bankruptcy. Each of these is a deliberate feature of the system, not an accidental outcome of neutral market forces.

The poverty trap is the political system demanding performed recovery from conditions the political system produced. The person in the poverty trap is not failing to lift themselves up by their bootstraps. They are carrying the accumulated weight of a system designed to keep specific people in specific positions, performing the agency the system denies them. The economic floor is not the complete solution to the poverty trap. It is the condition without which no solution is possible: the assurance that survival does not depend on the perfect navigation of a system designed to produce failure.

The Solidarity Economy

Worker Ownership and Cooperatives

The worker cooperative is the oldest and most thoroughly researched alternative to the conventional firm. In a worker cooperative, the people who work in the enterprise own and govern it. Decisions about wages, working conditions, investment, and strategy are made democratically by the worker-owners rather than by a separate class of capital owners or their hired managers. The profits, rather than flowing primarily to the capital owners, are distributed among the worker-owners or retained for investment in the enterprise.

The Mondragon Corporation in the Basque Country of Spain is the most studied large-scale example: a network of worker cooperatives employing over eighty thousand people across manufacturing, retail, education, and finance, with sixty years of evidence on its economic performance and its effects on the communities it operates in. The Mondragon cooperatives have consistently outperformed conventional firms in their sectors on the measure of employment stability: during economic downturns, cooperatives reduce hours and wages before they reduce employment, distributing the pain of the downturn across all worker-owners rather than concentrating it on the workers with the least power. The result is communities that are more economically resilient and more equal than communities organized around conventional firms.

The public investment in worker ownership includes the cooperative development infrastructure: the technical assistance, the financing mechanisms, the legal frameworks that make it possible for workers to purchase the enterprises that employ them when owners retire or businesses are sold. The conversion of conventional businesses to worker ownership at the moment of sale, rather than the sale to private equity that extracts value and then discards the workforce, is one of the most consequential economic policy choices available and one of the least politically contested. The owners get their market price. The workers get ownership. The community keeps the enterprise.

Community Land Trusts

The community land trust removes land from the speculative market and holds it in permanent affordability. The trust owns the land and leases it to the people who use it, whether for housing or for commercial purposes, at prices that reflect the actual cost of the use rather than the speculative value of the land. The buildings and improvements on the land can be bought and sold, but the land itself remains in the trust in perpetuity, and the sale price of the buildings is limited to prevent the speculative appreciation that makes housing progressively unaffordable.

Community land trusts have been operating in the United States since the 1960s and the research on their outcomes is consistent: they produce housing that remains affordable across generations rather than affordable only until the market appreciates it away, they build community wealth rather than transferring it to landlords and speculators, and they provide housing security to people for whom market-rate housing is permanently inaccessible. The Champlain Housing Trust in Burlington, Vermont, is one of the largest and most studied examples: forty years of evidence on the production and preservation of permanently affordable housing in a market that would otherwise have displaced the people it serves.

The scaling of community land trusts requires public investment in land acquisition: the use of public resources to remove land from the speculative market and place it in permanent community ownership. This is a direct application of the economic floor to the housing provision of Article VII: the assurance that the floor of housing security does not depend on the behavior of a market that is organized around the extraction of maximum rent from people who need shelter.

Public Banking

The Bank of North Dakota is the only publicly owned state bank in the United States and it has operated since 1919. It partners with local banks rather than competing with them, providing the capital and the backing that allows local banks to make loans that the national banks would not make. It has been consistently profitable, including during the 2008 financial crisis when many private banks required public bailouts. Its profits return to the state government rather than to private shareholders.

Public banking at the federal level means a public option for banking services: the person who cannot meet the minimum balance requirements of a private bank, who is therefore unbanked or subject to the predatory fees of check cashing services, has access to basic banking services through the public system. It means the availability of credit to communities and enterprises that the private banking system has decided are not profitable enough to serve. It means the retention of the profits from banking within the public system rather than their extraction to private shareholders. The Federal Reserve already performs many of these functions at the monetary policy level. The public bank extends them to the retail banking level where most people actually interact with the financial system.

The Care Economy

The care economy is the work of raising children, tending the sick and elderly, maintaining households, and sustaining the relationships on which all other economic activity depends. It is performed primarily by women. It is largely unpaid or poorly paid. It is not counted in GDP. It is the foundation on which the market economy rests and the thing the market economy most systematically refuses to recognize.

Economists working in the feminist tradition have developed rigorous methods for counting care work in economic accounting. Studies tracking what it would cost to replace unpaid household and care work at market rates estimate that this work represents between twenty and thirty percent of GDP. The economic system that does not count this work has decided in advance that the work of women does not count as economic activity. That decision is reversed here.

The counting of care work is not only a measurement change. It is the foundation for the policy changes that follow: the recognition that care workers deserve pay and benefits and protections commensurate with the economic value of the work they perform, the investment in care infrastructure that makes the care economy more productive and less dependent on the unpaid and undervalued labor of women, the design of the work system around the full range of human needs rather than around the needs of the worker who has no care responsibilities because someone else is performing them invisibly.

Paid family and medical leave, universal childcare, elder care as a public responsibility rather than a private burden: these are not benefits. They are the infrastructure of the care economy, the investment that makes the rest of the economy possible. The country that does not provide paid parental leave, as the United States uniquely does not among wealthy democracies, is not a country that values families. It is a country that has decided that the cost of care should be externalized onto the people performing it rather than shared by the economy that benefits from it.

Taxation and the Floor

What Taxation Is For

Taxation is the mechanism by which the collective funds the collective's obligations. It is not punishment for success. It is the return of a portion of what was made possible by the collective infrastructure: the educated workforce, the legal system, the public research, the stable currency, the transportation network, the public health system, the physical security, the accumulated social capital of the community in which the economic activity occurred. The person who has accumulated great wealth did so in the context of all of these public goods. The taxation of that wealth to fund the continuation of those goods is not redistribution. It is the return of what the commons made possible to the commons that made it possible.

The Current System's Inversions

The current tax system taxes income from labor more heavily than income from capital. The person who earns their income by working pays a higher marginal rate than the person whose income comes from investments that appreciate while they sleep. This is a political choice, not an economic law. It reflects the interests of the people who have accumulated enough capital that their income comes primarily from capital rather than labor, and the political power they have used to write a tax code that serves those interests.

Consider three specific mechanisms through which the wealthiest avoid taxation at the rates the rest of us pay. The carried interest loophole allows private equity managers to pay tax on their earnings at the lower investment rate rather than the higher rate that applies to regular income, even though the money is compensation for their work. The stepped-up basis rule means that when someone inherits assets, the tax on any increase in value accumulated over the original owner's lifetime is wiped out entirely: enormous wealth passes between generations without the taxation that would otherwise apply. And transfer pricing allows large corporations to manipulate their internal accounting to make profits appear in low-tax countries while keeping costs in high-tax ones, shifting the tax burden off corporations and onto everyone else. Each of these is a political choice presented as neutral tax planning. Each costs the public treasury billions of dollars per year.

What the System Requires

Progressive taxation of income, with top marginal rates adequate to fund the floor and the public goods the constitution establishes. The research on the economic effects of top marginal rates does not support the claim that higher rates produce lower economic growth: the postwar decades of American prosperity, the period of the greatest expansion of the middle class and the greatest reduction of inequality, were decades of top marginal rates above ninety percent. The claim that high marginal rates destroy economic activity is the defense of the dominant construction: the interests of the people who benefit from low top rates presented as universal economic law.

Taxation of wealth as well as income. An annual wealth tax on assets above a defined threshold, graduated to increase with the level of wealth, produces revenue and reduces the concentration that makes the democratic system impossible. Economists Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman, who have spent their careers documenting the scale of wealth concentration and designing policy responses to it, have demonstrated both the feasibility and the revenue potential of a wealth tax at the scale needed to fund the floor.

The closure of the loopholes, havens, and mechanisms that enable the largest accumulations of wealth to pay the lowest effective tax rates. The implementation of a global minimum corporate tax, which has been agreed in principle by most wealthy countries, that prevents the competitive lowering of corporate tax rates that has shifted the burden from capital to labor over the past decades. The taxation of financial transactions at a small rate that does not affect the long-term investor but significantly affects high-frequency trading that extracts value from markets without contributing to their function.

Measuring What Matters

GDP measures the total value of goods and services produced in the economy. It does not measure whether the people in the economy are healthy, educated, housed, safe, or able to live full lives. It counts the economic activity produced by the car accident alongside the economic activity produced by the medical care that follows. It counts the cost of cleaning up the oil spill. It does not subtract the cost of the environmental damage the spill produced. It counts the production of weapons. It does not count the care work that sustains the workers who produce everything else.

Economist Kate Raworth's doughnut economics framework provides a different accounting: the economy is doing well when it operates within the social foundation, the floor of human needs represented by the rights in Article VII, and within the ecological ceiling, the planetary boundaries within which human civilization can sustain itself. The space between the floor and the ceiling is the space where human life can be lived. Growth that pushes through the ecological ceiling or that leaves people below the social foundation is not success. It is failure, regardless of what the GDP figure shows.

Several alternative economic measures attempt to track what the economy is actually producing in terms of human flourishing rather than the market value of activity. None of them is perfect. All of them are more honest than GDP as a measure of whether the economy is doing what the economy is supposed to do. The adoption of a comprehensive wellbeing measure alongside GDP as the official measure of national economic performance is the second pillar applied to accounting: the refusal to let the dominant construction of what counts as economic success continue to present itself as the objective measure of how things are going.

The Transition

The transition from the current economic system to the one this document describes is the longest and most politically contested of all the transitions the companion documents require. The current system is deeply embedded and resistant to change, defended by people with enormous resources to deploy in its defense, and embedded in international legal frameworks, trade agreements, and financial architectures that would need to be renegotiated.

The transition is not instantaneous and does not require the simultaneous transformation of every element of the current system. It requires a direction and the consistent movement of policy in that direction. The direction is toward an economy that starts from the floor rather than the market price, that counts the care economy, that treats the natural world as a rights-holder rather than a resource, that measures wellbeing rather than growth, that is organized around the plural self rather than the singular productive unit, that builds the commons rather than extracting from it.

Every policy choice that moves in that direction is part of the building. Every cooperative formed, every community land trust established, every public bank opened, every care worker whose work is recognized and compensated, every loophole closed, every wellbeing measure adopted alongside GDP: these are the building that knows it is building. The map that knows it is wrong before the ink dries and draws itself anyway. The fifth pillar enacted as economic policy.

The floor, once established, is not retracted. The commons, once built, belongs to everyone.
