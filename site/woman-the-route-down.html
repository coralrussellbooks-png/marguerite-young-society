Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

The Route Down

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh

What this book is offering

Radical Immanence is a name this edition brings to Miss MacIntosh, My Darling. It is not a name Marguerite Young used. I believe she incorporated a complete philosophical system in her novel, but I do not know if she said this bluntly to anyone. Young did not call herself a feminist, and she did not claim that any system of thought stood behind the book.

Radical Immanence is a contemporary vocabulary, a set of reading tools, offered for what the novel already enacts. The claim is not that Young studied a theory and then dramatized it. The claim runs the other way. The novel arrived at these structures through fiction, working them out sentence by sentence across the eighteen years of its writing, before the language for describing them had settled. Naming them now does not smuggle a later philosophy back into an earlier book. It gives a reader words for what the book was doing all along.

That order matters, because the first objection to a reading like this one is that it is anachronistic, that it dresses an earlier novel in the clothes of a later theory. The answer is to grant the timing and deny the direction. The theory came later. The structures came first, in the novel, made of image and scene rather than argument.

A word on the term itself, since this book leans on it. Immanence, stripped of its usual heaviness, means that meaning lives inside experience rather than above it. There is no vantage point outside the world from which things can be sorted and judged in a clean way. Radical Immanence, as a way of reading, holds that the novel refuses any such outside position. Inside the book there is no stable floor from which to separate the real from the dreamed, the living from the dead, the single self from the many selves it scatters into. Everything happens inside consciousness, and consciousness, followed far enough, has no fixed categories at the bottom.

The Thesis

Here is the claim the rest of this book rests on.

Young goes so far down into consciousness that the categories a reader brings to the book stop holding.

Living and dead, waking and dreaming, man and woman, one self and the crowd of selves inside it: each comes apart under the pressure of how far the novel is willing to go. The book does not argue that these distinctions are false. It dissolves them by description, by staying inside a mind until the lines a reader counted on go soft.

Women are where the book goes in. This is the part most easily misread. The claim is not that Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is, in the end, a book about women. The claim is that women are the route. The women in the novel already have their selfhood broken open by the conditions of their lives.

Catherine, sealed in her opium dreams, has already scattered into a house full of guests who are all versions of her.

Esther, pregnant forever and delivering nothing, already lives at the place where creation and loss are the same event.

Miss MacIntosh, bald under her red wig and missing a breast, already wears her identity as something removable.

These are not women whose wholeness the book must break open. They arrive already broken open, and that openness is the door. The route runs through them toward something that has no gender at the bottom.

This is where the male figures matter, and why this edition puts one of them forward early. Mr. Spitzer cannot tell which brother he is. He stands as the bride at a wedding while a woman stands as the groom. He is called dead while he goes on walking, and late in the book he thins out almost to transparency. If the dissolving of the self were only a fact about the women in the novel, Spitzer would be a problem, a run of chapters to apologize for. He is not a problem. He is the proof. What the women reach through their conditions, he reaches too.

The bottom of consciousness, in this book, has no sex.

The conversation this reading joins

This is not the first time readers have noticed that the novel dissolves things. The reading here builds on work that saw the pattern and named parts of it, and it should be taken as company rather than as a wall of defense.

Miriam Fuchs, in "Liquescence as Form," read the novel's very form as a kind of melting. In her account the book does not hold its shapes still. Character, scene, and identity run together, and that flowing is not a loss of control but the form itself, the thing the book is built to do. This edition takes that insight about form and carries it into content. What melts, finally, is not only the shape of the prose but the categories the prose is made of, gender among them.

Steven Shaviro, in "Lost Chords and Interrupted Births," fixes on the motif of the birth that never completes, the pregnancy that will not deliver, the note that will not resolve. Esther Longtree is the clearest case, carrying and carrying and bringing nothing to term. Shaviro saw in that interruption something central to how the book works. This edition reads the interrupted birth as one of the pillars named below, the state in which the unfinished is not a failure but a way of being, and it follows that state past Esther into the whole novel.

Behind both essays stands the recovery work of Breaking the Sequence, which placed Young where she belongs, among the women experimentalists whose books had been pushed to the edge of the record. That placement did the first necessary thing. It said the novel was serious, and it said whose company it kept.

What this edition adds is not a correction of that work but a longer reach through it. The essays saw the dissolution and named its parts. This book follows the dissolution all the way down and gives it one account:

Young uses women as the route into consciousness, and going deep enough dissolves the category of gender itself.

The reading is offered as an extension of a conversation already underway, not as a claim to have started it.

The eleven pillars, briefly

The chapters that follow read the novel through eleven pillars. They are reading tools, not doctrine, and they are meant to be used and set down, not believed. Each is easiest to hold if it is attached to a character:

Plural selfhood: the "I" that is many. Catherine, scattered across the guests of her dream.

The crossing point: the place where man and woman trade. The wedding where Mr. Spitzer is the bride and Miss MacIntosh the groom, and the "secret man" the book finds inside women.

Unfinished is not failed: incomplete as a way of being. Esther, pregnant and never delivering.

Permanent grief: mourning with no end. Esther again, at her stillbirths.

The dissolving boundary between life and death: Catherine, living and dead in the same breath.

The real and the dream are one country: Catherine's opium world, treated as a place and not an error.

The body as the place the self comes apart: Miss MacIntosh, her baldness and her missing breast.

The illusion of the individual: the novel's own line, "Every heart is the other heart. Every soul is the other soul. The individual is the one illusion."

The self as mask: Miss MacIntosh's wig, worn and removed; Mr. Spitzer, clothed by another hand.

Suspended time: Catherine's stopped clocks, running and frozen at once.

Invisibility and erasure: Mr. Spitzer thinned to transparency, and Martha, the last passenger pigeon, mourned by a man who cannot save her.

Spitzer stands under several of these on purpose. He is the reminder, kept in view, that the frame reaches past gender.

The Crossing Point

Speaking to Friedman and Fuchs, she said: "I never thought of myself as either a woman or a man. I thought of myself as a person who was born to be a writer, who was doomed to be a writer."

Young wrote from the crossing point. She did not stand on one side of the line between man and woman and look across it. She wrote from the seam, from the place where the categories give way, which is exactly the place her characters keep finding. The line about being neither woman nor man is not Young declining to be political. It is Young naming where she wrote from.

Young went on: "there have been men who have seemed to want to destroy me or my writing, men I don't even know. …I think there is a rage against women. I've come to see that now although at the time I did not notice it." The two statements are usually treated as if they pull against each other, the refusal of gender on one side and the naming of misogyny on the other.

They do not pull against each other.

They are cause and result.

A writer who refuses the category the culture insists on does not escape the category. She draws its rage. The woman who says she is neither woman nor man, only a writer, and who then writes eighty-two chapters from the place where that distinction dissolves, is met by exactly the fury a culture keeps for those who will not stay sorted. The refusal and the rage belong to one story.

What this book asks

The book asks the reader to entertain the idea that the dissolving of fixed categories in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is not a flaw to be excused, not a difficulty to be managed, and not a private feminine mysticism, but the deliberate deep work of the novel, reached through women and carried past them. A reader can grant that much and still argue with any single reading in the chapters that follow. The pillars are tools, and tools can be picked up or set down. But the premise, that Young went down into consciousness until the categories dissolved and wrote from that depth, is the ground the rest of this edition stands on. It is offered here in the plainest terms it can be given, so that a skeptical reader can finish this and grant the premise while keeping every right to resist the conclusions.
