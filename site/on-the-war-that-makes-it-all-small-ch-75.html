On the War That Makes It All Small

Esther’s closing reflection: that there is going to be another war, that it will kill “all the men and some of the children, and one or two old murders should not” matter so much: places her grief in the context of the novel’s historical setting. Young was writing across the period of World War II and its aftermath: a moment when mass death had been normalized to a degree that individual deaths (especially the deaths of women’s stillborn children, hidden in rivers, unrecorded by any official system) would be genuinely invisible against the scale of what war produced. The logic Esther uses is the logic the world used to ignore her all along: in the face of large catastrophe, small private suffering is not counted. Young does not endorse this logic. She gives it to Esther to show how thoroughly Esther has internalized the world’s accounting of her own grief, and how, even knowing this, she persists.
