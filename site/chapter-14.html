To All My Darlings

Chapter 14

On Chapter 14 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 30, 2026

← Chapter 13 · Home · Chapter 15 →

Summary

Vera comes to the grave of the black coachman, who drove her grandfather’s carriage and four white horses and outlived them all. Catherine, who never visited the grave, cannot stop asking Mr. Spitzer about it: whether the coachman was buried, as she ordered, with the carriage and horses so that he could go on driving her in death, and in his livery. She insists the arrangement was his idea, though he had no wages for years and she cannot remember his name. Her questions turn on Mr. Spitzer himself. His twin Peron, the quick, brilliant gambler, fell from a tall building, and Joachim lives on in his brother’s shadow, finding his cigarette still burning in a coat pocket and an ace dropping from his sleeve, and being taken for him in the street. Catherine counts four Mr. Spitzers, mocks his train journeys, and accuses him of losing the coachman. She talks on after he has gone and, at dawn, asks for the grave of “the slow one, the sick musician she had always loved,” reversing which twin she lost.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with Vera’s reflection on the grave of the black coachman, a figure who drove the family carriage with four white horses. Catherine, in her illness and hallucinations, obsessively wonders about the black coachman’s grave and whether he was buried with the carriage and horses as she had instructed, by the sea where one might still hear the sounds of hooves and carriage wheels. This passage sets the tone of uncertainty and haunting memory, questioning the final resting place and fate of the coachman and symbolizing the fragile boundary between life, death, and memory.
  • Catherine believes in “two Mr. Spitzers,” referencing a duality or doubling that permeates the narrative. Mr. Spitzer himself is portrayed as a figure caught between realities as possibly the survivor of a tragic loss, burdened by his brother’s death and his own diminished existence. His silence about the black coachman and other mysteries reflects his internal conflict and the blurring of identities, especially between himself and his deceased brother.
  • The narrative delves deeply into Mr. Spitzer’s troubled life: his failed law practice, his haunted memories, and the confusion between himself and his brother. He is pursued by people who mistake him for his dead brother, and he carries the burden of that mistaken identity. His life is marked by a sense of loss, failure, and existential ambiguity, symbolized by repeated motifs such as the playing cards (four aces), the silent music he composes, and his futile attempts to make sense of his past and present.
  • Catherine’s hallucinations and visions serve as a counterpoint to Mr. Spitzer’s silence. She imagines vast, surreal scenes (funeral gondolas, angels, floating faces, and shifting landscapes) reflecting her mental state and the dissolution of clear boundaries between reality and illusion. Her insistence on the black coachman’s significance and her questioning of Mr. Spitzer highlights the themes of memory, loss, and the search for meaning in chaos.
  • The idea of multiple Mr. Spitzers (four in total) and the tragic fate of one brother killing himself to erase the other introduces a metaphysical layer about identity, fate, and the cosmic balance between opposites (life and death, presence and absence, order and chaos). The chapter explores the instability of self and the universe, where even laws (human and divine) seem uncertain or contested.
  • Throughout the chapter, both Catherine and Mr. Spitzer grapple with what is real and what is illusion. Catherine challenges Mr. Spitzer on his stories of travel, on the whereabouts of the black coachman, and on the nature of the world around them, pointing out contradictions and impossibilities, suggesting that much of what is accepted as real is mutable and uncertain. Mr. Spitzer’s evasiveness and Catherine’s accusations of his lying deepen this uncertainty.
  • The description of the brother’s funeral, with its symbolic white roses and horse racing imagery, stands out as a poignant moment that crystallizes the theme of mourning and the loneliness of the surviving Mr. Spitzer. The image of the corpse as the only mourner is a powerful metaphor for isolation and the burden of memory.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s retreat into composing “silent music,” music not heard by living ears but meant for the dead, symbolizes his withdrawal from the world and the limits of communication between the living and the dead, between reality and dream. This motif ties into the overarching theme of failed communication and the ineffability of certain experiences.
  • “Her body was the carriage, and her soul, now it seemed, was the passenger, and her horses were her will, and the coachman was her conscience.” Catherine’s own map of herself is the ancient chariot of the soul.
  • Catherine orders her Black servant buried with her carriage to serve her in death, claims it “had been the coachman’s idea, not hers,” cannot recall his name, and admits he “had had no wages for years.” She is the mistress who will not let even death end his service.
  • At dawn she asks for “the grave… of the other Mr. Spitzer, the slow one, the sick musician she had always loved.” The living twin is now the dead one.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “If she could find him, she could perhaps find herself, the bewildered passenger.” Why does everything depend on the coachman?
  • “He could not say no unless he should say yes.” What does this say about Mr. Spitzer’s silence?
  • “The loser was the victor.” Who won the game between the twins?

Characters

  • “What had been that black coachman’s name? … but what was a name?” What does Catherine’s forgetting reveal?
  • Joachim lets strangers believe he is his brother: “Thus his brother lived.” Is this love, guilt, or something else?
  • Catherine “was very much confused by clarities.” What is she clear about?

Connections

  • Mr. Spitzer’s “dead ear” from Chapter 13 has moved from right to left. Why does Catherine keep changing which ear is dead?
  • The life-sized chessmen and invisible players of Chapter 13 become the Devil playing “with man for man’s soul.” Who is playing here?
  • Rhadamanthus judged Mr. Spitzer in Chapter 11, and now Catherine tries “Spitzer versus Spitzer.” What is the charge?

Reflection

  • Can a person go on serving someone past death? Should anyone be asked to?
  • Is it possible to live another person’s life to keep him alive?
  • “One understands the mystery only by proposing the greater mystery.” Is that true?

Research: Characters

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s Mother)

  • Ailing and often delirious, she is obsessed with locating the black coachman and understanding the fate of her family’s legacy.
  • She experiences visions and hallucinations, blurring the line between reality and fantasy.
  • She is deeply distrustful of Mr. Spitzer and concerned that her last wishes have been ignored or betrayed.
  • In “codicil after codicil” she left the coachman first one thing and then “everything,” even her old, empty gowns, though he was dead or unpaid.

Joachim Spitzer

  • Joachim Spitzer is depicted as one of two Mr. Spitzers (twins or doubles) each reflecting and haunting the other. The chapter explores the idea that there were “four Mr. Spitzers,” due to reflections and mirrored images, and that the boundaries between them blur, creating a complicated identity crisis.
  • This duality extends to the notion that one Mr. Spitzer is “vacant and slow and smiling,” while the other had died by suicide, attempting to “kill the Mr. Spitzer who was the pale reflection in his own heart.”
  • The surviving Spitzer is burdened by his brother’s shadow, the “dark counterpart running through all his nights and days,” which disrupts his peace and confounds his existence.
  • Joachim is portrayed as a man who has not achieved conventional success.
  • He is described as a “failure,” living “the most retired, the most unassuming life” in the shadow of his brother’s more colorful and gregarious personality.
  • Despite his literal-mindedness and conservative habits, he is haunted by self-doubt and confusion, often unsure of his own actions and memories.
  • Instances such as finding his brother’s belongings in his coat pockets, or the mysterious “second cup of coffee” he supposedly drank but cannot explain, illustrate his fragmented sense of self and reality.
  • Joachim’s relationship with Vera’s mother is complex. He is her lawyer, executor, and companion in her illness, yet she doubts his trustworthiness and resents his evasiveness and silence.
  • Catherine’s obsession with the black coachman (a symbolic figure representing continuity, memory, and conscience) serves as a foil to Joachim’s own failures and ambiguities.
  • She accuses him of neglecting or betraying the black coachman and the family’s legacy, suggesting that he may have “cheated the black coachman out of his employment and inheritance” and failed to honor her dying wishes.
  • Joachim’s inability or unwillingness to answer her questions about the black coachman and other matters deepens the tension and highlights his evasive nature.
  • Joachim is a man of silence and restraint, in contrast to his brother’s vibrant life.
  • He has ceased composing “oral music” and instead writes “silent music, a dirge for a dead clam,” symbolizing his withdrawal from active life and communication.
  • This self-imposed silence and his “utter self-forgetfulness and selflessness” suggest a man resigned to obscurity and loss, attempting to protect himself and others from the chaos that surrounds him.
  • His life is overshadowed by the death of his brother, an event that has left him “hopelessly benumbed” and struggling to keep up with time and reality.
  • He is pursued by people who mistake him for his deceased brother, and he attempts to shield his brother’s memory, sometimes maintaining the illusion that the brother still lives.
  • This haunting presence causes him distress but also a certain pride in having “preferred the last footstep, the last doorway,” indicating a preference for quiet endurance over dramatic exit.
  • Joachim is enveloped in mystery and ambiguity. His actions and whereabouts are uncertain; he is sometimes imagined wearing the black coachman’s livery, sometimes unable to explain trivial facts like train journeys.
  • His existence blurs the line between the living and the dead, the real and the illusory.
  • He is “a man who did not know himself” and whose life is marked by “minor but palpable discrepancies” and “things which did not add up.”
  • Joachim’s character is embedded in a cosmic framework where ordinary human concerns intersect with metaphysical questions.
  • He and Catherine speak of “the music of the spheres,” “the silent music,” and the “lawless image of the dream,” suggesting a man grappling with the ultimate nature of reality and existence.
  • His legal work, particularly the “case of Spitzer versus Spitzer,” symbolizes his internal conflict and the unresolved tensions of his identity.
  • Joachim shows a degree of faithfulness and compassion, toward Catherine.
  • He visits Catherine regularly, tries to please her, and protects her interests “to the best of his present legal ability.” However, his “slumberous inertia” and evasiveness frustrate her, revealing a gap between his intentions and her expectations.
  • Joachim Spitzer’s life is inextricably linked to the death of his brother Peron. The brother’s suicide or accidental death seems to have precipitated a crisis in Joachim’s identity and existence.
  • He is described as having taken up his brother’s mantle in some respects: wearing his brother’s cufflinks, carrying his cane, and even being mistaken for him by acquaintances. Yet he is also deeply burdened by the consequences, trying to protect his brother’s reputation while simultaneously being haunted by his brother’s unresolved, chaotic life.
  • This complicated relationship fuels Joachim’s feelings of inadequacy and deepens his tendency toward silence and withdrawal.

Peron Spitzer

  • The quick, brilliant twin, a gambler “in the horse circles,” who “had fallen from a tall building.”
  • Catherine calls him the one “loved by her, yet never loving her,” yet at dawn she mourns “the slow one, the sick musician,” confusing which twin she lost.

The Old Clam Digger

  • The clam-digger is Mr. Spitzer’s manservant.
  • He swears Mr. Spitzer drank the second cup of coffee himself.

The Black Coachman

  • A symbolic and somewhat mythical figure associated with the family’s carriage and horses.
  • Represents continuity, memory, and the link between life and death.
  • Catherine’s wish was for the black coachman to be buried with the carriage and horses so that she might continue to “go driving” even after death.
  • His actual fate and whereabouts are uncertain, and this uncertainty fuels much of Catherine’s anguish.
  • Catherine has also defined the Black Coachman as her conscience.
  • Catherine insists the burial was “the coachman’s idea, not hers,” that “money had meant nothing to him,” though he had no wages for years, the old excuses of those who kept Black servants unpaid.
  • She cannot remember his name, only his “white, rolling eyes.”

Research: Themes

Memory, Identity, and Duality

See: Memory and the Past · Identity and the Self · Duality and Doubles

  • The chapter explores fractured identities, especially through the figure of Mr. Spitzer and his deceased brother.
  • Catherine perceives multiple overlapping realities and versions of Mr. Spitzer, reflecting her confusion and the blurred boundaries of life and death.
  • The motif of “four Mr. Spitzers” suggests layered selves and mirrored images, raising questions about selfhood and existence.

Death and the Afterlife

See: Death and Mortality

  • Catherine’s visions often revolve around death, funerals, and the journey beyond life.
  • The black coachman and the family carriage symbolize passage and continuity after death, and also her conscience and her body.
  • There is a persistent questioning of what happens after death and whether true peace or harmony exists beyond.

Illusion versus Reality

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Catherine’s hallucinations and the shifting perceptions of Mr. Spitzer’s reliability highlight the tension between what is real and what is imagined.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s evasiveness and Catherine’s accusations create a dynamic where truth is elusive and layered with doubt.

Law and Order versus Chaos

See: Guilt, Innocence, and Justice · Order, Chaos, and Discipline

  • Mr. Spitzer embodies legal order but is portrayed as overwhelmed by the disorder of life and his own ghosts.
  • Catherine’s world is chaotic, filled with shifting images, ghosts, and memories that resist neat categorization.

Family Legacy and Betrayal

See: Memory and the Past

  • Catherine suspects Mr. Spitzer of betraying the black coachman and mishandling her will and the family estate.
  • There is a theme of lost inheritance, broken promises, and the failure of caretakers to uphold traditions.

Race and Servitude

See: Society, Class, and Race

  • The black coachman is unpaid, nameless, and ordered to serve in death.
  • He is described by the minstrel-show stereotype of “white, rolling eyes.”
  • Catherine’s slip, “the carriage and the white coachman and the four black horses, what had become of the color line?” turns the whole arrangement inside out.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

The Black Coachman and the Family Carriage

See: Horses and Carriages

  • Symbolizes continuity beyond death, the passage of time, and the family’s legacy, Catherine’s conscience and body.
  • The mystery of his grave and whether he was buried with his horses signifies Catherine’s fear of disruption and loss.

White Horses (King, Queen, Prince, Spade)

See: Horses and Carriages

  • These horses have personified names and represent stability and tradition.
  • Their fate is ambiguous, mirroring the uncertainty in the narrative.

Mr. Spitzer’s Hat and Garments

See: Clothing, Hats, and Skirts

  • The black cape, high silk hat, silver buttons evoke the image of the coachman, suggesting blurred identities and roles.

Playing Cards (Aces)

See: Cards, Games, and Chance

  • The four aces that drop from Mr. Spitzer’s sleeve symbolize the four brothers and the layered selves.
  • The cards also represent fate, chance, and the game of life and death.

The Funeral and the Graveyard

See: Weddings and Funerals

  • The funeral scenes, especially Mr. Spitzer’s brother’s funeral, symbolize mourning, loss, and the passage of time.
  • Catherine’s absence from funerals and the details of the mourning rituals emphasize her detachment and the fragmented nature of her existence.

Imagery of Water, Snow, and Night

See: The Sea and Water · Snow, Ice, and Winter

  • Recurring references to water, foaming seas, snowflakes, and darkness evoke a liminal, otherworldly atmosphere.
  • These natural elements symbolize change, decay, and the boundary between life and death.

Research: Resources

  • “the four white horses”: White horses in mythology are often associated with the sun chariot, warrior-heroes, fertility, and an end-of-time savior. The white horses symbolize the solemn, mystical journey of the soul from life into death and beyond, carrying Catherine through the shifting landscapes of memory, dream, and existence by her will. They are a powerful emblem of transition, purity, fate, and the ethereal nature of mortality in the novel. In Revelation, “behold a white horse: and he that sat on him had a bow; and a crown was given unto him” (Revelation 6:2). See Horses and Carriages and Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “the barkings of spotted coach dogs running beside the wheels”: Dalmatians were bred to run beside carriages, clearing the road and guarding the horses. See Dogs and Horses and Carriages.
  • “a dark porte-cochere”: A porte-cochere is a covered entrance where a carriage stops so its passengers can step down out of the weather. The porte-cochere in the novel symbolizes: a transitional threshold between realms (life/death, reality/illusion), the arrival and departure point of spectral or symbolic visitors, a gateway into the mysterious, interior world of the house and the narrator’s family, a symbol of passage, change, and the inescapable movement between states of being. This layered symbolism reflects the novel’s exploration of liminality, the elusive nature of reality, and the haunting presence of the past and the dead within the living world.
  • “Nothing should be missing from the chaos, not one leaf that ever fell”: “Are not two sparrows sold for a farthing? and one of them shall not fall on the ground without your Father” (Matthew 10:29). See The Bible.
  • “her Seeing Eye dogs”: The Seeing Eye, the first American guide dog school, was founded in 1929. See Dogs and the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “she had asked, long, long ago, that the black coachman be buried with the carriage and the white horses”: Chariot burials are tombs in which the deceased was buried together with their chariot, usually including their horses and other possessions. An instance of a person being buried with their horse (without the chariot) is called horse burial.
    • In the royal tombs of Ur, Leonard Woolley found (1920s) grooms, attendants, and oxen buried with their masters’ carts, servants sent to serve in death.
  • “himself the food of worms”: “Your worm is your only emperor for diet” (Hamlet 4.3). See Shakespeare and the Chapter 10 resources.
  • “but what was a name?”: “What’s in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet” (Romeo and Juliet 2.2). Catherine says it of a servant whose name no one kept. See Shakespeare and The Black Coachman.
  • “King and Queen, Prince and Spade”: The horses are named like playing cards, and the aces fall later in the chapter. See Horses and Carriages and Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “The black coachman had had no wages for years”: The history of domestic work in the United States is deeply intertwined with the exploitation of Black women, men, and immigrant laborers through systemic racism, limited economic opportunities, and discriminatory labor practices. Enslaved Black women performed essential, unpaid domestic labor before emancipation, with conditions often leading to sexual abuse and the work continuing in a similar vein after abolition. For both Black and immigrant workers, this path was one of few available options, resulting in low pay, long hours, and a lack of protections, while modern policies continue to perpetuate a cycle of vulnerability. See The Black Coachman and Society, Class, and Race.
  • “the black coachman with the white, rolling eyes”: The “white, rolling eyes” are the stock caricature of minstrel shows and early films, which made Black servants figures of fear and comedy. See The Black Coachman and Society, Class, and Race.
  • “instead of a winding sheet”: A winding sheet is a cloth to wrap a body with for burial. The winding sheet here serves as a powerful symbol of the inevitable mortality that envelops all, including the black coachman and the other characters intertwined in the narrative’s exploration of life, death, and memory. It is a reminder of the mortality underlying the novel’s dreamlike and sometimes surreal atmosphere. See Death and Mortality.
  • “in codicil after codicil”: A codicil is an amendment added to a will.
  • “she had not been unduly influenced or coerced, and she had been possessed of all her mental faculties”: The language of a will: “being of sound mind,” free of “undue influence.” A will can be overturned if its maker lacked capacity or was coerced.
  • “the twelve moons shining over Jupiter”: Galileo found four of Jupiter’s moons in 1610. The twelfth was found in 1951, so twelve was the count when Young wrote. See Astronomy.
  • “Did his right hand know what his left hand did”: “But when thou doest alms, let not thy left hand know what thy right hand doeth” (Matthew 6:3). See The Bible.
  • “the law of gravity defied and golden apples falling upward”: Newton’s falling apple, and the golden apples of the Hesperides. See Greek Mythology.
  • “the excellent clam-digger”: Mr. Spitzer’s manservant, who swears Mr. Spitzer drank the second cup of coffee himself. See The Old Clam Digger.
  • “the Manchurian or some other conflict”: Manchuria is a region of northeast China. The conflict is likely Japan’s seizure of it in 1931, or the Russo-Japanese War of 1904-05, fought there.
  • “suits for heart’s balm”: Breach-of-promise and alienation-of-affection suits, which many states abolished in the 1930s.
  • “their cosmic union in the womb of time”: “There are many events in the womb of time which will be delivered” (Othello 1.3). See Shakespeare, Twins and Doubles, and the Chapter 5 resources.
  • “the plural melody which was not Mr. Spitzer’s composition or choice”: His “melody moving or attended by one or more related but independent arrangements of sounds” is nearly the dictionary definition of polyphony, or counterpoint, several independent melodies sounding at once. See Music and Composers.
  • “he had spoken to her out of the whirlwind”: “Then the Lord answered Job out of the whirlwind” (Job 38:1). See The Book of Job.
  • “There should be the same divisions and multiplications”: Multiplicity is a philosophical concept that refers to the parts of a whole that can be considered separately. It can also refer to an entity that is made up of simple elements that have twisted or folded together. Philosophers who have worked with the concept of multiplicity: Edmund Husserl and Henri Bergson.
  • “the Battle of the Roses”: The Wars of the Roses (1455-1487) were a series of civil wars in England between the houses of York, with its white rose, and Lancaster, with its red. See Roses and Flowers.
  • “the funeral gondola with the corrugated angel at its helm”: Venice carries its dead by black gondola to the cemetery island of San Michele. Liszt wrote “La lugubre gondola” (1882) foreseeing Wagner’s death in Venice, and Wagner’s body left by gondola in 1883. See Weddings and Funerals and Music and Composers.
  • “the golden cock perched at the foot of her bed, that it was really Pythagoras shedding his golden feathers”: In Lucian’s dialogue “The Dream, or the Cock,” a cobbler’s rooster reveals that it is Pythagoras reborn. Pythagoras was said to have a golden thigh. Rimsky-Korsakov’s opera The Golden Cockerel (1909), from Pushkin’s tale (1834), has a golden cock that warns a tsar. See Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres, Birds, and the Chapter 11 resources.
    • The teaching most securely identified with Pythagoras is the “transmigration of souls” or metempsychosis, which holds that every soul is immortal and, upon death, enters into a new body. He may have also devised the doctrine of musica universalis, which holds that the planets move according to mathematical equations and thus resonate to produce an inaudible symphony of music.
    • Alectryomancy is a form of divination involving roosters and a method of seeking answers or predictions by observing the behavior of roosters.
    • Weissenbach saw such a bird. Young was with her “when the golden bird, which was the spirit of Heraclitus, came and perched upon the bedpost” (Marguerite Young, interview with Charles Ruas, WBAI radio, New York), and remembered “a beautiful golden cockerel perched at the foot of her bed, sometimes emitting messages in the Greek language” (Marguerite Young, interview, WNYC radio, New York).
  • “Isabella of Spain”: Isabella I of Castile backed Columbus, established the Spanish Inquisition, and expelled Spain’s Jews in 1492.
  • “imaginary street Arabs”: “Street Arab” was the Victorian name for homeless children, a slur drawn from the image of Arabs as rootless wanderers. See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “a palimpsest of many hieroglyphic writings”: A palimpsest is a parchment scraped and written over, the old text still faintly showing. Thomas De Quincey called the human brain one in “The Palimpsest of the Human Brain” (1845). See The History of Opium.
  • “the appearance beyond the appearance”: Plato’s Allegory of the Cave, in which prisoners take the shadows on a wall for reality. See Plato and Platonism and the Chapter 12 resources.
  • “knew that the dog was Socrates”: Socrates was a Greek philosopher who wrote no texts of his own but is credited as one of the founders of Western philosophy. In the work Republic, written by Plato, there is a Socratic dialogue. In it Socrates argues that the dog is very much like a philosopher because the dog distinguishes the face of a friend and of an enemy only by the criterion of knowing and not knowing. Socrates swore “By the dog, the god of the Egyptians” (Gorgias 482b). See Plato and Platonism and Dogs.
  • “she had a third eye in her forehead”: The third eye is a mystical or esoteric concept which provides perception beyond normal sight. The third eye symbolizes a mystical insight and a deeper understanding of the unseen worlds as an insight that both enlightens and alienates Catherine from ordinary human experience. Descartes placed the soul’s seat in the pineal gland, which some have called the third eye. See Descartes and Dualism, Hinduism, and the Chapter 43 resources.
  • “a skeleton with an hour-glass in his hands”: Death’s figure in the Dance of Death, carrying the hourglass of a life’s measured time. See Death and Mortality and the Chapter 6 resources.
  • “her peacocks”: Aristotle referred to the peacock’s vain reputation. The Roman poet Ennius, following the Pythagorean teaching of rebirth, dreamed that Homer’s soul had passed into a peacock and then into himself. The peacock symbolizes the dazzling but ephemeral nature of beauty and social display, the multiplicity and fragmentation of perception, and the tension between illusion and reality that the characters navigate. See Birds.
  • “Mrs. Astor could not go, and Mrs. Vanderbilt would not be seen”: Mrs. Astor is Caroline Schermerhorn Astor, a famous American socialite who led the Four Hundred, the list of New York society in the Gilded Age. Mrs. Vanderbilt is likely Grace Graham Wilson Vanderbilt, another American socialite of the Vanderbilt family. In 1883 Alva Vanderbilt’s costume ball forced Mrs. Astor to call on the Vanderbilts, a famous defeat for old society. See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “Czar Kolokol, the King of the Bells”: It is known as the Tsar Bell. It is the largest bell in the world that has never rung. It was cast in 1733-35 and cracked in the Kremlin fire of 1737 when cold water struck the hot metal.
  • “Was it before or after the Revolution”: The Russian Revolution of 1917 refers to two distinct revolutions that occurred in that year, ultimately leading to the end of the Tsarist regime and the rise of Bolshevik rule in Russia. The first, the February Revolution, overthrew the Czar Nicholas II, while the second, the October Revolution, saw the Bolsheviks seize power. February Revolution (March 1917): Widespread strikes and protests in Petrograd (St. Petersburg) against food shortages and World War I led to the Tsar’s abdication and the establishment of a provisional government. October Revolution (November 1917): The Bolsheviks, led by Vladimir Lenin, seized control of the government, ending the provisional government and paving the way for a socialist state. The Bolsheviks’ seizure of power ignited a civil war with anti-communist forces, ultimately resulting in the Bolshevik victory and the establishment of the Soviet Union. The United States did not recognize the Soviet Union until 1933, the point of Catherine never breaking “her diplomatic relations with Moscow.”
  • “the coachman was her conscience”: The Katha Upanishad: “Know the Self as the lord of the chariot, the body as the chariot, the intellect as the charioteer, the mind as the reins, and the senses as the horses.” In Plato’s Phaedrus, the soul is a charioteer driving two horses. See Hinduism, Plato and Platonism, and Horses and Carriages.
    • G. I. Gurdjieff likened a person to a horse and carriage: the horse is the emotional body, the carriage the physical body, and the driver the mind. (“Horse-and-buggy,” not “horse and carriage,” is the idiom for something outdated.) Funerals were traditionally drawn by horse and carriage before being replaced by automobiles. Even in the present day they are still used by the wealthy, for a famous person or significant death, or by someone wanting a special funeral procession.
    • A conscience is a cognitive process that elicits emotion and rational associations based on an individual’s moral philosophy or value system. In common terms, conscience is often described as leading to feelings of remorse when a person commits an act that conflicts with their moral values.
  • “the case of Spitzer versus Spitzer”: A case in which he “must appear as both the defender and the accuser.” See Joachim Spitzer, Peron Spitzer, and Twins and Doubles.
  • “Which was alpha, and which was omega?”: “I am Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the ending, saith the Lord” (Revelation 1:8). See Revelation and the Apocalypse and the Chapter 8 resources.
  • “a papal divorce from her dead husband”: The Church grants annulments, not divorces, and a husband’s death already ends a marriage.
  • “a fundamental human weakness which was the desire to be deceived”: “The world wants to be deceived, so let it be deceived.” Mundus vult decipi, ergo decipiatur. The saying is first found in Sebastian Franck (1534).
  • “the financial bubbles”: The South Sea Bubble of 1720, when shares in the South Sea Company soared and collapsed and ruined thousands.
  • “the Admiralty Law, the Lords Justices, the Vice-Chancellors, the Masters of the Rolls”: These are offices of the English courts: Admiralty law governs ships and the sea, the Vice-Chancellors sat in the Court of Chancery, and the Lords Justices and the Master of the Rolls sit in the Court of Appeal. Gilbert and Sullivan satirized this world of wigs and titles in Iolanthe (1882), which features the Lord Chancellor as a leading comic character.
  • “the pettifoggers”: A pettifogger is a lawyer whose methods are petty, underhanded, or disreputable.
  • “what had become of the color line?”: W. E. B. Du Bois: “The problem of the twentieth century is the problem of the color-line” (The Souls of Black Folk, 1903). Catherine has just turned the coachman white and the horses black. See Society, Class, and Race and The Black Coachman.
  • “the stone fleet manned by stone figures”: In 1861 the Union sank old New England whaling ships, loaded with stone, to block Charleston harbor. They were known as the Great Stone Fleet. See The Civil War.
  • “the antinomies, both true, each contradicting the other”: Kant set out four antinomies in the Critique of Pure Reason (1781). An antinomy is a real or apparent contradiction between two conclusions, both of which seem justified. It is a term used in logic and epistemology, particularly in the philosophy of Immanuel Kant. Kant’s antinomies are four: two “mathematical” and two “dynamical.” They are connected with (1) the limitation of the universe in respect of space and time (whether the universe has a beginning or is infinite), (2) the theory that the whole consists of indivisible atoms (whereas, in fact, none such exist), (3) the problem of free will in relation to universal causality (free will vs determinism), and (4) the existence of a necessary being. The first two antinomies are dubbed “mathematical” antinomies, presumably because in each case the concern is the relation between what are alleged to be sensible objects (either the world itself, or objects in it) and space and time. The second two are dubbed “dynamical” antinomies, presumably because the proponents of the thesis are not committing themselves solely to claims about spatio-temporal objects. See Kant and Hegel.
  • “the octave, the eighth day of the week”: Early Christians called Sunday “the eighth day,” the day of the resurrection. A liturgical octave is a feast kept for eight days.
  • “a delusion and a snare”: From Lord Denman’s judgment in O’Connell v. The Queen (1844): “a mockery, a delusion, and a snare.”
  • “driving a coach and six through a legal statute”: Attributed to the Irish judge Stephen Rice in the 1680s: “I will drive a coach and six through the Act of Settlement.” See Horses and Carriages.
  • “a short life but a merry one”: “A merry life and a short one,” the motto of the pirate Bartholomew Roberts (1720s).
  • “a long weekend in Newport”: Newport, Rhode Island, where Gilded Age families summered in marble “cottages.” See Society, Class, and Race and the Chapter 10 resources.
  • “Mr. Spitzer had attended Mr. Spitzer’s funeral”: Tom Sawyer and Huck Finn watch their own funeral from the church gallery (The Adventures of Tom Sawyer, 1876). See American Writers.
  • “a horseshoe of white roses”: Floral horseshoes and saddles of roses honored racing men. Since 1932 the Kentucky Derby winner has worn a garland of roses. See Roses and Flowers, Horses and Carriages, and the Chapter 43 resources.
  • “the Phoenician fleet”: The Phoenicians, a seafaring people of the eastern Mediterranean, were the great sailors of the ancient world. Herodotus reports that Phoenician sailors went around Africa for Pharaoh Necho (c. 600 BC).
  • “the Thirty Years’ War”: The Thirty Years’ War (1618-1648) was fought in Central Europe by different countries and included fighting between Catholics and Protestants.
  • “Dr. Galen”: Galen was a Greek doctor in the Roman Empire. See the Chapter 13 resources.
  • “Copernicus, the author of that great celestial revolution”: Nicolaus Copernicus proposed the sun was stationary and the center of the universe, not the earth. His findings triggered the Copernican Revolution. His book was De revolutionibus (1543). Freud named Copernicus, with Darwin and psychoanalysis, as one of three blows to human pride (1917). See Astronomy.
  • “He had dreamed that he had climbed a tensile rope to heaven”: The Indian rope trick, in which a rope rises into the air and a boy climbs it out of sight, and Jacob’s dream of a ladder “set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven” (Genesis 28:12). See The Bible and the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “the music of the spheres, the silent music”: Boethius was a Roman senator, consul, magister officiorum, polymath, historian, and philosopher of the Early Middle Ages. He sought to reconcile the teachings of Plato and Aristotle with Christian theology. He broke logic into three parts: that which defines, that which divides, and that which deduces. There are three types of arguments: those of necessity, of ready believability, and sophistry. In De Musica there are three classifications: Musica mundana, Musica humana, Musica instrumentalis. In Dante’s Divine Comedy, the spirit of Boethius is pointed out by Saint Thomas Aquinas and is mentioned further in the poem. In his book De Institutione Musica (Fundamentals of Music) his drawings appear striking and may be an inspiration for the organization of Young’s novel. Musica universalis or music of the spheres or harmony of the spheres is a Greek philosophical concept founded by Pythagoras. This metaphysical principle stated that mathematical relationships could produce ‘tones’ of energy that could produce, among other things, sounds. Pythagoras proposed that the celestial bodies all have their own unique hum based on their orbital revolution. This idea influenced many scholars until the end of the Renaissance. Johannes Kepler, a German astronomer from the 1600s wrote extensively on the subject. Later astronomy set it aside. Boethius wrote The Consolation of Philosophy in prison awaiting execution, with its Wheel of Fortune. See Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres and Dante.
  • “one understands the mystery only by proposing the greater mystery”: The Latin obscurum per obscurius, explaining the obscure by the more obscure.
  • “paper-backed dream books sold by fortunetellers”: Dream books matched dreams to numbers for the policy, or numbers, game played in poor neighborhoods, as in Aunt Sally’s Policy Players’ Dream Book (1889). See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “They placed the planets forty degrees off their course”: Because of the precession of the equinoxes, the signs of Western astrology have drifted from their constellations by nearly a whole sign since antiquity. See Astrology, Alchemy, and Esoterica.
  • “The playing card dropping from his sleeve each morning had nearly always been an ace”: The “ace up the sleeve” is the cardsharp’s hidden card. In most fortune-telling, the ace of spades is the death card. See Gambling and Playing Cards and Cards, Games, and Chance.
    • In fortune telling the Ace of Hearts when among hearts implies love, friendship, and affection; with diamonds, money and news of distant friends; with clubs, festivities, and social or domestic rejoicing; with spades, disagreements, misunderstandings, contention, or misfortune; individually, it stands for the house; Ace of Diamonds means a ring or paper money; Ace of Clubs means wealth, a peaceful home, industry, and general prosperity; Ace of Spades means love affairs, or conveys a warning that troubles await the inquirer through bad speculations or ill-chosen friends.
    • The mystical meaning of cards for fortune telling are the fifty-two cards in the pack correspond with the fifty-two weeks in the year. The thirteen cards in each suit symbolize the thirteen lunar months, and the thirteen weeks in each quarter. There are four suits, as there are four seasons in the year. There are twelve court cards in the pack, just as there are twelve calendar months and twelve signs of the Zodiac. Counting ace as one and king as thirteen, the four suits add up to 364, and with the joker, 365, the days of the year.
  • “the Devil plays with man for man’s soul”: Moritz Retzsch’s painting The Chess Players (1831) shows Satan playing chess with a young man for his soul while an angel watches. See Fate and Chance.
  • “The club, which was trifoliate, might be transformed into the sign of the cross”: The club suit is a trefoil, and the shamrock was said to teach the Trinity. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “There were many transformations, many butterflies”: Steven Shaviro writes of the novel, “The difficulty of interpretation in this novel is that in a certain sense, there is nothing to interpret, since everything is right there; but the difficulty comes in grasping and holding onto just how these entirely clear phrases metamorphose into one another, intensify or double one another, contradict one another, derail one another, and so on.”
  • “a place set for the dead”: The “dumb supper,” a folk custom of setting a place at table for the dead. See Death and Mortality.
  • “a horse doctor talking as if he were an argumentative centaur”: Chiron, the wise centaur, was a healer and teacher of heroes. See Greek Mythology and the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “at shrouded parimutuel windows”: The parimutuel, a betting pool shared among the winners, was invented by Joseph Oller in Paris in 1867. See Gambling and Playing Cards and Peron Spitzer.
  • “incongruent harpers harping congruously”: “I heard the voice of harpers harping with their harps” (Revelation 14:2). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “changed trains at Hartford”: The New York, New Haven and Hartford Railroad electrified its lines from 1907. Hartford was “the insurance capital,” home of Aetna and Travelers, and Wallace Stevens worked there as an insurance executive. See the Chapter 13 resources.
  • “Queen Victoria was dead”: She died January 22, 1901.
