Chapter 4

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Pillars: The Sacred Is Here

The Questions

When Catherine says it is “better to think of walking than to walk,” is she offering wisdom, or the sealed invention taken to its end?

Vera longs for “normal life.” Is the ordinary she pictures The Sacred Is Here, or another dream of a better place?

What does the fat lady’s warning do to the small town where “Monday follows Sunday”?

Can loss be recovered from, or only carried?

Catherine’s sealed dream has become a doctrine she presses on Vera. “Better to think of walking than to walk, better to think of dancing than to dance.” The invention has hardened into a preference for the idea over the act, and she offers it as a gift: “oblivion as if I were born into death,” an immortal beauty bought by a “seeming death in the midst of my life.” This is the finished blueprint dressed as salvation, the sealed room sold as freedom from time and loss.

Against it Vera reaches for the ordinary, “the other America, the interior, the small town, the routine of Monday following Sunday,” the washings on the line, baking day. It looks like The Sacred Is Here, and it is the right direction. But the chapter is careful. Vera does not yet possess the ordinary, she pictures it, and a pictured ordinary can be its own promised beyond, a small town glowing at the far end of the search. The Sacred Is Here is the world underfoot, not a better place imagined elsewhere.

The fat lady on the train punctures the picture. Where Vera is going there is “no nature at all,” only “weeds and the glowing ashes,” old people “deaf as door knobs,” “desolation and weeds.” The warning is not the book despairing. It is the book refusing to let the ordinary be idealized. The real ordinary has grief in it, and desolation, and is still where meaning lives; an ordinary scrubbed clean of hardship would be one more sealed dream like her mother's.

Grief runs under all of it. “Time died when my love died” is Catherine’s grief stopping the clocks inside her sealed house, the walled grief stated outright. Vera’s grief at the “great, staggering blow” of Miss MacIntosh’s disappearance is the other kind, grief that sends Vera out in search of her nursemaid. The chapter sets the two griefs side by side and does not resolve them.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Catherine’s retreat for wisdom, and equally the reading that takes Vera’s dream of the small town for the answer. Both are sealed places, one dark and one bright. It refuses the idea that the ordinary is a clean refuge waiting at the end of the road.

The footing is that The Sacred Is Here is the world underfoot, grief and desolation included, not an idealized elsewhere. A meaningful life is neither the sealed perfection Catherine offers nor the glowing town Vera pictures; it is the actual ground, held open, weeds in plain sight. Loss is not recovered from but carried, and carrying it in the open is the difference between Vera’s grief and Catherine’s. The ordinary is where meaning is, weeds and all, and Vera has not arrived; she is still on the bus, still warned, still going.

For Reflection

What ordinary place has one idealized into a refuge it could never be?

Is there a difference between grief that sends one out into the weather and grief that shuts the weather out?

What in a plain life is easiest to picture and hardest to actually stand in?
