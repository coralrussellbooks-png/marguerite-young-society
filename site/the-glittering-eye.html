The Glittering Eye: The Ancient Mariner as Structural Model in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling

Movement Five: The Traditions the Novel Absorbs

It is an ancient Mariner, / And he stoppeth one of three. / 'By thy long grey beard and glittering eye, / Now wherefore stopp'st thou me?'

Samuel Taylor Coleridge, The Rime of the Ancient Mariner

The Compulsion to Tell

Coleridge's Ancient Mariner killed an albatross and brought disaster on his ship. All his crewmates died. He survived, cursed to wander and to retell the story of what he did to anyone he encounters, whether they want to hear it or not. The Mariner stops a wedding guest on his way into a wedding and holds him with his glittering eye and tells him everything. The wedding guest cannot move until the telling is complete. He misses the wedding. He rises the next day a sadder and a wiser man. This is the poem's formal structure: one who cannot stop telling, one who cannot stop listening, the wedding interrupted, the wisdom delivered at a cost.

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is 1,198 pages long and cannot stop telling. This is not a formal failure. It is the poem's structural logic extended to novel length: the compulsion to retell the experience of catastrophic loss in full, without abbreviation, because abbreviation would falsify it. The Ancient Mariner tells the whole voyage. He cannot summarize. He cannot say: I killed an albatross and everyone died and I learned a lesson. He must tell every detail, every dead crewmate's eyes, every degree of the becalmed ocean, every water-snake he blessed. The novel does the same. Young cannot summarize Miss MacIntosh or Catherine or Mr. Spitzer or the bus journey. The 1,198 pages are not excess. They are the formal requirement of the compulsion.

The Glittering Eye

Coleridge's Mariner stops people with his glittering eye. This is not a metaphor for compelling storytelling. In the poem it is literal and possibly supernatural: the eye holds the wedding guest against his will. He cannot choose to walk past. The eye fixes him.

The novel gives the glittering eye to Cousin Hannah twice in Chapter 35: “this old hunter with the glittering eye, the shining spear, the sword of ice,” and “this old New England suffragette with the glittering eye, the pointed silver beard, the lifted sword.” Cousin Hannah is the novel's most compulsive activist, the suffrage captain who cannot stop campaigning, who had “ridden against great turbaned Turks” and “negotiated great torrents of ice and snow and deserts of stone,” and whose rage against man left “loud detonations” that Catherine and Mr. Spitzer would seem to hear even after so many years (Chapter 31). She holds people with her argument the way the Mariner holds the wedding guest with his eye: not by persuasion but by some quality of the attention itself, a fixing that cannot be escaped.

The glittering eye returns elsewhere. Madge, in Chapter 7, says “I just face the truth when I see it coming,” with “her eyes glittering and hard.” The Gospel salesman in Chapter 65 is “he of the glittering eyeballs and lifted, expectant face,” poking with his black umbrella in dark doorways after souls to redeem. The hangman in Chapter 68 has eyes “especially bright and glittering, as if they were filled with a great distance.” The glittering eye in the novel is always the eye of compulsion: the person who has something to tell and will not let you go until they have told it. The Ancient Mariner, Cousin Hannah, Madge, the Gospel salesman, the hangman: all of them stopping people who are trying to get somewhere else and holding them with an eye that will not release.

Water Water Everywhere

The direct quotation appears in Chapter 68. The hangman has been telling of his first wife, and the landlord remembers piloting a yacht around the world “for a mad rich man who had killed his sweetheart.” The jury excused the rich man, but his conscience did not. He made the yacht into a prison. He kept an extra plate laid at the dinner table for his sweetheart and fed crumbs to invisible birds. “Water, water everywhere, and not a drop to drink,” he would say. He is a man who has done a terrible thing and cannot escape the knowledge of it. He has tried to escape by constant motion: “He travelled around the world, but he never saw it.” The ocean surrounds him everywhere and he cannot reach it. Right after, the hangman asks, “How could my conscience hurt me?” The Mariner's becalmed curse is the rich man's condition: to move through water without being able to use it, surrounded by a world he has cut himself off from by what he has done.

The Mariner's curse is the novel's working description of guilt that has become a permanent atmospheric condition. Water, water everywhere: not a shortage but a surfeit, not deprivation but saturation. Too much of what should sustain you. The Mariner's ocean is not empty; it is full of rotting things, of dead crewmates, of the albatross hung around his neck. The rich man's ocean is the same: full of the weight of what he carries, of the extra plate, of the invisible birds, of the sweetheart he killed and still sets a place for.

In the same interview Young spoke of the hangman, Mr. Weed, who on the night his wife hanged herself “had stopped to put on his galoshes” (Chapter 68), and she left the question open: “was he a murderer because he put on his galoshes.” Guilt in this novel does not need a crime to be complete.

The Albatross

Young named the albatross herself. In her 1965 radio interview with Studs Terkel, when Terkel guessed that Catherine's hidden birthmark is “the albatross on the neck of the mariner,” Young answered, “you frighten me by being so absolutely right,” and went on: “the secret heart of my novel is the albatross and guilt.” Opium, through Coleridge, meant “guilt and turning away.” Catherine faces Coleridge's own final choice, to be “cured of opium and die or continue with opium and die,” and “like Coleridge, she comes back to reality as she dies” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT, Chicago, October 27, 1965; see The Studs Terkel Interview, 1965).

Near the end of the last chapter, the word appears in a blues-like lament to Lucie Bell, who is leaving with another man: “Oh, you'll be going with him at dawn, little doubts have I. You'll hang around his neck like an albatross when you'll be gone.” The albatross here is love turned to burden: the beloved herself becomes the weight that will hang around the next man's neck, the dead bird the Mariner cannot take off.

This is the novel's deepest use of the Mariner structure. The Mariner kills the albatross out of impulse, without clear motive, and spends the rest of his existence carrying the consequence. He did not mean to destroy what he destroyed. Neither does anyone in the novel mean to destroy what they destroy. Catherine did not mean to disappear into dreams and leave Vera without a mother who was present. Mr. Spitzer did not mean to survive Peron. Cousin Hannah did not mean to lose her one battle, the one against Catherine's withdrawal. The Cartwheel house is full of people carrying albatrosses they did not mean to put on, hanging around necks that did not ask for the weight, marking them as the ones who were present when something beautiful died.

The Wedding Guest Who Misses the Wedding

The Ancient Mariner stops a wedding guest. The guest misses the wedding. He rises the next morning sadder and wiser. The wedding is not recoverable: while he was held by the eye and the story, the feast happened without him, the vows were said without him, the celebration that organized the day went forward without the one who was stopped on the threshold.

The novel's wedding passages enact this repeatedly. In Chapter 32, Catherine remembers her own wedding, when “She had been married to a phantom,” a bridegroom whose face she had never seen. Cousin Hannah is “this uninvited wedding guest,” whom Catherine sometimes thought she had ignored, sometimes “frozen her with her remoteness, her icy chill,” or given “a sidelong glance which might signify much or nothing.” Cousin Hannah had said that Catherine “was in love with no man on earth but with a fleeting shadow, the mere shadow of a man” (Chapter 31). She is the one stopped at the threshold of a wedding that never quite happens. She beat upon the bridegroom with her white umbrella and tried to carry her cousin away. She could not pull Catherine out. She was stopped at the door of a wedding to a ghost, and she died without bringing her cousin back to the ordinary life she wanted for her.

The novel itself is the wedding guest's experience formalized. The reader is stopped at the threshold of ordinary time, held by the glittering eye of the narrative, told the whole voyage before being released. You rise from it sadder and wiser. You have missed, while reading, whatever wedding you were on your way to. The Ancient Mariner does not apologize for stopping you. The novel does not apologize for its length. The wisdom is delivered at the cost of the wedding, and the wedding, as the novel says, was always also a funeral: “For weddings are funerals, and funerals are weddings” (Chapter 32). Mr. Spitzer says it of himself in Chapter 49: “I am that man who was always on his way to a wedding and came to a funeral, the funeral of himself and all dead days.”
