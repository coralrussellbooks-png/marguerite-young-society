Research notes: Chapter 79 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 79

Characters: Esther Longtree · Rosemary

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

Esther is constitutively multiple from the very first paragraph. She is described as simultaneously "weak and gentle" and "firm, muscular, wind-burned", a contradiction the narrative does not attempt to resolve. She is cloud-like and mulish. She is the child of her father's immensity and her mother's smallness, inheriting both and fitting neither.

More radically, the chapter establishes that Esther is not one person at all; she is at minimum three:

  • Herself, the living, kicking, cross-eyed woman
  • Rosemary, the stillborn shadow twin she may have killed in the womb
  • The go-between, the dark woman whose features she has inherited and whose identity bleeds into hers

"If the other was a shadow, it must have been the shadow of a little jackass, too, she had thought, finally, trying to make things clear."

The self here is not a unified subject who has a history; it is the intersection of multiple incomplete persons. Rosemary is not separate from Esther; she is the other face of the same impossible birth. The go-between is not merely Esther's biological father-figure; she is Esther's physical template, her inherited chin, her future face in death.

The prose enacts this plurality formally: the narrative voice shifts constantly between reported interiority, remembered speech, imagined reconstruction, and something closer to myth. The sentence never fully commits to a single perspective because Esther herself never fully coheres into one. This is not a problem the text is trying to solve. It is what the text is.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter is saturated with the impossibility of distinguishing dream from fact, memory from construction. Esther's entire origin story is received knowledge: told to her by her uncle laughing until his face turned purple, by her mother in moments of rage or grief, glimpsed at age seven in a dying woman's room. She was not present at her own conception in any ordinary sense, yet:

"she had been present, the moth flying from the spoon as it passed between her parents, and she had seen the whole transaction with her little eyes before her eyes were blinded by the light, the unholy light."

This is not memory. It is mythological self-construction: the self retroactively installing itself into its own origin. The cave is not something Esther can exit. Her entire sense of who she is has been built from other people's accounts, other people's shame, other people's need.

The silver spoon itself operates as a kind of primary illusion-made-real: an instrument that should not be capable of transmitting life, yet does. The chapter does not treat this as metaphor for something more real underneath. The spoon is the reality. There is no deeper, truer account available. The construction is the thing.

"Not that she was plucked out of heavens, out of the empty air, out of the earth—but the conception was painful and different from that of others."

Reality here is specifically what has been made through effort, shame, and the intervention of a stranger, not what was found naturally. Illusion and reality do not oppose each other; they are the same substance worked differently.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The chapter does not present grief as an event Esther moves through. It presents grief as the climate of her existence, the atmospheric condition in which all other experience occurs.

Her birthday, which should be a celebration, is also the anniversary of Rosemary's death, the day her mother bakes two cakes or none. There is no day that is not also a mourning. The April blizzard that killed all the flowers on the day of her birth is not a symbol pointing toward resolution; it is the permanent weather of her world, the condition under which everything grows or fails to grow.

"Esther's death had been gradual and was not yet achieved, though she was trying, through birth, to die."

Grief here is not even separable from existence. Esther's life is described as a gradual dying. Her many stillbirths, the striated body "because of the many births which were the stillbirths", are not tragedies that interrupt her life. They are her life, the recurring form her experience takes.

The mother's grief for Rosemary is equally unresolved and unresolvable; it does not diminish over years but instead becomes the medium through which she relates to Esther, constantly reminding her of the missing twin. Grief accumulates in this text with geological patience. It does not conclude. It deposits.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrative stance of this chapter is one of sustained, unflinching witness. The prose does not rush toward judgment or resolution. It stays in the room (the cold kitchen, the old mule farm, the dying woman's bed of chicken feathers) and it looks.

The seven-year-old Esther at the deathbed of the go-between is the chapter's central image of witnessing:

"But Esther had seen, staring through shadows, the woman's features, so much like her own, the great, crooked nose, the dimpled chin, and had heard the woman's rasping, final voice."

The child does not understand what she sees. The narrative does not explain it tidily. What is witnessed is held in suspension: the dying woman's face as mirror, as father, as stranger, as self. The text refuses to collapse this into a single meaning.

The ethical weight of the chapter falls on this act of seeing without flinching. Esther's crossed eyes, mentioned repeatedly, are not simply a physical trait. They are the figure of a consciousness that cannot look at anything straight on because what is there is too multiple, too painful, too contradictory to be taken in directly,. The witness here is impaired, partial, necessarily oblique, and the text honors that obliqueness rather than correcting it.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The chapter is itself a recursive structure. Esther circles her origin story repeatedly, each time approaching it from a slightly different angle, adding detail, revising emphasis, returning to the kitchen, the spoon, the go-between, the alarm clock. The narrative does not progress linearly toward revelation. It spirals.

"Why worry now as to how she had been conceived, as if someone had only thought of her, for the work was done?"

And yet she does worry. She returns again and again. This is not neurosis; it is the recursive construction of a self that was never given stable ground to stand on. Each return adds a layer. The conception takes seven years in the story, and the narrative's own reconstruction of it takes the entire chapter, never quite arriving at a finished account.

The building is never finished. The chapter ends not with resolution but with Esther as a child in a meadow, chewing clover blossoms, hearing voices that are "her other selves and all of different ages": a return to plurality, to the ongoing, to the unfinished sentence of a life.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter is formally incomplete in ways that feel intentional rather than accidental. Sentences trail into qualification. The go-between's story is never fully told. Rosemary's death is never confirmed with certainty; she is "stillborn" in some passages and in others seems to persist as a presence Esther searches for.

"there was one who still might testify, but one who was none of these and whose death was unknown by anyone, stranger, friend, cloud, angel."

The demand for a complete, coherent account of Esther's origins (who her real father was, whether Rosemary existed, what the agreement between the women really was) is never satisfied. The text treats this refusal to conclude as honesty rather than failure.

Esther's mother's body is the image of this ethics: too small to be completed, too rigid to open, requiring seven years of effort and a stranger's intervention to produce even the partial result that is Esther. The child born of incompletion is herself incomplete, and the text presents this not as tragedy requiring remedy but as the condition of all genuine existence.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Esther Longtree is marginal in every possible dimension simultaneously. She is:

  • Too large for her world (her parents' house, her mother's womb, the small men who surround her)
  • Born of an unconsummated marriage, conceived through a third party with a spoon
  • The survivor of a twin whose death she is blamed for
  • The inheritor of features from a woman who may have been her biological father
  • A woman of enormous reproductive life, she "who had spilled so many, as many as the autumn leaves", whose children are stillborn

"She, so natural, the child of the unnatural mating."

This paradox, the most natural person produced by the most unnatural means, is the chapter's central ontological claim. Esther does not exist at the margins despite being natural. She exists at the margins because she is natural, because she is too fully embodied, too present, too large for the categories available to her.

The go-between is similarly marginal: wearing men's shoes, a man's hat, a man's coat, smoking a black cigar, a foreigner who speaks with a strange accent, her gender unstable, her role between midwife and father and lover. She is not the exception to the social order. She is the evidence of what the social order requires and cannot acknowledge.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This pillar may be the most completely realized of all eleven in this chapter. The past does not recede for Esther; it is the ground she stands on, pressing up through everything.

The seven years of the conception are not behind her. They are in her body, in her dimpled chin inherited from the go-between, in her crossed eyes, in her crippled foot that reminds her mother of the sorrow of the conception. The April blizzard of her birth year is not a historical fact. It is the permanent weather described under Pillar 3; it has deposited itself into the present.

"The past does not recede. It accumulates beneath us, layer on layer."

The chapter enacts this structurally. The narrative does not move forward in time. It moves downward, drilling through layers of accumulated history: Esther's adult consciousness, her childhood memory of the seventh birthday, her mother's account of the conception, the seven years of nightly visits, the wedding night that never happened, the parents who were never truly mated. Each layer reveals an older layer beneath it. There is no bottom.

The grandfather clock ticking, the alarm clock set to ring: time in this chapter is not linear progression but rhythmic accumulation, the same hour returning, the same effort repeated, the same grief re-deposited.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The prose of this chapter is itself a demonstration of language's constructive power. It does not describe Esther from a neutral vantage point. It builds her out of contradictions, metaphors that undermine themselves, qualifications that proliferate until the sentence nearly collapses under its own weight.

"Esther Longtree was illusive—yes, she was—cloud-like, drifting, herself the weak and gentle even though, from the outward view, she might seem otherwise."

The narrative voice is never stable. It slides between Esther's interiority, reported speech, mythological register, and something like legal testimony. The phrase "as a matter of fact" appears multiple times, each time introducing something that is anything but a straightforward fact,.

The language of the chapter consistently names its own location; it knows it is constructing, not reporting. When the narrative says Esther "had been present" at her own conception, it does not pretend this is literally true. It acknowledges the construction while making it anyway. The language here is doing what Pillar 9 demands: it is honest about its own situatedness, its own partiality, its own role in making rather than finding.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

There is no heaven in this chapter, or rather, heaven appears only as something "far away" that Esther's parents "dreamed of" rather than found. The sacred in this text is entirely immanent, located in the grey kitchen, the alarm clock, the pots of geraniums at the window, the smell of clover in Esther's hair.

Her father's tenderness (calling her Little Jackass, rubbing his cheek against hers, saying she smells like "larkspur and ragged robin and buttercups and shepherd's purse, like sunburned oats") is the closest thing to grace in the chapter. It is entirely ordinary, entirely bodily, entirely immanent. It does not point beyond itself.

The go-between, for all her ugliness and cruelty, carries something the narrative treats as sacred:

"yet could she not have been beautiful, beautiful inside with something never to be visible, never to be seen, never to be expressed?"

This is immanent transcendence precisely: the sacred as something inside the ordinary, pointing deeper into the immanent rather than upward and out. The go-between's beauty is not supernatural. It is buried in the material, in the dark hands, the barnyard smell, the patient nightly return across seven years of winter and summer.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This final pillar may be the chapter's deepest philosophical claim. Esther is not a self moving through time. She is where time crosses itself.

"You are constituted by what was deposited before you arrived, and you are constituting the ground the not-yet-born will stand on."

Esther did not choose her conception. She did not choose her parents, their incompatibility, the go-between, the seven years, the silver spoon, the April blizzard, the dead twin. All of this was deposited before she arrived. And yet she carries it, in her body, her chin, her crossed eyes, her crippled foot, her enormous reproductive life that keeps producing stillbirths, as if she is trying to pass the accumulated grief forward and cannot.

"She stood in eternity and leaned her head toward life that was on the other side of the fence."

This image is the crossing point made visible. Esther is not on one side or the other. She is the fence. She is constituted by the past (the policeman, the tiny mother, the dark woman, the dead Rosemary) and she is constituting the future through her own endless, failing, necessary attempts at birth.

The chapter ends with Esther as a child hearing "little voices speaking in the wind as if they were her other selves and all of different ages". This is the crossing point as lived experience: the self as the place where multiple times intersect, where the not-yet-born and the already-dead speak simultaneously through the same body.

The obligation is not chosen. It is structural. Esther carries what she was given. She cannot put it down. The carrying continues.
