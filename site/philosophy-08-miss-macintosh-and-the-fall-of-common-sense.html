Chapter 8. Miss MacIntosh and the Fall of Common Sense

The Promise

Every trial so far has tested a system that a reader might already suspect of illusion: an opium dream, a man who cannot tell himself from his brother, a pregnancy that never ends, a crusade built over a secret grief. The last trial is different. Miss MacIntosh, Vera’s nursemaid, is the character the novel builds to refute illusion. She is the realist, and her fall is the decisive case. If realism survives, then Young has only shown that some lives are deluded. If realism falls, then no position is left standing outside the test.

Her doctrine is stated in the first chapter. She taught Vera “that no one must dream of what was not, of what would never be,” that Vera must “lead a useful life,” that “reality was very good and could be found by those who lived, could be seen even with the naked eye.” She had always said that common sense was the finest sense, and that the way of life “was very plain, quite direct. It was a granite road and not the sea road taken by the ships falling beyond a far horizon.” In the third chapter, “she did not believe in things invisible.” She is the voice of plain fact against the opium house.

Her doctrine has philosophical ancestors. A tradition of common sense philosophy, most closely tied to Scotland and to Thomas Reid, held that ordinary perception gives direct access to the world and that philosophical doubt is a kind of madness. Miss MacIntosh claims descent from Scotland, as the novel shows later. Whether Young had Reid specifically in mind is a question for further research, but the fit is striking.

The Loading

Young loads the realist with small details that do not quite fit. In the very passage that states her creed, her “knitting needles of ivory bone” are “clicking like her false teeth” (ch. 1). The voice of natural fact speaks through artificial teeth. In the storm lessons of the sixty-sixth chapter, she dresses for a sea voyage in the nursery, insisting “that common sense demanded we should be dressed for the gale,” although “We were not going anywhere.” Her common sense requires costume.

Her theology, too, has a gap in it. She holds that God “had God’s limitations” and “had not created everything. He had left many things uncreated” (ch. 3). On the beach she teaches that God made two mistakes: “The first was to have begun what He could not finish. The second was not to have finished what He had begun” (ch. 22). The realist’s world is not complete. Her creed of plain fact already contains the idea of the unfinished.

Even her ancestry is a romance. She tells Vera she is “descended from a Gaelic prince who was lost at sea and who was bald as she was” (ch. 64), a prince whose golden crown floated away while he washed ashore in Scotland and founded a family of sheep herders and coffin makers. The woman who forbade dreaming carries a legend at the root of her identity.

The Break

The break comes when Vera, as a child, enters Miss MacIntosh’s bedroom and sees her without her wig. “She was only this old man who had only one breast and no hair and no future prospect. Her baldness gave her away, coldly and utterly” (ch. 17). Vera writes, “She was other than herself, and she had deceived me.” The realist was wearing a disguise the whole time.

Then comes the most important detail in the scene. In the next chapter, Vera sees “one breast of milky whiteness lighted even as she tried to explain that what I saw, I did not see, that it would be banished with the honest dawn which should dispell these vagrant, foolish dreams” (ch. 18). This is where realism falls. Miss MacIntosh’s whole creed rested on the reliability of the naked eye. Now, to preserve herself, she must tell Vera that what Vera sees is not there. The doctrine of common sense can survive only by denying perception. The realist has become the dreamer, and the child who was taught to trust her eyes is told to disbelieve them.

In the sixty-third chapter, Miss MacIntosh speaks for herself, and her words complete the collapse from the inside. “Perhaps I have seemed deceiving, but you should have recognized me long ago, just by all that I was not and could not be, just by what was missing from these features!” She reflects on love: “what is the love of one person for another? It is a system of mutual deception.” She regrets: “I should have been an honest woman or an honest man.” And she clarifies her lifelong warnings: “If I warned you against those lies and illusions surrounding us, I meant only those which we ourselves have made. Every man has a porcelain crown like mine.”

Then she says the sentence that may be the novel’s deepest statement of its own philosophy, spoken by the one character who spent her life denying it: “There is one dream we do not awaken from. That, my dear, is life.”

In time she disappears into the mist by the sea and is believed drowned.

What Is Left

What is left is the most surprising thing in the novel. The realist, once exposed, becomes the object of a lifelong dream. Vera spends years searching for her, and the novel’s title is her cry. “For there had been no one else so true, so good, and, even in her unkindness, so kind” (ch. 1). In the seventieth chapter, Vera admits that Miss MacIntosh forbade all dreaming, “and then it was of her I dreamed the most when she was dead, in fact, of her only.”

Miss MacIntosh did not stop being true when her baldness was revealed. She became true in a different way. Her advice outlived her creed. “Be not afraid of life,” she told Vera (ch. 63), and Vera took it, traveling into the broad interior her nursemaid had recommended. The realism fell. The courage it was meant to protect survived. And this, finally, is the pattern of every trial in Part Two. Each system breaks, and something that was carried inside it, love, patience, respect, courage, walks on without it.
