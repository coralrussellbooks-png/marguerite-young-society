To All My Darlings

Chapter 25

On Chapter 25 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 24 · Home · Chapter 26 →

Summary

Vera considers Mr. Spitzer, whom Catherine accuses of being dead, or of being his own dead brother. Joachim, the twin born first, has lived on as “this perpetual and unconsummated suicide,” thinking his dead brother’s thoughts. We learn how Peron died. He fell through a fourteenth-floor window, perhaps by suicide, perhaps mistaking the window for a door. Joachim himself once walked through a glass wall in another lawyer’s office without noticing, and his valet later found glass behind his ears. Bills for the wall followed. When Joachim finally tried to collect damages for his discomfort, a clerk told him that Mr. Spitzer had died several years ago.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter immediately introduces Mr. Spitzer through Vera’s perspective, but filtered through Catherine’s eyes. He is described as “useless as regret,” having missed much of life but content with it. The most striking aspect presented is Catherine’s conviction that he is already dead, buried, or married to the worm or his brother. His living flesh does not convince her; she sees him as a “dead brother” and a “pale version of himself.” This passage is crucial because it establishes the central theme of the blurred line between life and death, particularly concerning Mr. Spitzer’s identity. It also highlights Catherine’s unique, perhaps delusional or enchanted, perception of reality, setting the stage for the surreal atmosphere of their interactions. Her ability to see beyond his physical presence, even if seemingly inaccurate, is presented as a form of “omniscience.”
  • The description of Mr. Spitzer’s appearance and behavior reinforces Catherine’s perception of his unreality or partial presence. He wears a “white tuberose or the white carnation” or perhaps just a “door knob” on his lapel, suggesting the ambiguity of his form or Catherine’s perception of it. His “inane apology,” “vapid face,” and habit of “only half listening” and “only half seeing” by closing one eye contribute to the sense of his being only partially present or engaged with the living world. This passage is important because it provides details that embody the abstract idea of his state of being. His actions, like blotting out half his vision and hearing “perhaps to please her,” suggest a passive resignation or even an attempt to conform to her reality.
  • The chapter contrasts Mr. Spitzer’s partial state with Catherine’s heightened, albeit unusual, perception. Her “omniscience, though he was dead, was only half of hers, for she was both living and dead, and she could see everything and much more besides.” She is described as an “excellent judge of reality, that which was so unreal, an infinitesimal particle.” This passage is vital for understanding the dynamic between the two characters and the narrative’s exploration of reality and illusion. Catherine isn’t just mistaken; her perception is presented as a different, perhaps deeper, form of knowing, one that encompasses both life and death and sees the unreality inherent in reality itself. Her ability to perceive Mr. Spitzer as “not the Mr. Spitzer she saw” but a “ghostly person” underscores her unique perspective and his ambiguous status.
  • Despite Catherine’s fantastical claims (tables being coffins of stars, chairs set above a sea of clouds, bumble bees in the bedroom, a rose being a lady’s ear, Mr. Spitzer being a tower of water or a duchess), Mr. Spitzer is “not inclined to deny completely the interesting possibility” and is “rather willing to concede that there was doubtless some right in her point of view.” This passage is important because it reveals aspects of Mr. Spitzer’s character: his passivity, his inability to argue, and his acceptance of the “mortal chaos” where identity and reality are fluid. It contrasts sharply with his later description as a lawyer, highlighting the transformative effect of Catherine’s presence or his own internal state. His concession suggests either a profound uncertainty about reality or a deep empathy/indulgence towards Catherine.
  • The motif of the twin is introduced early and becomes central to Mr. Spitzer’s identity. Catherine accuses him of being married to his brother, and later he is described as a “soul divided” because he is this twin, “and one was dead, and one lived.” The confusion between Peron and Joachim is a key element, suggesting a merging or confusion of their identities. This passage is crucial for understanding Mr. Spitzer’s internal conflict and his perceived state of living death where he embodies the dead brother, making him a divided soul. The ambiguity surrounding which twin is which (Peron the gambler vs. Joachim the lawyer/musician) adds to the sense of confusion and blurred identity.
  • Mr. Spitzer agrees with Catherine that “Time… was like her opium illusions.” His memory is vague, especially of the “immediate moment or image, even when it was presumably real.” His mind is described as “watery and vague,” even his own face not being real to him. This passage reinforces the theme of the dissolution of conventional reality. His difficulty grasping time and immediate reality contributes to his passive state and his acceptance of Catherine’s surreal world. It suggests that his internal state mirrors the external chaos he perceives.
  • The passage where Mr. Spitzer reflects on his past and what might have been is significant. He remembers having loved Catherine, even with his “dying breath,” and believes he found “a refuge from the unreal world, a safe harbor beyond the storms of life” with her. He contemplates how different his life might have been “if ever she had accepted his passive hand in marriage and his undying love.” This passage introduces a layer of pathos and regret to Mr. Spitzer’s character. It reveals a past desire for a conventional life and love, which was apparently unfulfilled, contributing to his current state of perpetual waiting and “living death.” His current life is explicitly described as a “life of patience and of waiting.”
  • The chapter delves into the circumstances surrounding the brother’s death and Mr. Spitzer’s ongoing relationship with this event. The confusion over the brother’s identity (Peron/Joachim) and the nature of his death (suicide or accident) is central. Mr. Spitzer feels a “heavy sense of woe, this stain of immortal guilt.” He continues to think “too much of the dead brother, of Peron’s tastes, his attitudes, likes, dislikes, furies and regrets,” suggesting that the dead brother lives on through him. The passage describing the brother’s death (falling from the fourteenth floor of an office building) is a concrete (though surreal) event in a chapter full of abstraction. This event is the catalyst for Mr. Spitzer’s current state, his divided soul, and his perpetual meditation on death. The brother’s suicide (or accident) is presented as final and decisive, contrasting with Mr. Spitzer’s “hesitant suicide” or “perpetual and unconsummated suicide.”
  • The anecdote about Mr. Spitzer walking through a glass wall is one of the most important and extended passages in the chapter. It serves as a metaphor for his inability to perceive or interact with conventional reality. He didn’t notice the wall, thinking it was “nothing, a transparency as thin as air or thinner.” This accident, which he recounts hesitantly and almost boasts about, is linked to his absent-mindedness, his perpetual grief, and his inability to distinguish between real obstacles and illusions. The fact that he only learned of it from his valet noticing glass particles and receiving bills underscores his detachment from physical reality. This passage is crucial because it provides a concrete example of Mr. Spitzer’s state of being: someone physically present but mentally elsewhere, prone to blurring the lines between the tangible and the intangible, mirroring his brother’s fatal fall through what looked like clouds. It also leads to the final, poignant revelation that, according to an “obtuse desk clerk,” Mr. Spitzer is already dead.
  • The chapter culminates with Mr. Spitzer attempting to collect compensation for his glass wall “injury” and being told by a desk clerk that he is dead, having “departed this mortal life several years ago.” This passage is the most significant as it brings Catherine’s initial perception into a different, external context. It confirms (or at least presents as a bureaucratic fact) Mr. Spitzer’s state of being dead, rendering his arguments and claims void. This final interaction encapsulates the chapter’s core themes: the unreality of life, the reality of death (or vice versa), the confusion of identity, and the ultimate futility of navigating the living world when one is already perceived, or actually is, among the dead. His arguments falling upon “empty air” is a powerful final image of his non-existence in the eyes of the living world.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “He was not his living brother.” Whom does Catherine think she is entertaining?
  • “It had made an impression only on the glass.” What does the glass record that Mr. Spitzer does not?
  • Why does the chapter end with a desk clerk telling Mr. Spitzer that he is dead?

Characters

  • Peron, “fickle and faithless,” had “the faith, the finality,” and Joachim, “who had been faithful had had no faith.” What does each brother’s faith cost him?
  • Catherine denies his existence, and yet he owes her “some sense of his own precarious being.” How can denial keep him alive?
  • “Only through him… his brother lived.” Is Joachim keeping Peron alive, or is Peron living Joachim’s life?

Connections

  • In Chapter 2, “Oblivion was his brother,” and Sleep and Death were twins. How does this chapter carry that on?
  • Miss MacIntosh is not seen at Niagara Falls (Chapter 22), and Mr. Spitzer is not seen at the lawyer’s office. Who in this novel is seen?
  • The old clam-digger notices the glass his master never felt. What do servants see in this house?

Reflection

  • Can grief make a person invisible?
  • Is a life of waiting a life?
  • What do we owe the dead we carry?

Research: Characters

Joachim Spitzer (Peron’s twin brother)

  • Described as “useless as regret,” a man trapped in liminality, between being alive and dead, reality and illusion, a “soul divided.”
  • Portrayed with physical and psychological frailty: fat, slow, hesitant.
  • He embodies indecision and ambiguity, unable to commit or fully engage with life.
  • Haunted by his brother Peron’s death and his own survival, which he views as a “living death.”
  • Experiences illusions and confusion; his senses and memory are unreliable.
  • His life is marked by routine, melancholy, and a lack of clear purpose, yet he retains a faint spark of life and pride.
  • He comes to Catherine each evening, “when the moon arose,” and there his ambiguous existence is both challenged and sustained.
  • He remembers that he loved Catherine “even with his dying breath,” and imagines what his life might have been “if ever she had accepted his passive hand in marriage.”
  • He reflects endlessly on Peron’s mysterious death.
  • He walks through a glass wall without noticing or being hurt, a sign of his tenuous grasp on reality.
  • The glass wall brings bills and threatening letters, and finally a claim of his own.
  • He is caught between a life unresolved and a death he keeps rehearsing.

Peron Spitzer (Joachim’s twin brother)

  • The deceased twin, described as quick, impetuous, and decisive.
  • Died in a fall through a fourteenth-floor window, perhaps suicide, perhaps an accident; his death is enveloped in mystery and doubt.
  • Contrasts with Mr. Spitzer’s hesitant survival.
  • Represents, to Mr. Spitzer, a life fully lived and a death perhaps fully embraced.
  • His legacy haunts Mr. Spitzer, who often thinks in his brother’s voice or perspective.
  • A gambler, “quick to live, quick to die,” a man of many superficial loves who would always “take another chance.”

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • A strong, discerning woman who sees through Mr. Spitzer’s facade.
  • She maintains a complex relationship with Mr. Spitzer, accusing him of falsehood yet drawing him into her dreamlike world.
  • Serves as a bulwark for Mr. Spitzer’s fragile existence.
  • Her bedroom is depicted as a magical, enchanted space where reality blurs and transformations occur.
  • She addresses him “as if he were already dead,” reminding him of “those vast areas of life he had surely missed.”

Research: Themes

Life and Death, Liminality

See: Death and Mortality · Suicide in History and Literature · Twins and Doubles

  • Mr. Spitzer exists in a state between life and death, embodying the ambiguity of human existence.
  • The chapter explores death as both a physical end and a metaphysical continuum.
  • The interplay between living and dead, memory and oblivion, is central.

Identity and Duality

See: Identity and the Self · Duality and Doubles · Twins and Doubles

  • The twin brothers symbolize duality: life/death, faith/faithlessness, decisiveness/hesitation.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s identity is fractured, as he carries both his own life and that of his dead brother.
  • The confusion over who he is and who he might become reflects existential uncertainty.

Reality versus Illusion

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Reality is unstable, shifting like “distorted mirror images.”
  • Objects and people are sometimes perceived as other things (e.g., a rose as a lady’s ear).
  • Mr. Spitzer’s experience walking through a glass wall symbolizes the fragility of boundaries between reality and illusion.

Memory and Forgetting

See: Memory and the Past

  • Memory is unreliable, often blending with imagination or dream.
  • Mr. Spitzer struggles with recalling events accurately and questions the nature of his own existence.
  • The dead are both remembered and forgotten, complicating the notion of legacy.

Faith and Doubt

See: Faith, God, and the Supernatural

  • Mr. Spitzer lacks faith in an afterlife, which contributes to his hesitant living.
  • His brother’s suicide suggests a belief in an afterlife or at least a finality that Mr. Spitzer cannot embrace.
  • The tension between hope and despair permeates the narrative.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

The Twin Iron Beds and Golden Cradles

See: The House · Pregnancy, Birth, and Children · Twins and Doubles

  • Symbolize the intertwined destinies of Mr. Spitzer and his brother.
  • Represent infancy, death, and the passage of time.

The Glass Wall Incident

See: Mirrors and Reflections · Keys, Doors, and Locks · Illusion and Reality

  • Symbolizes Mr. Spitzer’s fragile boundary between reality and illusion.
  • His walking through the wall without injury suggests his partial nullification and disconnection from the physical world.
  • The unnoticed shattering of glass reflects his invisible wounds and fractured self.
  • Peron mistook a window for a door, and Mr. Spitzer once “mistaken a door for a window.” The glass wall is where the two mistakes meet.

Music and Silence

See: Music and Instruments · Silence and Deafness · Music, Sound, and Silence

  • Mr. Spitzer is an ex-musician; music (or the lack thereof) symbolizes harmony, loss, and silent elegy.
  • The “cryptic silence” represents unfulfilled potential and the ineffable nature of existence.

The Rose in the Buttonhole

See: Roses and Flowers

  • Initially a symbol of life and vitality, it is doubted: Mr. Spitzer concedes it may be “in all probability a lady’s ear,” and to Catherine it may be a door knob. The white tuberose and the white carnation are things Jock wore.
  • Highlights the instability of perception.

Research: Resources

  • “of having been married to the worm or to his brother”: “I have said to corruption, Thou art my father: to the worm, Thou art my mother, and my sister” (Job 17:14). See The Book of Job.
  • “The white tuberose or the white carnation in his buttonhole”: Both white flowers were worn or sent in mourning. The white carnation became the emblem of a mother who has died when Anna Jarvis founded Mother’s Day (1908). See Roses and Flowers and Grief, Loss, and Mourning.
  • “and even in the twinkling of an eye”: “We shall all be changed, In a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trump” (1 Corinthians 15:51-52). See The Bible and the Chapter 10 resources.
  • “who did not believe in Heaven except as a state of mind”: The concept of heaven “as a state of mind” originates from interpretations of Jesus’ teachings, particularly the phrase “the kingdom of God is within you” (Luke 17:21), which suggests that a state of peace, love and spiritual fulfillment can be achieved here on Earth through one’s mindset and actions, rather than being solely a place one goes to after death; this interpretation is often associated with mystical or spiritual traditions that emphasize inner consciousness and personal transformation. It captures a key existential and metaphysical perspective in the novel, emphasizing the internal, often ambiguous nature of spiritual experience over fixed external realities. See The Bible and Faith, God, and the Supernatural.
  • “In the still chambers which had never bisected life from death, its twin”: In Hesiod’s Theogony, Sleep and Death are twin brothers, the sons of Night. See Twins and Doubles and the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “he being the ambiguous twin”: Joachim and Peron (Jock Cartwheel) Spitzer may refer to Castor and Pollux, also known as the Dioscuri, twin brothers in Greek and Roman mythology. They are depicted as demigods, associated with horses, sports, and considered patrons of travelers, especially sailors. The constellation Gemini, the twins, in the sky is named after them. When mortal Castor died, Pollux shared his immortality with him, and the brothers took turns between Hades and Olympus, one dead while the other lived. See Greek Mythology, Astronomy, and Twins and Doubles.
  • “the cold mirrors of Lethean streams”: Lethe is one of the five rivers in Hades, with the Styx, Acheron, Cocytus, and Phlegethon. When someone drinks from this river they forget everything. See Greek Mythology and the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “as if it had been mathematically planned by a higher power”: Fate refers to a predetermined destiny where events are beyond one’s control, while “self-determination” means actively shaping one’s own life through choices and actions.
  • “circles within circles, orbs within orbs, many spheres of influence”: The Pythagoreans held that the planets, turning in their spheres, make a music of the heavens that human ears cannot hear. See Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres.
  • “clouds of eyeless witnesses”: “Seeing we also are compassed about with so great a cloud of witnesses” (Hebrews 12:1). See The Bible.
  • “dead sparrows falling”: “One of them shall not fall on the ground without your Father” (Matthew 10:29), and Hamlet’s “There’s a special providence in the fall of a sparrow” (5.2). See The Bible and Shakespeare.
  • “for they were as fickle as wind or water or woman”: The Duke’s aria in Verdi’s Rigoletto (1851): “La donna è mobile / qual piuma al vento” (“Woman is fickle, like a feather in the wind”). See Music and Composers.
  • “her beautiful swan boat which was her bed”: Catherine’s bed is described as a “swan boat,” and there are Swan Boats in Boston, Massachusetts that have been in operation since 1877. See the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “his existence which she denied”: Proving existence through denial refers to a philosophical concept where the act of actively denying something’s existence can inadvertently reveal its presence, often used in arguments where the very act of trying to refute something implies its reality, particularly in discussions about abstract concepts like God or the nature of consciousness. In his Proslogion (1078), Anselm answers “the fool” who “hath said in his heart, There is no God” (Psalm 14:1): to deny God, the fool must first understand “that than which nothing greater can be conceived,” and such a being, Anselm argued, must exist in reality as well as in the mind. This concept can be misused to manipulate arguments by implying that simply denying a claim equates to its truth, which is a logical fallacy. See Faith, God, and the Supernatural.
  • “stealing the lanterns of the gods”: This recalls Prometheus, who stole fire from the gods for humankind (Hesiod, Theogony). See Greek Mythology.
  • “the black coachman’s property”: The black coachman who drives Catherine’s horses. See The Black Coachman and the Chapter 24 resources.
  • “those heavenly harmonies”: The push and pull between silent music and the music of the spheres or harmonious music form much of Mr. Spitzer’s tale, particularly in light of Schopenhauer’s view of music as the highest form of art. The philosophy of silence in music is that silence can be a powerful tool for making a statement, conveying emotion, and organizing structure. The novel embodies Schopenhauer’s belief in music as the highest art through its intricate portrayal of Mr. Spitzer’s silent and profound music, the use of musical metaphors to explore existential themes, and the depiction of music’s transcendence beyond the physical and intellectual world. The novel’s deep engagement with music as both expression and mystery reflects the Schopenhauerian vision of music as the direct and ultimate language of the will. Schopenhauer sets this out in The World as Will and Representation (1818), Book 3, where music, unlike the other arts, is a copy of the will itself. See Schopenhauer, Music, Sound, and Silence, and the Chapter 6 resources.
  • “bread tasted like ashes in his mouth”: “For I have eaten ashes like bread, and mingled my drink with weeping” (Psalm 102:9). See The Bible.
  • “as no man knew the time of his birth, no man knew when he died”: “For man also knoweth not his time” (Ecclesiastes 9:12). See Ecclesiastes.
  • “he had died with every minute since his brother’s death”: Paul’s “I die daily” (1 Corinthians 15:31). See The Bible.
  • “this perpetual and unconsummated suicide”: The will-to-live by Schopenhauer could be used to rationalize why Mr. Spitzer did what he did and how Jock, the Black Coachman, Joachim and Peron are all the same person. Mr. Spitzer’s actions (his tireless searches, his care for others, and his internal conflicts) are driven by this fundamental will, compelling him to persist despite loss and disintegration. The multiplicity of characters such as Jock, the Black Coachman, Joachim, Peron, and Mr. Spitzer symbolically represents different manifestations of this singular will struggling within one being, blurring the lines between self and other, life and death, reality and illusion. Schopenhauer’s term is Wille zum Leben, from The World as Will and Representation (1818). “In psychology, the will to live is the drive for self-preservation, usually coupled with expectations for future improvement in one’s state in life. The will to live is an important concept when attempting to understand and comprehend why we do what we do in order to stay alive, and for as long as we can.” (Wikipedia) See Schopenhauer, Suicide in History and Literature, and The Black Coachman.
  • “the only music worth hearing was the music he had never heard”: “Heard melodies are sweet, but those unheard / Are sweeter” (Keats, “Ode on a Grecian Urn,” 1819). See Romantic Poets and the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “only Mr. Chandelier”: Catherine’s companion of the candles. See Mr. Chandelier and the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “all the people who were more than three miles tall should be put out of the house”: The King’s “Rule Forty-two. All persons more than a mile high to leave the court,” in Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland (1865). See Alice in Wonderland.
  • “this life which was no life but a temporary incarnation, a mistaken embodiment”: Both Hinduism and Buddhism believe in reincarnation, the cycle of rebirth, but their understanding of it differs. Hinduism sees a soul (atman) that is reborn into various forms, while Buddhism views rebirth as a continuation of consciousness, not a soul, and focuses on Nirvana as a path to end suffering, not a destination for the soul. See Hinduism and Buddhism.
  • “What death was this which was no death?”: The philosophy of death explores the meaning of death, our relationship to it, and the various ways it impacts human existence and understanding. It delves into questions about what happens after death, the nature of consciousness, and the ethical implications of death, including suicide. The Nature of Death: Dualism (the soul continues to exist after death, potentially in a separate realm or afterlife), Materialism (death is the permanent cessation of all bodily functions, including consciousness), Annihilationism (death is the end of all conscious experience, and nothing exists after death). Philosophical Perspectives on Death: Stoicism (death is not something to be feared, as it is a natural part of life and a release from suffering), Existentialism (thinkers like Kierkegaard and Heidegger, in Being and Time, 1927, emphasized the importance of confronting our mortality to understand our own existence and the meaning of life), Nietzsche (proposed that individuals have the freedom to define their own death, suggesting it could be a conscious choice rather than an inevitable fate: “Die at the right time,” in “On Free Death,” Thus Spoke Zarathustra, 1883). Ethical Considerations: Suicide (philosophy engages with questions about the moral permissibility of suicide, including whether it’s ever justifiable). See Stoicism, Existentialism, Nietzsche, and Suicide in History and Literature.
  • “having been the jester with bells”: The court fool’s cap and bells. In Yeats’s “The Cap and Bells” (1894), a jester wins the queen only by sending her his cap and bells. See Shakespeare, whose fools wear them.
  • “into the outer darkness where was no individual life or memory”: “Cast ye the unprofitable servant into outer darkness: there shall be weeping and gnashing of teeth” (Matthew 25:30). See The Bible.
  • “Peron had rushed impetuously ahead, and he had fallen through a glass window on the fourteenth floor”: Peron’s death, left between suicide and accident: “this intention made to seem this irrational accident.” See Peron Spitzer and Suicide in History and Literature.
  • “Mr. Spitzer had once mistaken a door for a window”: The doors that are not doors in Chapter 24, where the servants “open doors which were not doors.” See Keys, Doors, and Locks and the Chapter 24 resources.
  • “tread softly where angels feared to rush ahead”: Pope’s “For fools rush in where angels fear to tread” (An Essay on Criticism, 1711), which gave E. M. Forster the title Where Angels Fear to Tread (1905). Young turns it around: Mr. Spitzer treads softly where angels fear to rush. Yeats wrote “Tread softly because you tread on my dreams” (“He Wishes for the Cloths of Heaven,” 1899). It underscores Mr. Spitzer’s uncertainty, a man who never knows “what the next step would bring.”
  • “whether he would stare at that wild, precipitous abyss which would stare at him”: Nietzsche’s quote, “If you gaze long into an abyss, the abyss gazes also into you,” is a famous aphorism warning against becoming consumed by the things you are confronting. It suggests that prolonged engagement with negativity, suffering, or challenging ideas can have a transformative effect on the person observing them, potentially altering their perspective and even their character. Mr. Spitzer, a man whose prolonged confrontation with death, identity, and existential uncertainty has led to a profound internal transformation, shows how the abyss he peers into ultimately gazes back, shaping and haunting his very being. The aphorism is from Beyond Good and Evil (1886), §146, which begins “He who fights with monsters should be careful lest he thereby become a monster.” See Nietzsche.
  • “a famous actress of the gay nineties”: “The Gay Nineties,” the American 1890s, remembered for music halls, gaslight, and stars such as Lillian Russell.
  • “The other lawyer had simply failed to see him”: Mr. Spitzer is not seen at the lawyer’s office, just as Miss MacIntosh is not seen at Niagara Falls. See the Chapter 22 resources.
  • “the lost chords, the silent keys, the broken strings”: Adelaide Procter’s poem “A Lost Chord” (1858), set by Arthur Sullivan as “The Lost Chord” (1877): “I struck one chord of music, / Like the sound of a great Amen.” See Music and Composers.
  • “his gentleman’s valet, the old clam-digger”: Mr. Spitzer’s valet. See The Old Clam-Digger and the Chapter 22 resources.
  • “There had been still, it seemed, a spark of life in Mr. Spitzer”: Friedrich Nietzsche proposed the “will to power” as a driving force behind human behavior, instead of the “will to live” of his predecessor Arthur Schopenhauer. Nietzsche develops it in Thus Spoke Zarathustra (1883-85), “On Self-Overcoming.” Will to live: an irrational force drives humans to strive endlessly. Will to power: a dynamic force drives humans to overcome resistance and become who they truly are. Nietzsche believed that the will to power is more useful than the will to live for explaining human behavior. He used the will to power to explain both life-affirming and life-denying impulses, as well as master and slave morality. He believed that the will to power is the expression of self-realization, and that it is a condition for growth. He held that life itself is will to power, a striving that is never done. In the chapter it reflects Nietzsche’s “will to power” through its exploration of internal conflict, the striving for control amid chaos, the tension between life and death, and the dynamic, creative force underlying human existence, as embodied in Mr. Spitzer’s character and experiences. See Nietzsche and Schopenhauer.

Slow to understand.
Ominous
Threatening; foreboding.
Omniscient
All-knowing.
Opalescent
Shimmering with changing colors, like an opal.
Phantasmagoric
Like a phantasmagoria, a shifting series of dreamlike or nightmarish images. A phantasmagoria was an eighteenth-century magic-lantern ghost show.
Phosphorescent
Glowing faintly in the dark.
Plaintive
Sad and mournful in sound.
Precarious
Unstable and likely to fall or fail.
Precipitous
Very steep; headlong.
Prism
A clear piece of glass with flat sides that splits light into the colors of the rainbow.
Quiescent
Still; at rest.
Raucous
Harsh and loud.
Retrospect
A looking back on the past.
Slumberous
Sleepy; inducing sleep.
Sporadic
Occurring now and then.
Tacitly
Silently; without being said.
Taciturn
Habitually silent; saying little.
Transpired
Happened; took place.
Tuberose
A Mexican plant with heavily fragrant white flowers.
Vapid
Dull and empty of life or interest.
Vicariously
Experienced through another person.
