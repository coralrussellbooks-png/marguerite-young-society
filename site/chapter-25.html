Summary

Vera considers Mr. Spitzer, whom Catherine accuses of being dead, or of being his own dead brother. We learn how Peron died. He fell through a fourteenth-floor window, perhaps by suicide, perhaps mistaking the window for a door. Joachim himself once walked through a glass wall in another lawyer's office without noticing, and his valet later found glass behind his ears. Bills for the wall followed. When Joachim finally tried to collect damages for his discomfort, a clerk told him that Mr. Spitzer had died several years ago.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter immediately introduces Mr. Spitzer through Vera's perspective, but filtered through Catherine's eyes. He is described as "useless as regret," having missed much of life but content with it. The most striking aspect presented is Catherine's conviction that he is already dead, buried, or married to the worm or his brother. His living flesh does not convince her; she sees him as a "dead brother" and a "pale version of himself." This passage is crucial because it establishes the central theme of the blurred line between life and death, particularly concerning Mr. Spitzer's identity. It also highlights Catherine's unique, perhaps delusional or enchanted, perception of reality, setting the stage for the surreal atmosphere of their interactions. Her ability to see beyond his physical presence, even if seemingly inaccurate, is presented as a form of "omniscience".
  • The description of Mr. Spitzer's appearance and behavior reinforces Catherine's perception of his unreality or partial presence. He wears a "white tuberose or the white carnation" or perhaps just a "door knob" on his lapel, suggesting the ambiguity of his form or Catherine's perception of it. His "inane apology," "vapid face," and habit of "only half listening" and "only half seeing" by closing one eye contribute to the sense of his being only partially present or engaged with the living world. This passage is important because it provides details that embody the abstract idea of his state of being. His actions, like blotting out half his vision and hearing "perhaps to please her," suggest a passive resignation or even an attempt to conform to her reality.
  • The chapter contrasts Mr. Spitzer's partial state with Catherine's heightened, albeit unusual, perception. Her "omniscience, though he was dead, was only half of hers, for she was both living and dead, and she could see everything and much more besides". She is described as an "excellent judge of reality, that which was so unreal, an infinitesimal particle". This passage is vital for understanding the dynamic between the two characters and the narrative's exploration of reality and illusion. Catherine isn't just mistaken; her perception is presented as a different, perhaps deeper, form of knowing, one that encompasses both life and death and sees the unreality inherent in reality itself. Her ability to perceive Mr. Spitzer as "not the Mr. Spitzer she saw" but a "ghostly person" underscores her unique perspective and his ambiguous status.
  • Despite Catherine's fantastical claims (tables being coffins of stars, chairs set above a sea of clouds, bumble bees in the bedroom, a rose being a lady's ear, Mr. Spitzer being a tower of water or a duchess), Mr. Spitzer is "not inclined to deny completely the interesting possibility" and is "rather willing to concede that there was doubtless some right in her point of view". This passage is important because it reveals aspects of Mr. Spitzer's character: his passivity, his inability to argue, and his acceptance of the "mortal chaos" where identity and reality are fluid. It contrasts sharply with his later description as a lawyer, highlighting the transformative effect of Catherine's presence or his own internal state. His concession suggests either a profound uncertainty about reality or a deep empathy/indulgence towards Catherine.
  • The motif of the twin is introduced early and becomes central to Mr. Spitzer's identity. Catherine accuses him of being married to his brother, and later he is described as a "soul divided" because he is this twin, "and one was dead, and one lived". The confusion between Peron and Joachim is a key element, suggesting a merging or confusion of their identities. This passage is crucial for understanding Mr. Spitzer's internal conflict and his perceived state of living death where he embodies the dead brother, making him a divided soul. The ambiguity surrounding which twin is which (Peron the gambler vs. Joachim the lawyer/musician) adds to the sense of confusion and blurred identity.
  • Mr. Spitzer agrees with Catherine that "Time... was like her opium illusions". His memory is vague, especially of the "immediate moment or image, even when it was presumably real". His mind is described as "watery and vague," even his own face not being real to him. This passage reinforces the theme of the dissolution of conventional reality. His difficulty grasping time and immediate reality contributes to his passive state and his acceptance of Catherine's surreal world. It suggests that his internal state mirrors the external chaos he perceives.
  • The passage where Mr. Spitzer reflects on his past and what might have been is significant. He remembers having loved Catherine, even with his "dying breath," and believes he found "a refuge from the unreal world, a safe harbor beyond the storms of life" with her. He contemplates how different his life might have been "if ever she had accepted his passive hand in marriage and his undying love". This passage introduces a layer of pathos and regret to Mr. Spitzer's character. It reveals a past desire for a conventional life and love, which was apparently unfulfilled, contributing to his current state of perpetual waiting and "living death." His current life is explicitly described as a "life of patience and of waiting".
  • The chapter delves into the circumstances surrounding the brother's death and Mr. Spitzer's ongoing relationship with this event. The confusion over the brother's identity (Peron/Joachim) and the nature of his death (suicide or accident) is central. Mr. Spitzer feels a "heavy sense of woe, this stain of immortal guilt". He continues to think "too much of the dead brother, of Peron's tastes, his attitudes, likes, dislikes, furies and regrets", suggesting that the dead brother lives on through him. The passage describing the brother's death (falling from the fourteenth floor of an office building) is a concrete (though surreal) event in a chapter full of abstraction. This event is the catalyst for Mr. Spitzer's current state, his divided soul, and his perpetual meditation on death. The brother's suicide (or accident) is presented as final and decisive, contrasting with Mr. Spitzer's "hesitant suicide" or "perpetual and unconsummated suicide".
  • The anecdote about Mr. Spitzer walking through a glass wall is one of the most important and extended passages in the chapter. It serves as a metaphor for his inability to perceive or interact with conventional reality. He didn't notice the wall, thinking it was "nothing, a transparency as thin as air or thinner". This accident, which he recounts hesitantly and almost boasts about, is linked to his absent-mindedness, his perpetual grief, and his inability to distinguish between real obstacles and illusions. The fact that he only learned of it from his valet noticing glass particles and receiving bills underscores his detachment from physical reality. This passage is crucial because it provides a concrete example of Mr. Spitzer's state of being: someone physically present but mentally elsewhere, prone to blurring the lines between the tangible and the intangible, mirroring his brother's fatal fall through what looked like clouds. It also leads to the final, poignant revelation that, according to an "obtuse desk clerk," Mr. Spitzer is already dead.
  • The chapter culminates with Mr. Spitzer attempting to collect compensation for his glass wall "injury" and being told by a desk clerk that he is dead, having "departed this mortal life several years ago". This passage is the most significant as it brings Catherine's initial perception into a different, external context. It confirms (or at least presents as a bureaucratic fact) Mr. Spitzer's state of being dead, rendering his arguments and claims void. This final interaction encapsulates the chapter's core themes: the unreality of life, the reality of death (or vice versa), the confusion of identity, and the ultimate futility of navigating the living world when one is already perceived, or actually is, among the dead. His arguments falling upon "empty air" is a powerful final image of his non-existence in the eyes of the living world.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Joachim Spitzer (Peron’s twin brother)

  • Described as "useless as regret," a man trapped in liminality, between being alive and dead, reality and illusion, a “soul divided”.
  • Portrayed with physical and psychological frailty: fat, slow, hesitant.
  • He embodies indecision and ambiguity, unable to commit or fully engage with life.
  • Haunted by his brother Peron’s death and his own survival, which he views as a “living death.”
  • Experiences illusions and confusion; his senses and memory are unreliable.
  • Visits Catherine regularly, where his ambiguous existence is both challenged and sustained.
  • His life is marked by routine, melancholy, and a lack of clear purpose, yet he retains a faint spark of life and pride.
  • Mr. Spitzer's ambiguous existence and his nightly visits to Catherine..
  • Reflections on his brother Peron’s mysterious death.
  • An episode where Mr. Spitzer physically walks through a glass wall without injury, symbolizing his tenuous grasp on reality.
  • The ongoing conflict involves legal claims and correspondence.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s internal conflict regarding his unresolved life and death.

Peron (Joachim’s twin brother) Spitzer

  • The deceased twin, described as quick, impetuous, and decisive.
  • Committed suicide by falling through a window; his death is enveloped in mystery and doubt.
  • Contrasts with Mr. Spitzer’s hesitant survival.
  • Represents a life fully lived and a death fully embraced.
  • His legacy haunts Mr. Spitzer, who often thinks in his brother’s voice or perspective.

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • A strong, discerning woman who sees through Mr. Spitzer’s facade.
  • She maintains a complex relationship with Mr. Spitzer, accusing him of falsehood yet drawing him into her dreamlike world.
  • Serves as a bulwark for Mr. Spitzer’s fragile existence.
  • Her bedroom is depicted as a magical, enchanted space where reality blurs and transformations occur.

Research: Themes

Life and Death, Liminality

See: Death and Mortality

  • Mr. Spitzer exists in a state between life and death, embodying the ambiguity of human existence.
  • The chapter explores death as both a physical end and a metaphysical continuum.
  • The interplay between living and dead, memory and oblivion, is central.

Identity and Duality

See: Identity and the Self · Duality and Doubles

  • The twin brothers symbolize duality: life/death, faith/faithlessness, decisiveness/hesitation.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s identity is fractured, as he carries both his own life and that of his dead brother.
  • The confusion over who he is and who he might become reflects existential uncertainty.

Reality vs. Illusion

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Reality is unstable, shifting like “distorted mirror images.”
  • Objects and people are sometimes perceived as other things (e.g., a rose as a lady’s ear).
  • Mr. Spitzer’s experience walking through a glass wall symbolizes the fragility of boundaries between reality and illusion.

Memory and Forgetting

See: Memory and the Past

  • Memory is unreliable, often blending with imagination or dream.
  • Mr. Spitzer struggles with recalling events accurately and questions the nature of his own existence.
  • The dead are both remembered and forgotten, complicating the notion of legacy.

Faith and Doubt

See: Faith, God, and the Supernatural

  • Mr. Spitzer lacks faith in an afterlife, which contributes to his hesitant living.
  • His brother’s suicide suggests a belief in an afterlife or at least a finality that Mr. Spitzer cannot embrace.
  • The tension between hope and despair permeates the narrative.

Research: Resources

  • The will-to-live by Schopenhauer could be used to rationalize why Mr. Spitzer did what he did and how Jock, the Black Coachman, Joachim and Peron are all the same person. Mr. Spitzer’s actions (his tireless searches, his care for others, and his internal conflicts) are driven by this fundamental will, compelling him to persist despite loss and disintegration. The multiplicity of characters such as Jock, the Black Coachman, Joachim, Peron, and Mr. Spitzer symbolically represents different manifestations of this singular will struggling within one being, blurring the lines between self and other, life and death, reality and illusion. “In psychology, the will to live is the drive for self-preservation, usually coupled with expectations for future improvement in one's state in life. The will to live is an important concept when attempting to understand and comprehend why we do what we do in order to stay alive, and for as long as we can. This can be related to either one's push for survival on the brink of death, or someone who is just trying to find a meaning to continuing their life. Some researchers say that people who have a reason or purpose in life during such dreadful and horrific experiences will often appear to fare better than those that may find such experiences overwhelming. Every day, people undergo countless types of negative experiences, some of which may be demoralizing, hurtful, or tragic. An ongoing question continues to be what keeps the will to live in these situations. People who claim to have had experiences involving the will to live have different explanations behind it.”
  • The push and pull between silent music and the music of the spheres or harmonious music form much of Mr. Spitzer’s tale. Particularly how Schopenhauer considers music the highest form of art. The philosophy of silence in music is that silence can be a powerful tool for making a statement, conveying emotion, and organizing structure. The novel embodies Schopenhauer’s belief in music as the highest art through its intricate portrayal of Mr. Spitzer’s silent and profound music, the use of musical metaphors to explore existential themes, and the depiction of music’s transcendence beyond the physical and intellectual world. The novel’s deep engagement with music as both expression and mystery reflects the Schopenhauerian vision of music as the direct and ultimate language of the will.
  • “where angels fear to tread” - may refer to the book, Where Angels Fear to Tread by EM Forster. This line can be interpreted as highlighting the existential and spiritual uncertainties faced by the characters, especially Vera and Miss MacIntosh, as they encounter realms (both literal and metaphorical) that transcend ordinary human experience and comprehension. It underscores the delicate balance between life and death, reality and illusion, and the profound, often frightening, mysteries that lie at the boundaries of human understanding.
  • Archimedes of Syracuse was an ancient Greek mathematician, physicist, engineer, astronomer, and inventor. He is known for his contributions to mathematics, physics, and engineering, and is often considered one of the greatest scientists and mathematicians of all time.
  • The concept of heaven “as a state of mind” originates from interpretations of Jesus’ teachings, particularly the phrase the “Kingdom of Heaven is within you,” Luke 17:21, which suggests that a state of peace, love and spiritual fulfillment can be achieved here on Earth through one’s mindset and actions, rather than being solely a place one goes to after death; this interpretation is often associated with mystical or spiritual traditions that emphasize inner consciousness and personal transformation. It captures a key existential and metaphysical perspective in the novel, emphasizing the internal, often ambiguous nature of spiritual experience over fixed external realities.
  • Fate refers to a predetermined destiny where events are beyond one’s control, while “self-determination” means actively shaping one’s own life through choices and actions.
  • Proving existence through denial refers to a philosophical concept where the act of actively denying something’s existence can inadvertently reveal its presence, often used in arguments where the very act of trying to refute something implies its reality, particularly in discussions about abstract concepts like God or the nature of consciousness. In philosophy, the most well-known example of this is Anselm’s Ontological Argument, which attempts to prove God’s existence by arguing that if you can conceive of a being greater than which nothing can be conceived, then that being must exist because the idea of a “greater being” inherently implies existence. This concept can be misused to manipulate arguments by implying that simply denying a claim equates to its truth, which is a logical fallacy.
  • Friedrich Nietzsche proposed the “will to power” as a driving force behind human behavior, instead of the “will to life” of his predecessor Arthur Schopenhauer. Will to life - an irrational force drives humans to strive endlessly. Will to power - a dynamic force drives humans to overcome resistance and become who they truly are. Nietzsche believed that the will to power is more useful than the will to life for explaining human behavior. He used the will to power to explain both life-affirming and life-denying impulses, as well as master and slave morality. He believed that the will to power is the expression of self-realization, and that it is a condition for growth. He believed that the will to power is a perpetual struggle against life, and that it is never done. In the chapter it reflects Nietzsche's "will to power" through its exploration of internal conflict, the striving for control amid chaos, the tension between life and death, and the dynamic, creative force underlying human existence, as embodied in Mr. Spitzer’s character and experiences.
  • Joachim and Peron (Jock Cartwheel) Spitzer may refer to Castor and Pollux, also known as the Dioscuri, as twin brothers in Greek and Roman mythology. They are depicted as demigods, associated with horses, sports, and considered patrons of travelers, especially sailors. The constellation Gemini, the twins, in the sky is named after them.
  • Both Hinduism and Buddhism believe in reincarnation, the cycle of rebirth, but their understanding of it differs. Hinduism sees a soul (atman) that is reborn into various forms, while Buddhism views rebirth as a continuation of consciousness, not a soul, and focuses on Nirvana as a path to end suffering, not a destination for the soul.
  • The philosophy of death explores the meaning of death, our relationship to it, and the various ways it impacts human existence and understanding. It delves into questions about what happens after death, the nature of consciousness, and the ethical implications of death, including suicide and capital punishment. The Nature of Death: Dualism (the soul continues to exist after death, potentially in a separate realm or afterlife), Materialism (death is the permanent cessation of all bodily functions, including consciousness), Annihilationism (death is the end of all conscious experience, and nothing exists after death). Philosophical Perspectives on Death: Stoicism (death is not something to be feared, as it is a natural part of life and a release from suffering), Existentialism (like Kierkegaard and Heidegger, emphasized the importance of confronting our mortality to understand our own existence and the meaning of life), Nietzsche (proposed that individuals have the freedom to define their own death, suggesting it could be a conscious choice rather than an inevitable fate). Ethical Considerations: Suicide (philosophy engages with questions about the moral permissibility of suicide, including whether it's ever justifiable), Capital Punishment (the death penalty raises complex ethical questions about state-sanctioned killing and the value of human life), Euthanasia (issues related to assisted dying and the right to choose one's own death). The Meaning of Life: some philosophers argue that the awareness of our mortality can give deeper meaning to life, prompting us to live more fully and intentionally; others suggest that facing death head-on can help us appreciate the present moment and the beauty of existence. Fear of Death: the philosophy of death explores the psychological and emotional impact of fearing death, including its effects on our choices and behaviors; some philosophers believe that the fear of death is irrational and stems from our ignorance of what happens after death. Implications for Living: many philosophical perspectives on death suggest that accepting our mortality can lead to a more fulfilling and meaningful life; by contemplating death, we may be better equipped to appreciate the present moment, make conscious choices, and live a life aligned with our values.
  • Nietzsche's quote, "If you gaze long into an abyss, the abyss gazes also into you," is a famous aphorism warning against becoming consumed by the things you are confronting. It suggests that prolonged engagement with negativity, suffering, or challenging ideas can have a transformative effect on the person observing them, potentially altering their perspective and even their character. Mr. Spitzer as a man whose prolonged confrontation with death, identity, and existential uncertainty has led to a profound internal transformation shows in the novel how the abyss he peers into ultimately gazes back, shaping and haunting his very being.
