Summary

Esther makes tiny clothes for no one and sets out crumbs for spirits. She tells of her first child, Walter. His father was a Black man who had escaped a lynching and came to her one night, then fled. Alone and frightened, she gave birth and pressed her thumb to still Walter's cry. Believing she had killed him, she hid him and ran. Yet she imagines him alive and growing somewhere, returning in visions to ask why she killed him and where his father is.

Key passages

From the research

  • Esther is deeply affected by the presence of spirits and the memory of her dead children. She engages in rituals like making little clothes for no one, setting out bread crumbs for spirits, and building a little house of straw in a tree for the spirit to come home to. This shows her ongoing connection to the lost children and her attempt to reconcile with the past.
  • Esther discusses her impending marriage but insists she will not marry until the child comes. This hesitation reflects her uncertainty about the child's paternity and her own feelings about love and responsibility. She contemplates the future of her progeny, hoping for strength and happiness despite her doubts.
  • A central and deeply moving passage details Esther's first-born, Walter, who she believes she "killed" but who lives on in some form, haunting her conscience and mind. Walter represents both her guilt and her love, a child who was born under tragic circumstances and then taken away, yet whose presence continues to torment her.
  • Walter accuses Esther of killing him and questions her about his father. Esther's guilt is palpable as she tries to reconcile her actions with her love for Walter, describing him as a "black mystery" and acknowledging the painful burden he places on her.
  • Esther distinguishes Walter from her many stillborn children, emphasizing that he was born alive and continues to grow. This passage highlights the profound difference between the living child and the numerous losses she has endured, all of which weigh heavily on her psyche.
  • The chapter provides a detailed narrative of how Walter was conceived by a "black stranger" in a night of love and how Esther was unprepared and frightened by the pregnancy. This passage is important as it contextualizes Esther's fear, shame, and the social consequences she faced, especially regarding race and societal judgment.
  • Throughout the chapter, Esther is described as perpetually pregnant, symbolizing her ongoing burden of guilt, loss, and hope. This "everlasting pregnancy" is a metaphor for her emotional state: caught between life and death, hope and despair.
  • Esther admits to having pressed her thumb to still Walter's birth cry, effectively killing him, but not without enduring lifelong remorse. She also recounts how she hid the body and fled to avoid detection. This revelation is crucial as it underlines the secret that haunts her and explains her psychological torment.
  • The chapter closes with Esther reflecting on her many miscarriages and stillborn children, the idea that life is filled with loss and unfinished stories. Despite everything, she clings to the memory of Walter, who symbolizes both her greatest sorrow and her deepest love.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Esther Longtree

  • Esther is complex, embodying resilience, confusion, sorrow, and hope.
  • She is aware of her societal marginalization and her own perceived "madness" but embraces her unique perspective.
  • Her character oscillates between despair and defiance, as she laughs at her barrenness and mourns her losses simultaneously.
  • Her first-born child Walter who both haunts and defines her. He symbolizes her greatest loss and source of emotional torment.
  • Walter’s Father is a black man whose identity and fate are shrouded in mystery and racial tension.
  • Many transient men appear in her life, symbolizing fleeting connections and failed attempts at establishing lasting family bonds.

Walter

  • Walter is a profoundly complex and symbolic character. He is Esther Longtree's first-born child, a figure who embodies both literal and metaphorical dimensions of life, death, guilt, and memory.
  • Walter is described as a child whom Esther had "killed," yet paradoxically, he "lived." This duality makes him a ghostly presence; both alive and dead in the narrative reality. Esther hears his cries, feels his accusations, and wrestles with the burden of her guilt and regret that he represents. Walter's persistent questioning, “Mother, why have you killed me?” and “Where is my father?”, haunts Esther’s conscience, embodying her unatoned sin and unresolved emotional pain.
  • Walter acts as a relentless accuser who confronts Esther with her past actions and their consequences. His presence "keeps her working," making her scrub floors and endure long hours in the restaurant. He embodies the emotional and psychological torment that Esther cannot escape. His accusations are unanswerable, and he seems to have a cruel, hard heart, adding to Esther’s suffering.
  • Walter is portrayed with specific physical features, "black as my true love, black as my heart", reflecting his mixed racial heritage, which complicates Esther’s feelings toward him. His blackness symbolizes difference and alienation, as well as the social challenges unresolved in Esther's life. The fact that his father was black and a "prince of Africa" adds layers to Walter’s identity, evoking themes of race, social stigma, and forbidden love.
  • Walter represents the "beloved obstacle" to Esther’s life and future. His existence and the guilt attached to him prevent her from fully embracing motherhood and happiness. Esther’s many stillborn children and miscarriages contrast with Walter’s unique status as the one child who lived but remained emotionally inaccessible. Walter is both the cause of her endless pregnancies and the reason for her emotional isolation.
  • Walter is inseparable from Esther’s memories, a persistent shadow in her mind. He symbolizes a trauma that has not been resolved: her fear, shame, and the consequences of a youthful mistake. The narrator, Vera, reveals that Walter's presence is intertwined with Esther’s perception of time, reality, and self, blurring lines between life and death, memory and present.
  • Walter is central to the chapter’s meditation on birth, death, life’s incompleteness, and the mysteries of existence. Esther’s dialogue with Walter, his questioning, and his haunting presence articulate the theme that life is an ongoing negotiation between presence and absence, love and loss, guilt and redemption.
  • Walter is not merely a character but a symbolic force representing Esther’s guilt, lost motherhood, racial complexity, and the unresolved past that haunts her. He is a living accusation and a ghostly memory, a child who should have died but has instead become an eternal presence in Esther’s life, shaping her identity and destiny.

Walter’s father

  • Walter's father is portrayed primarily through Esther Longtree's memories, feelings, and the symbolic weight he carries rather than through direct characterization or actions.
  • Walter's father is described as a black man, "as black as the ace of spades," a "Negro, a Negro, a Negro," who came into Esther's life unexpectedly and left an indelible mark upon her. He is portrayed as a "stranger," a "poor fellow," "a tramp," "a fugitive," or even a "prince," which indicates Esther's ambivalence and the mystique surrounding him. This man came into her life in the darkness of a summer night, in an act that was "half of sleeping and half of waking," suggesting a blend of innocence, confusion, and inevitability in their union.
  • His blackness is significant in the narrative: Esther did not initially realize the racial difference at the moment of conception because "his face had been white" in the moonlight, and only "at dawn... she had seen that he was black". This racial difference introduces a complex dynamic of love, fear, and societal judgment. Esther’s heart turns "black with the years," and she recognizes her bond to him as something that makes her a "slave to his memory". His departure is framed not as Esther leaving him, but as him fleeing "for fear the white people would see him and kill him", underscoring the racial tensions and dangers of the time.
  • Walter's father is not depicted as a traditional or nurturing figure in Esther's life. He is absent after the conception, and Esther carries the burden of his memory, the pregnancy, and the consequences largely alone. Despite this absence, his presence haunts Esther, symbolizing both the origin of her deepest grief and the source of a love she treasured but could not fully realize. His identity as a black man in a racially divided society adds a layer of tragedy and alienation to the story.
  • Walter's father is a mysterious, absent figure whose racial identity and sudden disappearance shape Esther's life and psyche profoundly. He embodies the forces of love, loss, societal exclusion, and the lingering pain of an unfinished past that Esther cannot escape.

Research: Themes

Motherhood and Pregnancy

See: Motherhood, Pregnancy, and Birth

  • Esther is perpetually pregnant, both literally and metaphorically. Her body swells with pregnancies that often end in stillbirths or miscarriages, yet she carries an enduring hope for a living child.
  • The physical and emotional toll of her pregnancies is immense; she experiences nausea, swelling, and an overwhelming sense of waiting and expectation.
  • Pregnancy is symbolic of life, death, and the cyclical nature of existence. It is her "widow’s weeds," representing loss and continual renewal.

Guilt and Redemption

See: Guilt, Innocence, and Justice

  • Esther harbors deep guilt over her first-born son, Walter, whom she believes she "killed" by abandoning him at birth.
  • Despite her guilt, Walter is alive in her memories and visions, accusing her of her sins and questioning her about his father.
  • Her attempts to redeem herself manifest in her ongoing pregnancies and her hope that the new child will compensate for the past losses.

Race and Identity

See: Identity and the Self · Society, Class, and Race

  • Walter's father was a black man, described metaphorically as "the prince of Africa" and "as black as the ace of spades."
  • Esther’s shock and societal stigma over this interracial relationship and child profoundly impact her life.
  • Walter’s blackness is a symbolic and literal source of conflict, shame, love, and fear for Esther, reflecting broader themes of racial tension and identity.

Life, Death, and the Supernatural

See: Death and Mortality · Faith, God, and the Supernatural

  • The narrative blurs boundaries between life and death, as Esther communicates with spirits and imagines an "other world" parallel to this one.
  • Her miscarriages and stillborn children are depicted as ghostly presences and delicate entities like moths, feathers, and snowflakes.
  • Walter exists both as a living child and a spectral presence haunting her conscience.

Memory and Time

See: Memory and the Past · Time

  • Esther’s memory is fragmented, cyclical, and tinged with confusion as she recalls her past pregnancies, loves, and losses.
  • Time is fluid; events from years ago feel present and immediate, especially as she contemplates the "unfinished murder" of Walter.
  • The narrative explores how memory shapes identity and the persistence of past trauma.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

  • Pregnancy is a central symbol representing creation, hope, and the possibility of life. However, in Esther's case, it also symbolizes loss, failure, and the burden of guilt, as she has experienced many stillbirths and miscarriages. Her "everlasting pregnancy" is not just physical but metaphysical, carrying the memory and shadow of her first child who died but also "lived" in a spiritual or haunting way. This duality of birth and death intertwined in her pregnancy reflects her conflicted emotions and the complexity of motherhood.
  • Walter, Esther's first-born, is a powerful symbol of both guilt and unresolved trauma. He is described as both dead and alive; he haunts her as a ghostly presence, accusing her of killing him, yet he "lives" and grows outside her. Walter’s contradictory existence symbolizes Esther’s inner turmoil, remorse, and the inescapable consequences of past actions. His blackness, in contrast to the white imagery surrounding Esther, also symbolizes otherness, racial difference, and social alienation.
  • The interplay of black and white is a recurring symbol throughout the chapter. Esther herself is dark-skinned, and her lover and child are black, while many surrounding images are white: white butterflies, white snow, white moonlight, white flowers, and the white detective. This contrast symbolizes racial difference, purity versus guilt, life versus death, and the blending or conflict of identities. The "black as the ace of spades" and white moonlight imagery evokes a sense of fate, mystery, and the intersection of love and tragedy.
  • Nature is used extensively to symbolize the cycle of life and death, the presence of spirits, and the passage of time.
  • Butterflies and moths symbolize fragile, ephemeral life and transformation.
  • Leaves stirring and falling evoke the whispering of spirits and the inevitability of decay.
  • Rain and umbrellas suggest cleansing, sorrow, and the connection between this world and the "other world" where spirits reside.
  • The moon and stars symbolize mystery, the unconscious, and the eternal presence of life beyond death.
  • Esther’s connection to nature signifies her role as a "go-between," mediating between life and death, reality and spirit.
  • The "Little Shadow Boxer" is a metaphor for the unborn child she carries now, representing hope and continuity but also uncertainty. The child is called a "shadow," indicating something not fully realized or tangible and a continuation of her past mistakes but also a potential for redemption. This image underlines the tension between reality and illusion, life and death.
  • Esther is described as "always knitting the invisible," a metaphor for her attempts to create and sustain life out of intangible dreams and memories. This symbolizes her efforts to reconcile with her past and to give form to what has been lost or broken. The invisible thread also suggests the fragile connections between generations, memory, and identity.
  • The Chicago Detective and Salesmen recurring characters symbolize societal judgment, surveillance, and the external pressures Esther faces. The white detective’s suspicions and the sterile salesmen represent the social forces that have failed to understand or accept her, adding to her isolation and guilt.
  • The knocking and Esther’s answering represent her confrontation with her past, her guilt, and the spirits of her children; both born and unborn. It symbolizes the inescapable nature of memory and conscience, and her role as the mediator between the living and the dead.
  • Walter is described as the "beloved obstacle" preventing her from moving fully into new relationships or new conceptions. This symbolizes how past trauma and guilt can block future happiness and freedom.
  • The "Cloud," "Shadow," and "Reflection" images symbolize ambiguity, duality, and the blurred boundaries between reality and imagination, life and death. Esther often reflects on these, questioning where the living and the dead reside, and suggesting her mental state blurs these distinctions.
  • The "Black Hand Laid Across the Womb" is a potent symbol of the legacy of her first child’s death and haunting presence. It represents both the physical and psychological obstruction preventing her from fully moving on, and the lasting impact of her guilt and loss.
  • The "White Bird on the Ice Floe" evokes both beauty and isolation, symbolizing the fragile hope and distance between Esther and what she desires or has lost. It suggests the elusiveness of her dreams and the coldness of her reality

Research: Resources

  • Esther Longtree is portrayed as a profoundly tragic and complex figure, embodying themes of maternal loss, guilt, and longing. Her character is suffused with a haunting sense of grief that permeates her existence, shaped by repeated pregnancies, stillbirths, and a particularly painful memory of her first-born child, Walter. Physically, Esther is described as a large, cross-eyed waitress who is perpetually pregnant or swollen, a physical manifestation of her inner burden and continuous state of expectation and sorrow. Her pregnancy is not merely biological but symbolic: it represents her ongoing attempt to atone for past mistakes and losses and to reclaim a maternal identity fractured by tragedy. A central aspect of Esther's character is her relationship with Walter, her first-born, who, despite being "killed" in a sense, has lived on outside her awareness. Walter is both a ghostly presence and a source of endless accusation and sorrow. He haunts her conscience, asking why she killed him and where his father is, embodying Esther’s unresolved guilt and the burden of an unfinished murder. Walter's existence outside her, his growth and vitality, contrasts sharply with the stillborn children and miscarriages that have otherwise defined her reproductive life. Her narrative reveals a deep, ambivalent love and a tragic recognition of her own limitations and mistakes as a mother, compounded by her youth, ignorance, and fear at the time of Walter’s birth. Esther’s story exposes the intersection of race and identity: Walter’s father was a black man, a "prince" from Africa, who was a fugitive and victim of racial violence. This adds layers of social and personal complexity to Esther’s grief, shame, and isolation. Her memories of this man are filled with shock and surprise, reflecting the racial tensions and prejudices surrounding her. Esther’s heart turning black metaphorically expresses her internalization of this complex legacy. Esther is shown as a woman caught between two worlds: the physical, harsh reality of her life as a waitress and the spiritual, shadowy world of her lost and unborn children, as well as the memory of Walter. She acts as a "go-between," navigating the liminal space between life and death, presence and absence, reality and dream. This is symbolized by her making a little house of straw for the spirit to come home to, answering every knocking, and feeding the spirits as if to soothe her conscience. Her repeated pregnancies and stillbirths symbolize not only personal tragedy but also a universal theme of loss and unfulfilled potential. Esther’s life is a continual cycle of hope and despair, birth and death, presence and absence. Her laughter, sometimes convulsive or mule-like, reflects a bitter acceptance of her fate, mingled with stubborn resilience. Esther Longtree is a figure of profound maternal suffering and endurance. She is marked by a deep, unresolved guilt and grief linked to her first child, Walter, whose living presence she both mourns and fears. Her story is one of tragic memory, racial and social complexity, and the haunting persistence of loss. She is a living embodiment of the liminal space between life and death, the seen and unseen, the known and the unknowable, and her narrative richly explores themes of motherhood, identity, and redemption within the novel.
  • Esther Longtree symbolizes a deep and tragic history of racial and social injustice in the United States, particularly relating to the legacy of slavery, interracial relationships under oppressive conditions, and the ongoing consequences of systemic racism. Her story reflects the painful history of a black father, described as a "Negro, a Negro, a Negro, poor young fellow, black as the black bough, the ace of spades," who was a fugitive, possibly an ex-convict, victimized by white supremacy: "the white people there had tried to kill him because he was black and had wanted to sleep with a white woman... they had tried to hang him from a white-blossoming tree for revenge, but the rope had broken". This narrative evokes the historical reality of lynching and racial violence in the American South. Esther’s pregnancy with this black man’s child, her subsequent abandonment of the child, and her lifelong torment symbolize the fraught and often violent intersections of race, gender, and power in America. Her child, Walter, who is black and whom she both loved and feared, becomes a haunting presence in her life: a symbol of the legacy of interracial unions condemned and punished by society, and the personal and social consequences of such relationships. The repeated motif of Esther being "always pregnant," carrying the memory and burden of lost or stillborn children, reflects the cycles of trauma and loss imposed on black and mixed-race families by systemic racism and social rejection. Her incarceration in the "Greasy Spoon" restaurant, her social marginalization, and her sense of isolation also symbolize the economic and social disenfranchisement experienced by many African Americans and women in American history. Esther’s struggle with guilt and the haunting presence of her first child, who lives outside her physically but not emotionally, represents the unresolved historical wounds of slavery’s legacy: the unacknowledged and hidden suffering passed down through generations. Esther Longtree embodies the history of racial violence, forbidden and punished interracial love, maternal suffering, social exclusion, and the enduring scars of America's racial past. Through her narrative, Young confronts the complexity of identity, memory, and historical trauma in the United States.
  • Esther Longtree's character carries symbolic weight that can be interpreted in relation to the abortion debate in the U.S., although the symbolism is complex, multifaceted, and embedded within the novel’s rich, dreamlike narrative. Esther Longtree is portrayed as a woman who is "everlastingly pregnant," carrying many stillborn children, miscarriages, and unfulfilled pregnancies over the years. She is described as both "brilliant but dumb," "mulish," "big-footed," and “pregnant with doubtfulness,” yet also maternal, loving, and burdened by her mysterious, repeated losses. Her pregnancies are often linked to a haunting mystery involving many absent or sterile fathers and the ambiguous presence of children who are never fully born or recognized. The narrative suggests that Esther has tried repeatedly to give birth to a "child who lived" but is also surrounded by countless stillborn, ghostly children, and shadows: symbolizing loss, grief, and the elusive nature of life and creation. In this light, Esther Longtree can be seen as embodying the tensions surrounding abortion debates: the conflict between life and death, fertility and sterility, birth and loss, hope and despair. Her "everlasting pregnancy" and the repeated miscarriages may symbolize the ongoing societal struggle over reproductive rights, the moral and emotional complexity of abortion, and the societal judgment and silence that often surround it. The ambiguity about the paternity of her children, the legal threats she faces, and her isolation also reflect the politicized and often stigmatized dimensions of abortion in American society. Esther’s story touches on themes of bodily autonomy, societal control, guilt, and secrecy. She is pursued by a detective who accuses her of murder, threatened with legal action, and yet she remains evasive, protective of her secret “pregnancies,” and burdened by the consequences of societal judgments. This can be interpreted as symbolic of the legal and moral scrutiny women face in abortion debates, where personal agency conflicts with external control and societal norms. The novel frames Esther’s experiences within a broader cosmic and existential context, blurring lines between life and death, reality and dream, and individual and universal experiences. This deepens the symbolism, suggesting that the abortion debate is not simply a social or political issue but also a profound human and metaphysical dilemma about creation, destruction, and the meaning of existence. Esther Longtree may symbolize the abortion debate in the U.S., the detailed portrayal of her character’s perpetual pregnancies, miscarriages, societal judgment, and emotional complexity strongly suggests an allegorical reflection on the themes central to abortion discourse. Her story embodies the profound personal and societal conflicts surrounding reproductive rights, loss, and the nature of life itself.

The court's landmark decision has been commemorated and celebrated every year on the Loving Day, June 12th. The number of interracial marriages in 1967 was three percent. The first interracial marriage in what is today the United States was that of Pocahontas, who married tobacco planter John Rolfe in 1614. When their plans to marry were announced, Allen narrowly escaped being lynched. Their marriage was secret, and they left the country immediately for England, never to return.   • Esther in admitting to killing or wanting to kill her children is considered infanticide. In most modern societies the practice is considered immoral and criminal. It still takes place in the Western world usually because of the parent's mental illness or violent behavior, in addition to some poor countries as a form of population control, sometimes with tacit societal acceptance. Female infanticide, a form of sex-selective infanticide, is more common than the killing of male offspring, especially in cultures where male children are more desirable.   • “Kentucky racehorses” Horse racing in Kentucky predates statehood (1792), with organized racing occurring in Louisville as early as 1783. All Kentucky thoroughbreds trace their lineage back to three foundation sires: the Darley Arabian, the Godolphin Arabian, and the Byerly Turk. On May 17, 1875, the first Kentucky Derby was held at Churchill Downs (then the Louisville Jockey Club). It was won by Aristides, trained by Ansel Williamson and ridden by Black jockey Oliver Lewis. In the first 28 runnings of the Derby, Black jockeys won 15 times. Notable jockeys included Isaac Murphy (three-time winner) and the aforementioned Oliver Lewis. Key winners included Vagrant (1876), the first gelding winner, and Hindoo (1881). In 1902, a syndicate led by Col. Matt Winn took over Churchill Downs, transforming it into a premier racing venue. In 1919, Sir Barton became the first horse to win the Kentucky Derby, Preakness, and Belmont Stakes, though the term "Triple Crown" was not commonly used until 1930 with Gallant Fox. Exterminator (1918) was a fan favorite who won despite being a last-minute substitute. Calumet Farm became the dominant force in American racing, with winners like Whirlaway (1941), Pensive (1944), Ponder (1949), and Hill Gail (1952). Often considered one of the greatest horses of all time, Citation won the Triple Crown and became the first horse to win $1 million.
