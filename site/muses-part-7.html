Inviting the Muses, Part 7

← Part 6 · Inviting the Muses: A Reading, Part by Part · Part 8 →

Part 7 finishes the Van Doren speech, covers the last two essays, "Feminine Sensibility" and "On Teaching," and opens the Reviews section, running through the early Poetry reviews, Jean Stafford's Boston Adventure, and most of a double review of Virginia Woolf and Katherine Anne Porter.

"Memorial Speech in Honor of Mark Van Doren," conclusion (previously unpublished)

The rest of the speech follows the same line: Van Doren's poems deal with fugitive things like a fallen barn that "have no history," and gain enchantment precisely "because they will be snatched away by time or change or inventive progress." Young casts him as a Prospero watching the island fade, knowing that Ariel and Caliban will outlast "the evocative magician." "Reality may be hidden but is not lost," she writes, and she reads his Indians as "the slain people" who are "also the phantoms of undying consciousness, that which cannot be slain." The speech ends on "the dream of a house, a house where the dead live." It is a fitting close to the essays proper, since that could describe much of what Young wrote.

"Feminine Sensibility" (Harvard Advocate, 1975)

A single page, and one of the most direct statements in the book. Young flatly says she does not "think that there is any difference between the works of men and women writers." Her reasoning is Jungian: a woman writer uses her animus and a man his anima, so "these sexual differences dissolve, bearing no relation to art." She turns the usual metaphor around by noting how many men have described their books as children and their writing as labor, citing Sterne and his "all-knowing embryo," and she says "the maternal is that by which the cosmic form is made to evolve from the chaos."

She also answers Woolf's A Room of One's Own without naming it outright. Women were not more protected than men but "less protected," "less in charge of their own fate," and "have not yet attained their liberation." Yet no writer is truly protected, "with or without a room of his own," because "the fact that he or she is a writer shows that he or she is unprotected, exposed to that reality from which others veil themselves by convention and habit." Coming from the author of a thousand-page novel largely about women, written over eighteen years, the essay reads less as theory than as self-description.

"On Teaching" (Review of Contemporary Fiction, 1989)

The latest piece in the collection, and a companion to "Inviting the Muses." Young describes her own work as prose "in a lyric form" or "epic form," and describes her Debs biography in progress as the story of "the failed millennial continent of America," a lost earthly paradise that socialism hoped to restore. She calls that project "history as a poem."

Her advice is to read poems first, because "a novel is an extension of metaphor," grounded in close observation of both dreams and outward events, which keeps a writer from "mechanical fantasies and inventions which simply creak with cleverness." The reading list is generous and revealing: Browning's The Ring and the Book, Dante, Blake, Coleridge, Eliot, Pound, Williams, Hopkins, Yeats, Melville, then Flannery O'Connor, Cynthia Ozick, William Gass, Anne Tyler, James Merrill, and biographies of Dalí, Freud, Jung, and others. Her central text is William James's Psychology, to be read beside Henry James's notebooks, with the note that William "wrote psychology like a novelist" and Henry "wrote novels like a psychologist." William James has now appeared in the Night Thoughts essay, the Patmore essay, and here, which makes him the closest thing she has to a single philosophical source.

The early reviews (Poetry, 1935–36)

These are Young at about twenty-seven, and the surprise is how strict she is. Reviewing Mrs. Hickler's Lower than the Angels ("Domestic Fables"), she praises "a combination of the fabulous with the domestic," which is her own later territory, but keeps her expectations cool. Mary Britton Miller's Intrepid Bird ("Leaning Backward") gets sharper treatment: four sonnets contrasting sparrows and doves are "heavy cargo for birds to carry through fifty-six lines," and on the dedication poem about a bird singing on a branch, "one only wishes the branch had been a little higher, a little more precarious." Florence Ripley Mastin's Cobwebs of Cable ("Uncertain Measures") is credited with extending human nature "beyond the prescriptions of the Marxian legislators," but she asks for poems where "every word of which must seem inevitable in its relation to the whole."

The young critic who scolds "stereotyped diction" and demands organic unity is recognizably the same writer who would later defend Proust's page-long paragraphs. What she wanted was not brevity but necessity.

"The Dusty Interiors of Boston" (Kenyon Review, 1944)

The Stafford review is one of the best pieces in the book. She admires Boston Adventure as "provocative" and "delightful," written "as if an insect recorded those minutiae which escape the average eye," with "a relentless capacity for fact" like Marianne Moore's octopus. But she diagnoses the world it depicts: a Boston where "the tragedy being that there is no tragedy," where "the irrational must be excluded," put out "to whine like an old dog in the dark," and where "God, to be real, would have to appear in a high silk hat." She adds a pointed parallel: the Greeks drowned the man who discovered irrational numbers.

The review is fair to Stafford in a way that is easy to miss. Young sees that the author is not waving "the red flag" and that behind the irony lies "much covert love" for a dying society, embodied in Miss Pride, "checkmating herself at chess throughout eternity, because her father did before her." She places the novel among English comedies of manners from Sheridan and Richardson to Thackeray and Austen, and she ends by saying popular novels like St. Elmo and Tempest and Sunshine deserve "a serious study." Two decades later in "Inviting the Muses" she would hold up those same titles as examples of forgotten bestsellers, so her view of them clearly shifted with context.

"Fictions Mystical and Epical" (Woolf and Porter)

Young reads Woolf's A Haunted House as philosophy, not mood. She places Woolf among "the philosophers of sensationalism," Locke, Hume, Berkeley, and Bergson, and calls her "of the order of grand mystic" whose impressionism is "the result of a grand intellectual discipline." In Woolf "there is a journey, but there is hardly a journeyer," and she ties Woolf to John Stuart Mill's definition of matter as the permanent possibility of sensation, the same Mill she cited in "Complex Life and Complex Letters." She grants that such a view "has never been popular," then concludes, "Mrs. Woolf may have, however, the final word."

With Porter's The Leaning Tower, she focuses on the Texas stories where "the conservation of the past was the great dream," and on the title story's papier-mâché tower in 1931 Germany, broken and mended, suggesting "the persistence of an older value." One sentence about the servant Nannie, noting "no disparagement then of the feudal order," is left unclear in its sympathies, and part 7 stops before Young finishes her judgment.

Across part 7

This section shows Young's whole career in a few dozen pages, from the precise young critic of 1935 to the teacher of 1989 handing students a reading list. The constants are her demand that language be necessary, her belief that reality includes the irrational and the invisible, and her refusal of anything that closes the door on either, whether that is a Boston drawing room, a gendered theory of literature, or a creaky plot. The Stafford and Woolf reviews also show that her sympathy extends to writers quite unlike her, as long as they see clearly.
