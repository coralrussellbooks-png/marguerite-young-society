Chapter 58

On Chapter 58 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Woman in Young's Magnum Opus.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

An old violinist plays music no one can hear in this chapter, and the unheard song is its pillar. Frail and ghostly, with a "wounded chin" and "no buttons on his shirt," he draws his bow across silence, and Mr. Spitzer perceives the music with full technical and emotional understanding, so that the invisibility of the unheard performance and the marginalized life it stands for arrive together, a man playing to a world that cannot hear him. The music evokes "the beating of his mother's heart," a "minor composition" that would be "less beautiful if heard by living ears," and the maternal heartbeat carries motherhood and the ephemeral, undervalued feminine experience the book keeps recovering, the "shadings of thought and unconscious memory" that belong to a realm the dominant order dismisses.

The music frees Mr. Spitzer from "corporeal woes," a detachment from the body that is the body as the place the self comes apart eased for a moment into flight, and its ethereal weather, "bird calls," "whispering rain," "sea shells buzzing like bees," is the feminine principle of connection to the organic and cyclical. The violinist shaking "like an aspen tree," "bare branches filled with singing birds," is feminized by the delicacy of the imagery, and Mr. Spitzer's dreaming of other lives for him, ball-player and gentleman and shepherd and judge and fisherman and king, is the plural selfhood the book distributes across everyone, the many masculine roles a self could wear tried on over a ragged coat that fits none of them. The "if only" his bow were another instrument names the limitation society imposes, the potential the world will not let a marginal life realize.

Reality goes "fugitive," "just so many sticks," a "pole or wand or staff or rod," and the arbitrariness of the symbols is the book's refusal of fixed and essential kinds, gendered emblems shown to be interchangeable objects. Mr. Spitzer's obsession with his brother anchors his understanding in a male figure while women stay absent, the patriarchal orientation of memory, and the "old fighters" and "old runners" "broken by life," the athletes he attends to only after Peron's death, are masculinity revealed as vulnerable and aging and mortal, the heroic ideal undone. The boundaries blur between living and dead, music and silence, real and dream, and the violinist's "wire-drawn body," his "mad puppet" movements, a "creature spewed up from the infernal depths," are a self with its agency gone, moved by strings it does not hold.

Mr. Spitzer cannot say whether the musician is "dead" or "dreaming," and the not-knowing is the boundary between life and death held open on a single body. The music "existing only in Mr. Spitzer's imagination," the hesitation to break the silence with applause, is the silencing of the unheard voice and the reverence the book pays it, and the violinist "whirling like a singing top dying down," the "recording angel" with "snow-white pinions," is the invisible labor of creation, the work that goes unwitnessed the way women's work does. Mr. Spitzer as a "walking score board" or abacus is the keeper of accounts the book keeps feminizing, and the music "not even for the white keyboard," "existing apart from transitory sound," is the non-dominant expression that defies the categories entirely. The chapter closes with his "black cape whirling," a "cord drifting like a bellrope," the protective darkness around him, and the ambiguity of whether the musician or the music or the man himself exists at all, the unheard song still sounding inside a mind that cannot tell its own presence from its absence.
