Movement Five: The Traditions the Novel Absorbs

The Captain in Armor

Cousin Hannah Freemount-Snowden is described in the novel's preface as the horsewoman, balloonist, mountain-climber and militant Boston feminist, known as Al Hamad through all the seraglios of the East. The novel develops her as a figure who has substituted the entire machinery of war for the domestic life she refused: she is cuirassed by suffrage and her shield is emblazoned with a cherub carrying a lightning bolt. She has long winter marches through heavy snows and storms of icicles as sharp as swords, desert treks through sand storms, journeys into forbidden Mecca and Medinah and Fez and sacred citadels of men. She has ridden against great turbaned Turks. She carries swords of ice and swords of fire.

This is the tradition of the warrior woman given one of its most fully articulated American forms. The genealogy is long: the Amazons of Greek mythology, who cut off one breast to draw the bow more accurately and founded their civilization on the refusal of the domestic. Boudicca, the British queen who led the revolt against Rome in 60 CE, burning Colchester and London before her final defeat. Joan of Arc, who took up the armor, heard the voices, led the armies, was burned at nineteen. The suffragette militant Emmeline Pankhurst, who called the suffrage campaign a war and conducted it as one. Cousin Hannah is all of these condensed into a single Boston feminist who climbed the Alps and rode into forbidden mosques and never married any man.

Married to the Movement

The novel's most precise statement about Cousin Hannah is not about her campaigns or her mountains. It is about her marriage. She had been married, it had seemed, to the suffrage movement, even as if she were a bride, as if the suffrage movement were her husband, her lord and master who had carried her away. She had been this great lord, scythe sword in hand, her head reaching among the stars, this mighty captain who had rescued so many fair ladies in distress, breaking their invisible chains.

The warrior woman tradition runs through this passage in two directions simultaneously. In one direction, Cousin Hannah is the knight: the rescuer, the one who rides against the chains, the one with the scythe sword. In the other direction, she is also the bride: carried away by the movement as by a lord and master. She is simultaneously the warrior who rescues fair ladies and the fair lady who has been swept into a consuming love. She has not escaped the marriage structure. She has transferred it. The movement is her husband. The battle is her domestic life. The convention she refused in its ordinary form has been adopted wholesale in its militant form: she is married more completely than most married women, to a cause that admits no rival, requires absolute loyalty, demands total sacrifice.

This is the AND Philosophy's Act Three. Act One: the world says women cannot vote, cannot hold property, cannot command. Act Two: Cousin Hannah says no, this is the reality, and the reality is unjust, and I will fight it. Act Three: she has no alternative to the structure she is fighting except the inverted form of it: not marriage to a man but marriage to the fight against marriage's constraints, not domestic submission but the submission of her entire life to a cause that has the same formal requirements as a domineering husband. She is free and she is not free. She is the AND Philosophy's most complicated figure because her Act Three is also still entangled with Act One's terms.

The One Battle She Lost

Cousin Hannah won enormous battles. She had negotiated great torrents of ice and snow, bearded human lions in their dens in Persia, startled great sheiks in their ivory beds. She had carried her emblazoned suffrage message to the far corners of the earth, even to countries where there was perhaps not even a male reindeer startled by her coming. She had ridden against great turbaned Turks and sacred citadels of men. She had the victories of a general.

And she lost the one battle she most needed to win: the opium lady. She had wanted to carry my mother away from this dark tower, to rescue her from opium dreams of the dead love, and so the valiant heart had fought her losing battle with shadows. She had won greater battles than this which she had lost. The precision is devastating. Cousin Hannah, who had moved mountains and crossed forbidden cities and climbed the Himalayas, could not move one woman from her bed. She could not break the opium lady's chains because the opium lady did not experience them as chains. She experienced them as her life.

This is the limit of the warrior woman tradition. Joan of Arc was burned. Boudicca was defeated. The Amazons lost their war with the Greeks in most versions of the myth. The warrior woman's story is not finally a story of victory. It is a story of a particular kind of courage that keeps going in the face of losses it should not be able to absorb. Cousin Hannah is dead before the novel opens. Her detonations of rage are still shaking the prisms in the Cartwheel house. She lost. She is still there.

The Uninvited Wedding Guest

Cousin Hannah is explicitly identified as the uninvited wedding guest in the novel: the one who arrived at the opium lady's life and was sometimes ignored, sometimes frozen with remoteness, sometimes given a sidelong glance that might signify much or nothing. In Coleridge's poem, the wedding guest is stopped against his will and made to hear the story of catastrophic loss. In the novel, Cousin Hannah is the one who is stopped, who cannot get past the opium lady's remoteness, who cannot attend the wedding to the dead man's ghost that is the opium lady's consuming passion.

She is the guest who comes to the wedding with a counteroffer: here is the world, here is the movement, here is a life that does not require a ghost for a husband. The opium lady does not hear her. Cousin Hannah rises from the encounter sadder and wiser. She rises, in fact, already dead, which is the only position from which her story can be told in this novel: dead, still shaking the prisms, still unable to let go of the battle she lost, still married to the movement that was her life.

The AND Philosophy Reading

Cousin Hannah is the AND Philosophy's figure for Act Three attempted and not completed. She takes up armor and marches. This is not small. Most people do not take up armor and march. She goes further than almost anyone else in the novel into the terrain of actual change: she goes to Persia, she climbs the Alps, she forces her way into citadels of men, she keeps the suffrage campaign going on fronts no one else has thought of. She is one of the novel's genuinely great figures and the novel loves her for it.

And she cannot build the thing she is trying to build with the opium lady. She cannot construct the Act Three reality in which her cousin also gets free. The plural self pillar: the opium lady is multiple selves, and the self that needs the ghost-husband is as real as any other self, and Cousin Hannah's Act Three cannot replace it, only fight it. The grief as permanent weather pillar: the opium lady's grief for her dead husband is not a problem Cousin Hannah can solve with campaigns or mountains. It is weather. It is the atmospheric condition of the house. The warrior tradition can win battles and cannot change weather.

This is not a failure of Cousin Hannah. It is the AND Philosophy's most honest assessment of what Act Three can and cannot do. It can change laws. It can break invisible chains. It cannot unmake a love or move someone from a bed where they have chosen, by slow degrees, to remain. Cousin Hannah is the novel's proof that the most valiant heart can fight its losing battle against shadows and still not win. She is dead. Her rage is still in the prisms. She is still fighting. This is what the warrior woman tradition actually looks like from the inside: not triumph but persistence, not arrival but continuation, still married to the movement, still shaking the house, still there.
