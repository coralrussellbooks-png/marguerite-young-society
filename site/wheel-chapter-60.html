Chapter 60

On Chapter 60 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of The Wheel and the Spokes.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Research notes: Chapter 60 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 60

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The chapter opens with a foundational question that it never answers: is Mr. Spitzer a musician or a gambler? But crucially, the text does not treat this as a mystery to be solved. The question is posed and re-posed, and both identities are developed with equal weight, equal interiority, equal validity.

"If he was a musician as he thought... if he was a gambler, on the other hand, if he was not a musician but had merely deceived himself..."

The grammatical structure, the endless conditional "if", is itself the philosophical statement. The self is not one thing awaiting discovery. It is multiple possibilities held simultaneously, irresolvably. The musician and the gambler are not competing hypotheses. They are co-present selves, each generating its own world of imagery: the harpsichord and the ace of clubs, the fugue and the horse race, Mozart and the moose call.

Furthermore, Mr. Spitzer is persistently doubled against his dead brother, who appears as a kind of shadow self: the gambler to his musician, the wild duck caller to his harpsichordist. The brother is not separate from Mr. Spitzer; he is another layer of the same plural identity, the self that might have been, the self that was in another version of the same life.

"Sound shared the silence, but his dead brother could not have imagined the silence."

The self here is not singular and bounded. It is a field of overlapping, simultaneous, irresolvable identities. This is not presented as tragedy. It is presented as the condition of being.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter is saturated with the question of whether Mr. Spitzer's perceptions are real or constructed. The moose encounter and the wild duck encounter are the central examples. In both cases, what begins as an accidental sound, a noise made without intention, is received as real by the animal, and then the animal's response makes the illusion real for Mr. Spitzer.

"Mr. Spitzer had seen the great antlers of four-foot spread and enormous tines looming like tree boughs through the blowing purple fog..."

The text notes explicitly: "as when the auditory hallucination ushers in the visual", a direct acknowledgment that perception constructs reality rather than simply receiving it. The moose is real because the call was made. The ducks are real because he called them. The line between illusion and reality is not a stable border but a threshold that is crossed by the act of attention itself.

The mandarin ducks are reconstructed entirely from fragments:

"he reconstructed from these fragments the memory, even as a dead man might build the world, providing that which had been missing"

This is Pillar 2 stated almost directly: reality is not found, it is built from fragments, and the builder is always partially dead, partially absent, always working from incomplete evidence. The comparison to a dead man building the world is one of the most precise articulations of constructivist ontology imaginable.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The elegiac tone is not incidental to this chapter; it is its climate. The word "elegy" appears explicitly:

he "had almost decided that it would be unnecessary to write his elegy now. For surely, his elegy was this wild duck’s horn."

But the grief here is not about any single loss. It accumulates across the chapter: the lost musical career, the dead brother, the forgotten fugues, the forgotten scores, the horse races on a dead man's shirt, the horses' heads on tombs, the marble eyeballs. Loss does not arrive and depart. It sediments.

The chapter's syntax enacts this. The sentences do not resolve. They extend. They absorb more and more material (the gambling, the music, the moose, the ducks, the obituary columns, the Stock Exchange) without arriving at a conclusion. Grief here is not an event but a grammatical condition, the condition of the sentence that cannot end because ending would mean a resolution that does not exist.

"had never read the obituary columns listing himself as one who had gone over to that death which levels all, leveling the jockey with the horse, leveling the greatest king as if he were some poor old tramp..."

Even death is absorbed into the accumulation rather than standing as a terminal point. Death levels, but it does not conclude. The grief continues past it.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrative stance of this chapter is precisely that of the Witness. The text does not judge Mr. Spitzer. It does not resolve his identity. It does not tell us whether he is deluded or perceptive, musician or gambler, sane or mad. It stays in the room with him through the full complexity of his experience.

The moose encounter could be read as comic. The duck encounter could be read as pathological. The text refuses both readings. It holds the experience in full seriousness:

"Perhaps he was a man already metamorphosed into wings — tremblingly, standing still — hearing those great wings roar past him, soaring, billowing, ebbing — not so much past him as through him..."

This is not mockery and not simple celebration. It is witness. The attention is complete and non-simplifying. The refusal to conclude: is he mad? is he mystical? is he a musician? is he a gambler?: is not evasion. It is the ethical act of the Witness: to see without flinching, without rushing toward resolution.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The chapter's structure is itself recursive. It returns again and again to the same questions (musician or gambler, music or track) each time with slightly different material, slightly different imagery, building not toward an answer but toward a richer, denser version of the question.

"Yet sometimes, being absent-minded because he was troubled by so many tremulous thoughts like an unfinished fugue running through his mind, like that music he had never written or could not even remember now, for he had forgotten many fugues, many antiphonies, many winding horns..."

The unfinished fugue is the perfect metaphor for recursive construction. A fugue returns to its subject, transforms it, develops it, but the fugue Mr. Spitzer carries is one he cannot remember and never completed. He builds it anyway, in the act of living, in the act of calling to animals he did not mean to call, in the act of reconstructing mandarin ducks from fragments of light. The building is provisional. It is never finished. That is not presented as failure.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter is, formally, a single sentence, or something very close to one. The syntax refuses the period. It refuses conclusion. The demand for completion is resisted at every level: thematic, grammatical, philosophical.

"the music like a path dividing sound from chaos, sound being an intellectual thing, sound moving like the human spirit through heavens of imagined silence"

Music itself is defined here not as a completed thing but as a path: ongoing, directional, never arrived. The honest life, by this logic, is the ongoing sentence. The chapter does not end so much as pause, and even its final note, "his elegy was this wild duck's horn", is an identification of the elegy with something wild, migratory, and passing rather than with a fixed monument.

Unfinished Is Honest is also visible in the treatment of Mr. Spitzer's career. He is an authority on instruments he may never have played, a musician who may never have written music, a gambler who may never have won. The incompletion is not shameful. It is the honest record of a life lived in the conditional tense.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Mr. Spitzer is marginal in multiple registers simultaneously. He occupies no clear professional identity (musician? gambler?), no clear social position (he has never been "in the carriage trade", never sat "in a diamond horseshoe or a silk-lined stall" ), no clear perceptual status (hallucinator? mystic? ordinary man on a street?).

But crucially, his marginality is not presented as deficiency. It is presented as the site where the constructed nature of identity becomes most visible. Because he is neither fully musician nor fully gambler, the text can examine both categories from the outside, revealing them as constructions rather than natural kinds.

The dead brother functions similarly; he is marginal to life itself, existing only in Mr. Spitzer's reconstruction of him, and yet he is more vivid in some ways than the living. The marginal figure (the dead, the amateur, the man who calls moose in empty streets) is not the exception. He is the evidence that categories are made, not found.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This pillar may be the most structurally central to the chapter. Time here does not move forward. It accumulates. The chapter layers geological strata of reference: horse races, the dead brother's shirt, old Locomobiles circa 1912, the Pleiades, golden shekels and drachmas, the Stock Exchange, marble tombs, the El. These are not arranged chronologically. They press up through the text simultaneously, like sediment that has never settled.

"the hieroglyphics of old horse races his brother had worn upon his shirt front, the hunched dwarf jockeys, the horses' heads upon the tombs, the marble eyeballs in the marble clouds"

The movement from horse races to shirt fronts to tombs to marble is a movement through sedimentary time: the present race, the worn record of past races, the tomb that preserves the image of the horse, the marble that outlasts the flesh. The past is not behind Mr. Spitzer. It is beneath him, pressing up.

The dead brother is the most powerful expression of this. He is gone but not past. He is beneath the present, still generating meaning, still being reconstructed, still answering when Mr. Spitzer makes the duck call without knowing he has made it.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The prose of this chapter is hyper-aware of its own constructedness. The long conditional sentences ("if he was a musician... if he was a gambler...") do not pretend to speak from a neutral position of knowledge. They name their own uncertainty. They locate themselves inside the problem they are examining.

The lists (of currencies (shekels, talents, florins, drachmas), of newspaper sections (financial news, obituary columns, sporting page), of instruments (harpsichord, viola da gamba, lute, tortoise-shell violin)) are not neutral catalogues. They are constructions of a world, and their excess reveals the constructedness. No neutral observer would list golden shekels alongside Boston dollars. The list itself is a made thing, shaped by a particular consciousness located in a particular place.

"the music of the Stock Exchange like a long surf blowing now in his ears"

This metaphor, the Stock Exchange as surf, does not describe reality. It constructs a reality in which financial abstraction and natural force are the same phenomenon. Language here is not a vehicle. It is the event.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter lives entirely within the ordinary world, but it is a world that has been opened by attention. The mandarin ducks are the clearest example:

"beautiful Asiatics gleaming like the dappled light cast by a dying dolphin, ducks with crested heads and veiled gold eyes as near as pools or a thousand city lights and gold-tipped wings and pale filaments drifting and blue under-feathers streaming"

This is transcendent language, but it describes ducks on a city street. The transcendence is not upward, toward a beyond. It is inward and downward, into the full reality of the thing itself. The dying dolphin's dappled light is not a symbol of something else. It is the thing. The sacred lives in the specificity of the gold-tipped wings, the blue under-feathers, the veiled gold eyes.

Similarly, the moose in the purple fog is not a symbol. It is a moose. But it is a moose that has been called into being by an accidental call, and it arrives with the force of revelation. The immanent transcendence is not found by leaving the world. It is found by accidentally calling to it in an empty street and hearing it answer.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This pillar finds its most explicit expression in the lighthouse image near the chapter's end:

"he being like the burning of the lighthouse on the distant rock as the wild sea billowed around him, and they being like those poor birds of passage who, with their eyes blinded by the light, are dashed to pieces against a lighthouse looming through the fog, for they have mistaken the lighthouse for the light, the reflection of a star. They are dashed to pieces against a star."

Mr. Spitzer here is not a self moving through time. He is a crossing point: a fixed light around which the migratory, the living, the beautiful, the transient break themselves. He is constituted by what has accumulated in him (the dead brother, the forgotten fugues, the lost career, the sedimentary past) and he is constituting something for those who pass through him (the ducks reconstruct their flight path by him; the moose answers his call; the wild duck's horn becomes his elegy).

The obligation is not chosen. It is structural. He did not choose to be a lighthouse. He stood still, and the world organized itself around his stillness. The ancestors (the dead brother, the old gamblers, the marble horses) are in the sediment beneath him. The carrying continues in the form of an elegy that is not a written thing but a living sound: the wild duck's horn, still moving, still calling, still answering.
