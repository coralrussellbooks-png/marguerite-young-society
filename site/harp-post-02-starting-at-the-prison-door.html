Chapter 2. Starting at the Prison Door

How Harp Song for a Radical begins at the end, and why Young built it that way

Most biographies begin with a birth. Marguerite Young's biography of Eugene Victor Debs begins with a prison cell. Its first sentence says Debs "took the long leap to the Ultima Thule of prison," and for the next several pages he is an old man in convict's clothes, jailed by a president who promised peace. The birth in Terre Haute does not arrive until page 184.

This is the first of Young's large choices, and it shapes everything after it. The reader of Harp Song knows from the start how the story ends: in a cell, in 1920, with Debs running for president from the Atlanta Penitentiary. Suspense about the outcome is gone. What remains is the slower question of how the ground under that cell was laid, layer by layer.

The end, first

The opening chapter (pages 3 to 11) is set mostly during the First World War. Woodrow Wilson has sent Debs to prison under the Espionage Act for speaking against the war. Young treats Wilson's reasoning as a piece of logic that appears solid because it is repeated. Wilson, she writes, "believed that in order to have universal peace the way must be prepared by universal war" (3). She does not argue with the sentence. She sets it down and lets it sound as strange as it is.

This is the principle of Reality Is Constructed at work. War fever, patriotism, and the law that jailed Debs all present themselves as facts of nature. Young keeps showing the seams. She passes along a joke of the period about the secretary of war, Newton D. Baker: the office needed not a man named Baker but "a man named Butcher" (10). She calls the president "wood-headed Woody Wilson" (4). The puns are not decoration. They are how she refuses the official language of the moment.

She also goes further than a historian usually would. "Indeed, it may be that Wilson wanted Debs, with his smashed utopian dreams, to suffer as he suffered," she writes (5), picturing Wilson's face twitching as he staggers after his own dream of world peace. She offers this only as a "may be." It is speculation, and she marks it as speculation. It still says something about her method: the jailer and the jailed are both utopians, and both are breaking.

The uncounted votes

The most moving pages of the opening concern the 1920 election. Debs ran from inside the Atlanta Penitentiary, "old and puny gentleman who might never live to be released" (5), and won his prison district easily. The convicts' votes could not legally be counted. Young writes that Debs remembered them anyway as "the most precious straws that had been cast" (5), and that he laughed about it with tears running down his cheeks.

Here The Margins Reveal the Truth becomes literal. The men with no legal voice at all gave Debs his most unanimous victory. The ballot box, one of the country's most trusted inventions, turns out to have a floor beneath it where people vote and nothing is recorded.

Young does not let this become a tidy triumph. In the same pages she writes that the old Debs, in his cell, "experienced in the darkness of the prison cell, where he wore a convict's garb, the sense of despair that had always been the twin of hope" (6). Then she widens it: "Hope and despair were two of the most famous twins of the nineteenth century" (6). This is Grief Doesn't End. Young will not present Debs as a saint who never doubted. He had, she says, a "comic sense which must sustain a larger tragic sense than most people know" (5). The laughter and the tears arrive together.

The first spoke: Tom Mooney

Before the opening chapter ends, Young leaves Debs entirely for several pages to tell the life of Tom Mooney. This is the first of the book's many long detours, and it shows how the book works.

Mooney was a labor organizer convicted of the 1916 Preparedness Day bombing in San Francisco, on evidence that included perjured testimony. Young goes back to his father, a Knight of Labor and coal miner in Indiana, shot in the leg by a strikebreaker, who later died of miner's lung. She follows Tom as a boy sorting rags beside his widowed mother in a Holyoke paper mill, then as an iron molder working stripped to the waist so that no shirt would catch the sparks. She finds him standing before a Rembrandt in Rotterdam, where a stranger starts talking to him about socialism. He once said, "I suppose the urge to serve the labor movement was born in me" (9).

Mooney rode Debs's campaign train, the Red Special, in 1908, and asked what he could do for the cause. Debs, perhaps seeing something hawklike in him, answered, "Go and read more books" (10). Young describes Mooney as a man who saw everything in black and white, and who was "always right by being left" (10).

Mooney matters here as Debs's shadow. He is another son of labor, jailed by wartime panic, with the Haymarket executions of 1887 hanging behind his case. Debs, as Young draws him, is a crowd of selves, gentle and stubborn at once. Mooney is one self, fixed and certain. Placing them side by side is Not One Self principle in action. The book's hero is defined partly by the man he told to go and read.

Ruas describes Young's structure as "spokes radiating from a center." Here that structure becomes visible for the first time. The center is the prison. Mooney is the first spoke.

The poet who died the same day

Chapter 2 opens on a coincidence that Young handles with great care. On the same day as the San Francisco bombing, July 22, 1916, James Whitcomb Riley died in Indianapolis. Riley was the Hoosier poet of "Little Orphant Annie" and "When the Frost Is on the Punkin," and a longtime friend and drinking companion of Debs.

Young sets the bomb and the poet's death on the same page, violence beside nostalgia. The country was grieving a writer of lost childhood on the very day it was stepping toward war. At the center of Riley's work was Little Bud, the imaginary boy who was really Riley himself. Young notes that Bud, "having never been born, could never die" (11).

Old and ill, isolated as friends deserted him for the war, Debs wrote an elegy for Riley as part of his collection "Pastels of Men." Young could have let the elegy stand on its own. Instead she keeps the real man in view beside the sentimental one. Riley carried popcorn in one pocket for Little Bud, "but in the other pocket was something for Pa," and it was a bottle. She recalls that he had once worked the Wizard Oil medicine wagon, reciting poems about little red hens and then selling bottles of opiate cure-alls to country crowds. Debs's elegy leaves these things out. Young does not.

This is Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act at work. Young stays in the room with the whole of Riley, the sweet and the shabby together, and does not rush to wrap him up.

Then she gives Debs a question that could stand as the motto for the book. Considering what was real and what was unreal, Debs asked who could say that "the child world of the Little Bench-Legged Poet was not after all the real world" (16). Young, who said all her writing was about "the recognition that there is no single reality," lets the question stand without an answer.

She calls Debs and Riley "both Don Quixotes in quest of the golden world of happiness and harmony and peace" (16). They never agreed on politics. Riley claimed he voted only once in his life, meaning to vote Republican, and his hand slipped. Yet Young sees them walking the same road. Riley looked for paradise backward, in childhood. Debs looked for it forward, in a transformed society. Riley's death, she writes, seemed to many to mark the death of "that American innocence which, of course, had never been unless in dreams" (16). The phrase "of course" carries a whole philosophy. The golden age never existed, and Young says so in two words.

A childhood without documents

Chapter 3 finally turns toward Debs's own childhood, and Young begins it with a confession. What socialist children learned of his early years came from Henry Schnitkind's popular biography, which borrowed from the old stories of mother and child, "for which there was no documentation" (17).

Young then tells those stories anyway, and marks them clearly as legend. Debs's mother, Marguerite, whom the children called Daisy, explains that the dew is God's tears, wiped on a handkerchief of grass, and that "Earth is the marble God spins" (17). When the small boy first hears of a baby's death, he stamps his foot and scolds God. When he hears the night express, Daisy tells him the sparks flying up are "golden bees flying out of a beehive" (17). The locomotive fireman he will become is already in the scene.

This is Language Shapes What's Possible handled honestly. Every biography of a famous person grows a myth around the childhood, because the real record is thin. Young does not pass the myth off as fact, and she does not throw it away either. She hands it over labeled, and trusts the reader to hold both at once: this is not what happened, and it is part of who Debs became. That is Unfinished Is Honest in practice. She marks the gap instead of filling it.

What the opening teaches

By page 18, Young is ready to go back to 1848, to Colmar in Alsace, where Debs's father fell in love with a mill girl. The opening chapters have set out everything the reader needs to read the rest of the book.

The first lesson is The Past Doesn't Go Away. Young starts at the top layer, the prison in 1920, and then digs. Mooney's father's strike, the Haymarket hangings, Riley's lost Indiana, and Daisy's stories all lie beneath the cell. Nothing in Debs's end was inevitable, and Young's digging puts back in view the people who built each layer.

The second lesson is that Young's detours are the argument. Mooney, Riley, and the myth of childhood are each a way of seeing Debs from the side. A reader who wants only Debs will be frustrated for five hundred pages. A reader who accepts the crowd will find that Debs is inside all of it.

Checking the record

Young's history is often right in surprising places. Riley did die on July 22, 1916, the same day as the San Francisco bombing, so that pairing is fact and not invention. John Swinton's 1894 account Striking for Life, which she cites, is real, and Mooney did ride the Red Special in 1908.

Several claims need correcting or flagging:

  • Mooney's time on death row. Young writes that Mooney spent "more than two decades on death row." His death sentence was commuted to life imprisonment in 1918, after Wilson asked California's governor to intervene. Mooney spent about twenty-two years in prison, not on death row, and was pardoned in 1939.

  • Ingersoll as "Speaker of the House." Robert Ingersoll appears as "Republican Speaker of the House" (12). Ingersoll never held that office. He was famous as a Republican campaign speaker, and the capital letters may be an editor's or a typist's error that changed the meaning. It is a small example of how other hands shaped this book.

  • Wilson and Riley. The scene of Wilson weeping over Little Bud and dancing a jig as Little Orphant Annie comes without a source. It is best read as legend, the kind Young loved to repeat.
