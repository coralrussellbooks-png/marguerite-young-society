Inviting the Muses, Part 2

Part 2 covers the rest of "Old James," the end of the Stories section, and the whole of the first essay, "The Midwest of Everywhere."

"Old James" (Kenyon Review, 1944)

The story turns out to be about authorship as much as about its title character. Old James, an Indianapolis stockbroker, is dictating his autobiography to his secretary, Miss Greenwood, and she quietly supplies whatever he leaves out. She adds "the portrait of Jesus Christ with the woolly lamb in his arms" to a church scene "which seemed rather too hurried in its realism," and he never objects to "these surreptitious additions to his life." He leaves the chronology for her to sort out and, when she looks bored, tells her to "add the details yourself." The pages end up read back to him "as if they were legal and sworn to before witnesses," which echoes the narrator of "My Grandmother's Foot," whose invented grandmother is also somehow sworn to.

His life, as it comes out in pieces, is a string of abandoned selves: a newspaperman who knew the streets and "a few domestic tragedies," a long-haired poet and "rival to James Whitcomb Riley," then a banker "almost overnight" after marrying a rich woman who "abhorred the poet in him." He apologizes for both poetry and banking. The nostalgia is lush and specific (crab apple orchards under the post office, a poultry house that was once a Greek Orthodox church, "a narrow angel, transparent as light"), and Young lets it be beautiful while making clear what it costs. He makes more money during the Depression than in normal times by buying up stocks that widows bring in "like so many soap coupons." He writes a law to protect property owners that collapses over the meaning of "emergency" and "act of God." He orders a thousand Chinese elms to plant a forest, and parasites eat them. He writes epitaphs for his club friends while "Hitler's armies were already gathering." Miss Greenwood keeps those files too, but "she filled in no blank spaces here," declining to add what she knows about men like Pierre Smith-Allen, who "thought more of his horses than of the girls at his glove factory."

The real center is Miss Greenwood. "Part of it was her story," Young writes. She is the one who notices the living pigeons, the skaters, the colts "splotched with their first golden hairs, like the impressions of maple leaves." The salesmen blame her for his "aberrations," he never notices her "faded dress, her tired look," and she is never paid for the extra work. When she finally leaves for Chicago with "a pasteboard box and a bag of oranges," she writes him about "riots in steel mills," her first private thought, and he answers with a form-letter acknowledgment. The blank on his side stays blank.

His death is handled with dry comedy: "Some say Hitler killed him, but others say it was just old time catching up with him." At the funeral, nobody remembers anything he said. Miss Greenwood, too poor to attend, "filled in this last blank space" by imagining it. The ending moves into present tense and asks the question the whole story has been building toward. "His left hand knew of the wild geese flying. It knew not of bombers over China." She cannot say how responsible he was "for the world's disaster," since she was "herself a collaborator." Published in 1944, that word carries its full wartime weight. The story indicts nostalgia as a way of not seeing, while admitting that the person who helps write the fantasy is implicated too.

"The Midwest of Everywhere" (Mademoiselle, 1945)

The essay opens with a thesis that is also a joke: "The Middle West is probably a fanatic state of mind." Young refuses the region as plain, flat, or ordinary. She is "sick of" the Andy Hardy picture and agrees with Sinclair Lewis's Main Street "only from his particular perspective." To critics who say the Midwest has no Main Stream, she replies that it has none "because it is oceanic," touching every shore. Her Midwest includes Xanadu, Byzantium, and "twelve unseen moons shining over the planet Jupiter."

The method is a catalogue of marvels, each told as plain fact: a dead whale in a boxcar in an Indiana cornfield, a dwarf driving Percherons, a sow at the wheel of a stalled Ford with fourteen piglets, a man who died in his sleep and was seen the next day mowing the grass and mentioning "his daughter's eczema." There is a village of old people deafened by malaria, which they pronounce so lovingly "you would think it was Miranda," and another where spirits like Pocahontas and Lincoln can be consulted for twenty-five cents. From New Harmony she passes along Charlie Chaffin's report of a nine-foot hoot owl that "made a noise like a flat tire while flying and had a bell around its neck," and his children named Thoughtless and Imadaisy, who do not exist.

That last detail is the key. Much of what she describes is absent or invisible: the children who aren't in the county, the old doctor's Chevrolet that cannot be seen though it is parked between a hearse and a milk truck, the doctor himself rising "out of his deathbed, as it were, to go deliver imaginary babies." The region's truest life, for her, is the unseen one. She calls herself "a good Heraclitean and lover of the flux" and hopes "the lower elements will never be got rid of," against a hostess who is proud of cleaning them out.

Underneath the comedy is the same darkness as the stories. Mark Twain "felt the coldness of the universe," and Riley "drank to forget the children," which sits sharply beside Old James, the former Riley rival, who insists he never drank in a dark room to forget the people he wrote about. She defines the Midwest finally as "a wild, hazardous, romantic experience of the individual and human character in juxtaposition with the coldness of the universe." The last line widens it to "the Egypt of Indiana, the Indiana of Egypt, the Russia of everywhere, the human soul."

One passing phrase dates the essay: the spirit-consultation signs are compared to fishing worms sold "in Negro shanty towns along a flower-infested railroad track," tossed off as local color in a way that sits uneasily beside her praise for the overlooked.

Across part 2

The two pieces read almost as a pair. "Old James" shows a man whose sentimental past blinds him to the present and to the woman beside him, while the essay argues for a vision that sees the marvelous in the ordinary without looking away from the cold. Both are built on blank spaces and what fills them, and both keep the war just offstage, in bombers over China and in the question of who collaborates in the world's disaster.
