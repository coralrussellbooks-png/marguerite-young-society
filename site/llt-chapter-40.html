Chapter 40

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: Not One Self · Unfinished Is Honest · The Past Doesn't Go Away

The Questions

Cousin Hannah, dead, speaks “the languages of the dead,” and Catherine understands them “without conscious effort.” What communication happens beneath words?

Catherine has “a splendid proficiency as to that which she did not know.” How is not-knowing a proficiency?

Catherine is “many beautiful, volatile Psyches wandering about.” Where has the reader met wandering psyches before?

If the dead commingle with the living, what has happened to the line between the gone and the here?

Cousin Hannah, now among the dead, speaks “the languages of the dead,” and Catherine understands them “without the slightest conscious effort.” The chapter is pointing beneath language as the book has used it. If words build worlds, they also fail at their seam, as the book has already said; here it names what happens past the seam, a communication that does not run on the noun-grammar at all, an understanding that arrives without the words that would construct it. The dead do not speak in the built language of the living, and Catherine hears them anyway, which suggests a knowing that precedes or exceeds construction, intuition rather than grammar, the thing the paltry language was always moving toward and never reaching.

Her strangest gift is “a splendid proficiency as to that which she did not know.” This is not a paradox to be smoothed away but Unfinished Is Honest turned into a mode of knowing. To be proficient in what one does not know is to hold the unfinished skillfully, to dwell in the open question without collapsing it into an answer, and to find that the dwelling itself is a kind of competence. The book has praised the unfinished as the truthful shape; here it goes further and calls the unfinished a proficiency, a thing one can be good at, and Catherine, who knows almost nothing accurately, is good at exactly this: the held-open unknown.

Her many selves return as “wandering Psyches,” and the phrase reaches back across the book. Mr. Spitzer’s butterflies were wandering psyches too, souls in transit seeking reunion; now Catherine is “many beautiful, volatile Psyches wandering about,” the self as a scatter of souls rather than one. Not One Self and the butterfly-soul meet here, in the image of the psyche as something plural and in motion, never housed in one body, never arrived. What flew through the butterfly chapters as an insect walks through this one as Catherine’s own multiplicity: the soul is not one and it does not stay put.

And the dead commingle with the living without a line between them. Cousin Hannah has “passed over into the realm of the dream,” but the realm is not elsewhere; she moves through Catherine’s present as freely as anyone, the gone and the here mingled in one room. This is The Past Doesn't Go Away at its most complete, not the past pressing up as weight but the dead simply present, conversant, understood. The line the living draw between themselves and the dead is one more construction, and in Catherine’s unsealed perception it does not hold: the dead are here, speaking their languages, and she answers.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Catherine’s understanding of “the languages of the dead” as mere hallucination, and the reading that takes her “proficiency as to that which she did not know” as nonsense or empty paradox. Neither the symptom nor the contradiction meets what the chapter offers.

The footing is that there is a communication beneath the built language, an understanding that arrives without the grammar that would construct it, and Catherine’s intuitive hearing of the dead is the book pointing past the seam where words fail. Proficiency in what one does not know is Unfinished Is Honest made into a mode of knowing, the held-open unknown as a competence rather than a lack. The self is a scatter of wandering psyches, the butterfly-soul and the plural self meeting in one image. And the line between the living and the dead is a construction that Catherine’s unsealed perception does not hold. Not-knowing can be a proficiency, and understanding can run beneath language. The dead go on speaking and Catherine goes on answering, and the chapter does not close the realm it has opened.

For Reflection

What understanding has one had that arrived beneath or before the words for it?

Can one be proficient at not-knowing, skilled at holding a question open?

Where is the line one draws between the dead and the living, and how firm is it?
