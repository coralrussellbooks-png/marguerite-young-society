Inviting the Muses, Part 8

← Part 7 · Inviting the Muses: A Reading, Part by Part · Part 9 →

Part 8 finishes the Woolf and Porter review and covers four more reviews: Dorothy Caruso's memoir of her husband, Broch's The Death of Virgil, a large poetry roundup called "Our Person Our World," and the first half of "The Anarchic Principle," a fiction roundup led by Alex Comfort. Nearly all of it dates from 1945, the last year of the war, and the war runs under every piece.

"Fictions Mystical and Epical," conclusion (Kenyon Review, 1945)

Young's verdict on Katherine Anne Porter sets her against Woolf. Porter's great theme is "the continuity of the individual's experience," and she "rejects the artificiality of chaos, even as an abstraction." She is an intuitionist who believes "life and its values are not fictions." Young borrows Aquinas's argument that a universe with both angels and stones is better than one with angels alone, and delivers a fine summation: "Miss Porter has realized the angel at the border, but she has chosen the phantasmagoria of stone." Porter "does not see life itself as a hallucination," and is "the unpretentious critic of a defective material, human nature."

Young clearly stands closer to Woolf, yet she honors Porter fully. Her last point is that Porter's stories, though carefully shaped, seem to be "fragments of some larger, though absent whole," which gives them "an epic state of mind." That is the reverse of Young's own practice, a single epic built of fragments, and she recognizes the kinship.

Enrico Caruso (New York Times Book Review, 1945)

A warm, generous review of Dorothy Caruso's memoir, written twenty-three years after the tenor's death. Young's central claim is that the book, though "not a work of art," approaches that stature because its subject does. Caruso becomes "an autonomous character, a literary one," and she repeats a line from "Complex Life": "Perhaps, in this case, God must be considered the greatest writer, for here the facts are more fabulous than any average fiction."

The quoted letters in broken English are the heart of it, especially Caruso on a train to Denver confessing "a sentiment of fraidness," feeling "like a boy without protection." Young also delights in the plain details: his proudest honor was a medal from the New York Police Department, and his favorite pleasure was cards with an Italian restaurant keeper on Forty-seventh Street. She notes that "a stone-deaf man could enjoy this book," a phrase that looks ahead to "The World of Silence." And she praises the widow as a biographer who "seems not so much a writer as one who takes down in shorthand the speech of her husband, living or dead," never inventing traits he lacked. It is Miss Greenwood's role again, this time filled honestly. The last line: "he was himself the great work of art, the masterpiece."

"A Poet's Last Hours on Earth" (Broch, The Death of Virgil)

Broch's novel was one of the books Young had held up in "Complex Life" as the opposite of Maxwell's metronome, and here she gives it full attention. She stresses that Broch began it "in a Nazi prison, as a private preparation for death," and finished it as an American citizen at Princeton. The book is a deathbed in which Virgil "re-creates the world" in the moment of extinction, and she makes it universal: "We are the only God we know of." Brundisium, the city he is carried through, becomes "a symbol of the mob, wherever the mob coagulates."

Her closest American comparison is Melville, since both writers share "the night which circles in its immense space, the loneliness of man, the impossibility of any simple statement." The book "fits into no pigeonhole," is "not one diamond but a mountain of diamonds," and she hopes it will be "a corrective to our fundamentalism, our departmentalism." That second word appeared in the Midwest essay too; departmentalized knowledge is one of her persistent enemies.

"Our Person Our World" (Partisan Review, 1945)

The roundup covers Allen Tate, Richard Eberhart, Allan Dowling, Charles Butler, Thomas Merton, Robert Frost, Herbert Read, and Conrad Aiken, and it opens with an attack on "glib" reviewers who file every poet under Auden, Yeats, Hopkins, or Eliot. Her larger thesis is that "our age is devoid of one informing myth," but the death of myth comes with "the birth cry of an art which integrates new worldviews." Science keeps moving "from elephant as elephant to elephant as light beams," yet poets need not retreat to dogma, since science "may also have its symbolic uses, just like the old saints who ran after their heads after decapitation." Then, simply: "Imagination does not cease."

Tate gets the most admiring treatment. His centaur descending into hell with a "notched, amazing beard" shows there is no border between the earthly and the transcendent: "Heaven is a beard shaped like a notched arrow!" For Tate, she says, "the search is everything and not the object searched for," which is the lesson of Riley and the old sweetheart in another key. From Eberhart she takes the phrase "the beautiful disrelation of the spiritual," the same phrase she used, in quotation marks, in "The Midwest of Everywhere" the same year. His war poems grasp that "what was our religion is now our long-range artillery."

Her judgments of the others are pointed. Dowling expects agreement about "the word God, for example, and no further exploration necessary." Merton "writes as if he were two men, a Catholic monk and a man of this world," and the worldly one "is the stronger poet"; she adds the striking thought that "the extreme of mysticism is in this case the extreme of skepticism." Frost's A Masque of Reason shows God still "inept," and Frost, having distrusted theory, "ceases thinking about the whole damned subject." Aiken's hopeful ending receives one dry line: "Such a faith cannot be argued with, naturally." She saves her warmth for Herbert Read, "a dignified humanist of the first water," for whom the cosmos depends on "man, the pitiful incident."

"The Anarchic Principle" (first half)

This roundup centers on Alex Comfort's The Power House, a war novel, and Young puts Comfort in a line with Sterne and Joyce as writers who work from "the anarchic principle that reality is given to us only through our sensations." For them, "the best of reality is an opinion" and "the accident is the only absolute." She defends Comfort's crowded detail, the "children's hands like pink rats' claws," against readers who call it extraneous, because in this view reality is made of "minor episodes" rather than "eyeless seraphim."

Her most searching point is about the enemy. In modern war "one never sees his enemy, his enemy being, alas, everywhere," and she dwells on the French officer Vernier dreaming that he is Ritter, the German who spared him. It carries forward the question at the end of "Old James" about who collaborates in the world's disaster. Part 8 then runs briefly through Denton Welch, Feike Feikema, and William Sansom, and breaks off as she reaches Maxwell's The Folded Leaf, calling it "the least pretentious" and "the most baffling." Given her mockery of the same book in "Complex Life" a year later, the next page should be worth reading.

Across part 8

These reviews show that Young's standards are philosophical before they are stylistic. She rewards writers who accept a world without fixed certainty, whether they are skeptics like Tate and Comfort or visionaries like Broch, and she regards the two as closer than they look, since in Merton and in Van Doren alike "the skeptic meets the mystic." She is cool toward anything that settles the question too early, be it Dowling's God, Aiken's staircase of light, or Frost's shrug. And in every piece, 1945 presses in: the Nazi prison, the long-range artillery, the enemy who turns out to be oneself.
