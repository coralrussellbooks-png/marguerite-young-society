Chapter 72

  • Esther is heavily pregnant, with a “bald spot shaped like a large coin” and “long black hair flowing free.”
  • She is described as “plum-colored but square,” with “ponderous red lips” and “lustrous, dull but shining” eyes.
  • Her body is both a source of strength and burden; she experiences morning and evening sickness, swollen breasts, and a deformed foot.
  • Esther is simultaneously robust and vulnerable, embodying vitality and suffering.
  • She is known as the “passive policeman’s only daughter,” indicating a background of isolation and misunderstanding.
  • She broods over her “everlasting pregnancy,” symbolizing continuous loss and hope.
  • Despite her hardships, Esther is compassionate, loving, and hopeful, often talking to herself and her unborn child.
  • Esther’s pregnancy is metaphoric and literal, reflecting repeated stillbirths and lost children.
  • She refers to the “little shadow boxer” inside her, a foetus representing hope, fear, and the unknown.
  • The pregnancy signifies the struggle between creation and destruction, life and death, hope and despair.
  • Salesmen and transient men pass through her life, often fathering her stillborn children.
  • Her parents misunderstand her, seeing her as a failure or beast of burden.
  • She holds a secret inner life that no one fully comprehends, except the “stillborn and the white birds.”
  • Esther experiences loneliness, resilience, and a profound sense of mystery about her condition.
  • She wrestles with feelings of failure and hope, often laughing, crying, and brooding.
  • She fears being discovered by her mother but also finds solace in her secret world, including her lovebirds.
  • “who could have been more robustly alive than she, Esther Longtree”: Esther Longtree emerges as a deeply tragic and complex figure who embodies themes of fertility, loss, unrecognized suffering, and resilience. Physically, she is described as large, swollen, with a deformed foot, a growing bald spot, and marked by signs of repeated pregnancies. Her body is a site of contradiction: robust and vital in some ways, yet burdened by an “everlasting pregnancy” that brings her both strength and sorrow. She is portrayed with vivid imagery (her “ponderous red lips,” “long black hair flowing free,” and swollen breasts like “milky breakers”) highlighting her physicality and her connection to life and creation, even as she suffers repeated stillbirths. Esther’s story is one of repeated loss and unfulfilled motherhood. She has borne many stillborn children, hidden their remains in various natural places, and carries the constant sorrow and haunting memory of these lost lives. Despite this, she harbors a fierce hope and determination to have a living child, the “little shadow boxer” moving inside her, symbolizing persistent life against overwhelming odds. Her repeated pregnancies symbolize both a curse and a source of identity and purpose; she is “always pregnant,” yet always losing the child before birth. This paradoxical state underscores a profound loneliness and alienation from the conventional experiences of womanhood and motherhood. Her relationships with men are fraught and ambiguous. Although she has had many “fathers” of her children (often passing salesmen and transient men) these men are sterile or absent, contributing to her losses and isolation. They remember her and sometimes send tokens or inquiries years later, but their involvement is superficial and transient. Esther’s parents also misunderstand and fail to support her, regarding her as an “old maid” and a failure, unaware of her hidden suffering and the complexity of her condition. Esther also exhibits moments of bright vitality and spirited defiance. She tosses her hair freely “at whatever cost to sanitation,” laughs uproariously despite her pain, and engages in small acts of rebellion such as knitting without thread or playing with lovebirds. These actions underscore her refusal to be wholly defeated by her circumstances, highlighting her strength and courage in face of despair. Psychologically, Esther is haunted by the “little shadow boxer,” a persistent presence and hope that sustains her. She experiences both the physical symptoms of pregnancy and the deep emotional turmoil of loss and expectation. Her mind is described as clear and luminous amid the confusion and sorrow of her body, suggesting a complex inner life where hope and despair coexist. Esther Longtree is a figure of profound maternal tragedy and endurance. She embodies the pain of unrealized potential, the mystery of fertility and loss, and the resilience of the human spirit. Her story is steeped in symbolism: her body as womb and grave, her lost children as ghosts and shadows, her struggle as a metaphor for unfulfilled creation and hope amid desolation.
  • “a braying, sighing sound almost like a mule”: Esther, “the mule woman,” came partly from Marc Chagall’s painting of a pregnant “mule lady” in a bridal veil, which Young “had not seen when I wrote the book,” and partly from Charlie Chaplin. Young remembered a scene in The Gold Rush (1925) in which Charlie invites a beautiful lady to dinner, “instead of that a mule arrives,” and he “entertains the mule as if it were a lady”: “Charlie has been a great inspiration to this book from start to finish” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). No such scene appears in the film as released. Steven Moore recalled that Young would have titled the Esther section “Mule” (Steven Moore, “Remembering Marguerite Young,” 1999, in Dalkey Days).
  • “Esther Longtree, the passive policeman’s only daughter”: The passive policeman was W. C. Fields. Young’s editor, Burroughs Mitchell, told her that his favorite character was “the passive policeman in the graveyard that separated couples,” who reminded him of Fields, and she told him that it was Fields (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983).
  • “buried layettes laid out in long white boxes”: Young’s first poem, written at sixteen, “was about preparing a layette for my unborn fatherless baby,” and “it does become the character of Esther Longtree in Miss Macintosh, My Darling, many decades later.” An English teacher told her “that a young girl should not have such thoughts on her mind” (Marguerite Young, interview, WNYC radio, New York). On a train to Chicago a man told her of a cocker spaniel whose false pregnancy was eased only by giving “her toys to play with as if the baby were really there” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).

Chapter 73

  • Big-boned, slow-moving waitress, cross-eyed, with a deformed foot and swollen womb.
  • Maternal, compassionate, yet burdened by a mysterious, continuous pregnancy with stillborn children.
  • Silent and evasive when questioned by fathers, embodying endurance, sorrow, and resilience.
  • Symbolizes fertility, loss, and the complex intersection of memory and identity.
  • “said Esther Longtree, a big-boned, slow-moving waitress”: Esther Longtree emerges as a profoundly tragic and complex figure burdened by perpetual pregnancy and the sorrow of countless stillborn children. Her character is marked by an overwhelming maternal instinct intertwined with mystery, loneliness, and a haunting sense of loss. Esther is described as a “big, dumb-headed waitress,” slow-moving and physically altered by her condition: one foot is deformed, her womb swollen, and her body constantly expanding with what she perceives as an “everlasting pregnancy.” This physical state symbolizes her ceaseless burden and the weight of her unresolved grief. Her dark hair often drifts before her eyes, symbolizing both a literal and metaphorical veil between her and the world around her, emphasizing her isolation and internal confusion. Despite the numerous pregnancies resulting only in stillborns or incomplete births (“an eye, an ear, a long gold hair, a fingernail, a cobweb”) Esther remains intensely maternal. She has mothered everything from her childhood thumb and big toe to a bullfrog, underscoring her nurturing nature even in the face of continual loss. This maternal impulse is paradoxical; she is both the source of life and a witness to death and absence, which causes her embarrassment and deep sorrow. Many men, termed “fathers,” have impregnated her, yet none seem to have produced a living child. These men are generally weak, sterile, or dying, and several write to Esther as they face death, seeking acknowledgment of their supposed offspring. Esther, however, rarely answers these inquiries. She prefers to maintain silence to preserve the dignity and hope of the dying fathers, fearing the cruelty that truth would impose. This silence also protects her from the pain of confronting the truth about her stillborn children and the uncertain paternity of her current perpetual pregnancy. A significant figure in Esther’s narrative is Joe Goldberg, the champion featherweight boxer, who wrote to her desperately in his dying days. He remembers their encounter in a graveyard: he raped her there, “had taken her against her will,” “deflowered her,” and “had knocked her out, stone cold.” Despite his vivid memories and longing for recognition, Esther cannot recall him clearly, only the graveyard and the circumstances of their meeting. This failure to remember symbolizes her fragmented and overwhelmed state of mind. Joe represents the strongest of the fathers who becomes weak and ephemeral like a feather, and Esther’s inability to anchor him in her memory contrasts sharply with her vivid remembrance of the weaker men. Esther’s narrative is steeped in symbolic imagery: the stillborn children as snowflakes, feathers, cobwebs, and shadows; the old mule representing sterility and loneliness; the graveyard setting for the conception of Joe Goldberg’s child; and the recurring motif of silence as both protection and sorrow. Her “everlasting pregnancy” could be read as a metaphor for enduring grief, unfulfilled potential, and the human condition of bearing loss silently. Her story is riddled with ambiguity about reality versus memory, truth versus illusion, and life versus death. Esther is socially isolated, working alone in a restaurant with fewer and fewer customers, burdened by her physical state and the mystery of her pregnancies. There is an undercurrent of potential accusation against her, from a Chicago detective who suspects her in a forgotten murder of stillborn children to the threat of a public trial. This looming social judgment contrasts with Esther’s own self-view as a suffering, unselfish mother who has never demanded support or recognition from the fathers. Despite her suffering and the enormity of her loss, Esther exhibits resilience. She continues “waxing, waxing, never waning,” embodying a strange, almost supernatural endurance. Yet there is also resignation and confusion: she cannot fully grasp her own condition, cannot remember many fathers, and exists in a liminal space between hope and despair, life and death. Esther Longtree is a figure of tragic maternal devotion, loss, and mystery. Her physical and emotional existence is defined by an endless cycle of pregnancy and stillbirth, a heavy silence toward the men who impregnated her, and a haunting ambiguity about what is real and remembered. Her story explores themes of fertility, death, memory, and the human tendency to shield others from harsh truths through silence. She stands as a poignant embodiment of suffering and endurance.

Chapter 74

  • The protagonist, a woman who is perpetually pregnant yet burdened with loss and mystery.
  • She is introspective, lonely, and caught between hope and despair.
  • She fears legal prosecution and societal judgment but is also fiercely protective of her own narrative and secrets.
  • “Esther Longtree had gone on with her expectations”: Esther Longtree in this chapter is portrayed as a profoundly tragic and complex figure, embodying themes of loss, mystery, maternal suffering, and societal judgment. Her character is marked by an overwhelming burden of perpetual pregnancy and stillbirths, an existence both physically and emotionally laden with pain and unanswered questions. Esther’s situation is unique and unprecedented in the legal realm, as she faces the threat of arrest and trial for the disappearance or death of her children: children who may never have been born or who remain unseen by others. She is caught in a web of suspicion and gossip, misunderstood and condemned by her community and the justice system, yet she remains deeply isolated, bearing her sorrow silently and without defense. This isolation is compounded by her physical condition (repeatedly pregnant yet without the relief of childbirth) and by the haunting presence of her absent children, described metaphorically as her “tears” and “stillborn” who haunt her mind and body. Her relationship with the Chicago detective, who is implicated as the father of her lost children, adds layers of complexity and darkness to her narrative. The detective’s repeated appearances (disguised as various characters) are both invasive and inscrutable. Esther’s encounters with him are marked by violence, mystery, and a sense of inevitability: she is raped by him, yet the experience is described with a strange passivity and detachment, as if she were “sleeping in the grass” and indifferent. This rapist-father figure represents a brutal intrusion into her life, one that leaves physical and psychological scars, but also a tangled mystery of conception, conception without life, and lost pregnancies. Esther’s internal world is rich with symbolism and metaphor: the “stillborn” children, the “broken gooseberry bushes,” the “buzzard hanging rounded like a period” all evoke a landscape of death, loss, and unfulfilled potential. Despite her suffering, Esther is radiant and self-sufficient in a strange way, carrying her burden with a stubborn dignity. She is aware of the social judgments (accusations of insanity, immorality, or deceit) but she resists these with a fierce inner knowledge of her own innocence and the mystery of her condition. Her loneliness and estrangement are deeply felt, yet she remains luminous, a figure of both vulnerability and strength. The chapter also explores Esther’s reflections on gender and societal double standards. She contrasts her own experience as a woman burdened with loss and silence to the freedom a man might have in fathering children without consequence or shame. Her pregnancy and stillbirths are not only physical but symbolic of her emotional and existential state: a life marked by “murder of her heart,” repeated disappointments, and unanswered questions about innocence and guilt. Esther Longtree is a character defined by enigmatic suffering, caught between the natural and the supernatural, the legal and the personal. She embodies a tragic maternal figure whose story is one of loss made visible and invisible, a woman burdened by an unexplainable condition and an unresolvable mystery. Her narrative is steeped in poetic imagery and a haunting sense of inevitability and silence, marking her as both victim and enigma within the novel’s broader themes of reality, illusion, and the passage of time.

Chapter 75

  • The protagonist of this chapter; a woman burdened by miscarriages, trauma, and social suspicion, yet clinging to hope and faith.
  • “So I listened to Esther Longtree’s tale of woe”: Esther Longtree in this chapter is portrayed as a deeply tragic and complex figure whose life is marked by sorrow, loss, and an overwhelming burden of maternal suffering. Her narrative is laden with symbolism and a haunting sense of ambiguity between life and death, reality and illusion. Esther is described as a large, swollen woman who is often pregnant or carrying the weight of pregnancies, many of which have ended in miscarriage or stillbirth. She is physically affected by her experiences, with her body growing and swelling “like a face under water” and her heart “roaring” with pain and confusion. This corporeal imagery reflects her emotional turmoil and the heavy burden she carries. Esther is haunted by the memory of her stillborn children, especially a particular “dead baby” who recurs in her dreams and visions. This child appears as a ghostly, mutable figure: sometimes a mud baby, sometimes a star, sometimes walking and whistling like a policeman (her father). This spectral child embodies Esther’s unresolved grief and the mysterious, painful legacy of her reproductive history. The child’s presence symbolizes the inescapable past that “spread its fingers” into her present, confusing her maternal identity and her sense of reality. Esther’s narrative is fraught with uncertainty about the paternity of her children, including both the living and the dead. She suspects her father, the night policeman, might be the father of one child, which adds a disturbing layer of incestuous implication and deepens her psychological conflict. She also grapples with the menace of a detective who raped her and later threatens her with accusations of murder regarding her lost children. Esther’s sense of guilt and fear is compounded by this external pressure, making her situation even more tragic and isolating. Despite the miscarriages and losses, Esther’s yearning for motherhood remains central to her identity. She imagines herself surrounded by children, calling to them, feeling on the verge of “something big.” Yet, she is tormented by doubts, wondering if what she feels is not motherhood but a “benign tumor,” a metaphor for something unnatural or pathological growing inside her. This tension between hope and despair, life and death, defines her tragic longing. Esther is a stubborn, “mulish” woman who resists explanation or confession. She keeps her pain hidden, crying aloud only when she is sure no one is near. She is wary of judgment and the legal consequences she might face, especially from the detective’s accusations. Her isolation is both physical and psychological, as she navigates a world that neither fully understands nor sympathizes with her. The chapter uses rich, poetic imagery to convey Esther’s inner life: the “quail rustling among the purple shadows,” the “marble children,” the “funeral wreath,” and the “mud baby” all evoke the blending of life, death, memory, and dream. The river motif, with the stillborn children thrown into its current, suggests the flow of time and the attempt to wash away pain and guilt, though ultimately unsuccessfully. Esther’s story reflects broader themes of female suffering, reproductive trauma, and social marginalization. Her experiences are not merely personal but speak to the harsh realities faced by many women, especially those who are poor, vulnerable, and victimized. Her connection to various men (the detective, the policeman father, other “dying fathers”) and her ambiguous reproductive history also hint at societal failures and abuses. Esther Longtree is a profoundly tragic character embodying the painful complexities of motherhood, loss, and survival. She is caught between hope and despair, reality and dream, the past and an uncertain future, her life shadowed by death and the haunting presence of lost children. Her narrative is a poignant exploration of grief, guilt, and the human desire for connection and meaning amid suffering.

  • “Poor Esther Longtree, who might be killed by little Johnny-jump-up”: Esther Longtree emerges in this chapter as a deeply tragic and complex figure, embodying themes of fertility, loss, sorrow, and marginalization. Her character is portrayed with a rich, symbolic density that reveals much about her internal struggles and her relationship to the world around her. Esther is described as physically large, often pregnant or swollen, with features like a long nose and crossed eyes, evoking a somewhat grotesque yet poignant image. She is burdened by repeated pregnancies, many of which end in stillbirths or miscarriages. This physical condition mirrors her emotional state: she is exhausted, confused, and isolated, caught in a cycle of hope and despair. Her body and mind are both “clouded” and “indistinct,” reflecting her fragmented sense of self and history. Esther’s experience of pregnancy and motherhood is fraught with sorrow and complexity. These spectral children symbolize her lost potential for motherhood and the deep trauma of repeated loss. However, this attachment is intertwined with guilt and confusion, as she contemplates the nature of her past conceptions and births. Esther fears his investigations, seeing them as threats that could expose her to legal and social punishment. Yet at the same time, she contemplates the possibility of the trial with a strange blend of hope and resignation, almost welcoming the exposure as a form of acknowledgment of her suffering and existence. This ambivalence highlights her isolation and the precariousness of her position as a woman burdened by reproductive tragedy and societal scrutiny. The chapter frequently returns to motifs of birth and death, conception and miscarriage, seen both literally and metaphorically in Esther’s story. She is described as knitting and unravelling a black hood, symbolizing the night and mystery surrounding her existence. Her pregnancies are “clouded,” and her children are often invisible or spectral, linking her personal tragedy to broader cosmic or existential mysteries. Esther’s life is likened to an “empty coffin on the water,” merging images of life and death, presence and absence. This symbolism situates her story within larger themes of loss, fate, and the unknowable forces shaping human experience. Despite her suffering, Esther exhibits a kind of wildness and defiance. She runs, she laughs, she knits, she endures. She is “wilder than people thought,” living a life bounded by routine but also marked by moments of freedom and rebellion. Her laughter in the face of the detective’s threats and the looming trial shows a resilience and complexity that defy simple victimhood. Esther’s narrative is one of survival amid despair, of a woman caught between social condemnation and her own intricate inner world. Esther’s experience is deeply intertwined with natural imagery: autumn leaves, winter bees, snowflakes, mists, rivers, and stars. These elements emphasize the cosmic scale of her suffering and the universality of her predicament. Her children are likened to “snowflakes drifting in eternity” and “robed” children and “old cherubim,” linking personal loss to the eternal cycles of life and death. This connection to nature and the cosmos reinforces the mystical and tragic dimensions of her character. Esther Longtree is a richly drawn figure whose story encapsulates themes of loss, fertility, maternal anguish, societal judgment, and existential mystery. She is at once vulnerable and resilient, tragic and wild, caught in a liminal space between life and death, hope and despair. The chapter paints her not merely as a victim but as a complex woman grappling with profound personal and cosmic questions, embodying the novel’s broader exploration of human suffering and endurance.

Chapter 77

  • The protagonist; a pregnant woman symbolizing loss and mystery.
  • Complex, haunted by past pregnancies and the deaths of children, real or imagined.
  • Her perspective is lyrical, introspective, and fragmented, blending reality and metaphor.
  • “the stillborn mother, stifling her cries”: Esther Longtree in this chapter is portrayed as a deeply tragic and complex figure, embodying sorrow, loss, mystery, and a profound connection to themes of fertility, death, and motherhood. Esther is described as a large, pregnant waitress, often weighed down not only by her physical condition but by the immense emotional burden of repeated stillbirths and lost children she carries within her memory and body. Her pregnancy is symbolic, described as “watery” and “wild,” with her body “pregnant with death,” indicating a continuous cycle of creation and loss intertwined in her existence. She is caught between hope and despair, weighted down “by the death of a small thing,” her body swollen but also fragile, her spinal column curved by the weight of memory. Esther represents the paradox of fertility and sterility simultaneously. Though she is repeatedly pregnant, the children are stillborn or lost, rendering her motherhood incomplete and haunted by absence. This condition makes her a symbol of the “Song of Songs of the sterility which is the creation,” where creation is intertwined with loss and death. Her pregnancy is not just physical but metaphysical, involving the vastness of the universe, stars, and the earth, all of which seem to be “killed” or destroyed, mirroring her personal tragedy. Her mental state is marked by suspicion, confusion, and a profound sense of mystery. She contemplates who might have “killed the heavens,” the earth, and love itself, reflecting a cosmic grief that parallels her personal losses. She imagines a detective who is both a pursuer and a murderer, embodying the threat of exposure and judgment, while the children she once had or never had remain elusive, “the child that never was.” Esther’s relationship with the detective is complex: she suspects him of having raped her and leaving her for dead, yet he is also the one who threatens to uncover her secret sorrows. Her narrative includes a haunting sense of being pursued and judged unfairly, and she is both victim and accuser in her own story. The chapter also touches on racial issues through the detective’s selective interest in “white” deaths while neglecting “black” ones, revealing societal biases and injustices. Esther, being associated with the “quiet, black mystery,” is marginalized and ignored by official justice, highlighting themes of racial invisibility and systemic neglect. Esther is haunted by old murders, tragedies, and lost children, both personal and historical. She reflects on a mass murderer, abortionist and other cases of maternal and infant death, drawing parallels to her own suffering and guilt. These old stories serve as a backdrop to her present torment and sense of isolation, deepening the theme of a past that refuses to be forgotten. Despite her suffering, Esther shows moments of defiant acceptance and even joy, laughing in her sleep and finding some beauty in the natural world and her own strange, wild condition. She is stubborn, proud, and insists on her innocence in the face of accusation. Her condition is “no dream” but a persistent reality, and she prefers the mystery and silence of her pain to exposure or judgment. Esther Longtree is a figure of immense sorrow and mystery, embodying the tragic intersection of life and death, fertility and sterility, hope and despair. She is pregnant with loss, carrying both physical and metaphysical burdens, haunted by memories, accusations, and societal neglect, especially in the face of racial injustice. Her story is a poignant, symbolic exploration of motherhood, tragedy, and the human condition.

Chapter 78

  • She was called “Little Jackass” due to her stubborn, awkward nature and her difficult birth. She was born kicking and fighting against life itself, a symbol of resistance and struggle. Her childhood was lonely, marked by accidents, physical scars, and emotional neglect. She made friends of “her natural enemies, the woodpeckers, the bluejays.”
  • Esther wrestles with her identity, sometimes wondering if she is the “shadow,” the lesser, the one who should not have lived. She is caught between the life she has and the ghost of the twin she lost, a metaphor for the conflict between existence and non-existence, presence and absence.
  • A complex figure embodying strength, stubbornness, and vulnerability. She is deeply affected by her family’s past and her own mysterious shadow, the lost twin. Her physical awkwardness and emotional turmoil underscore her unique resilience and tragic circumstances.
  • “she had been called by one name, Little Jackass”: Esther Longtree is portrayed in this chapter as a deeply tragic and complex character, burdened by loneliness, loss, and a profound sense of guilt that shapes her entire existence. Esther is introduced as a “mulish,” stubborn, and awkward individual, nicknamed “Little Jackass” because she was born kicking and resisting life from the very beginning. This obstinacy is both physical, shown in her accidents and bruises, and emotional, manifesting in her defiance against her circumstances and her family dynamics. Despite her struggles, Esther tries hard to embrace life, described poetically as a red rose “blooming in the heart of death” or a “white piano in a tinkling stream,” symbolizing her vitality and resilience amid suffering. Her family background is marked by estrangement and emotional coldness. Her father, a passive, slow, and timid policeman with a sad, isolated life, contrasts with Esther’s fiery nature. He patrols the graveyard to warn off lovers rather than arrest them, embodying a gentle but resigned presence. Esther inherits her strength and naturalness from her father’s lineage, which includes Irish, Indian, and possibly Negro ancestry, known for their robust fertility and natural births. In contrast, her mother is tiny, childish, emotionally cold, and narrow-hearted, representing a limiting and oppressive force in Esther’s life. A central element of Esther’s tragic identity is the burden of a lost twin, Rosemary, who was stillborn before Esther’s birth. Esther’s mother accuses her repeatedly, laughing and crying, that Esther “killed” Rosemary by kicking her out of the womb, condemning Esther as a murderer even before she was born. This accusation haunts Esther throughout her life, coloring her self-perception with guilt and shame. She grows up overshadowed by the memory of Rosemary, an idealized “perfect” child who would have been obedient, delicate, and beloved, the daughter her mother longed for. Esther, by contrast, is awkward, large, and defiant, and constantly reminded of her failure to be the child her mother wanted. Esther’s relationship with motherhood and fertility is deeply fraught. She is often pregnant or swollen but has lost many children, described metaphorically as “little birds,” “snowflakes,” or “stillborn,” which reflects the repeated cycle of hope and grief she endures. She carries a secret sorrow for these lost children, which she can never openly mourn or explain, and she battles both physical and emotional pain, including an enlarged heart and a crippled foot, which further symbolize her suffering. Her internal world is haunted by shadows, rustlings, and the presence of the lost children, hinting at a mystical or spectral dimension to her grief. Esther digs holes in the earth, searching for remnants of the past, the “shadow” of her twin, and the lost children, but never fully finds peace or closure. She experiences a profound alienation, sometimes doubting her own reality and identity, wondering if she herself is the shadow or the lesser existence, while Rosemary is the “real” one who should have lived. Despite all this, Esther is not without moments of fierce joy and strength. Her laughter can be loud and powerful, shaking the snow from clouds, and she fiercely protects and claims love for the children she does carry, even if they do not survive. Her stubbornness and vitality make her a figure of tragic endurance, struggling against the “nameless grave” and the weight of familial expectations and curses. Esther Longtree is a figure of intense contradiction: strong yet vulnerable, life-affirming yet haunted by death, natural yet marked by unnatural loss. Her story in this chapter reveals themes of maternal grief, the burden of inherited trauma, and the search for identity amid family secrets and personal suffering. Her character embodies the tragic complexity of fertility and loss, the interplay of life and death, and the painful legacies passed down through generations.

  • “Esther Longtree was illusive”: Esther Longtree is a deeply tragic and complex figure, embodying themes of unnatural conception, loneliness, maternal suffering, and existential isolation. Her characterization is rich with symbolism and psychological depth, revealing a woman who is both physically imposing and emotionally vulnerable. Esther is described as a large, muscular, wind-burned woman with dark skin and crossed eyes that reflect a dazzling inner light where there is no actual light. Her body is often swollen due to pregnancy, yet she carries a profound sadness and a sense of being out of place in the world. She is mulish, stubborn, and somewhat vulgar, traits that stem from her difficult origins and emotional turmoil. Despite her toughness, her heart is described as “big and leaking,” full of regrets, expectations, and memories, highlighting her emotional complexity and capacity for deep feeling. Esther’s origins are central to her character. She was conceived in a way that defies the natural order, her parents never consummated their marriage physically, and instead, a third person, an “old concubine,” using a silver spoon, facilitated her conception across a great emotional and physical distance between her parents. This unusual conception symbolizes the emotional barrenness and alienation in her family. Her parents’ marriage is described as arid and loveless, lacking the fundamental union of flesh and soul. Esther’s conception is painful, slow, and unnatural, taking seven years and involving this mysterious third woman who serves as a go-between. The figure of the “other woman,” the old concubine who carries the silver spoon and physically intervenes in the conception, is a haunting presence in Esther’s story. This woman is physically and symbolically powerful and described as large, dark, and foreign, with masculine traits. She embodies the harsh reality and cruelty of Esther’s conception and birth. She is portrayed as both cruel and strangely beautiful in an inner, invisible way, representing an essential but unsettling force in Esther’s life and origin. Esther was born kicking and afraid, a difficult birth requiring instruments. She is the only one to survive; her twin, Rosemary, was stillborn. This loss haunts Esther, who is burdened by her mother’s guilt and neglect, and by a sense of having caused harm. She carries the memory and presence of Rosemary as a kind of shadow or ghost, which deepens her loneliness and feeling of being an unnatural offspring. Esther’s relationship with her parents is fraught with pain and misunderstanding. Her mother is distant, delicate, and childlike herself (never truly matured or fulfilled) and she often blames Esther for the darkness and barrenness of their world. Her father is large, passive, and somewhat detached, calling Esther “Little Jackass,” a nickname that reflects both affection and cruelty. Both parents are emotionally unavailable, and Esther is left to navigate the world as an awkward, oversized child of a loveless union. Esther stands as a symbol of isolation born of a union that never was, a child who bridges eternity and life but belongs fully to neither. She is “cloud-like,” “illusive,” and driven to the edge of existence, a creature shaped by absence and distance. Her physical largeness contrasts with the emotional barrenness around her, making her a living paradox and a figure of existential struggle. Despite her tragic origins and the neglect she suffers, Esther has a “maternal heart” and a deep desire for love and motherhood. Yet she repeatedly experiences loss (stillbirths, shadows, unborn children) and carries the burden of these losses within her. She is a woman perpetually pregnant with doubtfulness and sorrow, embodying both fertility and barrenness simultaneously. On her seventh birthday, Esther and her mother undertake a difficult journey to visit the “other woman,” the go-between who had helped conceive Esther. This journey is symbolic, marked by hardship, loss of clothing, and the harshness of the natural and social world. The visit reveals the death and decay underlying Esther’s origin: the go-between is dying, blind, and stained by darkness, and Esther perceives her as a kind of paternal figure despite her appearance and gender. Esther is caught between life and death, reality and shadow, natural and unnatural. Her death is “gradual,” tied to her continuous experience of loss and alienation. She tries to be good despite the hatred and misunderstanding she faces, and she is marked by a profound loneliness that aligns her with the natural world’s shadows and ghosts. Her character embodies the tragic consequences of emotional and physical disconnection within a family and society. Esther Longtree in this chapter is a richly drawn figure representing the painful intersection of unnatural conception, family dysfunction, loneliness, maternal longing, and existential grief. She is a living paradox: large yet vulnerable, natural yet unnatural, maternal yet burdened by loss. Her story is woven with symbolism and mythic resonance.

Chapter 80

  • Esther is complex, embodying resilience, confusion, sorrow, and hope.
  • She is aware of her societal marginalization and her own perceived "madness" but embraces her unique perspective.
  • Her character oscillates between despair and defiance, as she laughs at her barrenness and mourns her losses simultaneously.
  • Her first-born child Walter who both haunts and defines her. He symbolizes her greatest loss and source of emotional torment.
  • Walter’s Father is a black man whose identity and fate are shrouded in mystery and racial tension.
  • Many transient men appear in her life, symbolizing fleeting connections and failed attempts at establishing lasting family bonds.
