To All My Darlings

Chapter 6

On Chapter 6 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 30, 2026

← Chapter 5 · Home · Chapter 7 →

Summary

The bus passes broken gates, fallen pillars, and a derelict windmill in a flooded field. Madge wakes and her story emerges. She is five months pregnant, older than Homer, and worked as a telephone operator and receptionist at a pottery. She married Homer to give her child a father, though she never names the real father; she speaks of “that old Adam,” and the chapter hints darkly at her own father (“the child of the dead father”). Homer, half asleep, dreams of his high school sweetheart, Jacqueline White, who is dying of tuberculosis. Madge accuses him of loving her still, and he denies it. Death runs through both families. Madge’s mother died giving birth to her. Years later her father mistook Madge for her dead mother, and she drove him out into a winter storm; he fell through the ice of the river and drowned. Homer’s father killed himself when he learned his wife was pregnant. Madge expects to die in childbirth. The chapter ends with her painting her lips in a darkened mirror and finding she has painted two mouths.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with vivid imagery of a desolate, dreamlike landscape: the thin tree branches, withered yellow leaves, broken gates, fallen pillars, and a derelict windmill turning furiously in the wind. This sets the tone of decay and melancholy and introduces the motif of a journey that is both physical and spiritual, with a bus driver, Moses, asleep at the wheel, symbolizing a loss of control and the blurring of reality and dream. The pregnant girl, Madge, is crying in her sleep, embodying sorrow and foreshadowing tragedy.
  • Madge is described as caught in a terrible trap. She is pregnant, married, yet betrayed and coming to an early grave. Her face is contorted with rage and sorrow, and she is haunted by an “old rival” gnawing at her heart, at once the unborn child and her other self, and later Jacqueline White, who has taken her place in her husband’s affections. This passage is crucial as it reveals her internal conflict, her sense of loss of self, and the inevitability of her fate as she struggles with her fragmented identity and the horrific loneliness of her condition.
  • The narrative delves deeply into Madge’s feelings about her recent marriage, which she perceives as empty and ghostly, greater in tragedy than her death or pregnancy. The husband is a young, oblivious football player, Homer, who does not love her and dreams only of another girl, Jackie White, the real object of his affection. Madge’s fierce jealousy and despair about this love triangle, which she calls “eternal,” drive much of the emotional intensity in the chapter. The passage also highlights the husband’s detachment and the theme of betrayal.
  • Madge’s voice is sharp and accusatory as she confronts her husband, Homer, calling him a beast and a numbskull who does not understand her suffering. She struggles with the injustice of bearing a child that is not his and the loss of her independence and identity. This passage is important for the raw emotional outpouring and the depiction of gender dynamics and suffering in marriage.
  • Here, Madge contemplates the futility of marriage, the absence of love, and the inevitable death that awaits. She speaks about the lack of a real wedding, the absence of proof or ceremony, and the symbolic meaning of her torn wedding veil and lost ring. The passage also includes reflections on death as birth, the blurring of life and death, and the haunting presence of ghosts in their marriage. The theme of duality and the merging of identities is emphasized, with a sense that Madge is losing herself completely.
  • Madge’s jealousy of Jackie White, the other woman who occupies the husband’s thoughts and dreams, is a central focus here. This “eternal triangle” is described as a source of endless torment. Madge accuses her husband of calling out Jacqueline’s name in his sleep and warns of the impossibility of peace between them. This section deepens the theme of fragmented identity, unfulfilled love, and the haunting nature of past and present relationships.
  • The dialogue between Madge and Homer shifts to reflections on death, youth, and memory. The husband clings to hope and the belief in eternal youth and rebirth, while Madge is grounded in grim reality and the inevitability of decay and loss. The mention of tuberculosis, the fading of Jacqueline, and the metaphor of sporting events emphasize the transitory nature of life and love. This passage contrasts hope and despair.
  • The chapter concludes with Madge’s acceptance of her fate, her loneliness, and her impending death. She mourns her lost youth and identity, questions the meaning of her suffering, and acknowledges the tragic triangle that defines her existence. Homer’s detachment and inability to fully grasp her pain are underscored, and Madge’s voice becomes a mournful, almost prophetic lament. The imagery of the old hunter, the dogs, and the toll-keeper reinforce the inevitability of death and the passage to the beyond.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • Madge asks, “Where were her two brides? One should be clothed, but one should be naked.” Who are the two brides?
  • The chapter ends with Madge’s “two mouths” and “two hearts.” How has the chapter prepared for that image?
  • Madge’s hat carries Brooklyn Bridge, ferris wheels, and a stuffed bird “always in agreement with the dual-minded wearer of the hat.” What does the hat say about her?

Characters

  • Homer says Jacqueline “was all of us, and she was none of us. We elected her to be immortal.” Why do the town’s men need her to stay young?
  • Madge says she married Homer “only to make him a man in the eyes of this world.” Who rescued whom?
  • Madge’s father mistook her for her dead mother, and Homer’s father killed himself when his mother became pregnant. How do the fathers shape this marriage?

Connections

  • In Chapter 5 Moses is “my mother’s son and not my mother’s daughter,” and here Madge would “wear a tuxedo and a high silk hat.” How does the novel keep crossing the line between men and women?
  • Madge dreams of “the great potter” and “the great glass-blower.” How do clay and glass describe what people are made of?
  • Miss Pebbles hears dead lovers quarreling in Chapter 5, and Madge hears “voices I hear even when I am asleep.” How does the bus collect the voices of the dead?

Reflection

  • “There is no time for man, but there is time for woman.” Is Madge right?
  • Madge says, “It’s easier to be married to the dead.” Why is a memory harder to compete with than a living rival?
  • Homer will “always look on the sunny side,” and Madge sees only the dark. Which of them sees more truly?

Research: Characters

Madge Capehorn (Edwards)

  • Central figure, struggling with her pregnancy, marriage, and identity.
  • She is haunted by memories and fears, feeling trapped and betrayed.
  • Experiences deep psychological conflict, torn between her past self and her present reality.
  • Symbolizes fragility, loss of innocence, and the burden of unwanted responsibility.
  • Her sleep is restless, and she cries in her dreams, revealing her subconscious anguish.
  • She is both alive and metaphorically dead, caught between states.

Homer Capehorn

  • Physically strong but emotionally absent and oblivious to Madge’s suffering.
  • Described as sleeping at her side, dreaming of himself rather than her.
  • Represents failed masculinity and emotional neglect.
  • He is not the father of Madge’s child, and their marriage is hollow and unfulfilling.

Jacqueline (Jackie) White

  • Madge’s rival, the girl her husband truly loves and dreams of.
  • Dying or already dead, symbolizing lost youth and unattainable ideals.
  • Represents the perfect, unattainable love and the ghost of the past haunting the present.
  • Eighteen, the banker’s daughter, class president, cheer leader, and Morality in the senior pageant, now dying of tuberculosis.

Madge’s Father (Mr. Edwards)

  • A ghostly presence who blames Madge for her mother’s death.
  • His death and absence symbolize broken family ties and unresolved guilt.
  • A drunken fisherman who drowned under the river ice after Madge drove him out.

Adam

  • Madge says, “That old Adam never tempted me . . . and I never tempted him.” The old Adam is the Bible’s name for fallen human nature, and whether Adam is a real man is never made clear.

Homer’s Father

  • He shot himself when his wife became pregnant. Homer hears him in the old hunter’s horn and the barking dogs, and in the North wind.

Homer’s Mother

  • A widow who never liked Madge. Madge dreads giving birth in her house under the sampler “Remember Thy Creator in the Days of Thy Youth.”

Research: Themes

Life, Death, and Rebirth

See: Death and Mortality · Motherhood, Pregnancy, and Birth

  • The narrative blurs the lines between living and dead, birth and death.
  • Madge’s pregnancy is a metaphor for birth and death intertwined; she “dies” in giving birth.
  • Death is portrayed as a complex continuation rather than an end.

Identity and Duality

See: Identity and the Self · Duality and Doubles

  • Madge struggles with her identity, feeling split between her past self, her present, and the “other” face she must become.
  • The motif of doubles: Madge vs. Jacqueline, life vs. death, awake vs. asleep.
  • Madge’s painted face and changing appearances symbolize the fluidity and fragility of identity.

Betrayal and Loneliness

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Madge feels betrayed by her husband, who loves another.
  • She is isolated emotionally and physically, carrying all burdens alone.
  • Loneliness is amplified by the absence of family, friends, and societal support.

Memory and Forgetting

See: Memory and the Past

  • The tension between remembering and forgetting is prominent.
  • Madge tries to hold onto memories but is haunted by the past she cannot fully grasp.
  • Memory is unreliable, fragmented, and painful.

Marriage and Gender Roles

See: Women, Gender, and Marriage

  • Marriage is depicted as a hollow, oppressive institution.
  • The double standard of fidelity and suffering between men and women is critiqued.
  • Madge’s role as a wife and mother is tragic rather than fulfilling.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

The Bus and the Road

See: The Bus and the Journey

  • The journey on the bus symbolizes the passage of life, the inescapable path Madge must follow.
  • The driver’s drunken sleep parallels Madge’s own unconscious navigation through life and fate.

The Stuffed Bird on Madge’s Hat

See: Birds · Clothing, Hats, and Skirts

  • Symbolizes fragile consciousness, the artificiality of appearance, and the precariousness of Madge’s situation.

Death and the Moon

See: Moon and Stars

  • The moon and stars symbolize time, fate, and the inevitability of death.
  • “Dead birds singing” and “falling star” evoke loss and the ephemeral nature of life.

Glass and Clay

  • Glass symbolizes fragility and transparency; Madge is often compared to glass or clay.
  • The potter and glassblower metaphors reflect themes of creation, shaping identity, and vulnerability.

The Wedding and Funeral Imagery

See: Weddings and Funerals

  • Madge’s “wedding” is a funeral; marriage equals death in her experience.
  • The absence of traditional wedding symbols intensifies the sense of loss and unrealized hopes.

Madge’s dress and accessories

See: Clothing, Hats, and Skirts

  • Madge’s dress and accessories are a kaleidoscope of symbols portraying a young woman caught in the painful tension between societal expectations and her grim reality.
  • Her artificial makeup and mismatched finery mask a deeper denial and despair about her pregnancy and future, while the sports motifs and exposed sexuality reflect a conflicted desire for life and normalcy.
  • The decayed and ill-fitting garments evoke the disintegration of her life and identity, and her restless sleep gestures toward her psychological torment and tragic fate.
  • These symbolic details richly underscore the themes of denial, vulnerability, and the tragic loss of innocence in Madge’s character within the novel.

Further notes from the text

  • The longest chapter in the novel opens with the road “turning and turning upon itself,” so that the bus seems to be going back the way it came, a small picture of the whole book’s structure.
  • Madge’s vanity case is decorated with tiny tarnished hands whose red fingernails drip like blood, and she sleeps a narrow, rigid sleep with her ornaments tinkling around her like a belated alarm.
  • Much of the chapter is Madge’s inner monologue about her own body: aching breasts small as withered flowers, last winter’s coat too tight to breathe in, a corset with rusted stays, glass-heeled shoes for a journey through the valley of the shadow of life.
  • Her view of marriage is bitter and exact. She thought she was marrying one man and married another, “this poor shadow of a man,” and he married “this poor shadow of herself.” She calls it a marriage of three, with Jacqueline White, and wonders whether it will become four.
  • Her accusation of Homer turns on time: “There is no time for man, but there is time for woman.” He would rather mourn Jacqueline, she says, because the dead stay forever young.
  • Jacqueline’s decline is told in hard small-town detail: she has lost her job at the saw mill and at the restaurant because of the germs she was spreading, yet she still draws the boys and the old married men “by a thread, a silken cable.”
  • The chapter returns again and again to footprints in snow and frost: the infant’s footprint found in the frost, and Madge’s father’s drunkard’s footprints zigzagging across the frozen river on the night she drove him out into the storm.

Research: Resources

  • “leading the dance in your little glass shoes”: Cinderella’s glass slipper comes from Charles Perrault’s tale (1697), and Madge travels in “glass-heeled shoes.” See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “Should she give birth to a prime minister? Should a flea give birth to a star?”: Horace turned it the other way: “The mountains labor, and a ridiculous mouse is born” (Ars Poetica).
  • “the old-fashioned sulky racers”: Harness racing, with drivers in light two-wheeled sulkies, was a fixture of Midwestern county fairs.
  • “Brooklyn Bridge like a spidery web”: The Brooklyn Bridge opened in 1883, and its steel-wire cables made it the great suspension bridge of its day.
  • “ferris wheels”: George Ferris built the first for the 1893 World’s Columbian Exposition in Chicago.
  • “a great conflict with the angel of death or the angel of life”: Jacob wrestles the angel at Peniel until dawn (Genesis 32:24-30). See The Bible.
  • “her little thoughts running and running about as if they could not stop”: Lucy Ellmann’s novel Ducks, Newburyport (2019) puts into black and white the constant thinking (mental and emotional labor) that many women do throughout the day. Gertrude Stein’s The Making of Americans (written 1903-1911, published 1925) runs nearly a thousand pages of repeating, circling sentences, another book that, like Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, tries to set down the whole of a mind.
  • “no consciousness which was her own and not somebody else’s”: In philosophy, the problem of personal identity is concerned with how one is able to identify a single person over a time interval, dealing with such questions as, “What makes it true that a person at one time is the same thing as a person at another time?” or “What kinds of things are we persons?” John Locke held that personal identity rests on consciousness and memory, the very things Madge lacks. See Empiricism (Locke, Berkeley, Hume).
  • “She was breaking into two, herself and the other, this face unknown”: Young seems to be describing through Madge a form of depersonalization-derealization that is now labeled a disorder and can be brought about by stress or trauma such as rape. See Mirrors and Reflections.
  • “for she died in giving birth”: At the time Young started this book maternal mortality was pervasive for multiple reasons. Around 1900 six to nine women died for every thousand births, and rates stayed highest through 1930. They fell 71% between 1939 and 1948 with the move to hospital births, antibiotics, and safe blood transfusions.
    • Maternal mortality rates were 30-40% higher before Roe v. Wade according to New York State, which saw maternal mortality fall by that much after New York legalized abortion in 1970, three years before Roe v. Wade. Depending on the state, maternal and infant mortality rates are rising due to inconsistent access to abortion. Some states that restrict abortion, rather than face the rise in mortality, have simply stopped reporting or disbanded the councils that were meant to monitor mortality rates in women and infants.
  • “her little wedding cake bridegroom with his polished mustache”: Cake toppers of a tiny bride and groom became fashionable in the United States in the early twentieth century. See Weddings and Funerals.
  • “Where was her little red stag?”: Stag symbolism in general: regeneration, intuition, and gentleness. In Jacobean symbolism a stag symbolizes Christ, nobility, wisdom, and acute hearing and musicality. The stag is a metaphor for the protective but ultimately fragile forces that surround Madge, highlighting her precarious position and the tension between safety and threat in her experience.
  • “Where was her little stalking horse?”: Hunters hid behind a horse, or a screen shaped like one, to creep up on game, and the term came to mean a decoy or pretext.
  • “Where was her little knight with his black plume”: The “knight in shining armor” trope, representing a brave and noble rescuer, originated in medieval literature with characters like King Arthur and his knights. This image of a knight in shining armor, often depicted with polished armor, evolved into a symbol of chivalric ideals like bravery, honor, and gallantry. The term has since expanded to represent any character who acts heroically to save another, even outside of traditional medieval settings. Madge’s knight carries only a “little paper shield and bright paper sword.”
  • “little red-winged blackbirds where the snow was falling”: Blackbirds can symbolize different things depending on the culture: death, change, magic, and mystery. Specifically in Christianity it is a symbol of temptation and carnal pleasure. Blackbirds in the chapter are multifaceted symbols embodying death, the haunting past, sorrow, and the thin veil separating life and death, reinforcing the novel’s pervasive atmosphere of melancholy and existential uncertainty. See Birds.
  • “this circle of creation, all of one piece having no beginning and no end”: The wedding ring has long stood for eternity. It is worn on the fourth finger, where an old belief placed a vein running to the heart. See Weddings and Funerals.
  • “a handful of rice or confetti or white rose petals”: Rice was thrown for fertility, a bride wore “something old” such as her mother’s veil, and the bride who caught the bouquet would be the next to marry. See Weddings and Funerals.
  • “the pale girl dancing herself to death”: In the ballet Giselle (1841) the wilis are the ghosts of brides who died before their weddings and dance men to death. See Music and Composers.
  • “to carry her across the threshold”: The custom goes back to Rome. A bride who stumbled at the door brought bad luck, and Madge “had stumbled on her way to the altar.” See Weddings and Funerals.
  • “dance at the old dance hall, dance at the river saloon”: Dance halls, ballrooms, and dance palaces were social spaces where couples could meet after work. They were common from the 1910s to the 1930s. In taxi-dance halls men paid ten cents a dance to dance with a hostess.
  • “though she had worked in a pottery”: Madge had left the town to work in a pottery or potter town that produced ceramics. In the Midwest through Ohio and Indiana, there was a variety of pottery and stoneware clays with common items made such as washbowls, jugs, and red roof tiles. East Liverpool, Ohio, which called itself the Crockery City of the World, is an example of a “potter town.” See Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest.
  • “her little shoes which danced and danced without her”: In “The Red Shoes” (1845) by Hans Christian Andersen a vain girl is condemned to dance even after she dies. The red shoes represent Madge’s precarious balance between appearance and reality, innocence and corruption, freedom and constraint. They are a vivid symbol of her tragic, conflicted condition as she rides the bus toward an uncertain fate. In the tale the executioner cuts off Karen’s feet, and the red shoes dance away with them, much as Jacqueline dances “on one foot.” See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “She would paint her face”: American beauty standards for women have historically favored a thin, tall, light-skinned, blonde-haired, light-eyed, and youthful appearance. Unrealistic beauty standards can contribute to body image issues, low self-esteem, and mental health problems. Women may feel pressured to conform to beauty standards, leading to unhealthy behaviors like excessive dieting and cosmetic procedures. Beauty standards can be used to discriminate against women based on their physical appearance, leading to job or social disadvantages. Beauty standards are often used by corporations to promote products and services, contributing to a culture of consumption.
    • The Beauty Myth by Naomi Wolf argues that the pursuit of physical beauty is a myth, not a natural or necessary requirement. Wolf contends that this beauty obsession is used as a tool to control and undermine women, diverting them from their goals and priorities. She highlights how the media and advertising industry actively promote and perpetuate the beauty myth, creating a constant pressure to conform. The book explores the negative consequences of the beauty myth, including self-esteem issues, body image problems, and a lack of focus on other important aspects of life. Wolf calls for a reevaluation of societal perceptions of women’s worth, arguing that beauty should not be the primary measure of a woman’s value. The book emphasizes how commercial interests benefit from the beauty myth, driving the sale of beauty products and promoting a consumerist culture. Wolf’s analysis offers a feminist perspective on the societal pressures placed on women and the impact of these pressures on their lives.
  • “she was just a poor skeleton in armor”: Longfellow’s “The Skeleton in Armor” (1841) is told by the ghost of a Viking who carried off his bride.
  • “She dreamed that she was not herself”: Philosophy of self examines the idea of the self at a conceptual level. Most philosophical definitions of self (per Descartes, Locke, Hume, and William James) are expressed in the first person. The particular characteristics of the self determine its identity.
  • “She dreamed that she was a sundial in a ruined garden”: Old sundials carry the motto “I count only the sunny hours.” See Clocks and Watches.
  • “the men whose feet were clay”: In Nebuchadnezzar’s dream the great image has “feet part of iron and part of clay” (Daniel 2:33). See The Bible.
  • “the prettiest telephone operator and receptionist, talking on four wires at once”: Emma Nutt became the first woman telephone operator in 1878, and by the 1920s operators, nearly all women, connected every call by hand at a switchboard.
  • “Never had she been a fisherman of these old human hearts”: “Follow me, and I will make you fishers of men” (Matthew 4:19). See The Bible.
  • “she would have to go down through this dark, thorny valley of this shadow of life”: Psalm 23:4 reads, “Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil.” Madge turns death into life. The phrase encapsulates the novel’s central themes of existential struggle, the tension between illusion and reality, and the painful quest for truth and connection amid the transience and shadows of life. See The Bible.
  • “come to the wedding feast and had stayed for the funeral”: Hamlet reversed: “the funeral baked meats / Did coldly furnish forth the marriage tables” (Hamlet 1.2). See Shakespeare.
  • “no dance programs”: Dance cards listed each dance and the partner promised it.
  • “champion skeet shooter, shooting the clay targets”: Clay pigeons replaced live birds in the 1880s, and skeet was named in 1926. Later Madge says, “Make me a clay pigeon.”
  • “he who would never again wear the laurel wreath”: Victors at Apollo’s Pythian Games wore laurel, and the two-handled loving cup became the sports trophy. See Greek Mythology.
  • “kicked from pillar to post”: The phrase is first found in the 1400s as “from post to pillar.”
  • “She was watching the old town clock very closely now”: Clocks can signify: time, schedules, business, modern man, new beginnings, endings, and the transitory nature of human beings. In Tudor times a clock could mean truth unveiled by time. Clocks in this chapter are not merely instruments measuring time but serve as potent symbols of the instability of human experience, the blending of reality and dream, and the inexorable flow of time that ultimately eludes human control. See Clocks and Watches.
  • “until Death did them part”: The phrase comes from the marriage vows of the Book of Common Prayer (1549), which read “till death us depart”; the wording later became “till death us do part.” The same vows promise “for better for worse.” See Weddings and Funerals.
  • “this sleeping beauty who never dreamed of her”: In Perrault’s and the Grimms’ tale a kiss wakes the sleeping princess. Here the bridegroom sleeps, and Madge says later, “Your kiss will not restore her beauty.” See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “three crescent moons interlinking”: Three interlaced crescents were the emblem of Henri II of France, an allusion to his mistress Diane de Poitiers, in a famous triangle with his queen, Catherine de’ Medici.
  • “he was not shaped by the great potter”: The Great Potter from Isaiah 64:8 in the Bible is an analogy where God is the potter and man is the clay. It is talking about how God molds and shapes people in this life. Jeremiah 18:6 says, “as the clay is in the potter’s hand, so are ye in mine hand.” See The Bible.
  • “She would wear a tuxedo and a high silk hat”: Marlene Dietrich wore a man’s tuxedo and top hat in Morocco (1930).
  • “the fox of her fur collar”: Fox furs, with the head and paws still attached, were high fashion from the 1920s through the 1940s.
  • “wearing this mask to hide her natural sorrow”: Masking in psychology refers to the act of concealing or suppressing one’s true thoughts, feelings, or behaviors to fit in with social norms or expectations. It involves presenting a different persona to the world than the individual’s actual self. Masking can be intentional, where an individual consciously chooses to hide their true self, or unconscious, where it occurs automatically in response to social pressures. Masking can involve hiding negative emotions, such as sadness, anger, or anxiety, to appear more socially acceptable. Individuals may change their body language, facial expressions, or even their interests to conform to societal expectations.
    • Marguerite Young did comment about how all people wear masks. They have at least an inner component and an outer component that is shown to the public.
  • “circles within circles, wheels within wheels, orbs within orbs”: Ezekiel saw “a wheel in the middle of a wheel” (Ezekiel 1:16), and Ptolemy’s astronomy set the planets on circles riding upon circles. See The Bible.
  • “stars should deceive us when they shine no more”: Starlight takes years to reach the earth, so a star might be gone while its light still arrives.
  • “She plucked her already invisible eyebrows”: Pencil-thin brows were the fashion of the 1930s, made famous by Jean Harlow and Marlene Dietrich.
  • “She was like a tired archer. Perhaps she would always miss her target”: The Greek word the New Testament uses for sin, hamartia, first meant missing the mark.
  • “Marriage, what was it but only this awakening which had disillusioned her”: Criticisms of marriage are arguments against the practical or moral value of the institution of marriage or particular forms of matrimony. These have included the effects that marriage has on individual liberty, equality between the sexes, the relationship between marriage and violence, the amount of control a person has over another, the financial risk when measured against alternatives and divorce, and questioning of the necessity to have a relationship sanctioned by government or religious authorities. Feminist activists often point to historical, legal and social inequalities of marriage, family life, and divorce in their criticism of marriage.
  • “it had not been a marriage of true souls”: Shakespeare’s Sonnet 116: “Let me not to the marriage of true minds / Admit impediments. Love is not love / Which alters when it alteration finds.” Later Madge asks whether love can “alter.” See Shakespeare.
  • “She had married him to give a name to the child”: In Young’s day a child born outside marriage was legally “illegitimate,” and the word appeared on many birth records.
  • “Marriage was not the realization of a young girl’s dreams”: This whole imagery about marriage from Madge is counter to every stereotype and idealization about marriage at the time Young started writing this book. Marriage was the dream of every girl, and the only path open to women at that time was marriage, wife, homemaker, and mother.
  • “his face like a death’s-head”: A death’s-head is a skull, a common emblem on gravestones and mourning rings.
  • “marrying when you knew not what you did”: “Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do” (Luke 23:34). See The Bible.
  • “That old Adam never tempted me”: The “old Adam” is fallen human nature (Romans 6:6). Madge reverses Eden, where Eve tempted Adam. See The Bible.
  • “I was the apple highest on the tree, hardest to reach”: Sappho: “As the sweet apple reddens on the high branch, high on the highest, which the apple-pickers forgot; no, not forgot, but could not reach.”
  • “what to show for her mental independence”: Even though Madge considered herself independent and living independently, in the end she was not and had nothing to show for it. This characterizes marriage in that women were not independent and were often handed from their father to their husband. Usually they had to marry for economic security and there were not many professions or choices except to be married and raise children.
  • “could she not hear the flutter of a moth in her ear?”: A moth can symbolize faith, light, vulnerability, sensuality, death, adaptation, divinity and the afterlife, personal growth and transformation. In Jacobean times it represented the return of the soul. The moth in this chapter symbolizes the delicate, transient, and liminal aspects of life, embodying the novel’s deeper meditation on mortality, transformation, and the interplay between light and darkness.
  • “too late for giving in marriage”: “In the resurrection they neither marry, nor are given in marriage, but are as the angels of God in heaven” (Matthew 22:30). See The Bible.
  • “Oh, Romeo, oh, Romeo!”: Juliet’s “O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?” (Romeo and Juliet 2.2). See Shakespeare.
  • “love is blind”: “But love is blind, and lovers cannot see” (The Merchant of Venice 2.6). See Shakespeare.
  • “the dragonflies were spinning their threads above her face”: Dragonfly is a symbol used all over the world to signify change, transformation, and self-realization. The dragonfly is part of the larger symbolic landscape of the novel that includes butterflies, moths, stars, and other natural phenomena representing the flux of life, the interplay of light and darkness, and the mystery of being. It underscores the novel’s exploration of impermanence, the blending of opposites, and the search for meaning amid change and uncertainty.
  • “the old glazier who had told her that she was a glass girl”: Cervantes’s “The Glass Graduate” (1613) is about a man who believes he is made of glass, a delusion recorded by doctors of the time.
  • “Ding, dong, bell”: Ariel’s song for a drowned father in The Tempest: “Full fathom five thy father lies . . . Ding-dong, bell.” Madge’s father drowned. The nursery rhyme “Ding, dong, bell, / Pussy’s in the well” also ends in drowning. See Shakespeare and Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “They say she went to bed one night when she was young and beautiful”: In H. Rider Haggard’s She (1887) the immortal Ayesha withers into old age in a moment and shrinks smaller and smaller.
  • “He was the king of heaven, but woman was the queen of hell”: Persephone, carried off by Hades, became queen of the underworld, and winter came to the earth. “Death was her first lover. Death was her first husband and her last.” See Greek Mythology.
  • “There was no autumn, no Indian summer”: Indian summer, a warm spell after the first frost, is an American term from the 1770s.
  • “Once you will mistake her for the wing of a white pheasant”: The pheasant can symbolize seductiveness, fertility, passion and attraction. The pheasant in the chapter symbolizes an element of natural life and beauty woven into the narrative’s complex symbolism, highlighting the contrasts between order and chaos, the real and the illusory, and the mortal and the eternal.
  • “He told me that all were clay”: In Edward FitzGerald’s Rubáiyát of Omar Khayyám a misshapen pot asks, “What! did the Hand then of the Potter shake?”
  • “the Justice of the Peace to tie the knot”: A justice of the peace could perform a civil marriage without a church or guests. See Weddings and Funerals.
  • “Tell them Papa’s gone a-hunting the rabbits”: Rabbits can symbolize good luck, prosperity, and abundance. The symbol of rabbits in the chapter encompasses innocence, vulnerability, lost youth, and the pervasive presence of death that shadows life in the novel. The nursery rhyme runs, “Bye, baby Bunting, / Daddy’s gone a-hunting, / Gone to get a rabbit skin / To wrap the baby Bunting in.” See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “He was carrying the torch for her”: To carry a torch for someone, to love without return, is an American phrase of the 1920s, which gave its name to the torch song.
  • “the way of a man with a maid”: Proverbs 30:19 names it among four things “too wonderful for me.” See The Bible.
  • “My head is pillowed on a stone”: Jacob slept with a stone for his pillow and dreamed of the ladder to heaven (Genesis 28:11-12). See The Bible.
  • “I’ve had no wink of sleep since the night I married you”: Through these paragraphs Madge has been thinking and talking to herself while Homer continues to sleep beside her. Research shows that women tend to worry and think and not let things go as easily as men do, so they tend to get less sleep.
  • “a tinker’s dam? Oh, who would solder this great hole”: A tinker’s dam was a wall of dough built to hold molten solder and thrown away after use, so it was worthless.
  • “Should the child give birth to the mother?”: Madge cannot find a harmonious transition from who she was to who she has become or is becoming. This is not as unusual as it sounds if one thinks about how life is different before and after having children. For women, since domestic duties still fall mainly on their shoulders, once a child is born it is like a career change and one that is all day, every day for at least the first six to seven years and lasts until the child is an adult. If there is more than one child then that cycle continues as it did for many women whose only goal given by society was to be a wife and mother.
  • “Death was birth, and birth was death”: Schopenhauer’s philosophy views death not as a final annihilation, but as the negation of the individual “will-to-live,” a fundamental force driving existence and causing suffering. He believed that life is inherently a state of suffering, a constant cycle of wanting and never truly being satisfied. Death, for him, is the end of this striving, the cessation of the individual will and a return to the underlying, impersonal Will. He argued that the fear of death is irrational, stemming from the attachment to the will-to-live. Although acknowledging the desire to escape suffering, Schopenhauer rejected suicide as a philosophical solution. He argued that suicide, like the will-to-live, is an act of will, preventing the potential for a more profound liberation through the negation of the will in death. Schopenhauer saw individuality as an illusion, a temporary manifestation of the underlying Will. See Schopenhauer.
  • “Remember Thy Creator in the Days of Thy Youth Ere the Evil Days Come on Thee”: Ecclesiastes 12:1, a favorite verse for girls’ needlework samplers. See Ecclesiastes.
  • “what eyes have not seen, what ears have not heard”: “Eye hath not seen, nor ear heard . . . the things which God hath prepared” (1 Corinthians 2:9). See The Bible.
  • “You think that beauty is as beauty does”: The proverb says a person’s true beauty is in their character and actions, not their physical appearance. The older proverb is “handsome is as handsome does” (1500s). The sentiment is found in the writings of Geoffrey Chaucer in “The Wife of Bath’s Tale” (c. 1387) from The Canterbury Tales. Chaucer’s lines suggest that the most virtuous person, regardless of physical appearance, is the most beautiful. The Greek philosophers Plato and Aristotle linked beauty to virtue, order, and symmetry. This perspective influenced the development of the proverb, suggesting that inner qualities are just as important as outward appearance in determining beauty. See Plato and Platonism.
  • “She was no Venus Adonis, no handsome girl with a broken arm”: The Venus de Milo, found in 1820, has lost her arms. Adonis was the youth Venus loved. See Greek Mythology.
  • “She was no Helen of Troy, no face to launch a thousand ships”: Marlowe’s Doctor Faustus asks, “Was this the face that launched a thousand ships / And burnt the topless towers of Ilium?” The Greeks entered Troy hidden in the wooden horse. See Greek Mythology.
  • “Quit barking up the wrong tree”: The phrase is American, from the 1830s, for a hunting dog barking at a tree its quarry has already left. See Dogs.
  • “the angels do not marry, for they are male and female in one body”: In Plato’s Symposium Aristophanes tells of the first humans, double beings later split in two, each half forever seeking the other. See Plato and Platonism.
  • “love, honor, and obey”: The older prayer book vows had only the bride promise to “obey.” Madge throws the word at Homer. See Weddings and Funerals.
  • “She had lifted her flaming sword”: God set cherubim and “a flaming sword which turned every way” at the gate of Eden (Genesis 3:24). See The Bible.
  • “The needle points to the star”: The compass needle points to the magnetic north, not quite to the North Star.
  • “a dime-store ring of brass”: F. W. Woolworth opened his first five-and-ten in 1879.
  • “those brass rings that are tossed out to the lucky riders at the merry-go-round”: A rider who grabbed the brass ring won a free ride, hence “grab the brass ring.”
  • “Always the bridegroom, I said, never the bride”: Listerine’s 1925 advertisement made famous “Often a bridesmaid but never a bride.”
  • “the little jacks-in-the-pulpit, little Johnny-jump-ups”: Both are wildflowers, and both carry Jackie’s name.
  • “She was little Jackie White who would scarcely remember Madge”: Jackie grew from a sentence Young overheard: “I remember crossing a street in Indianapolis and I heard a man say, ‘Poor Robert, he tried so hard to live long enough to see his sister graduate from high school.’” That “entered into the theme of Jackie and the high school team” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983). She named Jackie, she said, “long, long before the first lady” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See Jacqueline White and Marguerite Young on Her Craft.
  • “this eternal triangle”: The phrase for two lovers and a rival was first recorded in 1907.
  • “that old high school cheer leader”: Cheering began with a man, Johnny Campbell, at the University of Minnesota in 1898. Girls took it over in the 1920s and 1930s.
  • “she was Morality in the high school pageant”: Medieval morality plays such as Everyman cast virtues as characters, and the American pageant movement brought such spectacles to towns and schools in the 1910s.
  • “She was Morality, and I was Honesty”: Homer Capehorn shares the poet’s name. Homer’s works (the Iliad and the Odyssey) present a complex picture of morality and honesty, reflecting the values and norms of ancient Greek society. In the Odyssey, Odysseus frequently uses lies and deception for survival and self-preservation, particularly when disguised. He tailors his falsehoods to specific situations and individuals, demonstrating his cleverness and resourcefulness. While Odysseus’s actions are often portrayed as strategic and necessary for his survival, they can also be viewed as ethically problematic. Despite the prevalence of deception, Homer also portrays honesty as a valuable trait. Odysseus drops his disguise at last to Penelope and to his father, and honesty brings him home.
  • “They were playing taps for the World War soldiers”: General Daniel Butterfield set “Taps” in 1862, and it became the bugle call at military funerals. See Music and Composers.
  • “She was Juliet, and I was Romeo”: In the play Romeo kisses Juliet, believing her dead, and drinks poison before she wakes from the sleeping potion. See Shakespeare and Suicide in History and Literature.
  • “She was the drum majorette”: Majorettes twirling batons began leading high school and college bands in the 1930s.
  • “First in the hearts of her countrymen”: Henry Lee’s eulogy of Washington (1799): “First in war, first in peace, and first in the hearts of his countrymen.”
  • “If you did not know Theobald, how will you know her?”: The Ship of Theseus, also known as Theseus’s Paradox, is a paradox and a common thought experiment about whether an object is the same object after having all of its original components replaced over time, typically one after the other. In Greek mythology, Theseus, the mythical king of the city of Athens, rescued the children of Athens from King Minos after slaying the minotaur and then escaped onto a ship going to Delos. Each year, the Athenians would commemorate this by taking the ship on a pilgrimage to Delos to honor Apollo. A question was raised by ancient philosophers: If no pieces of the original made up the current ship, was it still the Ship of Theseus? Furthermore, if it was no longer the same, when had it ceased existing as the original ship? In contemporary philosophy, the thought experiment has applications to the philosophical study of identity over time. Within the contemporary philosophy of mind, it has inspired a variety of proposed solutions and concepts regarding the persistence of personal identity. The Ship of Theseus metaphor in the chapter underscores the novel’s ongoing exploration of the tension between permanence and change, illusion and reality, and how people understand themselves and the world around them in a mutable existence. See Greek Mythology.
  • “He told me that you will never pluck last summer’s rose”: This may refer to Thomas Moore’s poem “The Last Rose of Summer” (1805, published in Irish Melodies, 1813), which begins, “’Tis the last rose of summer, / Left blooming alone.” The metaphor underscores the futility of trying to recapture or resurrect what time has irrevocably taken, reinforcing the novel’s meditation on the inescapable flow of life and the illusions that both sustain and deceive.
  • “her poodle-dog hair-cut”: The poodle cut, short tight curls, came into fashion in the early 1950s while Young was writing.
  • “She was Abraham Lincoln’s sweetheart and Lincoln’s mother afterwards”: Ann Rutledge, Lincoln’s legendary sweetheart, died young in 1835. His mother, Nancy Hanks Lincoln, died of milk sickness in Indiana in 1818. See Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest.
  • “Jenny Lind”: The “Swedish Nightingale” toured America for P. T. Barnum in 1850-1852. See Music and Composers.
  • “Little Sir Echo! How do you do?”: The children’s song (1917) was a hit again in 1939. In myth the nymph Echo pined for Narcissus until only her voice was left. See Music and Composers and Greek Mythology.
  • “She carried the yellow tuberoses at the commencement”: Tuberoses, heavily fragrant, were favorite flowers at Victorian funerals. See Weddings and Funerals.
  • “To thine own self be true, and it will follow as the day the night”: Polonius gives this advice to his son Laertes in Hamlet (1.3): “This above all: to thine own self be true, / And it must follow, as the night the day, / Thou canst not then be false to any man.” Homer gets it backwards, “as the day the night.” See Shakespeare.
  • “Though the flesh might wither and die, even as the grass”: “All flesh is grass . . . The grass withereth, the flower fadeth” (Isaiah 40:6-8). See The Bible.
  • “Death comes to every wedding feast”: In Holbein’s Dance of Death (1538) a skeleton drums ahead of a newly married couple. See Weddings and Funerals.
  • “Everyone pays the piper”: In the tale of the Pied Piper of Hamelin, the town that would not pay the piper lost its children. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “She had never worn perfume”: Ashes of Roses is a perfume by Bourjois, France, 1909.
  • “Time was no respecter, Madge said, of persons”: “God is no respecter of persons” (Acts 10:34). See The Bible.
  • “it was not the malaria poisoning the air”: Malaria, “bad air,” or ague, plagued the marshy Midwest in the nineteenth century until drainage and quinine drove it out. It was carried by mosquitoes, not the air. See Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest.
  • “the galloping consumption she was spreading around”: Tuberculosis was also called consumption. It was a leading cause of death in the U.S. through the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. It was called the “Great White Plague” because of how pale people became who had it. Robert Koch identified the germ in 1882, which is why Jacqueline loses her jobs for spreading it. Patients were sent to sanatoriums until streptomycin (1944) gave the first cure.
  • “all dressed like British lords in scarlet coats”: Fox-hunters’ red coats are called “hunting pink.” See Dogs.
  • “They say it changes your color before you can say Jack Robinson”: The phrase means a very short amount of time. The phrase first appeared in print in 1778 in Frances Burney’s novel Evelina (“I’d do it as soon as say Jack Robinson”), but probably was in wide use before that time. Another story relates the origin of the phrase to a comic song of the 1840s, written and performed by Tom Hudson, which tells of a sailor who returns from a voyage to discover that his wife has married another sailor in his absence.
  • “Jack Frost who paints the window pane white”: The nursery rhyme runs, “Jack be nimble, Jack be quick, / Jack jump over the candlestick.” Madge’s string of Jacks, Jack Robinson, Jack Frost, little Jack Fleet, Jack White, Jack-in-the-pulpit, and later Homer’s jack-in-the-box, all circle back to Jackie. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “the oldest pinochle player in town”: German immigrants brought pinochle to America, and it filled Midwestern taverns and kitchens. See Cards, Games, and Chance.
  • “Oh, a little birdie told me”: “A bird of the air shall carry the voice” (Ecclesiastes 10:20). See Ecclesiastes.
  • “Love’s the ghostly reaper now. He asks not what flower he reaps”: Longfellow’s “The Reaper and the Flowers” (1839) begins, “There is a Reaper, whose name is Death.”
  • “taking them all over the hill and far away”: “Over the Hills and Far Away” is an old soldiers’ song, sung in The Beggar’s Opera (1728). See Music and Composers.
  • “Death was always the bridegroom”: Death and the Maiden, the skeleton embracing the girl, was a favorite subject of Renaissance painters and of Schubert’s song (1817). See Music and Composers.
  • “Two bodies can’t occupy exactly the same position in space”: Impenetrability is a fundamental property of matter that dictates that two bodies cannot occupy the same space at the same time. This concept is a cornerstone of classical and quantum mechanics, defining how matter interacts and exists in space. In quantum mechanics the Pauli exclusion principle (1925), which helps explain impenetrability, states that two identical fermions cannot occupy the same quantum state (including spatial coordinates, spin, etc.) in a system at the same time. Everyday objects, like a brick and a table, demonstrate impenetrability. A brick cannot sit in the space a table already fills.
  • “One must be a spirit not anywhere in space”: Philosophy of mind is a branch of philosophy that deals with the nature of the mind and its relation to the body and the external world. Dualism and monism are the two central schools of thought on the mind-body problem. Descartes held that the mind takes up no space, and Homer guesses that a spirit must be “outside of space.” See Descartes and Dualism.
  • “If one triangle is laid over another triangle”: Euclid proves triangles equal by laying one upon the other, since “things which coincide with one another are equal.”
  • “You are you, and she is she. I am I, or am I?”: In metaphysics the philosophy of identity is the relation each thing bears only to itself. The notion of identity gives rise to many philosophical problems, including the identity of indiscernibles (if x and y share all their properties, are they one and the same thing?), and questions about change and personal identity over time (what has to be the case for a person x at one time and a person y at a later time to be one and the same person?). The philosophical concept concerns a relation, specifically, a relation that x and y stand in if, and only if they are one and the same thing, or identical to each other (i.e. if, and only if x = y). The law of identity originates from classical antiquity. The modern formulation of identity is that of Gottfried Leibniz, who held that x is the same as y if and only if every predicate true of x is true of y as well. Ludwig Wittgenstein wrote (Tractatus 5.5301): “That identity is not a relation between objects is obvious.” At 5.5303 he elaborates: “Roughly speaking: to say of two things that they are identical is nonsense, and to say of one thing that it is identical with itself is to say nothing.” See Wittgenstein.
  • “The sunny side of the street”: “On the Sunny Side of the Street” (1930) was a Depression-era hit. See Music and Composers.
  • “Not that some pity would not have stirred in Madge’s heart if Jacqueline had been married”: Madge could not pity Jacqueline because she did not settle down and marry so that the other men would have lost interest. This is something women are still addressing. If a woman belongs to a man then she is not “available.” She is someone else’s property and a man respects another man’s property. He controls his woman and sees that she does not reflect badly on him.
  • “The grave gave up its dead as the river gave up its dead”: “And the sea gave up the dead which were in it” (Revelation 20:13). See The Bible.
  • “the drunkard’s walk which was the pattern upon the faded quilt”: The Drunkard’s Path quilt pattern, with curved zigzags, became popular late in the nineteenth century, and some accounts tie it to the temperance movement.
  • “you and the wounded bird. God never dropped anybody”: “Not one sparrow shall fall on the ground without your Father” (Matthew 10:29). See The Bible.
  • “It’s only the lead in my system makes me want to sleep”: Pottery glazes were made with lead, and glaze dippers suffered lead poisoning, with exhaustion, pallor, and drowsiness. Homer met Madge at the pottery (“I hardly remembered you until I saw you at the pottery”), so the lead may be real.
  • “Death is an old hunter. Wait until you hear his winding horn”: In the legend of the Wild Hunt a ghostly huntsman rides through the winter sky with baying hounds, and to hear him meant death. See Fairy Tales and Folklore and Dogs.
  • “My father was the North wind”: In Greek myth the North Wind is Boreas, who carried off the girl Oreithyia to be his bride. See Greek Mythology.
  • “She was painting her lips with a cupid’s bow”: The cupid’s-bow mouth, painted into a sharp double curve, was made famous by the actress Clara Bow in the 1920s. See Mirrors and Reflections.
