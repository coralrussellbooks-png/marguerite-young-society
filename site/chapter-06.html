Summary

The bus passes broken gates, fallen pillars, and a derelict windmill in a flooded field. Madge wakes and her story emerges. She is five months pregnant, older than Homer, and worked as an operator in a pottery town. She married Homer to give her child a father, though the real father is a man named Adam. Homer, half asleep, dreams of his high school sweetheart, Jacqueline White, who is dying of tuberculosis. Madge accuses him of loving her still, and he denies it. Death runs through both families. Madge's mother died giving birth to her. Years later her father mistook Madge for her dead mother, ran into a winter storm, and drowned. Homer's father killed himself when he learned his wife was pregnant. Madge expects to die in childbirth. The chapter ends with her painting her lips in a darkened mirror and finding she has painted two mouths.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with vivid imagery of a desolate, dreamlike landscape: the thin tree branches, withered yellow leaves, broken gates, fallen pillars, and a derelict windmill turning furiously in the wind. This sets the tone of decay and melancholy and introduces the motif of a journey that is both physical and spiritual, with a bus driver, Moses, asleep at the wheel, symbolizing a loss of control and the blurring of reality and dream. The pregnant girl, Madge, is crying in her sleep, embodying sorrow and foreshadowing tragedy.
  • Madge is described as caught in a terrible trap. She is pregnant, married, yet betrayed and coming to an early grave. Her face is contorted with rage and sorrow, and she is haunted by an "old rival" or unknown other self or figure (Jacqueline White) who has taken her place in the husband's affections. This passage is crucial as it reveals her internal conflict, her sense of loss of self, and the inevitability of her fate as she struggles with her fragmented identity and the horrific loneliness of her condition.
  • The narrative delves deeply into Madge’s feelings about her recent marriage, which she perceives as empty and ghostly, greater in tragedy than her death or pregnancy. The husband is a young, oblivious football player, Homer, who does not love her and dreams only of another girl, Jackie White, the real object of his affection. Madge’s fierce jealousy and despair about this love triangle, which she calls "eternal," drive much of the emotional intensity in the chapter. The passage also highlights the husband's detachment and the theme of betrayal.
  • Madge’s voice is sharp and accusatory as she confronts her husband, Homer, calling him a beast and a numbskull who does not understand her suffering. She struggles with the injustice of bearing a child that is not his and the loss of her independence and identity. This passage is important for the raw emotional outpouring and the depiction of gender dynamics and suffering in marriage.
  • Here, Madge contemplates the futility of marriage, the absence of love, and the inevitable death that awaits. She speaks about the lack of a real wedding, the absence of proof or ceremony, and the symbolic meaning of her torn wedding veil and lost ring. The passage also includes reflections on death as birth, the blurring of life and death, and the haunting presence of ghosts in their marriage. The theme of duality and the merging of identities is emphasized, with a sense that Madge is losing herself completely.
  • Madge’s jealousy of Jackie White, the other woman who occupies the husband’s thoughts and dreams, is a central focus here. This "eternal triangle" is described as a source of endless torment. Madge accuses her husband of calling out Jacqueline’s name in his sleep and warns of the impossibility of peace between them. This section deepens the theme of fragmented identity, unfulfilled love, and the haunting nature of past and present relationships.
  • The dialogue between Madge and Homer shifts to reflections on death, youth, and memory. The husband clings to hope and the belief in eternal youth and rebirth, while Madge is grounded in grim reality and the inevitability of decay and loss. The mention of tuberculosis, the fading of Jacqueline, and the metaphor of sporting events emphasize the transitory nature of life and love. This passage contrasts hope and despair.
  • The chapter concludes with Madge’s acceptance of her fate, her loneliness, and her impending death. She mourns her lost youth and identity, questions the meaning of her suffering, and acknowledges the tragic triangle that defines her existence. Homer's detachment and inability to fully grasp her pain are underscored, and Madge’s voice becomes a mournful, almost prophetic lament. The imagery of the old hunter, the dogs, and the toll-keeper reinforce the inevitability of death and the passage to the beyond.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Madge Capehorn (Edwards)

  • Central figure, struggling with her pregnancy, marriage, and identity.
  • She is haunted by memories and fears, feeling trapped and betrayed.
  • Experiences deep psychological conflict, torn between her past self and her present reality.
  • Symbolizes fragility, loss of innocence, and the burden of unwanted responsibility.
  • Her sleep is restless, and she cries in her dreams, revealing her subconscious anguish.
  • She is both alive and metaphorically dead, caught between states.

Homer Capehorn

  • Physically strong but emotionally absent and oblivious to Madge’s suffering.
  • Described as sleeping at her side, dreaming of himself rather than her.
  • Represents failed masculinity and emotional neglect.
  • Not the father of Madge’s child, their marriage is hollow and unfulfilling.

Jacqueline (Jackie) White

  • Madge’s rival, the girl her husband truly loves and dreams of.
  • Dying or already dead, symbolizing lost youth and unattainable ideals.
  • Represents the perfect, unattainable love and the ghost of the past haunting the present.

Madge’s Father (Mr. Edwards)

  • A ghostly presence who blames Madge for her mother’s death.
  • His death and absence symbolize broken family ties and unresolved guilt.

Adam

  • Madge names him as the father of her unborn baby.

Research: Themes

Life, Death, and Rebirth

See: Death and Mortality · Motherhood, Pregnancy, and Birth

  • The narrative blurs the lines between living and dead, birth and death.
  • Madge’s pregnancy is a metaphor for birth and death intertwined; she "dies" in giving birth.
  • Death is portrayed as a complex continuation rather than an end.

Identity and Duality

See: Identity and the Self · Duality and Doubles

  • Madge struggles with her identity, feeling split between her past self, her present, and the “other” face she must become.
  • The motif of doubles: Madge vs. Jacqueline, life vs. death, awake vs. asleep.
  • Madge’s painted face and changing appearances symbolize the fluidity and fragility of identity.

Betrayal and Loneliness

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Madge feels betrayed by her husband, who loves another.
  • She is isolated emotionally and physically, carrying all burdens alone.
  • Loneliness is amplified by the absence of family, friends, and societal support.

Memory and Forgetting

See: Memory and the Past

  • The tension between remembering and forgetting is prominent.
  • Madge tries to hold onto memories but is haunted by the past she cannot fully grasp.
  • Memory is unreliable, fragmented, and painful.

Marriage and Gender Roles

See: Women, Gender, and Marriage

  • Marriage is depicted as a hollow, oppressive institution.
  • The double standard of fidelity and suffering between men and women is critiqued.
  • Madge’s role as a wife and mother is tragic rather than fulfilling.

Further notes from the text

  • The longest chapter in the novel opens with the road “turning and turning upon itself,” so that the bus seems to be going back the way it came, a small picture of the whole book’s structure.
  • Madge’s vanity case is decorated with tiny tarnished hands whose red fingernails drip like blood, and she sleeps a narrow, rigid sleep with her ornaments tinkling around her like a belated alarm.
  • Much of the chapter is Madge’s inner monologue about her own body: aching breasts small as withered flowers, last winter’s coat too tight to breathe in, a corset with rusted stays, glass-heeled shoes for a journey through the valley of the shadow of life.
  • Her view of marriage is bitter and exact. She thought she was marrying one man and married another, “this poor shadow of a man,” and he married “this poor shadow of herself.” She calls it a marriage of three, with Jacqueline White, and wonders whether it will become four.
  • Her accusation of Homer turns on time: “There is no time for man, but there is time for woman.” He would rather mourn Jacqueline, she says, because the dead stay forever young.
  • Jacqueline’s decline is told in hard small-town detail: she has lost her job at the saw mill and at the restaurant because of the germs she was spreading, yet she still draws the boys and the old married men “by a thread, a silken cable.”
  • The chapter returns again and again to footprints in snow and frost: the infant’s footprint found in the frost, and Madge’s father’s drunkard’s footprints zigzagging across the frozen river on the night she drove him out into the storm.

  • Impenetrability is a fundamental property of matter that dictates that two bodies cannot occupy the same space at the same time. This concept is a cornerstone of classical and quantum mechanics, defining how matter interacts and exists in space. Impenetrability, also known as the Pauli exclusion principle, states that two identical fermions cannot occupy the same quantum state (including spatial coordinates, spin, etc.) in a system at the same time. Everyday objects, like a brick and a table, demonstrate impenetrability. You can't simultaneously place a brick on top of a table and have it occupy the same space as the table. In quantum mechanics, impenetrability applies to particles with half-integer spin (fermions) like electrons, protons, and neutrons. It explains phenomena like electron degeneracy pressure in stars, which prevents further collapse after the formation of white dwarf stars. Bosons (particles with integer spin) can occupy the same quantum state, such as photons in a laser, which is why multiple bosons can occupy the same space. Impenetrability is closely related to the idea of space being a physical entity. The fact that two bodies cannot occupy the same space implies that space itself has a definite structure and properties. While impenetrability restricts the spatial location of particles, quantum entanglement describes a correlation between particles, even if they are separated by large distances. These are distinct concepts in quantum mechanics.
  • Schopenhauer's philosophy views death not as a final annihilation, but as the negation of the individual "will-to-live," a fundamental force driving existence and causing suffering. He believed that life is fundamentally a cycle of suffering, and death offers a temporary escape from this cycle. While he acknowledged the fear of death as stemming from the will-to-live, he also argued that it's not a rational fear and that death should be understood as a natural part of a larger, impersonal cycle. Schopenhauer saw the "will-to-live" as the driving force behind all existence, a constant striving for pleasure and avoidance of pain. Death, for him, is the end of this striving, the cessation of the individual will. He believed that life is inherently a state of suffering, a constant cycle of wanting and never truly being satisfied. This suffering is a direct consequence of the will-to-live, which constantly seeks fulfillment. While not advocating for suicide, Schopenhauer suggested that death could be seen as a release from this endless cycle of suffering. It's a cessation of the individual will and a return to the underlying, impersonal Will. He argued that our fear of death is irrational, stemming from our attachment to the will-to-live. True understanding, he believed, would reveal death as a natural part of a larger, impersonal process. Although acknowledging the desire to escape suffering, Schopenhauer rejected suicide as a philosophical solution. He argued that suicide, like the will-to-live, is an act of will, preventing the potential for a more profound liberation through the negation of the will in death. Schopenhauer saw individuality as an illusion, a temporary manifestation of the underlying Will. Death, in his view, does not extinguish the fundamental Will, but rather the individual's temporary form of expression within it.
  • The Ship of Theseus, also known as Theseus's Paradox, is a paradox and a common thought experiment about whether an object is the same object after having all of its original components replaced over time, typically one after the other. In Greek mythology, Theseus, the mythical king of the city of Athens, rescued the children of Athens from King Minos after slaying the minotaur and then escaped onto a ship going to Delos. Each year, the Athenians would commemorate this by taking the ship on a pilgrimage to Delos to honour Apollo. A question was raised by ancient philosophers: If no pieces of the original made up the current ship, was it still the Ship of Theseus? Furthermore, if it was no longer the same, when had it ceased existing as the original ship? In contemporary philosophy, the thought experiment has applications to the philosophical study of identity over time. Within the contemporary philosophy of mind, it has inspired a variety of proposed solutions and concepts regarding the persistence of personal identity. The Ship of Theseus metaphor in the chapter underscores the novel's ongoing exploration of the tension between permanence and change, illusion and reality, and how we understand ourselves and the world around us in a mutable existence.
  • In metaphysics the philosophy of identity is the relation each thing bears only to itself. The notion of identity gives rise to many philosophical problems, including the identity of indiscernibles (if x and y share all their properties, are they one and the same thing?), and questions about change and personal identity over time (what has to be the case for a person x at one time and a person y at a later time to be one and the same person?). The philosophical concept concerns a relation, specifically, a relation that x and y stand in if, and only if they are one and the same thing, or identical to each other (i.e. if, and only if x = y).The law of identity originates from classical antiquity. The modern formulation of identity is that of Gottfried Leibniz, who held that x is the same as y if and only if every predicate true of x is true of y as well.Ludwig Wittgenstein wrote (Tractatus 5.5301): "That identity is not a relation between objects is obvious." At 5.5303 he elaborates: "Roughly speaking: to say of two things that they are identical is nonsense, and to say of one thing that it is identical with itself is to say nothing."
