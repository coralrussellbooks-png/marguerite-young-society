Movement Six: What the Novel Teaches

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, Chapter 68

The Other America in the Novel

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is a book of dreams, and it is easy to read it as if it floated free of its country. It does not. Under the opium and the fog runs the America of its century: the servant no one paid, the porter everyone called George, the lynching, the sundown town, the hotel that would not take Jews or Black guests, the vote that was won for some women and not for others. Young wrote the novel between 1947 and 1965, through the years of the civil rights movement, and it shows. To All My Darlings follows these threads through more than thirty chapters. This essay gathers them.

Young does not preach about them. She lets them sit in the same sentences as the dreams, which is its own kind of judgment. And she is a writer of her time as well as a critic of it, so the book carries some of the very stereotypes it exposes. Both have to be seen.

The Coachman With No Name

The clearest case is the oldest servant in the Cartwheel house. In Chapter 14, Catherine asks what her coachman's name was. “The name, like so many other things, had slipped her mind, a sieve which could not hold the water—but what was a name?” Then: “The black coachman had had no wages for years, even before his death. Money had meant nothing to him.” She remembers him only as “the black coachman with the white, rolling eyes,” and she wants him buried with “the carriage and the four white horses” so that she “might still go driving” after her own death.

Every piece of this is the history of Black domestic labor in America. The rolling eyes are the stock caricature of the minstrel show. The forgotten name, the unpaid years, and the claim that money meant nothing to him are the old excuses of those who kept Black servants unpaid. And even in death he is to go on serving. Then, a few pages later, Catherine's mind slips. She asks Mr. Spitzer about “the carriage and the white coachman and the four black horses, what had become of the color line?” W. E. B. Du Bois had written in 1903, “The problem of the twentieth century is the problem of the color-line” (The Souls of Black Folk). Catherine has turned the coachman white and the horses black, and in her confusion she asks the century's question.

The Men Called George

After the Civil War, the Pullman Company hired Black men, many of them formerly enslaved, as sleeping car porters. They worked long hours for low pay and tips, and passengers called every one of them “George,” after George Pullman, until A. Philip Randolph organized the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters in 1925. The porters move through the novel. In Chapter 19, Miss MacIntosh wears in the mornings “the cap of a sleeping car porter.” In Chapter 26, Catherine's visions include “sleeping car porters with golden roosters on their heads.” And in Chapter 49, when the last passenger pigeon is shipped to Washington, “Poor Martha was relegated to the baggage car, only an old night porter riding with her, and he was black as midnight.” The novel adds, “He was black as the ace of spades, that card of chance which was the world.” The simile was a common racist one. Young turns it toward chance and the world, but she uses it, and the porter, who sits up all night with the dead bird, never gets a name either.

What Happened Every Day in America

In Chapter 68, Mr. Weed, the Christian hangman, tells how his calling began. As a young man in Kentucky he saw “the hanging of a Negro, a very inefficient job, for the Negro was cut down a split second too soon. The Negro was hanged for sleeping with a white woman as he surely never did.” The novel goes on: “He never even smiled at her, the hangman believed, that altogether this was the tale of what never happened and yet happened, happened every day in America.”

That one sentence holds the history. In Southern Horrors: Lynch Law in All Its Phases (1892), Ida B. Wells showed that the charge of rape or of relations with a white woman was the usual excuse for lynching Black men, and was usually false. The Tuskegee Institute recorded 4,743 lynchings in the United States from 1882 to 1968, and 3,446 of the dead were Black. The hangman believes the man was innocent, yet his complaint is only that the job was done badly, and the man suffered: “The Negro must certainly have been aware of the fact that, officially dead, he was unofficially alive.” Young lets the horror show through the hangman's pride in his efficiency.

The White Detective

In Chapter 77, Esther Longtree fears a Chicago detective who solves the deaths of children. “But he only dealt with the deaths of little white children, for the deaths of the little black children were not his business, there being so many and so unimportant.” When he finds a murdered Black girl in the same woods as a white one, “He had stopped short then of solving the mystery, seeing that the death of something black was involved.” And then the phrase the whole essay is about: “As he had to draw the line somewhere in a world of so many deaths, he drew the line between the whiteness and the blackness.”

When Young wrote this, the civil rights movement had been going on since 1954. In the same chapter, Esther's accusing alphabet passes through “exhibit N, a Negro, the night, a noose,” and the letter that follows the question is “R was a rope.” Even the alphabet in this novel remembers lynching.

Esther's Son

Esther's first child was Black. In Chapter 80 she tells Vera about Walter, her first-born, and his father: “Her heart had gone out to him all the more because he was black, a Negro.” He did not stay. “He had fled for fear the white people would see him and kill him. He had gone farther North—where, she did not know, or if ever he had reached the point of equality.” Between 1910 and 1970 some six million Black Southerners went north and west in the Great Migration, fleeing lynching and Jim Crow. “Whenever she read, even in these days, of some poor Negro killed by the white people, she did think of him.”

The child did not live. Esther left him at dawn in a dresser drawer, wrapped in a sporting page, and Young told Studs Terkel that Esther had in fact killed “the only real child which was ever hers” (Terkel interview). He haunts her. His voice cries, “Mother, if you had not killed me, I might have been your greatest love!” and then, “Mother, I go to a fine school with the white children now!” In 1954, in Brown v. Board of Education, the Supreme Court struck down segregated public schools. The dead child's dream of a fine school with the white children is the dream of the decade Young was writing in.

Sundown Towns

Vera's journey ends in Indiana, Young's own state, and the novel does not soften it. In Chapter 71, a widow at the hotel rants against the town's Jewish doctor, whom “Hitler ran him out of Germany,” against Catholics, against Roosevelt, and against Black Americans: “There are no Negroes in this county, for the sun will not set on a Negro’s head here, and we won’t have them. We don’t want mulattoes.” Her rant goes on into a slur. Places like her county were called sundown towns: Black people had to be gone by nightfall or risk violence, and Indiana had scores of them. In the 1920s the Ku Klux Klan in Indiana enrolled perhaps a quarter of a million members. Indiana forbade marriage between white and Black people until 1965.

Miss MacIntosh and the Sons of Ham

Miss MacIntosh is better than her time and also of it. In Chapter 22 she reads the paper and worries about “the treatment of the underprivileged”: “How does the other half live? Who clothes the poor prostitute, the tramp, the orphan? Organized charity omits some, I am afraid. There is no money for coal. There is no money for flour.” Her first question is the title of Jacob Riis's book on the New York slums, How the Other Half Lives (1890). Slums were not, as some argued then and since, a stepping stone the poor chose for themselves. Miss MacIntosh knows who is left out.

In Chapter 65, as Vera tells it, she quarrels with Mr. Bonebreaker. He believes Black men should “be shipped back to Africa before the world’s end, as Mr. Bonebreaker said they should so that God should find them where God had put them, the sons of Ham.” The so-called Curse of Ham, from Genesis 9, was preached in the American South as God's warrant for slavery. Miss MacIntosh disagrees, but listen to how: “Why should the black men, the railroad porters, the shoe-shiners, such pleasant people, be shipped back to Africa,” “for she doubted that they could endure the climate in the jungle? They had got used to the cold.” Her kindness has the condescension of its day in it, and Young lets us hear both. She also objects that Mr. Bonebreaker singles out Jews in his heaven, and the novel calls him “in some ways a narrow-hearted man like all Missourians.”

A Hotel for Jews and Negroes

Catherine has her own plan. In Chapter 70, ordering six thousand dollars of damaged linen, “She was starting a hotel, she announced, speaking largely to herself. It would admit both Jews and Negroes. It would also admit sea birds.” It reads like one more of her dreams, and it was a dream: before the Civil Rights Act of 1964, many American hotels openly turned away both Jewish and Black guests. From 1936 Black travelers carried the Negro Motorist Green Book to find where they could sleep. The opium lady imagines, as a joke and a whim, the law the country had not yet passed.

Suffrage for Whom

Cousin Hannah fought all her life for women's votes. In Chapter 35, “She had wished to rescue the slave, not only the economic slave but also the slave of love.” Her old army coat “had seen so many campaigns in India and China, so many foreign and domestic wars for the emancipation of woman” (Chapter 30). But the movement she stands for was led above all by middle-class white women, and its victory was not shared. The Nineteenth Amendment of 1920 covered all women on paper. In practice Black women in the South were kept from the polls by poll taxes, literacy tests, and terror; Native American women were not citizens until 1924; Asian immigrant women could not become citizens until 1952; and the vote was not protected for all women until the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

Frederick Douglass had said it plainly in 1869: “When women, because they are women, are hunted down through the cities of New York and New Orleans; when they are dragged from their houses and hung upon lamp-posts,” then, he said, they would have “an urgency to obtain the ballot equal to our own” (History of Woman Suffrage, volume 2, 1882). And in Chapter 38, “The lonely cause of suffrage had seemed to my mother to be quite unworthy of Cousin Hannah’s adventuresome spirit.” Many rich white women of Catherine's class opposed the vote, and racism drove some of them, for they feared it would reach women of color. See Married to the Movement for Cousin Hannah herself.

The Old Woman Down the Road

At the end of the book, the stone deaf man remembers a moment from his boyhood, in Chapter 82: “When the white girls turned their faces away from him, an old Negro woman down the road said to him, And what difference, little boy, for you be a man, don’t you, and you dream on! You be like us now. All must suffers for something that be not their fault, you because your skin be white and we because our skin be black, black as the night when they are no stars like before this world was began.” She ends, “Dream on—dream on—for life is imaginary, and this be an imaginary world.”

Her words carry the novel's deepest teaching. But she has no name and no story of her own, and she appears only to console the white boy. That is the pattern of the Magical Negro trope in film, television, and literature: a Black character with no past who arrives to help a white protagonist, often with wisdom that seems to come from nowhere, and whose purpose is his. Racism historians Francisco Bethencourt and John Beusterien trace the trope back to the Spanish comedias de negros of the late 1400s and early 1500s. Young gives the old woman the book's truth and gives her nothing else. The Colony Under No Flag and Whatever Is Average Is the Dream look at her words; this essay asks the reader to see her too.

Bet on the Black Man

There is one small, telling moment early on. In Chapter 2, wandering the waterfront dives, Mr. Spitzer “had placed a wager upon which boxer would triumph, the black or the white, that he had laid his bet on the black man.” In 1908 Jack Johnson became the first Black heavyweight champion of the world, and white promoters spent years looking for a “Great White Hope” to beat him; when Johnson won in 1910, white mobs rioted across the country. The novel's gentlest, most uncertain man bets on the Black fighter. In Chapter 49, as Birds of Passage, Birds of Omen tells, he would have brought before the Senate what America “had done to Indians in their lonely tepees.” He loses his bet, and he would have been thrown out of court. But he sees.

The Radical Immanence Reading

In the language of Radical Immanence, the coachman, the porter, the old woman down the road, and the Black child in the drawer are Pillar 7 (The Margins Reveal the Truth): the people the house and the country push to the edge are where the truth of both shows. Esther reading of every Black man killed and thinking of her lover, and the hangman's tale “of what never happened and yet happened, happened every day in America,” are Pillar 4 (Witnessing Is the Core Ethical Act). And the forgotten name, the rolling eyes, the ace of spades, “exhibit N, a Negro, the night, a noose,” are Pillar 9 (Language Shapes What's Possible): the words a culture uses decide who can be seen as a person.
