Chapter 75: The Mud Baby

← Chapter 74 · Guide Home · Chapter 76 →

Content note: The Esther Longtree chapters (72 to 81) deal with stillbirth and pregnancy loss, rape, infanticide, racist violence including lynching, and cruelty to children. This chapter describes the drowning of an infant and hints at incest.

Research notes: Chapter 75

Characters: Esther Longtree

Themes: Guilt, Innocence, and Justice · Identity and the Self

Symbols: Pregnancy, Birth, and Children · Roses and Flowers · The Sea and Water

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft · William James and Pragmatism

Allusions: Fairy Tales and Folklore · Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery

This is the deepest and most disturbing chapter of Esther’s sequence, and it needs to be approached carefully.

The previous chapters gave you Esther in her fullness: her hope, her philosophy, her humor, her grief held in balance. Chapter 75 goes somewhere darker: a particular dead child, the one that keeps coming back. The mud baby. A body thrown into the river by a fisherman who may have been dreaming. A suspicion about the child’s father that Esther approaches and retreats from and cannot quite name. A body bigger in death than it was in life, covered with mud and brambles, returning in dreams “with a funeral wreath hanging from” its navel, its face lit by “awful starlight which was not starlight.”

Young does not soften any of this. You are not meant to feel comfortable. Esther’s story has arrived at its most unresolved and most haunted material, and the chapter holds it all without resolution: the mud baby’s ambiguous paternity, the implied incest, the body in the river, the detective’s accusations, the war coming that will make all of it seem small. Esther survives through it. She always survives. That survival is not peace.

What Actually Happens

Esther’s body swells “like a face under water”: a phrase that gives her pregnancy the quality of something drowned, something submerged and seen through distortion. She wonders whether what is growing inside her is a child or a benign tumor. She has wondered this before. The question is not rhetorical: she genuinely cannot always tell. Her certainty about the little shadow boxer (two hearts, the little shadow foot) exists alongside this uncertainty about the other thing, the growth that may or may not be what she hopes.

The detective comes again. He accuses her, again, of murdering his children. He raped her, she knows; she was already pregnant when he came, the child in her womb already dead. He claims it now as his. His accusation, that she killed what he says he fathered, is one of the chapter’s precise inversions: the man who violated her now presents himself as the bereaved father, the wronged party, the one seeking justice for a death she caused.

There is a particular dead baby in this chapter: the mud baby. Esther gave birth to it, and with the help of a fisherman (a compassionate man who may have been dreaming, who may have been sunstruck, whose state of consciousness is left uncertain) she “wrapped it in a gunny sack” and threw it “into the river above the dam where the current was rapid.” The river took it. The river did not keep it.

It comes back. In dreams, in nightmares, in waking visions. Bigger in death than it was in life. Covered with mud and brambles. “A funeral wreath hanging from its navel.” Walking sometimes, lying in a coffin-like boat at other times, whistling like a policeman. Its face lit by something that is not starlight. It is ugly and beautiful simultaneously: “glass, twigs, brambles, mud, sand, starlight.” It teases her. It will not answer any question.

The child whistles like a policeman. Esther’s father is a policeman. The chapter does not make this explicit connection; it places these two facts adjacent to each other and lets the reader understand what Esther herself cannot fully face: there is a possibility that her father, the night policeman, is the father of the mud baby. She does not confess this. She does not deny it. She has not borne witness against herself, because her father is the policeman, and to accuse him would be to accuse herself in the eyes of a system that would not protect her.

She imagines an eldest son who will not come indoors. She leaves food for him. She calls to him. He does not come.

The chapter closes with Esther’s dark pragmatism: there is “going to be another war soon, killing all the men and some of the children, and one or two old murders should not have mattered so much, especially if it was nothing murdered.”

The Mud Baby

The mud baby is the chapter’s central image, and it is one of the most unsettling figures in the novel. It is a particular dead child, a specific loss from a specific labor, that has taken on the quality of all of Esther’s losses simultaneously. It returns because it was not properly grieved, or because it cannot be grieved properly, or because some of the circumstances of its conception cannot be acknowledged and so the grief has nowhere to complete itself.

Young describes it in accumulation: mud baby, “seed that swelled into a star,” “glass, twigs, brambles, mud, sand, starlight.” It is assembled from what the river gave it and what Esther’s memory added. It is bigger in death than in life, which is the precise description of how unresolved loss works in the psyche. The thing that could not be held grows larger in its absence than it ever was in its presence. It spreads its fingers wide. It will not answer questions.

The “funeral wreath hanging from its navel” is the chapter’s strangest and most perfectly calibrated detail. The navel is the point of connection between mother and child: the umbilical cord’s trace. A funeral wreath hanging there marks the death of that connection, the formal mourning of what the umbilicus joined and then, in death, still joins. The wreath is for the child. The wreath is also for the bond.

The Fisherman

He is the chapter’s only figure of uncomplicated compassion, and he is barely present. He helps Esther wrap the mud baby and throw it into the river. He does this in a state of uncertainty; he wonders if he is dreaming, if he is sunstruck, if what he is participating in is real. Young gives him this uncertainty deliberately: the witness who helps but cannot fully confirm what he has witnessed. He asks no questions. He helps. He goes back to his fishing.

He is the only man in Esther’s story who is present at a moment of her greatest vulnerability and does nothing to compound it. He does not accuse her. He does not claim the child. He does not write her letters later asking for confirmation of a life. He simply helps her carry what she cannot carry alone, and then he goes away.

His compassion is so minimal and so unelaborated that it stands out sharply against the landscape of men in Esther’s life. This is what help looks like in this world: a man who may have been dreaming, who asked no questions, who helped throw a gunny sack into a river, and who did not stay.

The Father Who Is the Policeman

Young places two facts side by side: the mud baby whistles like a policeman. Esther’s father is a policeman. She has not borne witness against herself, because her father is the policeman, and her father is the law.

The implication is incest. Young does not state it directly. Esther cannot state it directly: her father holds authority over her in every sense, legal and familial. To accuse him would be to enter a system where she has no protection and he has every power. The Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination, which To All My Darlings mentions in this context, applies here with a particular bitter irony: Esther cannot testify because the testimony would implicate not only herself but the very figure of authority who surrounds her. Her father is the policeman. The policeman is the law. The law would not believe her.

The mud baby’s whistle, that policeman’s whistle, is the most disturbing detail in the chapter precisely because of its mundanity. It is not supernatural. It is just the sound that a dead child makes when it walks back to its mother in her dreams, and it sounds exactly like her father going about his work.

The Detective’s Accusation

He comes again, and his accusation in this chapter takes its fullest form: he claims the dead children as his, and he accuses Esther of murdering them. This is the complete inversion. He raped her. The children (such as they were, the dead things, the stillborn, the fragments) were produced by his assault. He has now claimed ownership of those deaths and turned them into her crime.

Young gives us the detective’s self-presentation with cold precision: he is a man who cannot solve his own mystery, who comes to Esther repeatedly in disguise, who leaves evidence of his own presence in her body, who then investigates that evidence as if it belonged to someone else’s crime. He has written the crime and cast himself as the investigator. He has fathered the deaths and accused her of murdering them. There is no appeal from this structure. It is sealed.

Esther’s response is what it has been: silence. Stubborn, luminous, unbreakable silence. She will not explain herself unless compelled. She cries only when she is sure no one is near. She faces the detective’s accusations with the same quality she brings to everything: a refusal to be defined by what others have done to her, even when she cannot name what they have done.

The Coming War

The chapter’s closing move is one of Young’s most characteristic: the expansion from the particular to the historical, from Esther’s private grief to the scale of collective catastrophe. There is “going to be another war soon,” Esther thinks. It will kill “all the men and some of the children, and one or two old murders should not have mattered so much, especially if it was nothing murdered.” “If it was nothing murdered”; this is the question at the center of Esther’s legal and moral situation. Were her stillborn children murdered? Were they children? Were they nothing? The law, which has no precedent for Esther’s case, cannot answer this. Esther, who loved every one of them, cannot answer it either. And against the coming war (which will produce deaths by the hundreds of thousands, deaths that will be commemorated and mourned and recorded and justified) Esther’s private losses look, by comparison, like nothing.

This is not consolation. It is the logic of scale that has always been used to make individual suffering invisible. Young gives Esther this thought not to endorse it but to show that Esther has absorbed it, has made a kind of peace with her own insignificance in the larger accounting of death, while remaining entirely, stubbornly, luminously herself.

What to Hold Onto

The mud baby: bigger in death than in life, covered with mud and brambles, the “funeral wreath hanging from its navel,” whistling like a policeman. The fisherman who helped in a state of dreaming, asked no questions, and went back to his fishing. The minimal compassion that is the only kind available.

The policeman’s whistle. The adjacent facts. The thing Esther cannot say because her father is the law.

The detective’s accusation; he claims the dead children as his, and accuses her of killing them. He has written the crime and cast himself as the investigator.

Esther’s final pragmatism: there will be another war. “All the men and some of the children, and one or two old murders should not” matter so much. “Especially if it was nothing murdered.”

And underneath all of it, still: the little shadow boxer, two hearts. She is more hopeful now than she has ever been. Time’s passage helps her. This is not a lie.
