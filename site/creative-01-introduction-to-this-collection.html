Introduction to this Collection

Part of The Creative Radical Collection, on Radical Immanence, the philosophy Coral Russell draws from the work of Marguerite Young (1908 to 1995).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

This is a collection of short books about how a mind gets captured, and how it stays free.

They were written across a stretch of time, though the thing they describe has been arriving for a long time. The forms it takes look different from the outside. A cult. A strongman at a podium. A high-control church. The algorithm running quietly inside the phone in your pocket. The movement that takes someone you love and, gradually and then completely, makes them unreachable. From a distance these look like separate problems. Underneath, they run on the same mechanism. Something gets between a person and their own perception. Over time the person stops trusting what they see and starts trusting the authorized version. By the end they are not making choices. They are running a script that someone else wrote.

Every book in this collection is an attempt to interrupt that process. And every one of them is built from a single source that most people have never read.

Some years ago I picked up a novel called Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, by an American writer named Marguerite Young. It runs to nearly twelve hundred pages. Young worked on it every day for close to two decades. It was published in 1965 to a reception that was half astonishment and half bewilderment, admired by writers like Anais Nin and Kurt Vonnegut, and then it slipped out of the conversation and mostly stayed out. It is still in print. It is still almost never read.

I did not expect a difficult midcentury novel about a woman on a bus in Indiana to become a manual for the present moment. But the longer I read, the more I recognized. Young had mapped, decades early, the exact conditions that make a person easy to capture. The manufactured nostalgia for a past that never existed. The relief of being handed a world with no gray areas. The slow work of teaching someone to distrust their own memory. The permanent emergency that keeps a person too frightened to think. She wrote all of it into the structure of a novel, filtered through the mind of a woman searching for the one person who had once felt like solid ground.

For a long time I read the book as an answer to the dictator. I think I had the aim slightly wrong, and the correction is the reason this collection exists.

Young was not writing about the strongman. She was writing about the longing he feeds on. Before there is a regime, there is a personality that craves one clean answer and someone to follow. And before there is that personality, there is an ordinary human hunger, worn down by grief and contradiction and the exhausting work of being several people at once, that would hand all of it over for a single redeeming story. The dictator is not the beginning. He is what swallows the bait. Young spent her life inside the bait, naming it, mourning it, and refusing in the end to be consoled by it.

Her answer to that hunger was not a better story. It was not a truer leader or a corrected paradise waiting at the end of the march. Her answer was to stay here, in the ordinary and unfinished present, and to find it enough. I have come to call this Radical Immanence: the idea that meaning is not found by escaping ordinary experience but by going more deeply into it, into its contradictions, its incompletions, its refusal to resolve. That idea is the ground the whole collection stands on.

The books are arranged to be read in order, though you can move through them however you like.

The first, The Captured Mind, is the practical one. It takes the mechanisms apart one at a time, using documented cases from cults, political movements, and the technology companies whose own internal research told them what they were doing. It stays close to the evidence, and it ends each chapter with one concrete thing you can do, not to rescue the person you are worried about, but to protect and sharpen your own attention.

The second, The Creative Radical, goes underneath the mechanics to the philosophy that answers them. It lays out eleven pillars, eleven ways of standing in the world that a captured mind cannot hold. Each one is a refusal of a specific lie that control depends on, and each is carried by a real person who lived it.

The third, The Longing, is where I work out the correction I described above, the shift from writing against the strongman to writing against the appetite he exploits, and they take each pillar back down to the particular hunger it answers.

The Longing Workbook is unique to this collection. It turns the philosophy into practice. It is a set of exercises for catching the hunger while it is active, usually in something small, and for practicing the alternative until it stops being an idea and becomes a habit. Nothing in it needs to be shown to anyone.

A word about who this is for, since that is sometimes clearer than saying who it is not. It is not for people who are certain they are immune, because certainty of immunity is one of the first things these systems exploit. It is not for people who believe the captured are simply less intelligent or less informed than the free, because the evidence does not support that and one of these books explains at length why. It is for people who have stood at the edge of something they could not name and felt the ground shift. It is for people who love someone who has become unreachable. It is for people who have noticed something happening to their own thinking and want a language for it.

The cage that every control system builds is made entirely of stories. So is the key. These books are your permission to examine both.

Here is enough. The sentence has not ended.
