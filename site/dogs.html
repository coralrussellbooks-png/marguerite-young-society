Definition from research

Chapter 6

  • “Quit barking up the wrong tree”: The phrase is American, from the 1830s, for a hunting dog barking at a tree its quarry has already left.
  • “all dressed like British lords in scarlet coats”: Fox-hunters’ red coats are called “hunting pink.”
  • “Death is an old hunter. Wait until you hear his winding horn”: In the legend of the Wild Hunt a ghostly huntsman rides through the winter sky with baying hounds, and to hear him meant death.

Chapter 14

  • “the barkings of spotted coach dogs running beside the wheels”: Dalmatians were bred to run beside carriages, clearing the road and guarding the horses.
  • “her Seeing Eye dogs”: The Seeing Eye, the first American guide dog school, was founded in 1929.
  • “knew that the dog was Socrates”: Socrates was a Greek philosopher who wrote no texts of his own but is credited as one of the founders of Western philosophy. In the work Republic, written by Plato, there is a Socratic dialogue. In it Socrates argues that the dog is very much like a philosopher because the dog distinguishes the face of a friend and of an enemy only by the criterion of knowing and not knowing. Socrates swore “By the dog, the god of the Egyptians” (Gorgias 482b).

Chapter 15

  • “The old sheep dog barking in the night had not barked for nothing”: The proverb is “An old dog barks not in vain,” from George Herbert’s Outlandish Proverbs (1640).

Chapter 16

  • “the effigy of a sleeping marble boy lying on his tomb”: Medieval tomb effigies often rest their feet on a dog, the sign of fidelity.

Chapter 18

  • “Why bark if one keeps a dog?”: “Why keep a dog and bark yourself?” (1583).

Chapter 21

  • “the old, blind sheep dog running ahead of us on three legs”: The dog still “guarding the pasture which was no more against the thief who would not come.”

Chapter 22

  • “An old dog barks at nothing”: This turns around the proverb of Chapter 15, “An old dog barks not in vain.”

Chapter 31

  • “the old St. Bernard”: The monks of the Great St. Bernard Hospice bred their dogs to find travelers lost in Alpine snow. Barry (1800-1814) is said to have saved forty lives. The brandy cask was the invention of the painter Edwin Landseer (1820).

Chapter 34 · The St. Bernard Dog

  • A prophetic figure in Catherine’s dreams, symbolizing guidance, loss, and perhaps spiritual rites.

Chapter 68 · The Blindfolded Dog

  • Innocence and cruelty intertwined, reflecting on blindness to suffering.
  • The village is described as dilapidated and eerie, with details like rusted machinery, broken wheels, and a sign "Gentle Incline — Bride Ahead" which is a false reading for "Bridge Ahead," evoking a sense of decay and death.
  • The Tavern, built in 1827 by "God’s servants," is a gloomy, sprawling hotel with mildewed brick, claw-like vines, and a haunting presence. Its interior surpasses Vera’s imagination, filled with human-faced wasps and thin walls that seem to hear everything.
  • The atmosphere is filled with silence, shadows, and a slow, suffocating sense of inevitability, preparing the reader for the philosophical and existential meditations that follow.

Chapter 71 · The Dogs’ Graveyard

  • Represents death, loyalty, and the neglect of forgotten beings, mirroring the hotel’s decay.

Chapter 82 · The Silent Dog and Barking

  • Represent intuition and knowledge beyond human perception, especially about death.
