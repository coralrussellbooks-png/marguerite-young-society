Chapter 77: Who Killed the Heavens

← Chapter 76 · Guide Home · Chapter 78 →

Research notes: Chapter 77

Characters: Esther Longtree · Justice O’Leary · Miss MacIntosh · Mrs. Hogden

Themes: Death and Mortality · Dreams and Imagination · Motherhood, Pregnancy, and Birth · Women, Gender, and Marriage

Symbols: Birds · Pregnancy, Birth, and Children · Shadows

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Fairy Tales and Folklore · Judaism · Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery · The Bible

For some, this may be a very dark chapter.

This chapter deepens everything that has come before in Esther’s sequence: the racial dimension of the detective’s work, the history of violence against unmarried mothers and their children, the cosmic scale of Esther’s grief, the parents who never consummated their marriage, the little boy who grew a shadow and mistrusts her, and the exhibits A through U, an abecedary of accusation that Young assembles from the materials of Esther’s life and the world’s indifference to it.

Young wrote this novel over eighteen years, and through all of it abortion and contraception were illegal in the United States. The newspaper clipping the detective sends Esther is not entirely fictional. The quiet murders are not metaphor alone. The chapter holds the literal and the symbolic together without separating them, because for Esther, and for the women whose histories the novel draws on, they were never separate to begin with.

What Actually Happens

The chapter opens with Esther asking who killed the heavens, the earth, love itself. She is pregnant with death, her body watery and vast, “her spinal column curved by the weight” of memory. She works at the Greasy Spoon. She tries to forget the moths and the stillborn who come to her at night. She cannot.

The detective sends her a yellowed newspaper clipping: “Mass Murderess Apprehended, Accused by Dead Mothers and Dead Children.” It describes an old woman abortionist (large, nearly three hundred pounds) who ran a hospital for poor, unmarried mothers. She and her timid little assistant treated the mothers well, right up until delivery. The fathers sent bouquets. Then, at the moment of birth, mother and baby were killed. The babies were buried with lime. The abortionist was caught because she made a fatal error: she killed a woman who was pregnant only in her dreams, who could not have been a mother, whose body showed the impossibility of what she claimed. She had murdered a phantom pregnancy. The impossible evidence exposed her.

Esther reads this clipping and understands what the detective is sending her: a mirror. A warning. A comparison. She is the woman always pregnant. She is always carrying what the world cannot see. He is the man who investigates quiet murders. He only solves the white mysteries. The deaths of little Black children are not his business. He fears what he calls “black mysteries.”

Esther learns something new in this chapter: her first child was born of a Black man. This appears without elaboration, as a fact she is thinking about while the clipping is in her hands. Her first child, of all the fathers, came from a Black man. The detective’s racial accounting (his selective attention to white deaths, his indifference to Black ones) is therefore personal: her first child falls in the category he has written off.

Esther recalls her parents: a large, passive policeman of a father and a small, fragile, child-like mother. They never consummated their marriage. Between them passed a silver spoon, carried by a third woman as go-between, enabling a conception that was strange and artificial from the start. This is how Esther came into the world: through a marriage that was not a marriage, a union that produced her without conventional intimacy. She was adopted, she has already told us. This is the prehistory behind the adoption: the parents who could not reach each other and needed an intermediary even for conception. There is also a little boy, an exception among the stillborn, who grew and cast a shadow, who cried like the night wind and falling leaves, who mistrusts Esther, who obstructs her path and causes her to stumble. He is not the little shadow boxer still living under her heart. He is something else: a child who came further than the others, who exists in a liminal space between living and not-living, who will not answer her questions and will not forgive her. She wants to “reach him once with her hands” and still him. She cannot.

The exhibits: A for agony, M for murder, N for Negro, S for shadow, and onward through the alphabet to U, a catalogue of everything that accuses Esther and everything the world has done. Young assembles the letters of accusation into an abecedary, the most elementary form of learning, as if to say: this is the alphabet of Esther’s life. These are the first things. Learn them before you learn anything else.

Esther asserts, in the end, her preference for silence, for mystery, for the void over explanation or judgment. She is always right, the chapter notes, like Miss MacIntosh, like Mrs. Hogden, she has arranged her world so she is never wrong, or never appears to be. She wants to remain outside old human laws and inquisitions, in the great serenity of her strange condition. She wonders why she should “be punished for a few mistakes” when she has already suffered so much. Why should she be bothered, when there is going to be another war anyway.

The Newspaper Clipping

The old woman abortionist is one of the most chilling figures Young introduces, and she is introduced at one remove, through a yellowed clipping, through the detective’s chosen mirror for Esther. She ran a hospital. She took in poor, unmarried mothers. She treated them well. The fathers sent bouquets. She killed the mothers and the babies at delivery. The babies were buried with lime so the bodies would dissolve into the earth and leave no trace.

She was caught because she applied her method to the wrong woman. The woman was pregnant only in her dreams: a false pregnancy, a pseudocyesis, a body that had produced all the symptoms of pregnancy with no child present. When the abortionist attempted to kill what she believed was there, the absence of a child made the murder impossible to conceal in the usual way. The phantom pregnancy exposed the real murders.

Young has been building toward this figure for several chapters. Esther’s condition (always pregnant, children that come as fragments or not at all) is, To All My Darlings notes, consistent with pseudocyesis: a real medical condition in which the body produces pregnancy symptoms, including abdominal enlargement, breast changes, and felt fetal movement, in the absence of a fetus. Young does not resolve whether Esther’s condition is this, or something supernatural, or something else entirely. What the clipping introduces is the shadow-Esther: the woman who is pregnant only in her dreams, who would have been killed by the abortionist for being exactly what Esther may be.

To All My Darlings notes that this story may have its roots in real events, particularly the practice of baby farming: the Victorian-era and early-twentieth-century practice of accepting custody of infants for payment, which in some cases became cover for systematic murder. The most documented case is Amelia Dyer, who murdered infants over thirty years, disposing of bodies in rivers, and whose “timid assistant” was her own daughter. The detail that the fathers sent bouquets is one of Young’s most precise indictments: the men who produced the children had the leisure for flowers. The women who carried them were killed.

Esther’s First Child

The disclosure arrives without ceremony: Esther’s first child was born of a Black man. Young places this beside the detective’s racial accounting: his exclusive attention to white deaths, his explicit statement that the deaths of Black children are not his business, there being too many and too unimportant, without commenting on the connection. The comment is the placement.

Esther’s first child, her first loss, falls in the category the detective has written off. If there is an investigation to be done into that first death, the detective will not do it. His pursuit of Esther is selective in a way that has nothing to do with justice and everything to do with the racial calculus he has already performed. He is interested in what she might have done to children he considers worth investigating. The first one (the one she carries longest, perhaps, in memory) is not among them.

Esther’s Parents and Her Artificial Origin

Esther’s parents never consummated their marriage. Her father is large and passive, a policeman whose authority is imposing and whose life is lonely. Her mother is small and fragile and child-like, proud of her delicacy, emotionally cold. Between them, conception required an intermediary: a third woman carrying a silver spoon between them, enabling what their bodies and relationship could not produce directly.

This is the strangest detail of Esther’s origin: she came into the world through a kind of assisted reproduction so oblique and archaic that it has the quality of a fairy tale. The silver spoon as an instrument of conception: the spoon born with the privileged child, the utensil of sufficiency and inheritance, here used to bridge an unbridgeable gap. Esther is the child of a union that could not quite happen, produced by a mechanism that no one would have designed if anyone had been thinking clearly.

And then she was adopted out from these parents, or adopted into them. The chapter is not entirely clear on which direction the adoption moved. What is clear is that her origin is strange, artificial, and mediated by others, which is the precise condition of her own reproductive life, where the children come through a process that no one can quite explain or control, and where the fathers are transient and the mother is the only constant.

The Little Boy Who Grew a Shadow

Among all the stillborn (the fragments, the snowflakes, the cobwebs) there is one exception: a “little boy who grew and cast his shadow.” He survived long enough to have a shadow, which is the minimum evidence of a body in the world. He cries like the night wind, like falling leaves. He mistrusts Esther. He walks in her way and causes her to stumble. He will not answer any question. He is not the little shadow boxer, who is still alive and fighting under her heart. He is something older, something from before the shadow boxer, something that came and did not quite die but did not quite live either. He is Esther’s accusation: the child who is just real enough to blame her, who has enough presence in the world to obstruct her, who will not let her pass without consequence. She wants to “reach him once with her hands” and still him. This is Esther’s most maternal impulse and her most tragic impossibility simultaneously: the child she wants to comfort is the one she cannot reach, the one who mistrusts her, the one whose crying she cannot stop. The stillborn who hover around her and applaud at least do not accuse. This one does.

The Exhibits: A Through U

Young assembles an abecedary of accusation: A for agony, M for murder, N for Negro, S for shadow, and onward. The alphabet is the first thing a child learns: the building block of all literacy, all language, all the tools by which the world makes sense of itself. Young gives Esther’s situation an alphabet. These are the first letters of what has happened to her. Before you can read anything else, you must read this.

The letter N for Negro in Esther’s alphabet of accusation is not incidental. It is placed precisely. The racial dimension of Esther’s story (the detective’s selective attention, her first child’s father, the quiet black mysteries that no one investigates) belongs in the first letters of what the world has done. The abecedary runs through twenty-one letters. Twenty-one exhibits. Agony through: whatever U stands for. Young does not give us all the words, only some. The gaps are part of the accusation too.

Pregnant with Death

The chapter’s central image of Esther pregnant with death (her womb watery and vast, her body the site of creation and sterility simultaneously) is Young’s most cosmic rendering of Esther’s condition. She is the “Song of Songs of the sterility which is the creation.” The Song of Songs is the Hebrew Bible’s erotic poem: the text where two bodies reach toward each other in equal desire, where creation is celebrated as sensual and mutual. Young inverts it: the song of the sterility that is also a creation, the pregnancy that is also a death, the body that generates and loses simultaneously. Who killed the heavens, the earth, love itself? Esther asks this at the chapter’s opening and does not answer it, because the answer is everywhere and nowhere, distributed through every institution and every indifference and every detective who only deals with white mysteries. The question is the same question she has been asking since Chapter 72: who killed Cock Robin? Nobody. And everybody. And the forces are too large for individual blame.

The answer in this chapter is broader: it is not just the stillborn children who have been killed. It is the heavens. It is love. The cosmic scale of loss that Young has been building toward this sequence arrives here in its fullest form: Esther’s personal grief is not metaphor for the world’s grief. It is continuous with it. The same forces. The same accounting.

Esther’s Serenity

Despite everything in this chapter, the darkest material yet, Esther ends in something like serenity. Not peace, she is not at peace, but the serenity of someone who has decided to prefer mystery over exposure, silence over the judgment of old human laws. She is stubborn. She is proud. She insists on her innocence. She wonders why she should “be punished for a few mistakes” when she has already suffered enough.

Young notes that she laughs in her sleep. That she finds beauty in the natural world. That she is wilder than people think. These glimpses of Esther’s inner freedom (her laughter, her wildness, her refusal to be entirely defined by what has been done to her) are the novel’s quiet argument against its own darkness. The darkness is real. So is the laughter. Both are Esther.

She is always right, the chapter says, in the way that Miss MacIntosh was always right, in the way that Mrs. Hogden was always right. She has arranged her world so the accusation cannot land. Whether this is wisdom or self-protection or delusion, Young will not say. It is the last thing we will learn about Esther in her own voice: she prefers the void. She reaches toward love, toward “death which is another form of love. She was always right.”

What to Hold Onto

The “newspaper clipping—Mass Murderess Apprehended Accused by Dead Mothers and Dead Children.” Nothing about the fathers. The fathers sent bouquets. Esther’s first child, born of a Black man: falling in the category the detective has written off as too many and unimportant.

The silver spoon carried between her parents by a third woman, bridging what could not be bridged. The artificial origin of the woman who cannot produce a living child.

The little boy who grew a shadow, who mistrusts her, who obstructs her path and will not be stilled.

A for agony. M for murder. N for Negro. S for shadow. The abecedary of accusation, assembled from the first letters of what has happened.

And Esther, in the end, laughing in her sleep. Wilder than people think. Preferring the void. Always right.
