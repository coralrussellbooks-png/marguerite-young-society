To All My Darlings

Chapter 40

On Chapter 40 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 4, 2026

← Chapter 39 · Home · Chapter 41 →

Summary

Catherine remembers Cousin Hannah’s voice, a husky baritone like iron bells, and believes Hannah taught her every language of the living and the dead, from Saracen and Provençal to the speech of seals and Platan rails, though Catherine herself had studied nothing but the French of a Peacock Alley menu. In her room she still converses with Alcibiades, Dante, Daniel Boone, and Darius, who answers the kitchen telephone. Her memories come without sequence, the echo before the song, the death before the life. She once greeted every stranger as a cousin and went to bed to escape the embarrassment, and now she introduces the living among the dead, to Mr. Spitzer’s patient wonder. She suspects that Hannah’s adventures, like her own, may have been dreams. She had no faith to lose, and she cannot say whether opium caused her somnolence or her somnolence the opium. She is many selves at once, the horse and the rider, and she believes that a ship’s figurehead knows more than its captain, because it always goes first.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a vivid, almost surreal description of Cousin Hannah’s voice as “a husky baritone, ranging between bass and tenor…as loud, as heavy as iron bells ringing in a leaden-colored northern sky.” This voice is not merely physical but carries an oratorio-like Gothic cathedral weight, symbolizing a profound, almost otherworldly presence. Cousin Hannah is portrayed as a polyglot fluent in “dead languages,” “languages of the dead,” and innumerable tongues, both real and imagined, from “fourteenth-century Saracen or Spanish cavalier” to “Sanskrit from a holy text printed on a snowflake” and “the hieroglyphic language of the drowned Egyptian sailor.” This linguistic breadth represents a connection across time, cultures, and even worlds: living and dead. It suggests a mystical or dreamlike knowledge that transcends normal human experience.
  • The narrative explores the disordered nature of Catherine’s memories and perceptions, emphasizing non-linear time and chaotic mental states. Events do not follow sequentially but occur in a “medley, the imbroglio, Pandemonium, Saturnalia of her deranged imagination.” Time is depicted as fluid, with “a step forward would be a step backward,” and phenomena such as “the after-glow came before the sunset” or “the echo came before the song.” This passage captures the confusion between reality and dream, suggesting a psychological or metaphysical state where logic and chronology dissolve.
  • Catherine is described as “many selves, many beautiful, volatile Psyches wandering about” yet physically “lying in bed, propped up among satin pillows.” This duality between her fragmented inner selves and her physical frailty conveys themes of identity fragmentation and alienation. She greets strangers as friends or relatives, blurring social boundaries, and retreats into solitude to avoid “social embarrassments.” The passage evokes loneliness and the tension between imaginative expansiveness and corporeal confinement.
  • The chapter reflects deeply on death and what follows, with Catherine’s dreams merging “life… and death” into one indistinct realm. The phrase “life was that which, as it had had no beginning, should have no end, not even this death” reveals a mystical vision of existence as continuous and cyclical rather than finite. The suffragette’s struggles are framed as part of a cosmic drama involving light and darkness, “cloud-rack and storm” rather than “eternal darkness.” This suggests hope and transformation rather than finality in death.
  • The closing passages emphasize fluidity and interconnectedness: “All was water. The water flowed over the mooned sand. The sentinels slept at the shifting borderlands.” This dreamlike imagery expresses a world without fixed boundaries, where “there was no place, but there were all places dreaming together, flowing like water into each other.” Catherine’s sense of ubiquity, being in many places at once, or being both rider and horse, symbolizes a dissolution of individual selfhood into a larger, flowing existence.
  • The final metaphor contrasts the figurehead of a ship with its captain, with Catherine believing “the figurehead knew more, the waters roaring at its mouth. For one thing, it always went first.” This image can be read as a symbol of intuition, the unconscious, or the spiritual leading the conscious self through the turbulent seas of life and death.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • English was, Catherine says, “the only foreign language to her.” What does she mean?
  • “Perhaps the most important thoughts were those which never found expression.” Why should Hannah have spoken at all, if “silence should have spoken for her”?
  • Had opium caused her somnolence, or had her somnolence been the cause of her taking opium? Which came first?

Characters

  • Hannah “had come not to create but to destroy the last romance on earth.” Why does Catherine remember her as romance itself?
  • Mr. Spitzer waits “all his life here to prove that he no longer lived.” What would such proof look like?
  • Catherine greeted every stranger as “Cousin Jack” or “Cousin Wilbur.” Is her mistake a kind of welcome?

Connections

  • In Chapter 39 Catherine was “the chair… the lamp… Brooklyn Bridge.” Here she is “both the fox-hunter and the fox.” How do her selves multiply?
  • Hannah always whispered, “I am dead.” How does this echo Mr. Spitzer, who in Chapter 36 “had led a single life— knowing he was dead”?
  • The wheels of the coach are “the sleeping seraphim,” as the cherubim were in Chapter 39. Where is this carriage going?

Reflection

  • “If one had no knowledge, should one not have freedom to imagine”?
  • Can a person understand a language she has never learned?
  • Is it better to go first, like the figurehead, or to steer?

Research: Characters

Cousin Hannah

  • A suffragette and member of an old, aristocratic family (“Old Guard”).
  • Portrayed as a spectral, dream-like figure, both vibrant and ghostly.
  • Speaks in multiple languages, including dead and mythical tongues, embodying the “languages of the dead” and those never spoken.
  • Eccentric, passionate, and combative, yet enigmatic in her presence and communication.
  • Her voice is described as powerful and haunting, a “Gothic cathedral weight.”
  • Seems to exist partly in the realm of dreams or memory rather than pure reality.
  • Her voice was “a husky baritone, ranging between bass and tenor and partaking somewhat of the quality of both.”
  • She swore like “an old veteran of the cavalry,” with “zounds,” “egad,” “caitiff,” “craven,” and “tallyho.”
  • “I am dead—had not old Cousin Hannah always whispered, even when she was vitally alive, the beau ideal of suffrage.”

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • Reflects on her memories and impressions of Cousin Hannah.
  • Although limited in formal education, she surprisingly understands many languages and voices, real or imagined, symbolizing a deep intuitive or dreamlike comprehension.
  • Lives a life intermingled with dreams, illusions, and layers of time and memory.
  • Struggles to order events or states of being logically, experiencing time and experience as fragmented and fluid.
  • Has an opium habit that blurs reality and dreams, contributing to the fluidity between life and death, reality and imagination.
  • English was “the one language she really knew, the only foreign language to her.”
  • She had greeted every stranger as a cousin, “so that she had gone to bed to save herself from further social embarrassments. Then she had begun to speak where there was no one.”
  • “She had had no faith to lose, so change had followed change, and chance had followed chance.”
  • She was always “the errant somnambulist,” and she believes “the figurehead knew more.”

Mr. Spitzer

  • Embodies a skeptical and analytical perspective amid the swirling dreamlike and often chaotic narrative centered on Catherine and Cousin Hannah.
  • Portrayed as a patient observer who frequently comments on Catherine’s behavior and mental state.
  • He is described as believing himself to be “non-competitive,” a detail that suggests a self-aware, possibly detached attitude toward social or intellectual contests.
  • His habitual gestures like pulling the jowl of his cheek, tapping his teeth with a toothpick which is noted as a “tiny diamond-headed gold walking stick,” add a layer of characterization that conveys both a certain meticulousness and an almost aristocratic fastidiousness. This imagery paints Mr. Spitzer as a man deeply engaged in subtle, perhaps cynical reflection.
  • His observations reveal an inclination toward rational skepticism. For example, he frequently notices Catherine’s “serene unconcern for facts,” and how she “would introduce the living among the dead,” blurring the boundaries between reality and fantasy, life and death. Mr. Spitzer seems to see Catherine’s tendencies as both a kind of madness and a poignant human frailty, noting how she assumes that some living people have already “passed over to the other shore,” thus reflecting her confusion or perhaps her unique perception of existence.
  • He is described as “waiting all his life here to prove that he no longer lived, that he had died long ago,” which imbues his character with a certain existential irony or fatalism. This suggests that he is preoccupied with themes of mortality and the nature of existence, paralleling the chapter’s broader meditations on life, death, time, and memory.
  • In the context of the chapter’s dreamlike atmosphere, where time is non-linear, identities are fluid, and reality merges with imagination, Mr. Spitzer functions as a grounding figure, representing a rational, if somewhat resigned, viewpoint. His presence highlights the tension between Catherine’s mystical, fragmented experience of reality and the desire for logical coherence and explanation.

The Black Coachman

  • “She heard the coachman’s whip like the dark tree branches whirling,” and “horses and coach and coachman were one entity passing through the rolling fog.”

Research: Themes

Language and Communication

See: Music, Sound, and Silence

  • Language is a major motif: Cousin Hannah’s polyglot abilities symbolize the bridge between past and present, life and death.
  • Vera’s mother understands languages not through formal study but through mystical or dreamlike insight.
  • The narrative suggests some languages transcend spoken word, being part of an ineffable, universal communication.

Memory, Dream, and Reality

See: Illusion and Reality · Dreams and Imagination · Memory and the Past

  • The chapter blurs the boundaries between what is remembered, what is dreamed, and what is real.
  • Time is non-linear; events and voices intermingle without chronological order, reflecting Catherine’s fragmented memory and dream state.
  • Catherine frequently questions the reality of her experiences, wandering “not only the known world but the unknown world which she knew so very well because no one knew the unknown.”

Death and Immortality

See: Death and Mortality

  • Characters like Cousin Hannah and references to historical or mythical figures suggest a liminal state between life and death.
  • For Catherine, death is not an end but a transformation or continuation of existence in another form: “life was that which, as it had had no beginning, should have no end, not even this death.”
  • The motif of ghosts, spirits, and the “dead languages” reinforces the permeable boundary between the living and the dead.

Identity and Multiplicity of Self

See: Identity and the Self

  • Catherine perceives herself as many selves, reflections, and chimeras, sometimes unable to distinguish her true self from her images or memories.
  • This multiplicity reflects the complexity of identity within the realms of memory, dream, and consciousness.

Time and Order

See: Time · Order, Chaos, and Discipline

  • The chapter challenges conventional notions of time and causality (“the echo came before the song,” “death came before life”).
  • Time is cyclical, layered, and full of “refractions” and “oblique passages,” making logic and sequence unreliable.

Research: Resources

  • “Was her vizor up or down”: A vizor, or visor, is the movable front of a helmet, lowered to cover the face in battle.
  • “for all was fair in love and war”: “all was fair in love and war” is a proverb generally attributed to John Lyly’s 1578 novel, Euphues: The Anatomy of Wit. Euphues is described as a type of student who is “apt by goodness of wit, and applicable by readiness of will, to learning, having all other qualities of the mind and parts of the body.” Although the exact phrasing used today, “All’s fair in love and war” didn’t appear until Francis Edward Smedley’s 1850 novel, Frank Fairleigh Scenes from the Life of a Private Pupil. A domestic English drama of virtue rewarded and vice getting its just deserts, or just desserts. Based on Smedley’s own experiences. It signifies the breakdown of normal social and emotional boundaries in the face of deep personal conflict and desire, a theme that resonates with the novel’s broader exploration of the tensions between illusion and reality, love and suffering.
  • “like turreted steeplechase riders”: See the steeplechase entry in the Chapter 35 resources.
  • “which was fourteenth-century Saracen or Spanish cavalier”: Saracen were primarily Arab Muslims, Turks, Persians, or other Muslims according to Christian writers during the Middle Ages in Europe.
    • Cavalier is associated with the Royalist supporters of King Charles I and across Europe as a style of dress during the seventeenth-century.
  • “the language of the Nubian boy”: Nubians are an ethnolinguistic group of people indigenous to what is now present-day Sudan and Egypt. See Ancient Egypt.
  • “the language of Volpone who died an imaginary death”: Volpone is a comedy play by English playwright Ben Jonson. It is a satire of greed and lust.
    • In Act 5 of Volpone (1606) he feigns death to watch his would-be heirs, and is unmasked.
  • “which was Sanskrit from a holy text printed on a snowflake”: Sanskrit is a classical language of South Asia. It is the sacred language of the Vedas. See Hinduism.
  • “which was ancient Eskimo hunter”: Eskimo are the indigenous peoples who live from eastern Siberia, Alaska, Canada, and Greenland.
    • Eskimo is Young’s word. Many Inuit and Yupik people now reject it. See Exploration and the Poles.
  • “the hieroglyphic language of the drowned Egyptian sailor”: Egyptian sailor may refer to the Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor. The proverbs from this story are: the mouth of a man saves him; do not act arrogant; why give water to a bird before it is slaughtered. It is interpreted as a shipwrecked traveler goes on a spiritual quest through the cosmos and meets a primordial god that provides the traveler with a moral vision to return home with. See Ancient Egypt.
  • “the language of the dead Finnish king with the long heron toes”: Finnish is from Finland and herons are considered the king of the wading birds.
    • See the heron-footed King Canute in the Chapter 35 resources.
  • “third-century Chinese polo players storming over the roofs of Peking”: Polo is a horseback ball game and one of the oldest known team sports.
    • Peking, now known as Beijing, is the capital of the People’s Republic of China.
    • Polo was the passion of the Tang court in China in the seventh and eighth centuries.
  • “which was Provencal, that language of the wandering Troubadour”: Provencal is a language and region of France.
    • Troubadour was a composer and performer of lyric poetry during the High Middle Ages.
    • Jaufre Rudel sang of amor de lonh, love from afar, for a countess of Tripoli he had never seen. His legend says he sailed to her and died in her arms.
  • “which was Greek shepherd singing to his flock”: Greek shepherd may refer to several references listed. The Passionate Shepherd to His Love is a poem by Christopher Marlowe which was responded to by Raleigh in Nymph’s Reply to the Shepherd and later by different poets over the years. See Sir Walter Raleigh.
    • Pan in Greek mythology is the god of the wild, shepherds and flocks, rustic music and impromptus, and companion of the nymphs. He has the hindquarters, legs, and horns of a goat, in the same manner as a faun or satyr. See Greek Mythology.
    • In Greek mythology, Daphnis was a Sicilian shepherd who was said to be the inventor of pastoral poetry.
  • “her furies against archaeologists with pick and spade”: Feminist archaeology employs a feminist perspective in interpreting past societies. It often focuses on gender, but also considers gender in tandem with other factors, such as sexuality, race, or class. Feminist archaeology has critiqued the uncritical application of modern, Western norms and values to past societies. It is additionally concerned with increasing the representation of women in the discipline of archaeology, and reducing a masculine bias within the field. In archaeology this includes: focusing on presumed male roles such as hunter, warrior, chief, and farmers; under-analyzing activities considered to be in the female domain by western tradition; interpreting data from the perspective of middle-age, middle-class, western white men (Margaret W. Conkey and Janet D. Spector, “Archaeology and the Study of Gender,” Advances in Archaeological Method and Theory 7, 1984). See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “like her furies against believers in a future life”: In Chapter 36 Catherine wondered whether Hannah had “gone over from the cross to the crescent.” Here Hannah rages against “believers in a future life or a past life,” as in Chapter 35, where she “had had no faith… in any after-life.”
  • “the French of a Peacock Alley menu”: “the French of a Peacock Alley menu” Peacock Alley was the long corridor joining the Waldorf and Astoria hotels in New York, opened in 1897, where fashionable society strolled to see and be seen. The name moved with the Waldorf-Astoria to Park Avenue in 1931 and lived on in its restaurant.
  • “owls and nightingales and whirling dervishes”: Whirling dervishes or Sufi whirling is a meditation practice performed within a worship ceremony.
    • The whirling dervishes are the Mevlevi order, founded in Konya by the followers of the poet Rumi in the thirteenth century.
  • “chantings of Tibetan monks”: Tibetan monks in Tibetan Buddhism chant mantras which are phrases that protect the mind from negativity. See Buddhism.
  • “cries of Chinese mandarins with great butterfly sleeves”: Chinese mandarins or Mandarins was a bureaucrat scholar in ancient China, Korea and Vietnam. They were officials appointed through the imperial examination system.
  • “screaming contests of Platan rails like the human voice exerted to its utmost pitch”: Young’s source is W. H. Hudson, who describes the giant wood rails, the ypecaha, of La Plata: “This is a tremendous screaming concert,” and “The screams they utter have a certain resemblance to the human voice, exerted to its utmost pitch” (W. H. Hudson, The Naturalist in La Plata, 1892, chapter 19, “Music and Dancing in Nature”). See Birds.
  • “as if it were she who had driven the sun against the clouded net, stopping time”: “And the sun stood still, and the moon stayed” (Joshua 10:13). See The Bible.
  • “who else had talked to so many water carriers in clouds”: The water carrier is Aquarius, the constellation of the zodiac. See Astronomy.
  • “a rider in the great Coffin of Stars”: Coffin of Stars may refer to Delphinus and the main group of stars called Job’s Coffin. See The Book of Job and Astronomy.
  • “voices buzzing like bees in Magellanic Clouds”: Magellanic Clouds are two irregular dwarf galaxies in the Southern Celestial Hemisphere.
    • They are named for Ferdinand Magellan, whose crew recorded them on the first circumnavigation of the world (1519-1522). See Exploration and the Poles.
  • “so many pharaohs in swan boats passing with the sentinel moon”: The pharaohs’ boats recall the solar barque of Ra, which carried the sun god across the sky by day and through the underworld by night. See Ancient Egypt.
  • “or Boston Greek”: Boston Greek - a significant wave of immigration between 1890 and 1921 to the US with many settling in Boston. The Plato Society was formed in 1890 to aid these immigrants. It disbanded after several years due to financial difficulties. In 1899 the Hellenes in American Society was formed.
  • “how should she be this human Tower of Babel”: Tower of Babel in Genesis 11 is an origin myth to explain why the world’s peoples speak so many different languages. “Therefore is the name of it called Babel; because the LORD did there confound the language of all the earth” (Genesis 11:9). See Genesis.
  • “just as a thought is changed by speech”: “A thought once uttered is a lie” (Fyodor Tyutchev, “Silentium!,” 1830).
  • “the king of the Medes”: King of the Medes may refer to Darius the Mede who is mentioned in the Hebrew Bible in the Book of Daniel as a King of Babylon but is not known to history. See The Bible.
  • “the language of bawling Alcibiades, that drunken war lord”: Alcibiades was a prominent Athenian statesman, orator, and general.
    • In Plato’s Symposium (212d) Alcibiades bursts in drunk, crowned with ivy and violets, and praises Socrates. See Plato and Platonism.
  • “Alexander the Great, King of Macedon”: Alexander the Great, King of Macedon, was a king in ancient Greece.
  • “the bird king whose head was turned backward upon his shoulders”: In Dante’s Inferno 20 the diviners walk with their heads twisted backward: “For tow’rds the reins the countenance was turned, / And backward it behoved them to advance” (trans. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, 1867). See Dante.
  • “many who were kings before Christ and many who came afterward”: The famous people listed in the chapter serve as a symbolic gallery within Catherine’s dream world, reflecting the opulent, complex, and often chaotic spiritual and intellectual atmosphere that surrounds her in her suspended and liminal state between life and death. They underscore the novel’s themes of memory, history, mortality, and the interplay between reality and illusion.
  • “Boethius, Balboa, Baldwin”: Boethius or Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius was a Roman senator, consul, magister officiorum, and philosopher.
    • Balboa or Vasco Nunez de Balboa was a Spanish explorer, governor, and conquistador. In 1513 he became the first European to see the Pacific from the Americas.
    • Baldwin may be Baldwin I, the first crusader King of Jerusalem (1100-1118).
  • “Christolpho Colombo”: Christolpho Colombo or Christopher Columbus was an Italian explorer and navigator who completed four voyages across the Atlantic Ocean. See Exploration and the Poles.
  • “Canovas del Castillo”: Canovas del Castillo or Antonio Canovas del Castillo was a Spanish politician and historian known principally for serving six terms as Prime Minister and instrumental in reinstituting the Bourbon monarchy.
    • He was assassinated by the anarchist Michele Angiolillo in 1897.
  • “Hugh Capet who had arisen from his watery grave”: Hugh Capet was the King of the Franks.
    • He reigned from 987 to 996 and founded the Capetian dynasty.
  • “ageless Dante”: Dante or Durante di Alighiero degli Alighieri was a Florentine poet, writer and philosopher. See Dante.
  • “revolutionary Diderot”: Diderot or Denis Diderot was a French philosopher, art critic, and writer.
    • He edited the Encyclopédie (1751-1772).
  • “Harold Harefoot”: Harold Harefoot or Harold I was King of England.
    • After his death in 1040 his half-brother Harthacnut had his body dug up and thrown into a fen, and it was later buried again (Anglo-Saxon Chronicle).
  • “that she listened for hours to Ahaseuris, the Wandering Jew”: Ahaseuris or Ahasuerus is the name of a king, the husband of Esther, in the Book of Esther from the Bible. A German pamphlet of 1602 gave the same name to the Wandering Jew, and Young follows it. See The Bible and Judaism.
  • “as she preferred to think of him, the Everlasting Jew”: Der ewige Jude, the Everlasting Jew, is the German name of the Wandering Jew. The Everlasting Jew is also the title of a modern collection of the writings of Rabbi Isaac Lichtenstein (1825-1908), ed. D. Thomas Lancaster (First Fruits of Zion, 2013). Lichtenstein who after an encounter with the Gospel became an outspoken disciple of Yeshua (Jesus) of Nazareth, but he remained in his post as district rabbi for several years. Despite pressure from rabbinic authorities, Jewish believers, and Christian leaders, he could not be compelled to renounce Judaism or his devotion to the Torah. He is regarded as a pioneer of the Messianic Jewish movement. The significance lies in the metaphorical portrayal of a timeless, wandering consciousness that parallels the novel’s motifs of loss, memory, and the search for self amid shifting, ambiguous realities. See Judaism.
  • “Hebrew parables in palaces of red sandalwood”: Hebrew parables or Mashal/nimshal is a short parable with a moral lesson or religious allegory.
    • Solomon made pillars and harps of almug trees (1 Kings 10:12), often identified as red sandalwood. See The Bible.
  • “tinkling bells and many horns in faraway Cathay”: Cathay is an alternative European historical name for China.
  • “How else could she communicate now so easily with Darius the Magnificent”: Darius the Magnificent is most likely Darius I, the Great, King of Persia (522-486 BC). Another possibility is Dara Shikoh, who was the eldest son and heir-apparent of the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan. He was defeated by his younger brother.
  • “the sleepy voice of Potiphar”: Potiphar is the captain of the Pharaoh’s guard who is said to have purchased Joseph as a slave in the Hebrew Bible and Quran. (Genesis 39:1). See Genesis.
  • “Cousin Hannah had also spoken the King’s English”: “Here will be an old abusing of God’s patience and the King’s English” (Shakespeare, The Merry Wives of Windsor 1.4). See Shakespeare.
  • “such words as zounds”: Zounds is short for “God’s wounds,” the wounds of Christ, as egad is for “ah, God.”
  • “defender of the faith”: “Defender of the faith” is a title traditionally held by the British monarch, symbolizing their role as the supreme governor of the Church of England. The title was originally granted by Pope Leo X to Henry VIII in 1521 for his defense of the Catholic Church against Martin Luther. While the title was later revoked by the papacy, it was reestablished by Parliament and continues to be used by the monarch today. Here it is one of Cousin Hannah’s oaths. This phrase also serves to underscore the tension between faith and practicality, belief and skepticism, a recurring theme throughout the novel.
  • “Avaunt , villains, knaves, blackamoors, blackbirds, Boanerges”: Avaunt means to move away, depart. Boanerges is a vociferous preacher or orator. A nickname applied by Jesus to James and John in Mark 3. “Boanerges, which is, The sons of thunder” (Mark 3:17). See The Bible.
  • “the medley, the imbroglio, Pandemonium, Saturnalia”: Medley is a varied mixture of people or things.
    • Imbroglio is an acutely painful or embarrassing misunderstanding.
    • Pandemonium is a wild uproar among a crowd of people. John Milton invented the name in Paradise Lost for the capital of Hell. (Paradise Lost 1.756). See Milton.
    • Saturnalia was an ancient Roman festival and holiday in honor of the god Saturn. It was held on December 17th of the Julian calendar and later expanded with festivities through to December 23rd. The Saturnalia reflects the contradictory nature of the deity Saturn himself with joyful and utopian aspects of careless well-being and disquieting elements of threat and danger.
    • The actual date of Jesus’s birth is unknown. A reported correspondence between Cyril of Jerusalem and Pope Julius I, quoted by John of Nikiu in the 9th century, is sometimes given as a source for a claim that, in the fourth century AD, Pope Julius I formalized that the nativity of Christ should be celebrated on December 25th as possibly a way to entice more converts to Christianity. Vestiges of the Saturnalia festivities may still be preserved in some of the traditions now associated with Christmas. The custom of gift-giving at Christmas time resembles the Roman tradition of giving small or gag gifts and the lighting of Advent candles resembles the Roman tradition of lighting torches and wax tapers. Likewise, Saturnalia and Christmas both share associations with eating, drinking, singing, and dancing. (Wikipedia)
  • “occultations as when a star crossed the moon”: An occultation is the passing of one heavenly body in front of another, as when the moon hides a star. Catherine has it the other way round. See Astronomy.
  • “maybe Daniel Boone, wearing his coonskin hat”: Daniel Boone: See the Chapter 70 resources.
    • Boone himself wore a broad-brimmed felt hat. The coonskin cap belongs to the Davy Crockett craze of the 1950s. See American Writers.
  • “the life of the oppossum which, when caught, feigns death”: Playing possum: the opossum, when caught, falls limp and seems dead for minutes or hours.
  • “the ubiquitous pigeons of St. Mark’s Square”: St. Mark’s Square or Plaza San Marco is the principal public square of Venice, Italy.
  • “She was struck by moon blindness”: Catherine claims to have moon blindness even though she knew it affected horses and not humans. Moon blindness or equine recurrent uveitis is the leading cause of blindness in horses with the specific cause unknown. See Horses and Carriages.
  • “an emperor’s riding party hunting the unicorn”: See the Unicorn Tapestries entry in the Chapter 35 resources.
  • “lutenists who had known Mozart who had not known them”: By Mozart’s day the lute had nearly fallen out of use. See Music and Composers.
  • “harpists harping on a sea of glass”: “And I saw as it were a sea of glass mingled with fire… stand on the sea of glass, having the harps of God” (Revelation 15:2). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “what anachronism could there possibly be in the realm of the dead”: Anachronism is a chronological misplacing of persons, events, objects, or customs.
  • “A horse knew what the horseman did not know”: Balaam’s ass saw what Balaam could not: “And the ass saw the angel of the LORD standing in the way, and his sword drawn in his hand” (Numbers 22:23). See The Bible and Horses and Carriages.
  • “combing her hair in a psyche knot”: Psyche in psychology is the totality of the human mind, conscious and unconscious. Psyche knot is a woman’s hair style in which the hair is brushed back and twisted into a conical coil usually just above the nape. See the Psyche entry in the Chapter 38 resources.
  • “who should know when the dream ended”: In philosophy there is the question of dreams by Zhuangzi, Plato, and Aristotle. Descartes asked and tried to answer the following question “How can I know that I am not now dreaming?” which seems to be the same question Catherine is preoccupied with. Descartes’ answer was that we cannot trust our senses in waking life without invoking a benevolent God who would not deceive us. Catherine takes this one step further at the end of Chapter 39 and asks, “What if God is dreaming?” In psychology the question “Is Life a Dream?” is answered with the coherence of the waking world does give evidence that it is not just a figment of our imagination. However, the objects that we perceive as permanent are in reality through the science of quantum physics mostly made up of empty space. In a sense people do live in a dream world that is created by the mind. When people are awake, their minds conform their dreaming to a reliable set of patterns, which they assume to be determined by a reality that exists independently of their experience of it, though they have no way of knowing that reality except through the complex ways in which it affects their dream. There is also reality that is experienced and affected by psychedelic drugs and other altered states of consciousness. The chapter weaves together the philosophical inquiries of Zhuangzi, Plato, Aristotle, and Descartes on dreams to underscore the complexity and mystery of human consciousness and the elusive quest for truth amid illusions. See Taoism and Zhuangzi, Plato and Platonism, Aristotle, and Descartes and Dualism.
  • “I am dead—had not old Cousin Hannah always whispered”: Nietzsche’s phrase, “God is dead” (Friedrich Nietzsche, The Gay Science 125, 1882), refers to the demise of traditional religious belief and the moral structures built upon it. He believed this shift opened up new possibilities for humanity to create its own values and meaning. Marcus Aurelius wrote, “Consider thyself to be dead, and to have completed thy life up to the present time” (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 7.56, trans. George Long, 1862), where one focuses on present experiences and accepts the inevitability of death, rather than clinging to the past or fearing the future. It is a metaphor for the collapse of comforting spiritual narratives, forcing the characters to face the harsh truths of life and death without the crutch of religious faith or afterlife expectations. It marks a turning point where faith is supplanted by a focus on human endurance, common sense, and the acceptance of life’s limitations. See Nietzsche and Stoicism.
  • “the dust returning always to the dust”: “For dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return” (Genesis 3:19). See Genesis.
  • “non angli sei angeli”: Non angli sei angeli (They are not angles but angels) is attributed to Pope Gregory I after seeing pale-skinned boys at a slave market. The Latin is properly non Angli sed angeli, “not Angles but angels.” Bede tells the story (Ecclesiastical History of the English People 2.1, 731).
  • “ignotum per ignotius”: Everything is explained as “ignotum per ignotius” which is explaining the unknown by means of the more unknown. It is not a logical fallacy but rather a criticism that is not useful in a particular context.
  • “a golden mean between extremes”: Aristotle placed virtue at the mean between two extremes, as courage lies between cowardice and rashness (Nicomachean Ethics 2.6). See Aristotle.
  • “a sound mind in a sound body”: “Orandum est ut sit mens sana in corpore sano,” “one should pray for a sound mind in a sound body” (Juvenal, Satire 10.356).
  • “as if the truth were mighty and would prevail”: “Magna est veritas, et praevalet,” “great is truth, and it prevails” (1 Esdras 4:41, Vulgate). See The Bible.
  • “her succumbing to this fatal drug habit by gradual degrees”: Addiction is a complicated phenomenon that overwhelms people and can harm an individual and those around them.
  • “the errant somnambulist”: In Bellini’s opera La Sonnambula (1831), the sleepwalking Amina crosses the high, broken bridge of a mill. See Music and Composers.
  • “Would the sheathbills have told”: Sheathbills are Antarctic shore scavengers, ruddy turnstones are wading shorebirds, and stormy petrels are seabirds of the open ocean. See the petrel entry in the Chapter 39 resources. See Birds.
  • “A rider on a bicycle was to her one entity”: “How can we know the dancer from the dance?” (W. B. Yeats, “Among School Children,” 1928).
  • “the long-bodied coach dogs barking at the wheels”: Coach dogs, usually Dalmatians, ran beside carriages to guard the horses. See Dogs.
  • “As between the figurehead and the greatest captain of the greatest boat”: Plato’s parable of the ship (Republic 6.488), where a quarrelling crew ignores the true navigator, symbolizes the dangers of a political system not based on expertise. It is often called the Ship of State, where the ship represents a nation or government, with the crew, captain, and passengers symbolizing different social classes or politicians. It encapsulates the novel’s exploration of human vulnerability, the elusiveness of truth, and the interplay between reality and fantasy. It stands as a haunting image of mortality, the passage of time, and the inescapable drift toward oblivion, yet it also hints at the persistence of memory and the faint glimmers of hope amid the surrounding darkness. See Plato and Platonism.
    • The Ship of Fools is Sebastian Brant’s Das Narrenschiff (1494), and Katherine Anne Porter’s novel Ship of Fools (1962).

Fashionable society.

Blackamoor
An old term for a Black African or any dark-skinned person, common in European art and in decorative figures of Black servants. It is now considered offensive.
Caitiff
A base, despicable person.
Cavalier
A horseman or gallant gentleman; also offhand and disdainful.
Chimera
A monster of Greek myth, part lion, part goat, part serpent; any wild, impossible fancy.
Citadel
A fortress, usually on high ground, that protects a city.
Convex
Curved outward.
Craven
Cowardly.
Crusader
A soldier in the Crusades, the Christian military campaigns to take the Holy Land, fought between 1096 and 1291.
Crystal-hunter
A prospector in the Alps who climbs into rock clefts to find quartz crystals.
Debacle
A sudden collapse or disaster.
Dervish
A member of a Sufi order known for whirling in worship.
Elixir
A medicine sold as a cure-all.
Errant
Wandering; straying from the proper course.
Feigned
Pretended.
Figurehead
A carved figure, often a woman, mounted on the bow of a sailing ship. See On the figurehead and intuition (Ch 40).
Gauntlet
An armored glove.
Griffin
A mythical beast with the head and wings of an eagle and the body of a lion.
Halloo
A shout to urge on hounds.
Halo
A ring of light around the sun or moon, caused by ice crystals high in thin clouds. In art, a ring of light around the head of a saint.
Hieroglyph
A picture-sign of a writing system, as in ancient Egypt.
Imbroglio
A confused or complicated situation.
Inchoate
Only begun; unformed.
Iridescence

Undecided, wavering.
Knave
A dishonest man; a rogue. Also the jack in a deck of cards.

Clearly.
Mandarin
A high-ranking official of imperial China.

The shining, rainbow-colored lining of some shells, used for inlay.

Indirect; slanting.
Oratorio
A large musical work for voices and orchestra on a sacred theme.
Parable
A short story that teaches a lesson.
Phantasmal
Ghostly; like a phantom or an illusion.
Pollarded
Cut back to the trunk to make a dense head of branches.
Prism
A clear piece of glass with flat sides that splits light into the colors of the rainbow.

The bending of light as it passes from one medium to another.
Retrospect
A looking back on the past.
Sandalwood

A horse race over fences, ditches, and water jumps, first run cross-country from one church steeple to another.

A woman who campaigned for women’s right to vote. The word was coined by a London newspaper in 1906 for militant British activists. Most American activists called themselves suffragists. See Women’s Suffrage.

The huntsman’s cry on sighting the fox.

Ubiquity

Venturesome
Daring.
Vizor
An old spelling of visor, the movable front of a helmet.
