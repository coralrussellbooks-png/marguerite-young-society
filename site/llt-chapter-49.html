Chapter 49

On Chapter 49 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 48 · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 50 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: The Past Doesn't Go Away

The Questions

Mr. Spitzer navigates by one great star that is “this sediment greater than all other stars.” What is a star made of all the others worn away?

The ice forms “formation over formation, snow pack over snow pack…like time containing all time.” What kind of time accumulates rather than passes?

The one butterfly “was always two butterflies conjoining.” Why can the single thing never quite stay single?

He is “living when he was dead, waking only when he was sleeping.” What has happened to the line between the states?

The star Mr. Spitzer steers by is sediment, and the image is The Past Doesn't Go Away made cosmic. It is “this star which seemed the remainder of many stars honed away, this sediment greater than all other stars had been or would become.” The one point of light he navigates by is not a thing behind the others but the others pressed down into one, the accumulated remainder of everything worn away. Time here is not a line the stars travel along but a settling, a honing-down, the many becoming the dense single sediment that is left. The past is not behind this star. It is what the star is made of.

The long passage of forming ice is the same plank as landscape. “Ice had grown old, formation over formation, snow pack over snow pack, unmelting snowflake over unmelting snowflake, star over star,” until it was “like the roof of a tomb over this world, even like time containing all time.” This is The Past Doesn't Go Away at its widest reach in the book: not a season that passes but a layering that accumulates and never melts, each snowfall preserved beneath the next, the world roofed in a time that holds all its own past at once. Nothing is lost to sequence here because there is no sequence, only the pack deepening, the sediment thickening, the past kept under the present.

And the single butterfly will not stay single. What “had seemed one butterfly…was always two butterflies conjoining over the suddenly shining wave, one with its image.” The plural self reaches even the emblem of transformation: the butterfly that carried Incompletion in the earlier chapters is here doubled, one and its reflection, never quite the single thing it appeared. Nothing in Mr. Spitzer’s world resolves to one, not the self, not the brother, not even the insect on the wave. To look closely is always to find the one becoming two, the single crumbling into its plurality, as every individual crumbles into waters and clouds when watched long enough.

He himself lives across the line between the states. He is “living when he was dead, waking only when he was sleeping,” disoriented “by his great experience of living when he was dead.” The oppositions that are supposed to sort experience, living and dead, waking and sleeping, do not hold in him; he is on both sides of each at once, or on neither. This is not presented as breakdown but as the condition of a man who has stopped believing the lines are real. The sediment-star and the accumulating ice are the outer form of it: in a world where time contains all time, the dead are not past and the sleeper is not absent, and Mr. Spitzer moves through all the states at once because none of them has been sealed off from the others.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes the frozen, sedimented world for mere bleak scenery, and the reading that takes Mr. Spitzer’s living-when-dead for a symptom to be diagnosed. Neither the bleak backdrop nor the pathology is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that time here accumulates rather than passes, the star a sediment of all the stars honed away, the ice a layering of every unmelting snowfall, a time that contains all time and keeps the past under the present. The single thing will not stay single, the one butterfly always two, the self always crumbling into its plurality. And the lines between living and dead, waking and sleeping, do not hold in a world where nothing has been sealed off from anything. Time is sediment, the one is always many, and the oppositions do not sort. Mr. Spitzer moves through all the states at once, and the chapter offers no line to sort him by.

For Reflection

What in one’s present is not the past left behind but the past pressed down into what is here now?

When a single thing is looked at closely, why does it so often turn out to be two?

What would it be to stop believing the lines between waking and sleeping, past and present, are sealed?
