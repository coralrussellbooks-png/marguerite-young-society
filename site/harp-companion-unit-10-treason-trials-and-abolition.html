Unit 10, pp. 201-224: Treason trials and abolition

← Unit 9 · Harp Song for a Radical: A History Companion · Unit 11 →

Background for this unit: The History in Harp Song for a Radical

The Civil War years in Indiana run through John Brown, the Copperhead treason trials, Lincoln's assassination, and the funeral train that passed through Indianapolis and Chicago. Young ends with the boy Debs at school in Terre Haute, learning from McGuffey's Readers. The unit shows how close the war came to the Debs doorstep and how its heroes became his models.

Major entries

John Brown (pp. 202-205)

  • Who he was: An abolitionist (1800-1859) who fought proslavery settlers in "Bleeding Kansas." In May 1856 his band killed five proslavery men at Pottawatomie Creek. That August his son Frederick was killed in the fight at Osawatomie (p. 203).

  • Harpers Ferry: On October 16, 1859, Brown and twenty-one men seized the federal armory at Harpers Ferry, Virginia, hoping to arm enslaved people for a rising. Marines under Robert E. Lee captured him. He was hanged at Charles Town on December 2, 1859.

  • Why it matters: Northern writers made him a martyr. Victor Hugo pleaded publicly for his life, which Young connects to Debs's namesake (p. 204). Louisa May Alcott and Henry Wadsworth Longfellow mourned him (p. 203). The South saw proof of Northern aggression, and secession followed within a year.

  • How Young uses him: Brown is the ancestor of Debs's moral absolutism. Debs was later called "the second John Brown" (p. 210).

  • Further reading: David S. Reynolds, John Brown, Abolitionist (2005).

Lambdin P. Milligan and *Ex parte Milligan (pp. 207-211)

  • What happened: Milligan, a lawyer and Copperhead Democrat of Huntington, Indiana, was arrested in October 1864 with William A. Bowles, Stephen Horsey, and others. They were accused of plotting an uprising for August 16, 1864, to free Confederate prisoners and kidnap Governor Morton (p. 208). A military commission in Indianapolis sentenced Milligan to hang on May 19, 1865 (p. 209). President Andrew Johnson commuted the sentence to life.

  • The ruling: In Ex parte Milligan (1866) the Supreme Court held that civilians cannot be tried by military tribunals where civilian courts are open. Milligan was freed. He then sued the officials who had arrested him. Benjamin Harrison defended them, and in 1871 a jury awarded Milligan five dollars.

  • Why it matters: It remains a landmark of civil liberties, cited in debates over military trials to this day. Young draws an implied line from Milligan's military trial to the federal injunctions and wartime prosecutions later used against Debs.

  • Where the record differs: Young calls Milligan "Irish-born" (p. 207). He was born in Belmont County, Ohio, in 1812, and was admitted to the Ohio bar with Edwin Stanton, as she notes.

  • Further reading: Frank L. Klement, Dark Lanterns (1984), and Mark E. Neely Jr., The Fate of Liberty: Abraham Lincoln and Civil Liberties (1991).

Lincoln's assassination and funeral train (pp. 205-219)

  • What happened: John Wilkes Booth shot Lincoln at Ford's Theatre on Good Friday, April 14, 1865 (p. 206). The funeral train retraced his inaugural route west. His body lay in state at the Indiana State House on April 30, a Greek temple with a dome designed by the architect Ithiel Town (p. 217). It reached Chicago on May 1 and Springfield on May 3.

  • Pullman's Pioneer: George Pullman's first luxury sleeping car, the Pioneer, is traditionally said to have joined the funeral train from Chicago to Springfield, which made his name. Young writes that the widow suggested the car "for her passage and that of her surviving sons" (p. 217). Mary Lincoln was too prostrate to travel and stayed in Washington. Historians also question how much of the Pioneer legend is true.

  • Booth and the Knights: Young links Booth to the Knights of the Golden Circle (p. 205). The claim circulated widely at the time but has never been proved.

  • Further reading: Michael W. Kauffman, American Brutus (2004).

Robert Todd Lincoln and the Pullman connection (pp. 217-219)

  • Who he was: Lincoln's eldest son (1843-1926), a lawyer, secretary of war (1881-1885), and minister to Britain.

  • Why it matters: In 1875 he had his mother committed to an asylum, which Young compares to driving nails into her heart, calling him the "future Prince of Nails" (p. 217). He served as Pullman's lawyer and became president of the Pullman Company after George Pullman's death in 1897. So the Great Emancipator's son ended up running the company whose 1894 strike sent Debs to jail, the irony Young is setting up (p. 219).

  • Further reading: Jason Emerson, Giant in the Shadows: The Life of Robert T. Lincoln (2012).

Wendell Phillips (pp. 214-216)

  • Who he was: A Boston patrician (1811-1884) who became the most eloquent abolitionist orator. His career began with a speech in 1837 denouncing the murder of Elijah Lovejoy.

  • The Disunion speech: On January 20, 1861, at the Boston Music Hall, he argued for letting the South go. He praised democracy as a force "crumbling classes into men" and said, "For this, Leggett labored and Lovejoy died" (pp. 214-215). He was often a harsh critic of Lincoln, though he delivered a eulogy for him at Tremont Temple.

  • Why it matters: After the war Phillips turned to labor reform, the eight-hour day, and the Greenback cause, bridging abolition and the labor movement. Debs counted him among the master spirits of his life (Unit 1).

  • Further reading: James Brewer Stewart, Wendell Phillips: Liberty's Hero (1986).

McGuffey's Readers (pp. 221-223)

  • What they were: Schoolbooks compiled by William Holmes McGuffey (1800-1873), born in Washington County, Pennsylvania, and later a professor and college president (pp. 221-222). The first appeared in 1836. They sold an estimated 120 million copies and taught most American children to read for the rest of the century.

  • Why it matters: They mixed moral lessons, patriotic speeches, and literary selections. Recitations from them on Friday afternoons (p. 223) gave Debs's generation its oratorical style. Young notes Debs was later called "the Terre Haute Rienzi" (p. 223), after the fourteenth-century Roman tribune Cola di Rienzo, whose rousing address from a popular play was a schoolroom favorite.

  • Debs's schooling: Terre Haute could not yet fund public schools well, so Debs attended the Old Seminary School (p. 224). He left school at fourteen for the railroad shops.

  • Further reading: John H. Westerhoff III, McGuffey and His Readers (1978).

Shorter entries

  • Dred Scott's last years (pp. 201-202): After his freedom he worked as a porter at Barnum's Hotel in St. Louis. He died of tuberculosis in September 1858 and was buried in Calvary Cemetery.

  • Henry Ward Beecher and "Beecher's Bibles" (pp. 206, 213-214): The Brooklyn preacher said a Sharps rifle would do more good in Kansas than a Bible. His Plymouth Church paid for twenty-five rifles for a Kansas colony, whose congregation became the Beecher Bible and Rifle Church at Wabaunsee. Young calls it "the Church of the Holy Rifles" (p. 206).

  • Harriet Beecher Stowe (pp. 204, 206): Beecher's sister, author of Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852).

  • Copperheads (p. 206): Northern Democrats who opposed the war, named by their enemies after the venomous snake.

  • William A. Bowles and French Lick (pp. 207, 210): A Mexican War officer and doctor who owned the French Lick springs hotel. He was sentenced to death with Milligan, then commuted. The springs later sold "Pluto Water" nationwide.

  • Stephen Horsey (p. 207): A farmer of Shoals, Indiana, tried and sentenced with Milligan.

  • Camp Morton and Burnside Barracks (p. 209): Indianapolis military posts. Camp Morton held Confederate prisoners.

  • Daniel Voorhees (p. 210): Terre Haute's Democratic congressman and later senator (1877-1897), "the Tall Sycamore of the Wabash." In 1859 he defended one of John Brown's raiders. Covered further in Unit 15.

  • "Waving the bloody shirt" (pp. 210-211): The Republican tactic of reminding voters that Democrats had led the rebellion.

  • Lincoln's Indiana boyhood (p. 213): The Lincolns lived on Little Pigeon Creek in Spencer County from 1816 to 1830. He took a flatboat to New Orleans in 1828.

  • The Quaker model farm (p. 207): Quaker missionaries set up a farm among the Miami on the upper Wabash around 1804, associated with the chief Little Turtle. Young describes Harrison's troops burning it in the War of 1812.

  • The plot to steal Lincoln's body (p. 218): In 1876 counterfeiters planned to take Lincoln's body from its Springfield tomb and hide it in the Indiana dunes for ransom. Young credits Pinkerton's detectives with foiling it. Standard accounts credit the Secret Service.

  • Robert Ingersoll (p. 220): The agnostic orator, whose ideas Daniel Debs shared. Covered in Unit 13.

Allusions

  • Brutus and Caesar, Sic semper tyrannis (p. 205): Booth cast himself as Brutus and shouted Virginia's motto, "Thus always to tyrants."

  • Macbeth and Duncan (p. 213): Shakespeare's murdered king, one of Lincoln's favorite plays.

  • Canaan (p. 212): The biblical promised land that Moses saw but did not enter, a common image for Lincoln.

  • Perrault, Grimm, Aesop, Pilgrim's Progress (p. 221): The fairy tales, fables, and Christian allegory of a nineteenth-century child's reading.

  • Pluto and Prospero (p. 207): The Roman god of the underworld and Shakespeare's magician, names of the French Lick springs.

  • Little Nell (pp. 206, 223): Dickens's dying child heroine, a staple of McGuffey-era recitation.
