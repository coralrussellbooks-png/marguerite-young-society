The Philosophy in MMMD

Chapter 21. The Politics of Distrusting Leaders

← Previous · Philosophy of MMMD Home · Next →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

Themes: Music, Sound, and Silence

Symbols: Eyes and Blindness · Music and Instruments

Philosophy: Nietzsche · Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres

Chapters discussed: 9, 30, 42, 49, 52, 61, 62

Definition from research

Young insisted that her novel had a politics, and she located it exactly. “If you understand hallucination and illusion, you don’t blindly follow any leader,” she told Friedman and Fuchs. “You must know if the person is sane or insane, over the abyss.” The last phrase deserves attention. In Nietzsche, the abyss was something the solitary thinker looked into. In Young’s interview, it is the place where a leader stands. And in her novel, as Part One showed, it is the floor everyone walks on. A leader who claims solid ground is either deceived or deceiving.

The argument that runs through the trials of Part Two now becomes political. If every system fails under the accumulation of lived experience, then every leader who offers a complete system is offering something that will fail. The danger is not only that the leader might be wrong. It is that followers, trusting the system, will stop looking at the experience that would show them the failure.

The Blind Leader

The novel’s sharpest political image comes from Cousin Hannah’s past. According to an old newspaper story, she once skied “with blind skiers following a leader’s bell, and none had known that which Mr. Spitzer knew, that the leader was blind” (ch. 52). The image works on two levels. On the surface it is simply strange, a feat of mountain daring. Beneath it is a parable. The followers are blind and know they depend on the bell. What they do not know is that the one ringing it is blind too.

Hannah was a leader herself, “the leader of those great armies riding thunderously into war, flying their silken petticoats as flags” (ch. 42), and her cause was just. The novel does not suggest that causes are false or that action is worthless. It suggests that certainty is the danger. Hannah acted as though she had “no use for further experience or knowledge” (ch. 30). A leader who needs no further experience is a leader who cannot learn that the path has changed.

The Despotic Conductor

The second political image is musical. Mr. Spitzer, a musician who never plays, reflects on the orchestra. “The man kicked out of an orchestra knew more about music than any playing in an orchestra, for the player was under the powers of the despotic conductor and could play only his own part” (ch. 62). The player may even be led astray “if the leader should misread the score and turn South instead of North.” Mr. Spitzer gives his sympathies “not to the conductor” and “not to the mad fellow imagining he could conduct all the music of all the singing spheres.”

Here the thread from Part One comes full circle. Pythagoras entered the novel as a guest in Catherine’s house, the man who “had discovered heaven’s harmony” (ch. 9). The dream of a single cosmic harmony was a philosopher’s dream. In the conductor, it becomes a political fantasy: one man who believes he can direct all the music there is. The novel names that belief madness. Young’s politics begin here, in the refusal of any total harmony imposed from a podium.

The Frog Musician

Young named the frog musician as one of the passages that “investigate the nature of illusion,” and his scenes show how the voice of guidance can arrive from anywhere. He is a derelict who steps from doorways in the fog. He asks Mr. Spitzer for a light, for a nickel, for a coin. Then he begins to give advice. “Watch out for the butterflies. Watch out for the weeds in the garden.” Later: “Watch out for the pickpockets. Watch out for the glass-smashers.” And later still, he tells Mr. Spitzer to “Put your money on the Robins” (ch. 61).

Mr. Spitzer, “very much agitated,” cannot tell what the tip means, and for a moment it is “almost as if he had heard his brother’s voice.” This is the psychology of the follower in miniature. A voice speaks with confidence out of the fog. It warns of dangers and promises winners. And the listener, lonely and grieving, begins to hear in it the voice of someone he loved. Mr. Spitzer does not follow the tip. He wonders about it. That wondering is Young’s politics in its smallest unit.

The Unelected Representative

Against the blind leader and the despotic conductor, the novel offers Mr. Spitzer himself as a model of public life. He was, the narrator says, “the pleader of lost causes,” causes “of which he had been an unsolicited and unelected representative, a minority representing a majority, a hidden majority like those sleeping in the churchyard” (ch. 49). He represents the dead and the forgotten. He holds no office. He claims no system. He simply refuses to let the lost be erased.

This is close to the figure Young would spend her last decades writing about. Eugene V. Debs was a leader, but a leader of an unusual kind, one who said he would not lead people into a promised land because others could lead them out again. Young’s long biography of him grew from the same conviction that runs through her novel: that the only trustworthy public figure is one who belongs to the people rather than standing above them with a master key.

What the Politics Requires

The politics of the novel is not a program. It does not tell readers what to vote for. It tells them how to hold their beliefs. Every system is a room in a crowded house. Every leader rings a bell in the fog. The task is not to stop following all bells, which would leave a person alone on the mountain. The task is to remember that the one ringing may be blind, and to keep listening to the experience that no system has yet absorbed.
