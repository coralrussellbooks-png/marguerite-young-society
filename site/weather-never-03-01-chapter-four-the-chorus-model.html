The Weather You Were Never Allowed to Have

Chapter Two: The Chorus Model

← Part Two: The Five Frameworks · The Weather You Were Never Allowed to Have · Chapter Three: The Working Story →

You learned early that certain parts of you were not welcome at the table.

Not in those words. Nobody said: leave that part of yourself outside. What they said was quieter and more thorough than that. They said it in the way the room went still when you said the wrong thing. In the way your father's face changed, or didn't change, which was worse. In the way the other boys responded to the one who showed too much, who needed too much, who was moved too visibly by something the framework had decided was not worth being moved by. In the way the coach or the teacher or the older man who was supposed to model what you were becoming demonstrated, through every interaction, what was allowed and what was not.

You learned. You learned which parts of yourself were welcome and which parts had to wait in the car. And because you were smart and you wanted to survive and survival required a specific kind of fluency, you got very good at the management of it. You learned to assemble the right version of yourself before you walked into the rooms that required it. You learned which parts belonged where. You learned to read the temperature of a situation before you added yourself to it.

You probably got very good at this.

You may have been told it was a gift. Steady. Reliable. Doesn't make things complicated.

What it actually was: exhausting. And underneath the exhaustion, something else. The slow erosion of being unable to remember, after enough years of management, which parts of you were real and which parts were the performance of the parts that were allowed.

This is the chapter about that erosion. And about what is on the other side of it.

The novelist Marguerite Young spent eighteen years writing a novel about this. She never meant it as self-help, but it is an account of consciousness. In Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, there is no single reality, and the self is not a destination but a weather system: always multiple, always moving, always in conversation with itself.

She was not writing about pathology. She was not writing about dissociation or dysfunction or the fragmentation that trauma produces. She was writing about the ordinary condition of being a conscious human being, which is that you contain multitudes and the multitudes are not a problem to be solved.

You knew this already.

You have been knowing it your whole life.

What you may not have had is permission to stop pretending otherwise.

Here is what we know from the research.

Richard Schwartz spent decades as a family therapist before he noticed something his training had not prepared him for: his clients did not have a self. They had parts. Not in the dissociative sense. In the ordinary sense. The part that was ambitious and the part that was terrified of success. The part that wanted intimacy and the part that had learned intimacy was dangerous. The part that was still waiting, at forty-five, for an acknowledgment from his father that was never coming.

Schwartz called his framework Internal Family Systems. The name is less important than what it found: that the goal of psychological health is not the elimination of the parts that cause trouble. It is the development of a relationship among them. A Self, in his framework, with a capital S, that can hear all the parts without being hijacked by any of them. Not a unified self. A self that is capable of hosting the whole chorus.

The chorus.

This is the metaphor we will use throughout this chapter, because it is more accurate than excavation and more useful than parts. You are not a thing to be dug up. You are not broken into pieces. You are a chorus: multiple voices, different registers, different histories, all of them yours, none of them the whole of you.

The question is not: which one is real?

They are all real.

The question is: who is conducting?

For most of your life, if you grew up inside the dominant framework of masculinity, the answer to that question was: not you.

The conductor was the mold.

The mold had a shape. In the family or the community or the culture that transmitted it to you, the shape was specific: strong, self-sufficient, not too much, knows what he thinks, doesn't need to talk about it. The mold is not unique to any one geography. It exists wherever men have been organized around the performance of invulnerability, which is most places, which is most of male history. The specific contents of the mold differ. The function is the same: to designate which parts of the chorus are permitted to sing and which parts must be silent.

And the silent parts do not disappear.

This is the thing the mold never accounts for. The parts that are not permitted do not cease to exist. They go underground. They find other ways to speak. They show up in the anger that arrives disproportionate to the situation. They show up in the distance you keep in the relationships where the closeness would require you to bring the waiting parts back into the room. They show up in the choices you make that you cannot explain to anyone's satisfaction, including your own. They show up in the three in the morning feeling, the one the framework has no category for, the one where some part of you that has been waiting a long time gets briefly heard in the quiet before the performance resumes.

The waiting has a cost.

The cost is paid over years and decades and it accumulates in the body and in the choices and in the slow erosion of knowing who you actually are beneath the management.

And then something happens.

For some men it is a crisis. The mold breaks under pressure and everything suppressed comes through at once. For some men it is slower: a book, a conversation, a relationship that required more than the managed version could give, a moment of recognition so quiet it barely registered until later when you looked back and understood that something shifted.

For some men it is simply exhaustion. The performance finally costing more than it purchases.

Whatever opens the door, what is on the other side is the same thing: the chorus, which has been there the whole time, waiting to be heard.

Here is what it looks like from the other side.

Not fixed. Not resolved. Not the arrival at a unified self that finally makes sense of everything. Something different and more useful than that: the ability to look back at the decisions you made from inside the management and understand them without the shame that the framework attached to them.

The relationship you could not sustain because it required a depth the managed version could not reach: not failure. The limit of what the performance could hold.

The friendship that faded because it needed something you did not know how to give: not failure. The cost of the management in the domain of connection.

The anger that came out sideways, that surprised the people around you with its force, that cost you things: not a character defect. The waiting parts speaking in the only language the framework left available to them.

This is not a reframe that excuses everything. It is a reframe that allows you to read your own life accurately for the first time. The plural self is not permission to avoid accountability. It is the recognition that the choices you made under the pressure of suppression were made by a person doing the most available thing: keeping the chorus alive by whatever means the situation allowed.

And now you get to do something different.

Portrait One: The Coach

You have been coaching for eleven years.

You are good at it. This is not in question. The boys come back, or their fathers do, and they tell you what you said that stayed with them, and you receive it with the warmth that is genuine and also something else, the awareness that what they are thanking you for is a version of you that you are always performing in that gym, in that role, among those families who have known you since you were twenty-eight and who have never once asked how you are doing in a way that expected a real answer.

You have a name for the feeling you get on Sunday evenings.

You call it the preparation. The assembling of the version of yourself the week requires. The coach who has it figured out. The man with the plan. The one the boys look to when things go sideways, which means you cannot go sideways, which means the part of you that sometimes wants to go sideways has learned to wait until the parking lot is empty and you are alone in your car and the performance is briefly suspended.

What happens in the car is not nothing.

You know this. You have known it for years in the way you know things you have decided not to examine: peripherally, carefully, with the specific skill of the person who has learned to look slightly past the thing rather than directly at it. In the car you are something other than the coach. Not better or worse. Larger. The version that did not become the player you were supposed to become and that still has not fully made peace with that. The version that got divorced and told everyone he was fine and mostly was and sometimes at eleven o'clock on a Wednesday was not fine at all. The version that reads more than he tells anyone he reads and thinks about things he has no one to think about them with.

That version is not a problem.

That version is the rest of the chorus.

The management has been running for eleven years because the room required it and you were good at it and the alternative was unclear. But the management is not free. You feel the cost in the specific quality of the Sunday evening assembly. In the distance that never quite closes with the people who are close to you. In the sense that the man everyone knows is real but is not the whole of what you are.

This chapter is about learning to conduct the whole of it.

Portrait Two: The Controlled Man

You have not cried in eleven years.

You know the exact number because you know the last time, which was your father's funeral, and you decided during it that you were done: not dramatically, not as a vow, just as the quiet conclusion of a person who had absorbed enough signals about what the crying meant in the rooms he needed to survive that the conclusion felt less like a choice than a fact of the terrain.

You are not a cold person. The people who know you well know this. The specific warmth you offer, the showing up, the practical care, the presence in the crisis: these are real and they are yours and they are not small. You have built a life that contains real love, expressed in real ways, and the life works by most available measures.

But there is a weather that you have not been in for eleven years.

You know it is still there because you sometimes feel the approach of it, the specific atmospheric pressure change that preceded the last time, and you feel yourself doing the thing you learned to do: the slight adjustment, the subtle management, the redirecting of the internal conditions before they become visible. You are very good at this. You have been practicing it for longer than eleven years.

What you have not examined carefully is what the management costs.

Not in the dramatic sense. In the cumulative sense. The energy that runs continuously in the background of a life being managed at that level. The distance it creates, not from others exactly, but from the full version of your own experience. The sense, arriving sometimes at the edges of things, that you are present but not quite inhabiting your own life. That the version of yourself you bring into the room is real but is not complete.

That sense is not a malfunction.

That sense is the rest of the chorus asking to be heard.

The weather you have not been in for eleven years is still yours.

It has been yours the whole time.

Portrait Three: The Manosphere Arrival

You found it at twenty-three.

You were working a job that paid poorly and felt temporary and probably was. You had had two relationships that ended in ways you did not fully understand and that left you with the specific confusion of someone who had done what he thought was expected and received an outcome he had not anticipated. You were not doing badly by objective measures. You were doing badly by the measure of what you had been told to expect, which was that if you were a decent person and worked hard and showed up, things would arrange themselves into a shape that felt like a life.

Things had not arranged themselves into that shape.

And then someone sent you a video.

The man in the video named something. He named the confusion. He named the jobs and the relationships and the gap between the expectation and the reality, and he named it in a language that felt like recognition, the specific relief of the person who has been carrying something unnamed and is finally handed the word for it. The word he gave you was: you were lied to. The lie being that the old arrangement was still operative, that decency and effort would produce the promised outcome, that the framework you had been handed was functional.

He was right about the lie.

The lie was real. The confusion was real. The gap between what you were told to expect and what you actually found was real and it had a cause and it deserved to be named.

He was wrong about almost everything else.

What came after the recognition was the explanation, and the explanation required an enemy, and the enemy required a war, and the war required a self that did not fracture, that had resolved the interior confusion by answering it rather than inhabiting it. The plurality you were feeling, the confusion and the grief and the genuine uncertainty about who you were supposed to be in a world that had changed the rules without telling you, was renamed weakness. The weakness was renamed choice. The solution was a harder, more defended, more singular version of the self you had already been trying to maintain.

The explanation took your real suffering and handed you a false cause and a false cure, and the cure was more of what was already costing you.

You stayed for a while.

You are reading this book, which means some part of you has already registered that the explanation was false. Not necessarily the whole of it. Not necessarily easily. But enough that you are here, with this book, looking for something that does not require you to foreclose the interior life in order to have a self.

The questions that sent you to that video are still your questions.

They were never wrong questions.

The answer you were given was wrong.

This book is a different answer to the same questions.

The History of the Chorus

William James wrote about this in 1890.

Not in these terms. James was a philosopher and a psychologist at a time when the two were not yet separate disciplines, and he wrote in the language available to him, which was more formal than the language of this book but no less precise. He said: a man has as many social selves as there are individuals who recognize him. He said the self was not a single thing but a relational one, shaped by context, by audience, by the specific demands of the room you were in. He said this in 1890 and the psychological establishment largely set it aside for the next hundred years in favor of the unified self, the stable core, the man who knows what he is.

The unified self was a cleaner theory.

It was also wrong.

Thirty years after James, the novelist and philosopher Marguerite Young began building what would become the most comprehensive literary argument for the plural self in the Western tradition. She spent eighteen years writing Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, twelve hundred pages that enact rather than argue the multiplicity of consciousness: a self that is never singular, never stable, never the same person twice, and not broken for any of it. Young's argument was that the plural self is not the product of trauma or dysfunction. It is the ordinary condition of being conscious. The self is plural before anything goes wrong. The multiplicity is not the wound. It is the weather.

Richard Schwartz found it in his clinical practice. Decades of work with the parts, each with its own history and its own logic and its own relationship to the whole, leading to the same conclusion Young had reached from the literary direction: that the goal is not unity. The goal is the capacity to host the full chorus.

And then Niobe Way spent decades in middle and high schools watching what happened to boys.

What she found was not the natural emergence of the stoic male self. What she found was its construction, under duress, across a specific developmental window. Boys at ten and eleven and twelve had intimate friendships, the kind that included the full range of emotional material, the fears and the hopes and the things that mattered. By sixteen and seventeen, under the sustained enforcement of the peer culture and the adult culture and the framework they were absorbing from every direction, most of those same boys had learned to close down that capacity.

The closing was not natural.

It was learned.

It was enforced.

It was the chorus being told to stop singing.

The chorus did not stop existing.

It went underground and waited.

Here is where Young's argument and Schwartz's clinical findings and Way's developmental research converge on the same truth from three different directions:

The plural self is what you were before the enforcement began.

The enforcement did not eliminate it.

The work is not the creation of something you never had.

The work is the recovery of what was always yours.

The Chorus Model

Here is what nobody tells you about the work of becoming yourself.

It is not harder than what you have already been doing.

It is easier.

The hard work, the work that has been costing you for years, is the suppression. The management. The daily labor of deciding which parts are allowed in the room and keeping the others quiet. That work runs in the background of everything: every conversation, every decision, every morning before you walk out the door. It is the noise you have gotten so used to that you stopped hearing it as noise and started hearing it as the sound of being a man.

It is not the sound of being a man.

It is the sound of a chorus being told to sing one part.

When the suppression lifts, even partially, even briefly, the first thing that arrives is not chaos. This is what the framework predicts when you imagine releasing the managed self: that everything held in check will come flooding out and the result will be disorder, the loss of the life you have built, the performance that everyone has been depending on suddenly unavailable. That prediction is understandable and it is wrong.

The first thing that arrives is quiet.

Not the quiet of absence. The quiet of a room where the fighting has stopped. The noise you didn't know you were living inside becomes audible in its departure. The pressure in the body, the low-grade tension of the management, releases. Something that has been braced relaxes.

And then: presence.

The ability to move through an ordinary day without the continuous background labor of the performance. To be in a conversation with the person you are actually in the conversation with rather than simultaneously monitoring which version of yourself is acceptable in that room. To make a decision from what you actually want rather than from what the managed self is permitted to want.

To discover, on a Tuesday morning, that you are interested in your own life.

This is what the Chorus Model produces.

Here is how it works.

The Chorus Model begins with a single replacement. You replace the performance question, am I doing this right, am I the right kind of man, with the listening question: who is speaking right now, and what does that part need?

The performance question assumes there is a correct version of the male self that you are either producing or failing to produce. The problem with this assumption is that the correct version is always slightly out of reach, always requiring more management, always generating the low-grade anxiety of the performance that cannot be sustained indefinitely.

The listening question assumes something different. It assumes that whoever is speaking right now is real. Not the whole of you. Not the final answer. But real, present, worth hearing. The part that is anxious before the difficult conversation is real. The part that is unexpectedly moved by something is real. The part that is still carrying something from twenty years ago is real. You do not have to resolve any of them into a unified position before you are allowed to act. You have to hear them.

Hearing is not the same as obeying.

This is the distinction the Chorus Model requires you to hold. The part that wants to burn everything down is real and worth hearing and you do not have to do what it says. The part that wants to stay small and defended and not risk anything is real and worth hearing and you do not have to do what it says either. The conductor of the chorus does not play every instrument simultaneously. The conductor listens to the whole and makes a choice about what the music needs right now.

You are the conductor.

Not the parts. Not the framework. Not the version of you that the job or the family or the culture requires. You, the one who can hear all of it and still choose.

The chorus is already assembled.

It has been assembled your whole life.

The work is not to reduce it to a soloist.

The work is to learn to conduct.

Practice: The Chorus

One.

Name three selves you have inhabited in the last year. Not roles. Selves. The one who showed up at the difficult family conversation. The one who exists only in certain friendships. The one who appeared during the crisis and surprised you with what it knew.

Give each one a name that isn't yours.

Write down what each one wanted. Not what you did about it. Not whether the want was reasonable. What it wanted. Do this without editing or justifying. The wanting is the information.

Two.

Which part of you is most in charge right now?

Not the part you wish were in charge. The one actually running things today, this week, this season of your life.

How long has it been running things? What is it protecting you from? Write one sentence for each answer. One sentence only. The constraint matters: you are not solving this, you are seeing it.

Three.

Write a letter from one of your older selves to the one you are now.

Not the self you hope to become. A self you have already been: the one from ten years ago, or twenty, or the one from the hardest year. What does that self know that you keep forgetting? What did it survive that you are still not giving it credit for?

Write the letter. Read it once. Do not revise it.

Coda: What the Chorus Knows

You cannot hear a chorus from inside one voice.

This is something singers know in the body before they know it in the mind. When you are inside your own part, following your own line through the piece, you hear yourself and the voices nearest to you and a general sense of the whole. But you do not hear what the chorus actually sounds like. That sound exists only at a distance. Only when you step back far enough to let all the voices reach you at once.

The conductor's position.

You have been inside one voice for a long time. The voice the framework required. The voice that was permitted in the rooms you needed to survive. You followed that line with everything you had because following it was what the situation demanded and you were good at it.

But you were never only that voice.

The other voices were present the whole time. The part that comes in unexpectedly in the third movement and changes what you thought the piece was about. The bass holding the foundation nobody notices until it's gone. The dissonance before the resolution, the most alive moment in the music because it contains the most possibility.

The parts of you that seem to contradict each other are not evidence of incoherence.

They are the dissonance before the resolution.

The novelist Marguerite Young knew this. She built it into the architecture of her work: the recursive sentences that return transformed, the voices that layer and interrupt and resolve and reopen, the themes that come back in the third movement and change what you thought the piece was about. She understood that the self is polyphonic not as metaphor but as fact.

In a chorus, no voice is the wrong voice.

The bass is not a failed soprano. Each voice is exactly what it is and the music requires all of it.

The chorus is already assembled.

It has been assembling your whole life.

It knows parts you have not heard yet.

It has been waiting, with the patience of every voice that has ever held its note in the dark, for you to step back far enough to hear what you actually are.

You are not one voice.

You never were.

Step back.

Listen.
