Chapter 20

On Chapter 20 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Christianity and Religion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 24, 2026

Characters: Miss MacIntosh

Allusions: Ancient Egypt · The Bible

“shall bind up the wounds in our sides”: “He healeth the broken in heart, and bindeth up their wounds” (Psalm 147:3). The wounds “in our sides” recall the wound in Christ’s side (John 19:34), which Thomas was told to touch (John 20:27). See The Bible.

"like Saul or Solomon or Sennacherib in the midst of our corruption" names three fallen powers, Saul the first king undone, Solomon the wise man turned to idols, Sennacherib the proud Assyrian struck down, all figures of greatness gone to decay.

“when we shall put on our shining armor”: “Let us put on the armour of light” (Romans 13:12), and “Put on the whole armour of God” (Ephesians 6:11). See The Bible.

“those who dwell in hidden caves beneath the overhanging, moss-grown rocks”: The kings and great men “hid themselves in the dens and in the rocks of the mountains; And said to the mountains and rocks, Fall on us” (Revelation 6:15-16). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.

“the experimental light of the first day of crazed creation”: “And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters. And God said, Let there be light: and there was light” (Genesis 1:2-3). See Genesis.

“There was no crowning glory of woman’s hair”: “If a woman have long hair, it is a glory to her: for her hair is given her for a covering” (1 Corinthians 11:15). See The Bible, Hair, Wig, and Baldness, and Women, Gender, and Marriage.

“the wheels within wheels”: Ezekiel’s vision: “their work was as it were a wheel in the middle of a wheel” (Ezekiel 1:16). See The Bible.

“Calvinistic rigors and denials and aggressions”: John Calvin (1509-1564) taught predestination, that God has chosen from eternity who will be saved. Calvinism shaped the Puritans and the Presbyterian and Reformed churches. Young sets its rigor against a woman’s “vague evasions and whimsicalities.” See Augustine and Christian Theology.

“this which was like sackcloth and ashes”: Mordecai “put on sackcloth with ashes” (Esther 4:1), and Christ says the cities “would have repented long ago in sackcloth and ashes” (Matthew 11:21). See The Bible.

“the digit or fingerbreadth, the tophach or handbreadth, the cubit, the fathom, the reed, the foot”: Miss MacIntosh’s lessons follow the tables of measures, weights, months, and Exodus stations printed in the back of many old Bibles. See Judaism. The digit, or fingerbreadth, was about three-quarters of an inch. The tophach, or handbreadth, was four fingers, about three inches. The table of the tabernacle has “a border of an hand breadth” (Exodus 25:25). The cubit ran from the elbow to the fingertip, about eighteen inches. The long, or royal, cubit was about twenty inches. The fathom is six feet, the span of outstretched arms. Paul’s sailors “sounded, and found it twenty fathoms” (Acts 27:28). The measuring reed of Ezekiel’s temple vision was “six cubits long” (Ezekiel 40:5), about nine to ten feet. John is given “a reed like unto a rod” to measure the temple (Revelation 11:1). A foot is a unit of measurement for length that is equal to 12 inches.

“the value of the Attic drachma, the gerah, the beka, the maneh, the talent”: The coins and weights of the Greeks and Hebrews, part of humanity’s effort to impose order and meaning on life. See Judaism. The Attic Drachma was a silver coin and monetary unit of ancient Athens. It was one of the world’s earliest coins and was used from the 6th century BC until the Roman period. It had the head of Athena on one side and an owl on the other. (Wikipedia) A gerah was an ancient Hebrew unit of weight and currency, the smallest the Hebrews used: “a shekel is twenty gerahs” (Exodus 30:13). The gerah also appears in Leviticus 27:25, Numbers 3:47 and 18:16, and Ezekiel 45:12. The beka was a unit of weight equal to half a shekel: “A bekah for every man, that is, half a shekel” (Exodus 38:26). It was used as a counterweight for the silver half shekel brought to the temple each year. Abraham’s servant gave Rebekah “a golden earring of half a shekel weight” (Genesis 24:22). In the Bible, maneh is a unit of weight that is sometimes translated as “pound.” It appears in Ezekiel 45:12, 1 Kings 10:17, Ezra 2:69, and Nehemiah 7:71-72. A maneh is estimated to weigh around 2.5 or 3 pounds. In the Bible, a talent was a unit of weight and money and it also appears in the parable of the talents. A talent weighed about 75 pounds (34 kilograms), or 3,000 shekels (“Bekahs, Shekels, and Talents,” Ensign, August 1987). The Israelites used 29 talents of gold to build the tabernacle (Exodus 38:24). A talent was worth 20 years of labor. The parable of the talents (Matthew 25:14-30) is about a master who entrusts his wealth to three servants while he goes away on a journey. The parable is often interpreted as a lesson on the responsible use of one’s abilities, resources, and gifts.

“I would learn the Jewish calendar”: The Jewish calendar is a lunisolar calendar, which means it’s based on both the moon and the sun. It has 12 months, with each month containing either 29 or 30 days. To keep it in step with the solar year, a leap month is added seven times in every 19 years, the Metonic cycle. The months are Nisan, Iyar, Sivan, Tammuz, Av, Elul, Tishrei, Heshvan, Kislev, Tevet, Shevat, and Adar. In leap years, Adar becomes Adar II and another month, Adar I, is added before it. (Wikipedia) The civil year begins on Rosh Hashanah, but Nisan is the first month of the religious year: “This month shall be unto you the beginning of months” (Exodus 12:2). “Jyar” is an old spelling of Iyar. The calendar’s structure ensures that Jewish holidays take place in the correct season. It is significant as a motif of disciplined learning, historical consciousness, and the search for order in a world otherwise marked by instability and dreamlike disorientation. See Judaism.

“the names of the antediluvian patriarchs”: The antediluvian patriarchs are the individuals listed in Genesis 5 who lived before the Noachian flood: Adam, Seth, Enosh, Kenan, Mahalalel, Jared, Enoch, Methuselah, Lamech, and Noah. (Wikipedia) They represent the lineage of humanity before the Great Flood. Methuselah lived “nine hundred sixty and nine years” (Genesis 5:27). See Genesis.

The "itinerary of the children of Israel from Egypt to Canaan," the stations Rameses, Succoth, Etham, and the rest, traces the Exodus route, the long wilderness march toward the promised land.

The questions about "the desert of El-Tih," and whether "some die... on their way from Egypt to Canaan, the blessed land," keep to that same journey and to the many who fell before reaching it.

“Better were two heads than one”: “Two heads are better than one,” in John Heywood’s proverbs (1546), and “Two are better than one” (Ecclesiastes 4:9). See Ecclesiastes.

“grey hair does not signify a necessary wisdom”: “The hoary head is a crown of glory, if it be found in the way of righteousness” (Proverbs 16:31), but “great men are not always wise: neither do the aged understand judgment” (Job 32:9). See The Book of Job.

"baldness as of a babe cast into purgatory" ties Miss MacIntosh's bare head to the limbo of the unbaptized, her lifelong baldness read as a soul suspended, never held against the creature that bears it.

“the dead tree of life”: “The tree of life also in the midst of the garden” (Genesis 2:9). See Genesis.

“Was God bald, too”: Children mocked Elisha, “Go up, thou bald head” (2 Kings 2:23), and the Ancient of Days has “the hair of his head like the pure wool” (Daniel 7:9). See The Bible and Hair, Wig, and Baldness.

“What was God, defined in so many terms”: Young’s litany gathers images of God and of Christ from across the Bible. The descriptions emphasize the profound mystery of divinity, the coexistence of opposites within the divine, and the limited capacity of human beings to grasp the full nature of God. See The Bible. “as a fuller’s soap, as a refining fire”: “He is like a refiner’s fire, and like fullers’ soap” (Malachi 3:2). A fuller cleaned and whitened cloth. “as the light of the morning when the sun arises, even as a dawn without clouds”: “As the light of the morning, when the sun riseth, even a morning without clouds” (2 Samuel 23:4). “as rivers of water in a dry place, as a hiding place from the wind”: “A man shall be as an hiding place from the wind… as rivers of water in a dry place, as the shadow of a great rock in a weary land” (Isaiah 32:2). “as a stone of grace”: Proverbs 17:8 reads “a precious stone” in the King James Version and “A stone of grace” in Young’s Literal Translation (1862). “a friend that sticketh closer than a brother” (Proverbs 18:24). “a brother born for adversity” and “a friend that loveth at all times” (Proverbs 17:17). “a root out of a dry ground” (Isaiah 53:2). “a nail fastened to a sure place”: “I will fasten him as a nail in a sure place” (Isaiah 22:23). “as the tender grass by clear shining after the rain” (2 Samuel 23:4). “as a casket”: In older English a casket is a small box for jewels, not a coffin. Compare “in that day when I make up my jewels” (Malachi 3:17). “as an ark”: In the Bible, the Ark of the Covenant was a symbol of God’s presence and a holy place where God dwelt with his people. Noah’s ark carried life through the Flood (Genesis 6-8). “as a crown of glory, as a diadem of beauty”: “In that day shall the Lord of hosts be for a crown of glory, and for a diadem of beauty” (Isaiah 28:5). “as a belt of stars”: Orion’s Belt: “Canst thou bind the sweet influences of Pleiades, or loose the bands of Orion?” (Job 38:31). See Astronomy and The Book of Job. “as the shadow of a great rock in a weary land” (Isaiah 32:2). “Was there the plant not planted by God?”: “Every plant, which my heavenly Father hath not planted, shall be rooted up” (Matthew 15:13). “Were there the strayed sheep?”: the parable of the lost sheep (Matthew 18:12; Luke 15:4). “Was there the relapsing demoniac?”: the unclean spirit that returns with “seven other spirits more wicked than himself” (Matthew 12:43-45).

A litany of images for God, "a fuller's soap," "a refining fire," "rivers of water in a dry place," "a hiding place from the wind," chains Malachi's purifying fire to Isaiah's water and shelter, God as cleanser, refuge, and relief.

A longer litany, "a friend that sticketh closer than a brother," "a root out of a dry ground," "a nail fastened to a sure place," "the shadow of a great rock in a weary land," strings biblical figures for God from Proverbs, Isaiah, and Samuel, loyalty and humble origin and firm footing and refuge, the divine turned over facet by facet.

"the plant not planted by God," "the strayed sheep," "the relapsing demoniac" gather three of Jesus's images, the false teaching uprooted (Matthew 15), the lost sheep sought and found (Luke 15), and the swept house reclaimed by worse spirits (Matthew 12).

"something which none should see and live to relate" reaches for Exodus 33, no one may see God and live, the vision too great to survive.
