Definition from research

Chapter 3

  • “the hard way which closed behind us, which had provided no way of turning back, of walking twice in the same path, of living once again in memory.” - This is a take on a quote by Heraclitus that no man ever steps in the same river twice, for it's not the same river and he's not the same man.

Chapter 3

  • The philosophy of impermanence or panta rhei or becoming is in Western philosophy it is most famously known through its first appearance in Greek philosophy in the writings of Heraclitus. Panta Rhei is translated as everything flows. Heraclitus was famous for his insistence on ever-present change as being the fundamental essence of the universe. “No man ever steps in the same river twice.”

Chapter 13

  • Heraclitus (Greek philosopher) main lessons: "The straight and the crooked path of the fuller's comb is one and the same"; "The way up is the way down"; "Beginning and end, on a circle's circumference, are common"; and "Thou shouldst unite things whole and things not whole, that which tends to unite and that which tends to separate, the harmonious and the discordant; from all things arises the one, and from the one all things." "Mortals are immortals and immortals are mortals, the one living the others' death and dying the others' life"; "As the same thing in us is living and dead, waking and sleeping, young and old. For these things having changed around are those, and those in turn having changed around are these"; and "Cold things warm up, the hot cools off, wet becomes dry, dry becomes wet." Diogenes Laërtius summarizes Heraclitus's philosophy as follows: "All things come into being by conflict of opposites, and the sum of things ('the whole') flows like a stream." Chapter 13

  • "We both step and do not step into the same river, we both are and are not" – Heraclitus Homericus, Homeric Allegories

Chapter 13

  • Heraclitus said "strife is justice" and "all things take place by strife". He called the opposites in conflict, "strife", and theorized that the apparently unitary state, "justice", results in "the most beautiful harmony"

Chapter 13

  • Aristotle said Heraclitus disagreed with Homer because Homer wished that strife would leave the world, which according to Heraclitus would destroy the world; "there would be no harmony without high and low notes, and no animals without male and female, which are opposites". It may also explain why he disagreed with the Pythagorean emphasis on harmony, but not on strife.

Chapter 13

  • Another of Heraclitus's famous sayings highlights the idea that the unity of opposites is also a conflict of opposites: "War is father of all and king of all; and some he manifested as gods, some as men; some he made slaves, some free"; war is a creative tension that brings things into existence. Heraclitus says further "Gods and men honour those slain in war"; "Greater deaths gain greater portions"; and "Every beast is tended by blows."

Chapter 38

  • Heraclitus and the Flux - he emphasized the constant state of flux and change in the universe, implying that perpetual motion is also a metaphor for the ever-changing nature of reality. Heraclitus’s philosophy in this context conveys that the world and ourselves are never static but perpetually evolving, and this continuous movement is both the source of life’s mystery and its challenge. The chapter meditates on how characters like Mr. Spitzer and Vera’s mother (Catherine) experience this flux (caught between reality and dreams, life and death, presence and oblivion) highlighting the fragile, transient, and often ambiguous nature of human existence.

Chapter 38

  • Parmenides and the Paradox of Motion - Parmenides, an ancient Greek philosopher, argued that change and motion are illusions and that reality is unchanging and indivisible. This paradox (the question of how motion and change can exist if reality is fundamentally fixed and eternal) echoes the novel's exploration of the fluid boundaries between life and death, reality and illusion, presence and absence. In this chapter, the narrative reflects on the elusive nature of being, the difficulty of grasping a stable self or reality amid constant flux and transformation. The novel discusses how perceptions and memories shift, how the self might be divided or multiple, and how time and space themselves seem unstable or illusory. The paradox of motion in the context of the novel underscores the characters' struggles with their own existence, the impermanence of life, and the search for meaning in a world that seems to continuously dissolve and reform, much like Parmenides' philosophical dilemma. The mention of Parmenides and the paradox of motion highlights the novel's deep engagement with questions about the nature of reality, the illusion of change, and the elusive quest for permanence amidst inevitable flux.

Chapter 38

  • Zeno’s paradoxes - the finite can contain the infinite. Neil deGrasse Tyson “there are an infinite number of steps to a finite destination”. Young discusses Zeno's paradoxes in the context of the characters’ reflections on life, death, and existence. They are trying to understand the idea of progress and the nature of time, for example, by questioning how two parallel lines might meet or how one might justify sorrows if one had experienced nothing. The chapter conveys a sense of confusion about the continuity of life and death and the elusive nature of reality. It refers to Zeno's paradoxes by highlighting the puzzlement about motion and the passage of time, such as the difficulty in knowing if one has truly moved forward or if one is caught in an endless cycle, like walking in circles or being lost between two polarities. They reflect on the idea of being between two worlds, the living and the dead, and the uncertainty of identity and existence, which echoes the philosophical challenge posed by Zeno's paradoxes regarding infinite divisibility and the nature of motion. This discussion illustrates the novel's broader themes of ambiguity, the interplay between reality and illusion, and the complexity of human consciousness and experience.

Chapter 44

  • “eternal transmutation” - Transmutation is change and alchemy is a process of elevation. There is a subtle difference between the two and both are associated with the idea of turning lead into gold. They’re often used as a metaphor for spiritual or personal growth. With transformation it is inevitable because life is in a constant state of flux. People are in a perpetual process of becoming. Another way of pointing to Thales of Miletus and Heraclitus. This concept aligns with the novel’s broader themes of illusion, identity, and the blurred boundaries between life and death, reality and dream. It highlights the paradox of permanence in impermanence or the idea that while individual forms or lives may end or change, the process of transformation is ongoing and eternal.

Chapter 52

  • This may refer to Heraclitus, a Greek philosopher, who proposed universal flux and unity of opposites. The universal flux statement is that it is not possible to step twice into the same river, or to come into contact twice with a mortal being in the same state. Unity of opposites statement points to ways things are the same and not the same over time. Things are interconnected but not identical. An example of this is ocean water is fine for fish but undrinkable for man.

Chapter 52

  • Mr. Spitzer's meticulous accounting and listing is directly tied to his struggle with his identity, particularly his relationship with his deceased brother. He seeks a perfect balance, a mirrored reflection where everything in his life corresponds to his brother's, suggesting a desire to understand himself through this dualistic lens. The fear of "no differing counterpart" speaks to the existential anxiety of lacking a defined self, relying on another for definition. This resonates with philosophical concepts of identity formation, the "self" versus the "other," and the influence of external relationships on self-perception. Thinkers like Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir explored similar themes in existentialism. Spitzer's obsessive listing and indexing of even the most mundane objects (pearl buttons, rusted nail heads, etc.) reflects a deep-seated need to impose order on a chaotic world. He's trying to find patterns, connections, and a "common denominator" to make sense of existence. This aligns with the philosophical pursuit of metaphysics: trying to understand the fundamental nature of reality. The futility of Spitzer's efforts mirrors the inherent difficulties in finding absolute order or meaning in a world often perceived as absurd or chaotic (a key theme in existentialism and absurdism). The questions about the sail ("Was the sail unwinding the same as the winding sail?") touch on the philosophical problem of identity over time. Does something remain the same if its state changes? This is further complicated by the cyclical nature of the tides and the wagon wheels, implying a sense of eternal return or repetition. This connects to Heraclitus's famous saying, "No man ever steps in the same river twice," emphasizing the constant flux and change in the universe. The paragraph also hints at Nietzsche's concept of eternal recurrence. Spitzer's attempts to capture reality through lists and measurements are ultimately limited. His fear that "all the weight, as he had often complained, was on this weightless star" alludes to the disparity between the tangible and intangible, the concrete and abstract. He can list the physical properties of objects, but he cannot capture their essence or meaning. This is related to epistemology, the study of knowledge and its limitations. The paragraph suggests the impossibility of fully knowing or representing reality, as there will always be something missing or lost in translation. The description of Spitzer's actions creating "division as in division there was creation," suggests that in every act of creation, there is also an act of destruction. This mirrors the belief that every action has an equal and opposite reaction. This is connected to the concept of the "Tao" which states that everything is composed of two opposites, and that without the other, it cannot exist.
