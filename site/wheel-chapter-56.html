Research notes: Chapter 56 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 56

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

"Like Peron, he was becoming all others, all except himself"

Mr. Spitzer's selfhood is structurally plural from the beginning. He dressed his dead brother in his own clothes, of his own dimensions, his own cut. The brothers are not merely similar; they are ontologically entangled. Peron wore the checkered suit; Mr. Spitzer wore the swallow-tails. But Mr. Spitzer asks:

"Or what if Mr. Spitzer should put on the brown derby, the checkered suit such as his brother had worn to a race-track, carry the bright cane? Would he be his brother?"

The question is not rhetorical. It is genuinely unanswerable. Not One Self here is not a metaphor for complexity of personality; it is a literal philosophical crisis about whether identity is constituted by clothing, by posture, by the rose one wears. The self is sartorial before it is essential. Mr. Spitzer exists as Mr. Spitzer only insofar as he wears the Prince Albert cutaway, the white rose, the Inverness cape:

"there was perhaps not even Mr. Spitzer in his black suit and cape"

Remove the garments and there is no remainder. Not One Self is not a richness here; it is a terror. He is simultaneously himself, his dead brother, a shadow, a copy, a counterfeit, a tailor's dummy, a puppet. All at once. Irresolvably.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

This is perhaps the chapter's most explicit philosophical argument, stated with almost doctrinal directness:

"Reality was that which was unreal and was clothed with the dream of reality, dream with dream, body of this chameleon dream"

The chapter does not merely suggest that illusion is pervasive; it argues that stripping away appearance produces not reality but nothing:

"if one should strip away the appearances, there should be no reality — no visible colors, no white rose if one should strip away the rose"

This is the larger cave more carefully lit. Mr. Spitzer's nightmare about being unable to distinguish propositions from furniture is the epistemological core of the chapter: the boundary between the conceptual and the material has collapsed. Golden mountains, baroque mirrors that mirror nowhere, the furniture of the dream; these are not failures of perception. They are the accurate report of a world where illusion is constitutive rather than derivative.

The philosopher's version: there is no sunlit world outside the cave. Mr. Spitzer's version: he cannot tell whether he is awake or dreaming, and has concluded the distinction may not hold.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

"his sorrows, tremulous as clouds with silver linings, were diffused and almost indefinite, vague of outline"

The chapter's great catalogue of mourning, spanning nearly two full pages, is one of the most extraordinary enactments of grief-as-climate in literary prose. Mr. Spitzer does not mourn for specific things in a sequence that moves toward resolution. He mourns everything simultaneously and permanently:

"he mourned for everything — for the dew drop trembling on the lip of the helmet flower, for the moth flying away from the flower, for the mirror of the flower"

Notice the structure: not just the dewdrop, but the moth leaving it, but also the mirror of the flower, the representation of the thing, not just the thing. Grief here has become so total it consumes not only the object but the object's reflection, its departure, its trace. This is grief that has no object because it has absorbed all objects.

The passage continues through tides, moles, bats, whales, swallows, lighthouses, whelks, dolphins, dogs, windows: an accumulation that never arrives at catharsis because catharsis is not the point. The point is that this is the climate of Mr. Spitzer's world since his brother's death. He does not move through it. He lives in it permanently.

"Since that day, he had never been the same, he verily believed"

The honest life stops pretending otherwise. Mr. Spitzer has stopped pretending.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The chapter itself, and Mr. Spitzer within it, performs the ethics of the Witness in a paradoxical way. Mr. Spitzer is simultaneously the one who sees and the one being seen, the observer and the observed, the mourner and the mourned. His practice is relentless attention to the world's dissolution:

"He was the anthropologist counting mollusks, trying to determine which had been mortal"

This is the Witness in its purest form: cataloguing without concluding, observing without fixing. The anthropologist does not solve the mollusks. He counts them. He tries to determine. The trying is the practice.

The chapter's refusal to simplify Mr. Spitzer's condition (is he alive or dead? is he himself or his brother? is he sane or mad?) is itself an act of witnessing. The narrative stays in the room with his irresolution rather than resolving it. When he asks:

"Ah, what crucible was this, he still must ask, or were there two deaths, one at either pole of life, or were there many deaths, more than even he knew?"

The answer is not provided. The question is the act. Attention without conclusion is the practice.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

"he had come to the conclusion that he was not real, for reality had forsaken him... and there was no bridge but that bridge one built by walking where there was no bridge"

This is the most precise statement of Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary in the chapter: you build the bridge by walking it, knowing there is no bridge, knowing the building is provisional. Mr. Spitzer returns to the beginning constantly (to his brother's funeral, to the clothes, to the question of identity, to the question of reality) not to resolve these returns but because the returning is itself the structure of his being.

He has paid the tailor, dressed the corpse, distributed the wardrobe, asked the questions, and then he does it again, in memory, in thought, in the chapter's own recursive prose structure which spirals back on itself like the pagoda shells it describes :

great pagodas "of many spirals spiralling toward the water-reflecting heavens"

The pagoda is the formal image of Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary: each level returns to the shape of the previous level, slightly transformed, never finished, never a failure.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter's prose enacts this pillar structurally. The sentences are themselves incomplete in their completion; they accumulate through dashes, through catalogues that never close, through questions that receive no answers. The famous inventory of mourning does not end with a period of resolution. It ends with more mourning.

Mr. Spitzer's coat is the material emblem of this pillar:

"this old coat which had been mended so many times... with the loosest basting stitches imaginable as life was temporary — so that he was about to lose his sleeve — or part of his being — or perhaps there had been no patch to cover the great hole — perhaps the hole was covered only with a greater hole"

The patch over the hole covered by a greater hole: this is Unfinished Is Honest made visible as textile. The demand for a final, complete repair would be violence against the coat's history, against the life it has registered. The basting stitches are honest. The finished seam would be a lie.

"he was like his brother now—like a man in his grave or in a stranger’s grave, yet though he lived on like the tattered clouds, the tattered birds"

Tattering is not failure. It is the record of duration.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Mr. Spitzer is the marginal figure made central, or rather, made the only figure. He is:

  • The man who dresses the dead in his own clothes
  • The man who gives away a dead man's wardrobe to homeless men in alleys
  • The man who cannot be sure whether he is alive or dead
  • The man who wears the same mourner's/bridegroom's suit for decades

But the chapter extends the marginal outward. The homeless men of Boston who receive Peron's jockeys' silks and harlequin suits; they are not background figures. They are the recipients of identity, the inheritors of selfhood's cast-offs. The dwarf's suit found in Peron's wardrobe :

"he wondered — who was the little man, as tiny as a flower, a snowflake, a moth? Should the things which were always dying die? Was the little man the one who could not die?"

The dwarf, the most marginal of marginal figures, becomes the philosophical crux. The miniature, the anomalous, the one who does not fit the standard dimensions: this is where the constructed nature of identity becomes most visible. Peron, a large man, owned a dwarf's suit. The categories do not hold.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This pillar is the chapter's geological substructure. Mr. Spitzer does not remember his brother's funeral as a past event; it presses upward through everything he does:

"He remembered that it had been his painful duty, after his lamented brother's death, to dispose of his brother's clothing"

But the remembering is not retrieval. It is eruption. The clothes distributed to homeless men years ago still surface:

"still to this day would sometimes catch a glimpse of a tattered silk or a bright rag"

The past is not behind him. It is beneath him, appearing in the streets of Boston as fragments of Peron's wardrobe worn by strangers. The earth itself is described in terms of this sedimentary pressure:

"The earth was moved by the moon like the tide in ripples and furrows — earth in an old orchard swelled, heaved like a woman in child labor. He had seen the hummocks in the soil which had moved like the moving waters"

The ground rises. What is buried pushes upward. Sedimentary time is not metaphor here; it is the literal behavior of the world Mr. Spitzer inhabits.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter is acutely aware of language's constructive and ultimately unstable role. Mr. Spitzer's nightmare about propositions is precisely a nightmare about language's failure to be neutral:

"he could not, for the life of him, distinguish propositions as to reality from tables and chairs and rose-colored divans"

Propositions, the basic units of linguistic truth-claiming, have become furniture. They have weight, dimension, solidity. The statement "There is not a golden mountain" does not dissolve the golden mountain; it creates a mysterious entity of the golden mountain, a something that must be defined by its nonexistence. Language that says there is not still conjures the is.

The chapter's own language performs this: the prose constructs Mr. Spitzer through accumulation, through the list, through the catalogue, forms that name their own location as provisional and ongoing rather than claiming the authority of the concluded statement. The dashes, the qualifications, the perhaps and sometimes and it seemed; these are honest language, language that knows it is constructing rather than reporting.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

"the dead musician lived, and he lived in spite of all that Mr. Spitzer could do to stop his singing, his creaking like the frozen clouds, the creaking mast-heads, the demented sea shells singing to themselves"

The sacred in this chapter is emphatically not elsewhere. It is in the horse barnacles, the wentletraps "like staircases to heaven", the luna moths on the beach, the Dog Star. These are not symbols pointing outward to a transcendent beyond; they are the sacred itself, immanent in the material world, pointing deeper into the world's own strangeness.

Mr. Spitzer's mourning catalogue is a liturgy of immanent transcendence: each item (the star-nosed mole, the flower-faced bat, the spouter whale) is treated with the reverence usually reserved for the divine, but the divinity is located in the thing, not beyond it. The empty lighthouse filled with dead moths is sacred not because it points to God but because it is, in itself, a complete image of life, death, guidance, failure, and beauty simultaneously.

The track bugler who played at Peron's grave, "former first trumpet of the Boston Symphony, former first trumpet of the Philadelphia Symphony", plays the starter music at a funeral. Racing and death and music collapse into a single immanent moment where the sacred is not separate from the ordinary but is the ordinary fully attended to.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is the pillar that most completely describes Mr. Spitzer's ontological condition. He is explicitly described not as a person moving through time but as a crossing point:

"he, old Joachim, was his own angelic sarcophagus and a great city and the city gates — was this world"

He is the city and its gates simultaneously: the container and the threshold, the past deposited in him and the future passing through him. He is constituted by what came before:

"He was the horse barnacles forming in constantly shifting patterns like the seething foam... and the great hermits, the great kings in their shells"

And he is constituting ground for what comes after: the clothing he distributed to the homeless men of Boston will be worn, will be seen as bright rags in alleys, will carry Peron's identity forward into lives neither brother knew.

The chapter's most devastating statement of this pillar:

"He only knew that he was Peron, his dead brother"

He is not Peron's mourner. He is not Peron's survivor. He is Peron, not through confusion but through the structural logic of the Crossing Point. The ancestors are in the sediment. Mr. Spitzer is the sediment. The carrying continues in spite of everything, in spite of his not knowing whether he is alive, in spite of the cables being broken:

"when the cables were broken, danced on with many jerks and whirls, not knowing that the cables were broken"
