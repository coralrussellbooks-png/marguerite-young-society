A shipwrecked traveller engages upon a spiritual endeavour or quest, journeying through the cosmos, to meet a primordial god, providing to the traveller a gift of moral vision with which to return to Egypt. Further, Richard Mathews writes that this "oldest fantasy text contains archetypal narrative of the genre: an uninitiated hero on a sea journey is thrown off course by a storm, encounters an enchanted island, confronts a monster, and survives, wiser for the experience…” The tale itself begins with a framing device in which an attendant ("follower" or "the sailor") tries to comfort his master, who is returning from an apparently failed expedition and is anxious about how the king will receive him. (The Land of Punt was an ancient kingdom, likely located in the Horn of Africa, that traded extensively with ancient Egypt. It was a source of valuable goods like gold, ebony, ivory, and aromatic resins, which were highly sought after by the Egyptians. While the exact location remains debated, current evidence points towards a coastal region encompassing parts of present-day Somalia, Djibouti, Eritrea, and northeast Sudan. The kingdom's existence is primarily known through Egyptian records and depictions, particularly from the Middle and New Kingdom periods.) An attendant reassures him, advising him on how to behave before the king, and repeating the proverb, “The mouth of a man saves him.” To encourage his master, he tells a tale of a previous voyage of his in which he overcame disaster, including meeting with a god and the king. The tale ends with the master saying, “Do not make the excellent (that is, do not act arrogantly) my friend; why give water to a goose (literally, bird) at dawn before its slaughtering in the morning?” A Middle Kingdom text, "The Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor," describes a star falling to earth, which led to the extinction of giant serpents on an island. This is sometimes interpreted as a folk memory of a meteorite impact as in the Netflix series Ancient Apocalypse. The sailor’s voyage appears to be a type of Otherworld Journey, a dangerous crossing-over to a divine or magical realm beyond the edge of the known world. “William K. Simpson observes that the Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor is ...one of the most interesting, straightforward, and yet puzzling compositions of ancient Egyptian literature. A curious emphasis is placed on numbers, the height of the wave, the dimensions of the ship, the number of sailors, the length of the serpent’s hood, the number of months of the sailor’s stay, and so on. It is almost as if the tale is an allegory involving the movement of stars. The Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor is considered to contain esoteric knowledge, much of it related to the serpent’s story. Whatever its meaning, this tragedy is enshrined as the central motif of the narrative: it is element X in a structure A-B-C-X-C'-B'-A'. Unlike all of the other motifs, its form is mythic rather than folkloric; it seems to be a religious allegory. As a mythic island (X) set in the dead centre of a sea of folktale elements (A-C and A'-C'), it may even be the “payload” of the whole story.”

  • Menpehtyre Ramesses I (or Ramses) was the founding pharaoh of ancient Egypt’s 19th Dynasty. The dates for his short reign are not completely known but the timeline of late 1292-1290 BC is frequently cited as well as 1295-1294 BC. While Ramesses I was the founder of the 19th Dynasty, his brief reign mainly serves to mark the transition between the reign of Horemheb, who had stabilized Egypt in the late 18th Dynasty, and the rule of the powerful pharaohs of his own dynasty, in particular his son Seti I, and grandson Ramesses II.
  • Ramesses II, commonly known as Ramesses the Great, was an Egyptian pharaoh. He was the third ruler of the Nineteenth Dynasty. Along with Thutmose III of the Eighteenth Dynasty, he is often regarded as the greatest, most celebrated, and most powerful pharaoh of the New Kingdom, which itself was the most powerful period of ancient Egypt.
  • Usermaatre Meryamun Ramesses III was the second pharaoh of the Twentieth Dynasty in ancient Egypt. Some scholars date his reign from 26 March 1186 to 15 April 1155 BC, and he is considered the last pharaoh of the New Kingdom to have wielded substantial power. He took Ramesses II’s throne name and modeled his temple at Medinet Habu on Ramesses II’s, one king dreaming another’s dreams.
  • “Rameses I, II, III”: The royal mummies were found in 1881 in a hidden tomb at Deir el-Bahri, where priests had moved and rewrapped them, some in other kings’ coffins. Shelley’s “Ozymandias” (1818) takes its name from the Greek form of Ramesses II’s throne name, User-maat-re.

  • “But why should any man weep if the heavens wept for him?” Found in Islamic, Ancient Egyptian, and Jewish tradition, it is a metaphorical way to express profound sorrow and the impact of a loss on a cosmic scale. Moses 7, Jeremiah 8:19, Genesis 6:6, John 11:35 It reflects a profound meditation on sorrow, mourning, and the vastness of grief that transcends individual human experience. This rhetorical question suggests that if the heavens themselves are mourning a person’s fate, then the individual weeping might seem redundant or insignificant in comparison. It evokes the idea that cosmic forces or the universe acknowledge and encompass the sorrow of human lives on a scale far greater than any one person could express. This notion ties into the novel’s theme of the interconnectedness of human suffering with the natural and cosmic order. The mourning of the heavens symbolizes a universal sympathy and recognition of loss, making individual grief a part of a larger, perhaps more meaningful, cosmic process. It also hints at the shared destiny of all beings, where personal sorrow is enveloped within the universal cycles of life and death. The question serves to elevate the concept of mourning beyond the personal, suggesting that human tears are part of a grander, universal lamentation that the heavens themselves embody. It invites the reader to contemplate the scale of grief and the place of individual suffering within the vastness of existence.

Chapter 49

  • porphyry is a type of igneous rock characterized by a distinctive texture that happens from a two-stage cooling process where magma cools slowly underground, forming the larger crystals, then erupts and cools rapidly at the surface, creating a fine-grained matrix. Porphyry is also known for its historical significance as a prized stone, particularly in ancient civilizations for sculpture and architecture. Porphyritic texture is the defining characteristic, indicating a complex cooling history. It involves two distinct crystal sizes: larger phenocrysts and a finer-grained groundmass. The phenocrysts form during the slow cooling of magma underground, while the fine-grained matrix forms during the faster cooling at the surface. Porphyry rocks are important hosts for mineral deposits, especially copper, molybdenum, and gold. Porphyry copper deposits, in particular, are economically significant sources of these metals. These deposits form when mineral-rich fluids concentrate in fractures around igneous intrusions. Porphyry, especially the purple variety, was highly prized in ancient civilizations like Egypt and Rome. It was used for sculpture, imperial architecture, and other prestigious objects. The color purple was associated with royalty and imperial power, further enhancing porphyry's value.

Chapter 55

  • Wealth, like many other things in the story, is portrayed as a form of illusion or "counterfeit," reflecting the transient and unstable aspects of life and human experience that the characters grapple with.The history of money is a journey from direct barter to symbolic representations of value, including commodity items, metal coins, paper notes, and modern digital currencies. This evolution was driven by the need for a more efficient and reliable medium of exchange as societies grew more complex. For most of early human history, transactions were based on barter or the direct exchange of goods and services. Early societies began to use easily tradable items with inherent value, known as commodity money. Around 9000– 6000 B.C., cattle, sheep, and camels were among the first forms of money. They were both a medium of exchange and a symbol of wealth. From around 1200 B.C., cowrie shells became the most widely and longest-used currency in history, particularly in Asia and Africa. Their durability, size, and scarcity made them a popular choice. Salt, grain, and even whale teeth were used as currency in various cultures. The use of precious metals marked a significant step in money's history, offering a more durable and standardized medium of exchange. As early as the 3rd millennium B.C. in Egypt and Mesopotamia, standardized weights of gold, silver, and other metals were used for trade. The Lydian kingdom (in modern-day Turkey) is widely credited with issuing the first standardized and officially stamped coins around 650–600 B.C.. Made from electrum (a gold and silver alloy), these coins featured a royal symbol that guaranteed their weight and value. Around the same time, Chinese mints produced metal coins in the shape of spades and knives. The invention of paper money was a solution to the inconvenience of carrying heavy metal coins for large transactions. Chinese merchants would deposit their metal coins with goldsmiths and receive a paper receipt, which they then used for purchases. This early system was known as "flying money". During the Song Dynasty (1020s), the Chinese government took control of the system, issuing the first official paper currency. Inspired by Marco Polo's accounts of Chinese paper money, Europeans began adopting it in the 17th century. Sweden was the first to introduce European-style banknotes in 1661. The formal backing of currency evolved from commodities to government decree. To control inflation and ensure stability, governments in the 19th and early 20th centuries adopted the gold standard, where they issued paper money redeemable for a fixed amount of gold. After World War II, many countries pegged their currencies to the US dollar, which was, in turn, fixed to gold. In the 1970s, this system was abandoned, and money became a fiat currency. Its value is not derived from a physical commodity but from a government decree and public trust in the issuing authority. The rapid evolution of technology has pushed money into the digital realm, from traditional systems to decentralized networks. Starting in the 1950s, modern credit cards allowed for cashless purchases, with the first successful general-purpose card being the Diners Club Card in 1950.

Chapter 56

  • “herring-bone” Herringbone fabric has ancient roots, first seen in Roman road construction (Opus spicatum) and later in Egyptian textiles, with the name coming from the V-shaped herring fish bones. While its textile use began in the 19th century, the fabric gained prominence in the early 20th century for its use in tough, practical hunting and workwear, like the US Army's herringbone twill (HBT) uniforms in the 1940s, and became associated with formal wear in the 1950s.

Chapter 56

  • “basket” Basket weaving is an ancient craft, with some of the oldest examples dating back over 10,000 years in Egypt and the Middle East, predating pottery and cloth weaving. Made from readily available natural materials like reeds, twigs, grasses, and bark, baskets were essential for gathering, storing, and transporting food and goods for early humans. This versatile craft has been integral to cultures worldwide, including Native American communities and African societies, and remains a significant cultural and artistic practice today.

Chapter 57

  • “bird-cages” date to antiquity, first appearing in Mesopotamia, Egypt, and China for symbolic, religious, and wealth display purposes, with the term "bird cage" emerging in late 15th century Europe. During the 16th and 17th centuries, exotic birds became status symbols, leading to ornate, expensive cages in Europe, a trend that peaked in the Victorian era with elaborate designs. The use of birds in cages has also served practical functions, such as navigation by sailors and safety checks in mines, though their primary role shifted from food source to domestic pets and decorative objects.

Chapter 57

  • Sphinx began in ancient Egypt, where sphinxes were depicted as mythical guardians, typically with a lion's body and a human head, often wearing a pharaoh's headdress. The Great Sphinx of Giza, a monumental statue carved from the bedrock, is the most famous example, though its exact construction date and builder are still debated, with most theories placing it between 2600 and 2500 BCE. While the Great Sphinx primarily served as a spiritual guardian for the pyramids and monuments of Giza, the concept of the sphinx also appeared in Greek mythology, where it was a more monstrous and treacherous figure.

  • The Cow Who Jumped over the Moon is an English nursery rhyme. There are numerous theories about the origin of the rhyme. James Orchard Halliwell suggested that it was a corruption of ancient Greek, probably advanced as a result of a deliberate hoax. It was connected with Hathor worship. It refers to various constellations (Taurus, Canis Minor, etc.). It describes the Flight from Egypt (the Exodus) or the founding myth of the Israelites. It depicts Elizabeth, Lady Katherine Grey, and her relationships with the earls of Hertford and Leicester. It deals with anti-clerical feeling over injunctions by Catholic priests for harder work. It describes Catherine of Aragon or Catherine I of Russia, the wife of Peter the Great or Canton de Fidèle, a supposed governor of Calais and the game of cat, a popular English pup game. This profusion of unsupported explanations was satirized by J. R. R. Tolkien in his fictional explanations of "The Man in the Moon Stayed Up Too Late". Most scholarly commentators consider these to be unproven and state that the verse is probably meant to be simply nonsense.
