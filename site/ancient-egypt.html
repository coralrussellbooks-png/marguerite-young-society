Chapter 2

  • The Egyptian Sailor may refer to the “Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor”. A shipwrecked traveller engages upon a spiritual endeavour or quest, journeying through the cosmos, to meet a primordial god, providing to the traveller a gift of moral vision with which to return to Egypt. Further, Richard Mathews writes that this "oldest fantasy text contains archetypal narrative of the genre: an uninitiated hero on a sea journey is thrown off course by a storm, encounters an enchanted island, confronts a monster, and survives, wiser for the experience…” The tale itself begins with a framing device in which an attendant ("follower" or "the sailor") tries to comfort his master, who is returning from an apparently failed expedition and is anxious about how the king will receive him. (The Land of Punt was an ancient kingdom, likely located in the Horn of Africa, that traded extensively with ancient Egypt. It was a source of valuable goods like gold, ebony, ivory, and aromatic resins, which were highly sought after by the Egyptians. While the exact location remains debated, current evidence points towards a coastal region encompassing parts of present-day Somalia, Djibouti, Eritrea, and northeast Sudan. The kingdom's existence is primarily known through Egyptian records and depictions, particularly from the Middle and New Kingdom periods.) An attendant reassures him, advising him on how to behave before the king, and repeating the proverb, “The mouth of a man saves him.” To encourage his master, he tells a tale of a previous voyage of his in which he overcame disaster, including meeting with a god and the king. The tale ends with the master saying, “Do not make the excellent (that is, do not act arrogantly) my friend; why give water to a goose (literally, bird) at dawn before its slaughtering in the morning?” A Middle Kingdom text, "The Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor," describes a star falling to earth, which led to the extinction of giant serpents on an island. This is sometimes interpreted as a folk memory of a meteorite impact as in the Netflix series Ancient Apocalypse. The sailor’s voyage appears to be a type of Otherworld Journey, a dangerous crossing-over to a divine or magical realm beyond the edge of the known world. “William K. Simpson observes that the Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor is ...one of the most interesting, straightforward, and yet puzzling compositions of ancient Egyptian literature. A curious emphasis is placed on numbers, the height of the wave, the dimensions of the ship, the number of sailors, the length of the serpent’s hood, the number of months of the sailor’s stay, and so on. It is almost as if the tale is an allegory involving the movement of stars. The Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor is considered to contain esoteric knowledge, much of it related to the serpent’s story. Whatever its meaning, this tragedy is enshrined as the central motif of the narrative: it is element X in a structure A-B-C-X-C'-B'-A'. Unlike all of the other motifs, its form is mythic rather than folkloric; it seems to be a religious allegory. As a mythic island (X) set in the dead centre of a sea of folktale elements (A-C and A'-C'), it may even be the “payload” of the whole story.”

Chapter 11

  • Menpehtyre Ramesses I (or Ramses) was the founding pharaoh of ancient Egypt’s 19th Dynasty. The dates for his short reign are not completely known but the timeline of late 1292-1290 BC is frequently cited as well as 1295-1294 BC. While Ramesses I was the founder of the 19th Dynasty, his brief reign mainly serves to mark the transition between the reign of Horemheb, who had stabilized Egypt in the late 18th Dynasty, and the rule of the powerful pharaohs of his own dynasty, in particular his son Seti I, and grandson Ramesses II.
  • Ramesses II, commonly known as Ramesses the Great, was an Egyptian pharaoh. He was the third ruler of the Nineteenth Dynasty. Along with Thutmose III of the Eighteenth Dynasty, he is often regarded as the greatest, most celebrated, and most powerful pharaoh of the New Kingdom, which itself was the most powerful period of ancient Egypt.
  • Usermaatre Meryamun Ramesses III was the second pharaoh of the Twentieth Dynasty in ancient Egypt. Some scholars date his reign from 26 March 1186 to 15 April 1155 BC, and he is considered the last pharaoh of the New Kingdom to have wielded substantial power. He took Ramesses II’s throne name and modeled his temple at Medinet Habu on Ramesses II’s, one king dreaming another’s dreams.
  • “Rameses I, II, III”: The royal mummies were found in 1881 in a hidden tomb at Deir el-Bahri, where priests had moved and rewrapped them, some in other kings’ coffins. Shelley’s “Ozymandias” (1818) takes its name from the Greek form of Ramesses II’s throne name, User-maat-re.
  • “partitioned into many selves without his knowing it”: The Egyptians held that a person was made of many parts: the ka, the life force; the ba, the soul that flies from the tomb; the name; the shadow; and the heart, weighed at judgment.
  • “pyramid-shaped ices”: Ices were molded into shapes for grand dinners. The pyramids Catherine serves come just before the pharaoh arrives.

Chapter 20

  • “the itinerary of the children of Israel from Egypt to Canaan”: The Israelites’ journey from Egypt to Canaan involved a circuitous route through the wilderness, lasting approximately 40 years. Chapter 26

  • “the reincarnation of Cleopatra”: Cleopatra VII (69-30 BC), the last ruler of Ptolemaic Egypt and Mark Antony’s lover. Belief in reincarnation runs through Hinduism, and through Theosophy, founded in New York in 1875.
  • “a Sphinx in her garden… a greater pyramid than her neighbor could afford”: The Great Sphinx and the Great Pyramid of Khufu at Giza. Egyptian Revival garden ornaments were a fashion after Napoleon’s expedition to Egypt (1798).
  • “Egyptian securities, and New England shipping”: Egypt’s foreign debts led to the Anglo-French Dual Control (1876) and the British occupation of 1882. Egyptian bonds were a favorite of cautious Victorian investors.
  • “eating from golden dishes fit for the dead pharaohs”: In ancient Egypt, cats were sacred to the goddess Bastet and were mummified by the hundreds of thousands.
  • “like an old mummified pharaoh wrapped in his body which was his boat”: Pharaohs were buried with boats to carry them through the afterlife. Khufu’s cedar ship was found beside the Great Pyramid in 1954.

Chapter 49

  • “the cat miaowed”: “Miaow” is the older spelling of the cat’s cry; earlier forms miaow, miau, and meaw date from the 1630s, and “meow” is recorded from 1842 (Online Etymology Dictionary). It is miau in Spanish and German and miaou in French. Cats in Ancient Egypt were called mau, after their cry.
  • “But why should any man weep if the heavens wept for him?”: In the Bible God grieves and Jesus weeps: “it grieved him at his heart” (Genesis 6:6); “Oh that my head were waters, and mine eyes a fountain of tears” (Jeremiah 9:1); “Jesus wept” (John 11:35). In the Book of Moses of the Latter-day Saints, “the God of heaven looked upon the residue of the people, and he wept” (Moses 7:28). The Quran says of Pharaoh’s drowned people, “And the heaven and the earth wept not for them” (44:29, trans. Marmaduke Pickthall, 1930), and the Egyptians said the Nile rose with the tears of Isis for Osiris (Pausanias, Description of Greece 10.32.18). If the heavens themselves mourn a person’s fate, the individual weeping might seem redundant or insignificant in comparison: cosmic forces acknowledge and encompass human sorrow on a scale greater than any one person could express. Mourning rises beyond the personal, and personal sorrow is enveloped in the universal cycles of life and death.
  • “the porphyry clouds”: Porphyry, from the Greek porphyra, purple, is an igneous rock with large crystals set in a fine-grained matrix, the mark of two-stage cooling. The purple porphyry of Egypt was prized by Rome and Byzantium for imperial sculpture and architecture, since purple was the color of royalty, and a Byzantine emperor’s child born in the palace’s porphyry chamber was porphyrogenitus, “born in the purple.” See Porphyry of Tyre, above.

Chapter 53

  • Evidence suggests that ancient Egyptians, Greeks, and Romans utilized various forms of punching bags for combat training. These bags were often crafted from materials like leather and filled with sand or other readily available materials. Ancient Chinese and Japanese civilizations also used animal skin punching bags filled with sand or grain for martial arts training. Indian wrestling traditions also employed practice equipment called "mukha dhanda," which were essentially hanging leather pouches filled with sand, pebbles, or grain.

Chapter 53

  • Ancient Egyptian wrestling, depicted in tomb paintings and sculptures, showcased a variety of holds and techniques, many of which are still recognizable in modern wrestling styles. These depictions, particularly those at Beni Hasan, reveal over 4000-year-old techniques, including throws, takedowns, and ground grappling, emphasizing both athletic prowess and strategic combat training.

Chapter 55

  • Wealth, like many other things in the story, is portrayed as a form of illusion or "counterfeit," reflecting the transient and unstable aspects of life and human experience that the characters grapple with.The history of money is a journey from direct barter to symbolic representations of value, including commodity items, metal coins, paper notes, and modern digital currencies. This evolution was driven by the need for a more efficient and reliable medium of exchange as societies grew more complex. For most of early human history, transactions were based on barter or the direct exchange of goods and services. Early societies began to use easily tradable items with inherent value, known as commodity money. Around 9000– 6000 B.C., cattle, sheep, and camels were among the first forms of money. They were both a medium of exchange and a symbol of wealth. From around 1200 B.C., cowrie shells became the most widely and longest-used currency in history, particularly in Asia and Africa. Their durability, size, and scarcity made them a popular choice. Salt, grain, and even whale teeth were used as currency in various cultures. The use of precious metals marked a significant step in money's history, offering a more durable and standardized medium of exchange. As early as the 3rd millennium B.C. in Egypt and Mesopotamia, standardized weights of gold, silver, and other metals were used for trade. The Lydian kingdom (in modern-day Turkey) is widely credited with issuing the first standardized and officially stamped coins around 650–600 B.C.. Made from electrum (a gold and silver alloy), these coins featured a royal symbol that guaranteed their weight and value. Around the same time, Chinese mints produced metal coins in the shape of spades and knives. The invention of paper money was a solution to the inconvenience of carrying heavy metal coins for large transactions. Chinese merchants would deposit their metal coins with goldsmiths and receive a paper receipt, which they then used for purchases. This early system was known as "flying money". During the Song Dynasty (1020s), the Chinese government took control of the system, issuing the first official paper currency. Inspired by Marco Polo's accounts of Chinese paper money, Europeans began adopting it in the 17th century. Sweden was the first to introduce European-style banknotes in 1661. The formal backing of currency evolved from commodities to government decree. To control inflation and ensure stability, governments in the 19th and early 20th centuries adopted the gold standard, where they issued paper money redeemable for a fixed amount of gold. After World War II, many countries pegged their currencies to the US dollar, which was, in turn, fixed to gold. In the 1970s, this system was abandoned, and money became a fiat currency. Its value is not derived from a physical commodity but from a government decree and public trust in the issuing authority. The rapid evolution of technology has pushed money into the digital realm, from traditional systems to decentralized networks. Starting in the 1950s, modern credit cards allowed for cashless purchases, with the first successful general-purpose card being the Diners Club Card in 1950.

Chapter 55

  • “hieroglyph” a stylized picture of an object representing a word, syllable, or sound, as found in ancient Egyptian and other writing systems.

Chapter 56

  • “herring-bone” Herringbone fabric has ancient roots, first seen in Roman road construction (Opus spicatum) and later in Egyptian textiles, with the name coming from the V-shaped herring fish bones. While its textile use began in the 19th century, the fabric gained prominence in the early 20th century for its use in tough, practical hunting and workwear, like the US Army's herringbone twill (HBT) uniforms in the 1940s, and became associated with formal wear in the 1950s.

Chapter 56

  • “basket” Basket weaving is an ancient craft, with some of the oldest examples dating back over 10,000 years in Egypt and the Middle East, predating pottery and cloth weaving. Made from readily available natural materials like reeds, twigs, grasses, and bark, baskets were essential for gathering, storing, and transporting food and goods for early humans. This versatile craft has been integral to cultures worldwide, including Native American communities and African societies, and remains a significant cultural and artistic practice today.

Chapter 57

  • “bird-cages” date to antiquity, first appearing in Mesopotamia, Egypt, and China for symbolic, religious, and wealth display purposes, with the term "bird cage" emerging in late 15th century Europe. During the 16th and 17th centuries, exotic birds became status symbols, leading to ornate, expensive cages in Europe, a trend that peaked in the Victorian era with elaborate designs. The use of birds in cages has also served practical functions, such as navigation by sailors and safety checks in mines, though their primary role shifted from food source to domestic pets and decorative objects.

Chapter 57

  • Sphinx began in ancient Egypt, where sphinxes were depicted as mythical guardians, typically with a lion's body and a human head, often wearing a pharaoh's headdress. The Great Sphinx of Giza, a monumental statue carved from the bedrock, is the most famous example, though its exact construction date and builder are still debated, with most theories placing it between 2600 and 2500 BCE. While the Great Sphinx primarily served as a spiritual guardian for the pyramids and monuments of Giza, the concept of the sphinx also appeared in Greek mythology, where it was a more monstrous and treacherous figure.

Chapter 65

  • In the Bible, Ham, one of Noah's three sons, had four sons: Cush, Mizraim, Phut, and Canaan, who became ancestors of peoples in Africa and the Middle East, particularly Ethiopia, Egypt, Libya, and Canaan (modern Israel/Palestine). The story in Genesis details how Ham saw his father Noah naked while drunk, leading Noah to curse Ham's son, Canaan, rather than Ham himself, a narrative known as the Curse of Ham. Chapter 71

  • The Cow Who Jumped over the Moon is an English nursery rhyme. There are numerous theories about the origin of the rhyme. James Orchard Halliwell suggested that it was a corruption of ancient Greek, probably advanced as a result of a deliberate hoax. It was connected with Hathor worship. It refers to various constellations (Taurus, Canis Minor, etc.). It describes the Flight from Egypt (the Exodus) or the founding myth of the Israelites. It depicts Elizabeth, Lady Katherine Grey, and her relationships with the earls of Hertford and Leicester. It deals with anti-clerical feeling over injunctions by Catholic priests for harder work. It describes Catherine of Aragon or Catherine I of Russia, the wife of Peter the Great or Canton de Fidèle, a supposed governor of Calais and the game of cat, a popular English pup game. This profusion of unsupported explanations was satirized by J. R. R. Tolkien in his fictional explanations of "The Man in the Moon Stayed Up Too Late". Most scholarly commentators consider these to be unproven and state that the verse is probably meant to be simply nonsense.

Chapter 73

  • Chaldeans may refer to Chaldean Catholics who are adherents of the Chaldean Catholic Church, which originates from the historic Church of the East. The Chaldean community originates from groups of adherents of the Church of the East that entered into communion with the Roman Catholic Church in the 16th and 17th centuries. They were indigenous to the regions of northern Iraq, southeastern Turkey, northeastern Syria and northwestern Iran, many have migrated to Western countries including the United States, Canada, Mexico, Australia, Sweden and Germany. Many of them also live in Lebanon, Egypt, Palestine, Israel, Jordan, Iran, Turkey, and Georgia.

Chapter 73

  • Ancient Egyptian civilization, emerging around 3100 B.C. after the unification of Upper and Lower Egypt, thrived for over 3,000 years along the Nile River. Known for pharaohs, hieroglyphs, and monumental architecture like the pyramids. This advanced society was sustained by the Nile's predictable floods. The history is divided into Old, Middle, and New Kingdoms.

Chapter 79

  • A "dark woman" who assisted with births ,may refer to the following: 1) the Biblical midwife Shiphrah, who was a Nubian woman mentioned in the Book of Exodus. Shiphrah, along with her colleague Puah, defied the Pharaoh's order to kill newborn Hebrew males. 2) Biddy Mason, who was born into slavery, was a midwife and advocate for other enslaved people in 19th-century California. After gaining her freedom, she became a successful entrepreneur and philanthropist. 3) Margaret Charles Smith was an African American midwife in Alabama who delivered over 3,000 babies during a career spanning over 30 years, often in rural areas with limited healthcare access. 4) Mary Francis Hill Coley was a midwife from Georgia who delivered over 3,000 babies during her career. Her work was featured in the 1952 documentary, All My Babies. 5) Susie Carey was a third-generation midwife featured in a photograph at the National Museum of African American History and Culture. 6) Sarah Hudson Grant was a Black Southern midwife whose work is remembered today.
