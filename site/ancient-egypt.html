Chapter 2

  • The Egyptian Sailor may refer to the “Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor”. A shipwrecked traveller engages upon a spiritual endeavour or quest, journeying through the cosmos, to meet a primordial god, providing to the traveller a gift of moral vision with which to return to Egypt. Further, Richard Mathews writes that this "oldest fantasy text contains archetypal narrative of the genre: an uninitiated hero on a sea journey is thrown off course by a storm, encounters an enchanted island, confronts a monster, and survives, wiser for the experience…” The tale itself begins with a framing device in which an attendant ("follower" or "the sailor") tries to comfort his master, who is returning from an apparently failed expedition and is anxious about how the king will receive him. (The Land of Punt was an ancient kingdom, likely located in the Horn of Africa, that traded extensively with ancient Egypt. It was a source of valuable goods like gold, ebony, ivory, and aromatic resins, which were highly sought after by the Egyptians. While the exact location remains debated, current evidence points towards a coastal region encompassing parts of present-day Somalia, Djibouti, Eritrea, and northeast Sudan. The kingdom's existence is primarily known through Egyptian records and depictions, particularly from the Middle and New Kingdom periods.) An attendant reassures him, advising him on how to behave before the king, and repeating the proverb, “The mouth of a man saves him.” To encourage his master, he tells a tale of a previous voyage of his in which he overcame disaster, including meeting with a god and the king. The tale ends with the master saying, “Do not make the excellent (that is, do not act arrogantly) my friend; why give water to a goose (literally, bird) at dawn before its slaughtering in the morning?” A Middle Kingdom text, "The Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor," describes a star falling to earth, which led to the extinction of giant serpents on an island. This is sometimes interpreted as a folk memory of a meteorite impact as in the Netflix series Ancient Apocalypse. The sailor’s voyage appears to be a type of Otherworld Journey, a dangerous crossing-over to a divine or magical realm beyond the edge of the known world. “William K. Simpson observes that the Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor is ...one of the most interesting, straightforward, and yet puzzling compositions of ancient Egyptian literature. A curious emphasis is placed on numbers, the height of the wave, the dimensions of the ship, the number of sailors, the length of the serpent’s hood, the number of months of the sailor’s stay, and so on. It is almost as if the tale is an allegory involving the movement of stars. The Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor is considered to contain esoteric knowledge, much of it related to the serpent’s story. Whatever its meaning, this tragedy is enshrined as the central motif of the narrative: it is element X in a structure A-B-C-X-C'-B'-A'. Unlike all of the other motifs, its form is mythic rather than folkloric; it seems to be a religious allegory. As a mythic island (X) set in the dead centre of a sea of folktale elements (A-C and A'-C'), it may even be the “payload” of the whole story.”

Chapter 11

  • Menpehtyre Ramesses I (or Ramses) was the founding pharaoh of ancient Egypt’s 19th Dynasty. The dates for his short reign are not completely known but the timeline of late 1292-1290 BC is frequently cited as well as 1295-1294 BC. While Ramesses I was the founder of the 19th Dynasty, his brief reign mainly serves to mark the transition between the reign of Horemheb, who had stabilized Egypt in the late 18th Dynasty, and the rule of the powerful pharaohs of his own dynasty, in particular his son Seti I, and grandson Ramesses II.
  • Ramesses II, commonly known as Ramesses the Great, was an Egyptian pharaoh. He was the third ruler of the Nineteenth Dynasty. Along with Thutmose III of the Eighteenth Dynasty, he is often regarded as the greatest, most celebrated, and most powerful pharaoh of the New Kingdom, which itself was the most powerful period of ancient Egypt.
  • Usermaatre Meryamun Ramesses III was the second pharaoh of the Twentieth Dynasty in ancient Egypt. Some scholars date his reign from 26 March 1186 to 15 April 1155 BC, and he is considered the last pharaoh of the New Kingdom to have wielded substantial power. He took Ramesses II’s throne name and modeled his temple at Medinet Habu on Ramesses II’s, one king dreaming another’s dreams.
  • “Rameses I, II, III”: The royal mummies were found in 1881 in a hidden tomb at Deir el-Bahri, where priests had moved and rewrapped them, some in other kings’ coffins. Shelley’s “Ozymandias” (1818) takes its name from the Greek form of Ramesses II’s throne name, User-maat-re.
  • “partitioned into many selves without his knowing it”: The Egyptians held that a person was made of many parts: the ka, the life force; the ba, the soul that flies from the tomb; the name; the shadow; and the heart, weighed at judgment.
  • “pyramid-shaped ices”: Ices were molded into shapes for grand dinners. The pyramids Catherine serves come just before the pharaoh arrives.

Chapter 17

  • “old pharaohs voyaging to heaven upon clouds of incense”: The Pyramid Texts tell of the king rising to heaven on the smoke of incense.

Chapter 20

  • “the itinerary of the children of Israel from Egypt to Canaan”: The Israelites’ journey from Egypt to Canaan involved a circuitous route through the wilderness, lasting approximately 40 years. The Israelites’ long journey is often attributed to their disobedience, doubt, and lack of faith in God’s leadership. God led them by a longer route to protect them from enemies and allow them time to develop their faith and trust.

Chapter 22

  • “Where is Cleopatra’s Needle?”: Cleopatra’s Needle is the popular name for each of a pair of ancient Egyptian obelisks re-erected in London and New York City during the nineteenth century.

Chapter 26

  • “the reincarnation of Cleopatra”: Cleopatra VII (69-30 BC), the last ruler of Ptolemaic Egypt and Mark Antony’s lover. Belief in reincarnation runs through Hinduism, and through Theosophy, founded in New York in 1875.
  • “a Sphinx in her garden… a greater pyramid than her neighbor could afford”: The Great Sphinx and the Great Pyramid of Khufu at Giza. Egyptian Revival garden ornaments were a fashion after Napoleon’s expedition to Egypt (1798).
  • “Egyptian securities, and New England shipping”: Egypt’s foreign debts led to the Anglo-French Dual Control (1876) and the British occupation of 1882. Egyptian bonds were a favorite of cautious Victorian investors.
  • “eating from golden dishes fit for the dead pharaohs”: In ancient Egypt, cats were sacred to the goddess Bastet and were mummified by the hundreds of thousands.
  • “like an old mummified pharaoh wrapped in his body which was his boat”: Pharaohs were buried with boats to carry them through the afterlife. Khufu’s cedar ship was found beside the Great Pyramid in 1954.

Chapter 26 · The Siamese Cats

  • Symbolize mystery, immortality, and the blurred boundary between life and death. Their dominance in the Boston lady’s house reflects ongoing life amid death.

Chapter 26 · The Pyramid and the Sphinx

Chapter 29

  • “many other gods, the god of the sun, the goddess of the moon, the judge of the dead, the busy clerk of the underworld”: The list of gods and goddesses symbolizes the novel’s exploration of the boundaries between life and death, reality and illusion, and the universal archetypes that inform human experience and consciousness. It enriches the novel’s mythic texture and reflects the fragmented, liminal state of the characters, especially Vera’s mother.
  • “the bed of Charlemagne, the amethyst bed of Catherine the Great”: Historical people in the chapter are symbolic bridges between the personal and the universal, the real and the mythic, the past and the present. They enrich the novel’s exploration of identity, memory, mortality, and the elusive nature of reality, reinforcing its complex, dreamlike atmosphere and thematic depth.

Chapter 30

  • Salah ad-Din Yusuf ibn Ayyub or Saladin was the founder of the Ayyubid dynasty. Hailing from a Kurdish family, he was the first sultan of both Egypt and Syria. He was an important figure of the Third Crusade. At the height of his power, the Ayyubid realm spanned Egypt, Syria, Upper Mesopotamia, the Hejaz, Yemen, and Nubia.

Chapter 39

  • “bevies including the Egyptians and the Florentines”: Egyptians are from Egypt.
  • “a skull grinning at a banquet table”: At Egyptian banquets, Herodotus says, a man carried round a wooden corpse in a coffin to remind the guests that they would be like it when dead (Histories 2.78).

Chapter 40

  • “the language of the Nubian boy”: Nubians are an ethnolinguistic group of people indigenous to what is now present-day Sudan and Egypt.
  • “the hieroglyphic language of the drowned Egyptian sailor”: Egyptian sailor may refer to the Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor. The proverbs from this story are: the mouth of a man saves him; do not act arrogant; why give water to a bird before it is slaughtered. It is interpreted as a shipwrecked traveler goes on a spiritual quest through the cosmos and meets a primordial god that provides the traveler with a moral vision to return home with.
  • “so many pharaohs in swan boats passing with the sentinel moon”: The pharaohs’ boats recall the solar barque of Ra, which carried the sun god across the sky by day and through the underworld by night.

Chapter 42

  • “an ancient pharaoh sleeping in a swan boat”: See the solar barque of Ra in the Chapter 40 resources.

Chapter 43

  • “Did she lift her veil embroidered with the stars”: The veiled goddess of Sais declared, “I am all that has been, and is, and shall be, and my robe no mortal has yet uncovered” (Plutarch, On Isis and Osiris 9, trans. Frank Cole Babbitt, 1936).

Chapter 48

  • “a small pharaoh in his tomb or boat”: The pharaoh was buried with a boat to sail with the sun through the underworld. In 1954 the cedar ship of Khufu, 143 feet long, was found dismantled in a sealed pit beside the Great Pyramid.

Chapter 49

  • “the cat miaowed”: “Miaow” is the older spelling of the cat’s cry; earlier forms miaow, miau, and meaw date from the 1630s, and “meow” is recorded from 1842 (Online Etymology Dictionary). It is miau in Spanish and German and miaou in French. Cats in Ancient Egypt were called mau, after their cry.
  • “But why should any man weep if the heavens wept for him?”: In the Bible God grieves and Jesus weeps: “it grieved him at his heart” (Genesis 6:6); “Oh that my head were waters, and mine eyes a fountain of tears” (Jeremiah 9:1); “Jesus wept” (John 11:35). In the Book of Moses of the Latter-day Saints, “the God of heaven looked upon the residue of the people, and he wept” (Moses 7:28). The Quran says of Pharaoh’s drowned people, “And the heaven and the earth wept not for them” (44:29, trans. Marmaduke Pickthall, 1930), and the Egyptians said the Nile rose with the tears of Isis for Osiris (Pausanias, Description of Greece 10.32.18). If the heavens themselves mourn a person’s fate, the individual weeping might seem redundant or insignificant in comparison: cosmic forces acknowledge and encompass human sorrow on a scale greater than any one person could express. Mourning rises beyond the personal, and personal sorrow is enveloped in the universal cycles of life and death.
  • “the porphyry clouds”: Porphyry, from the Greek porphyra, purple, is an igneous rock with large crystals set in a fine-grained matrix, the mark of two-stage cooling. The purple porphyry of Egypt was prized by Rome and Byzantium for imperial sculpture and architecture, since purple was the color of royalty, and a Byzantine emperor’s child born in the palace’s porphyry chamber was porphyrogenitus, “born in the purple.” See Porphyry of Tyre, above.

Chapter 50

  • “not even the book of death”: The Egyptian Book of the Dead, the Book of Coming Forth by Day, was a collection of spells written on papyrus and buried with the dead from about 1550 BCE to guide them through the underworld.

Chapter 51

  • “more hieroglyphs to the alphabet of reality”: Egyptian hieroglyphs could not be read until Jean-François Champollion deciphered them from the Rosetta Stone in 1822.

Chapter 52

  • Saint Maurice, commander of the Theban Legion raised in Roman Egypt, was martyred in the late third century at Agaunum, now Saint-Maurice in the Swiss canton of Valais, for refusing orders to persecute Christians; his historicity is not certain. A statue of about 1240 in Magdeburg Cathedral is the earliest known depiction of him as a Black African knight in armor, and he was venerated across Europe. Black knights could also be called this because of the color of their armor. A black knight is a literary stock character who masks his identity and that of his liege by not displaying heraldry. Black knights are usually portrayed as villainous figures who use this anonymity for misdeeds. They are often contrasted with the knight-errant (white knight). The character appeared in Arthurian literature and has been adapted by various authors, in film and popular culture, and is sometimes associated with death or darkness.

Chapter 53

  • “a man who was a Bridge of Sighs, a Bridge of Lies, a lake of fire”: Bridge of Sighs is a bridge in Venice, Italy. Built about 1600 over the Rio di Palazzo, it led prisoners from the Doge’s Palace to the prisons, and their sighs at a last view of Venice gave it its name. Bridge of Lies is a legendary pedestrian bridge in Sibiu, Transylvania, Romania. Lake of fire is a place of after-death punishment in Egyptian and Christian religions. “And death and hell were cast into the lake of fire. This is the second death” (Revelation 20:14, KJV).
  • Evidence suggests that ancient Egyptians, Greeks, and Romans utilized various forms of punching bags for combat training. These bags were often crafted from materials like leather and filled with sand or other readily available materials. Ancient Chinese and Japanese civilizations also used animal skin punching bags filled with sand or grain for martial arts training. Indian wrestling traditions also employed practice equipment called “mukha dhanda,” which were essentially hanging leather pouches filled with sand, pebbles, or grain.
  • “old Egyptian wrestling holds”: Ancient Egyptian wrestling, depicted in tomb paintings and sculptures, showcased a variety of holds and techniques, many of which are still recognizable in modern wrestling styles. These depictions, particularly those at Beni Hasan, reveal over 4000-year-old techniques, including throws, takedowns, and ground grappling, emphasizing both athletic prowess and strategic combat training.

Chapter 55

  • “a mere hieroglyph which had dropped from the alphabet of heaven”: “hieroglyph” a stylized picture of an object representing a word, syllable, or sound, as found in ancient Egyptian and other writing systems.

Chapter 57

  • “a Sphinx had settled on his coat sleeve”: This sphinx is a sphinx moth, or hawk moth, named for the caterpillar’s habit of rearing up its head like the Great Sphinx. See The Life of the Sphinx Moth in the Chapter 55 resources.

Chapter 61

  • “The Egyptians had put their dead into swan boats under the sand”: Boats were buried with Egyptian kings for the voyage of the dead; Khufu’s cedar boat, buried beside the Great Pyramid, was found in 1954.

Chapter 63

  • “this, the missing breast which gave her the pain”: The Edwin Smith Papyrus, an Egyptian surgical text of about 1600 BC copying older sources, describes bulging tumors of the breast and says there is no treatment.

Chapter 64

  • The Egyptian Book of the Dead is an ancient Egyptian funerary text generally written on papyrus and used from the beginning of the New Kingdom (around 1550 BCE) to around 50 BCE.

Chapter 65

  • “Weep not, Hagar, for thy little son”: According to the Bible, Hagar was the Egyptian slave of Sarah, Abram’s wife. Sarah had been barren for a long time and sought a way to fulfill God’s promise that Abram would be father of many nations, especially since they had grown old, so she offered Hagar to Abram to be his concubine. Hagar bore Abraham’s son, Ishmael. “What aileth thee, Hagar? fear not” (Genesis 21:17, KJV). Where the King James has “he will be a wild man” (Genesis 16:12), the Hebrew reads “a wild ass of a man,” as Mr. Bonebreaker has it; see Ishmael in the Chapter 62 resources.
  • “the Egyptians were spoiled”: “And they spoiled the Egyptians” (Exodus 12:36, KJV): the Israelites, leaving Egypt, carried off the Egyptians’ silver and gold.
  • “he had dwelled for a long time in the Land of Ham”: In the Psalms the land of Ham is Egypt: “Israel also came into Egypt; and Jacob sojourned in the land of Ham” (Psalm 105:23, KJV).

Chapter 66

  • “that a path would open through the sea for her”: “And Moses stretched out his hand over the sea… and made the sea dry land, and the waters were divided” (Exodus 14:21, KJV).
  • “a pillar of clouds by day, a pillar of fire by night”: “And the LORD went before them by day in a pillar of a cloud, to lead them the way; and by night in a pillar of fire, to give them light” (Exodus 13:21, KJV). The “pillar of clouds by day, a pillar of fire by night” alludes to the biblical story of the Israelites’ exodus from Egypt, where God guided them through the wilderness by a pillar of cloud during the day and a pillar of fire by night. This symbolizes divine guidance, protection, and presence through times of uncertainty and hardship.

Chapter 71

  • “The Cow Who Jumped over the Moon”: The Cow Who Jumped over the Moon is the English nursery rhyme “Hey Diddle Diddle.” There are numerous theories about the origin of the rhyme. James Orchard Halliwell suggested that it was a corruption of ancient Greek, probably advanced as a result of a deliberate hoax. It was connected with Hathor worship. It refers to various constellations (Taurus, Canis Minor, etc.). It describes the Flight from Egypt (the Exodus) or the founding myth of the Israelites. It depicts Elizabeth, Lady Katherine Grey, and her relationships with the earls of Hertford and Leicester. It deals with anti-clerical feeling over injunctions by Catholic priests for harder work. It describes Catherine of Aragon or Catherine I of Russia, the wife of Peter the Great or Canton de Fidèle, a supposed governor of Calais and the game of cat, a popular English pup game. This profusion of unsupported explanations was satirized by J. R. R. Tolkien in his fictional explanations of “The Man in the Moon Stayed Up Too Late.” Most scholarly commentators consider these to be unproven and state that the verse is probably meant to be simply nonsense. (Wikipedia)

Chapter 73

  • Ancient Egyptian civilization, emerging around 3100 B.C. after the unification of Upper and Lower Egypt, thrived for over 3,000 years along the Nile River. Known for pharaohs, hieroglyphs, and monumental architecture like the pyramids. This advanced society was sustained by the Nile’s predictable floods. The history is divided into Old, Middle, and New Kingdoms.

Chapter 76

  • “not like Cleopatra”: Cleopatra may refer to Cleopatra VII Philopator who was Queen of the Ptolemaic Kingdom of Egypt and its last active ruler. Her death in 30 B.C., by the bite of an asp smuggled in a basket of figs as Plutarch tells it, is history’s most famous unsolved case of murder or suicide.

Chapter 79

  • “This third person, this dark woman”: A “dark woman” who assisted with births may refer to the following: 1) the Biblical midwife Shiphrah, one of the “Hebrew midwives” of Exodus 1:15, though some readers have taken her and Puah for Egyptians. Shiphrah, along with her colleague Puah, defied the Pharaoh’s order to kill newborn Hebrew males. 2) Biddy Mason, who was born into slavery, was a midwife and advocate for other enslaved people in 19th-century California. After gaining her freedom, she became a successful entrepreneur and philanthropist. 3) Margaret Charles Smith was an African American midwife in Alabama who delivered over 3,000 babies during a career spanning over 30 years, often in rural areas with limited healthcare access. 4) Mary Francis Hill Coley was a midwife from Georgia who delivered over 3,000 babies during her career. Her work was featured in the 1952 documentary, All My Babies. 5) Susie Carey was a third-generation midwife featured in a photograph at the National Museum of African American History and Culture. 6) Sarah Hudson Grant was a Black Southern midwife whose work is remembered today.
