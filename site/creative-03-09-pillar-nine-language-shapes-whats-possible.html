Pillar: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible · Across every book

Or, Why Grammatical Rebellion Can Set You Free

Words are never just neutral tools for describing the world. Marguerite Young used language as the very brick and mortar we use to build reality, and changing how we talk is a vital political act.

We dug deep into sedimentary time, exploring how the past is never truly behind us but rather accumulated right beneath our feet like layers of rock. Now we arrive at a crucial question: how do we talk about what we discover? What language do we use when we dig into the sediment? And does the language we have been given even allow us to tell the truth?

We tend to think of language as a neutral delivery truck. We imagine that facts and thoughts exist perfectly formed inside our heads, and we simply use words as a vehicle to ship those ideas over to someone else. We assume that standard grammar is just a set of harmless rules designed to keep our speech orderly and clear. We use language without thinking about it, as if the words are transparent and we can look right through them to the reality beneath.

But Marguerite Young steps in to show us that language is never neutral. It is not a transparent window. It is the active medium in which our reality is built, argued over, and either honored or betrayed. The grammar we use is not innocent. The words we choose are not arbitrary. The sentence structures we inherit carry an entire philosophy of what the world is and what is possible. Change the language, and you change what people can think.

The Trap of the Noun

To explain this pillar, the text connects Young's writing with the experimental insights of author Gertrude Stein. Stein spent her entire career proving that standard English grammar comes pre-packaged with massive assumptions about how the universe works.

Think about how standard sentences are set up: the primary building block of English grammar is the noun. Nouns represent solid, unchanging things or people. A noun sits there. It has identity. It has definition. A woman is a noun. A building is a noun. A nation is a noun. Verbs, on the other hand, are treated as secondary. They are just the actions that happen to or between those solid nouns. The woman does something. The building stands. The nation acts. In this grammatical structure, the verbs are servants of the nouns. The actions are less important than the things.

This structural setup quietly trains our brains to believe that the world is made of fixed, permanent objects that hold perfectly still. It makes us think that there are things that have stable identity, the noun, and then there are the temporary actions that happen to those things, the verb. This is a profound metaphysical claim. It is a claim about what exists and what is real. And it is built right into our grammar.

Authoritarian systems absolutely love this noun-heavy mindset. They rely heavily on a rigid grammar of control, using flat, simplified labels to categorize human beings. In the grammar of dominant political theories, you are required to be a unified, predictable agent: a single, solid noun. You are a woman or a man. You are a worker or a boss. You are a citizen or a criminal. You are loyal or disloyal. If your identity is complex, shifting, or does not fit into their pre-made bins, standard political language simply erases you from the conversation. The language cannot hold you. You become unspeakable.

Young combats this linguistic trap by turning her writing into a magnificent grammatical rebellion. She refuses to work in the grammar of nouns and passive verbs. Instead, she builds a grammar of process and becoming.

She writes in verbs. Her prose treats the self and the world as an ongoing process of becoming rather than a collection of static things. People are not fixed identities. They are processes. They are movements. They are verbs. Instead of describing what someone is, Young describes what they are doing, what they are becoming, what they are in the middle of. The sentence does not settle. It keeps moving.

She embraces the loop. The sentences in her book are famously long, circular, and packed with layers, resisting the pressure to settle into easy, simplified answers. A sentence in her novel might go on for a page, looping back on itself, adding new material, revising what it just said, refusing to arrive at a final meaning. The form enacts the philosophy. The endless sentence is a refusal to be finished, to be reduced, to be simplified into a single meaning.

She gives voice to the erased. By breaking traditional sentence structures, she constructs a fresh grammar capable of holding the full, messy truth of our inner multitudes. She finds a way to write about complexity that standard grammar could not contain. She finds a way to make the unspeakable speakable.

Stein did similar work in her poetry and essays. She wrote phrases like a rose is a rose is a rose, not as a celebration of fixed identity but as an investigation into how meaning changes each time we speak it. The rose is not a noun with a fixed definition. Each time you say rose, you are creating a new meaning. The repetition is not stale. It is generative. It is fresh.

The Danger of Speaking from Nowhere

Propaganda thrives when leaders pretend to speak from nowhere. When an authoritarian figure claims to speak with absolute, objective, universal authority, they are using that pretense as a tool of total domination. They are claiming that what they are saying is not a perspective. It is not a partial point of view. It is truth itself. It is objective reality. It has no location. It is not spoken by anyone. It is simply true.

Young's philosophy demands that we pull our language back down to earth. She insists that all speech is located. All speech comes from a body. All speech comes from a specific moment and a specific place. True accountability happens when we explicitly name our location, acknowledge that our perspectives are partial, and build our speech from the honest ground where we are actually standing.

When a writer or a leader says I am speaking from here, from this place, from this position, with these limitations, they are disarming the propaganda machine. They are saying: I do not claim to speak for everyone. I am not objective. I am not detached. What I am saying is rooted in where I stand. And you get to decide whether what I am saying resonates with where you stand.

Young's own stories do not pretend to be detached or universal. They are deeply rooted in the specific. The narrator of her novel is a specific woman, living in a specific place, shaped by specific relationships. The reader always knows where the speaker is standing. The reader always understands that this is one perspective, one location, one partial view. This honesty about location is what allows the complexity to breathe. Because the reader is not being asked to accept one universal truth. They are being invited into a specific location and asked to witness what is visible from there.

When we keep our language flexible, grounded, and honest about its own location, we destroy the machine's ability to manipulate our thoughts. A person who has learned to ask where is this speaker standing is a person who cannot be hypnotized by propaganda. They can never again accept a claim to speak from nowhere, to speak with absolute authority, to speak for everyone.

The Body That Speaks: Adrienne Rich

To understand what located speech actually costs and what it makes possible, consider Adrienne Rich, who spent the first decade of her career writing in a language that was not quite hers and the rest of it figuring out why.

Rich began publishing poetry in the early 1950s. Her first book won the Yale Younger Poets prize. The poems were technically accomplished, carefully measured, formally correct. W.H. Auden selected the manuscript and praised its craft. By every standard the literary establishment recognized, she had arrived.

But Rich knew something was wrong with those early poems. She could feel it. The language was elegant and controlled, and it was also, she later wrote, a performance of detachment she did not actually feel. She was writing in the voice of no one in particular, from nowhere specific, in the tradition of a literary culture that had been built largely by men who treated the universal as their natural inheritance. She had learned to speak from nowhere because nowhere was what the tradition rewarded. A woman who spoke from her specific body, her specific life, her specific location as a woman in a particular marriage in a particular era in a particular country, was not writing literature. She was writing confession, or complaint, or something lesser.

It took her years to understand that the nowhere voice was not neutral. It was a costume. And wearing it had cost her something she needed back.

When she started writing the poems that would become Snapshots of a Daughter-in-Law in the late 1950s, she began, haltingly, to speak from somewhere. From the specific experience of being a woman in a culture that had plans for her that she had not made herself. The poems were messier than her early work. Less polished. More uncomfortable. The literary establishment was not entirely pleased. The universal had been interrupted by the particular, and the particular, when it is a woman's body and a woman's fury, tends to make people nervous.

She kept going. By the time she wrote Diving into the Wreck in 1973 and Of Woman Born in 1976, she had rebuilt her practice from the ground up. She was writing from her body, from her history, from her specific location as a woman and eventually as a lesbian, from the sediment of everything that had shaped her. The language she made from that location was not smaller than the nowhere language she had started with. It was larger. It was more honest. It had more grip.

Her essay "Notes Toward a Politics of Location," written in 1984, named what she had been doing all along. She argued that all knowledge is embodied, that all speech comes from somewhere, that the claim to speak from nowhere is not neutrality but a specific kind of power move, available mainly to those whose location the culture has decided to treat as the default human position. When a white Western man speaks from "the universal," he is not transcending his location. He is hiding it. He is using the privilege of his position to make his particular view appear to have no view at all.

Rich's insistence on located speech was not a retreat into the personal. It was an expansion of what counts as political. The body is political. The household is political. The specific history of a specific woman in a specific place is political. To speak from there, honestly and without apology, is to refuse the nowhere voice that power depends on. It is to say: I am not speaking for everyone. I am speaking from here. And from here, I can see things the nowhere voice cannot see and will not name.

A person who has learned to ask where is this speaker standing cannot be captured by propaganda that claims to speak from nowhere. A political system that insists on located speech, that names its interests and its position and its partial view, is a system that can be held accountable. It cannot hide behind the claim of universal truth. It is always a someone, standing somewhere, making choices that could have been made differently. That accountability is exactly what power tries to avoid, and exactly what Rich spent her life making impossible to avoid.

The Practice

Notice when you use totalizing, flat words to describe yourself or the people around you. Labels like I am a failure, or they are just lazy. These are nouns, fixed identities, simplified categories. Catch those rigid nouns and try replacing them with active, located verbs instead.

Instead of a fixed label, describe the specific, messy process of what is happening right now in your unique corner of the world. Instead of I am a failure, try I have not yet figured this out, or I am learning how to do this, or this particular thing did not work and I am adjusting. Instead of they are lazy, try they are tired, or they are struggling with something I do not fully understand, or their values around work are different from mine.

Pay attention to the way these grammatical shifts change what becomes possible to think. When you speak in verbs instead of nouns, suddenly people become complex again. They become alive. They become changeable. They become human instead of fixed categories.

Also notice when language is being used to hide location. When a politician or a news anchor speaks as if they are stating objective facts from nowhere, pause. Remember Rich's politics of location. Ask: where is this person standing? What is their interest? What location are they speaking from? What are they not naming about their own position?

And pay attention to where you are standing when you speak. Try beginning sentences with a location: from where I sit, in my experience, from what I have seen, in this particular place. This honesty about location does not weaken your argument. It strengthens it. It makes it possible to listen to you, because you are not claiming to speak for everyone. You are claiming to speak truthfully from where you are.
