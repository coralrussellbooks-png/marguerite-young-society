Chapter 3: The Truest Thing in the House

Research notes: Chapter 3

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Madge Capehorn · Miss MacIntosh · Moses Hunnecker · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Illusion and Reality · Women, Gender, and Marriage

Symbols: Twins and Doubles

Philosophy: Augustine and Christian Theology · Heraclitus and Flux · Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Angels and Archangels · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · The History of Opium

By now you have spent two chapters in the fog traveling on that grey bus, inside Vera’s circling mind, watching Moses Hunnecker drink and pray, watching Madge sleep her resistant, unhappy sleep. You have had glimpses of Miss MacIntosh, heard her name, understood that she is the reason for this journey. But you haven’t met her yet. Not really.

Chapter 3 is where you meet her.

And she is worth the wait.

What actually happens in Chapter 3

Catherine Cartwheel, Vera’s mother and the opium addict who never leaves her bed, has been confined to her room and her hallucinations for some time now. Someone needs to look after Vera. The family lawyer, Mr. Joachim Spitzer, hires a nursemaid: Miss Georgia MacIntosh, from What Cheer, Iowa.

That is the external event. The chapter is Vera’s portrait of her.

Vera describes Miss MacIntosh’s appearance: her marcelled red hair, her greyish-green eyes, her sensible shoes (size eight, like a man’s), her black umbrella, her serge dress, her corset laced tight. She describes her habits: the early morning swims in the surf regardless of weather, the insistence on schedules and physical exercise, the mending of clothes, the building of fires. She describes her philosophy: hard work, self-forgetfulness, the acceptance of necessary deprivations, and a bone-deep suspicion of illusion in any form. She describes an encounter with an Australian bushman who has wandered into Catherine’s household and become obsessed with Miss MacIntosh’s head (he wants to sculpt it) and whom Miss MacIntosh dispatches with contempt.

And then, at the end of the chapter, Vera tells us what she already told us in Chapter 1: Miss MacIntosh is gone. Her clothes were found on the beach. Her purse contained two pennies. Her body was never recovered. Vera blames herself.

The chapter is both introduction and elegy. Young gives us Miss MacIntosh whole, luminous, and entirely herself and then reminds us she is already lost.

What Vera is thinking about

Everything in this chapter is filtered through Vera’s love for this woman and her grief at losing her.

Think about what Vera’s life looked like before Miss MacIntosh arrived. Her mother lived inside hallucinations, receiving imaginary visitors, lost in opium visions of poets and grandeur. The household was full of dreamers, illusionists, and people who had decided that reality was optional. Vera had no one to tell her what was real.

And then: Miss Georgia MacIntosh. A woman who swam in cold water. Who mended things when they tore. Who said that life was made up of necessary deprivations and that you should not waste your time chasing golden apples that weren’t there. Who did not believe in six-winged seraphim or furnished heavens or any of the other elaborate fantasies that the people around Vera used to avoid the fact of being alive.

For Vera, this was not austerity. It was rescue.

So when she describes Miss MacIntosh’s red hair catching the light like sparks, or her umbrella raised against the wind like a challenge, or the way she used a jar of fireflies as a lamp in the dark, these are not just memories. They are acts of preservation. If she can make Miss MacIntosh vivid enough in language, she hasn’t entirely lost her.

This is, quietly, what the novel is doing. Young wrote Miss MacIntosh, My Darling over eighteen years. It is 1,198 pages long. A large part of what drives it is exactly this: the refusal to let someone disappear.

The two women at the center of the book

Chapter 3 is the first place where Young lays out the novel’s central opposition in full, and everything that follows depends on it.

Catherine Cartwheel lives entirely inside illusion. Her opium visions are not mere escapism, they are her whole world. She receives guests who are not there. She inhabits a reality she has constructed and furnished to her own specifications. She is, in a certain light, the novel’s portrait of what happens when the imagination is given everything and the real world nothing.

Miss MacIntosh is her perfect opposite. She believes in what can be touched, worn out, scheduled, and endured. She does not dream, or if she does, she does not trust her dreams. She is the novel’s embodiment of the real, of the position that life requires us to see it clearly, accept its hardships, and do the work.

Young holds these two women in the same story for a reason. She is not asking you to choose between them. She is asking you to hold both: to feel the pull of Miss MacIntosh’s hard, clear-eyed practicality and the pull of Catherine’s bottomless inner world and to sit with the question of what it costs to live at either extreme.

Vera grew up between them. She loves them both. The novel is her attempt to make sense of what that did to her.

The Australian bushman, and what he’s doing here

He seems to come from nowhere this figure who has wandered into Catherine’s household, who has some connection to the wider world of the book’s strange cast, and who fixates on Miss MacIntosh with an intensity she finds contemptible.

He wants to sculpt her head. He corners her. He speaks to her with what Young calls a “calculated, incalculable eloquence” a beautiful phrase and she is completely unimpressed.

Miss MacIntosh sees through pretense immediately. She does not care about his eloquence or his obsession. She dispatches him.

But notice what Young is doing here: the bushman is interested in Miss MacIntosh’s head specifically. Not her practicality, not her values, not the whole woman, just the head. He wants to possess a piece of her, reduced to an aesthetic object. And she refuses. This refusal is entirely in character, and it is also, given what the novel will eventually reveal about Miss MacIntosh’s body and her secrets, quietly significant. Young plants these seeds without calling attention to them. Trust that they will matter.

There is also a feminist dimension worth naming: the bushman feels entitled to her attention regardless of her feelings. He interrupts, corners, and pursues. Miss MacIntosh is having none of it. In 1965, when this novel was published, that was a more radical stance than it might appear now.

The disappearance

Young does not save this for later. We already know, from the novel’s opening pages, that Miss MacIntosh is gone. Chapter 3 confirms it: the clothes on the beach, the two pennies, the unrecovered body. Mr. Spitzer thinks she may have simply returned to the working poor she came from. Vera knows she did not.

What actually happened to Miss MacIntosh? The novel will give you more information as you go, information that will change the shape of what you already know. But it will not give you a clean answer. This is Young’s way: she is more interested in the weight of an unanswered question than in the relief of resolution.

For now, hold what you have: a woman who was the truest thing in the house, and an absence shaped exactly like her.

What to watch for as you keep reading

Light spoilers: This section hints at what happens later in the novel. Skip it if you want to meet the story fresh.

Miss MacIntosh’s corset. She laces herself into it every morning as a philosophical act, the body held to its proper shape by an act of will. Young will return to this garment in ways you don’t yet expect. Notice it each time it appears.

The two pennies. In ancient Greek burial practice, a coin was placed in the mouth of the dead as payment for Charon, the ferryman who carried souls across the river Styx to the underworld. Two pennies in a purse on a beach. Young knew exactly what she was doing.

The sea. Miss MacIntosh swam in it every morning, whatever the weather. She was strengthened by it. And then it took her or she walked into it. The sea in this novel is the border between what we know and what we cannot know. Watch where it appears.

Vera’s habit of description. Notice how much of her grief comes out as physical detail: the red hair, the greyish-green eyes, the size-eight shoes. When we lose someone, we often try to hold them through the inventory of their particulars. Young understands this. So does Vera.

What to hold onto

If Chapter 3 overwhelms you with its length and its layering, come back to this: a child grew up in a house full of illusions, and the one person who taught her that reality was real has disappeared. Everything Vera says about Miss MacIntosh, every detail, every memory, every piece of her philosophy is Vera trying to keep her alive in language.

That is what this chapter is. That is what this novel is, in one of its many dimensions.

You are not lost. You are watching someone grieve in the only way she knows how.
