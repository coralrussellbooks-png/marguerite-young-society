I. The Philosophy: Turn the Stand Away From the Shop

Every chapter so far has pulled in the same direction: away from the market's demand for one clean, sellable, finished maker, and toward the core tension of staying with what is plural, unfinished, grieving, layered, and true. The turning of the stand is the name for that pull made into a daily act. You do not set the sculpture stand once and leave it. You turn it, again and again, to see the form from every side, and the turning is part of the work. Your practice is the same. The market's voice does not go away after one good decision. It comes back every morning: what will sell, what will photograph, what will brand you, what will finish on time. Turning the stand is the daily, repeated act of reorienting away from those questions and back toward the real ones: what is true, what am I seeing, what am I carrying, what is this exact moment asking of my hands.

Think of it as a wheel that keeps rotating. At the top of the wheel you are spread wide in the noisy horizontal world of shows, commissions, praise, and reach. At the bottom, the commission falls through, the grant is cut, the gallery goes quiet, and you feel kicked out of the conversation. You have not been kicked off the wheel. You have rotated. At the bottom, stripped of the noise, you are left in direct contact with the vertical core running straight through you. The wheel turns wildly. The core holds still, bearing the load. The figure at the bottom is not the failure; they are the one standing most honestly at the crossing point.

Because that reorientation has to happen so often, this final chapter gives you a tool for it. The Artist's Tarot is not fortune-telling and it does not predict anything. It is a small deck of the manual's selves and pillars, made to be drawn from when you are stuck, or when the noise of the market has flattened what you can see. You pull a card and let it set the terms of the session. It is a way of handing the decision to the philosophy instead of to the shop, a way of turning the stand one more time.

II. The Artist's Tarot

Write or cast these onto small cards. Keep them in a bag by the stand. When you do not know how to begin, or when you catch yourself making the safe, sellable choice, draw one and follow it for the session. The deck has two parts: the eleven Pillars, which set the terms of the work, and the Selves from Chapter 1, which decide whose hands lead. Because Chapter 1 refused to cap the selves at four, your Selves cards are yours to make. Add a card for every self who keeps showing up in your studio, named or unnamed, and add blanks for the ones who have not arrived yet. Draw one Pillar and one Self when you want both a direction and a driver.

III. Studio Exercise

The Three-Card Turning

When the market's voice is loudest and you cannot choose freely, lay out three cards and let them run the session instead of you.

The ground. Draw one Pillar. This is the truth the session is built on. Read it and do not argue with it.

The hands. Draw one Self. This is who leads. Hand the work over to them for the whole session, even if it is uncomfortable. If you draw a blank, a self who has not shown up before, spend the session finding out who they are.

The cut. Draw one more Pillar and apply it only at the end, as the thing you must do before you stop: leave it unfinished, promote a failure, add a mass, locate yourself. Then stop, whatever state the work is in. You have turned the stand once. Tomorrow you turn it again.

IV. Cross-Medium Interference

The Turning Log

Keep a plain log beside the deck. Each session, write down the cards you drew and one honest sentence about what they made you do that you would not have chosen on your own. Over time the log becomes the record of your practice turning away from the shop, again and again, one draw at a time. It will not show a straight line of progress, because the stand does not turn in a straight line. It will show a form being made: uneven, broken back down, real.
