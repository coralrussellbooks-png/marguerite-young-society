Chapter 31: Here Lies the Dead Love of the World

On Chapter 31 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of A Guide to Reading Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 30 · Guide Home · Chapter 32 →

Research notes: Chapter 31

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Themes: Grief, Loss, and Mourning

Symbols: Keys, Doors, and Locks

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Deafness and Sign Language · The History of Opium

Chapter 31 deepens Cousin Hannah’s portrait in two directions at once: it gives us the great captain’s inner life (her secret grief, her failed mission to rescue Catherine, the love she never spoke of) and it gives us Mr. Spitzer’s relationship to her: the faithful caller at her perpetually empty house, the one who buried her, the one still composing her epitaph.

This chapter contains some of the most beautiful writing in the novel. Read slowly.

What actually happens in Chapter 31

The chapter moves through three connected strands. First: the enduring relationship between Catherine and Mr. Spitzer in Cousin Hannah’s absence, their mutual admiration of the captain who made “their own adventures seem forever unfulfilled.” Second: the full portrait of Hannah’s secret, her grief, never named directly, her failed battle to rescue Catherine from her opium dreams, her verdict on both Spitzer brothers. Third: Mr. Spitzer’s long faithfulness to Hannah’s empty house, his calling cards pushed through the slot, his knocking on the door he was sure would not open, his grief at her death, and his inability to settle on an epitaph.

The chapter’s great set piece is Mr. Spitzer’s cascade of draft epitaphs for Cousin Hannah, each one more expansive and more grieving than the last, all of them crossed out, ending in a simple headstone with only her name and no dates.

Hannah’s secret grief

Chapter 30 told us that Hannah’s “entire life had been perhaps an act of sorrow and redemption,” caused by the loss of a skirt. Chapter 31 tells us a little more, but only a little. “She had never married, for her life had been shadowed with grief which was mentioned whisperingly with hesitation even now by my mother and Mr. Spitzer, both of whom seemed to wish to bury the great secret of Cousin Hannah’s life.”

Young will not name the secret directly. She gives us its shape instead: Hannah searched all her life for a “dead love, a body covered by” snow. She had taken what amounts to a vow against the external man, “giving her gentleman’s word of honor,” as if serving “as a barrier between herself and any other love which wore a human face.” “Her furies had not diminished but had increased as she had furiously tried to climb the walls of forbidden cities of brides, those who were held in thralldom by a cruel bridegroom.”

What Young is tracing, in the most oblique language available to her in 1965, is the shape of a love that could not be named or lived. Hannah’s great adventure, all those decades of disguise and action and desert crossings, was built over a grief she could never speak. She had given her gentleman’s word of honor, which is itself the tell: not a lady’s vow, but a gentleman’s. She lived in the register she was given. The skirt that was lost is the world she could not inhabit.

Hannah’s mission to rescue Catherine

Young reveals something in this chapter that reshapes how we understand Cousin Hannah’s relationship to the household: she had wanted to carry Catherine “away from this dark tower, to rescue her from” the opium dreams of dead love. It had been her losing battle. “She had won greater battles than this” one. She had lost this one.

Hannah’s verdict on Catherine was direct, as everything about her was direct: Catherine “was in love with no man on earth but with a fleeting shadow, the mere shadow of a man.” And her verdict on both brothers was equally unsparing: Joachim “was weak as water, deceitful and wavering,” blown about like foam; Peron was equally deceitful without even pretending otherwise. Hobson’s choice. “A choice between death and death.”

Hannah was right about many things. She was wrong about one: “she did not seem to know that death was necessary to my mother’s love.” Catherine requires the dead man. The shadow is the point. Hannah, who was always in motion, who pitched her tent in far deserts and never stayed, could not understand a love that required stillness and darkness and the opium-maintained preservation of loss. Her directness, which served her in every other battle, failed her here.

Mr. Spitzer at Hannah’s door

Young gives us one of the novel’s great comic-elegiac passages in Mr. Spitzer’s visits to Hannah’s perpetually empty house. He came always “when he knew she was absent”: absent in the Alps, absent in Arabia, absent at sea in an open boat without captain or compass. He timed his visits as an elegant strategy, “a countermove to her ignoring him.”

The signs of her absence catalogued at her door are extraordinary: the closed shutters, the broken lanterns, “the summer furniture left out all winter” under white trees with “long white hair blowing in the wind, the tattered” awnings like Arab tents, the frozen fountains, the old newspapers blowing on the porches. He reads the newspapers for news of her journeys: knowing they are not always accurate, that she had taken many journeys they had not known.

And yet, through the great keyhole large enough for a butterfly to fly through, he sometimes sees a great rounded eye staring back “at him with equal astonishment, its iris streaked like a sunken rainbow.” He calls out, hallo, hallo. He bangs on the door. He concludes “it was a star passing in a cloud.” He pushes “his calling card through the slot” (embossed with the butterfly, “his sign of resurrection and eternal life”) and goes away.

He continued these visits, Young tells us with perfect deadpan, even for a brief period after she was already dead and the house was torn down. He was this empty cocoon, he thought, standing before an address that no longer existed.

The epitaphs

The cascade of draft epitaphs Mr. Spitzer composes and crosses out is one of the novel’s greatest passages, and it deserves to be read aloud. Young gives us eight or nine of them, each one reaching for something truer, each one abandoned:

“Here lies the great captain unhorsed.” “Here lies that great Crusader who rode against the Saracens and the Greeks” and “the Romans and the English and the French. She raided the Etruscan towns. She invaded the cities of night.” “Here lies the greatest captain who fought against all nations and rescued many ladies from their dark towers and cruel lords but could not rescue herself. Here lies one who searched all her life for her dead love, a body covered by the snow. Here lies one who was pitiful. Here lies one who shared our common lot of mortal frailty and died of her life.”

Each epitaph is true. None is complete. The one he settles on is the simplest: her name only, and no dates, because he cannot remember the date of her death and she had never told him the date of her birth. So he gives her, in his own inaccuracy, this extension of time.

The final phrase, “here lies the dead love of the world,” is the one Young uses as the chapter’s emotional summit, and it is Mr. Spitzer’s most honest statement about what Cousin Hannah was. Not the dead love of one person. “The dead love of the world”: the capacity for love itself, as it exists in the world, lying “in a grey New England churchyard” with a jeweled sword and a St. Bernard.

What to watch for as you keep reading

Light spoilers: This section hints at what happens later in the novel. Skip it if you want to meet the story fresh.

Cousin Hannah will return in later chapters with her own narrative space. What this chapter establishes is the emotional register she inhabits in the novel: she is the figure of action whose inner life is entirely secret, the woman who fought every battle except the one closest to her, the captain whose ship, in the end, came home to a domestic war.

“She died at home. She perished in a domestic war.” Young places this quietly, near the end of Mr. Spitzer’s draft epitaphs, without elaboration. After all those Alps and deserts and Moroccan pirates and Spanish cavalcades, Cousin Hannah “died in her own old tent bed set in a windy place with no aide-de-camp.” The greatest captain “was taken by a greater captain” wearing a winding shroud.

Mr. Spitzer’s inability to settle on an epitaph for Hannah mirrors his inability to settle on one for Peron. He has two graves he cannot adequately mark: his brother’s and the great captain’s. Both headstones are incomplete. Both people exceeded his capacity to find the right words. This is, quietly, the novel’s statement about Mr. Spitzer’s music: the silence is not failure. It is the only honest response to what exceeds language.
