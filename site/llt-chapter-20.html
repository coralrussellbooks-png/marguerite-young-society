Chapter 20

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Pillars: Grief Doesn't End · Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The Questions

Vera wants to “protect her from the knowledge of herself.” Is that the Witness’s compassion, or Mr. Spitzer’s refusal to disturb the dreamer, worn now by Vera?

Faced with the baldness and the missing breast, Vera reaches for ironing and the Hebrew calendar. What is the routine for?

Is Miss MacIntosh’s stripped body a barren “mystery no one can solve,” or the plainest mortal fact there is?

What does Vera owe the vulnerable person she can no longer un-see?

This is where Vera’s long apprenticeship to the Witness arrives. She has moved from the embryonic pity of the dream-wedding, through the horror of the unsealing and the dawning charity that followed it, to this: faced with Miss MacIntosh’s baldness and her missing breast, the body stripped to its mortal vulnerability, Vera turns to compassion. She wants to “protect her from the knowledge of herself,” to remember her even when forgotten. She stays with the exposed, deteriorating person and attends to her rather than recoiling. The Witness has come fully into her.

And the chapter is honest that it comes mixed. Vera also reaches for the ordinary as a shield, the wish to return to “the commonplace where nothing should be deranged,” the ironing of shirts, the learning of Hebrew measures and the Jewish calendar, routine pulled over the disturbing sight like a cloth over the crumbling cherubim. This is the idealized ordinary again, the plain used as cover rather than tended as ground, and it marks the flinch inside Vera’s compassion. Witnessing is hard, and she does it with one hand while the other reaches for something to not-see by.

The deepest ambiguity is in the protection itself. Vera wants to shield Miss MacIntosh from the knowledge of her own baldness, to let her keep her self-image intact. This is tender. It is also exactly what Mr. Spitzer did for Catherine, refusing to disturb the dreamer, keeping the sealed construction sealed out of love. Vera has learned the Witness and inherited its problem in the same motion: to witness a sealed person compassionately may be to help keep them sealed. The book sets her beside Mr. Spitzer without telling us whether either of them is right.

Under it is the plainest fact the book has reached. The baldness and the missing breast are called a “mystery no one can solve,” a barren, impoverished landscape, and Vera reaches for God, asking whether God too is bald. But the mystery is also simply mortality, the “dead tree of life,” the body’s deterioration that no housekeeping holds off. This is Grief Doesn't End at the body itself, the stripped mortal fact of a person one loved as solid, and Vera meets it with grief and compassion and the flinch toward routine, all at once, none of them resolved.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that makes Vera’s protection a pure and settled virtue, compassion with no shadow, and the reading that makes her retreat into routine a simple failure of nerve. Both flatten what the chapter holds open.

The footing is that the Witness arrives fully in Vera here, the stance the book has been teaching her since the dream-wedding, and it arrives mixed, with a flinch toward the idealized ordinary as cover. Her wish to protect Miss MacIntosh from the knowledge of herself is tender and is also the same ambiguous act as Mr. Spitzer’s refusal to disturb Catherine: to witness the sealed compassionately can be to keep them sealed. The stripped body is mortality plainly, held in Grief Doesn't End. Vera has learned to witness, and witnessing the sealed carries a real ambiguity the book will not resolve. Vera protects, flinches, grieves, and reaches for God, and the chapter closes without deciding whether her protection is kindness or one more locked door.

For Reflection

Is protecting someone from a hard truth about themselves compassion, or the keeping of their door locked?

What routine has one pulled over a sight one could not bear to keep looking at?

What does one owe a person one can no longer un-see as vulnerable?
