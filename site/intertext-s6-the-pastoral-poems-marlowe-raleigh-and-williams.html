The Pastoral Poems: Marlowe, Raleigh, and Williams

← Previous · Intertextuality in MMMD Home · Next →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Mr. Titus Bonebreaker

Allusions: Sir Walter Raleigh

Definition from research

Category: optional. Sir Walter Raleigh is named twice and asks to be called "Ocean or Water," which points to his poems of love and longing.

The pastoral poems form a chain of answers. In "The Passionate Shepherd to His Love," printed in 1599, Christopher Marlowe's shepherd invites his beloved: "Come live with me and be my love." Sir Walter Raleigh answered with "The Nymph's Reply to the Shepherd," which begins "If all the world and love were young" and goes on to say that such promises fade with the seasons. Much later, William Carlos Williams answered both in "Raleigh Was Right."

Young brings Raleigh into Catherine's bedroom. When he removed his hat, "removed also his head," and "asked that he be addressed by his intimate name, Ocean or Water, for he had crossed many waters" (chapter 11). "Water" was Queen Elizabeth's pet name for him. "Ocean" is the name he takes in his unfinished poem of love and longing to the queen, "The Ocean to Cynthia." The lost head recalls his execution in 1618. Elsewhere he "had played cards with his executioners" (chapter 53).

The novel's courtships answer one another in the same pattern of invitation and sober reply. Mr. Bonebreaker's "I will make you my wife in Christ's name" (chapter 65) is an invitation, and his flight is the reply. The pastoral scene itself appears on an old wall-hanging, "where the shepherd plays his flute to the sleeping shepherdess" (chapter 16).
