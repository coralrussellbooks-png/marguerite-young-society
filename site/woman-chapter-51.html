Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 51

← Chapter 50 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 52 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

The mute governs this chapter, and voicelessness is its pillar. A "mantle like the shell of mother-of-pearl" grows around consciousness, the nights unweave with no "loom" or "harp" to hold them, and the moon crosses as a "schooner" and a "lamp-lighter," and the historical silencing of women and the book's own erasure through severed sense arrive together, a self wrapped in a shell that cannot speak. Love here "was its own subject because it had no object," "dead love," "love which could not be born," running toward "doors leading nowhere" and a "darkened tower where no one lived," and the illusion of the individual, love with no separate object to fix on, is stated in the same breath as women's thwarted emotional lives.

Mr. Spitzer is "of neither world nor world," multiplying into doctor, lawyer, postman, lighthouse keeper, musician, "lawyer to starfish," "lawyer for a moon in the cloud," "old lamp-lighter," and the many roles are the fragmented, imposed self and the plural selfhood the book prefers to any single one. He signs wills, scatters "ink and sand," holds "power of attorney," acts "in another's place," and the acting-for-another is women's exclusion from legal personhood and it is the book's claim that no self is fully its own, each standing in for others. A "mouse" gnaws the documents and the musical manuscripts alike, the record and the song eroded together, invisibility working on both.

He is "woven from wrong chains," missing a "keyboard," missing a "string in the harp of God," burdened with "errors" and "sins" of spirit, and the fragmentation is the psychological cost of oppression and it is the body as the place the self comes apart, a person assembled wrong from the start. Illusion and mirage blur the real and the unreal, "blind Eros" loves past "the limitations of the individual," an umbrella of zodiac signs twirls time, and the woman's dismissed reality and the book's fused country of real and dream are one weather.

The chapter's sharpest figures are women crossing out of their assigned shapes. "Old women" believe themselves stags with antlers and fear the "man in a red coat," a reindeer-woman is found dead with a "golden arrow," an "old maid" cuckolds her husband, and the surveillance and violence women meet when they take non-normative forms and the crossing point, gender and species giving way, arrive as one dark comedy. "Obsessive loves" and "splittings" fuse identities in dream, living love and dead love and multiple selves running together, and Mr. Spitzer, evicted from the "citadel" where he hears "another heart above his heart," identifying with his dead twin, is the plural self at its most unhoused. The "careless dreamer" who is "the heart of God upon a world never created" closes the chapter on a feminine creative power still unrealized, a maker whose world was never let into being.
