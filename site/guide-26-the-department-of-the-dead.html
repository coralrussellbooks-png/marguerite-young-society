Chapter 26: The Department of the Dead

← Chapter 25 · Guide Home · Chapter 27 →

Research notes: Chapter 26

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Symbols: Moon and Stars · Twins and Doubles

Philosophy: Hinduism · Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Music and Composers

Chapter 26 continues where Chapter 25 left off (still inside Mr. Spitzer’s world, still at the breakfast table in Vera’s mind) but it shifts the angle. Where Chapter 25 gave us the key events of Mr. Spitzer’s life, Chapter 26 gives us his philosophy. His professional world. His theory of heaven. His understanding of what separated him from his brother, and what that separation cost.

It is a long, ruminative chapter, and it rewards slow reading. Young is building Mr. Spitzer into one of literature’s great portraits of a certain kind of man: the faithful one, the patient one, the one who stayed.

What actually happens in Chapter 26

Vera continues her meditation on Mr. Spitzer. Young takes us into his professional life first: he is a lawyer of the dead, his department “the hidden past, the wills and deeds” and dusty archives, the secret drawers of old houses haunted by those who are gone. He has spent his career locating lost heirs, heirs who turned out to be heiresses, heiresses who turned out to be heirs, wandering derelicts shipwrecked on other coasts, people who could not be found or would not be.

We get the story of the two Boston spinsters, each the reincarnation of Cleopatra, each with a Sphinx in her garden, both clients of Mr. Spitzer, one dying and leaving everything to her “Siamese cats, with the provision that” Mr. Spitzer supervise the arrangement. The cats, having nine lives each, appear to be flourishing indefinitely.

Then the chapter turns to the question at its center: if Peron had lived and Joachim had died, what would Peron have done? Would he have come faithfully to Catherine’s bedside, year after year, bringing winter violets plucked from secret places? Would he have finished Mr. Spitzer’s unwritten music, even adding a hesitant note? Young’s answer, delivered through Mr. Spitzer’s own understanding, is no. Peron would have swept Catherine into the living sunlight, that “magnificent moment of defeat, even though the living sunlight might be” her death.

The chapter ends with Mr. Spitzer contemplating the lower world, the one that lies just under the surface of life, acknowledging “that in order to reach the upper world, one must first travel through the lower world, visiting all streets, lanes, alleys, and the journey might be endless.”

The department of the dead

Mr. Spitzer’s professional world is a perfect mirror of his personal one. He works in the hidden past: the wills departments, the dusty archives, the pigeon-holes and secret drawers of dead people’s houses. He is always entering old houses, “searching through old secretaries in dim, ghost-haunted rooms where he had felt that at any minute the living might appear.”

He has come to doubt that there should be any real heirs whatsoever, any real property handed down in golden perpetuity. There have been so many lost heirs, secret sons and daughters, heirs who turned out to be heiresses. He has come to feel that even “our features, our hair, our eyes, our” hands; these properties too have been confused in metamorphic storms, and are “very, very makeshift, very uncertain, the properties” of a dream.

This is Mr. Spitzer’s professional conclusion applied to the question of identity itself. We do not even inherit our own faces reliably. The self we believe we own is as disputed as any estate, as likely to have a secret claimant, as likely to turn out to belong to someone else.

The two Cleopatras

The story of the two Boston spinsters (rival beauties in youth, rival reincarnations of Cleopatra in old age, rival Sphinxes in adjacent gardens, both clients of Mr. Spitzer) is one of the novel’s great small comic passages, and Young gives it more space than you might expect.

The one who dies leaves everything “to her innumerable Siamese cats, with the provision that” Mr. Spitzer supervise the arrangement for as long as the cats live. The cats, he notes with a sighing remark, each having nine lives, appear to have suffered no deaths whatsoever. He last visited several years ago. The old caretaker had since died and been replaced. The cats were flourishing. A cat walked up and down the piano keys playing a tune from “one of the more obscure eighteenth-century musical composers.” This is Young doing what she does so well: a miniature of the novel’s larger concerns (contested identity, immortal pretensions, the faithful guardian of someone else’s estate, the music that continues without human hand) compressed into a cat story told to amuse a dying woman. Mr. Spitzer is not sure he is not teasing Catherine. He is probably not sure he is not teasing himself.

What Peron would not have done

The chapter’s most important passage is Mr. Spitzer’s extended meditation on what Peron would have done if their positions had been reversed: if Joachim had died and Peron had lived.

He “would not have sat up all night with any corpse.” He would not have brought winter violets from secret places in the whirling snow. He would not have endured Catherine’s pleasure in ignoring him. He would not have finished Mr. Spitzer’s music or attempted to add a hesitant note. He would have swept Catherine out into the living sunlight: one magnificent moment, even if it killed her beauty, even if it killed her.

Peron cared only, it seemed, for old hags, Mr. Spitzer says with painful candor. Catherine holds it against Mr. Spitzer that he did not rescue her. She yearns “for her escape from this illness.” She would rather have been swept away and died in the sunlight than preserved indefinitely in the dark.

And Mr. Spitzer knows this. He has always known it. He is the wrong brother. His faithfulness, his patience, his undying love; these are exactly what she does not want. She wanted Peron’s impetuous, careless, finite love. She got Joachim’s infinite, cautious, immortal devotion instead.

This is the novel’s most heartbreaking statement of the Spitzer situation: the faithful one is faithful precisely because he cannot give her what she wants, and the one who could give it to her is dead.

Mr. Spitzer’s heaven

Near the chapter’s center, Mr. Spitzer offers his vision of what heaven should be, and it is completely in character: perfect oblivion. All souls equal when all are dead. “No rivalry between person and person, none between the divisions of self. There should be no immortal derangement as of this present world with all its stains and cracks and flaws.” Complete forgetting, not Catherine’s complete memory, but the opposite: the silence that “requires neither instruments nor voices, the heavenly harmonies forever fixed above the floods of” dark time.

Mr. Spitzer wants, above all things, to stop being himself. Or rather, to stop being both of them. He believes in “no hell more than this present individual life already” affords. He is not sure that heaven is a place so much as the state when we “have completely forgotten who we were, when we” have been united “as equal brothers, one and the same.”

One and the same. The twin who was always divided wanting, at the last, to be undivided. Not Joachim, not Peron, not the one who survived and not the one who fell. Just the silence where neither needs to be remembered.

What to watch for as you keep reading

Mr. Spitzer’s meditation on the lower world at the chapter’s end (the one that lies under this world, “close to the surface of life,” full of thieves and painted street women and dark lanes) points forward to chapters that will take us into exactly those places. The novel is about to widen its geography considerably. Remember that Mr. Spitzer believes you must travel the lower world to reach the upper one, and that the journey might be endless.

Catherine’s desire to be swept into the living sunlight, even at the cost of her life, is one of the novel’s great withheld actions. Mr. Spitzer cannot do it. He knows only the darkness and the night. This is what he means when he says his love is immortal: it is precisely because it cannot be enacted in mortal life. The love that can be acted on belongs to Peron. The love that survives Peron belongs to Joachim. Neither is what Catherine wants.

The lost heirs Mr. Spitzer spends his professional life locating (heirs who turned out to be heiresses, heiresses who turned out to be heirs, people who inherited features that were not quite theirs) are a professional echo of the Miss MacIntosh sequence. Everyone in this novel is the uncertain heir of a self they did not fully choose. Mr. Spitzer, whose profession is to locate such people and establish their claims, cannot quite locate himself.
