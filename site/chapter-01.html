Summary

Vera Cartwheel rides a Grey Goose bus through the mist of the American Midwest. The driver, Moses Hunnecker, has long bushy hair. He whistles, drinks openly from a whiskey bottle, calls on God and the angels, and drives erratically. Vera imagines a wife and children waiting for him at home, then wonders whether she has invented them. The only other passengers are a sleeping couple, Madge and Homer Capehorn. Madge is pregnant and seems to resent it, and Homer sleeps in placid innocence. Along the roadside Vera sees a moving van piled with furniture and a double-headed man staring into nothing. These sights lead her to reflect that individuality is an illusion and every soul is joined to every other. She explains why she is traveling. She is searching for Miss MacIntosh, the plain, red-haired nursemaid from What Cheer, Iowa, who disappeared or died. She was the one solid presence in Vera's childhood, in a house by the New England sea ruled by her mother's opium dreams.

Key passages

From the research

  • The opening depiction of the bus-driver, his erratic driving, and his imagined family life introduces a sense of instability mixed with hopeful human connection. Vera speculates about the bus-driver’s wife and children, highlighting a longing for established human relationships and happiness that seems almost mythical or legendary. The bus-driver’s erratic driving amid heavy mist and his open drinking juxtaposed with calls upon God and angels create a complex character who embodies both chaos and faith.
  • The detailed description of Madge contrasts sharply with Homer’s blissful innocence and placid smile. This passage explores themes of denial, despair, and innocence amidst difficult circumstances. Madge’s appearance and behavior suggest a deep inner conflict and suffering, while Homer's peaceful sleep offers a counterpoint of hope or oblivion.
  • Vera’s descriptions of the landscape create an atmosphere of desolation, otherworldliness, and existential uncertainty. This setting evokes a sense of isolation and the fading of reality into illusion, emphasizing Vera’s internal struggles and sense of alienation.
  • The image of the double-headed man standing by the roadside, staring into nothingness, symbolizes the fragmented self and the elusive nature of reality. Vera reflects on the “organization of illusion” and the interconnectedness of all souls, suggesting that individuality is an illusion and that deeper unity might exist beyond appearances. This philosophical meditation on identity and connection is central to the chapter’s thematic core.
  • Vera’s long and restless search for Miss MacIntosh, an old red-headed nursemaid who was a moral guide and symbol of truth and constancy, represents a quest for stability, love, and truth in a world of illusions and loss. Miss MacIntosh embodies simple virtues, common sense, and a grounded view of life, contrasting with Vera’s experience of chaos and dreams. This passage reveals Vera’s deep loneliness and yearning for a meaningful human connection.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s rejection of illusions and adherence to “truth to nature” underscores the tension between the dream world and reality. Her practical, no-nonsense attitude highlights Vera’s struggle to reconcile the dreamlike, illusory aspects of existence with the desire for concrete, simple truth. This tension is a major thematic element of the chapter.
  • The closing passage emphasizes Miss MacIntosh’s practical strength and sensibility amidst desolation. Her rejection of luxury and illusion in favor of plainness and resilience serves as a counterpoint to Vera’s journey through a world of fading images and lost souls. This passage reinforces themes of endurance, reality, and the search for meaning in a harsh world.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • Vera imagines the bus-driver’s wife and children, then wonders whether his family life is “an emanation of my over-active imagination.” What does it suggest that the novel opens with its narrator inventing other people’s lives and then doubting her inventions?
  • The pregnant girl is covered from head to toe in charms, sports, butterflies, and a stuffed canary, “discursive details which should not focus attention, yet drew attention to her.” What is she hiding, and what does the hiding reveal?
  • The flat road becomes “the first creation when only the spirit of God had moved upon the deep,” and then “There was now no landscape but the soul’s.” How does the chapter turn an Indiana highway into an inner landscape?
  • A man with a child on his shoulders becomes “a double-headed man.” Why might this passing image stay “forever engraved on memory’s whirling disc”?

Characters

  • Miss MacIntosh’s religion is “truth to nature,” and she insists that “Common sense is the finest sense.” Yet Vera remembers her as “the dead steersman” and “the heart of all hearts.” How does the plainest person in the book become its most mythic figure?
  • Vera says the years of Miss MacIntosh’s death “had added to her stature, making her seem almost vague.” How does memory both enlarge and blur the people it keeps?
  • Vera gave up architecture “because I could not plan a house if there was one soul which could not live in it,” and later typed “mortality rates through a blur of tears.” What do these jobs reveal about her?
  • The blind juggler asks, “When shall the light, Peter, enter my soul?” Why does Vera hear his question “like my own”?

Connections

  • “Every heart is the other heart. Every soul is the other soul. Every face is the other face. The individual is the one illusion.” How does this claim prepare for the novel’s wheel of characters turning around Miss MacIntosh at the hub? See The Wheel and the Spokes.
  • Vera comes from a house by the sea and enters the Midwest because Miss MacIntosh told her to “see America first.” How does the chapter set the ocean of the Cartwheel house against the plain interior of the country?
  • Don Quixote and Tantalus both appear in the chapter’s first pages. What do a knight who fights imaginary enemies and a man forever reaching for what recedes have in common with Vera?
  • The Cartwheel garden is full of statues, cupids, psyches, and the Apollo Belvedere, “sculptured by man dreaming that he was other than he was.” Why does Miss MacIntosh distrust them?

Reflection

  • “All my life I had been reaching for the tangible, and it had evaded me.” What in ordinary life seems most solid and turns out hardest to hold?
  • Vera’s quest is “the search for life, for love, for truth that does not fail.” Is a truth that does not fail possible, or is the search itself the point?

Research: Themes

The Bus Journey and Characters

  • The story begins with a bus driver whistling and driving erratically through heavy mist in a rural, indistinct part of interior America. The bleak, mist-shrouded landscape evokes a feeling of desolation and uncertainty.
  • Moses Hunnecker (The Bus Driver) is described as a large-headed man with bushy hair, who drinks whiskey openly and calls upon God and angels, suggesting a complex mix of spiritual longing and reckless behavior. His family life is ambiguous, possibly a projection of Vera’s desires.
  • A sleeping young couple (Madge and Homer) joins the bus at a pottery town. The girl is pregnant but resists her condition, described in vivid detail with a mix of artificiality and natural growth, symbolizing conflict and alienation. The boy contrasts her with innocence and blissful stolidity.
  • The bus brushes against a moving van carrying an array of household items, including a feathered hat bobbing in the mist like a bird, evoking a surreal atmosphere where everyday objects gain symbolic weight.
  • The journey progresses into a formless, oceanic-like mist-covered plain with no distinct landmarks, symbolizing the dissolution of the familiar world and the encroachment of the unknown or the subconscious.

Philosophical Reflection on Illusion and Connection

Vera reflects on the nature of memory, illusion, and the human soul

See: Illusion and Reality · Memory and the Past · Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos

  • The idea that individuality is an illusion and that every heart, soul, and face is interconnected.
  • The metaphor of ships passing in the night, representing fleeting human connections.
  • Vera’s lifelong search for tangible truth and connection, which has often evaded her, leaving only illusions and disconnected images.

Vera Cartwheel’s Personal Quest and History

See: Memory and the Past · The Search for Meaning

  • Vera reveals herself as searching for a lost companion, Miss MacIntosh, an old nursemaid who symbolized stability, truth, and moral guidance in Vera’s life.
  • Miss MacIntosh is described as a simple, practical woman with a broken nose and red hair, who rejected illusions and fanciful dreams in favor of truth to nature. She was a grounding figure during Vera’s turbulent childhood amid a family haunted by opium and dreams.
  • Vera has wandered through various cities, employment, and social strata, always searching for Miss MacIntosh who had disappeared or died. This search is both literal and symbolic of Vera’s deeper quest for meaning, love, and truth.
  • Vera contrasts the opulent but empty grandeur of her mother’s house, filled with ghosts and illusions, with the plain, unpretentious life Miss MacIntosh led by the sea. This contrast highlights the theme of reality versus illusion in the narrative.
  • Vera contemplates the ephemeral nature of life, the inevitability of death, and the elusive quest for light and meaning in a world filled with darkness and dreams that fail.
  • Vera’s journey is both physical and spiritual, seeking a reality not distorted by illusion.
  • The narrative questions what is real, suggesting individuality and perception may be illusory.
  • The human condition is described as one of alienation, with fleeting and often failed attempts at connection.
  • The past haunts the present, and memory is both a source of pain and a guide.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s simple life contrasts with grand illusions, suggesting truth lies in everyday reality.

Research: Resources

  • Jehovah’s Witnesses is a millenarian (the belief by a religious, social, or political group or movement in a coming fundamental transformation of society, after which "all things will be changed") restorationist (is the belief that Christianity has been or should be restored along the lines of what is known about the apostolic early church, which restorationists see as the search for a purer and more ancient form of the religion) Christian denomination with nontrinitarian (is a form of Christianity that rejects the mainstream Christian doctrine of the Trinity: the belief that God is three distinct hypostases or persons who are coeternal, coequal, and indivisibly united in one being, or essence) beliefs distinct from mainstream Christianity. Jehovah's Witnesses are best known for their door-to-door preaching, distributing literature such as The Watchtower and Awake!, and for refusing military service, refusal to salute state symbols and blood transfusions. They consider the use of God's name vital for proper worship. They reject Trinitarianism, inherent immortality of the soul, and hellfire, which they consider to be unscriptural doctrines. They do not observe Christmas, Easter, birthdays or other holidays and customs they consider to have pagan origins incompatible with Christianity. They prefer to use their own Bible translation, the New World Translation of the Holy Scriptures, although their literature occasionally quotes and cites other Bible translations. Adherents commonly refer to their body of beliefs as "The Truth" and consider themselves to be "in the Truth". They consider "human society" to be morally corrupt and under the influence of Satan, and most limit their social interaction with non-Witnesses. Congregational disciplinary actions include disfellowshipping, their term for formal expulsion and shunning, a last resort for what they consider serious offenses. Baptized individuals who formally leave are considered disassociated and are also shunned. Disfellowshipped and disassociated individuals may eventually be reinstated if deemed repentant. The movement grew out of the Bible Student movement that Charles Taze Russell began in the 1870s, and it took the name Jehovah’s Witnesses in 1931.
  • The “Grey Goose” bus: “Gray Goose” became American slang for a prison bus, often a converted Greyhound painted grey, used to move prisoners between jails, courts, and prisons, always migrating like geese from one prison to another. Green’s Dictionary of Slang first records the term in 1976, after the novel was published, so it is a later echo rather than Young’s source, but read that way the bus suggests displacement and a loss of freedom, movement without arrival. The name also recalls the old song “Go Tell Aunt Rhody,” whose old grey goose is dead, which the bus-driver sings in Chapter 5. See On the bus (Ch 1) and Go Tell Aunt Rhody (Ch 5).
  • Harold Bloom says Don Quixote is the first modern novel, and that the protagonist is at war with Freud's reality principle, which accepts the necessity of dying. Bloom says that the novel has an endless range of meanings, but that a recurring theme is the human need to withstand suffering. Edith Grossman, who wrote and published a highly acclaimed English translation of the novel in 2003, says that the book is mostly meant to move people into emotion using a systematic change of course, on the verge of both tragedy and comedy at the same time. Grossman has stated: “The question is that Quixote has multiple interpretations [...] and how do I deal with that in my translation. I'm going to answer your question by avoiding it [...] so when I first started reading the Quixote I thought it was the most tragic book in the world, and I would read it and weep [...] As I grew older [...] my skin grew thicker [...] and so when I was working on the translation I was actually sitting at my computer and laughing out loud. This is done [...] as Cervantes did it [...] by never letting the reader rest. You are never certain that you truly got it. Because as soon as you think you understand something, Cervantes introduces something that contradicts your premise.” The novel's structure is episodic in form. The full title is indicative of the tale's object, as ingenioso (Spanish) means "quick with inventiveness", marking the transition of modern literature from dramatic to thematic unity. The novel takes place over a long period of time, including many adventures united by common themes of the nature of reality, reading, and dialogue in general. Although burlesque on the surface, the novel, especially in its second half, has served as an important thematic source not only in literature but also in much of art and music, inspiring works by Pablo Picasso and Richard Strauss. The contrasts between the tall, thin, fancy-struck and idealistic Quixote and the short, stout, world-weary Panza is a motif echoed ever since the book's publication, and Don Quixote's imaginings are the butt of outrageous and cruel practical jokes in the novel. Even faithful and simple Sancho is forced to deceive him at certain points. The novel is considered a satire of orthodoxy, veracity and even nationalism. In exploring the individualism of his characters, Cervantes helped lead literary practice beyond the narrow convention of the chivalric romance. He spoofs the chivalric romance through a straightforward retelling of a series of acts that redound to the knightly virtues of the hero. The character of Don Quixote became so well known in its time that the word quixotic was quickly adopted by many languages. Characters such as Sancho Panza and Don Quixote's steed, Rocinante, are emblems of Western literary culture. The phrase "tilting at windmills" to describe an act of attacking imaginary enemies (or an act of extreme idealism), derives from an iconic scene in the book. It stands in a unique position between medieval romance and the modern novel. The former consists of disconnected stories featuring the same characters and settings with little exploration of the inner life of even the main character. The latter are usually focused on the psychological evolution of their characters. In Part I, Quixote imposes himself on his environment. By Part II, people know about him through "having read his adventures", and so, he needs to do less to maintain his image. By his deathbed, he has regained his sanity, and is once more "Alonso Quixano the Good". Vera wonders whether the bus-driver is “some mad Don Quixote chasing windmills.” See On Don Quixote (Ch 1).
  • Blaise Pascal thought imagination was powerful but often led people astray. He also argued that believing in God is more beneficial than not believing in God, which is known as Pascal’s wager. It is a thought experiment where one “bets” on God’s existence. If God exists, the believer gains eternal happiness, while the non-believer loses eternal happiness. If God does not exist, the believer loses nothing, and the non-believer loses nothing. According to Pascal, it’s always better to wager on God’s existence since the potential gain is infinite, and the potential loss is zero.
  • Madge is a traditional nickname for Margaret, which comes from the Greek word for “pearl.” Margaret’s French form is Marguerite, the author’s own name. The girl on the bus wears “ten cent store pearls” dripping from her ears.
  • Madge’s maiden name is Edwards. John Edwards, an eighteenth-century Welsh translator and poet, put the second and third parts of Bunyan’s Pilgrim’s Progress into Welsh in the 1760s.
  • Homer means hostage, pledge. It is also the name of the poet of the Iliad and the Odyssey.
  • Capehorn is a French occupational name for a robe-master or maker of hoods. It also suggests Cape Horn, the stormy southern tip of South America, where many sailing ships were lost rounding the Horn.
  • “old Coca-Cola and aspirin signboards”: Painted advertisements for Coca-Cola covered barn walls and the sides of buildings across the country from the 1890s on, and Bayer began selling aspirin in 1899. By the 1930s such signs lined every highway, and Young’s are “tattered as tramps.” See Burma-Shave signs (Ch 5).
  • “the depths of southern Indiana, a state as yet unknown to me”: Young was born in Indianapolis and lived for years in New Harmony, in the far southwestern corner of the state, where she wrote Angel in the Forest. Vera, raised by the New England sea, enters Young’s own home state as a stranger. See Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest.
  • The descriptions of what is seen from the bus window are reminiscent of what might have been seen on the historic Route 66. Route 66 ran from Chicago to Los Angeles and never entered Indiana, where this bus is traveling, but the roadside culture it made famous ran along highways all over the country in the 1930s and 1940s.
  • “an enormous coffee urn with its lid opened against the sky, a wigwam nightclub”: These are examples of novelty or mimetic roadside architecture, buildings shaped like the things they sold or advertised, which sprang up along American highways in the 1920s and 1930s. Real examples include the Coffee Pot in Bedford, Pennsylvania (1927), the Wigwam Village motels that began in Cave City, Kentucky, in 1937, and the Haines Shoe House in Pennsylvania (1948). “The old woman who lived in the shoe” comes from the Mother Goose rhyme.
  • “when only the spirit of God had moved upon the deep”: This echoes Genesis 1:2, “And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters.” The flat, misty Midwest becomes the world before creation, “the face of the ambiguous waters, of no boundary line, no shore.” See Genesis.
  • Tantalus in Greek mythology was famous for his punishment - to stand in a pool of water beneath a fruit tree, the fruit never within reach and the water always receding before he could drink.
  • “passing like ships in the night”: The phrase comes from Henry Wadsworth Longfellow’s Tales of a Wayside Inn: “Ships that pass in the night, and speak each other in passing, / Only a signal shown and a distant voice in the darkness.” Young extends it to “ships manned by dead helmsmen, by ghostly crews.” See American Writers.
  • Phenomenology is a philosophical study and movement largely associated with the early 20th century that seeks to objectively investigate the nature of subjective, conscious experience. It attempts to describe the universal features of consciousness while avoiding assumptions about the external world, aiming to describe phenomena as they appear to the subject, and to explore the meaning and significance of the lived experiences.
  • Logic is the study of correct reasoning. It includes both formal and informal logic. Formal logic is the study of deductively valid inferences or logical truths. It examines how conclusions follow from premises based on the structure of arguments alone, independent of their topic and content. Informal logic is associated with informal fallacies, critical thinking, and argumentation theory. Informal logic examines arguments expressed in natural language whereas formal logic uses formal language. When used as a countable noun, the term "a logic" refers to a specific logical formal system that articulates a proof system. Logic plays a central role in many fields, such as philosophy, mathematics, computer science, and linguistics.
  • Dialectic is the art of investigating or discussing the truth of opinions and an inquiry into metaphysical contradictions and their solutions.
  • “deep calling to deep”: Psalm 42:7 reads, “Deep calleth unto deep at the noise of thy waterspouts: all thy waves and thy billows are gone over me.” The same psalm opens with the soul’s thirst: “My soul thirsts for God, for the living God. When can I go and meet with God?” (Psalm 42:2). Vera repeats the phrase three times in this chapter, and it captures her spiritual hunger and the existential tension between life, death, and the hope for a transcendent encounter that will end her wandering and questioning.
  • “like a drowning man who clutches at a straw”: The old proverb says that a drowning man will clutch at a straw, any small hope however useless. Vera flees illusion the way a drowning man grabs for the straw, and finds illusion again in front of her.
  • “dead souls like the autumn leaves”: The comparison of the dead to falling leaves runs through the whole epic tradition. In the Iliad, the generations of men are like leaves; in the Aeneid, the souls crowding the shore of the underworld river are as many as the leaves that fall in the first frost of autumn; Dante repeats the image in Inferno 3; and Milton’s fallen angels lie “thick as autumnal leaves that strow the brooks / In Vallombrosa.” Young, who studied the epic at the University of Chicago, also named Gogol’s novel Dead Souls as one of the books that formed her.
  • “a patient asks under the ether mask”: Ether was one of the first surgical anesthetics. William Morton demonstrated it publicly at Massachusetts General Hospital in Boston in 1846, and for a century patients breathed it through a mask over the nose and mouth before an operation. Vera compares her half-sleeping life to the dreams of a patient going under.
  • “I, Vera Cartwheel”: Vera means “true” in Latin and “faith” in Russian. The narrator who searches for “truth that does not fail” carries both meanings in her first name. See On the names (Ch 1).
  • Cartwheel was a symbol of industry or used as a pun as in the arms of Carter. A cartwheel is also the wheel of a cart and an acrobat’s sideways spin. Young described the whole novel as a wheel with Miss MacIntosh at the hub, so Vera’s family name carries the book’s central image.
  • Hegel described his philosophical system as “a circle of circles.” Young described the novel the same way in the Friedman and Fuchs interview: “I always thought of Miss MacIntosh as the center of the wheel, the hub, then the spokes as the subsidiary, secondary characters, and the wheel as endlessly expanding like a universe.” Here Vera calls Miss MacIntosh “the central heart, the heart of all hearts.” See On the structure (Ch 1) and The Wheel and the Spokes.
  • “the dead steersman”: In Virgil’s Aeneid, Palinurus, the helmsman of Aeneas’s ship, is lulled to sleep by the god Sleep and falls overboard, and in the underworld his unburied shade begs Aeneas for rest. Vera’s name for Miss MacIntosh, lost to the sea, makes the old nursemaid the steersman of the whole voyage, now gone overboard.
  • The name Georgia means tiller of the soil, farmer.
  • MacIntosh comes from the Gaelic Mac an Tòisich, “son of the chief” or leader.
  • “harvests of dragons’ teeth and reaping the whirlwind”: In Greek myth, Cadmus and later Jason sowed a dragon’s teeth in the ground, and armed men sprang up from them and fought. “Reaping the whirlwind” comes from Hosea 8:7: “For they have sown the wind, and they shall reap the whirlwind.” The street preachers promise harvests of war and ruin grown from what was sown.
  • The most famous Macintosh is Charles Macintosh, the Scottish chemist who patented a way of waterproofing cloth with a rubber solution in 1823. The raincoat named for him, the mackintosh, is Miss MacIntosh’s “plaid, faded waterproof,” and she always wears it outside. See Charles MacIntosh (Ch 3).
  • “the salt of common sense”: The phrase recalls Matthew 5:13, “Ye are the salt of the earth,” said of the plain and faithful. Miss MacIntosh’s gospel is common sense: “Common sense is the finest sense.” See Common sense (Ch 23).
  • “that I must lead a useful life and see America first”: “See America First” was a real slogan. It began with a 1906 movement to persuade Americans to travel in the American West instead of Europe, and the Great Northern Railway used it to promote Glacier National Park. Miss MacIntosh’s advice sends Vera into “the broad interior, the spacious Middle West.”
  • “the search for life, for love, for truth that does not fail”: Vera’s statement of her motive gives the novel one of its governing phrases. See The Search for Life, for Love, for Truth That Does Not Fail.
  • “a dead girl’s love letters scattered on the floor of her empty bedroom”: The chapter does not name the dead girl. One reading takes her to be Miss MacIntosh, the “dead steersman,” Vera’s lost companion and moral guide who had disappeared or died, whose memory drives Vera’s search for life, love, and truth. She may also be one of the many lost souls Vera met in the harbor cities, each of whom sends her on the journey.
  • “the palm leaves crossed above the marble mantel piece” Palm leaves traditionally symbolize victory, peace, or martyrdom, especially in Christian iconography. Their presence “crossed” above a cold, marble mantelpiece evokes a memorial or shrine, reinforcing the idea of death and remembrance, perhaps hinting at a spiritual or solemn aspect of the lost figure.
  • “her rosary hanging on a brass bedpost” The rosary is a symbol of faith, prayer, and the search for spiritual meaning or solace. Its placement on the bedpost of an empty room suggests a clinging to faith or hope in a place now vacant, emphasizing spiritual yearning amid loss.
  • “because of her suicide”: This stark fact grounds the previous symbols in tragedy and finality. The suicide adds emotional weight and explains why these relics are scattered and why the search is urgent and poignant: there is a desperate need for understanding or closure.
  • “because of a deaf musician” This figure introduces the theme of communication and isolation. A deaf musician cannot hear the music he creates or the world around him, symbolizing a disconnect or failure of connection, similar to the unanswered questions Vera faces.
  • “because of a drunkard’s celestial dream of childhood” This phrase suggests a vision or hope, however fragile or impaired, that persists despite hardship or degradation. The drunkard’s “celestial dream” implies a longing for innocence, transcendence, or redemption.
  • “because of the answers not heard” This line expresses frustration and yearning for understanding, the elusive nature of truth, or the silence in response to crucial questions. It conveys the theme of searching for meaning in a world that is often unresponsive or inscrutable.
  • “because of a blind man’s groping for his coffee cup at an all-night quick-lunch stand on the fog-shrouded waterfront of that great harbor city” This vivid image symbolizes the human condition of blindness or ignorance, fumbling in darkness or uncertainty. The fog-shrouded waterfront amplifies the atmosphere of mystery, obscurity, and searching for clarity or sustenance (coffee cup) in a confusing and cold environment.
  • “as he had asked of his companion” This question encapsulates the existential yearning for illumination, understanding, hope, and spiritual awakening.
  • “When shall the light, Peter, enter my soul?” - 1 Peter 2:9 highlights that believers are called out of darkness into God's light. 2 Peter 1:19 compares the prophetic message to a light in a dark place, offering hope for a new spiritual dawn. 1 Peter 5:10 assures believers that after suffering, God will restore and strengthen them. 2 Corinthians 4:6 speaks of God's light shining in hearts, providing knowledge of His glory. Psalm 43:3 is a prayer asking God for His guiding light and truth. Micah 7:8 expresses hope that even in darkness, the Lord will be a light. Ephesians 5:14 encourages those who are spiritually asleep to wake up and receive Christ's light. The blind man’s question is a metaphor for the quest for spiritual illumination and the deep human need to find light within the soul, to overcome both literal and figurative darkness
  • “If your eyes are healthy, your whole body will be full of light.” - Matthew 6:22 The phrase suggests that when one has a clear and true vision (healthy eyes), it positively affects the whole self, filling it with light which is understood as awareness, understanding, and vitality
  • “the world’s greatest juggler, performing for the Lord’s sake and glory”: The blind man recalls the medieval legend of Our Lady’s Juggler, a poor tumbler who, having nothing else to offer, performs his tricks before a statue of the Virgin in a monastery chapel, and the statue comes to life to bless him. Anatole France retold it as “Le Jongleur de Notre-Dame” in 1892, and Massenet made it an opera in 1902.
  • “by evening comet plane”: The de Havilland Comet, the first jet airliner, began passenger service in 1952, while Young was writing the novel. Whether or not she meant the Comet, Vera’s flight looks down on “the earth’s abstract curvature in space.”
  • Marguerite Young in the Dalkey Interview said she talked to coal miners in the Ozarks and it was because of them that she decided to write about New Harmony (a utopia in Angel in the Forest) and that it is based upon social consciousness.
  • The history of coal mining in the United States is the story of class warfare where the corporations held all the wealth and left the workers with very little. Unions were formed to try and combat this. The worst coal mine explosion in American history, at Monongah, West Virginia, in 1907, killed three hundred and sixty-two men. There were skirmishes where the National Guard were called in and during the Ludlow Massacre in Colorado in 1914 killed men, women, and children.
  • “the disc throwers, the fat cupids, the thin psyches with flowing curls, the mute Apollo Belvedere”: The statues in Catherine’s garden are the classics of European sculpture. Myron’s Discobolus, the discus thrower, is known from Roman copies of a Greek bronze of about 450 B.C. Psyche, whose name is Greek for both “soul” and “butterfly,” is the mortal bride of Cupid in Apuleius’s tale. The Apollo Belvedere in the Vatican, a Roman marble copy of a Greek bronze, was praised for centuries as the ideal of human beauty, and here it is “mute.”
  • “Her religion was truth to nature”: “Truth to nature” was the watchword of the art critic John Ruskin, who in Modern Painters (1843) urged artists to paint the world exactly as it is, and it became the creed of the Pre-Raphaelite painters. Young gives the art critics’ doctrine to a nursemaid who has no use for art at all.

Constitutional
