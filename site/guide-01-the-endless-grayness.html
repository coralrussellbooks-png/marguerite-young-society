Research notes: Chapter 1

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Homer Capehorn · Madge Capehorn · Miss MacIntosh · Moses Hunnecker · Vera Cartwheel

Symbols: The Bus and the Journey

If you’ve just opened Miss MacIntosh, My Darling and put it down in bewilderment after the first twenty pages, this is for you.

You are not failing the book. The book is doing something unusual to you on purpose, and once you understand what that is, the bewilderment transforms into something closer to awe.

Here is the first thing to know: Marguerite Young is not hiding the story from you. She is giving it to you in the way memory actually works by circling, doubling back, lush with digression, lit from odd angles. The story is there. Let’s find it together.

What actually happens in Chapter 1

A young woman named Vera Cartwheel is on a bus somewhere in the American Midwest. It is grey out. The bus driver, Moses Hunnecker, is large-headed, bushy-haired, drinking whiskey openly, and calling on God and angels while driving erratically through heavy mist. A young married couple, Madge and Homer Capehorn, are asleep in their seats. Madge is pregnant and visibly unhappy about it. Homer is placidly, blissfully asleep.

That is all that happens, in terms of external events. The bus moves through a grey landscape. Vera watches.

Everything else in the chapter and it is considerable resides in Vera’s mind.

What Vera is thinking about

Vera is on this bus because she is looking for someone: Miss Georgia MacIntosh, her childhood nursemaid, who disappeared years ago and may be dead. The search is both literal (she is actually going to Miss MacIntosh’s hometown of What Cheer, Iowa) and something more: a search for truth, for stability, for a self that isn’t consumed by illusion.

Vera grew up in a household ruled by illusion. Her mother, Catherine, is an opium addict who has taken to her bed and never left it, living entirely inside her own hallucinations. Miss MacIntosh was the opposite: plain, practical, red-haired, broken-nosed, a woman who believed in facts and schedules and the cold, honest surface of things. She was the one solid thing in Vera’s life. And then she was gone.

So when Vera watches Moses Hunnecker drinking and praying at the wheel, and when she watches Madge sleeping her resistant, unhappy sleep while Homer sleeps his innocent one, she isn’t just watching passengers on a bus. She is watching the question her life has been organized around: what is real, what is illusion, and what do we do when the only person who taught us the difference has disappeared?

Why Young writes this way

Young herself described the structure of Miss MacIntosh, My Darling as “spokes of a wheel, with Miss MacIntosh in the middle.” Everything radiates outward from that absent center. And the prose radiates the same way: each sentence proliferates into the next, association breeding association, the way a mind actually moves when it is obsessively circling something it cannot resolve.

This is not accidental difficulty. It is the form enacting the content. Vera cannot think in straight lines because the thing she is thinking about (loss, truth, the gap between reality and dream) does not resolve into straight lines.

Once you accept this, the prose stops feeling like an obstacle and starts feeling like weather you’re moving through. Let it wash over you. The meaning accumulates.

The people on the bus, and what they mean

Young chose her characters’ names with great care. Moses Hunnecker, the bus driver calling on God while driving recklessly, is named for the prophet who led people through the wilderness but never reached the promised land himself. His enormous hair (he never cuts it), his whiskey, his invocations: he is a figure of chaotic faith, which is to say, faith under pressure. Vera imagines he has a wife and children at home. She may be inventing this entirely. The novel will keep doing this to you: offering you something that might be real, might be projection, might be both.

Madge is short for Madeleine, which means “woman from the high tower.” She is pregnant with a child that may not be her husband’s, trapped in a marriage she didn’t choose, returning to a town she doesn’t want to return to. Homer, whose name means “hostage” or “pledge”, sleeps on, peacefully, pledged to a life whose complications he does not register. The contrast between them is one of the book’s first quiet jokes: the woman who knows too much, awake and suffering; the man who knows too little, peacefully asleep.

What to hold onto

Roadside, the bus passes a double-headed man standing in the mist, staring at nothing. Young uses this figure, strange and briefly glimpsed, to open one of the chapter’s great philosophical passages about the illusion of individual selfhood: the idea that every face, every soul, every heart is somehow interconnected, that the borders of the self are not where we think they are.

It sounds abstract, but hold this image. The double-headed man will make sense later in ways you cannot yet anticipate. This is a book that rewards trust.

What to watch for as you keep reading

Miss MacIntosh’s whaler’s hat and rubber mackintosh coat and her name itself comes from Charles MacIntosh, who invented waterproofed cloth. She is, literally and symbolically, a woman who keeps the rain out. Keep noticing what she wears, what she refuses to wear, and when.

The mist. Young will use fog and grayness throughout the novel as the atmosphere of uncertainty, the dissolution of clear borders between real and imagined. The “endless grayness” of this first chapter is not just a weather condition.

Vera has a tendency to imagine backstories for the people around her. She does not know that Moses has a family, she supposes it. This habit of supposing is central to who Vera is, and to what the novel is doing.

What to hold onto

If you feel lost, come back to this: a young woman on a bus in the grey American Midwest is looking for the only person who ever told her the truth, and she isn’t sure if that person is alive or dead. Everything else, every digression, every philosophic spiral, every strange figure in the mist is Vera’s mind trying to prepare itself for what she might find.

That search is the spine of the entire novel. You now have it in your hands.
