Summary

Vera thinks of all she could have confessed to Esther. Her mother is dead. The sea swept away the house with its golden towers, leaving chandeliers strewn along the shore, and only the string of pearls she wears remains to her. She is mourning three deaths. At the end, Catherine rose from her bed and walked. Mr. Spitzer carried her into the garden by the sea and declared his love. He told her he was really Peron, and that it was Joachim who had died. But Catherine had already died in his arms before he spoke. He also lost his old valet, the clam-digger, in the storm. Vera sees Miss MacIntosh anew: "Whatever is average is the dream. Whatever is hidden is the revealed." At dawn in the hotel corridor she meets a stone deaf man, who lost his hearing at fourteen. They become lovers, and he whispers that their child will be his ear. They plan to marry and invite everyone, the flowers and birds and Esther Longtree, who hangs a misspelled sign in the restaurant window, "Owt to luntsch. bee bak in a whale."

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, Vera reflects on the death of her mother, Catheine, the destruction of the house with its golden turrets and towers by the sea, and the scattering of chandeliers along the shore. This passage sets a tone of mourning and the dissolution of past grandeur and illusions. The only remaining possession is a string of pearls worn by Vera, symbolizing a fragile connection to her lost heritage and memories. Vera mourns "three deaths" and is "in love with an older love than memory provided".
  • A significant section meditates on the experience of deafness as a metaphor for exile and loss. Vera compares the deaf to marble statues who perceive much but miss the vital auditory dimension of reality. The silence is described as a cold, alien world, yet it also holds a unique, possibly more beautiful mode of communication beyond speech. This passage deepens the theme of isolation and the limits of human perception, emphasizing that even those who hear do not perceive everything, while the deaf may be haunted by imagined sounds or the absence of words.
  • The narrative details Catherine's gradual return from a drug-induced dreamlike state to a fragile awareness before her death. Mr. Spitzer, who had loved Catherine secretly and faithfully, waited for a moment to confess his love, which ultimately came too late. The image of Mr. Spitzer carrying Catherine in his arms into the garden by the sea, speaking of his long-buried love as the sea and nature respond around them, is poignant and symbolic of unfulfilled love and the mystery of death. Catherine dies as Mr. Spitzer speaks, underscoring the bittersweet timing and the intersection of life, death, and love.
  • Mr. Spitzer reveals that he is actually Peron/Jock, not the dead brother Joachim, introducing a complex identity theme and the idea of living with the memory of a lost self. His grief over Catherine’s death and the loss of the old clam-digger valet is profound. The old clam-digger, a loyal companion who drowned in the storm, symbolizes the loss of grounding and connection in a world slipping into chaos and dissolution.
  • The descriptions of the old clam-digger’s habits (burying himself in the sand, being mistaken for part of the landscape, developing nervous tics) serve as a metaphor for the human condition in the face of time and loss. Despite physical preservation, there is a spiritual or intellectual departure, resulting in a "living corpse." This passage contemplates the nature of life, death, and decay in a subtle, haunting way.
  • Toward the end of the chapter, Vera hears the voice of a stone deaf man who, though unable to hear, speaks and sings in a world of silence. His voice, full of longing, loss, and fragmented thoughts, expresses the paradox of communication without sound. Vera falls in love with this silent traveler, highlighting a theme of love transcending conventional senses and communication. The deaf man’s reflections on missed sounds and the imaginary world emphasize the elusive boundary between reality and dream.
  • Throughout the chapter, there is a recurring motif of life as a dream, of love preceding words, and of silence having its own language. Vera and the stone deaf man imagine a wedding attended by wildflowers, birds, and nature itself, a symbolic union beyond earthly conventions. The chapter closes on a note of tender acceptance and mystery, with Vera embracing love and silence as intertwined realities.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Vera

  • Reflective and deeply emotional, Vera grapples with mourning, love, and the nature of existence.

Esther Longtree

  • Mentioned as someone Vera might have confided in but who perhaps would not fully understand or be interested. Represents a contrasting perspective on loss and endurance.

Mr. Spitzer

  • A complex figure who reveals his true identity as Peron, the brother of Joachim. He has waited years to confess his love and to close a chapter on the past. His grief is profound, encompassing the loss of Catherine, the old clam-digger, and his own illusions.

Catherine

  • Once beautiful and dreamlike, she lived in a state between life and death, sustained by drugs and illusions. Her death and the destruction of her home symbolize the dissolution of a dream or an era.

The Old Clam-Digger (Valet)

  • A loyal servant and companion to Mr. Spitzer, embodying endurance and the passage of time. His disappearance in the storm represents another loss for Mr. Spitzer.

The Stone Deaf Man

  • A poignant figure who cannot hear but communicates and experiences the world through other senses. He is symbolic of isolation yet profound inner life and love beyond words. The woman he is with when Vera first meets him is Lucie Bell.

Lucie Bell

  • She appears as a complex and symbolic figure intertwined deeply with themes of love, silence, and loss. She is depicted not merely as a character but almost as an embodiment of elusive and silent love within a world fraught with communication barriers and existential solitude. Lucie Bell is portrayed as a "silent girl" who is intimately connected to silence and absence of speech. The narrator, Vera, hears a man's voice repeatedly calling out to her, lamenting that Lucie Bell be "a little bird girl with such a small, pecking face and such long, thin legs" who "make[s] too much noise" and is "not my love" because she cannot answer him or give love in return through words. This man’s voice speaks of a deep yearning for a silent love, one that transcends verbal communication, which reflects the novel’s broader preoccupation with the limits and possibilities of communication. Lucie Bell represents a love that exists beyond spoken language, a theme emphasized by the stone-deaf man's narrative. He explains how he hears not with ears but with his eyes and fingers, experiencing the world through a different sensory reality. Lucie Bell’s silence and absence echo the deaf man’s experience, symbolizing a love that must be understood through nonverbal means, through "the tongues of silence speaking the name of love". Her silence is not emptiness but a different form of communication and connection, one that is accessible only through deep empathy or alternative perception. The man who calls out to her also expresses feelings of abandonment and unfaithfulness, suggesting that Lucie Bell left him or was "fickle," loving a jukebox or a "tired marathon skater" more than him. This reflects a tension between presence and absence, fidelity and loss, which permeates the narrative. Lucie Bell’s departure before Vera arrives adds to the sense of missed connections and unfulfilled love in the novel. Although Vera never sees or hears Lucie Bell directly, she deeply loves the deaf man who loved Lucie Bell, indicating that Lucie Bell serves as a bridge or catalyst for Vera’s understanding of love in a silent, fragmented world. Lucie Bell’s absence and silence foreground Vera’s own quest for connection amidst loss and the failure of conventional communication. Lucie Bell can be seen as part of the novel’s meditation on sensory loss, altered perception, and the mysteries of human connection. Just as the narrator reflects on the deafness, blindness, and the nature of communication beyond the ear and eye, Lucie Bell embodies the challenge of loving and being loved when traditional modes of interaction fail or are absent.

Research: Resources

  • Nepenthe is a mythical, ancient Greek potion or drug used to induce forgetfulness of pain, sorrow, or grief. It is famously mentioned in Homer's Odyssey as a gift to Helen of Troy to ease her suffering.
  • Scaling refers to the adjustment of size, scope, or intensity, with meanings ranging from enlarging/reducing dimensions in mathematics and design to increasing business operations, or removing deposits from teeth. It fundamentally involves changing the scale of a subject.
  • A jackdaw is a small, highly social bird in the crow family, known for its striking pale-gray eyes and a distinctive silver-gray "shawl" on the back of its head. It is commonly found in urban areas, farmland, and woodlands across Europe, Asia, and North Africa.
  • Tatterdemalion describes a person wearing ragged, torn, or disreputable clothing, or refers to something in a state of decay, filth, or disrepair. Originating in the early 1600s, this term often used for a "ragamuffin" implies a shabby, unkempt appearance. As an adjective, it means ragged or dilapidated.
  • Gannets are seabirds, closely related to boobies. They are known as 'solan' or 'solan goose' in Scotland. A common misconception is that the Scottish name is 'guga' but this is the Gaelic name referring to the chicks only.
  • The Arctic tern is a tern in the family Laridae. This bird has a circumpolar breeding distribution covering the Arctic and sub-Arctic regions of Europe, Asia, and North America.
  • “beach cherry” Eugenia reinwardtiana is a shrub to small tree in the family Myrtaceae. Native to tropical forests in Indonesia, the Australian state of Queensland, and many Pacific Islands, its common names include Cedar Bay cherry, beach cherry, Australian beach cherry, mountain stopper, nīoi, and a'abang.
  • “eel grass” Zostera is a small genus of widely distributed seagrasses, commonly called marine eelgrass, or simply seagrass or eelgrass. The genus Zostera contains 15 species.
  • The hymns Miss MacIntosh sings reveal a profound spiritual dimension to her character. While she often dismisses religious belief as "mockery of faith" or "human exaggerations", these songs from her childhood represent an enduring, if conflicted, faith. They expose her inner struggles with vulnerability, physical and emotional wounds, and the existential human condition of being lost and in need of guidance. These moments of singing, even as she performs mundane tasks, highlight the tension between her practical life and her deep, unarticulated spiritual longings, demonstrating that her common sense and brusqueness are often a defense against profound personal suffering and a search for meaning in a chaotic world.
  • "Lo, she would lift her eyes to the hills from whence cometh help." This references Psalm 121, often the basis of hymns about looking to the hills for help.
  • "Thou didst turn Thy face from me, and I was troubled" echoes lament or penitential psalms.
  • "For Thou art my strong rock and my castle, so also be my guide." This refers to God as a "strong rock and castle," a common biblical metaphor used in hymns.
  • "Thou hast covered me, and Thou hast numbered the hairs of my head, and Thou hast closed the wound of my side. Thou hast plucked out the arrows." These lines evoke biblical imagery associated with God’s protection and healing, reminiscent of hymns about divine care and salvation.
  • "Song of the shepherd who would lead all sheep home, losing none in narrow defiles" alludes to the Good Shepherd theme common in Christian hymns.
  • "Song of the Rock of Ages cleft for me" directly mentions the famous hymn "Rock of Ages."
  • "Songs of the Rugged Cross" refers to the hymn "The Old Rugged Cross," focused on the crucifixion.
  • Songs of the shaded valley, of the still stream" evoke traditional hymn imagery of peaceful pastoral scenes symbolizing God's care.
  • "Songs of those who reel to and fro and stagger like a drunken man, of the lost soul, of the pinioned wings" suggest hymns that address human frailty, being lost, and spiritual struggle.
  • Songs "of the lighthouse beam which lights the way to the poor wanderer tossed upon the rolling waves far from any harbor" is a metaphor often used in hymns about guidance and salvation.
  • "Awake, thou lute and harp, she sang. I myself will awake right early." This references the hymn "I Will Awake and Watch with Tears," based on Psalm 59.
  • "The waters have drowned us. The stream has gone over our soul." echoes lament themes of being overwhelmed, common in many hymns.
  • “When the white girls turned their faces away from him, an old Negro woman down the road said to him, And what difference, little boy, for you be a man, don’t you, and you dream on! You be like us now. All must suffers for something that be not their fault, you because your skin be white and we because our skin be black…” This sudden inclusion of a Negro woman plays into the Magical Negro trope in cinema, television, and literature. The character often has no past but simply appears one day to help the white protagonist. They usually have some sort of vague magical power. The character is patient and wise, often dispensing various words of wisdom. Racism historians traced the trope to the late fifteenth century and early sixteenth century. The Magical Negro stereotype serves as a plot device to help the white protagonist get out of trouble, typically through helping the white character recognize his own faults, overcome them and teaching him to be a better person. Although the character may have magical powers, their magic is used to help and enlighten a white male character.
  • Vera comes to terms with the other adults in her life:
  • Catherine: Vera’s view of her mother changes drastically by the end of the novel. While Vera used to see her mother as a beautiful lady sleeping in her swan boat, Vera comes to terms with her mother's death and sees her mother as poor, as if she had been evicted from her house long before she died and wandered like a beggar through the streets. This is vastly different from the beautiful woman Vera once idolized.
  • Mr. Spitzer: Vera learns the truth about Mr. Spitzer's identity, that he was Peron, not Joachim, and that his life had been a "celestial joke". She understands his love for her mother, a love hidden and unfulfilled for many years. Vera sees him as someone who has lost everything and is living in the past. She recognizes his grief over the loss of the old clam-digger was greater than that of her mother.
  • Miss MacIntosh: Vera acknowledges that her love for Miss MacIntosh came late, only when she saw beyond the surface of her character. Vera realizes that Miss MacIntosh's apparent mediocrity hid a phantasmagoric dream. Vera understands that despite Miss MacIntosh's seemingly sensible nature, she was drawn to the ocean's depths and eventually drowned.
  • Vera says, "Esther Longtree would be me," this holds deep symbolic and thematic significance. This statement reflects Vera's identification with Esther Longtree as a representation of a universal, enduring love and connection to all forms of life and experience, especially those that are broken, silent, or marginalized. Throughout the narrative, Vera contemplates profound themes of loss, silence, death, and the complexities of human love and perception. Esther Longtree emerges as a figure who embraces life and love despite her own hardships, embodying a kind of maternal, nurturing spirit who "loved us all and all things hurt or silent or broken or dead or living". Vera’s final identification with Esther suggests a merging of identities, where Vera aspires to embody Esther’s compassion, endurance, and openness to the multiplicity of loves and sufferings that define human existence. Esther is not presented as a traditional role model in terms of success or societal status but rather as a symbol of the "average," the "hidden," and the dream-like qualities of life and love that transcend ordinary perception. Vera recognizes in Esther a power and enchantment beneath the surface that she had been too wrapped up in herself to fully perceive earlier. Esther represents a bridge between the ordinary and the extraordinary, the visible and the hidden, the living and the dreaming. By choosing Esther as her role model, Vera acknowledges the importance of embracing the "average" and the "hidden" aspects of life, which can "crumble into the most phantasmagoric dream that man has dreamed" and that "all the shining waters dream beyond the surface cold and bare". Esther’s figure embodies the theme that through acceptance of loss, silence, and the unseen dimensions of existence, one can attain a deeper, more inclusive love and one that is not dependent on words or conventional communication but flows through "tongues of silence" and "all our loves". Vera’s statement is not merely an adoption of Esther’s persona but a profound recognition of the values Esther symbolizes: compassion for the silent and broken, an embrace of the unseen dimensions of reality, and a love that transcends ordinary understanding. It marks a transcendence of Vera’s earlier isolation and mourning toward a more universal, interconnected understanding of love and existence and an acceptance of the dream-like, silent world that Esther inhabits and loves. Vera chooses Esther Longtree as her role model because Esther represents the enduring, silent love that encompasses all life’s contradictions, sufferings, and mysteries, and through this identification, Vera finds a way to affirm her own place in the continuum of love and loss that the novel so poignantly explores.
  • The final chapter marks a significant shift in Vera's narrative. After recounting the stories of Esther Longtree, Miss MacIntosh, Mr. Spitzer and her mother, Vera turns her attention to her own experiences and a newfound love with a stone deaf man. This signals a move towards personal redemption and finding meaning amidst the chaos and illusions that permeate the novel.
  • Vera's relationship with the stone deaf man highlights the theme of communication beyond conventional means. Since he cannot hear, their connection relies on visual cues, touch, and unspoken understanding. This echoes the novel's broader exploration of how people connect despite their individual limitations and the illusory nature of reality.
  • The stone deaf man's perspective offers a unique insight into the world of silence. He claims that all the waters of creation had smashed against his ears, leaving him stone deaf. He is bombarded by loud noises in his mind, but it is a silent world. Silence allows Vera and her lover to connect on a deeper level, transcending the superficiality of spoken words. The silence also seems to be a way for him to be closer to death.
  • The stone deaf man's experiences also emphasize the importance of imagination. Having lost his hearing at fourteen, he lives in a world where sounds are memories or creations of his mind. This reinforces the novel's recurring motif of the power of imagination to shape reality and find beauty in the face of adversity.
  • The relationship between Vera and the stone deaf man shows an acceptance for one another. He accepts that she can hear and she accepts that he cannot. This can be compared to the acceptance that Miss MacIntosh has for death and Vera has for her mother's opium addiction.
  • Recurring Themes
  • The line between illusion and reality continues to blur in this chapter. The stone deaf man's perception of the world is shaped by his unique sensory experience, highlighting the subjective nature of reality. He says that life is imaginary and this is an imaginary world.
  • Vera's loss of her pearls symbolizes the shedding of material possessions and the pursuit of deeper meaning. Her relationship with the stone deaf man offers a path towards redemption, as she finds love and connection in unexpected circumstances.
  • The chapter explores different facets of love – maternal love, romantic love, and universal love. Vera's love for the stone deaf man transcends physical limitations and societal norms, representing a pure and unconditional form of affection. She feels elegiac even before her love arrives.
  • The thought of death, especially Vera's death, is brought up many times. The stone deaf man cannot hear the silent horn that Gabriel will blow.
  • Concluding Thoughts
  • The ending remains ambiguous, leaving the reader to ponder the ultimate meaning of Vera's journey. The novel does not offer easy answers but instead celebrates the complexities and contradictions of human existence.
  • The final chapter reinforces the novel's celebration of the unconventional and the marginalized. Vera's choice to embrace a relationship with a stone deaf man challenges societal expectations and highlights the beauty of finding love in unexpected places.
  • The final chapter suggests that the power of imagination and the capacity for love can transcend the limitations of reality. Despite the losses and disillusionments Vera has experienced, she finds solace and meaning in her connection with the stone deaf man, embracing a world where silence speaks volumes and love knows no bounds.
  • Vera and the stone deaf man plan to get married, and they plan to invite everyone. The stone deaf man believes that their procreation will provide life that can transcend his limits. There is hope because the next generation will lead the way. I explore Marguerite Young's work on Substack.
