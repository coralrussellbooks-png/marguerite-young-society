To All My Darlings

Chapter 82

On Chapter 82 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 13, 2026

← Chapter 81 · Home

Content note: Vera’s mother, Catherine Cartwheel, dies after a lifetime of opium addiction.

Summary

Vera thinks of all she could have confessed to Esther. Her mother is dead. Weaned at last from her drugs, Catherine rose from her bed and walked, and suddenly she was old, so changed that no one would have known her. Riding with her in the old surrey at the sea’s edge, Mr. Spitzer declared his long love and told her he was really Peron, and that it was Joachim who had died, but she had died before he spoke. A great storm then swept away the house with its golden towers and drowned the old clam-digger, leaving chandeliers strewn along the shore; only the string of pearls Vera wears remains. Vera sees Miss MacIntosh anew, “Whatever is average is the dream. Whatever is hidden is the revealed,” and remembers her singing the psalms and hymns of her childhood over the ironing board. At the Tavern Vera hears a man shouting all night to Lucie Bell, a girl who had already left him, and at dawn she meets him, a stone deaf man, deaf since he was fourteen. They become lovers, he whispers that their child will be their ear, and they plan to marry and invite everyone, the flowers and the birds and Esther Longtree. “Esther Longtree would be me.” Esther hangs a misspelled sign in the restaurant window: “Owt to luntsch. bee bak in a whale.”

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, Vera reflects on the death of her mother, Catherine, the destruction of the house with its golden turrets and towers by the sea, and the scattering of chandeliers along the shore. The only remaining possession is a string of pearls worn by Vera. Vera mourns “three deaths” and is “in love with an older love than memory provided.”
  • A significant section meditates on the experience of deafness. Vera compares the deaf to marble statues who perceive much but miss the vital auditory dimension of reality. The silence is described as a cold, alien world, yet it also holds a unique, possibly more beautiful mode of communication beyond speech. Even those who hear do not perceive everything, while the deaf may be haunted by imagined sounds or the absence of words.
  • The narrative details Catherine’s gradual return from a drug-induced dreamlike state to a fragile awareness before her death. Mr. Spitzer, who had loved Catherine secretly and faithfully, waited for a moment to confess his love, which ultimately came too late. Mr. Spitzer holds Catherine in his arms in the old surrey at the sea’s edge, speaking of his long-buried love as the sea and nature respond around them. But Catherine “had died at the beginning of his whispers,” in fact “had died before he could speak.”
  • Mr. Spitzer declares that he is Peron, not the dead brother Joachim, though Young said that he himself does not know which brother he is (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983). His grief over Catherine’s death and the loss of the old clam-digger valet is profound. The old clam-digger, a loyal companion, drowned in the storm.
  • The descriptions of the old clam-digger’s habits (burying himself in the sand, being mistaken for part of the landscape, developing nervous tics) serve as a metaphor for the human condition in the face of time and loss. Despite physical preservation, there is a spiritual or intellectual departure, resulting in a “living corpse.” This passage contemplates the nature of life, death, and decay in a subtle, haunting way.
  • Toward the end of the chapter, Vera hears the voice of a stone deaf man who, though unable to hear, speaks and sings in a world of silence. His voice, full of longing, loss, and fragmented thoughts, expresses the paradox of communication without sound. Vera falls in love with this silent traveler.
  • Throughout the chapter, there is a recurring motif of life as a dream, of love preceding words, and of silence having its own language. Vera and the stone deaf man imagine a wedding attended by wildflowers, birds, and nature itself, a symbolic union beyond earthly conventions. The chapter closes on a note of tender acceptance and mystery, with Vera embracing love and silence as intertwined realities.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “Whatever is average is the dream. Whatever is hidden is the revealed.” What has Vera learned about Miss MacIntosh?
  • “My beginning was my end, and my end was my beginning.” How does the novel end where it began?
  • “Perhaps there is always a sense missing, that sense which would make the world real.” What sense is missing in Vera?

Characters

  • Mr. Spitzer tells Catherine he is Peron, but “she was dead, had died at the beginning of his whispers.” Why must the confession come too late?
  • Vera loves the stone deaf man “as a mother might love a child” and as a woman loves a man. Why both?
  • Of Miss MacIntosh: “Her doubt was her own wound.” What else did she keep hidden?

Connections

  • “Esther Longtree would be me.” What does Vera take from Esther?
  • Esther’s sign reads “Owt to luntsch. bee bak in a whale.” Why end the novel with a misspelling?
  • The old horse turns into “the golden mare.” Where else has the novel shown one thing passing for another?

Reflection

  • “There are ways more beautiful than the ear, the eye.” What are they?
  • “Life is imaginary, and this be an imaginary world.” Do you agree?
  • The lovers “would invite everybody to our wedding,” the flowers, the birds, the old mule, and Esther. Who would be at yours?

Research: Characters

Vera

  • Reflective and deeply emotional, Vera grapples with mourning, love, and the nature of existence.

Esther Longtree

  • Mentioned as someone Vera might have confided in but who perhaps would not fully understand or be interested. Represents a contrasting perspective on loss and endurance.

Mr. Spitzer

  • A complex figure who reveals his true identity as Peron, the brother of Joachim. He has waited years to confess his love and to close a chapter on the past. His grief is profound, encompassing the loss of Catherine, the old clam-digger, and his own illusions.

Catherine

  • Once beautiful and dreamlike, she lived in a state between life and death, sustained by drugs and illusions. Her death and the destruction of her home symbolize the dissolution of a dream or an era.

The Old Clam-Digger (Valet)

  • A loyal servant and companion to Mr. Spitzer, embodying endurance and the passage of time. His disappearance in the storm represents another loss for Mr. Spitzer.

The Stone Deaf Man

  • A poignant figure who cannot hear but communicates and experiences the world through other senses. He has a profound inner life and love beyond words. Young said that “the deaf are normal in every way” and that those who lack a sense “develop a hypersensitivity in every other way” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). His lost love is Lucie Bell, who “had left him before I ever came”; Vera never saw her.

Lucie Bell

  • Lucie Bell is the deaf man’s lost love, gone before Vera came, so Vera never sees or hears her. He calls her “a little bird girl with such a small, pecking face and such long, thin legs,” fickle, loving a jukebox or a “tired marathon skater” more than him: “You make too much noise. I am going to leave you. Man loves a silent woman.” He hears with his eyes and fingers and longs for a silent love, and Vera comes to love him “for all the tongues of silence speaking the name of love.”

Research: Themes

Loss and Mourning

See: Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • Vera mourns not only Catherine but also the loss of a dreamlike past, the family estate, and symbolic “pearls.” Mr. Spitzer’s layered grief includes his brother Joachim, Catherine, and the old clam-digger.

Illusion and Reality

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Catherine’s life was sustained by dreams and drugs, blurring the line between waking life and sleep, life and death. Vera questions what is real and what is dream.

Silence and Communication

See: Music, Sound, and Silence

  • Extensive meditation on deafness, silence, and the limitations and beauty of communication beyond words. The stone deaf man represents the world of silence yet full of inner sound and love.

Time and Timelessness

See: Time

  • Time is experienced unevenly, accelerating at moments and suspended in others. Catherine’s slow death, Mr. Spitzer’s long wait, and the clam-digger’s timeless endurance reflect different relationships with time.

Love Beyond Death

See: Death and Mortality · Love

  • Mr. Spitzer’s confession of being Peron and his love for Catherine after her death touches on eternal love transcending mortality.

Nature and the Sea

See: Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos

  • The sea is a powerful force of destruction and transformation, symbolic of change, loss, and perhaps renewal. It carries away the house and treasures but also represents the boundary between worlds.

Further notes from the text

  • Vera’s mourning begins with the house: the sea has swept over the opium paradise, the divan upheld by dolphins dreams under the flood, and no lawyer will be needed for the estate, because nothing is left to administer.
  • Catherine’s last hours are framed as a paradox Mr. Spitzer understood: she would die if her enchantment continued, and she would die if she rose and walked again. She rises, takes a few steps, and dies.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s declaration, that he was really Peron, comes with his usual rolling periods, as if to assure himself that what he says is true, or not quite true. Vera does not decide which.
  • The old clam-digger, Mr. Spitzer’s valet, used to bury himself in the sand with only a hole for his mouth and nose, so that the herring gulls mistook him for a moss animal or a rock. The storm takes him with the house.
  • Left alone like an old barnacled boat, Mr. Spitzer asks how he will pass his time, and the novel turns to Miss MacIntosh’s creed of the average and the middle way, which Vera now sees anew.
  • The songs and hymns of the chapter gather the book’s images, the Rock of Ages, the lighthouse beam for the wanderer, the waters that have gone over the soul, and repeat its oldest line: my beginning was my end, and my end was my beginning.
  • The stone deaf man’s ears are in his fingertips. His former love, Lucie Bell, left him before Vera came. Vera loves him “as a mother might love a child” and as a woman loves a man, and they plan a wedding to which everyone is invited, Esther Longtree included.

Research: Resources

  • “how my mother, after many years of sleeping, arose from her bed and walked”: So did Weissenbach. She “had been able to get out of bed after many, many years, but this is what killed her, so that she turned old overnight. She had never shown the mark of time.” She died in 1943 in New York, and Young, who kept writing to her, learned of it months later from her daughter: “as long as you kept writing to Mother and I read your letters, I felt as if my mother was somehow alive” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983). See Catherine Cartwheel, Marguerite Young on Her Craft, and The History of Opium.
  • “With Braille, he teaches”: Louis Braille, blinded as a child, published his raised-dot alphabet in 1829. The Seeing Eye, the first American school for guide dogs, was founded in 1929 by Dorothy Harrison Eustis. See Eyes and Blindness.
  • “One drop more of nepenthe”: Nepenthe is a mythical, ancient Greek potion or drug used to induce forgetfulness of pain, sorrow, or grief. In Homer’s Odyssey (Book 4), Helen had it as a gift from Polydamna of Egypt and mixed it into the wine to ease the grief of Menelaus and his guests. See Greek Mythology and Homer’s Epics.
  • “seeming to be scaling like the moon”: Here “scaling” means flaking off in scales, as old paint or skin does: the old white horse is “streaked with grey and scaling, flaking like the moon.” See Horses and Carriages.
    • Scaling refers to the adjustment of size, scope, or intensity, with meanings ranging from enlarging/reducing dimensions in mathematics and design to increasing business operations, or removing deposits from teeth. It fundamentally involves changing the scale of a subject.
  • “if he did not speak now, he might never speak”: Young took the scene from the death of the poet Elinor Wylie (December 16, 1928) as she remembered it: William Rose Benét explained his feelings to her at length, “and then he realized that she was sitting there dead and had never heard a word. She missed his confession” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983). See Joachim Spitzer and Catherine Cartwheel.
  • “the reason no one had heard his music since Peron’s death was that he was Peron”: Young compared Mr. Spitzer’s “great impersonation in this life” to “the Titchborn claimant” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965), Arthur Orton, who claimed in 1866 to be the lost heir Roger Tichborne and was convicted of perjury in 1874. In her first draft “Mr. Spitzer knew that he was really Peyron the gambler,” which “seemed so tricky to me” that she rewrote it so that he does not know (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983). See Joachim Spitzer, Marguerite Young on Her Craft, and Peron Spitzer.
  • “the old nag turning into the golden mare”: Young read about racehorse “ringers” painted to race under false colors: “I read of one marvelous case at Saratoga Springs when it was a rain storm in the midst of the race, and people with absolute horror looked out to see the old nag turning into the speeder, losing the mottled coat.” She also read lives of John W. “Bet-a-Million” Gates and Diamond Jim Brady (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See Joachim Spitzer and Horses and Carriages.
  • “for hers would be an urn burial”: Sir Thomas Browne’s Urne-Buriall (1658), a meditation on ancient burial urns, ends: “Man is a Noble Animal, splendid in ashes, and pompous in the grave.” See Death and Mortality.
  • “like a jackdaw picking up sticks”: A jackdaw is a small, highly social bird in the crow family, known for its striking pale-gray eyes and a distinctive silver-gray “shawl” on the back of its head. It is commonly found in urban areas, farmland, and woodlands across Europe, Asia, and North Africa. See Birds.
  • “tatterdemalions”: Tatterdemalion describes a person wearing ragged, torn, or disreputable clothing, or refers to something in a state of decay, filth, or disrepair. Originating in the early 1600s, this term often used for a “ragamuffin” implies a shabby, unkempt appearance. As an adjective, it means ragged or dilapidated.
  • “Snow-breasted gannets and Arctic terns”: Gannets are seabirds, closely related to boobies. They are known as ‘solan’ or ‘solan goose’ in Scotland. A common misconception is that the Scottish name is ‘guga’ but this is the Gaelic name referring to the chicks only. (Wikipedia) See Birds.
    • The Arctic tern is a tern in the family Laridae. This bird has a circumpolar breeding distribution covering the Arctic and sub-Arctic regions of Europe, Asia, and North America. (Wikipedia) See Exploration and the Poles.
  • “beach plum bushes and beach cherry”: On the Atlantic coast the beach plum (Prunus maritima) and the sand cherry (Prunus pumila) grow wild in the dunes.
    • “beach cherry” Eugenia reinwardtiana is a shrub to small tree in the family Myrtaceae. Native to tropical forests in Indonesia, the Australian state of Queensland, and many Pacific Islands, its common names include Cedar Bay cherry, beach cherry, Australian beach cherry, mountain stopper, nīoi, and a’abang. (Wikipedia) This tropical beach cherry does not grow on the Atlantic shore.
    • “eel grass” Zostera is a small genus of widely distributed seagrasses, commonly called marine eelgrass, or simply seagrass or eelgrass. The genus Zostera contains 15 species. (Wikipedia)
  • “Map-makers of the heavenly bodies”: In 1930 the International Astronomical Union adopted the constellation boundaries drawn by Eugène Delporte, dividing the whole sky among 88 constellations so that no star falls outside. See Astronomy.
  • “as if this were the coast of Charon”: Charon ferried the dead across the river Styx into Hades, for a coin laid in the mouth. See Greek Mythology and Death and Mortality.
  • “golden chanticleer”: Chauntecleer is the proud rooster of Chaucer’s “Nun’s Priest’s Tale,” nearly carried off by the fox. See Birds.
  • “Thou hast wrapped me in Thy fleece”: The hymns Miss MacIntosh sings reveal a profound spiritual dimension to her character. While she keeps her doubt to herself (“Her doubt was her own wound”) and will not be “the stumbling block in another’s path,” these songs from her childhood represent an enduring, if conflicted, faith. They expose her inner struggles with vulnerability, physical and emotional wounds, and the existential human condition of being lost and in need of guidance. These moments of singing, even as she performs mundane tasks, highlight the tension between her practical life and her deep, unarticulated spiritual longings, demonstrating that her common sense and brusqueness are often a defense against profound personal suffering and a search for meaning in a chaotic world. See Miss MacIntosh and The Bible.
    • “Lo, she would lift her eyes to the hills from whence cometh help.” This references Psalm 121, often the basis of hymns about looking to the hills for help. “I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills, from whence cometh my help” (Psalm 121:1, KJV).
    • “Thou didst turn Thy face from me, and I was troubled” echoes Psalm 30:7: “thou didst hide thy face, and I was troubled” (KJV).
    • “For Thou art my strong rock and my castle, so also be my guide.” This refers to God as a “strong rock and castle,” a common biblical metaphor used in hymns. In the psalter of the Book of Common Prayer (Coverdale’s translation), Psalm 31:3 reads, “For thou art my strong rock, and my castle: be thou also my guide,” nearly Miss MacIntosh’s very words.
    • “Thou hast covered me, and Thou hast numbered the hairs of my head, and Thou hast closed the wound of my side. Thou hast plucked out the arrows.” These lines evoke biblical imagery associated with God’s protection and healing, reminiscent of hymns about divine care and salvation. “The very hairs of your head are all numbered” (Matthew 10:30); “thou hast covered me in my mother’s womb” (Psalm 139:13); “one of the soldiers with a spear pierced his side” (John 19:34); “thine arrows stick fast in me” (Psalm 38:2, KJV).
    • “Song of the shepherd who would lead all sheep home, losing none in narrow defiles” alludes to the Good Shepherd theme common in Christian hymns. “I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep” (John 10:11, KJV).
    • “Song of the Rock of Ages cleft for me” directly mentions the famous hymn “Rock of Ages.” Augustus Toplady’s hymn (published 1775): “Rock of Ages, cleft for me, / Let me hide myself in thee.”
    • “Songs of the Rugged Cross” refers to the hymn “The Old Rugged Cross,” focused on the crucifixion. George Bennard wrote it in 1912.
    • Songs “of the shaded valley, of the still stream” evoke traditional hymn imagery of peaceful pastoral scenes symbolizing God’s care. “He leadeth me beside the still waters” (Psalm 23:2, KJV); “As the hart panteth after the water brooks” (Psalm 42:1, KJV).
    • “Songs of those who reel to and fro and stagger like a drunken man, of the lost soul, of the pinioned wings” suggest hymns that address human frailty, being lost, and spiritual struggle. “They reel to and fro, and stagger like a drunken man, and are at their wit’s end” (Psalm 107:27, KJV).
    • Songs “of the lighthouse beam which lights the way to the poor wanderer tossed upon the rolling waves far from any harbor” is a metaphor often used in hymns about guidance and salvation.
    • “Awake, thou lute and harp, she sang. I myself will awake right early.” This is Psalm 57 in the psalter of the Book of Common Prayer: “awake, lute and harp: I myself will awake right early.” The King James has “awake, psaltery and harp: I myself will awake early” (Psalm 57:8).
    • “The waters have drowned us. The stream has gone over our soul.” is Psalm 124 in the psalter of the Book of Common Prayer: “Yea, the waters had drowned us: and the stream had gone over our soul.”
  • “My beginning was my end, and my end was my beginning”: Mary, Queen of Scots, embroidered the motto “En ma fin gît mon commencement” (“In my end is my beginning”); T. S. Eliot’s “East Coker” (1940) opens “In my beginning is my end” and closes “In my end is my beginning.” See Miss MacIntosh.
  • “Little boidies don’t have no tits”: The stone-deaf man was Young’s friend Howard Mitcham, a deaf painter in Greenwich Village. “He used to love to sing this, little boydies don’t have no tits. Little boydies don’t have no umbilical cords,” and “he knew that I was putting him in” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983). For a magazine article on the deaf she interviewed deaf leaders in New York and heard the Lord’s Prayer in sign language at St. Mark’s (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See The Stone Deaf Man, Deafness and Sign Language, and Marguerite Young on Her Craft.
  • “Man loves a silent woman”: In Ben Jonson’s comedy Epicoene, or The Silent Woman (1609), Morose, who cannot bear noise, marries a bride for her silence; once wed, she talks without stopping. See Love.
  • “A hand was arisen on the road and spelled out a name for me”: “That was a dream, actual dream of a deaf friend I knew that he solved. He thought he solved a mystery down in Louisiana by the hand speaking to him in a dream” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See The Stone Deaf Man.
  • “like an albatross”: The sailor who shot the albatross wears it around his neck: “Instead of the cross, the Albatross / About my neck was hung” (Coleridge, The Rime of the Ancient Mariner, 1798). See Romantic Poets and Birds.
  • “When the white girls turned their faces away from him”: This sudden inclusion of a Negro woman plays into the Magical Negro trope in cinema, television, and literature. The character often has no past but simply appears one day to help the white protagonist. They usually have some sort of vague magical power. The character is patient and wise, often dispensing various words of wisdom. Racism historians Francisco Bethencourt and John Beusterien trace the trope to the Spanish comedias de negros of the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries. The Magical Negro stereotype serves as a plot device to help the white protagonist get out of trouble, typically through helping the white character recognize his own faults, overcome them and teaching him to be a better person. Although the character may have magical powers, their magic is used to help and enlighten a white male character. (Wikipedia) See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “when Gabriel blew his horn”: “For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God” (1 Thessalonians 4:16, KJV); The Bible does not name the angel who sounds the last trump; the spiritual “Blow Your Trumpet, Gabriel,” printed in Slave Songs of the United States (1867), gives it to Gabriel. See Angels and Archangels and The Bible.
  • “Esther Longtree would be me”: When Vera says, “Esther Longtree would be me,” the line holds deep symbolic and thematic significance. This statement reflects Vera’s identification with Esther Longtree as a representation of a universal, enduring love and connection to all forms of life and experience, especially those that are broken, silent, or marginalized. Throughout the narrative, Vera contemplates profound themes of loss, silence, death, and the complexities of human love and perception. Esther Longtree emerges as a figure who embraces life and love despite her own hardships, embodying a kind of maternal, nurturing spirit who “loved us all and all things hurt or silent or broken or dead or living.” Vera’s final identification with Esther suggests a merging of identities, where Vera aspires to embody Esther’s compassion, endurance, and openness to the multiplicity of loves and sufferings that define human existence. Esther is not presented as a traditional role model in terms of success or societal status but rather as a symbol of the “average,” the “hidden,” and the dream-like qualities of life and love that transcend ordinary perception. Vera recognizes in Esther a power and enchantment beneath the surface that she had been too wrapped up in herself to fully perceive earlier. Esther represents a bridge between the ordinary and the extraordinary, the visible and the hidden, the living and the dreaming. By choosing Esther as her role model, Vera acknowledges the importance of embracing the “average” and the “hidden” aspects of life, which can “crumble into the most phantasmagoric dream that man has dreamed” and that “all the shining waters dream beyond the surface cold and bare.” Esther’s figure embodies the theme that through acceptance of loss, silence, and the unseen dimensions of existence, one can attain a deeper, more inclusive love and one that is not dependent on words or conventional communication but flows through “tongues of silence” and “all our loves.” Vera’s statement is not merely an adoption of Esther’s persona but a profound recognition of the values Esther symbolizes: compassion for the silent and broken, an embrace of the unseen dimensions of reality, and a love that transcends ordinary understanding. It marks a transcendence of Vera’s earlier isolation and mourning toward a more universal, interconnected understanding of love and existence and an acceptance of the dream-like, silent world that Esther inhabits and loves. Vera chooses Esther Longtree as her role model because Esther represents the enduring, silent love that encompasses all life’s contradictions, sufferings, and mysteries, and through this identification, Vera finds a way to affirm her own place in the continuum of love and loss that the novel so poignantly explores. See Esther Longtree and Vera Cartwheel.
    • Vera comes to terms with the other adults in her life:
    • Catherine: Vera’s view of her mother changes drastically by the end of the novel. While Vera used to see her mother as a beautiful lady sleeping in her swan boat, Vera comes to terms with her mother’s death and sees her mother as poor, as if she had been evicted from her house long before she died and wandered like a beggar through the streets. This is vastly different from the beautiful woman Vera once idolized. See Catherine Cartwheel.
    • Mr. Spitzer: Vera learns the truth about Mr. Spitzer’s identity, that he was Peron, not Joachim, and that his life had been a “celestial joke.” She understands his love for her mother, a love hidden and unfulfilled for many years. Vera sees him as someone who has lost everything and is living in the past. She sees that his grief over the loss of the old clam-digger “had seemed far greater than when my mother died.” See Joachim Spitzer.
    • Miss MacIntosh: Vera acknowledges that her love for Miss MacIntosh came late, only when she saw beyond the surface of her character. Vera realizes that Miss MacIntosh’s apparent mediocrity hid a phantasmagoric dream. Vera understands that despite Miss MacIntosh’s seemingly sensible nature, she was drawn to the ocean’s depths and eventually drowned. See Miss MacIntosh.
    • The final chapter marks a significant shift in Vera’s narrative. After recounting the stories of Esther Longtree, Miss MacIntosh, Mr. Spitzer and her mother, Vera turns her attention to her own experiences and a newfound love with a stone deaf man. This signals a move towards personal redemption and finding meaning amidst the chaos and illusions that permeate the novel.
    • Vera’s relationship with the stone deaf man highlights the theme of communication beyond conventional means. Since he cannot hear, their connection relies on visual cues, touch, and unspoken understanding. This echoes the novel’s broader exploration of how people connect despite their individual limitations and the illusory nature of reality.
    • The stone deaf man’s perspective offers a unique insight into the world of silence. He claims that all the waters of creation had smashed against his ears, leaving him stone deaf. He is bombarded by loud noises in his mind, but it is a silent world. Silence allows Vera and her lover to connect on a deeper level, transcending the superficiality of spoken words. The silence also seems to be a way for him to be closer to death.
    • The stone deaf man’s experiences also emphasize the importance of imagination. Having lost his hearing at fourteen, he lives in a world where sounds are memories or creations of his mind. This reinforces the novel’s recurring motif of the power of imagination to shape reality and find beauty in the face of adversity.
    • The relationship between Vera and the stone deaf man shows an acceptance for one another. He accepts that she can hear and she accepts that he cannot. This can be compared to the acceptance that Miss MacIntosh has for death and Vera has for her mother’s opium addiction.
    • The final chapter returns to the novel’s recurring themes.
    • The line between illusion and reality continues to blur in this chapter. The stone deaf man’s perception of the world is shaped by his unique sensory experience, highlighting the subjective nature of reality. He says that life is imaginary and this is an imaginary world.
    • Vera’s loss of everything but the string of pearls she wears symbolizes the shedding of material possessions and the pursuit of deeper meaning. Her relationship with the stone deaf man offers a path towards redemption, as she finds love and connection in unexpected circumstances.
    • The chapter explores different facets of love: maternal love, romantic love, and universal love. Vera’s love for the stone deaf man transcends physical limitations and societal norms, representing a pure and unconditional form of affection. She feels elegiac even before her love arrives.
    • The thought of death, especially Vera’s death, is brought up many times. The stone deaf man cannot hear the silent horn that Gabriel will blow.
    • Some concluding thoughts on the ending follow.
    • The ending remains ambiguous, leaving the reader to ponder the ultimate meaning of Vera’s journey. The novel does not offer easy answers but instead celebrates the complexities and contradictions of human existence.
    • The final chapter reinforces the novel’s celebration of the unconventional and the marginalized. Vera’s choice to embrace a relationship with a stone deaf man challenges societal expectations and highlights the beauty of finding love in unexpected places.
    • The final chapter suggests that the power of imagination and the capacity for love can transcend the limitations of reality. Despite the losses and disillusionments Vera has experienced, she finds solace and meaning in her connection with the stone deaf man, embracing a world where silence speaks volumes and love knows no bounds.
    • Vera and the stone deaf man plan to get married, and they plan to invite everyone. The stone deaf man believes that their procreation will provide life that can transcend his limits. There is hope because the next generation will lead the way.
  • “Owt to luntsch”: The last line was a friend’s idea, about ten years before Young finished: a painter told her to have Esther “hang up a sign in the restaurant window, out to lunch,” “And I said, I’ll do exactly that” (Marguerite Young, interview with Duncan MacDonald, Observation Point, 1965). See Esther Longtree.
