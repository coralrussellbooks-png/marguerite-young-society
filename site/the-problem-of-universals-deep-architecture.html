Movement Two: The Five Pillars in the Traditions

See also: The Problem of Universals

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Philosophy: Aristotle · Marguerite Young on Her Craft · Plato and Platonism · The Problem of Universals

Allusions: Astronomy · Music and Composers · Napoleon and Wellington · The History of Opium

Definition from research

The Problem of Universals is one of the oldest questions in Western philosophy: do general concepts exist independently of the particular things that seem to instantiate them, or are the particulars all there is, with the general concept being nothing more than a name we apply after the fact? The realist says the universal exists; the nominalist says only the particular exists and the universal is an abstraction we impose. The conceptualist tries to split the difference and says the universal exists but only in the mind. None of these positions has ever conclusively won the argument, because the problem is not actually solvable. It is generative. It keeps producing questions that open into other questions, and those questions are the philosophical engine of Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.

Young did not inherit this problem from a textbook. She inherited it from the whole of the literary tradition she inhabited: Plato, Aristotle, the medieval scholastics, the empiricists who tried to dismantle them. She built it into the novel's architecture not as a theme to be resolved but as a structural condition, the same way a building has load-bearing walls. Remove the Problem of Universals from this novel and the novel collapses, because the question of whether universals are real is also the question of whether any of its characters exist in the way they appear to exist, whether any of its truths hold, whether the novel itself is one thing or many.

The most direct statement of the problem arrives early and nakedly: "Every heart is the other heart. Every soul is the other soul. Every face is the other face. The individual is the one illusion." This is the nominalist position pushed past nominalism into something stranger. The nominalist says only particulars exist. Young says the particular itself, the individual, is the illusion, and what is real is the universal heart, the universal soul, the universal face that every particular merely approximates. This is a Platonic claim but Young gives it no comfort. The universal here is not the ideal form shining behind imperfect copies. It is the dissolution of the boundary between self and other, the erasure of the very distinction that makes an individual a particular rather than a universal. The individual is the one illusion not because there is a truer individual somewhere behind appearances, but because individuation itself is the error.

The plural self, the first pillar, is where this plays out most directly in the novel's characterization. Mr. Spitzer cannot locate himself on one side of a boundary because no reliable boundary exists. He tries, as Young writes, to "separate his being into constituent parts or elements and tried to recognize what was necessary and what was accidental or adventitious, what was real and what was unreal," and he cannot do it, because the categories of necessary and accidental, of essential and adventitious, are precisely the categories the Problem of Universals puts in question. The scholastics needed those categories: the essence was the universal, the accidents were the particulars. Mr. Spitzer's inability to perform this separation is not a personal failure. It is Young's argument that the scholastic distinction does not hold, that there is no essence to be separated from the accidents, that Mr. Spitzer is "essentially inessential, having no essence or being, at least not of his own." The plural self is the character who has lived long enough inside the Problem of Universals to stop pretending it has an answer.

Illusion as primary is the pillar that makes the Problem of Universals livable rather than merely devastating. Vera's mother inhabits a world in which the boundaries between general and particular, between concept and instance, have dissolved entirely: a phantom companion might be simultaneously "many persons, for he was not confined to any one form by the laws of nature, which had been suspended in his case." He is a mockingbird, an old woman, an old man, Wellington, a Venetian valet, a murdered gentleman, a blind astronomer. These are not delusions in the clinical sense. They are the mother's mind working correctly inside a universe where the universal and the particular genuinely cannot be separated. The refractions of that "drugged, dazzling atmosphere" produce not chaos but a kind of terrible accuracy: if the individual is the one illusion, then the multiplicity of forms is the truth, and the mother's opium rooms are epistemologically more honest than Miss MacIntosh's flat-footed certainty. Young does not choose between them. She holds both. The illusion is primary not because reality does not exist but because the mind's perception of forms is the only access anyone has to whatever existence is.

Grief as permanent weather finds its expression in the Problem of Universals through the question of what survives particulars. Miss MacIntosh is described as having "the face of every face, the heart of every heart," and this is offered as consolation, as explanation for why her absence cannot be a simple absence. If she was the universal face, the universal heart, she cannot be simply gone the way a particular thing is gone when it ceases to exist. And yet she is gone. The golden bowl is broken. The grief does not dissolve because the universal remains; it intensifies, because the universal is not a thing one can hold or bury or properly mourn. You cannot grieve the face of every face. You can only grieve the particular face you saw walking along the seashore, and that face is irretrievably lost. The Problem of Universals in Young's hands becomes the problem of loss: we love particulars, we lose particulars, but we live inside the general, and the general does not stop. Mr. Spitzer preferring "abstractions and reasonings to the particularities of harsh, incalculable experience" is not a character flaw. It is a survival strategy, and Young treats it with tremendous compassion even as she shows us exactly what it costs him.

The witness pillar is where Young positions the question of what it means to see clearly in a universe where the boundary between universal and particular is unstable. The mute in the novel "had missed all abstract conceptions and ideas for which there were no images, no nesting spider, hermit crab, wild rose, water wheel, swan, star expressing them." The mute cannot access the universal because he has no language; he lives entirely in the particular, the image, the thing present to the senses. This is presented not as freedom but as a kind of loss, the loss of music and law and justice and all those things that exist only as abstractions, only as universals, only in the mind that can move from the particular instance to the general concept. The witness in Young is the one who can hold both: who sees the particular thing in front of them and also perceives the universal behind or through or dissolved into it. Vera's role throughout the novel is exactly this double attention, and it is exhausting, because there is no stable position from which to witness when the thing you are witnessing keeps showing you that it is also everything else.

Recursive construction is where the Problem of Universals becomes the novel's formal argument about itself. Young describes "the end of creation" as being "also the beginning," and this is not just a cyclical image but a structural claim: the novel is built so that each element contains within it every other element, so that the particular instance is also the universal type is also the next particular instance is also the structure that generates all instances. Mr. Spitzer is described as "the composer of his life, of the secret intuitions others thought were not his, of the faded thoughts he himself could not remember, of the imperial abstractions like the separation of the notion of dimension from space, the dream of space from dimension." The separation of dimension from space and the dream of space from dimension: this is not decorative language. This is Young describing what the novel is doing formally, separating the abstract structure from the particular instances that instantiate it, then dreaming the abstract structure back out of those instances, then building both together into something that is neither the universal nor the particular but the relationship between them, which is the only place the Problem of Universals has ever actually lived. The problem has never been solved because it was never meant to be solved. It was meant to be inhabited. Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is 1,198 pages of that inhabitation: the universal heart that is every heart, beating inside the particular chest of one unrepeatable person who walked along one specific seashore, bearded with salt crystals, carrying a lamp made of fireflies, refusing the infinite, insisting on the ground under her feet, which was also the ground under all feet, which was also the only ground there was.
