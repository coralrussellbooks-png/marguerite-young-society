Light and Darkness

In the research: Chapter 3, Chapter 20, Chapter 23, Chapter 48

Definition from research

Chapter 3

  • “The shadow created the light, but the light created the shadow”: Light and darkness carry profound symbolic significance, reflecting the tension between reality and illusion, knowledge and ignorance, life and death. Miss MacIntosh, the practical and no-nonsense nursemaid, embodies a clear preference for reality and common sense or the “light” and is skeptical of illusions, dreams, and the “darkness.” She insists on facing life’s facts plainly, avoiding the “soporific luxury” of dreaming or indulging in fanciful visions. For her, the light symbolizes clarity, truth, and the tangible aspects of existence that must be accepted and dealt with. She values labor, self-forgetfulness, and living in the present moment, and she warns against chasing illusions or “things which the conditions of this mortal frame could not simply provide”. Conversely, darkness in this chapter is associated with illusion, uncertainty, and the unknown. It represents the realm of dreams, memories, and the ethereal, all of which are unstable and elusive. The darkness is also linked to death and the unconscious, a place where boundaries dissolve and individual identity blurs into a collective or universal soul. Vera’s mother, for example, lives in a twilight state between life and death, surrounded by shadows, dreams, and hallucinations, unable to firmly grasp reality. The interplay of light and darkness captures the novel’s thematic exploration of the fragility of human perception and the elusiveness of truth. The light is never fully stable or pure, there is always shadow and ambiguity and the darkness is not merely absence but a realm charged with mystery and the possibility of other forms of knowledge or existence. Miss MacIntosh’s pragmatic insistence on the light and rejection of the darkness is thus a stance against the chaos and uncertainty that the darkness represents, but the novel complicates this by showing how darkness is also an inescapable part of life and consciousness. Light and darkness symbolize the conflict between the tangible, knowable world and the intangible, mysterious realms of dreams and death. Miss MacIntosh’s character aligns with the light, representing order and reason, while the darkness embodies the dreamlike, uncertain, and often threatening aspects of existence. This duality frames much of the novel’s meditation on human experience.

Chapter 20 · Light as a Revealing and Destructive Force

  • Dawn and light symbolize both revelation and destruction. Vera suggests that light has “already killed the heart of Miss MacIntosh” before her physical death, exposing wounds that cannot heal and deprivations that are eternal.
  • The light strips away illusions, revealing the “fearful forehead” and the “bald and naked, cloudless summit” of Miss MacIntosh’s head, symbolizing a harsh unveiling of truth and vulnerability.
  • There’s also a paradoxical beauty in the light, described as “diamond dawn” and “celestial aureole,” which offers hope for redemption and healing, yet simultaneously exposes harsh realities.

Chapter 23 · Critique of Modern Technology: Electric Lights

  • A significant portion of the chapter is devoted to a critical view of electric lighting and its consequences.
  • Miss MacIntosh blames Benjamin Franklin for pioneering electricity, which has disrupted natural cycles of day and night.
  • Electric lights are portrayed as a destroyer of privacy, natural rhythms (sleep and work), and even marital harmony.
  • She laments the loss of darkness, which she sees as essential for personal privacy and the natural order.
  • The chapter contrasts the artificial glare of electric lights with the natural, gentle light of stars and candles used by ancestors.

Chapter 23 · Electric Lights

  • Symbolize modernity’s intrusion into natural human rhythms and privacy.

Chapter 48 · Light, Darkness, and the Celestial Imagery

  • Extensive use of celestial imagery: stars, moonlight, dawn, dusk, the morning and evening star.
  • Light often symbolizes consciousness, life, and hope, while darkness suggests death, mystery, and the unknown.
  • The fading stars symbolize the impermanence of life and the universe.
