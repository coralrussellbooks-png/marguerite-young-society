Summary

Night falls on the bus. The roads are flooded and there are no stars or signposts. The bus waits at a Pennsylvania Railroad crossing while Madge moans in her sleep, accusing every man but her husband. Moses sings, drinks, and holds forth. He refuses ever to cut his hair. He rails against the Democrats and the New Deal, though he never has time to vote. He lives with his old mother, has never married, and talks constantly of pregnant women and of old Doc O’Leary, who once delivered babies and has now lost his mind. Birth and death fill his talk. The chapter leaves open whether Moses is driving the bus or only dreaming that he drives it.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with the depiction of the bus-driver, Moses Hunnecker, losing his way, driving through a landscape of washed-out roads, fallen trees, and empty air. This journey seems endless and cyclical, capturing a sense of existential disorientation and decay. The imagery of “no stars, no sign-posts now,” and “a crazy bus-driver losing his way that he knew too well” evokes a world without guidance or hope, emphasizing the theme of wandering and searching for meaning.
  • A large portion of the chapter focuses on the bus-driver Moses’s refusal to cut his hair. His hair is described as growing continuously, becoming intertwined with his identity and his “immortal” self. It is his “only virtue,” his “angel,” his connection to life amid desolation. The hair is also political as a symbol of his resistance to the Democrats and the societal changes he despises, representing his claim to personal freedom and his soul. His hair grows like “golden tassels tossing in the wind,” full of “burning eyes like the eyes of Argus,” symbolizing watchfulness, memory, and vitality amidst decay.
  • The bus-driver, Moses, is portrayed as a lifelong Republican, openly hostile to the Democrats whom he blames for the country’s ruin (“running this old country straight into its grave.”) He links his hair to his political identity, refusing to let the government control or “tax” even that last piece of himself. His tirades against political corruption, free public projects, and the decline of society are interwoven with his personal narrative and symbolic hair.
  • The chapter outlines a tension between the bus-driver, Moses, and an old, senile doctor, Old Doc/Justice O’Leary, who persists in imagining a world of birth and vitality or a world that no longer exists. The doctor’s practice is described as a delusion, with “no patients but the stormy petrels in a land where were no stormy petrels.” The bus-driver mocks the doctor’s illusions and refuses his medical help, reflecting a conflict between life and death, reality and imagination. This interplay highlights themes of denial, madness, and the persistence of hope or delusion in a dead country.
  • The chapter contains intense imagery of death and desolation from the flooded graves, dead fish, drowned seeds, barren trees, to the wild mares dreaming of pregnancy but carrying only death. Yet, paradoxically, it also speaks of rebirth and cyclical renewal (“you died every minute that you lived... and you were born every minute that you died”) though this cycle appears to be trapped in despair and futility. This duality underscores the existential and metaphysical concerns of the chapter.
  • Towards the end, the bus-driver, Moses, questions his own reality and wonders if he is truly driving or merely dreaming in his four-poster bed. He contemplates death, resurrection, and the meaning of existence. His long hair, the enemies (the Democrats), the old doctor’s empty practice, the empty hotel, and the desolate landscape all blend into a hallucinatory vision of a world on the brink of oblivion. His repeated references to his mother, his hair, and his political stance deepen the sense of personal and societal collapse.
  • The closing passages have the bus-driver, Moses, singing and murmuring amidst the rain, addressing the old doctor and his imaginary patients, declaring the futility of birth and renewal in a dead land. He embraces his identity as the bus-driver, calls old Doc “the bringer of pain,” and expresses a bitter, resigned acceptance of the endless, repetitive journey through a world without stars or hope.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • Moses says his hair “was his angel, his dead mother who lived in him, his dream of life.” Why does a man who has kept nothing else keep his hair?
  • Old Doc sees “the breath on the mirror where there was no breath, the feather move where there was no feather.” How does the chapter keep turning the signs of life into signs of death?
  • The road keeps “passing the same things again and again.” Why might the novel make Vera’s journey a circle?

Characters

  • Moses insists he is “my mother’s son and not my mother’s daughter,” yet says he is “Penelope weaving this web, not Ulysses.” What is he afraid of becoming?
  • Moses and old Doc are mirror images, two bearded men who never married. Why does Moses need an enemy so like himself?
  • Madge accuses “every man but her husband who slept at her side.” What does this reveal about her before her story is told?

Connections

  • In Chapter 4 the fat lady warned of “the jumping-off place,” and here Moses uses the same words. How does the Middle West begin to take shape out of other people’s warnings?
  • Moses calls the passengers “four angels at four bed-posts, Matthew, Mark, Luke, John.” What changes when a bus ride becomes a bedtime prayer?
  • The chapter returns to Moses’s politics again and again. How is his hatred of the Democrats really about fear of losing himself? See The New Deal (Ch 5).

Reflection

  • “There may be only one way to get into this world . . . but there is more than one way to get out of this world.” How does this chapter blur the line between birth and death?
  • Moses swears never to cut his hair “as long as the Democrats were running us.” What vows do people keep long after the reason is gone?
  • Moses says, “I never saw life come, but I have seen life go.” What does it mean to know death better than birth?
  • Is Moses driving the bus or dreaming that he drives it? Does it matter to the passengers?

Research: Characters

Vera Cartwheel

  • Vera watches the bus-driver and the other passengers, including Madge, capturing their physical and emotional states with a keen, almost clinical eye. She notes Madge’s moaning and accusing, Moses’s erratic behavior and long hair, and the desolate landscape outside. Her observations are detailed, conveying a sense of decay, confusion, and suspended time.
  • Vera’s narration is imbued with philosophical undertones. She reflects on themes of life and death, creation and destruction, reality and illusion.
  • She engages with the bus-driver’s thoughts and conversations, especially his long monologue about his hair, politics, and existential musings.
  • Vera seems to understand the symbolic weight of the bus-driver’s hair as a metaphor for identity, resistance to corruption, and his connection to life amid death.
  • Vera’s narration conveys a sense of uncertainty about the reality of their situation. The bus-driver himself seems caught between dreaming and waking, life and death, and Vera shares this ambiguity.
  • She hints that the bus-driver may be dead or dreaming, trapped in a liminal space where time loops and places vanish. This uncertainty colors her perspective, making her more contemplative and less definitive in her judgments.
  • Vera is empathetic to the people around her. She also maintains a certain detachment. She does not intervene or change the course of events but rather records them with a mixture of sorrow, irony, and resignation. Her tone suggests an understanding of the tragic absurdity of their journey and the existential plight of the characters.
  • Vera functions as a bridge between the reader and the symbolic, almost hallucinatory world of the chapter and for the rest of the novel. Through her narration, the reader gains access to the bus-driver’s inner turmoil, the desolate landscape, and the metaphysical questions raised.
  • Vera’s bus trip exists in a liminal space between reality and dream, serving as a profound metaphor for her search for life and truth amid the illusions and uncertainties that surround her.

Moses Hunnecker (The Bus-Driver)

  • Central figure, described as wild-eyed, drunken, and somewhat erratic.
  • His long hair and beard symbolize his identity, freedom, and resistance to external control.
  • He is a life-long Republican, openly hostile to Democrats, whom he blames for the country’s decay.
  • He is a caretaker figure for his mother and is caught between delusion and reality, possibly dead or dreaming while driving an old bus.
  • His internal monologue reveals a deep existential crisis and philosophical reflection on life, death, and meaning.

Justice O’Leary (Old Doc)

  • Represents a figure of lost hope and failed medical practice.
  • He is senile, long-bearded, and imagines he is still delivering babies and practicing medicine in a dead world.
  • Symbolizes a dying or decayed authority, clinging to illusions and the past.
  • Moses criticizes him for bringing misery and being out of touch with reality.

Miss Pebbles

  • She is a figure embodying deafness and detachment from reality, both literally and metaphorically.
  • Her physical deafness also isolates and makes her unable to engage fully with the world around her.
  • This deafness symbolizes a disconnection from the living world and the ongoing turmoil and a futile or incomplete attempt to connect or listen.
  • Her perceptions and experiences are filtered through dreams or illusions rather than reality which further emphasizes her detachment and perhaps a coping mechanism against the bleakness surrounding her.
  • She complains to the desk clerk about being kept awake by noises that are in fact spectral or imaginary. These hauntings reflect a world where the boundaries between life and death, reality and illusion, have blurred, and Miss Pebbles exists trapped in this liminal space.
  • Her character serves as a poignant contrast to the bus-driver’s chaotic, vivid awareness of the world’s decay and the surreal events transpiring around them. While the bus-driver wrestles with his drunken consciousness and the metaphysical implications of his journey, Miss Pebbles is insulated, unable or unwilling to fully confront these realities. Her state can be seen as symbolic of denial or the human tendency to retreat into subjective realities when faced with overwhelming despair and madness.

Madge Capehorn and Homer Capehorn

  • Madge is a figure of tragic endurance and profound emotional pain, trapped in a loveless and alienating marriage with Homer, who is depicted as passive, innocent, and disconnected. Their relationship embodies the novel’s exploration of despair, loss, and the elusive nature of human connection, with Madge’s pregnancy serving as a potent symbol of both life and death, hope and despair.

The Pregnant Women (Imaginary Patients)

  • Appear as spectral or hallucinated figures in the old doctor’s dreams.
  • Symbolize the barren or dead future, as none are truly alive or giving birth.
  • Their cries and suffering are echoes of lost hope and the cyclical nature of life and death.

Research: Themes

Death and Resurrection

See: Death and Mortality

  • The bus-driver’s journey is metaphorical, possibly representing a passage between life and death.
  • References to being buried alive, resurrection, and dying twice highlight the blurred line between existence and nonexistence.
  • The cyclic nature of life and death is emphasized by imagery of birth cries, death moans, and eternal return.

Identity and Resistance

See: Identity and the Self · Fear and Courage

  • The bus-driver’s refusal to cut his hair symbolizes holding onto personal identity and autonomy despite external pressures.
  • Hair represents a personal dream, a physical manifestation of selfhood that remains even when everything else decays.

Political and Social Decay

See: Decay and Ruin · Society, Class, and Race

  • The bus-driver’s tirades against Democrats reflect frustration with political corruption and societal decline.
  • The country is depicted as a “dead country,” with broken roads, empty towns, and lost hopes.

Illusion vs Reality

See: Illusion and Reality

  • The narrative questions what is real and what is imagined: the bus-driver might be dead, dreaming, or trapped in a cycle of madness.
  • The old doctor’s practice is a hallucination; the pregnant women are ghosts of a lost future.

Isolation and Loneliness

See: Isolation and Connection

  • The bus-driver is isolated, both physically on his journey and mentally in his state of drunken delirium and existential despair.
  • No one else truly communicates or understands him; the old doctor and the passengers are figments or distant presences.

Research: Resources

  • “Oh, the old grey mare, she ain’t what she used to was”: The “Old Gray Mare” song is believed to have originated as a campaign ditty in the 19th century, specifically as a political jab at seven-term Baltimore mayor Ferdinand Latrobe by Democratic political operative Thomas Francis McNulty. The tune has been adapted for various purposes, including Civil Rights songs and as a way to underscore the decline of aging. This song reflects the bus-driver’s weary, somewhat erratic state as he navigates the flooded roads, facing obstacles and detours, mirroring the theme of decline and weariness pervasive in the narrative. The old gray mare can be seen as a metaphor for the bus-driver himself, his vehicle, or even the broader journey of life marked by fatigue, struggle, and a sense of things not being what they once were. The tune itself comes from J. Warner’s minstrel song “Down in Alabam’” (1858).
  • “We were stopped by the shunting cars of the Pennsylvania Railroad”: The Pennsylvania Railroad was once the largest railroad in America by traffic and revenue, and it called itself “the Standard Railroad of the World.”
  • “Three shakes of a dead dog’s tail”: The bus-driver gives a grim twist to the old phrase “two shakes of a lamb’s tail,” meaning in no time at all.
  • “It’s the jumping-off place. It’s where creation stops”: The fat lady on the train used the same words in Chapter 4. See the Chapter 4 resources.
  • “seeing the breath on the mirror where there was no breath, the feather move where there was no feather”: Holding a mirror or a feather to the lips was the old test for death. King Lear, holding the dead Cordelia, calls, “Lend me a looking-glass; / If that her breath will mist or stain the stone, / Why, then she lives,” and then, “This feather stirs; she lives!” See Shakespeare.
  • “the elegiac music of the spheres or with those heavenly harmonies no mortal ears could hear”: Pythagoras taught that the planets make music as they move, a harmony too constant for human ears to notice. See Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres.
  • “the great dolphins uplifting the stone boats of the dead upon the turbulent waves”: In Greek myth and funeral art dolphins carry souls across the sea and rescue the drowning, as they saved the poet Arion when sailors threw him overboard.
  • “the Burma-Shave signs, the broken rail fences”: The Burma-Shave sign series first appeared on US Highways in 1926 and remained a major advertising component until 1963. It was usually a series of six consecutive signs along the edge of highways. See Burma-Shave signs (Ch 5).
  • “holy Lucifer and Lucifer matches”: Friction matches were sold as “Lucifers” from 1829, and the name stuck to matches for a century.
  • “so that he might hide himself away from the face of God”: Adam and Eve hid “from the presence of the Lord God amongst the trees of the garden” (Genesis 3:8), and the murderer Cain says, “from thy face shall I be hid” (Genesis 4:14).
  • “His hair had grown three inches since he left Persia at sunset”: Persia is a real small town in western Iowa. Midwestern towns often borrowed faraway names, such as Peru, Lebanon, Cairo, Moscow, and Athens. See Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest.
  • “the sky had been filled with red-winged blackbirds”: The red-winged blackbird is one of the most common birds in North America, and in winter it gathers with grackles and cowbirds in flocks of millions. See Birds.
  • “turning her broken earphones off and on”: Early electric hearing aids, with a microphone, a battery, and an earphone, came into use in the early twentieth century and replaced the ear trumpets of Chapter 4. See the Chapter 4 resources.
  • “full of burning eyes like the eyes of Argus if he had lived, full of eyes like the peacock’s burning tail”: Argus Panoptes is a many-eyed giant in Greek mythology. This is known to have generated sayings like the eyes of Argus, which means to be subject to scrutiny of one’s actions to an invasive degree. The “eyes of Argus” metaphorically convey the idea of a penetrating, multifaceted vision or insight or seeing many facets or truths simultaneously, reflecting the novel’s themes of layered realities and the complex perceptions of its characters. Hera set Argus to guard Io, and after Hermes lulled him to sleep and killed him, Hera set his hundred eyes in the tail of her peacock. See Argus Panoptes (Ch 5).
  • “he could always, if things got too much for him, hang himself by the hair of his head”: King David’s son Absalom was caught by the head in an oak, by tradition by his long hair, and was left hanging until he was killed (2 Samuel 18:9). Moses’s vow never to cut his hair is also the Nazirite vow, “there shall no razor come upon his head” (Numbers 6:5), and Samson lost his strength when his hair was cut (Judges 16).
  • “the way the Democrats were running this old country straight into its grave”: The New Deal faced opposition due to concerns that it strayed too far towards socialism, expanded presidential power unconstitutionally, and potentially redistributed wealth. Some critics believed the government was not doing enough to combat the Great Depression, while others argued the New Deal’s policies hindered private enterprise and individual liberty. Many critics, including the American Liberty League, saw the New Deal as a step towards socialism, arguing it involved excessive government intervention in the economy. Opponents, like Robert A. Taft argued that Roosevelt was exceeding his constitutional authority and expanding the powers of the presidency. Some feared that the New Deal policies would lead to a massive and potentially detrimental redistribution of wealth. Critics, like Herbert Hoover, argued the New Deal would create a bloated bureaucracy, intrude on business, and limit personal liberty. Some believed the New Deal’s policies, such as increased business taxes and Social Security excise taxes, harmed businesses and discouraged hiring. Critics also questioned the New Deal’s effectiveness in addressing the Great Depression, arguing it didn’t create enough jobs or revive the economy. The reaction of Moses to the New Deal policies symbolizes the broader social and economic upheavals of the era, seen through the eyes of a common man who feels alienated and distrustful of political leaders and their solutions. The chapter uses his perspective to illustrate the anxieties and conflicts within American society at that historical moment. See The New Deal (Ch 5).
  • “What with all these free-lunch counters”: Saloons once set out a “free lunch” of ham, pickles, and bread for anyone who bought a nickel glass of beer, the salt in the food keeping customers drinking.
  • “to sleep in the country churchyard”: Thomas Gray’s “Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard” (1751) mourns the humble dead of a village, where “The rude forefathers of the hamlet sleep.”
  • “will cover me with a coat of hair like the wild asses that live in the desert places”: In Job 39:5-8 God asks, “Who hath sent out the wild ass free?” and says that he scorns the city. Jacob says of his brother, “Esau my brother is a hairy man” (Genesis 27:11).
  • “all those Brain Trusters were busted with their W.P.A. projects”: Brain Trusters may refer to a term that originally described a group of close advisers and is most associated with advisers to Franklin Roosevelt. A newspaper gave the name in 1932 to Roosevelt’s circle of professors, chiefly Raymond Moley, Rexford Tugwell, and Adolf Berle.
  • “their W.P.A. projects and their public parks and their marble bird baths and their rotten roads and their free skating rinks”: WPA (Work Projects Administration) was an American New Deal agency which employed millions of mostly unskilled men to carry out public works projects to provide employment during the Great Depression. It began in 1935 as the Works Progress Administration and was renamed in 1939. Its workers, about 8.5 million in all, built parks, roads, and skating rinks and ran nursery schools, which is why Moses complains of “free kindergartens.”
  • “why don’t you just close their wombs, tie the knots?”: Indiana passed the first eugenic sterilization law in 1907, and in Buck v. Bell (1927) the Supreme Court upheld forced sterilization. Tens of thousands of Americans were sterilized under such laws.
  • “the barber who was once a blood-letter, too”: Bloodletting gained popularity in the 18th and 19th centuries, with various instruments and techniques developed to facilitate the process. Leeches were also used to draw blood, and lancets and scarificators were employed to create incisions for blood removal. Prominent physicians and surgeons, including John Hunter, endorsed bloodletting for various conditions. In 18th and 19th century America, physicians, barber-surgeons, and even barbers could practice bloodletting. While physicians often recommended bloodletting, barbers and barber-surgeons, who also performed minor surgeries and grooming services, frequently carried out the procedure. The “bloodletting” in the chapter symbolizes obsolete medical practices and, by extension, the ineffectiveness of attempts to control or cure the pervasive decay and suffering in the world depicted in the novel. In America Benjamin Rush bled patients heavily during the Philadelphia yellow fever epidemic of 1793. See the Chapter 3 resources on the barber pole.
  • “is the blood of Abel crying from the ground that I should live to see these bloody flowers?”: This may refer to the story of Cain and Abel in Genesis 4:10, where God says, “the voice of thy brother’s blood crieth unto me from the ground,” and where Abel’s blood carries a power that doesn’t let Cain settle again on the earth. The first human death could symbolize the future violence which pollutes the earth. God uses a flood later on in Genesis to purify the earth. This phrase encapsulates Vera’s anguished contemplation of human suffering’s enduring cry through history and its impact on her perception of life and the world around her.
  • “have we elected a king or a congress? What’s the senate doing?”: This could be referring to the presidency of Franklin D Roosevelt (FDR) who served a record four terms as president before the Twenty-second Amendment, ratified in 1951, limited a president to two terms.
  • “remember that you are a good Republican and that we shall never have free medicine”: The US government’s opposition to universal healthcare stems from a complex mix of political, economic, and ideological factors. These include concerns about government control, potential for inefficiency and long wait times, the high cost of universal healthcare, and opposition from various stakeholders, including insurance companies and the American Medical Association (AMA). Some argue that universal healthcare would lead to excessive government control over the healthcare system and individual choice. The narrative that government involvement in healthcare equates to socialism is still prevalent in US political debates. A strong belief in individualism and limited government is deeply ingrained in American political culture, which can clash with the idea of a universal healthcare system. Universal healthcare would require substantial government funding, potentially leading to increased taxes or cuts in other areas of government spending. Some argue that a government-controlled system could stifle innovation and entrepreneurship in the healthcare industry. The US has a complex mix of public and private health insurance, and a universal system could disrupt this existing structure. The AMA has historically opposed universal healthcare, fearing that it could lead to lower physician fees, government regulation, and a reduced role for private practice. Private insurance companies have opposed universal healthcare as it would likely reduce their profits and could lead to a decline in their business. Pharmaceutical companies, hospitals, and other for-profit entities within the healthcare sector may also oppose universal healthcare as it could disrupt the current market structure and potentially reduce their profits. Some argue that universal healthcare could lead to longer wait times for appointments and procedures due to increased demand. Some critics suggest that a government-controlled system could result in lower quality of care or reduced access to specialized treatments. Universal healthcare in the US could lead to a healthier population with better health equity, improved quality of life, and reduced financial hardship from medical costs. Other potential benefits include lower overall healthcare costs through administrative savings and cost controls, better public health outcomes by focusing on preventive care, and improved economic security for individuals. The universal healthcare discussion in this chapter serves to underscore one of the novel’s key themes: the tension between the ideal and the practical, between dreams and reality, and the social ethics of care and responsibility. It represents a call for tangible, practical action in a world marked by suffering and deprivation.
    • Despite the Affordable Care Act passed in 2010 and Medicare and Medicaid (both created in 1965 by amendments to the Social Security Act; Medicare coverage began in 1966) free or reasonable health care has never been universally available in the United States.
  • “his White Horse, his Long John, his Johnnie Walker, his fire water”: The different whiskeys represent both Moses’s personal struggles and the broader themes of despair, escape, and the search for meaning in the novel’s interior American setting.
    • White Horse Scotch Whisky is a blended Scotch whisky from Edinburgh, first produced by James Logan Mackie in about 1890.
    • Long John Whisky may refer to Long John Macdonald, a bootlegger who took out a license and founded the Ben Nevis distillery at Fort William in 1825.
    • Johnnie Walker is a brand of Scotch whisky now owned by Diageo that originated in the Scottish burgh of Kilmarnock in East Ayrshire. The brand began with the grocer John Walker, who opened his Kilmarnock shop in 1820 and was selling whisky by 1825. The Johnnie Walker name and the Striding Man logo came from his grandsons’ rebranding in 1908 and 1909.
    • Firewater is a strong alcohol and may also be called moonshine. Some sources suggest that “firewater” may have originated as a term used by Native Americans to describe alcohol. The term “firewater” is thought to be a translation of the Algonquian Indian word “scoutiouabou,” which means the same thing.
  • “old fool gives up his sceptre and his crown of stars and his golden throne”: Scepter is a staff or wand held in the hand by a ruling monarch as an item of royal insignia. A crown of stars is a circle of stars often representing unity, solidarity and harmony in flags, seals, and signs and is also in motifs related to the Woman of the Apocalypse and Baroque allegorical art depicting the Crown of Immortality. The Crown of Immortality is a metaphorical symbolic representation of eternal life, often depicted in art and literature. It’s a traditional symbol of honor, achievement, and immortality, often associated with religious or spiritual concepts. It can be visualized as a laurel wreath or a circle of stars. A golden throne symbolizes divine authority, high status, and opulence. In various cultures, it represents the power and majesty of rulers, deities, or figures of significance. The use of gold, a precious and revered metal, further enhances this symbolism, connecting it to concepts of wealth, divinity, and triumph. The phrase captures a theme of resistance to inevitable change, the stubbornness of old power figures, and the futility of clinging to decaying authority in a world that moves on. It underscores the chapter’s atmosphere of decay, entrapment, and social criticism expressed through the bus-driver’s bitter, drunken monologue.
  • “in a dark night of the human soul”: The Dark Night of the Soul has been described as a type of evolution where it has been recognized that those things that are limited need to be set aside and to press forward with patience and perseverance. In this chapter, the “Dark Night of the Soul” signifies the profound inner crisis of confronting the shadow side of existence (birth and death, loss and love, illusion and reality) captured in Madge’s tragic situation and the surrounding desolate imagery of the journey. See the Chapter 4 resources and The Dark Night (Ch 5).
  • “stormy petrels in the way of the old bus-driver”: The petrel may be named for Saint Peter, since it seems to walk on the water. Sailors took the storm petrel as an omen of bad weather, and “stormy petrel” came to mean a troublemaker. See Birds.
  • “Jezebel!”: Jezebel is in the Book of Kings in the Hebrew Bible. She is known for violently purging the prophets of God from Israel and instituting the worship of Baal and Asherah. She was later put to death by defenestration or being thrown from a high place or window. In the Book of Revelation (2:20) she is symbolically associated with false prophets. “Jezebel” in this context represents a symbol of moral judgment and inner conflict within the bus-driver’s troubled psyche during a moment of dark reflection and suffering.
  • “the woodticks ticking like old town clocks”: The deathwatch beetle bores into old timbers and taps its head against the wood to call a mate. The ticking, heard in quiet rooms at night, was long taken as an omen of death.
  • “he was Penelope weaving this web, not Ulysses who wandered over the wide seas”: Penelope is a character in Homer’s Odyssey. The reference to Penelope highlights themes of waiting, fidelity, and steadfastness amid uncertainty and absence. Penelope, the faithful wife of Odysseus in Greek mythology, is renowned for her patience and loyalty, waiting many years for her husband’s return from the Trojan War while warding off numerous suitors. She put off her suitors by weaving a shroud for her father-in-law Laertes by day and unraveling it each night.
  • “his immortal hair was the part of him that was immortal, that would never die, that would keep on growing”: Hair and nails do not continue to grow after death. While it may appear that way due to dehydration and skin shrinking, it’s an optical illusion. The actual growth of hair and nails requires complex hormonal regulation and the continued production of new cells, which ceases upon death. Hair and nails symbolize the fragile boundary between appearance and reality, life and death, strength and vulnerability, serving as potent metaphors for the characters’ inner lives and existential conditions.
  • “He had been told of people buried alive and crying in their graves”: While there appears to be an overestimation of the number of people buried alive accidentally in this chapter, in 1885 a victim was found inside his coffin with much of his hair pulled out. The fear was widespread in the nineteenth century. Poe’s story “The Premature Burial” (1844) turns on it, and inventors patented “safety coffins” with bells, air tubes, and flags so that anyone who woke underground could signal. See Edgar Allan Poe.
  • “not even if Gabriel should blow his horn to resurrect us all”: Gabriel is an archangel first described in the Hebrew bible. The reference to Gabriel serves to mark the bus journey as not merely physical but spiritual, underscoring the novel’s exploration of mortality, transcendence, and the search for meaning beyond the material world. The Bible does not name the angel of the last trumpet, only “the trump of God” (1 Thessalonians 4:16); tradition gave the horn to Gabriel.
  • “There would still be those rusted forceps of his”: Obstetric forceps for delivering babies were invented by the Chamberlen family in England, who kept them a family secret for more than a century.
  • “the apothecaries’ weights that he used for paper weights”: Druggists weighed medicines in grains, scruples, drams, and ounces, the apothecaries’ system, until the metric system replaced it.
  • “bare footprints in the dust, long and light, some with webbed toes”: Syndactyly affects 1 in 2000/3000 births and most frequently in white males where there is webbing (extra skin between the toes and/or hands). Throughout history, people with birth defects, including those with syndactyly, faced stigma and marginalization. In some cultures, birth defects were seen as a sign of divine displeasure or as having supernatural origins, leading to fear or even infanticide. Misinterpretations of Darwin’s work contributed to the eugenics movement, leading to harmful practices like forced sterilization in some countries. In this chapter the webbed toes belong to old Doc, who goes about in “his long white nightgown with his webbed toes,” and to the bare footprints in his office, “some with webbed toes, footprints of things unhuman.”
  • “you died every minute that you lived, got a new skin every seven years”: The popular belief that the body renews itself every seven years is a myth. Skin in fact replaces itself in about a month, though some cells last a lifetime.
  • “as if he had been old Father Time himself”: Father Time is the old man with a long beard, a scythe, and an hourglass, the figure of passing time and death.
  • “the undertaker . . . had done an excellent sidetrade in the furniture business”: In nineteenth-century America the town cabinetmaker often made coffins as well as tables and beds, and many furniture dealers became undertakers.
  • “no credit in the book of human life, none by the recording angel”: In Jewish and Christian tradition an angel records every deed of a person’s life. In Laurence Sterne’s Tristram Shandy the recording angel drops a tear on a sinful oath and blots it out forever.
  • “old Doc with his broken stethoscope”: The French doctor René Laennec invented the stethoscope in 1816, at first a simple wooden tube, so that he would not have to put his ear to a patient’s chest.
  • “these shotgun weddings”: A shotgun wedding was a marriage hurried along because the bride was pregnant, as if her father stood behind the groom with a gun.
  • “imagining he was drunk on ether and starlight”: In the nineteenth century people breathed ether for fun at “ether frolics” before doctors used it to put patients to sleep. See the Chapter 1 resources on ether.
  • “He should have been a horse-and-buggy doctor”: The country doctor who drove his buggy to farmhouses at all hours was celebrated in Arthur Hertzler’s bestselling memoir of practice in Kansas, The Horse and Buggy Doctor (1938).
  • “not a cent with which to pay old Charon when they crossed the river in a leaking boat”: Charon in Greek and Roman mythology is the ferryman to Hades or the underworld. Charon symbolizes the passage through death or the threshold between life and death, reinforcing the novel’s exploration of mortality, memory, and the liminal spaces of existence. See the Chapter 3 resources.
  • “It was a dead country”: Moses’s dead country of the empty box factory, “the foundry that had no bricks and no fires and the bell-tower that had no bells,” and “the filling station that was always closed” is the small-town Middle West emptied by the Depression. The decline of rural America is a multifaceted issue with roots in the 19th century and continuing into the present day. It’s driven by factors like industrialization, urbanization, and economic shifts, leading to population loss, declining infrastructure, and increased poverty. Rural communities have also been impacted by government policies, financialization, and the changing nature of work. As the US industrial sector grew, urban areas became magnets for jobs and economic opportunities, drawing people from rural areas. This shift began in the late 18th and early 19th centuries, and continued into the 20th century. Technological advancements in agriculture led to increased efficiency and the displacement of farm workers, further contributing to out-migration from rural areas. Rural areas struggled to keep pace with industrialization and urban growth, often experiencing higher poverty rates and fewer opportunities compared to cities. From the mid-20th century on, rural areas lost manufacturing jobs as factories closed and industries moved to urban centers. Some argue that government policies focused on deregulation and free trade also contributed to the decline of rural areas by favoring corporate interests over local communities. The financialization of the economy, where financial institutions prioritize profits over long-term investment in local communities, has further exacerbated the challenges faced by rural areas. Automation and technology have also played a role in displacing rural workers, particularly in industries like agriculture and manufacturing. Rural areas often have older populations, with fewer young people choosing to stay and contribute to the workforce. Between 2010 and 2020, rural America experienced its first population decline in history. The reclassification of counties from rural to metropolitan has also contributed to the perception of rural decline, as some areas previously defined as rural have transitioned into urban or metropolitan areas. Many rural areas struggle with limited access to broadband internet, transportation, and other essential services. Rural communities often face challenges related to poverty, unemployment, and limited access to education and healthcare. The decline of manufacturing jobs in rural areas has led to economic hardship and out-migration. Technological advancements in agriculture have displaced farm workers and contributed to a decline in the agricultural sector. Many rural areas have limited access to healthcare services, which can impact the health and well-being of residents. Some rural areas struggle with strained educational systems and limited access to higher education. Many experts predict that rural areas will continue to experience population loss in the coming years. Rural communities face ongoing challenges related to poverty, unemployment, and limited access to resources. Addressing the challenges of rural decline requires a multi-pronged approach, including investments in infrastructure, education, and economic development.
  • “only dragonflies as big as stars”: Giant dragonflies once existed. Meganeura, which lived about 300 million years ago, had a wingspan of about 70 centimeters, more than two feet.
  • “four angels at four bed-posts, Matthew, Mark, Luke, John”: Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John are the authors of the first four books of the New Testament, collectively known as the Gospels. These books provide accounts of the life, teachings, death, and resurrection of Jesus Christ. They are not simply historical accounts but are also theological interpretations of Jesus’ life. Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John in the chapter symbolize the four passengers as spiritual or angelic figures, evoking the Four Evangelists and thus linking the narrative to themes of spiritual journey, life, death, and cosmic judgment. Moses is reciting the old bedtime prayer known as the White Paternoster, recorded in the 1600s: “Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John, / Bless the bed that I lie on. / Four corners to my bed, / Four angels round my head.”
  • “Conceived on boxwood, born on cedarwood, died on wormwood”: These three woods form a thematic triad reflecting the novel’s exploration of reality versus illusion, preservation versus decay, and comfort versus bitterness. Boxwood and cedarwood evoke the desire for stability and lasting truth, as embodied by Miss MacIntosh and the grounded aspects of Vera’s search for reality, while wormwood represents the bitterness, loss, and spiritual malaise that haunt Vera’s life and her mother’s opium-induced coma.
    • Boxwood has several meanings in the bible: 1) In Isaiah 41:19, boxwood is listed among trees God will plant in the desert, symbolizing divine provision and the restoration of Israel. 2) Isaiah 60:13 mentions boxwood as part of the adornment of God’s sanctuary, representing beauty and God’s presence. 3) The use of boxwood in prophetic visions symbolizes resilience and God’s ability to shape creation with purpose and care.
    • Cedarwood has several meanings in the bible: 1) Cedar wood, known for its strength and resistance to decay, is associated with the construction of sacred spaces like the Temple of Solomon, symbolizing endurance, holiness, and divine presence. 2) The cedars of Lebanon symbolize the flourishing and prosperous life of the righteous, often associated with grandeur and splendor. 3) The use of cedar in palaces and homes represents security and shelter. 4) Cedar can also symbolize human pride and the subsequent downfall, as seen in Ezekiel 31:3.
    • Wormwood has several meanings in the bible: 1) Wormwood, known for its bitter taste, is used metaphorically to represent bitterness, affliction, remorse, and punitive suffering. 2) Wormwood symbolizes the bitter consequences of turning away from God, like idolatry, disobedience, and injustice. 3) In Revelation 8:10-11, the falling star called “Wormwood” symbolizes a catastrophic event, divine judgment, and the consequences of spiritual infidelity. 4) In Lamentations, wormwood is used to describe the profound grief and suffering caused by God’s judgment.
  • “don’t come after me with those old pruning hooks”: Isaiah 2:4 foresees a time when nations “shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruninghooks.”
  • “Take away your bellows. There’s no living spark”: God breathed into Adam “the breath of life” (Genesis 2:7). In the eighteenth century the humane societies founded to rescue the drowned tried to revive them by blowing air into their lungs with bellows.
  • “Go play your double flutes somewhere else”: The aulos, the double pipe of ancient Greece, was the instrument of satyrs, of Dionysian revels, and of funerals.
  • “as long as I’m my mother’s son and not my mother’s daughter”: In this time period writers like Ernest Hemingway and others were concerned about the masculine point of view in their writing. Through recorded time there has been a fear of the feminization of men and boys that will bring about the downfall of society. For all the hysteria that successive waves of feminism have wrought, the patriarchy continues to fight back with the rise of authoritarianism and fascism. Both fully constrict women and children to their assigned “natural roles.” Choice for women and children is restricted or completely eliminated. Firestone writes in the Dialectic of Sex (1970), to make both women and children totally independent would be to eliminate not just the patriarchal nuclear family, but the biological family itself.
  • “the angels crying, crying like loons”: The common loon’s wailing call carries for miles across northern lakes at night, and its wild sound gave rise to the phrase “crazy as a loon.” See Birds.
  • “you black Republican”: Historically, the term “Black Republican” has had different meanings: 1) Before the Civil War, it was used as a derogatory term for abolitionist members of the Republican Party. 2) During Reconstruction the term was also used for Republicans who supported legislation that favored African Americans. 3) During and after the Civil War the term was used to refer to members of the Negro Republican Party, a faction of the Republican Party in the South that was predominantly made up of Black Americans. 4) From the 1870s to the 1960s: It was associated with the “black-and-tan faction” of the Republican Party in the South, which opposed the segregationist “lily-white movement”.
  • “It ain’t going to rain no more, no more”: “It Ain’t Gonna Rain No Mo’,” Wendell Hall’s comic song of 1923, was one of the great hits of the decade.
  • “The old grey goose is dead. Died in the morning”: The folk song “Go Tell Aunt Rhody” (also known as “The Old Gray Goose is Dead”) has a rich history rooted in both European and American traditions. The melody originated as a gavotte in Jean-Jacques Rousseau’s 1752 opera Le Devin du Village. An English version of the opera was produced in London in 1766, and the tune subsequently inspired various English lyrics, including “Sweet Melissa”. Around 1825, the tune began appearing in U.S. hymnals, and the Aunt Rhody version, a common American folk song, has appeared in numerous song anthologies. The lyrics tell the story of a gray goose that is being saved to make a feather bed, but dies. The song’s lyrics are often interpreted as expressing grief or loss, specifically the death of a loved one, but it’s also been suggested that the goose’s death is a matter of gain for those who need feathers for their beds. In prison slang, “grey goose” refers to the prison bus that transports inmates. “The old gray goose is dead” symbolizes the pervasive presence of death and the fragility of life in the narrative. It underscores the chapter’s mood of desolation, the cyclical nature of existence, and perhaps the inevitability of loss that haunts the characters on their journey. See Go Tell Aunt Rhody (Ch 5).
  • “the cigar store Indian puffing his pipe”: Carved wooden figures of Native Americans stood outside tobacco shops from the nineteenth century, because Europeans had learned tobacco from Native peoples. The figures were a stereotype and have mostly disappeared.
  • “You know me, Moses Hunnecker, the bus-driver”: Like the Moses of Exodus, the bus-driver leads his people through a wilderness toward a promised land, and the biblical Moses saw the Promised Land from Mount Nebo but never entered it (Deuteronomy 34). See Moses Hunnecker’s name carries Biblical freight (Ch 5).
  • “maybe he should have let that excellent old doctor look at him”: Moses refuses old Doc again and again and only at the end wonders whether he should have let him look. This trend of men avoiding doctors and medical help because of fear and feeling vulnerable has only now started to change with the millennial generation.
