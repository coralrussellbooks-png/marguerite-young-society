Chapter 54

On Chapter 54 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 53 · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 55 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: Not One Self · Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The Questions

Mr. Spitzer understands the sunken, unconscious world because it demands only “a passive role.” What kind of knowing asks for no participation?

Since his brother’s death he knows “many heterogeneous acquaintances he had not previously known.” Whose world has moved into his?

The musician now pores over “old horse scores, track figures, unintelligible turf accounts.” What has the gambler’s life to do with the composer’s?

If the dead brother’s world furnishes the living brother’s mind, where does one self end and the other begin?

The chapter opens on the Witness’s particular way of knowing. Mr. Spitzer understands the sunken, dreamed, subterranean world, the drowned kings and underwater fountains, “perhaps because they did not demand his understanding or even his participation—his was but a passive role.” This is the attention the book most trusts: a knowing that does not seize or use its object, that stays with the sunken world without diving in to master it. He can hold the vast unconscious the way he holds Catherine’s dream and the butterfly he cannot classify, by attending without possessing, and the passivity is not weakness but the discipline of the Witness.

But something has crossed into him that he did not attend from a distance. Since his brother’s death, “he had come to know many heterogeneous acquaintances he had not previously known, those rattling in his conscience now,” and each morning he pores over “old horse scores, track figures, unintelligible turf accounts,” the gambler’s world that was his brother’s and never his. Not One Self deepens here into incorporation: the dead brother’s life has moved into the living brother’s mind, so that the composer now carries the racetrack, the turf accounts “as alien to him as his music would have been” to his brother. He is not merely doubled with the dead man. He is being furnished by him.

The two worlds are opposites, and that is the point. The musician and the gambler, the elegy and the racing form, the silent harmony and the whistled foolish tune, could not be further apart, and Mr. Spitzer now holds both, the one native and the one inherited from a corpse. The book has said a person is a crowd; here the crowd includes one’s own opposite, the alien life of the dead absorbed until the composer keeps the gambler’s ledgers. To be plural is not only to be many versions of oneself. It is to be invaded by the other, to carry the world of someone one was nothing like.

So the border between the brothers dissolves from the inside. It is not that strangers confuse them, though they do, but that the dead one’s concerns are now the living one’s waking business, rattling in his conscience, sorted through each morning. Where his brother ends and Mr. Spitzer begins can no longer be drawn, because the contents have mingled: the gambler’s alien acquaintances keep the musician company, and the musician tends the gambler’s accounts. The plural self, in this chapter, is a house two men live in, one of them dead, and the furniture has been rearranged so thoroughly that neither could now say which of it is his.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Mr. Spitzer’s passive knowing for mere detachment or incapacity, and the reading that takes his absorption of his brother’s world for a symptom of breakdown. Neither the detachment nor the pathology is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that the Witness knows by not demanding participation, holding the sunken world without seizing it, and that this passivity is a discipline rather than a weakness; and that Not One Self here becomes incorporation, the dead brother’s alien gambler-world moving into the living musician’s mind until neither can be sorted from the other. To be plural is to be invaded by one’s opposite, to carry the world of someone one was nothing like. Mr. Spitzer keeps the gambler’s ledgers and the composer’s silence at once, and the chapter draws no border between the brothers it could honestly draw.

For Reflection

What can be known by attending to it passively that could not be known by seizing it?

Whose world, alien to one’s own, has moved in and become one’s daily business?

If one carries the concerns of someone one was nothing like, where does one self end and the other begin?
