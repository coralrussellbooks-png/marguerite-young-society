Research notes: Chapter 80

Characters: Esther Longtree · The Chicago Detective · Walter

Themes: Grief, Loss, and Mourning · Guilt, Innocence, and Justice · Memory and the Past · Society, Class, and Race

Philosophy: Freud and Jung · Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Lynching and Racial Violence · The Civil War

We have been nine chapters with Esther Longtree. We know her stillborn children, her dying fathers, the mud baby in the river, the Chicago detective who is both the crime and its investigation, the twin she was accused of killing before she was born, the silver spoon, the go-between who was more wonderful than either parent. We have come to know her everlasting pregnancy, her luminous stubbornness, her knitting and unraveling in the dark. And now Young gives us the thing Esther has been holding back through all of it: the one child who actually lived. His name is Walter. He is alive somewhere in the world. And Esther pressed her thumb to still his birth cry.

This is the darkest confession in Esther’s sequence, and Young delivers it with the same unhurried obliqueness she has used for everything else. Walter was born. Esther was young and frightened and alone. She pressed her thumb to his mouth to silence his first cry. She believed she had killed him. She hid the body and fled. But Walter did not die. He lived. He is out there, somewhere, growing, and he comes back to her in visions asking: “Mother, why have you killed me? Where is my father?

What Actually Happens

Esther is still at the Greasy Spoon, still pregnant with the little shadow boxer, still performing the rituals of the woman who lives between the living and the dead: making small clothes for no one, setting out breadcrumbs for spirits, building a little house of straw in a “tree for the spirit to come home to,” answering every knocking at the door.

She tells the story of Walter’s father. He came to her in the darkness of a summer night, half sleeping and half waking. His face was white in the moonlight. At dawn she saw that he was Black. “A Negro, a Negro, a Negro”; she says it three times, the epizeuxis of shock and revelation. He was a stranger, a “poor fellow, a tramp, a fugitive,” a prince. The white people in that place “had tried to kill him because he was black and had wanted to sleep with a white woman.” They had “tried to hang him from a white-blossoming tree for revenge, but the rope had broken.” He “fled for fear the white people would see him and kill him.” He had been nearly lynched. He ran.

Esther was pregnant. She was young and frightened and had no one to tell. She delivered Walter alone, or nearly alone, in circumstances the chapter renders fragmented and terrible. She pressed her thumb to still his birth cry. She believed she had killed him. She hid what she believed was a body. She fled. But Walter lived. He has grown up somewhere outside her knowledge. He is “black as my true love, black as my heart”; she loves him and fears him simultaneously, in the particular way of someone who believes she has committed an irreversible act and then discovers the act was not irreversible, only terrible. He comes to her in visions. He asks her why she killed him. He asks where his father is. She has no answers. He haunts her with a cruel, hard heart, not the hovering innocent stillborn who applaud at the edges, but an accuser, a child who has lived long enough to be angry.

He is the “beloved obstacle”: the one who prevents her from moving fully forward, the one whose memory blocks the path between her past and any possible future. Every subsequent pregnancy has been, in some sense, an attempt to atone for or replace or redeem what happened to Walter. Every stillborn is haunted by his presence as the one who should have been the first stillborn and wasn’t.

Walter’s Father

He is described through Esther’s shock and retrospective love simultaneously: a Black man, a stranger, who came to her in the dark of a summer night when his face looked white in the moonlight, and whose race she only saw at dawn. He had been nearly lynched for being Black in proximity to a white woman. He ran because running was the only alternative to death.

Young is writing this in the period between 1945 and 1965, in a country where anti-miscegenation laws remained on the books in most Southern states until the Supreme Court’s 1967 decision in Loving v. Virginia declared them unconstitutional. Interracial marriage was not merely socially stigmatized; it was illegal in most of the South and several other states. A Black man perceived as having had a sexual relationship with a white woman faced not just legal penalty but lynching. This is not metaphor in Walter’s father’s case: the white people “tried to hang him from a white-blossoming tree.” The rope broke. He survived. He ran.

Esther’s description of him carries the ambivalence of someone who loved in fear and lost what she loved before she could fully acknowledge it: a stranger, a “poor fellow, a tramp, a fugitive,” a prince of Africa. Each descriptor carries a different weight. Together they form the portrait of a man who was real and present and then gone, whom Esther has spent years trying to hold in memory across the gap of everything that happened.

His face was white in the moonlight. Only at dawn did she see he was Black. This moment (the revelation of race at dawn, after intimacy) is one of the chapter’s most precisely situated details. It is not that Esther did not know. It is that the dark erased the visible category, and then the light restored it, and by then the category had already been irrelevant for a night.

The Thing Esther Did

Young has been careful, across nine chapters, to distinguish between what happened to Esther and what Esther did. The stillborn children happened to her. The detective’s assault happened to her. The mud baby in the river; she buried it because she loved it, not because she was guilty of anything. She has been accused of murder for the children who came and did not live. She has not murdered them. Walter is different. She pressed her thumb to still his birth cry. She believed she had killed him. This is what Esther actually did, or tried to do, and it is the thing that has organized her guilt ever since. Not the accusation her mother installed before her birth. Not the detective’s manufactured case. This.

She was young. She was alone. She had no one. The father had fled. She had just delivered a child in secret, in fear, in a country where the child’s race would have compounded every other stigma she already carried. The circumstances do not erase the act. They also do not erase the circumstances. Young holds both.

Walter lived. This is the twist that makes the guilt permanent: she tried to end his life and failed, and he has gone on living, somewhere, without her, growing into a child who asks why she killed him. She did not kill him. She tried to. He does not know the distinction. Neither, quite, does she.

The Haunting

Walter is qualitatively different from the other presences in Esther’s inner life. The stillborn hover and applaud and whisper. The mud baby returns swollen and beautiful and ugly in dreams. These are the presences of the lost, who have nowhere to be except in the consciousness of the person who lost them.

Walter accuses. He has a cruel, hard heart. He asks questions she cannot answer. He is not the presence of the lost; he is the presence of the living, of someone who is out there in the world, growing, carrying his own history, asking his own questions, and visiting Esther’s conscience with the specificity of someone who survived. The black hand laid across the womb, one of the chapter’s central images, is Walter’s hand, or its shadow: the presence of what she did blocking the path forward. Every subsequent pregnancy is gestated in the presence of that hand. Every stillborn carries his shadow. The little shadow boxer, the current pregnancy, the one Esther believes will finally live; it too must make it past Walter’s hand.

Race, the Lynch Rope, and the White-Blossoming Tree

The image Young chooses for the attempted lynching of Walter’s father is exact: a white-blossoming tree. Not a bare tree, not a dead tree: a flowering one, beautiful in the way that spring trees are beautiful, and from its branches a rope, and below the rope a Black man who survived because the rope broke. Young was writing this during the era of the Civil Rights Movement: the NAACP’s anti-lynching campaigns, the murder of Emmett Till in 1955 and the published photographs that catalyzed the movement, the ongoing documentation of racial terror. She does not name these events. She gives Esther a Black lover who fled for his life, and she gives him the white-blossoming tree and the broken rope, and she lets these stand in the landscape of Esther’s memory without commentary.

To All My Darlings notes the history of anti-miscegenation law in the United States: the first prohibition on interracial marriage was passed by Maryland in 1691, more than three hundred years before Loving v. Virginia finally ended them all. The prospect of Black men marrying white women was described by many Americans before the Civil War as the greatest threat to society. When William G. Allen, a Black professor, announced his plans to marry a white student in 1853, he narrowly escaped lynching and had to leave the country permanently. In 1958, Sammy Davis Jr. briefly married a Black actress to protect himself from mob violence after his relationship with a white actress became known. Walter’s father is not a named historical figure. He is a Black man in a summer night who survived a lynch rope and ran. He is every Black man who ran. His child (Walter, black as my true love) is the child of that history, born into Esther’s fear and her love simultaneously, born into a country where his existence was illegal in some states and dangerous in many more.

Breadcrumbs for Spirits

Alongside the revelation about Walter, the chapter also gives us Esther’s daily rituals of the liminal: making small clothes for no one, setting out breadcrumbs for spirits, building a little house of straw in a “tree for the spirit to come home to.” These are the practices of someone who lives at the border between the living and the dead, who sets a place at the table for what cannot come to dinner, who leaves the door open for what has no body to walk through it.

They are also, in context, the practices of someone who has had a child she does not know the location of. Walter is out there, alive, growing. Esther does not know where. She cannot reach him through the ordinary channels because she does not know where to look and because the circumstances of his birth mean that looking would be dangerous. So she leaves breadcrumbs for spirits, and perhaps one of those spirits is Walter’s: the child who is alive but lost to her, who is both in the world and in the category of what she cannot touch.

What to Hold Onto

Walter: the first child who actually lived. Born in a summer night, his father Black and nearly lynched, Esther young and alone and frightened. She pressed her thumb to still his birth cry. He survived. He is somewhere in the world, asking why she killed him.

Walter’s father: a stranger, a “poor fellow, a tramp, a fugitive,” a prince. His face white in the moonlight, Black at dawn. He ran because the rope had broken and the white people would finish what they started. He had no other choice.

The white-blossoming tree and the broken rope. The particular beauty Young gives to the instrument of racial terror.

The black hand laid across the womb. Walter’s shadow blocking the path to every subsequent child.

The breadcrumbs set out for spirits. The little house of straw in the tree. The small clothes made for no one. Esther keeping the table set for what cannot come. And the little shadow boxer, still fighting. Still two hearts. Still the one she believes will finally make it through.
