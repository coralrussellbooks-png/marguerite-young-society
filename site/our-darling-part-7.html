Marguerite Young, Our Darling, Part 7: What It Reveals About Young and Her Work

Part of a reading of Marguerite Young, Our Darling (1994), a book of tributes and essays about her.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Part 6 · Marguerite Young, Our Darling: What It Reveals, Part by Part

Source: Marguerite Young, Our Darling: Tributes and Essays, edited by Miriam Fuchs (Dalkey Archive Press, 1994). This final part contains the two conversations that close the book, a long interview conducted by Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs on 23 March 1988 and a brief conversation with Fuchs at St. Vincent's Hospital on 1 June 1993, followed by the contributors' notes. Because these are Young's own words, this is the most valuable section in the book for understanding her aims. Only material that illuminates Young and her work is kept below; quoted phrases are marked with quotation marks.

Everything she wrote is a search for utopia

Young traces the origin of Angel in the Forest to an accidental encounter with coal miners from the Ozarks living a wandering life that reminded her of Steinbeck's The Grapes of Wrath; talking with them led her to write about New Harmony. From this she draws a general principle: “Every real book is, no matter what the subject,” based on social consciousness, and a good writer cannot avoid it. Asked to describe her own obsession, she gives the clearest statement of her whole project: all her work explores the human desire for utopia, and the structure of every book is the search for utopias lost and rediscovered, true of Miss MacIntosh, Moderate Fable, Prismatic Ground, Angel in the Forest, and the Debs manuscript alike. Underlying all of it is the recognition that there is no single reality, and yet, as she puts it, the beauty is that you go on walking toward a utopia that may not exist, “on a bridge which might end before you reach the other side.”

The flour miller and the passage of the soul

In her own voice Young confirms the detail the critics kept circling. She was first inspired to write about New Harmony by an account of a flour miller who tried to pass his thumbmark to his son, a son who then killed himself. She wrote a short story about it that failed because it was too difficult, and ever since she has folded that flour miller and his thumb into everything she has written, because it is “the thing I never could write, the idea of identity and passage of the soul.” She explains that the recurring objects in Miss MacIntosh, a coin becoming a drachma becoming a poker chip, are references to transmutation, transmigration, metempsychosis, resurrection, and loss of identity. She also names the unfinished novellas she still hoped to publish, several built around Mr. Spitzer, including one about his effort to reach King of Prussia, Pennsylvania, to find a pony that had supposedly belonged to Queen Victoria, which she calls “a quest for something that cannot be found.”

Genre belongs to critics, and she is traditional, not experimental

Young insists the distinctions between poetry and prose, and between fiction and nonfiction, belong “not to writers, but to critics.” She finds no real difference between fiction and biography, holds that the poetry of nonfiction is as fabulous as any poetry in a novel, and says the novel as poem differs from the lyric only in length. She has never altered her style in anything she wrote, would never write realistic prose, and has been willing to go years without publishing, because “the style is the writing.” Strikingly, she rejects the label of experimentalist and calls herself traditional, placing her work in a Grand Tradition that runs back through Sterne, Gogol, Dickens, and Poe to Don Quixote, and rooted in the Bible, Saint Augustine, and, she stresses, the poetry of Joseph Smith and the Mormons. She denies being influenced by Joyce and resents the common claim that she echoes Eliot, explaining that she and Eliot merely studied the same sources at the University of Chicago, where she majored in epic literature under Robert Morss Lovett and read Tom Jones thirteen times in Ronald Crane's class. Miss MacIntosh has no beginning, middle, or end, she says, simply “because life has no beginning, middle, or end.”

“I don't make up anything”

The 1993 hospital conversation confirms, from Young herself, the Browning credo the earlier essays reported. Pressed on whether the stone-deaf community and the doctor who delivered imaginary babies could really be true, she answers flatly that they are: she lived in the house with the doctor, knew him, knew Dr. O'Leary and his violin, the man who could not hear the telephone ring and who told Esther Longtree she would never die in childbirth. “I don't make up anything,” she says. “I always know if it didn't happen.” When Fuchs presses on the strangeness of these recollected details, insisting they seem somewhat improbable, Young holds her ground, replies that they were all true, and finally turns the question back: “Well, what is the truth?” This is the same conviction that surfaced throughout the tributes, that she transcribes a real if transfigured world rather than inventing one.

Debs, Riley, and obsession as the engine

Young explains the turn to Eugene Debs as a matter of utopia: knowing what she knew about utopia, the only question was why not Debs, who spoke to ordinary people in poems and images drawn from The Arabian Nights, Poe, Don Quixote, and Dickens. She did not choose him, she says; “He chose me.” The path ran through James Whitcomb Riley, the poet of her Indiana childhood and, she notes, Debs's drinking companion; their conversations about how to reach utopia drew her from the Riley biography, nearly finished, into the Debs project, which grew from a short book into a three-volume epic of some 2,400 pages. She frames obsession as the precondition of all writing: if you have no obsessions, don't write. Debs was obsessed, Riley was obsessed, her characters are obsessed, and so are the historical figures in the Debs book, among them Emma Goldman and Margaret Anderson. She describes a lost book on the art of presenting a whole life in three or four pages, a method she adopted, and recalls the punishing regimen behind Miss MacIntosh: teaching from nine to four, sleeping an hour, writing from six until midnight, night after night. Mark Van Doren, she notes, named this quality in his introduction to Angel in the Forest, calling her effort to capture illusion an “unkillable concern.”

The rage against women, and her own neglect

Young is candid about the reception of her work. At the time she did not resent the attention paid to male experimental writers, because she always believed many of the great writers were women, naming Kay Boyle, Katherine Anne Porter, Christina Stead, Katherine Mansfield, Anna Kavan, and Jane Bowles, and because she thought of herself not as a woman or a man but as someone “doomed to be a writer.” But after Miss MacIntosh she found herself the victim of brutal male reviewers whose tone was “sometimes hysterical,” who suggested that had she married she never would have written such a book, when, as she says of Kay Boyle, one could have “a baby with every book.” She grants that William Goyen and some other men wrote beautiful reviews, but she has come to believe “there is a rage against women” that she did not notice at the time because she was absorbed in teaching and writing. She adds a practical afterthought: the novel would have sold far more copies had it been published one volume at a time.

Her closing self-definition

The book ends on Young's own answer to the question she used to put to her students, why anyone writes. Her reply, which Fuchs also placed at the front of the volume, is that people write to be known and loved beyond their time: “Here was I, this was what I thought, this was what I felt.” A few facts from the contributors' notes round out the record: Charles Ruas conducted the Paris Review interview with Young in its Winter 1981 issue and situated her among peers such as Capote, Tennessee Williams, Burroughs, Morrison, and Sontag; Martha Sattler completed a full descriptive bibliography of Young's work for Dalkey Archive; and Ann Tyler credits Miss MacIntosh as having proved helpful to her own The Accidental Tourist.
