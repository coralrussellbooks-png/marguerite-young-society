Definition from research

Chapter 14 · The Black Coachman and the Family Carriage

  • Symbolizes continuity beyond death, the passage of time, and the family’s legacy, Catherine’s conscience and body.
  • The mystery of his grave and whether he was buried with his horses signifies Catherine’s fear of disruption and loss.

Chapter 14 · White Horses (King, Queen, Prince, Spade)

  • These horses have personified names and represent stability and tradition.
  • Their fate is ambiguous, mirroring the uncertainty in the narrative.

Chapter 16 · Falada

  • Falada, the little black pony given to Vera by Mr. Spitzer, carries rich symbolic meaning that intertwines with Vera's inner world and her journey.
  • Falada is described as an old, tired, wind-broken pony, light as a spirit, with a sagging back, a scrappy mane, and a knotted tail. Despite his physical frailty and advanced age, Falada moves with grace and seems almost magical, responding to Vera's desires as if he were her own intelligence or head. The pony is said to be descended from a horse that inspired the jewel-studded horses of merry-go-rounds and carousels, connecting him to themes of childhood innocence and the cyclical nature of life and dreams.
  • Falada embodies a loyal, gentle companion who carries Vera through her internal struggles and the uncertain landscapes of her life. His responsiveness to her unspoken commands suggests an intimate connection between Vera's mind and her means of navigating reality and illusion.
  • Although Falada is physically frail, he continues to carry Vera with grace and resilience. This mirrors Vera's own vulnerability and strength as she faces the challenges of growing up and understanding complex realities.
  • Falada's origin as a model for carousel horses evokes childhood fantasy and cyclical repetition, resonating with Vera's oscillation between dream and reality. His presence blurs the lines between the tangible and the imaginary, reflecting the novel's themes of perception and illusion.
  • Given by Mr. Spitzer, Falada also symbolizes a connection to a past that is both real and mythical, and perhaps a hope for guidance and stability amid chaos. His lineage to historical and artistic forms evokes continuity and the search for meaning.
  • Falada is described as seeming like Vera's head or intelligence, suggesting that the pony is an extension or manifestation of her self-awareness and agency, her "headless rider" identity where the horse seems to guide the rider as much as the rider guides the horse.
  • Falada is a potent symbol of Vera's complex inner life: her innocence, her challenges, her search for identity and direction, and the interplay between dream and reality that defines her experience in the novel.

Chapter 16 · Falada

  • Represents freedom, imagination, and the link between past and present.
  • Moon and Stars are symbols of mystery, guidance, and the unconscious.
  • Marble Statues and Tombs are symbols of death, memory, and permanence.
  • Water and Sea Motifs represent transition, silence, and the unknown.
  • The entire ride is a metaphor for Vera’s inner journey. The horse is described as “almost only an idea,” and the house is likened to a tomb and an underwater castle.
  • References to historical furniture styles (Queen Anne, Chippendale), mythological creatures (phoenix, Triton), and fairy tales (the Arabic tale of Falada) enrich the novel’s layers.
  • The setting is a magical, moonlit garden and a grand, mysterious house filled with symbolic statues and motifs.

Chapter 18 · The Rider and the Horse

  • Metaphor for the unity of self and illusion, indicating the inseparability of dream and dreamer.

Chapter 32 · The Riderless White Horse and Rider Imagery

  • The image of a riderless white horse "white as snow" coming through the window, bringing snow and cold with it, is a powerful symbol of death or the passing of Cousin Hannah.
  • The white horse traditionally symbolizes a harbinger of death or a psychopomp figure in literature, and here it represents the transition from life to death for this warrior woman. Its entrance is supernatural, blending reality and dream, reinforcing the theme of liminality between life and death.

Chapter 33 · The Black Coachman and Carriage

  • Symbolize passage, possibly death or transition.
  • The carriage on the streets of Boston, after years of absence, signals a disruption of normal order.
  • Catherine also said the Black Coachman was her conscious and the carriage was her body.

Chapter 37 · The Black Coachman

  • A symbol of death or transition, Catherine said it was her consciousness evoked in her dreams.

Chapter 37 · The Riderless Horse and Conch Trumpet

  • Symbolize death’s arrival and the end of an era or life.

Chapter 40 · The Horse and Rider

  • Symbolize unity and inseparability of entities, reflecting on identity and consciousness.

Chapter 42 · Glass Carriage and Alabaster Horses

  • Symbolize the ethereal, dreamlike journey between worlds or states of being. The horses’ heads turning like foam and their journey through clouds evoke a passage beyond the physical.

Chapter 55 · The Coach and Horses

  • Symbolize the journey of life and death; Joachim often finds himself in the wrong carriage, lost in time and space.

Chapter 72 · The grey mule and manure wagon

  • Symbolize burden, endurance, and the dead-end nature of Esther’s existence.

Chapter 82 · The Old White Horse and Surrey

  • Symbolize the passage toward death and the journey between worlds. The horse turning into silver, blue, green, and gold hints at transformation and transcendence.
