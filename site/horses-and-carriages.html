Definition from research

Chapter 12

  • “put his bet on the dark horse”: The phrase comes from Disraeli’s The Young Duke (1831): “a dark horse, which had never been thought of.”

Chapter 14 · The Black Coachman and the Family Carriage

  • Symbolizes continuity beyond death, the passage of time, and the family’s legacy, Catherine’s conscience and body.
  • The mystery of his grave and whether he was buried with his horses signifies Catherine’s fear of disruption and loss.

Chapter 14 · White Horses (King, Queen, Prince, Spade)

  • These horses have personified names and represent stability and tradition.
  • Their fate is ambiguous, mirroring the uncertainty in the narrative.

Chapter 14

  • “the four white horses”: White horses in mythology are often associated with the sun chariot, warrior-heroes, fertility, and an end-of-time savior. The white horses symbolize the solemn, mystical journey of the soul from life into death and beyond, carrying Catherine through the shifting landscapes of memory, dream, and existence by her will. They are a powerful emblem of transition, purity, fate, and the ethereal nature of mortality in the novel. In Revelation, “behold a white horse: and he that sat on him had a bow; and a crown was given unto him” (Revelation 6:2).
  • “the barkings of spotted coach dogs running beside the wheels”: Dalmatians were bred to run beside carriages, clearing the road and guarding the horses.
  • “King and Queen, Prince and Spade”: The horses are named like playing cards, and the aces fall later in the chapter.
  • “the coachman was her conscience”: The Katha Upanishad: “Know the Self as the lord of the chariot, the body as the chariot, the intellect as the charioteer, the mind as the reins, and the senses as the horses.” In Plato’s Phaedrus, the soul is a charioteer driving two horses.
  • “driving a coach and six through a legal statute”: Attributed to the Irish judge Stephen Rice in the 1680s: “I will drive a coach and six through the Act of Settlement.”
  • “a horseshoe of white roses”: Floral horseshoes and saddles of roses honored racing men. Since 1932 the Kentucky Derby winner has worn a garland of roses.

Chapter 16

  • “The fierce, exultant pride that was mine”: In “What Is It with Women and Horses?” (Intelligent Horsemanship, 2019), Dr. Rosie Jones-McVey, drawing on her PhD research among British horsewomen, writes: “In Victorian novels, as well as in the more modern genre of pony-girl books, it is often aboard a horse that girls find their wings, often proving some snot-nosed boy wrong in the process, or enabling them to escape various romantic dramas and find solace in the barn. Just like the pony-girl books, many of my participants’ life stories entwined horseyness with coming-of-age stories. Horses were the means through which many women first acted in a truly independent way, often pursuing equestrian endeavours despite/without their parents, or else, proudly taking up adult-like responsibilities in being the teacher, pilot, and carer of their ponies, rather than the one being looked after and told what to do.” She argues that horses give many women a space for independence and self-assertion that their everyday lives do not.
  • “It was a steeplechase, and we would jump over all the church steeples”: The first steeplechase (1752) was a race across Irish country from one church steeple to the next.
  • “I was a bareback rider, wearing a plume, a pair of pink tights”: Philip Astley’s London circus (1768) began as trick riding in a ring, and the bareback equestrienne in tights became the star of American circuses.
  • “Falada could dance on his hind feet”: The levade and capriole of the Lipizzaners of Vienna’s Spanish Riding School, where horses rear and leap on command.
  • “served as a model for merry-go-round horses because of his grace in keeping all fours off the earth at once”: In 1878 Eadweard Muybridge photographed Leland Stanford’s horse Sallie Gardner and proved that a galloping horse lifts all four hooves at once, though not stretched out, as on carousel horses.
  • “the cabriole, the furniture of good Queen Anne’s day, whole forests of Chippendale”: Queen Anne furniture stands on curved cabriole legs, shaped like an animal’s leg. The name comes from the French cabriole, a goat’s leap. In dressage, the capriole is the horse’s leap with all four feet off the ground. Thomas Chippendale published his Director in 1754.
  • “following the movements of an imaginary polo ball”: Polo came to England from India with British cavalry officers in the 1860s, and Vera wears her “polo hat cocked back at a jaunty angle.”

Chapter 16 · Falada

  • Falada, the little black pony given to Vera by Mr. Spitzer, carries rich symbolic meaning that intertwines with Vera’s inner world and her journey.
  • Falada is described as an old, tired, wind-broken pony, light as a spirit, with a sagging back, a scrappy mane, and a knotted tail. Despite his physical frailty and advanced age, Falada moves with grace and seems almost magical, responding to Vera’s desires as if he were her own intelligence or head. The pony is said to be descended from a horse that inspired the jewel-studded horses of merry-go-rounds and carousels, connecting him to themes of childhood innocence and the cyclical nature of life and dreams.
  • Falada embodies a loyal, gentle companion who carries Vera through her internal struggles and the uncertain landscapes of her life. His responsiveness to her unspoken commands suggests an intimate connection between Vera’s mind and her means of navigating reality and illusion.
  • Although Falada is physically frail, he continues to carry Vera with grace and resilience. This mirrors Vera’s own vulnerability and strength as she faces the challenges of growing up and understanding complex realities.
  • Falada’s origin as a model for carousel horses evokes childhood fantasy and cyclical repetition, resonating with Vera’s oscillation between dream and reality. His presence blurs the lines between the tangible and the imaginary, reflecting the novel’s themes of perception and illusion.
  • Given by Mr. Spitzer, Falada also symbolizes a connection to a past that is both real and mythical, and perhaps a hope for guidance and stability amid chaos. His lineage to historical and artistic forms evokes continuity and the search for meaning.
  • Falada is described as seeming like Vera’s head or intelligence, suggesting that the pony is an extension or manifestation of her self-awareness and agency, her “headless rider” identity where the horse seems to guide the rider as much as the rider guides the horse.
  • Falada is a potent symbol of Vera’s complex inner life: her innocence, her challenges, her search for identity and direction, and the interplay between dream and reality that defines her experience in the novel.

Chapter 16 · Other Motifs

  • Falada represents freedom, imagination, and the link between past and present.
  • Moon and Stars are symbols of mystery, guidance, and the unconscious.
  • Marble Statues and Tombs are symbols of death, memory, and permanence.
  • Water and Sea Motifs represent transition, silence, and the unknown.
  • The entire ride is a metaphor for Vera’s inner journey. The horse is described as “almost only an idea,” and the house is likened to a tomb and an underwater castle.
  • References to historical furniture styles (Queen Anne, Chippendale), mythological creatures (phoenix, Triton), and fairy tales (the fairy tale of Falada, which Mr. Spitzer calls Arabic) enrich the novel’s layers.
  • The setting is a magical, moonlit garden and a grand, mysterious house filled with symbolic statues and motifs.

Chapter 17

  • “It was a horse ballet, a Spanish carousel with many mirrors”: Baroque courts staged horse ballets, and the carrousel was first a tournament of horsemen, like Louis XIV’s Grand Carrousel of 1662.

Chapter 17 · Animal Imagery

  • Falada, Vera’s old pony, a present from Mr. Spitzer, represents loyalty and alertness but is powerless.
  • Vera’s comparison of Miss MacIntosh to a “codfish swept up upon a barren shore” and “old man” adds to the grotesque transformation.

Chapter 18 · The Rider and the Horse

  • Metaphor for the unity of self and illusion, indicating the inseparability of dream and dreamer.
  • Vera wonders if she has “become Falada,” with Miss MacIntosh now the rider.

Chapter 18

  • “as if I myself had become Falada”: Vera’s pony of Chapter 16, named for the horse in the Grimm tale.

Chapter 22

  • “has won the famous steeplechase at Aintree where is the dreaded water-jump”: Master Robert, who had once pulled a plow, won the Grand National at Aintree in 1924.

Chapter 24

  • “Some had seen the black coachman”: The coachman and the white horses of Chapter 14.

Chapter 24 · Black and White Horses and Coachman

  • Black and white horses and coachman symbolize the passage between realms, possibly life and death, or reality and illusion. Earlier (Chapter 14) Catherine said the white horses were her will and the coachman was her conscience.

Chapter 26

  • “the great crowds of Derby Day”: The Derby at Epsom, run since 1780. William Powell Frith painted its crowds in “The Derby Day” (1858).

Chapter 27

  • “the great breakers coming in with their foaming manes and tails”: Poseidon was god of the sea and of horses, and breaking waves are still called “white horses.”

Chapter 32

  • “should have been employed by the old Pony Express”: Relays of riders carried the mail between St. Joseph, Missouri, and Sacramento, California, from April 1860 until the telegraph replaced them in October 1861.
  • “the black horses who wore black plumes”: Victorian hearses were drawn by black horses wearing tall plumes of black ostrich feathers.
  • “a riderless horse white as snow came through the window”: A riderless horse follows the coffin in state funerals. Lincoln’s horse, Old Bob, was led behind his hearse in Springfield in 1865, and Black Jack followed John F. Kennedy’s coffin in 1963.

Chapter 32 · The Riderless White Horse and Rider Imagery

  • The image of a riderless white horse “white as snow” coming through the window, bringing snow and cold with it, is a powerful symbol of death or the passing of Cousin Hannah.
  • The white horse traditionally symbolizes a harbinger of death or a psychopomp figure in literature, and here it represents the transition from life to death for this warrior woman. Its entrance is supernatural, blending reality and dream, reinforcing the theme of liminality between life and death.

Chapter 33 · The Black Coachman and Carriage

  • Symbolize passage, possibly death or transition.
  • The carriage on the streets of Boston, after years of absence, signals a disruption of normal order.
  • Catherine also said the Black Coachman was her conscious and the carriage was her body.

Chapter 37 · The Black Coachman

  • A symbol of death or transition, Catherine said it was her consciousness evoked in her dreams.

Chapter 37 · The Riderless Horse and Conch Trumpet

  • Symbolize death’s arrival and the end of an era or life.

Chapter 40 · The Horse and Rider

  • Symbolize unity and inseparability of entities, reflecting on identity and consciousness.

Chapter 42 · Glass Carriage and Alabaster Horses

  • Symbolize the ethereal, dreamlike journey between worlds or states of being. The horses’ heads turning like foam and their journey through clouds evoke a passage beyond the physical.

Chapter 55 · The Coach and Horses

  • Symbolize the journey of life and death; Joachim often finds himself in the wrong carriage, lost in time and space.

Chapter 72 · The grey mule and manure wagon

  • Symbolize burden, endurance, and the dead-end nature of Esther’s existence.

Chapter 82 · The Old White Horse and Surrey

  • Symbolize the passage toward death and the journey between worlds. The horse turning into silver, blue, green, and gold hints at transformation and transcendence.
