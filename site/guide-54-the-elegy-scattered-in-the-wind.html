Chapter 54: The Elegy Scattered in the Wind

← Chapter 53 · Guide Home · Chapter 55 →

Research notes: Chapter 54

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Music, Sound, and Silence

Symbols: Pearls and Jewels

Philosophy: Freud and Jung · Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · Milton · Romantic Poets · The History of Opium · Women's Suffrage

Mr. Spitzer writes music on scraps of paper.

He carries the fragments in his pockets and on the lining of his coat and on whatever surface is available when the notes come to him. He is composing an elegy, for his brother, for Cousin Hannah, for the lost, for the downtrodden, for the passenger pigeon, for everything that has been extinguished or forgotten or left at a threshold it could not cross. The elegy is the project of his life. It has been the project of his life since Peron died, and before that, in the long anticipation of the loss it now mourns.

At the end of Chapter 54, the elegy is scattered in the wind. The scraps of paper, the musical notation, the fragments of the composition that might one day have cohered into something; they flutter away from him in a city that keeps moving and shifting and placing the wrong buildings on the wrong streets. He watches them go. He does not chase them.

Young does not present this as a tragedy. It is simply what happens to the music written in fragments, carried in pockets, composed in a world that will not hold still long enough to be notated accurately. The elegy was real. The wind is also real. Both things are true simultaneously.

What actually happens in Chapter 54

Mr. Spitzer moves through a Boston that is simultaneously the city he knows and a city that is rearranging itself around him: streets shifting, the Flatiron Building appearing where it should not be, the Fishback Building misplaced, the Steinway Building in the wrong location. The city of his memory and the city of the present keep failing to correspond. He moves through them both at once, navigating by a map that is perpetually becoming inaccurate.

He encounters the figures who populate the underside of the city: derelicts, forgotten heirs, persons who have lost their bank accounts and their identities and sometimes their names. He helps them when he can: buying clothes and necessities for those in need, trying to restore what has been lost. He is the unofficial representative of the hidden majority, still, the man who carries the causes that were never popular and never formally entrusted to him.

He is agitated about a missing jewel from a rajah's crown: an incident he is somehow implicated in but cannot clearly remember. The great rajah's jewel, foreshadowed chapters earlier, surfaces here as a source of anxiety rather than a resolved narrative. He was accused of being a jewel thief. He does not know if he did it. He prefers glass jewels to real ones, which tells you everything you need to know about where Mr. Spitzer stands on the question of illusion versus reality: the glass jewel is honest about what it is, which makes it more trustworthy than the real one.

The chapter ends with his elegy fluttering away, and Mr. Spitzer standing alone in the city, present and absent simultaneously, a figure the chapter describes as caught in a liminal space beyond human knowledge.

The sound effects man

Among the chapter's figures is the sound effects man: a mysterious character associated with Peron, capable of producing the entire sonic world: trains, roosters, barking dogs, wind, ice creaking. He fills the environment with chaotic noise, a performance of everything the world sounds like when compressed into a single voice.

Mr. Spitzer, who has always sought silence, who is the musician of “silence flowing under the sound of” incidental music, regards this man with an unwilling envy. The sound effects man can produce the world. He can make any sound on demand. He is chaos made vocal, the opposite of the silence Mr. Spitzer values and cannot quite achieve.

Young associates the sound effects man with Peron, with the flamboyant, volatile, many-sided brother who could become anything. Peron was the sound effects man of the family: able to produce any register, any tone, any identity required by the occasion. Joachim was the silence under the sound, the music that was never played because it could not compete with the noise.

But the envy is real. Young records it without judgment. To be the silence is honest, and perhaps even necessary, but it is also lonely. The sound effects man fills every room he enters. Mr. Spitzer empties rooms by arriving in them a minute late, finding only the echoes of what was there before.

The shifting city

The Boston of Chapter 54 is one of Young's great composite cities: part geographical Boston (Beacon Hill, the Common, the State House, Paul Revere's North End, Faneuil Hall with its grasshopper weathervane), part psychological projection of Mr. Spitzer's unstable inner landscape, part memory of a city as it was and a present that keeps displacing it.

The buildings are wrong. The Flatiron Building belongs in New York. The Fishback Building belongs in Indianapolis. The Steinway Building belongs in Midtown Manhattan. Mr. Spitzer moves through a city that has imported the architecture of everywhere he has been or thought about or absorbed from his brother's wandering life, and placed it all on Boston's streets without regard for what was there before.

This is what Catherine's mind looks like from the outside. Vera, narrating this chapter, notes that her mother has influenced Mr. Spitzer so profoundly that he can no longer distinguish reality. He has absorbed Catherine's mode of perception along with Peron's underworld knowledge. He is, by this point, a composite of everyone he has spent time with in this household: the cautious lawyer, the absent twin, the opium invalid's atmospheric logic, the suffragette's legendary life. He is multiple, and the city he walks through reflects the multiplicity.

Glass jewels and real ones

Mr. Spitzer's preference for glass jewels over real ones is one of the chapter's smallest and most precise details.

The real jewel is valuable because it is what it is: its beauty is inseparable from its genuineness. The glass jewel is only beautiful in the sense that it performs beauty, that it catches the light in the way beauty is supposed to catch the light, without the underlying substance that makes that catching matter. Mr. Spitzer prefers the glass one. This is not simply an inversion of values. It is the consistent position of a man who has spent his life trusting the false because the true has already failed him. The glass jewel is honest about being a substitute. The real jewel makes claims about permanence and value that the world keeps failing to honor. Mr. Spitzer, who has watched the most permanent-seeming things dissolve (the suffrage captain, his twin, the music on paper, the cities of his memory) has arrived at the rational conclusion that the thing which admits its substitution is more trustworthy than the thing that does not.

The great rajah's jewel that has gone missing (that he may or may not have taken, that he cannot remember clearly) is a real jewel. Of course it is missing. Of course he cannot account for it. Real jewels in this novel are always already on their way to being lost.

Lost heirs and forgotten legacies

Mr. Spitzer's wandering through the underside of Boston (seeking the lost heirs, the persons who have forgotten their bank accounts, the derelicts and the forgotten) is the chapter's most direct portrait of the man as he actually functions in the world.

He is the unofficial executor of everything unexecuted. The people with legitimate claims who cannot assert them. The people with legacies who have lost track of the fact that they have them. The people whose identities have become so attenuated by poverty or grief or the simple passage of time that they are no longer sure what they are entitled to. Mr. Spitzer finds them and helps them remember.

This is the practical expression of what the novel has been saying philosophically: he could love only by indirection, he was the representative of causes “never popular in any public sense.” In the streets of the shifting city, this means buying a coat for a man who has forgotten he deserves one. Finding the heir who does not know they are an heir. It is unglamorous. It produces no public recognition. It is the love available to a person who cannot love directly, and it is real love, and Young insists on this.

The elegy

The elegy Mr. Spitzer has been composing on scraps of paper, an elegy for everything he has lost and everything that was lost before he arrived to witness it, is the musical form that names his purpose. An elegy is a mournful poem or musical composition for the dead. It is the form that converts loss into artifact, that makes the fact of the gone-thing into something that can be heard or read by those who remain.

Mr. Spitzer's elegy is fragmented because his experience is fragmented. It is composed on scraps because scraps are what are available in a life organized around secondhand experience and belated arrival. The motifs of mistake and truth intertwine in it, as they do in everything he produces: he has been stamping the wrong seals on the wrong documents for decades, and his music is similarly marked by the grace note of error, the way things turned out rather than the way they were intended.

The elegy scattering in the wind at the chapter's close is not a defeat. Young is too honest to present it as one. The elegy was always going to be impermanent; that is the condition of anything composed on scraps, in transit, by a person who was always a minute late. The wind takes it. This is what happens to the music of the dead. It disperses into the atmosphere it was always already part of. It was never going to stay on paper. Paper doesn't hold this kind of music. The air does. The air has it now.

What to hold onto

Chapter 54 is the chapter where Mr. Spitzer is most fully present in the world and most fully lost in it simultaneously: moving through a city that corresponds to his mind rather than to geography, helping the people who are as lost as he is, composing music that will not survive the wind.

What to carry forward: the sound effects man as the form of love Mr. Spitzer cannot perform, the capacity to fill the room, to produce the world on demand, to be all noise where he is all silence. The glass jewel as the honest substitute preferred over the genuine that keeps disappearing. And the elegy, scattered but not gone, now part of the atmosphere of a city that was already half-invented anyway. It is in the air. You are breathing it. This is as close as it was ever going to get to being heard.
