Chapter 7. The Witness and the Burning Glass

Part of a reading of Angel in the Forest (1945), Marguerite Young’s history of the two failed utopias at New Harmony, Indiana.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 6 · Angel in the Forest: A Reading in Fourteen Chapters · Chapter 8 →

Marguerite Young / Angel in the Forest, Chapter 7

Every book has an ethics, whether it means to or not, some stance it takes toward the people and things it describes. Most histories take the stance of the judge. They gather the evidence, weigh it, and hand down a verdict: this leader was wise, that experiment was doomed, here is the lesson. Angel in the Forest refuses the bench. Its ethics is something harder and stranger, and Young never names it directly because she is too busy performing it on every page. The closest anyone has come to naming her instrument is Mark Van Doren, who described her gaze as a remorseless burning-glass. A burning glass gathers light to a single point until it scorches. That is exactly what Young does with her attention. She focuses it so hard it burns. And then, crucially, she never once uses the heat to reach a verdict. The stance is witnessing, and it is the most demanding thing the book asks of anyone, the author first and the reader after.

What witnessing is not

It is easy to mistake this for something weaker than it is, so it is worth saying what witnessing is not.

It is not neutrality. It is not the pose of the objective observer standing outside the scene with no stake in it. The very first pages of the book, as this study began by noting, are Young refusing that pose, arriving on the creaking ferry and planting herself inside the haunted present rather than above it. She has a position, and it is intensely felt. She loves this ruined town. She is appalled by Rapp's cruelty. She grieves the collapse of both dreams. Witnessing is not the absence of a position. It is the discipline of not letting your position foreclose the complexity of what you are looking at.

It is not both-sidesism either, that tired performance of having no view and calling the emptiness fairness. The both-sidesist has no position and pretends the vacancy is virtue. The witness has a strong position and refuses to let it blind her. Those are opposite things.

And it is not the same as sympathy, or as judgment, though it can look like either from a distance. Sympathy dissolves the thing you are looking at into your feeling about it. Judgment freezes the thing into your verdict on it. Witnessing does neither. It keeps the object fully itself, in all its contradiction, standing there in front of you, neither melted by your pity nor fixed by your sentence. This is why it is so hard. You have to hold the whole thing at once, and get nothing in return, no resolution, no relief.

Four things held without resolving

The book is a sequence of impossible objects that Young refuses to simplify, and it is worth walking through the hardest of them.

There is the Rapp problem. He was a genuine spiritual visionary and a man who ruled his community through sexual terror, and both of those are completely true at the same time. The reader wants Young to choose. Tell us whether to admire him or condemn him, whether he was a holy man or a monster, and let us close the book with a clean feeling. She will not. Van Doren caught the tone of it when he called Rapp a mountebank, perhaps, and certainly a tyrant, and in the same breath unforgettable, a man to be forgiven even for his monstrous ideas about sex. To resolve Rapp in either direction would be to look away from half of him, and looking away is the one thing the witness may not do.

There is the Owen problem, which is the same shape in a different man. Owen was a person of real moral seriousness who looked hard at the misery of the mill children and set out to end it, and he was a man whose certainty about human nature crushed the actual people placed in his care. Both true, held together, unresolved.

There is the Pears problem, and this is the subtlest and the most uncomfortable, because there is no grandeur in it to soften the difficulty. Thomas and Sarah Pears are witnesses to the collapse of a noble experiment, their letters the ground-level record of a dream failing. They are also small people complaining about mud, about the dancers arriving filthy at the balls, about the loss of comfortable Pittsburgh, fussing and homesick and a little snobbish. The temptation is overwhelming to pick one: to honor them as testimony or to dismiss them as petty. Young does neither. She does not excuse their smallness, the class fussiness, the endless looking backward. And she does not for one second dismiss their grief, which is real, Sarah worn out to the point where she felt like an animated mummy. Young lets them be small and grieving at the same time, because that is what people actually are, and this is where witnessing costs the most, because there is no nobility to take shelter in, only ordinary human pettiness and ordinary human pain held in the same hand without flinching and without condescending.

And there is the angel problem, which is the purest case of all. Rapp said an angel descended and left its footprint in stone. Was it real? Young never says. Van Doren noticed that she lets the question float and never brings it down, and that suspension is not evasion. It is the most precise possible statement of what a witness does: she stays in the room with a question she cannot answer, and she refuses to force it shut. She will not even grant herself the small comfort of debunking, the easy modern relief of "of course there was no angel," because that comfort would be a lie about the limit of what anyone can know, and it would flatten Rapp into a mere liar, foreclosing the possibility that he genuinely saw something. The angel hangs there, unresolved, for four hundred pages. It is the emblem of the Young’s method.

Attention as the only available sacredness

None of this is cold, and that is the part most easily missed. The burning glass is remorseless, but it is not indifferent. The heat is a kind of love.

Van Doren ended his introduction with a line that gives the Young’s engine away. He wrote that no one sees the world so well as the person who is afraid that nothing in it will turn out to be real. That fear is Young's fuel. Because she cannot count on any world beyond this one, because there is no guaranteed heaven to redeem the sandpipers and the ruined gardens and the failed dreamers, she attends to them with an intensity that amounts to reverence. She looks at the morning glories flourishing among the stinkweeds, at the yellowing lilies thriving in neglect, at a pair of baby shoes and an empty doll's cradle and a bracelet made of hair, and she does not use these things as signposts pointing upward to something better elsewhere. She rests her whole attention on them, here, and that resting is what makes them matter.

This is the sacred as this philosophy understands it, pointing not up toward heaven but down into the actual, ordinary, unredeemed world. Full attention to what is really here turns out to be the only sacredness a person can be sure of, and it turns out to be enough. Young's four hundred pages of relentless looking are the book's central argument about what holiness looks like once you stop expecting it to arrive from outside. To stay in the room with something, to refuse to reduce it to a verdict, to keep looking especially when the looking is uncomfortable, is what love is when it is being honest about the world. The witness and the lover, it turns out, do the same thing. They both refuse to look away.

Why the reader is the one being tested

The reason witnessing is the hardest thing the book asks is that the book asks it of the reader, not just the author. Young practiced this discipline writing it. She imposes the same discipline on anyone who reads it, through the form itself. Page after page withholds the relief you keep reaching for. You want to be told whether Rapp was good or evil, whether the angel was real, whether the Pearses deserve your sympathy or your scorn, whether the whole New Harmony experiment was noble or foolish, and the book never once hands you the verdict. You cannot finish it with a tidy opinion. If you have one, you were not paying attention, because paying attention is precisely what dissolves the tidy opinion.

So reading the book is itself the practice. The discipline Young performed in writing it is the discipline she requires of us in receiving it: hold the contradiction, stay in the room, love the thing enough to keep looking at it without needing it to resolve. She does not fix New Harmony and she does not judge it and she does not weep over it and walk away. She stays, and she looks, and she holds the whole impossible place in the burning light of an attention that will not quit and will not conclude. That staying is the book's ethics and its love at once, and it asks us, quietly and relentlessly, to learn to do the same.
