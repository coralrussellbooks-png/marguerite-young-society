Research notes: Chapter 25 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 25

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Mr. Chandelier · Peron Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

Mr. Spitzer is structurally, not metaphorically, multiple. He is:

  • Himself (Joachim)
  • His dead brother (Peron)
  • The ghost his mother perceives
  • The lawyer
  • The ex-musician
  • The man who was told he is dead

"He, Mr. Spitzer, was this soul divided, for he was this twin, and one was dead, and one lived, this confusion reigning like order in his great heart."

The word "reigning like order" is philosophically precise. The plurality does not feel like chaos from the inside; it has its own internal logic, its own governance. This is exactly Pillar 1's insistence that multiplicity is not a problem to fix. Mr. Spitzer does not try to resolve which brother he is. He cannot. He carries both.

More radically, the text refuses to stabilize the identity question:

Why should "he always think his dead brother’s thoughts, even at the expense of his own conservative life?"

The self here is not divided between two options. It is the ongoing, unresolvable occupation of one self by another. The living man thinks the dead man's thoughts. The boundary of personhood is permeable, not as pathology, but as ontological fact. The plural self is not a wound. It is the architecture.

The desk clerk's declaration that Mr. Spitzer is dead is the chapter's darkest joke and its most serious philosophical statement: from the outside, the plural self cannot be verified as existing at all.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The mother's bedroom is the chapter's laboratory for this pillar. She sees:

  • The table as a coffin of stars
  • The rose in his buttonhole as a lady's ear
  • Mr. Spitzer himself as a tower of water, a basket of peaches, a fat duchess

And crucially, Mr. Spitzer does not simply dismiss these perceptions:

"He being rather willing to concede that there was doubtless some right in her point of view which shifted constantly."

This is not politeness. This is epistemological honesty. He cannot disprove her constructions because he knows his own perceptions are equally constructed:

"There were also those illusions which he had taken for mountains."

The glass wall episode is the chapter's central parable of Pillar 2. Mr. Spitzer walks through a wall because he perceives no barrier, and his perception is, in its own terms, accurate: the floor level continues, his thoughts are uninterrupted, no sensation registers. Reality, for him in that moment, contains no wall. The wall's existence is confirmed only afterward, by glass particles behind his ears and a bill arriving in the mail. Reality is invoiced to him retrospectively.

The text refuses to say which version, wall or no wall, is more real. Both leave evidence. Neither is primary.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

This pillar may be the chapter's deepest subject. Grief here is not an event or a response. It is the climate in which all action occurs.

"He had died with every minute since his brother's death, perhaps before."

The "perhaps before" is devastating. Grief precedes its own cause. This is not psychological disorder; it is the text's recognition that in a world of sedimentary time (Pillar 8), loss is always already present before the event that names it.

Mr. Spitzer's grief has not moved him toward resolution in any of the years since Peron's death. Instead:

"He had lost, as a matter of fact, almost all feeling for himself."

"Food had lost its taste for him, even its texture."

"Bread tasted like ashes in his mouth."

These are not symptoms awaiting treatment. They are the permanent weather of his world. The chapter never suggests he should feel otherwise, never implies a path toward healing. The honest life, as Pillar 3 demands, stops pretending otherwise, and this text has stopped pretending with extraordinary thoroughness.

Most tellingly, grief has become generative rather than merely destructive:

"He was indebted to her, he knew, for some sense of his own precarious being."

The grief-saturated evenings with his mother give him the only confirmation of his existence he receives. Grief is not weather to escape. It is the only atmosphere in which he remains real.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Mr. Spitzer is, structurally, a witness. He comes every evening. He does not cure the mother. He does not argue her back to consensus reality. He does not leave.

"He came when the moon arose. If he did not come, she still believed that he was there."

His ethical practice is presence without intervention. He weighs, considers, concedes partial rights to her shifting point of view, and makes no permanent commitments. This is not weakness or evasion; it is the practice Pillar 4 describes: attention as the practice, refusal to simplify as the act.

His manner is described with precision:

"His manner of procedure being tacitly to weigh all waning possibilities and come to no direct conclusion."

From a conventional standpoint this looks like failure: a man who cannot decide, cannot act, cannot conclude. From Pillar 4's standpoint it is the most rigorous ethical posture available in a world where every conclusion forecloses what it doesn't see. He stays in the room. He keeps the eyes open. He does not rush toward resolution.

The one moment he does act, trying to collect damages for the glass wall incident, ends in the announcement of his own death. Action, in this world, is what gets you declared nonexistent.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Mr. Spitzer's legal practice is a perfect figure for this pillar. He searches for:

"Lost deeds and lost heirs and bastard sons and unnatural mothers and lost heirlooms and lost musicians."

Every case is a reconstruction of something that has come apart: a lineage, a claim, a belonging. He builds cases knowing they will likely fail (the lost heir whose mother denies him ). He writes painstaking letters. He makes careful arrangements. He is turned away without an interview.

And he returns. The next evening. The next case.

The recursive structure is also formal: the chapter itself circles back repeatedly to the same images: the glass wall, the brother's death, the evening visits, the twin iron beds and twin golden cradles. Each return deposits a new layer of meaning without resolving the previous one. The text enacts what it describes.

"He was not, he must admit, the sudden, untimely suicide as his brother had been but rather the hesitant suicide who takes his luxurious time."

The hesitant suicide is the recursive builder: always returning to the beginning, never completing the final act, finding in the return itself a form of continuation that is not quite living and not quite dying, but is undeniably building.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

This pillar is everywhere in the chapter's texture, but it is most explicit in the description of Mr. Spitzer's music:

"The music which should never be written and which should never be heard upon this earth."

"Sometimes he felt, shaking with a new, implausible anguish, that the only music worth hearing was the music he had never heard, perhaps his own which he had never written."

The unwritten music is not presented as tragedy. It is presented as the truest music: the one that has not been falsified by completion, by the violence of the concluded form. This is Pillar 6 at its most radical: the unfinished work is not the failed work. It is the honest one.

His legal work follows the same logic. The cases he takes have no estates involved. There is nothing to win in the conventional sense. The work is its own justification: the ongoing sentence, not the concluded one.

Even his love for the mother is unfinished and unrequited:

"If ever she had accepted his passive hand in marriage and his undying love... his life might not have been so different from this life of patience and of waiting."

The conditional, if ever, has never resolved. Decades of waiting. And the waiting is not framed as wasted time. It is framed as the shape his devotion has taken, the form his love has found. Incompletion as integrity.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Mr. Spitzer occupies every marginal position simultaneously:

  • Living but declared dead
  • Present but perceived as absent or ghostly
  • A lawyer who loses cases
  • A musician who writes no music
  • A twin who is also both twins
  • A man in love who has never been accepted

He is the chapter's evidence in Pillar 7's sense, not the exception to the categories of living/dead, self/other, success/failure, but the proof that those categories are constructions that cannot hold at their edges.

The mother's perception makes this explicit:

"The Mr. Spitzer who came to see her was not the Mr. Spitzer she saw."

There are at least two Mr. Spitzers in every room: the one who arrives and the one who is perceived. Neither is more real. Both are constructed. The marginal figure (the twin whose twin is dead, the man who is told he doesn't exist) makes visible what the centered figure conceals: that all identity is this unstable, always.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The twin golden cradles are the chapter's most concentrated image of sedimentary time:

"Mr. Spitzer would have sooner parted from his life than have parted from those cradles, they reminding him of infancy and death."

Infancy and death in the same object, the same sentence. The cradles are not relics of the past. They are the past pressing upward through the present, still structuring where Mr. Spitzer sleeps, still organizing his bedroom, still insisting on their presence alongside the twin iron beds that have replaced them in function but not in meaning.

Time in this chapter does not move forward cleanly:

"He living in either the remote future or the past when they should join and be as one, as brother and brother."

The present has "little meaning". This is not dissociation; it is an accurate description of how sedimentary time works. The present is the thinnest layer. Beneath it, the past accumulates, still active, still determining. The future is where the past will finally resolve (brother joining brother), but that resolution is always deferred, always not-yet.

Peron's death is not behind Mr. Spitzer. It is beneath him, pressing up through every step:

"He who blamed the dust for that which the dust had not forgotten."

The dust remembers. The sediment holds what the living mind tries to release.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter's prose style is itself an argument for this pillar. Sentences extend through multiple subordinate clauses, qualifications, reversals, and concessions until the original assertion has been so thoroughly complicated that no single meaning can be extracted:

"He had suffered egregiously for the fact that if he was the right man, then he was the right man in the wrong world, and if he was the wrong man, then he was the wrong man in the wrong world."

This sentence constructs a logical structure that appears to offer two options and then demonstrates that both options arrive at the same place. Language here is not conveying a pre-existing truth; it is performing the impossibility of stable location, using its own grammar to enact what it describes.

The mother's language is equally constructive but differently so; she names the table a coffin of stars, the rose a lady's ear. Her language does not describe reality; it makes it. And the text grants her constructions equal standing with consensus perception.

Most tellingly, Mr. Spitzer's own existence is language-dependent:

"He had been told, by an obtuse desk clerk, that Mr. Spitzer was dead."

He is declared dead by administrative language. His arguments, his counter-language, fall upon the empty air. Language has the power to unmake him. His existence depends on whether the right language is spoken in the right institutional context. This is Pillar 9's darkest implication: language does not just shape what can be seen. It determines who can exist.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The chapter's transcendence is entirely immanent: located in the most ordinary, even degraded objects and moments:

  • Glass particles behind an old man's ears like chandelier prisms
  • Twin golden cradles beside twin iron beds
  • A white carnation or tuberose in a buttonhole
  • Coffee that tastes like yesterday
  • A cracked, wave-warped mirror

These are not symbols pointing toward a beyond. They are the sacred itself, fully located in the material world, in the specific texture of a grief-saturated ordinary life.

The chandelier is the chapter's most sustained sacred image: Mr. Chandelier with his candle lights like dim stars in some dim, glassy heaven. The heaven is not outside the room. It is in the room, hanging from the ceiling, made of glass and candlelight, fully immanent, fully present, perceived only by a woman on opium who may be seeing more clearly than anyone else.

"He existed only where separateness from Heaven could not be denied."

Heaven here is not a destination. It is a condition of existence: the place where the separation between here and there becomes undeniable, which is to say, the place where immanence is most fully itself.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is perhaps the pillar that most completely describes Mr. Spitzer's ontological situation. He is not a self moving through time toward resolution. He is the point where multiple times cross:

  • His own past and his brother's past
  • The time before Peron's death and the time after
  • His own potential death (the glass wall, the near-fall) and his continued life
  • The infancy of the golden cradles and the old age of the iron beds
  • The future where brother joins brother and the present where one is dead

"He had been born first, the first of two... Should he not have died first, or should not both have died together?"

The question of birth order and death order is a question about the crossing point: about which time is primary, which self is the carrier, which death is owed. There is no answer. There is only the crossing.

His obligation to carry Peron (to think Peron's thoughts, to pursue Peron's unfinished business, to keep Peron alive through his own living) is exactly Pillar 11's structural obligation:

"Only through him, as he could not too often insist, his brother lived."

He did not choose this. It was assigned by the fact of twinhood, by being born first, by surviving. The ancestors (in this case, the dead twin) are in the sediment. The carrying continues. Mr. Spitzer is not a man who has decided to honor his brother's memory. He is the crossing point where Peron's time and Joachim's time intersect, and the intersection is not voluntary. It is structural. It is what he is.
