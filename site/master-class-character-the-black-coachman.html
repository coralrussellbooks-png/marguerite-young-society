The Black Coachman: The Life Made From a Face Turned Away

← James the Chauffeur · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · The Sea-House Servants →

Characters: Jacqueline White · Joachim Spitzer · Madge Capehorn

If James is the invented driver the mother can describe down to his crimes, the black coachman is the one she cannot describe at all. He has no face she has ever seen, barely a name she can recall, and almost no history beyond the driving itself, and he is the one on whom, she insists, everything depends. He is her other chauffeur, older and deeper than James, and where James turned out not to matter, the book says plainly of this one that "it was the black coachman upon whom all things depended." The emptier he is, the more he carries.

What little is known of him is loss. He drove her father and a whole vanished family on their daily circuit, the father, his blind mother, his unmarried sister, his blind wife, and he "survived them all, just by a shade," and then he died too. Now he is dead and still driving. She never sees his face. Through years of dreaming she sees only "the back of the coachman's head," nodding as if in sleep under a high silk hat, and she asks the question she can never answer, "Why did the black coachman never turn his face toward her?" He does not turn, does not answer, does not stop. The carriage never waits and never arrives. He is fused to the thing he drives, so that "horses and coach and coachman were one entity," a driver who cannot climb down from his own box because there is no separate man to climb down.

She keeps him past death the way she keeps everything, by arranging it. She wrote him into her will and left him the carriage, the four white horses, his livery and silver buttons and plume, and asked to have him buried in all of it so that he could go on "driving the horses under the earth." The idea, she is careful to say, was his, not hers. He "wanted the carriage and the horses, just so that he might continue his old employment," a servant who applied to keep working after he was in the ground. His grave, meanwhile, is "hypothetical and changing," never visited, maybe never real, and she has arranged instead that "the black coachman should visit her." The dead man is on call. In this house the dead do not leave, and this one she has put under contract not to.

The reason he matters more than James is that he is not really a servant at all. He is an allegory she rides inside. "Her body was the carriage," her soul was the passenger, "her horses were her will, and the coachman was her conscience." He is the faceless thing that steers her, the part of her that knows where the journey goes and will not turn around to tell her. That is why locating him becomes the whole problem of her nights: "If she could find him, she could perhaps find herself, the bewildered passenger." She is a passenger in the carriage of her own body, driven by her own conscience, and she cannot see either the driver or the road, only that the wheels keep turning on a circular avenue where the end is the beginning.

The construction is so complete that it finally swallows her. Riding behind him she loses track of whose carriage it is, whether the coachman is her coachman or "any old coachman" picked up somewhere, whether the passenger in her seat is even herself, "who was this passenger in her carriage, she would shrilly ask, and who had taken her place?" The invention has grown larger than the woman who made it. When Mr. Spitzer ends up in the seat, he is "the wrong passenger" in the wrong coach, and the coachman "did not know," being now a skeleton driving toward a funeral whose name has been forgotten. Death drives whoever climbs in. The coachman does not check the fare.

This is the strange rule the coachman reveals, and it is the reverse of what James suggested. James was drawn in full, an ex-convict with a rap sheet and a moose to shoot, and the book set him aside in one line, "It had not been her chauffeur upon whom her life depended, however." The coachman is drawn as nothing, no face, no name, no story, and everything depends on him. The more a made figure can be seen, the more it can be checked and found unnecessary. The less it can be seen, the more weight it holds, because a conscience with a face would only be a man, and a driver with a name could be replaced. He carries the whole journey precisely because there is nothing to him but the driving. "Her drugged vision provided more of reality than reality provided," and the most real thing in it is the one she can least make out.

The lesson that gathers here is the darkest of the set. Madge and Jacqueline and James are built for company, to be quarreled with and grieved and loved. The black coachman is built for something else. He is the driver every passenger in this book turns out to have, the invented figure at the reins of a life that is going somewhere its owner cannot see, and the honest thing, the thing the mother does and cannot stop doing, is to keep riding behind him with her eyes open. She never makes him turn around. She only asks, again and again, why he will not, which is the same as asking where the carriage is going, which is the one question the faceless driver she built will never answer and she will never stop needing to ask.
