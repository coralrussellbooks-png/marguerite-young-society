To All My Darlings

Chapter 39

On Chapter 39 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 4, 2026

← Chapter 38 · Home · Chapter 40 →

Summary

Vera reflects on the irony that Cousin Hannah, a rationalist in the avant-garde of suffrage, became for Catherine a figure of dead romance, a knight errant who could not be held to mortal law. Catherine, lonely and drugged, half believes she imagined Hannah’s visit: “It was as if the perpendicular pronoun I had come to see my horizontal mother.” Her “I” means many things. She is the chair, the lamp, Brooklyn Bridge, a great hostess and a recluse at once, travelling everywhere without leaving the house. Her soul is a house of endless rooms, and in every room someone is missing. She knows that opium has poisoned all pleasures at their source, and that knowledge of so many selves has only added to her torment. Her sanity, she believes, is simply knowing that she is insane, and she would not trade her beautiful false selves for one dull true one. She greets her phantoms, Lord Murdock Burdock Thrushmorton, who twitters like a cage of canaries, and Mr. Res Tacamah, the drug bottle with very large ears, and wonders whether God, too, is dreaming opium dreams, and whether dream and reality are the same.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early on, Catherine reflects on a great Bostonian lady, an avant-garde suffrage leader, who paradoxically evokes a “dead romance” despite her rational, modern activism. Catherine senses this figure as “not quite of this modern world,” caught between reality and a haunting, unreal past marked by interruptions and failures of communication (telephones ringing, busy signals, wrong parties). This sets the tone for Catherine’s own sense of isolation and distance from life itself, revealing her fragile grasp on reality and her compensatory use of imagination.
  • The description of “Cousin Freemount-Snowden” as an immortal phantom, “a creature of the immortal night,” fighting dragons and monsters, symbolizes Catherine’s perception of this suffrage figure as timeless and mythic. This passage explores themes of mortality, idealism, and the tension between dreams and reality. Catherine questions whether such a figure should be subject to “mortal law” or if she belongs to a realm beyond normal human experience.
  • Catherine’s experience of hallucinations and the fragmentation of the self is vividly portrayed: “It was as if the perpendicular pronoun I had come to see my horizontal mother,” and the sense of multiple selves dancing or dividing. She questions her own reality, the reality of the visitor, and the continuity of identity, wondering if she or the visitor is dead, if she is dreaming, or if the “I” she knows is stable or shifting.
  • A profound metaphor describes Catherine’s soul as a vast, infinite house with “many rooms,” “many stairways,” and “many heavens,” where she moves endlessly without finding an exit. Each room contains different versions of herself, different guests, and missing elements, symbolizing her fractured identity and the elusive nature of self-knowledge. The imagery of “floors wavering like water” and “walls seeming to move” underscores the instability of her mental state.
  • Catherine experiences time as fluid and overlapping, seeing “faces like old town clocks” striking different, “deranged” hours and existing simultaneously in many places: ski hotels, regattas, ancient cities. This dissolves the linear notion of time and blends memories, fantasy, and present experience, emphasizing the theme of timelessness and Catherine’s alienation from ordinary reality.
  • Catherine acknowledges that opium “poisoned all pleasures at their source,” creating a “swirling fog” that distorts reality, moves stars out of their stations, and shifts castles from mountain to mountain. This highlights the role of addiction in her mental fragmentation and the blurring of the rational cosmos she once loved, showing how her perception is both enchanted and trapped by drugs.
  • Catherine’s self-awareness of her madness is striking: she believes her “own sanity was simply this—she knew she was insane.” The novel explores how troubles and madness are cyclical, manifesting repeatedly in different guises, symbolized by dragons turning into saints and vice versa. This passage conveys the tragic and complex nature of mental illness and the futility of self-knowledge in curing it.
  • Catherine interacts with imaginary or symbolic figures, Mr. Spitzer, Lord Murdock Burdock Thrushmorton, Mr. Res Tacamah, who represent parts of her fractured mind or external influences. Despite her isolation, she enacts social rituals, greeting these “guests,” which reflects her remaining attachment to social life and her imaginative engagement with her fragmented reality.
  • The chapter ends with a powerful image of the sea meeting the shore, the dead meeting the living, and the fog as a place for “absent-minded persons” who “walked with their eyes hidden behind their hands.” It suggests a liminal space between life and death, reality and dream, emphasizing Catherine’s existential state of existing in a world of blurred boundaries where neither clear truth nor stable identity exists.
  • The final reflection questions whether reality and dream are distinct or the same, if God too is dreaming, and if reality is a shared hallucination or creation. This philosophical meditation ties Catherine’s personal experience to universal themes about perception, existence, and the meaning of life, ending the chapter on a note of profound ambiguity and mystery.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “If the visit was real, then the visitor was not.” What does Catherine gain by doubting one or the other?
  • Catherine “really was enslaved… perhaps for the very simple reason that she imagined herself free.” How can believing oneself free be a chain?
  • Her “sanity was simply this—she knew she was insane.” Is that sanity?

Characters

  • Hannah must “ride, ride, ride to that victory which would be her own extinction.” What victory ends in extinction?
  • Catherine would not give up “all the beautiful false selves for the sake of one who was true.” What would the true self be like?
  • Lord Murdock Burdock Thrushmorton is “a wooden leg stamping along old Gothic streets.” Why might Catherine welcome him at all hours?

Connections

  • Catherine’s soul is “a many-spiraling house with many rooms.” How does this house compare to the real house by the sea?
  • In Chapter 37 Catherine asked who the dreamer was. Here she asks whether God is “dreaming these opium dreams.” Is the question the same?
  • The perpendicular I and the horizontal mother: how does Vera’s place in this chapter differ from her mother’s?

Reflection

  • Does self-knowledge heal, or can it add “to these wild waves of thoughts”?
  • Is a world with no mistakes, as Catherine says, a pity?
  • Which is the greater mystery, order or “that disorder which seemed itself innate”?

Research: Characters

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • Central figure, a woman caught between reality and dreams.
  • A social hostess and recluse, a traveler and prisoner of her mind.
  • Struggles with mental illness, opium addiction, and fragmented identity.
  • “She said that her imagination was all there was.”
  • She “really preferred confusion to order, these interesting, illusive ambiguities.”
  • She is “this horizontal lady, her heart like wandering snowflakes, her eyes congealed.”

Cousin Hannah Freemount-Snowden

  • A symbolic visitor, representing a heroic figure from a bygone era.
  • Described in mythic terms, she embodies ideals and struggles that contrast with Catherine’s condition.
  • “Her great Coriolanian eyebrows bristling, her hair streaked with white, her great Roman nose casting its shadow.”
  • She was “never recreant from her great cause,” and “she must ride, ride, ride to that victory which would be her own extinction.”

Mr. Spitzer

  • A character that also imbues symbolic or imaginary concepts like rationality, science, or a caretaker.
  • Envious of Catherine’s imaginative light, yet also a voice of reason.
  • “He had supposed he was the lamp-lighter and the flame, the city of trembling bridges.”
  • He “most loved or claimed he loved” the rational cosmos.

Lord Murdock Burdock Thrushmorton

  • An eccentric figure “twittering like a cage of canaries,” blending the absurd with the gothic.
  • Represents the chaotic, unpredictable elements in Catherine’s world.
  • He is “a wooden leg stamping along old Gothic streets,” “a perfect English gentleman,” “really quite canary-brained.”
  • “Twitter on, Burdock. Twitter on.”

Mr. Res Tacamah

  • Author of fantasies, controlling madness through dreaming rather than acting.
  • His drug bottle symbolizes Catherine’s opium use and the blurred line between control and chaos.
  • He is “the porcelain drug bottle with very large ears,” which “contained the purple fog and the glowering genii.”

The Black Coachman

  • Catherine goes out with him, “his eyes rolling like dice,” to make afternoon calls in the great world capitals.
  • He is “wound in his cloth of gold, for surely he was her prince of the East.”

Research: Themes

Duality of Reality and Imagination

See: Illusion and Reality · Dreams and Imagination · Duality and Doubles

  • Catherine exists simultaneously in multiple realms: physical, mental, spiritual.
  • She is described as living through dreams and hallucinations, often unable to distinguish between real visits and imagined ones (e.g., the visit from Cousin Freemount-Snowden).
  • Her imagination is described as “all there was,” compensating for isolation and loss.

Fragmented Identity and Multiplicity of Selves

See: Identity and the Self

  • Catherine perceives herself as many selves, sometimes contradictory, never unified.
  • She contemplates which self is true or dominant, fearing that choosing one would mean losing the many fascinating false selves that sustain her.
  • This fragmentation extends to her questioning of self-knowledge and sanity, recognizing her madness but finding it integral to her existence.

Timelessness and the Fluidity of Time

See: Time

  • Time is portrayed as elusive, nonlinear, and sometimes meaningless.
  • Catherine experiences multiple times and places at once: simultaneous winters in different ski resorts, many historical eras.
  • The narrative questions the nature of time, blending past, present, and future, life and death.

Isolation and Alienation

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Catherine feels isolated, both physically and psychologically.
  • Despite her social prominence and many guests, she is ultimately alone in her infinite, maze-like mental house.
  • The imagery of empty rooms, phantom guests, and the sea’s roar emphasizes her alienation.

Illness and Madness

See: Madness and Sanity

  • Catherine’s opium use and mental state are central to her experience.
  • She is aware of her insanity and the limitations imposed by it.
  • Madness is depicted as both a torment and a source of her vivid imagination and insight.

Imagery of the Sea and Fog

See: Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos

  • The sea symbolizes the boundary between life and death, reality and dream.
  • Fog and mist represent the unclear, shifting nature of perception and consciousness.
  • The sea’s spray and the fog’s enveloping nature create a liminal space where conventional rules do not apply.

The Role of Memory and History

See: Memory and the Past

  • Catherine recalls historical figures and events with intimate detail, blending fact and fantasy.
  • Her memory is selective, fragmented, and often symbolic.
  • This reflects the human tendency to reconstruct identity and meaning through past narratives.

Stars, Moons, and Light

Dragons, Saints, and Mythical Creatures

Sea and Shore

The Umbrella

Research: Resources

  • “the rational suffrage movement”: Cousin Hannah is described as being part of the “avant-garde” women’s suffrage movement and that is a real thing with Inez Milholland Boissevain as a prominent figure who led the 1913 suffrage parade in Washington, D.C., and was known for her radical activism. She was part of a Greenwich Village group of progressives and socialists who were involved in creating The Masses, a progressive magazine that combined art, satire, and political commentary. She was dressed in a flowing white cloak and riding a white horse. She was a lawyer, a peace activist, and part of a group that embraced radical art and political commentary, making her a figure who pushed the boundaries of activism. Her beauty and the symbolism of her attire during the parade helped to capture public attention and advance the cause of suffrage. She was part of a broader movement that included other notable figures like Dorothy Day, Crystal Eastman, and Louise Bryant, all involved in radical and progressive movements. While other leaders like Susan B. Anthony, Elizabeth Cady Stanton, and Alice Paul were also key figures in the suffrage movement, Milholland’s involvement in a group that pushed the boundaries of art and activism, along with her leading role in the parade, made her a prominent figure in the avant-garde of the movement. See Women’s Suffrage.
    • During the American Revolution some women could and did vote. New Jersey’s constitution of 1776 gave the vote to “all inhabitants” worth fifty pounds, and women voted there until 1807.
    • Inez Milholland collapsed on a speaking tour in 1916. Her last public words were, “Mr. President, how long must women wait for liberty?”
  • “destroyed by telephones and Western Union messengers”: The telephone had its critics who thought that it would degrade face-to-face conversation, break up the home life, and it allowed people to talk too much. No law or official rule barred women from the telephone. The limits they met at first came from wealth, from where the telephones were, and from changing social norms. The industry sold the telephone for business, and men complained that women were frivolous with a tool built for serious business, using the lines for gossip and long social calls. Some early companies tried, without success, to set strict time limits on residential calls to keep the lines open for men. Yet the system would have collapsed without women. Emma Nutt became the first woman telephone operator in Boston in 1878, the switchboards were soon run almost entirely by women, and women’s calling at home became a mainstay of the business (Claude S. Fischer, America Calling: A Social History of the Telephone to 1940, University of California Press, 1992; Michèle Martin, “Hello, Central?”: Gender, Technology, and Culture in the Formation of Telephone Systems, McGill-Queen’s University Press, 1991). It is a potent symbol of the yearning for connection amid pervasive isolation and the inadequacy of human means to bridge the gaps created by time, death, and misunderstanding. It underscores the novel’s exploration of the fragile and often illusory nature of communication and the human condition.
  • “buzzings like thousands of Libyan honey bees”: Herodotus wrote of the Gyzantes of Libya, “in their country much honey is made by bees, and much more yet, it is said, by men who have the skill” (Herodotus, Histories 4.194, trans. A. D. Godley, 1920). The Libyan honey bees symbolize the wild, continuous, and haunting sensory presence surrounding Catherine, embodying the oppressive and dreamlike atmosphere that defines her fragile state between life and death.
  • “Someone had gone from France to Hades”: Hades in Greek mythology is the god of the Greek underworld. See Greek Mythology.
    • Hades is also the name of his realm, the land of the dead.
  • “that great knight errant storming against this castle perilous”: In Malory’s Le Morte d’Arthur (1485), Sir Gareth rescues the Lady Lyonesse, besieged in the Castle Perilous.
  • “coming with her petard”: A petard was a bomb for blowing in a gate. “For ’tis the sport to have the engineer / Hoist with his own petar” (Shakespeare, Hamlet 3.4). See Shakespeare.
  • “her great Coriolanian eyebrows bristling”: Coriolanian eyebrows means a feature referring to Gaius Marcius Coriolanus, a Roman general famed for his exceptional valor.
    • Shakespeare’s Coriolanus (1608) is the proud general who scorns the people. See Shakespeare.
  • “her great Roman nose”: Roman nose or Aquiline nose is a human nose with a prominent bridge, giving it the appearance of being curved.
  • “the seven-headed monsters wearing golden turret crowns”: The seven-headed monsters may refer to the beast from the sea: “and saw a beast rise up out of the sea, having seven heads and ten horns, and upon his horns ten crowns” (Revelation 13:1). In the Sumerian religion it was killed by Ninurta and hung on his chariot. These monsters are part of the rich symbolic and mythological tapestry of the novel, representing the complex and often overwhelming forces, both internal and external, that the characters face. They emphasize the epic and surreal nature of struggle and the broader themes of conflict, transformation, and endurance in the story. See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “the terrifying, red-eyed basilisks”: The basilisk is a legendary reptile reputed to be a serpent king who can cause death with a single glance.
    • Pliny the Elder wrote that the basilisk kills with its glance and its breath (Natural History 8.33).
  • “She had slain the minotaur”: Minotaur is a large, bull-headed man in Greek mythology who is trapped in a labyrinth. See the Chapter 35 resources and Greek Mythology.
  • “she imagined herself free”: “No one is more a slave than he who thinks himself free without being so” (Goethe, Elective Affinities 2.5, 1809).
  • “the perpendicular pronoun I had come to see my horizontal mother”: English is almost alone in capitalizing the pronoun I, which grew into a single tall stroke in Middle English so that it would not be lost in the line. These concepts are much more complicated. This phrase encapsulates the complex emotional and spiritual dynamics between Vera and her mother: the clash of their states of being, their different modes of existence, and the challenge Vera faces in seeking connection, understanding, and meaning in the shadow of her mother’s fading life.
  • “The dead were those who travelled fastest”: “Die Todten reiten schnell,” “the dead ride fast,” is the refrain of Gottfried August Bürger’s ballad “Lenore” (1773). Bram Stoker quotes it in the first chapter of Dracula (1897).
  • “The personal pronoun I did not always mean”: “I AM THAT I AM” (Exodus 3:14). Jesus uses the phrase “I am” in seven distinct statements each emphasizing a specific aspect of His divine nature and purpose, connecting him to the God revealed in the Old Testament. Descartes wrote, “I think, therefore I am” (René Descartes, Discourse on Method, 1637), which then leads into Existentialism and Absurdism. Existentialism proposes the idea that people are free to choose their own meaning and are responsible for their choices, there are no inherent meaning or purpose to life and instead people are responsible for creating meaning, personal experience and subjective reality are used to shape understanding of the world, freedom, responsibility, choice, self-discovery and the absurdity of human existence are explored. Anaïs Nin wrote that Young’s work was not absurdist, because Young believed there was an inherent beauty to the world (Anaïs Nin, The Novel of the Future, Macmillan, 1968). It symbolizes the core being of a character or presence who asserts existence and identity beyond the chaotic flux of illusions and transformations depicted in the novel. It is a moment of profound existential affirmation amid the pervasive theme of identity’s fragmentation and the quest for meaning. See Descartes and Dualism and Existentialism.
  • “She was Brooklyn Bridge”: Brooklyn Bridge: See the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “what was her personal identity among so many transshifting objects”: The chapter explores the complex and ambiguous nature of identity. It suggests that identity is not fixed or unitary but rather elusive, multifaceted, and constantly shifting. The chapter highlights the difficulty of defining a person, noting that people can appear as different selves or reflections and may be surrounded by illusions, dreams, and multiple selves that complicate any clear understanding of who they truly are. Catherine, reflects on the fragmented, transient, and sometimes contradictory aspects of identity, suggesting that a person is composed of many transient selves, some of which may be hidden or unknown even to the individual. It is said that the self is often divided, with inner conflicts and shifting moods, and that external appearances can be deceiving or insufficient to capture the true nature of a person. The novel emphasizes that identity can be like a mask, a dream, or a reflection in a mirror, all subject to change and interpretation. It touches on the idea that a person may be different at various times or in different contexts, and that the boundaries between life and death, self and other, reality and illusion are blurred. The chapter raises questions about the permanence of identity and whether it is possible to truly know oneself or others, given these complexities. Identity is portrayed as a fluid, dynamic phenomenon that resists simple categorization, shaped by internal contradictions, external perceptions, and the interplay of reality and illusion. Young spoke of “the profound influence upon me of William James and Saint Augustine in ‘The City of God’” (Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs, “A Conversation with Marguerite Young,” Review of Contemporary Fiction 23, no. 1, Spring 2003 (1989 original)). She told Studs Terkel, “William James made a beautiful statement, man is body, soul, and clothing” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). William James wrote The Varieties of Religious Experience with twenty lectures. William James also supported Pragmatism that considers words and thought as tools and instruments for prediction, problem solving, and action, and rejects the idea that the function of thought is to describe, represent, or mirror reality. See William James and Pragmatism and Augustine and Christian Theology.
  • “one for a murdered archduke”: Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria was shot at Sarajevo on June 28, 1914, and the First World War followed.
  • “beginning memento mori”: Memento mori is an artistic or symbolic reminder of the inevitability of death.
  • “the spokes of the wheels of her carriage were sleeping cherubim”: “And when I looked, behold the four wheels by the cherubims, one wheel by one cherub, and another wheel by another cherub” (Ezekiel 10:9). See The Bible.
  • “She was immured”: Immured means to enclose within or as if within walls.
  • “she was extra muros”: Extra muros means outside the walls or community.
  • “an old friend from Newport”: Newport is a city set on Aquidneck Island in Rhode Island.
    • In the Gilded Age the very rich built summer “cottages” there, such as The Breakers (1895).
  • “Czar Kolokol, king of the Kremlin bells”: Czar Kolokol also known as Tsar Bell is a bell on display at the Moscow Kremlin.
    • It was cast in 1733-1735, cracked in a fire in 1737, and has never been rung.
  • “though this lady had lived and died in the twelfth century”: The twelfth-century lady may be Rosamund Clifford, mistress of King Henry II. Legend says he hid her in a labyrinth at Woodstock, where Queen Eleanor found her and offered her the dagger or the poisoned bowl, and Rosamund drank the poison. Young’s lady dies by the dagger.
  • “those hidden from history”: History is the study and the documentation of the past. Events before the invention of writing systems are considered prehistory. History is an umbrella term comprising past events as well as the memory, discovery, collection, organization, presentation, and interpretation of these events. Historians seek knowledge of the past using historical sources such as written documents, oral accounts, art and material artifacts, and ecological markers (Wikipedia). Many historians believe that the production of history is embedded with bias because events and known facts in history can be interpreted in a variety of ways. Constantin Fasolt suggested that history is linked to politics by the practice of silence itself (Constantin Fasolt, The Limits of History, University of Chicago Press, 2004). According to Michel-Rolph Trouillot, the historical process is rooted in the archives, therefore silences, or parts of history that are forgotten, may be an intentional part of a narrative strategy that dictates how areas of history are remembered (Michel-Rolph Trouillot, Silencing the Past: Power and the Production of History, Beacon Press, 1995). Historical omissions can occur in many ways and can have a profound effect on historical records. Information can also purposely be excluded or left out accidentally. Gerda Lerner, a twentieth century historian who focused much of her work on historical omissions involving women and their accomplishments, explained the negative impact that these omissions had on minority groups. Her books Black Women in White America: A Documentary History (Pantheon Books, 1972), The Majority Finds Its Past: Placing Women in History (Oxford University Press, 1979), and Why History Matters: Life and Thought (Oxford University Press, 1997) have been used to examine women’s history. Women’s history is the study of the role that women have played in history and the methods required to do so. It includes the study of the history of the growth of women’s rights throughout recorded history, personal achievement over a period of time, the examination of individuals and groups of women of historical significance, and the effect that historical events have had on women (Wikipedia). Howard Zinn, in A People’s History of the United States (Harper and Row, 1980), presented what he considered to be a different side of history from the more traditional, fundamentally nationalist glorification of the country. Zinn portrays a side of American history that can largely be seen as the exploitation and manipulation of the majority by rigged systems that hugely favor a small aggregate of elite rulers from across the orthodox political parties. Zinn inspired Roxanne Dunbar-Ortiz to write An Indigenous Peoples’ History of the United States (Beacon Press, 2014). It is the third of a series of six ReVisioning books which reconstruct and reinterpret U.S. history from marginalized peoples’ perspectives. Human history is portrayed as something that may be largely imaginary, transient, and unknowable. The narrative suggests that human beings often live with fragmentary selves and multiple identities, making it difficult to grasp a complete or unified history of any individual. The chapter emphasizes that history and memory are not straightforward but are clouded by illusions, dreams, and shifting perceptions. The past is described as being as uncertain as the future, with no total way of knowing what truly happened or what meaning to assign to events. The novel also explores the idea that death and life, truth and falsehood, and memory and oblivion are intertwined in ways that defy simple historical narratives. The chapter touches on the limits of human understanding, the impermanence of life, and the inevitability of change and loss. It conveys a sense of melancholy about the elusiveness of truth and the continual presence of mystery within what is recorded as history. Human history is a multifaceted and elusive phenomenon, highlighting the difficulty of fully comprehending the lives and experiences of individuals, even remarkable ones. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.

    • Florentines are from Florence, Italy.
  • “numerous variable sahibs”: Sahib comes from the Arabic for companion or owner. In India it became a courteous title like “Mister,” used especially for Europeans.
  • “the Midnight Express”: Midnight Express has been referenced in art where it refers to a large locomotive. It seems to mean transportation at night while the passengers sleep. The Midnight Express (1924) is also a silent film about trains. It is a poetic and symbolic way of expressing the experience of crossing from one existence to another, the uncertain and often haunting transit through the unknown, as portrayed in the dreamlike and ethereal atmosphere of this chapter.
  • “great leviathans rolling, lifting like islands in the flood”: “Leviathan, which God of all his works / Created hugest that swim the ocean stream,” is taken for an island by the pilot of a night-foundered skiff (John Milton, Paradise Lost 1.201-208, 1667). See Milton.
  • “for surely he was her prince of the East”: The black coachman is in Catherine’s dreams as her “prince of the East” which refers to Lucifer, the light bringer, Venus, morning star. It bears rich symbolic meanings of illumination and transformation. The black coachman is “wound in his cloth of gold,” and the narrative connects him to the realm of dreams, stars, and spiritual guides, emphasizing his role as a liminal figure between life and death, reality and illusion, much like the symbolic light bearer Lucifer or the morning star Venus. “How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!” (Isaiah 14:12). See The Black Coachman and The Bible.
  • “the purple roof-tops of pagodas”: A pagoda is an Asian tiered tower with multiple eaves that serves a religious function in Buddhism. See Buddhism.
  • “the faces with Roman numerals”: Roman numerals are a number system which originated in ancient Rome.
  • “She saw an idiot who had become a great philosopher”: Wittgenstein, despite his genius, was known for his unconventional behavior and challenging personality. He even questioned his own sanity, asking Bertrand Russell if he was an idiot. Russell asked him to write something over the vacation, and after reading it told him not to become an aeronaut (Bertrand Russell, “Ludwig Wittgenstein,” in Portraits from Memory and Other Essays, Simon and Schuster, 1956). His profound insights into philosophy, language, and logic led to his recognition as a major philosopher. The idea of a philosopher becoming an “idiot” is a common trope often referencing Diogenes of Sinope. Arthur Schopenhauer was also considered eccentric. See Wittgenstein and Schopenhauer.
  • “the stormy petrels flying”: Petrels are said to be named for St. Peter, because they seem to walk on the water (Matthew 14:29). See Birds.
  • “the great regattas at Cowes”: The regatta at Cowes is called Cowes Week and is one of the longest running regular regattas in the world.
    • The first regatta there was held in 1826.
  • “the horse races at Deauville”: Deauville, in Normandy, France, has a racecourse for thoroughbred horses.
    • The racecourse opened in 1864.
  • “Florentine plazas”: Florentine plazas are in Florence, Italy.
    • The Champs-Élysées is an avenue in the 8th arrondissement of Paris, France.
    • Champs-Élysées means Elysian Fields, the paradise of heroes in Greek myth, which is why Young sets them “after this life is over.” See Greek Mythology.
  • “how opium had poisoned all pleasures at their source”: Thomas De Quincey turned from “The Pleasures of Opium” to “The Pains of Opium” in Confessions of an English Opium-Eater (1821). See The History of Opium.
  • “her runic dirges”: Runic describes runes which are the letters in the runic alphabet used in Germanic languages before the adoption of the Latin alphabet.
    • Dirges are a song or hymn of grief or lamentation before a funeral or memorial. The word comes from the Latin “Dirige, Domine,” the first words of an antiphon in the Office of the Dead.
  • “take arms against a sea of troubles”: “Take arms against a sea of troubles” is from Hamlet, Act III, Scene I, the famous soliloquy “To be, or not to be.” The metaphor resonates with the novel’s exploration of the ambiguous and often turbulent nature of existence, where the “sea of troubles” can be seen as the emotional and existential storms that engulf the characters. Taking up arms is a way of asserting agency and maintaining one’s integrity amid the chaos. See Shakespeare.
  • “Equalities, which had always been innate”: Gender fluid philosophy challenges the traditional binary view of gender, suggesting that gender is not fixed but rather a spectrum or a dynamic process that can change over time. This fluidity can apply to both gender identity and gender expression, meaning how a person presents themselves to the world. Simone de Beauvoir in, The Second Sex, was foundational in distinguishing between biological sex and social gender, laying the groundwork for later explorations of gender as a social construct. The novel embraces gender fluid philosophy primarily through its poetic and symbolic depiction of identity as unstable, multiple, and mutable. Characters traverse and blur gender boundaries; metaphors of transformation underscore the ephemeral nature of identity; and the narrative challenges fixed binaries by portraying a world where selves, including gendered selves, are in constant flux. This fluidity is not articulated in explicit gender theory terms but is deeply woven into the novel’s exploration of selfhood, embodiment, and the interplay between reality and illusion. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
    • Gender fluid mythological figures: Zeus, Aphrodite, Hermaphroditus, Teiresias
  • “a blushing coward who would leave woman to fend for herself”: Research about masculinity expanded in the 1970s onward. Julián Marías wrote in his book Metaphysical Anthropology (1971) that man’s condition is insecurity, neediness, ignorance, indecision and helplessness. However, males are seen as having the main quality of strength. If he doesn’t then he feels he is inferior and must make up for this lack. Male strength may be physical, but can also consist in cunning, intelligence, prestige, experience, authority, aplomb, capacity of expression, aptitude for revealing or feigning those faculties. The paradox according to Marias is that man is ignorant, weak and threatened which leads to man’s pretension that he is the source of knowledge, strength, power and security. Joseph Pleck examined masculinity by doing a meta analysis of the male role starting with the 1930s and found it to consist of numerous instances of blatant misrepresentation of data, twisted reinterpretations of disconfirming results, misogyny, homophobia, and class bias (Joseph H. Pleck, The Myth of Masculinity, MIT Press, 1981). Alan G. Miller examined the myth of masculinity in patriarchal society as men are supposed to be competitive, diligent, disciplined, level-headed, strong, virile, controlled, successful (Alan G. Miller, “The Myth of Masculinity: A Panel,” American Journal of Psychoanalysis 33, no. 1, 1973, 65-67). Women, if considered at all, were presumed to be the opposite if for no reason other than no one ever asked them. Thomas J. Gerschick and Adam S. Miller later studied how men with physical disabilities come to terms with these ideals (Thomas J. Gerschick and Adam S. Miller, “Coming to Terms: Masculinity and Physical Disability,” in Men’s Health and Illness: Gender, Power, and the Body, ed. Donald Sabo and David Frederick Gordon, Sage, 1995). Young disparages the stereotypical feminine virtues as well. Young presents masculinity and femininity as fluid, multifaceted, and deeply intertwined with themes of identity, power, loss, and illusion. Both are shown as complex, sometimes contradictory forces shaping the characters’ experiences in a shifting, often surreal world. The novel critiques rigid gender norms and reveals the porous boundaries between male and female, reality and dream, life and death. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “It was not merely the void—it was the aching void”: “But they have left an aching void / The world can never fill” (William Cowper, “Walking with God,” Olney Hymns, 1779).
  • “And how should my mother have self-knowledge”: Self-knowledge and its limitations: inaccessibility of the mind, cognitive biases, excessive self-criticism, self-deception, over-analysis, paranoia. The chapter portrays self-knowledge as a complex, often troubling state that reveals the fragmented, uncertain nature of identity. It brings no simple truths but rather leads to deeper awareness of inner contradictions and the limits of understanding oneself fully.
  • “even like dragons slain by saints”: The best known is St. George, who slays the dragon in The Golden Legend of Jacobus de Voragine (about 1260).
  • “she knew she was insane, quite mad”: “Divine madness” or “theia mania” refers to unconventional, sometimes unpredictable behavior attributed to divine inspiration or the influence of a god, often associated with mystical experiences or spiritual practice. Plato explored the idea as a source of creativity and inspiration: “the greatest of blessings come to us through madness, when it is sent as a gift of the gods” (Plato, Phaedrus 244a, trans. Harold N. Fowler, 1925). It can take the form of “religious ecstasy” where a person has a profound connection to the divine, transcending normal perceptions and societal norms. It is connected to the transcendence of conventional reality and rationality, embracing the enigmatic, paradoxical, and dreamlike nature of existence, as seen through the experiences and reflections of the characters. See Plato and Platonism.
  • “which of many selves should she have knowledge of”: Know thyself is an Ancient Greek aphorism that has been attributed to almost a dozen Greek sages. It is the first of three Delphic maxims. The two maxims after know thyself were nothing to excess and surety brings ruin. The maxim, or aphorism, know thyself, has had a variety of meanings attributed to it in literature, and over time, as in early ancient Greek the phrase means know thy measure. In the chapter it is a complex, fraught pursuit marked by ambiguity and the interplay of illusion and reality, emphasizing the limits of human understanding of the self and the fluid nature of identity.
  • “her rival Belshazzar”: Belshazzar was the son and crown prince of Nabonidus, the last king of the Neo-Babylonian Empire.
    • “In that night was Belshazzar the king of the Chaldeans slain” (Daniel 5:30). See the Chapter 33 resources and The Bible.
  • “instead of wandering stars”: “Wandering stars, to whom is reserved the blackness of darkness for ever” (Jude 1:13). See The Bible.
  • “wandering Jews”: Wandering Jew is a mythical, immortal man whose legend began to spread in Europe in the 13th century. Young explained her interpretation in the essay “The Artist as Wanderer” (Marguerite Young, Inviting the Muses: Stories, Essays, Reviews, Dalkey Archive Press, 1994), where to her this represents the external and internal wandering of an artist. See Judaism.
  • “a simple scientific truth or statement of true or false”: Absolutism versus relativism: absolutism asserts universal moral truths, while relativism denies them. Absolutism emphasizes the inherent rightness or wrongness of actions, while relativism considers the context of the action and the perspective of the individual or group. Absolutism allows for universal moral condemnation, while relativism may hesitate to judge actions based on another culture’s standards. The opposite of absolutism in government is democracy (liberality, ease, mercy, relaxation, understanding, laxity, leniency, tolerance). It presents a tension between absolutism and relativism. On one hand, it acknowledges the human desire for clear, stable truths and order. On the other hand, it confronts the fluidity, ambiguity, and uncertainty of experience and reality, reflected in the characters’ meditations on dreams, illusions, and the mutable nature of truth. The chapter ultimately suggests that reality is a complex interplay of both absolute and relative elements, where truth may be as fleeting and elusive.
  • “great torch-bearers with streaming meteoric hair”: A comet is a long-haired star, from the Greek komētēs. See Astronomy.
  • “striking thirteen bells far out at sea”: A ship’s bell strikes at most eight bells, at the end of each four-hour watch. Thirteen bells is no time at all. See The Sea and Water.
  • “Oh, come in, she would say”: This is what Young would do in her classroom. At Iowa she “would pause to greet Henry James at the door” (David O. Dowling, “The Guru,” in A Delicate Aggression: Savagery and Survival in the Iowa Writers’ Workshop, Yale University Press, 2019). She told Studs Terkel of Miss MacIntosh’s source, dying and greeting guests who were not there: “Oh, isn’t that Mr. Henry James? How do you do, Mr. James?” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). That is a big clue as to what is going on here. The novel is rich with a dialectical engagement of ideas and beliefs that operate beyond the limits of time and space, reflecting on the nature of existence, identity, memory, and the interconnectedness of all souls. This ongoing conversation is woven into the narrative structure and the psychological depth of the characters throughout the book. See Marguerite Young on Her Craft.
  • “Is it God’s drug”: Karl Marx famously described religion as “the opium of the people” (Karl Marx, A Contribution to the Critique of Hegel’s Philosophy of Right, 1844), suggesting that it numbs the pain of social inequality and exploitation. Lenin called it “a sort of spiritual booze” (Vladimir Lenin, “Socialism and Religion,” 1905). Religion may be used to maintain the status quo and discourage social change. This view might assert that religion offers easy answers to complex questions about the universe, life, and death. It may appeal to those who lack resources and/or education to form different perspectives. The novel suggests that religion, like opium, can be both a source of comfort and a form of escapism or a way to cope with the harshness and uncertainties of mortal life. Catherine’s opium paradise, filled with angels and ethereal visions, embodies this duality: it is an enchantment that extends her life beyond the physical but also confines her to a dreamlike state, disconnected from concrete reality. This resonates with the Marxist critique of religion as “the opium of the people,” implying a narcotic that dulls awareness and delays confrontation with real suffering. However, Young also complicates this view by showing that such illusions, whether religious or drug-induced, can be necessary for survival amid profound existential despair. Catherine inhabits a liminal space between life and death, where the opium-induced religious visions provide a fragile structure amid chaos and loss. In Chapter 1, Miss MacIntosh’s critique highlights the need to face reality soberly and practically, warning against being seduced by illusions that reject nature and the “bare face” of existence. The novel portrays religion as a double-edged phenomenon: a kind of opium that can numb pain and offer transcendence but also one that risks imprisoning the believer in illusions. This ambivalence is embodied in the contrast between Catherine’s opium dreams and Miss MacIntosh’s grounded common sense, reflecting the complex role religion plays as both a solace and snare in human life. See The History of Opium and Miss MacIntosh.
  • “the porcelain drug bottle with very large ears”: Tacamahac, or tacamahaca, is an aromatic resin once sold by apothecaries, and Mr. Res Tacamah may take his name from it. The “glowering genii” recall the Arabian Nights tale of the fisherman who uncorks a brass bottle from the sea and lets out a jinni. See Fairy Tales and Folklore and The History of Opium.
  • “Lord Montrose of Montrose”: Lord Montrose of Montrose may refer to James Graham a Scottish nobleman, poet and soldier, lord lieutenant and later viceroy and captain general of Scotland.
    • He “changed his personality whenever he changed his high silk mourner’s hat.” Young told Studs Terkel, “William James made a beautiful statement, man is body, soul, and clothing” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See William James and Pragmatism and Clothing, Hats, and Skirts.
  • “a skull grinning at a banquet table”: At Egyptian banquets, Herodotus says, a man carried round a wooden corpse in a coffin to remind the guests that they would be like it when dead (Histories 2.78). See Ancient Egypt.
  • “and if it had been, then what a pity for the world”: Utopias and dystopias are both capable of producing oppressive environments.
    • Young’s Angel in the Forest (1945) tells of New Harmony’s two utopias, the Rappites and the Owenites. See Marguerite Young on Her Craft.
  • “And was not God dreaming, too”: In Hindu myth Vishnu sleeps on the serpent Shesha upon the cosmic ocean, and the world is said to be his dream. See Hinduism.
  • “should not Gabriel lose his plume”: Gabriel is the archangel who brings God’s messages, to Daniel (Daniel 8:16) and to Mary (Luke 1:26). Michael is the guardian of Israel (Daniel 12:1). See Angels and Archangels.
