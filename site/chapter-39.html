Summary

Ironically, Hannah the rationalist became for Catherine a figure of dead romance. Vera maps her mother's mind as an infinite house, visited by figures such as Lord Murdock Burdock Thrushmorton and Mr. Res Tacamah. Catherine admits that opium has poisoned her pleasures at their source. Vera describes their meeting as "the perpendicular pronoun I" coming to see "my horizontal mother." The chapter ends asking whether God, too, dreams opium dreams, and whether dream and reality are the same.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early on, Catherine reflects on a great Bostonian lady, an avant-garde suffrage leader, who paradoxically evokes a “dead romance” despite her rational, modern activism. Catherine senses this figure as “not quite of this modern world,” caught between reality and a haunting, unreal past marked by interruptions and failures of communication (telephones ringing, busy signals, wrong parties). This sets the tone for Catherine’s own sense of isolation and distance from life itself, revealing her fragile grasp on reality and her compensatory use of imagination.
  • The description of “Cousin Freemount-Snowden” as an immortal phantom, “a creature of the immortal night,” fighting dragons and monsters, symbolizes Catherine’s perception of this suffrage figure as timeless and mythic. This passage explores themes of mortality, idealism, and the tension between dreams and reality. Catherine questions whether such a figure should be subject to “mortal law” or if she belongs to a realm beyond normal human experience.
  • Catherine’s experience of hallucinations and the fragmentation of the self is vividly portrayed: “It was as if the perpendicular pronoun I had come to see my horizontal mother,” and the sense of multiple selves dancing or dividing. She questions her own reality, the reality of the visitor, and the continuity of identity, wondering if she or the visitor is dead, if she is dreaming, or if the “I” she knows is stable or shifting.
  • A profound metaphor describes Catherine’s soul as a vast, infinite house with “many rooms,” “many stairways,” and “many heavens,” where she moves endlessly without finding an exit. Each room contains different versions of herself, different guests, and missing elements, symbolizing her fractured identity and the elusive nature of self-knowledge. The imagery of “floors wavering like water” and “walls seeming to move” underscores the instability of her mental state.
  • Catherine experiences time as fluid and overlapping, seeing “faces like old town clocks” striking different, “deranged” hours and existing simultaneously in many places: ski hotels, regattas, ancient cities. This dissolves the linear notion of time and blends memories, fantasy, and present experience, emphasizing the theme of timelessness and Catherine’s alienation from ordinary reality.
  • Catherine acknowledges that opium “poisoned all pleasures at their source,” creating a “swirling fog” that distorts reality, moves stars out of their stations, and shifts castles from mountain to mountain. This highlights the role of addiction in her mental fragmentation and the blurring of the rational cosmos she once loved, showing how her perception is both enchanted and trapped by drugs.
  • Catherine’s self-awareness of her madness is striking: she believes her “own sanity was simply this—she knew she was insane.” The novel explores how troubles and madness are cyclical, manifesting repeatedly in different guises, symbolized by dragons turning into saints and vice versa. This passage conveys the tragic and complex nature of mental illness and the futility of self-knowledge in curing it.
  • Catherine interacts with imaginary or symbolic figures, Mr. Spitzer, Lord Murdock Burdock Thrushmorton, Mr. Res Tacamah, who represents parts of her fractured mind or external influences. Despite her isolation, she enacts social rituals, greeting these “guests,” which reflects her remaining attachment to social life and her imaginative engagement with her fragmented reality.
  • The chapter ends with a powerful image of the sea meeting the shore, the dead meeting the living, and the fog as a place for “absent-minded persons” who “walked with their eyes hidden behind their hands.” It suggests a liminal space between life and death, reality and dream, emphasizing Catherine’s existential state of existing in a world of blurred boundaries where neither clear truth nor stable identity exists.
  • The final reflection questions whether reality and dream are distinct or the same, if God too is dreaming, and if reality is a shared hallucination or creation. This philosophical meditation ties Catherine’s personal experience to universal themes about perception, existence, and the meaning of life, ending the chapter on a note of profound ambiguity and mystery.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • Central figure, a woman caught between reality and dreams.
  • A social hostess and recluse, a traveler and prisoner of her mind.
  • Struggles with mental illness, opium addiction, and fragmented identity.

Cousin Hannah Freemount-Snowden

  • A symbolic visitor, representing a heroic figure from a bygone era.
  • Described in mythic terms, she embodies ideals and struggles that contrast with Catherine’s condition.

Mr. Spitzer

  • A character that also imbues symbolic or imaginary concepts like rationality, science, or a caretaker.
  • Envious of Catherine’s imaginative light, yet also a voice of reason.

Lord Murdock Burdock Thrushmorton

  • An eccentric figure who “twittering like a cage of canaries,” blending the absurd with the gothic.
  • Represents the chaotic, unpredictable elements in Catherine’s world.

Mr. Res Tacamah

  • Author of fantasies, controlling madness through dreaming rather than acting.
  • His drug bottle symbolizes Catherine’s opium use and the blurred line between control and chaos.

Research: Themes

Duality of Reality and Imagination

See: Illusion and Reality · Dreams and Imagination · Duality and Doubles

  • Catherine exists simultaneously in multiple realms: physical, mental, spiritual.
  • She is described as living through dreams and hallucinations, often unable to distinguish between real visits and imagined ones (e.g., the visit from Cousin Freemount-Snowden).
  • Her imagination is described as “all there was,” compensating for isolation and loss.

Fragmented Identity and Multiplicity of Selves

See: Identity and the Self

  • Catherine perceives herself as many selves, sometimes contradictory, never unified.
  • She contemplates which self is true or dominant, fearing that choosing one would mean losing the many fascinating false selves that sustain her.
  • This fragmentation extends to her questioning of self-knowledge and sanity, recognizing her madness but finding it integral to her existence.

Timelessness and the Fluidity of Time

See: Time

  • Time is portrayed as elusive, nonlinear, and sometimes meaningless.
  • Catherine experiences multiple times and places at once: simultaneous winters in different ski resorts, many historical eras.
  • The narrative questions the nature of time, blending past, present, and future, life and death.

Isolation and Alienation

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Catherine feels isolated, both physically and psychologically.
  • Despite her social prominence and many guests, she is ultimately alone in her infinite, maze-like mental house.
  • The imagery of empty rooms, phantom guests, and the sea’s roar emphasizes her alienation.

Illness and Madness

See: Madness and Sanity

  • Catherine’s opium use and mental state are central to her experience.
  • She is aware of her insanity and the limitations imposed by it.
  • Madness is depicted as both a torment and a source of her vivid imagination and insight.

Imagery of the Sea and Fog

See: Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos

  • The sea symbolizes the boundary between life and death, reality and dream.
  • Fog and mist represent the unclear, shifting nature of perception and consciousness.
  • The sea’s spray and the fog’s enveloping nature create a liminal space where conventional rules do not apply.

The Role of Memory and History

See: Memory and the Past

  • Catherine recalls historical figures and events with intimate detail, blending fact and fantasy.
  • Her memory is selective, fragmented, and often symbolic.
  • This reflects the human tendency to reconstruct identity and meaning through past narratives.

  • Absolutism vs relativism (democracy) - absolutism asserts universal moral truths, while relativism denies them. The opposite of absolutism in government is democracy (liberality, ease, mercy, relaxation, understanding, laxity, leniency, tolerance). It presents a tension between absolutism and relativism.
