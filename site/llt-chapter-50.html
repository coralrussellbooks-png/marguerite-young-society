Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 50

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh

Pillars: Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The Questions

The relationships Mr. Spitzer seeks are “never to be proved and codified and made certain…forever shifting.” Can a thing be real that will not hold still to be codified?

Faced with two golden chairs, one of which “might collapse if he touched it,” how does he decide where to sit?

He is “a man continually checking, rechecking, altering his impressions which altered of themselves, making little notes.” Is that anxiety, or a way of living once the seals are gone?

“Among dreamers he was the one person who dreamed not.” What does it mean to face the void without building a dream to hide in?

This chapter is where Mr. Spitzer’s arc arrives, and it arrives as Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary lived all the way down. The relationships he seeks are “never to be found…or if found were never to be proved and codified and made certain as a map to the traveller who was yet to come, for they were forever shifting.” He wants to understand, and he refuses to freeze what he understands into a fixed map. The world will not hold still to be codified, and Mr. Spitzer does not force it to; he navigates “by present disorientations” rather than by a settled chart, holding his knowledge open because the thing known keeps moving. This is the open construction as a way of being in the world, not a doctrine about it.

The two golden chairs are the condition of living without a sealed floor. He sees them “in the clouds” and cannot know “in which chair to sit, for one chair might be only the dream, and one might be real, and one might collapse if he touched it.” Catherine, in her sealed dream, would simply sit; the dream would hold because she has forgotten it is a dream. Mr. Spitzer cannot forget, and so he cannot be sure of the chair, and he lives in that uncertainty rather than sealing it away. To not know which construction will bear your weight, and to move among them anyway, is the price and the honesty of the unsealed life.

His endless checking is the practice the book has been building toward since the provisional anchors of the earlier chapters. He is “a man continually checking, rechecking, altering his impressions which altered of themselves, making little notes to mark his journeys back and forth,” pausing before each door to “be sure it did not open on the void, be sure that he was himself and not the hallucination.” This is not anxiety, or not only. It is how one lives once the seals are gone: not by building a fixed dream and moving in, but by keeping small, revisable notes, checking the ground each time because the ground shifts, holding oneself together provisionally. “Caution was thus his only musical note,” and the caution is the discipline of the open.

And the chapter names what sets him against everyone else in the book. “Among dreamers he was the one person who dreamed not, the one person who knew the void of reality as it is given without dreams or substances or material conformations…one who had never lived by dreams himself.” Catherine sealed a dream against the void; Miss MacIntosh sealed a plainness; Cousin Hannah sealed a cause. Mr. Spitzer alone faces the void without a sealed dream to hide in, holding even his own identity open, unsure whether he is the same man or a changed one, checking the door before he knocks. He is the book’s model of the truth that does not fail, the one kept under revision, and the paradox the chapter leaves him in is all of it: “because he took so much for granted, it might seem that he took nothing for granted,” great faith looking like little faith, the most open man the one who trusts the shifting world enough to walk it without a map.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Mr. Spitzer’s uncertainty and endless checking for a breakdown to be pitied or cured, and the reading that takes his refusal to codify the world for a failure to know it. Neither the broken man nor the failed knower is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that a thing can be real and still refuse to be codified, forever shifting, so that the honest knower holds it open rather than freezing it into a map; that living without a sealed floor means not knowing which chair will bear one’s weight and moving among them anyway; and that the continual checking, the little notes, the caution, are not anxiety but the practice of the unsealed life. Above all it is that Mr. Spitzer, alone among the book’s figures, dreams not, facing the void without a sealed dream to hide in, holding even his own self open, which is why he is the book’s nearest thing to the truth that does not fail, the one kept under revision. The open construction is a way of living, not a doctrine, and the most open man is the one who walks the shifting world without a map. Mr. Spitzer pauses before each door, unsure and unsealed, and the chapter asks for nothing more certain than that.

For Reflection

Can something be real and true and still refuse to be pinned into a fixed, certain map?

How does one live among constructions when one cannot be sure which will bear one’s weight?

Is the continual checking and revising of one’s impressions a weakness, or the discipline of living without a seal?
