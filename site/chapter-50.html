Summary

The universe seems off key to Mr. Spitzer: two golden chairs where there should be one, his life a dream dreamed by someone else, himself a blank page between testaments. He considers what he absorbed from Catherine and concludes that "all that happened happened only once." He thinks of Hannah's hyphenated name, Freemount-Snowden, and how the hyphen joining the two names mattered more to her than either name. He writes a nocturne for her. Volume One ends.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with Mr. Spitzer lamenting how "everything seemed off key and out of course," and how the universe "tack[ed] like the wild white snow bird away from central fires of jewels." This passage introduces the core theme of disorientation and the impossibility of fixed maps or certainties in this universe. The imagery of drifting moons, vanished stars, and shifting mirrors sets the tone for a world where nothing is stable or fully knowable.
  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates seeing "two golden chairs in the clouds" and not knowing which is real or if both are illusions. This metaphor reflects his deeper fear of indistinguishable realities and the collapse of boundaries between dream and reality, self and other. His caution about touching the "strings of the constellation Lyre" for fear of interacting with imaginary elements highlights his existential uncertainty.
  • A significant passage is Mr. Spitzer's reflection that he is living his "after-life, his life after death," questioning when he had ever lived before death. He is cautious and uncertain, "continually checking, rechecking" his impressions as he navigates this liminal state. His wildness and caution are balanced by his awareness that he may be the one who "dreamed not," forced to live by the dreams of others, symbolizing a profound detachment from conventional experience and identity.
  • Although Mr. Spitzer spends much time with the opium lady, he did not learn from her the sense of mortality or the failure of the sensorium (his perception). Instead, he also recalls the invalid lady, who lived so immersed in imagination that she blurred the lines between life and death, day and night. Her failure to distinguish these states contrasts with Mr. Spitzer's heightened awareness of the ephemeral nature of existence.
  • Mr. Spitzer recognizes that "all that happened happened only once" and that life is a "moment’s fading luminosity." He mourns the impossibility of going back, the non-recurrence of events, and the uniqueness of every moment. This awareness adds a tragic beauty and pathos to life’s fleeting nature. The metaphor of a train derailed in grass or a star derailed captures his sense of displacement and loss.
  • The chapter explores places that are neither: land nor water, one nor the other, ambiguous or undefined regions. This reflects existential ambiguity and the difficulty of defining states of being, identity, or even reality itself. Mr. Spitzer identifies with these liminal spaces, drifting and lost like sailors or farmers swallowed by the earth.
  • Mr. Spitzer sees himself as a "mere flyleaf stuck between two passages or testaments," not the main text of life or death but a transient, fragile presence. The imagery of silver and gold illuminations shining through him like candle flames evokes a faint spiritual light, but ultimately one that is transient and delicate. He reflects on the absence of any lasting mark or memory of his passage, emphasizing mortality and the erasure of individual existence.
  • Among all the vast and complex imagery, the motif of the "little firefly" stands out as Mr. Spitzer’s "key of gold," his soul, and the light he reads by. This small light represents the essence of life or consciousness amid the vast silence and uncertainty. It suggests that perhaps all that remains or matters is this fragile inner light.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s movement is described as a tide advancing and retreating, embodying hesitation, uncertainty, and the interplay of opposites. This rhythm of contradictory motion captures the existential tension he experiences, the constant questioning and the inability to find firm ground or certainty.
  • Towards the end, Mr. Spitzer recalls lessons from an old suffragette captain whose hyphenated name symbolizes the joining of opposites and the importance of connection ("the hyphen was all"). Her life was solitary, and her death represented a pause in a broken melody, suggesting unresolved narratives and the persistence of longing or unfinished business beyond death.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • Mr. Spitzer is a liminal figure, caught between life and death, reality and dream, certainty and doubt.
  • He exhibits caution born of experience, aware of the fragility of perception and existence, yet he also harbors a "wildness" that contrasts with his cautiousness.
  • His relationship with other characters reveals his search for meaning through others’ dreams and experiences, though he himself "dreamed not".
  • His journey is symbolic of human consciousness confronting mortality and the unknown beyond.

Research: Themes

The Fluidity and Ambiguity of Reality and Identity

See: Illusion and Reality · Identity and the Self

  • Mr. Spitzer experiences a world where "everything seemed off key and out of course" and where familiar structures (rooms, stars, chairs) multiply or dissolve, making it impossible to distinguish reality from dream or hallucination.
  • The doubling of images and uncertainty about which chair to sit on symbolize the instability of identity and the multiplicity of existence. The text questions whether one can trust the senses or even oneself: "one chair might be only the dream, and one might be real".

Death and the Afterlife as Continuums, Not Endings

See: Death and Mortality

  • The text portrays death not as a final cessation but as a passage to another form of existence or consciousness. Mr. Spitzer’s "life after death" is a realm where time, space, and form are mutable and indistinct.
  • There is a tension between the desire for continuity (to find a “way-station” or star where love and identity persist) and the fear of absolute dissolution ("he feared that one should be one, both the dream and the reality").

The Limits of Knowledge and Perception

See: The Search for Meaning

  • Mr. Spitzer is cautious, "continually checking, rechecking, altering his impressions," recognizing that perceptions themselves alter and deceive.
  • The text explores the idea that some beings or states ("cells which knew not death or life") may exist beyond human comprehension.

The Transience and Uniqueness of Life

  • Life is portrayed as a fleeting moment, a "moment’s fading luminosity," never repeating or returning ("there was nothing twice the same").
  • This impermanence imbues life with pathos and beauty, likened to snowflakes melting or fireflies flickering.

Philosophical and Existential Questions

See: The Search for Meaning

  • What is reality if perceptions and identities are unstable or illusory?
  • Is death an end or a transformation?
  • Can one separate the self from the dream or the other?
  • What is the nature of time and experience if moments never repeat and life is a singular event?
  • How does one live in the face of uncertainty and impermanence?

  • The history of hyphenating a woman's name is tied to shifts in social customs, legal frameworks, and evolving gender roles. Initially, hyphenated surnames were primarily associated with the British aristocracy and were a way to preserve family names and inheritances, especially when there were no male heirs. Over time, the practice spread to other social classes and eventually to the United States, where it became a symbol of gender equality and a way for women to maintain their professional identity while acknowledging their marital status. In Britain as part of the aristocratic tradition, hyphenated double surnames were historically used by the aristocracy to combine family fortunes and affiliations through marriage. This practice was particularly relevant when a family had no male heirs to carry on the family name, allowing the name to be passed down through a daughter's marriage. Double-barreled surnames became associated with social status and prestige, reflecting the wealth and connections of the families involved. During the Victorian Era hyphenation gained popularity among the British upper middle class during the Victorian era, moving beyond the aristocracy. In the United States, the tradition of women taking their husband's surname was firmly established, with hyphenation being less common. The American suffragist Lucy Stone, who famously retained her birth name after marriage in 1855, became a symbol of women's rights and the movement to retain maiden names. Formed in the early 20th century, the Lucy Stone League advocated for women to keep their surnames upon marriage. The second-wave feminist movement in the 1960s and 70s challenged traditional gender roles and societal expectations, leading to greater acceptance and adoption of hyphenated surnames. Several states in the US began to make it easier for women to retain their surnames legally, removing barriers like restrictions on passports, bank accounts, and other legal documents. Today, hyphenating a woman's name is often a personal choice, driven by a desire to maintain professional identity, honor family heritage, or express a commitment to gender equality.
