To All My Darlings

Chapter 50

On Chapter 50 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 12, 2026

← Chapter 49 · Home · Chapter 51 →

Summary

Everything seems off key and out of course to Mr. Spitzer, the universe tacking like the wild white snow bird and every room doubling, so that when he sees two golden chairs in the clouds he does not know which is the dream. He steps lightly, breathes softly, and pauses before every door lest it open on the void, for he believes he is living his life after death and may be the one dreamer who dreams not. It was not from the opium lady, “the invalid lady flourishing on the enchantment of nightshade,” who waits for him as for his dead self and mistakes life for death, that he learned that all that happened happened only once. Life is a moment’s fading luminosity, and he is not the Holy Writ but the flyleaf stuck between the testaments, ground between the zenith stone and the nadir stone, leaving no water-mark, no signature, no name. His little firefly is his key of gold and perhaps all there is. He walks like the tide, advancing as he retreats. He learned much from the old suffragette captain, for whom the hyphen in her name was more important than the two names it joined; she died before he came, and for her he wrote this nocturne.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with Mr. Spitzer lamenting how “everything seemed off key and out of course,” and how the universe “tack[ed] like the wild white snow bird away from central fires of jewels.” This passage introduces the core theme of disorientation and the impossibility of fixed maps or certainties in this universe. The imagery of drifting moons, vanished stars, and shifting mirrors sets the tone for a world where nothing is stable or fully knowable.
  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates seeing “two golden chairs in the clouds” and not knowing which is real or if both are illusions. This metaphor reflects his deeper fear of indistinguishable realities and the collapse of boundaries between dream and reality, self and other. His caution about touching the “strings of the constellation Lyre” for fear of interacting with imaginary elements highlights his existential uncertainty.
  • A significant passage is Mr. Spitzer’s reflection that he is living his “after-life, his life after death,” questioning when he had ever lived before death. He is cautious and uncertain, “continually checking, rechecking” his impressions as he navigates this liminal state. His wildness and caution are balanced by his awareness that he may be the one who “dreamed not,” forced to live by the dreams of others, symbolizing a profound detachment from conventional experience and identity.
  • Although Mr. Spitzer spends much time with the opium lady, he did not learn from her the sense of mortality or the failure of the sensorium (his perception). She is “the invalid lady flourishing on the enchantment of nightshade,” who lived so immersed in imagination that she blurred the lines between life and death, day and night. Her failure to distinguish these states contrasts with Mr. Spitzer’s heightened awareness of the ephemeral nature of existence.
  • Mr. Spitzer recognizes that “all that happened happened only once” and that life is a “moment’s fading luminosity.” He mourns the impossibility of going back, the non-recurrence of events, and the uniqueness of every moment. This awareness adds a tragic beauty and pathos to life’s fleeting nature. The metaphor of a train derailed in grass or a star derailed captures his sense of displacement and loss.
  • The chapter explores places that are neither: land nor water, one nor the other, ambiguous or undefined regions. This reflects existential ambiguity and the difficulty of defining states of being, identity, or even reality itself. Mr. Spitzer identifies with these liminal spaces, drifting and lost like sailors or farmers swallowed by the earth.
  • Mr. Spitzer sees himself as a “mere flyleaf stuck between two passages or testaments,” not the main text of life or death but a transient, fragile presence. The imagery of silver and gold illuminations shining through him like candle flames evokes a faint spiritual light, but ultimately one that is transient and delicate. He reflects on the absence of any lasting mark or memory of his passage, emphasizing mortality and the erasure of individual existence.
  • Among all the vast and complex imagery, the motif of the “little firefly” stands out as Mr. Spitzer’s “key of gold,” his soul, and the light he reads by. This small light represents the essence of life or consciousness amid the vast silence and uncertainty. It suggests that perhaps all that remains or matters is this fragile inner light.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s movement is described as a tide advancing and retreating, embodying hesitation, uncertainty, and the interplay of opposites. This rhythm of contradictory motion captures the existential tension he experiences, the constant questioning and the inability to find firm ground or certainty.
  • Towards the end, Mr. Spitzer recalls lessons from an old suffragette captain whose hyphenated name symbolizes the joining of opposites and the importance of connection (“the hyphen was all”). Her life was solitary, and her death represented a pause in a broken melody, suggesting unresolved narratives and the persistence of longing or unfinished business beyond death.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “He was often at a loss when he saw two golden chairs in the clouds to know in which chair to sit.” Why is choosing a chair so dangerous?
  • Mr. Spitzer is “not the Holy Writ but was the mere flyleaf stuck between two passages or testaments.” What does it mean to live between the testaments?
  • “His little firefly was his key of gold.” What is the firefly?

Characters

  • “Perhaps among dreamers he was the one person who dreamed not.” How can someone who dreams not live “by the dreams of others”?
  • The opium lady “waited for him as for his dead self whom she had loved or thought she loved.” Who does she think Mr. Spitzer is?
  • For the old suffragette captain “the hyphen was all.” What does the hyphen join?

Connections

  • In Chapter 49 Mr. Spitzer could not tell whether he was one or two. Here “he feared that one should be one.” Why is being one more frightening?
  • Mr. Spitzer places Hannah “with her head pointing North and her feet pointing South.” How does this echo the poles and reversals of earlier chapters?
  • The chapter closes with a nocturne. How does night music close what came before?

Reflection

  • “All that happened happened only once.” Does this make life more precious or more terrible?
  • “Because he took so much for granted, it might seem that he took nothing for granted.” Is caution a form of faith?
  • “There would be no star of this world in memory of him.” What would you want to leave behind?

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • Mr. Spitzer is a liminal figure, caught between life and death, reality and dream, certainty and doubt.
  • He exhibits caution born of experience, aware of the fragility of perception and existence, yet he also harbors a “wildness” that contrasts with his cautiousness.
  • His relationship with other characters reveals his search for meaning through others’ dreams and experiences, though he himself “dreamed not.”
  • His journey is symbolic of human consciousness confronting mortality and the unknown beyond.

Catherine Cartwheel

  • The opium lady “waited for him as for his dead self whom she had loved or thought she loved,” and “she had always lived by such indirections and evasions that she would never know when he was gone.”

Cousin Hannah

  • The old suffragette captain, “for whom the hyphen was more important than the two names it joined… for the hyphen was all.”
  • “So solitary her life had been. How could death be different or provide any great change for her? Her mind had died before her heart had died.”

Research: Themes

The Fluidity and Ambiguity of Reality and Identity

See: Illusion and Reality · Identity and the Self

  • Mr. Spitzer experiences a world where “everything seemed off key and out of course” and where familiar structures (rooms, stars, chairs) multiply or dissolve, making it impossible to distinguish reality from dream or hallucination.
  • The doubling of images and uncertainty about which chair to sit on symbolize the instability of identity and the multiplicity of existence. The text questions whether one can trust the senses or even oneself: “one chair might be only the dream, and one might be real.”

Death and the Afterlife as Continuums, Not Endings

See: Death and Mortality

  • The text portrays death not as a final cessation but as a passage to another form of existence or consciousness. Mr. Spitzer’s “life after death” is a realm where time, space, and form are mutable and indistinct.
  • There is a tension between the desire for continuity (to find a “way-station” or star where love and identity persist) and the fear of absolute dissolution (“he feared that one should be one, both the dream and the reality”).

The Limits of Knowledge and Perception

See: The Search for Meaning

  • Mr. Spitzer is cautious, “continually checking, rechecking, altering his impressions,” recognizing that perceptions themselves alter and deceive.
  • The text explores the idea that some beings or states (“cells which knew not death or life”) may exist beyond human comprehension.

The Transience and Uniqueness of Life

  • Life is portrayed as a fleeting moment, a “moment’s fading luminosity,” never repeating or returning (“there was nothing twice the same”).
  • This impermanence imbues life with pathos and beauty, likened to snowflakes melting or fireflies flickering.

Philosophical and Existential Questions

See: The Search for Meaning

  • What is reality if perceptions and identities are unstable or illusory?
  • Is death an end or a transformation?
  • Can one separate the self from the dream or the other?
  • What is the nature of time and experience if moments never repeat and life is a singular event?
  • How does one live in the face of uncertainty and impermanence?

  • “Should a demoniac butterfly appear”: A demoniac butterfly may refer to a restricted demon or grass demon butterfly found in Asia. The Greek psyche means both soul and butterfly, and the butterfly rising from the fire is the soul leaving the body. See Butterflies and Moths and Greek Mythology.

  • “never again would he leave his calling card”: Victorian callers left engraved cards on a silver tray in the hall; a card with a corner turned down meant one had come in person.

  • “she saw the human-sized blackbird at the door”: See the human-sized blackbird of Weissenbach’s dreams in the Chapter 33 resources. See Catherine Cartwheel.

The poles wander by a few meters over a few years, partly from the Chandler wobble, a free oscillation with a period of about 433 days, but for maps they are taken as fixed, the points where the lines of longitude meet.   • “stuck between the millstone above him and the millstone below him”: “No man shall take the nether or the upper millstone to pledge: for he taketh a man’s life to pledge” (Deuteronomy 24:6), and “Though the mills of God grind slowly, yet they grind exceeding small” (Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, “Retribution,” 1846).   • “fleets of nautiluses turning their sails”: “This is the ship of pearl, which, poets feign, / Sails the unshadowed main” (Oliver Wendell Holmes, “The Chambered Nautilus,” 1858), the shell built “chamber after chamber,” as Young writes here. See American Writers.
  • “no minaret which had written him spiral after spiral”: The spiral minaret of the Great Mosque of Samarra in Iraq, the Malwiya, built between 848 and 852, winds upward in a single ramp.
  • “no water-mark, no signature, no name”: Keats asked that his grave bear no name, only “Here lies One Whose Name was writ in Water” (Protestant Cemetery, Rome, 1821). See Romantic Poets.
  • “His little firefly was his key of gold”: See the firefly lamplighter in the Chapter 48 resources. See Fireflies, Lamps, Candles, and Light, and Keys, Doors, and Locks.
  • “the great huntsman passing at the far zenith of the glassy sky”: Orion hunts with his two dogs, Canis Major and Canis Minor; see the frozen constellations in the Chapter 49 resources. See Astronomy and Greek Mythology.
  • “those gleaming cells which knew not death or life”: Mr. Spitzer imagines the smallest units of life existing beyond the human definitions of living and dead, not knowing or aware of them. “Perhaps no messages had ever arrived”: they may be cut off from any experience that would mark them as alive or dead in a conscious sense. Life and death may be limited concepts, and existence at a deeper level may transcend them.
    • There is an actual case. Henrietta Lacks, a Black woman treated for cervical cancer at Johns Hopkins in 1951, had cells taken without her knowledge or consent; they became HeLa, the first immortal human cell line and an important source of medical data. Neither she nor her family was compensated, and the family did not learn of the cell line until the 1970s (Rebecca Skloot, The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks, 2010). Its use for medical research and for commercial purposes continues to raise concerns about privacy and patients’ rights; in 2023 her estate settled a lawsuit against a company that had sold the cells. See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “like the scarlet pimpernels folding at the first approach of rain”: The scarlet pimpernel closes its flowers when the sky clouds over, so it was called the poor man’s weatherglass. Baroness Orczy took it for the sign of her hidden hero in The Scarlet Pimpernel (1905).
  • “sea urchins who knew not whether they lived or were dead”: The sea urchins stand for creatures caught between life, death, and sleep, uncertain of their own condition, and for the mutual ignorance of all beings: even if the urchins know their state, humans cannot know it, nor can the urchins know what humans understand. Much of existence is enveloped in layers of unknowing; “Perhaps all human beings were also mystified, even as Mr. Spitzer was.”
    • It is hard to tell whether a sea urchin is dead or alive, and the red sea urchin may live 200 years. Scientists now hold that humans are not the only beings with the neurological substrates that generate consciousness: “Non-human animals, including all mammals and birds, and many other creatures, including octopuses, also possess these neurological substrates” (The Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness, 2012).
    • Dualism was defended by Descartes who believed that the mind and body were separate and only humans had a mind. Many modern philosophers maintain that the mind is not separate from the body. See Descartes and Dualism.
  • “perhaps at that very time she had lost her love”: The phrase conveys Cousin Hannah’s experience of losing the actual love or connection she once had, yet still being defined or constrained by the formal tie that remains: a poignant reflection on the nature of identity, love, and loss in the story. See Cousin Hannah.
    • Hyphenated double surnames began among the British aristocracy as a way to keep a family name and inheritance alive when there was no male heir, and spread to the Victorian upper middle class. In the United States wives took their husbands’ names; the suffragist Lucy Stone kept her own at her marriage in 1855, and the Lucy Stone League, founded by Ruth Hale in 1921, urged women to do the same. Second-wave feminists of the 1960s and 1970s made the hyphenated name a common choice, and states removed barriers to keeping a birth name on passports, bank accounts, and other documents. For the old suffragette captain “the hyphen was all.” (Wikipedia) See Women’s Suffrage.
  • “without paying a toll”: See Charon’s fare in the Chapter 46 resources. See Greek Mythology.
  • “So he had written this nocturne”: The Irish composer John Field published the first nocturnes in 1812, and Chopin made the form his own. The chapter closes on this night music. See Music and Composers.

False.
Staggy
Like a stag; rangy and long-legged.
Starboard
The right-hand side of a ship, facing forward.
Suffragette
A woman who campaigned for women’s right to vote. The word was coined by a London newspaper in 1906 for militant British activists. Most American activists called themselves suffragists. See Women’s Suffrage.
Surrey
A light four-wheeled horse-drawn carriage with two seats, often with a fringed canopy on top.
Translucence
The quality of letting light pass through without being clear.
Transpired
Happened; took place.
Turret
A small tower on a building or castle.
Zenith
The highest point.
