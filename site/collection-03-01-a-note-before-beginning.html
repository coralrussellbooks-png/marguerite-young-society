A Note Before Beginning

← The Crossing Point: The Eleventh Pillar of Radical Immanence · Radical Immanence Collection · Part One: The Vertical →

The ten pillars of Radical Immanence were built to hold a specific weight: the weight of the plural self, moving laterally through a world of constructed realities and permanent grief, witnessing without concluding, building recursively without arriving. They hold that weight. They were built honestly and they stand.

This book is not a revision of those pillars. It is the discovery of the axis they were built around.

Every pillar stands vertical. Every pillar holds a horizontal load. The architecture of the ten pillars is already, in its form, a crossing point: the vertical supports bearing the horizontal weight of what the system carries. What has not yet been named is the intersection itself. The place where the two axes meet. The place where you are already standing.

That place is the eleventh pillar. It is not added to the ten. It is found inside them, at the center of the architecture, where the weight crosses.

It has been there the whole time.

Prologue: Standing Somewhere

There is a field in central Indiana, where the soil is a particular color of red-brown that geologists call oxidized glacial till. The glaciers that made it retreated twelve thousand years ago. They left behind a layer of compressed material: rock flour and clay and the ground-up remnants of everything the ice had carried from somewhere else. The field does not remember the glaciers. It is the glaciers, transformed. It is also the prairie grass that grew in the glacial till for ten thousand years before the plows came. It is also the plows. It is also the people who held the plows and the people who were displaced so the plows could be held and the people who will stand in this field after every person alive today is in the sediment.

Stand in that field for long enough and you will feel something that has no name in standard philosophical vocabulary. It is not nostalgia, which is a longing for a past that was yours. It is not awe, which is a response to scale. It is something more structural than either: the specific vertigo of being simultaneously a recipient and a carrier, of feeling the weight of what made you possible pressing up through the soles of your feet while the weight of what you will make possible presses down from somewhere above your head, from a future that does not yet exist and is already, in this moment, being shaped by whether you are paying attention.

Marguerite Young felt this. She built 1,198 pages trying to hold it. The pages hold the horizontal: the lateral spread of grief and illusion and the plural self moving through a world that does not resolve. What they are reaching toward, in every long sentence that circles back and refuses to finish, is the vertical: the line that runs through the crossing point from the ancestors in the sediment to the not-yet-born in the not-yet-formed ground.

This book names that line. It names the crossing point where the line meets the horizontal spread of the ten pillars. It names the eleventh pillar, which is not a pillar in the architectural sense at all. It is the axis the architecture was always rotating around.

You are not a self moving through time.

You are the place where time crosses itself.

Here is what that means, and what it requires, and why it is enough.
