Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 80

← Chapter 79 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 81 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Esther Longtree · Walter

Walter is the one child who lived, and his living is this chapter's trouble. Esther bore him to a black father, a white woman's child across the line the town will not let her cross, and the personal tragedy of his birth and his "reproach" folds intersecting erasures of race and gender into a single figure, a son who accuses his mother and a mother who loves and grieves him at once. She tends him "in another country," and the distance is the emotional labor of a motherhood the world will not sanction, care performed across a separation the arrangement forces on her.

Esther is the mediator between the living and the dead, a "wet-nurse to the dead babies," and her perpetual pregnancy that flowers into loss is the interrupted birth held against the one child who survived it. The dead infants she nurses and the living son who accuses her occupy the same maternal ground, the dissolving boundary between life and death worked through a woman who mothers the vanished and the present in the same breath, kept at the threshold with all the book's dead.

Her paradox, that she "killed a child" and yet "the child lived," is the illusion of the individual at its most disturbing, a son who is both the murdered and the surviving, both accuser and beloved, and the child's voice as her conscience is the internalized weight of a motherhood judged by everyone. The lost inheritance, the old farm "erased," its fences changed until she cannot recognize them, is the failure of the record to hold a woman's lineage, her heritage and her reproductive history alike rubbed out.

Walter's conception, Esther "taken in the summer darkness," holds an ambiguity of consent the book refuses to resolve, vulnerability and survival and desire folded together in the dark. Her love endures past the town's rejection, and her ironic laughter about her child's race reclaims a complex, crossing motherhood the world would shame, whiteness and blackness held in one woman's arms. The chapter closes on her "everlasting pregnancy," the resilience of a body that goes on creating and losing and loving at the margin, a woman the arrangement cannot erase however hard it tries to unrecord her farm, her son, and her name.
