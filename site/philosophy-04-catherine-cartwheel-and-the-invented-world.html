Chapter 4. Catherine Cartwheel and the Invented World

See also: Catherine Cartwheel

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Eustace · Joachim Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Philosophy: Empiricism (Locke, Berkeley, Hume)

Allusions: Astronomy · The History of Opium

Chapters discussed: 14, 24, 27, 30, 82

Definition from research

The Promise

Catherine Cartwheel, Vera’s mother, lives in an opium dream in a great house by the sea, and she has built there the most complete system in the novel. Its promise is freedom. In her world nothing is fixed by fact, and so nothing can wound her by being true. The narrator states the principle plainly: “it was this lack of faith which made all things possible to her” (ch. 27). Where a believer is bound to what she believes, Catherine is bound to nothing. She can populate her rooms with anyone from any century.

Her system also promises knowledge. She has, the novel says, “a third eye in her forehead, the eye of her omniscience” (ch. 14). She knows the comings and goings of her guests, the moods of the dead, the thoughts of people she has never met. And the system promises company. Her favorite companion, Eustace, is invisible, and so he can be anything. “Sometimes he was a mocking bird. Sometimes he was an old woman, and sometimes he was an old man bearded like the cypress,” and “he was not confined to any one form by the laws of nature, which had been suspended in his case” (ch. 30).

This is idealism lived all the way through: the view, found in different forms from Berkeley onward, that what exists is what is perceived by a mind. Catherine goes further than any philosopher dared. She is the mind, and the world is what she perceives.

The Loading

Young does not argue against Catherine’s system. She fills it. The house becomes “crowded to the roof” (ch. 24). The guests sleep “tier above tier.” The P’s crowd upon her, planets and petunias and Prussians. Eustace multiplies into “many persons, varying according to her whims” (ch. 30). The more she invents, the more there is to hold, and the house of pure mind becomes a house of pure clutter.

Time loads the system too. Catherine telephones Cousin Hannah for years after Hannah’s death, the telephone “ringing and ringing through empty rooms” of a Boston mansion long since torn down for a highway (ch. 30). The invented world has no way to register loss, and so loss accumulates in it unseen, like water behind a wall.

The Break

The break comes from two directions. The first is a real person. In the fourteenth chapter, among all the imaginary guests, “Only Mr. Spitzer aroused, as she did not like to admit, her feelings of hostile doubt, perhaps because he was flesh and blood and real, making her feel that there was that about him which exceeded her comprehension and might be vastly different from anything either she or God had dreamed.” Idealism cannot hold a real other. The one thing Catherine’s omniscient eye cannot see is a living man who sits beside her bed every day. The narrator notes elsewhere that “The real persons were apparently the only persons who were unreal to my mother” (ch. 30). The system inverts reality so thoroughly that it can no longer find the people who love her.

The second break is sorrow. Catherine has arranged her life so that nothing can make her grieve, and the cost of that arrangement finally comes due. In the forty-first chapter, the narrator says Catherine “had kept herself, at all costs, vastly amused, sacrificing her life to her amusements, just as she remarked when she thought of the infinite sorrows she had missed.” Then comes one of the most important sentences in the novel: “The lack of sorrow was a sorrow, too.” The system promised escape from grief. Lived long enough, it produces a new grief, the grief of a life that has not been felt. In the same passage Catherine admits she “could not tell whether a thing had been a star or a firefly,” and the narrator adds, “Perhaps my mother had never really known anyone.”

At the end of the novel, the invented world is literally destroyed. Vera reports “that the opium paradise had crumbled, that the sea had swept over the dark house with its golden turrets and towers” (ch. 82). But before the flood, something else happens. Catherine, “after many years of sleeping, arose from her bed and walked, not quite sleeping, awake when she died” (ch. 82). The dreamer wakes at the very end. The system that denied death gives way in the one moment that cannot be invented.

What Is Left

Catherine is not simply refuted. The novel says early and plainly that “There was always some right in what my mother said, even when she was wrong” (ch. 27). What was right was her discovery that the world is far more invented than it looks, and that people live inside what they and others have made up. What was wrong was her belief that invention could protect her from what is not made up: another person’s reality, and her own sorrow. Her daughter will inherit both halves of that lesson. Vera never forgets that the world is dreamed. But she will spend the novel searching for the one person she believed was real.
