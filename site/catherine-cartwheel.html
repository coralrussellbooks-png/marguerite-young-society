Chapter 2

Listed as: Catherine Helena Cartwheel (Snowden)

  • A central figure who has withdrawn from life into a dreamlike, opium-fueled existence.
  • She suffers from an invisible birthmark (a blot on her left cheek), which symbolizes hidden flaws and may represent her motivation for retreating from reality.
  • She is portrayed as a "beautiful corpse," eternally young yet fixed in a horizontal, motionless state.
  • Surrounds herself with phantoms, dreams, and visions including historical figures, spirits, and imaginary visitors.
  • She is complexly caught between sanity and madness, reality and fantasy, embodying a state of suspended animation.
  • Her retreat to bed and isolation is linked to personal trauma: an unhappy marriage (Jock Cartwheel), abandonment, social humiliation, and a controlling father.

Chapter 13

  • The recurring motif of the "dead ear," especially in relation to Mr. Spitzer and Catherine, underscores a world where communication fails and the essence of existence is isolated from meaning.
  • Catherine’s insistence that "the ear was dead" and that "there was not a sound to be heard" suggests a bleak vision of human experience as disembodied from relational and sensory engagement.
  • Catherine is depicted as a woman caught between life and a kind of suspended existence.
  • Catherine is enveloped in a self-imposed or circumstantial state of near-death or withdrawal from reality, an enchantment that protects her but also alienates her from the world around her.
  • The “garden of the deaf” is a powerful metaphor for Catherine’s mental and emotional state.
  • This deafness is not just literal but symbolic of a disconnection from the external world and from meaningful human interaction.
  • In this garden, "one should see the bird and the shadow of the bird, but one should not hear the song," encapsulating Catherine’s experience of perceiving the world without fully engaging with it or being able to communicate effectively.
  • Her interactions with Mr. Spitzer, an old friend and legal professional, further reveal her state of mind.
  • Despite his efforts to speak and argue, Catherine insists that the "ear was dead," and that no sound truly reaches them. This exchange highlights her detachment and the barrier she has erected between herself and others, emphasizing her internal silence and the futility of communication.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s "subdued roaring" in his ear, a noise which "did not come from this world," parallels Catherine’s own internal condition, suggesting shared but differently manifested forms of alienation.
  • Catherine’s fear of the silence "unbroken by the sound" and her particular dread "when a frog screamed" indicate deep psychological distress. The silence is oppressive, a void that she cannot endure, and the rare disruptive sounds become sources of terror rather than comfort. This paradox of fearing both silence and sudden noise points to a troubled mind caught between extremes, fragile and haunted.
  • Throughout the chapter, images of decaying flowers, fading colors, and ghostly sounds surround Catherine, reinforcing the themes of impermanence and dissolution.
  • She is part of a world slipping away from life into something spectral or unreal. This imagery parallels her mental state which is caught in a liminal space between reality and illusion, life and death.
  • Despite her distance, Catherine remains a focal figure in Vera’s consciousness. Her presence is felt strongly even as she withdraws, indicating Catherine’s paradoxical role as both a source of life and a symbol of loss and mystery.

Chapter 14

Listed as: Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

Chapter 32

Listed as: Catherine (Vera’s mother)

  • Catherine is described as bedridden, veiled in white lace and surrounded by an atmosphere of drifting snowflakes, candle flames, and reflections. She is physically confined and vulnerable, a "creature of such fragility" who is easily overwhelmed by the presence of Cousin Hannah and the harshness of reality. Her bed is a "floating swan bed in a dazzling cloud," which evokes both fragility and an ethereal, almost otherworldly quality. This imagery underscores her physical and emotional weakness.
  • Catherine’s essential loneliness and isolation is emphasized. She is abandoned by the "mysterious bridegroom” and all her loves are imaginary: pharaohs, captains, and other ethereal figures who "had left her, and they had never been". This abandonment extends to emotional desolation, as no one, "not even Cousin Hannah," has ever truly touched her "cold heart," which is "cold and hard as the mountain rock". This coldness suggests an emotional deadness or protective numbness born from pain.
  • Catherine lives largely in a world of dreams, illusions, and shifting realities. She imagines weddings to phantoms, sailboats, stars, and icebergs, reflecting a detachment from conventional life and relationships. Her marriage is portrayed as an ambiguous event, possibly symbolic or imaginary, rather than a concrete historical fact. The wedding imagery ("blushing behind her billowing wedding veils," "married to a phantom") conveys a sense of being caught between life and death, reality and fantasy.
  • Catherine's mental state is described as "changeful and capricious," with a wandering attention and vague memories. She is inconsistent in her accounts and perceptions, which may be interpreted as a symptom of her illness, age, or psychological state. This instability contrasts strongly with Cousin Hannah’s forceful and direct manner, highlighting Catherine’s passivity and internal turmoil.
  • Catherine’s relationship with Cousin Hannah reveals much about her character. Hannah is vigorous, militant, and confrontational, while Catherine simulates emotions she does not feel and "dissembles her feelings" when Hannah visits. Catherine’s indifference masks inner panic and vulnerability. Whereas Hannah is a symbol of active struggle and outward battle, Catherine is an embodiment of internal retreat and quiet suffering. Yet, Catherine admires Hannah’s energy and persistence, even as she is overshadowed by it.
  • Catherine is associated with death-like sleep and a liminal state between life and death. The novel suggests that she is "already dead" in a metaphorical sense, living in a "pretense of death". Her life is marked by a profound stasis, and she may be seen as a "sleeping lady" awaiting awakening or final rest. The imagery of snow, cold, and fading light reinforces this theme of suspended animation and the passage between worlds.
  • Catherine’s marriage and attitude toward men are ambiguous. She married "that great traveller who never would return," possibly to defy Cousin Hannah’s militant feminism or to assert independence, but the marriage was doomed. Her symbolic wedding to various natural and mystical entities rather than a man suggests rejection or transcendence of traditional romantic or marital relationships.
  • Catherine is depicted with a strong aesthetic sensibility: her fingers wear jewels that "gleam like northern lights," and her surroundings are rich in poetic symbolism (flutes, harps, snowflakes, candle flames). She is a "beautiful femme fatale," but her beauty is cold and distant, more a vision than a presence. This artistic and ethereal quality adds to her complexity as a character who inhabits a realm between reality and dream.

Chapter 66

  • She is described as being "pent up in bed" and dreaming in an "over-heated room". This suggests she is physically unwell and possibly confined to her bed for an extended period.
  • Vera mentions that her mother sometimes dreams "opium dreams" and entertains "too many hallucinations" for her health's sake. This implies a detachment from reality and a possible reliance on drugs or other means to escape her physical condition.
  • Vera notes that her mother has strange dreams where she never recognizes living men, only the dead ones. This indicates a preoccupation with the past and a possible inability to connect with the present.
  • Miss MacIntosh criticizes her mother for lacking common sense, specifically citing the gift of an Eskimo cookbook as an example. This suggests a disconnect between the mother's actions and practical reality.

  • Vera wonders if her mother always knew Miss MacIntosh was bald, despite it being a secret. If true, this could indicate a deeper understanding or perception than she lets on.

Chapter 70

  • Catherine, Vera's mother, is a profoundly eccentric, wealthy invalid whose grasp on reality is tenuous and fragmented. Her character is defined by a series of contradictions, delusions, and a self-centered, often manipulative, approach to those around her.
  • Initially, Vera establishes Catherine as an unconventional figure, noting that Miss MacIntosh, Vera's former nursemaid, raised Vera to understand that her "mother’s way of life was far from average and was not to be imitated by a good woman". Catherine is also revealed as the "true subject of [Vera's] dreams," indicating her deep, almost obsessive, significance to Vera.
  • Catherine's disconnection from reality is a dominant trait. She "dreamed that she was poor," contrasting with Lisa Lunde's mother who "dreamed that she was rich". Her physical and mental state is described as one of profound invalidism, often sinking into "deeper and deeper coma" and experiencing hallucinations, such as "a huge toad was eating my mother’s face" or "the balloon fish floating over my mother’s head", and "shadowy figures stepped out of the walls. There were moths with human faces". She herself admits to having "lost my mind several times" and declares, "I am not responsible for my actions". Her identity is fluid; she believes she has "killed her former self and that my mother’s former self was walking through the house while my mother lay with her hair streaked out like twilight on a pillow, for my mother was dead—added, subtracted, multiplied, divided".
  • Despite her profound mental disarray, Catherine exhibits moments of surprising shrewdness and practicality, particularly concerning financial matters. She can display a "shrewd gleam in her eyes, the absolute efficiency with which she inquired into her financial concerns, past and present," even seeking to collect "Old loans which she had forgotten making" from dead parties. Her instruction to meticulously inspect the house for missing items after Mrs. Hogden's departure further underscores this trait.
  • Catherine's interactions with Mrs. Hogden, her newly arrived companion, highlight her manipulative and self-centered nature. Initially, she feigns "profound, glazed astonishment," denying any knowledge of Mrs. Hogden or having sent an invitation, calling her an "impostor, a liar, a day-dreamer, a thief". Despite Mrs. Hogden possessing intimate details of Catherine's life like "the head of Alcibiades was in the drainpipe" or "a whale swimming into my mother’s bedroom" which only Catherine could have divulged, Catherine still refuses to see her. She employs elaborate excuses, claiming to be "gone to Boston to see a podiatrist" or "to the North Pole". She blames Lisa Lunde for Mrs. Hogden's presence and attempts to discourage Mrs. Hogden by assigning her an uncomfortable "deserted playroom" and a "hospital cot" that was "too short and too narrow". Her ultimate directive to Mr. Spitzer to "push her off a high building or throw her into the ocean" to get rid of Mrs. Hogden reveals a cruel and extreme impulse.
  • Catherine's desire for an arithmetic teacher who would teach "Two and two always making four...over and over again" suggests a yearning for simple, undeniable truths in a chaotic personal reality. However, she rejects Mrs. Hogden's attempts to teach, deeming her "mad as a loon" for repeating the lesson. She views Mrs. Hogden as a "rival invalid" and a "gross, stinking parody" of her own "distended world of shimmering, loosely woven textures", indicating a fierce protectiveness over her unique inner world.
  • Catherine's memory and awareness fluctuate. After Mrs. Hogden's departure, she is "disappointed now that she had not been taught arithmetic and blamed Lisa Lunde", even considering hiring another "lonely old woman" to teach her. This cyclical nature underscores her persistent detachment from the consequences of her actions and her inability to learn from past experiences.

Chapter 71

  • Catherine is a figure deeply immersed in a subjective, dream-like reality, detached from the conventional world. Her character is defined by her peculiar philosophies, eccentric habits, and a luxurious but illusory existence that profoundly shapes Vera's own journey and desires.
  • Catherine is often referred to by Vera as "my sleeping mother", suggesting a state of perpetual dreaming or detachment from waking life. Her physical presence is limited; she "had not walked for many years because of the birthmark staining her cheek and because of her fear of the wavering floor, the sea sweeping in, and her dead past". This immobility further anchors her to her internal world, within her great, "sea-blackened house with gold towers and cornices", which, despite its grandeur, is described as being "gone", implying its existence is more in memory or illusion than current reality.
  • Her unique philosophical outlook is evident in her beliefs about her "real pearls," which, she claimed, "could not be lost or stolen or sold. Whatever the human, unhuman situation might be, those real pearls could not really be lost, and I should wear them, for they were her lost estate". She believed that losing pearls paradoxically led to their multiplication, creating "infinities of pearls," a concept she explained as "just simple arithmetic, simple addition". This belief system defies logic, suggesting a mind that operates on its own fantastical rules. She also held a profound view of existence, stating that it was "probably not... eternity, something everlasting, this being so temporary a world, the world already gone. Only the shadow... ever does come here".
  • Catherine's eccentricity is vividly illustrated through her interaction with a thief who broke into her house one winter night. Far from being alarmed, she "smilingly greeted him, extending her ivory-colored hand, asking him how far he had come, and was the world truly dead, and had offered him drugged sherry". She engaged him in a languorous conversation, "sympathizing with her that she was rich, just as she had sympathized with him that he was poor". In a grand, yet impractical, gesture, she offered to "finance a Revolution" with a blank check, contingent on the thief naming "an exact sum needed and an exact date," which, caught in her "shimmering enchantment," he could not do. Her generosity extended to outfitting the thief in an aristocratic gentleman's costume, including a "high silk opera hat," "long white motor coat," and "white flowers behind his ears," symbolizing the "coming Revolution" and making him "the melting snowman upon the first day of spring". This act demonstrates her theatricality and her desire to transform reality according to her own grand, symbolic visions.
  • Vera carries the tangible and intangible legacy of her mother. She wears Catherine's "black velvet which had been her abandoned shroud," her "lace collar and the lace cuffs," and the "necklace of pearls". These items are not just mementos but symbols of her mother's unique existence and Vera's connection to that past. However, Vera's own desire for "something quite plain and poor and bare" and her search for "order, as if it were a beautiful island in the midst of life" suggest a yearning for a reality distinct from her mother's opulent, yet ultimately vanished, dream-world. Catherine Cartwheel, therefore, represents a captivating, almost mythical past for Vera, a world of intricate illusions and profound detachment, which Vera both inherits and seeks to transcend.
