Esther Longtree: The Grief That Would Not Deliver

← Lisa Lunde · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Rosemary Longtree →

Characters: Esther Longtree · Mr. Weed · Mrs. Hogden · Vera Cartwheel

She is the waitress in the desolate Indiana town where Vera comes to rest in the first cold of spring, in a lunchroom called the Greasy Spoon whose window is dressed with "a large spoon, a small coffin, and pots of elephant ears." The place smells "more like a grave than a restaurant," and "a grave, it surely was," because Esther Longtree carries her dead with her wherever she goes, and she has a great many. She is the town's one perpetual event, "known for her everlasting pregnancy and her loving heart which knew no lover and her everlasting loneliness." She is always about to give birth, this month and every month, and she never does, or rather she always does and it is always nothing, and she begins again. She is the sentence in this book that will not end, walking on a deformed foot behind a long counter, immense, swelling, braying softly to the child inside her, forever.

She is unforgettably alive, which is the first one of the novel. In a room full of "pale, wavering figures like ghosts of customers," "who could have been more robustly alive than she," with "vitality breathed in her," her black hair loose and free, her cupid's-bow mouth smiling "from jug ear to jug ear," slapping the blood back into her own red hands. And she is set against the grey mule standing in harness outside, "that being from which no mortal issue comes," stopped "at the dead-end of creation." That is her, and the book means it exactly. The most fiercely living woman in the town is the one who brings nothing to term, who conceives everlastingly and delivers only "an eye, an ear, a long gold hair, a fingernail, a cobweb, nothing." Her aliveness and her barrenness are the same fact seen twice.

She is grief made into a body. The philosophy this book keeps testing says grief does not process and resolve, that "it accumulates," that "you carry it," and Esther Longtree is that sentence taken past metaphor into flesh. Every loss adds to the last. She never empties out. Her children are "as many as the autumn leaves where no spring ever was, the snowflakes," and she has "dropped them," she says, "everywhere," under the leaves and the snow and in the rivers, and she is the only one who ever mourned them: "none had any funerals or mourners but me." So she still carries them. They come back to tease her and ask who their father was. She keeps their toys, buries their layettes in long white boxes under the persimmon and the peach, and knits a little black suit for the one who will live. She is everlastingly pregnant because she has never once been allowed to finish. The body that will not deliver is the grief that will not close.

And she is the sole witness of the unmade. This is where she stops being only pitiable and becomes something the book honors. The figures who fail in its moral world are the ones who look away, who will not stay in the room with what is actually there. Esther Longtree never looks away from a single loss. She keeps vigil over what never quite came into being, the unformed thing with "no ears and no eyes," and she will not let it go unmourned. When Vera, who came here as "the mourner," orders "seven cups" of coffee, Esther sets out seven white cups "for seven skulls but no coffee for them, the little bird bones." She is the one who counts the uncounted, who lays a place for what has no place, who says to the stillborn what no one said to them. In a book terrified of the imagined thing that leaves a real body, she is the woman who gathers every such body up and carries it and calls it hers.

The cost is that she can no longer find the seam between herself and what she has lost. Her own voice comes back to her as a child's crying for water, "her own voice interrupting her voice." She nurses her clenched right fist "as if her right hand were a new-born baby's head." She keeps lovebirds so their noise will cover the crying only she can hear. The old doctor tells her there is "no form within the form," that "it was a cloud," that she is "the immortal medical case and baffling," and that "there is something about all of us that is unrealized." She is the marginal figure the book keeps pointing to, the one who fits no category and so reveals that the categories were built. There is "nothing about her case in the law books," no name for a woman who is mother and barren at once, pregnant with the snowflakes, "brilliant but dumb." When she imagines the jury of townswomen who will accuse her, the book notes that they are "her pale reflection." She frightens them because she carries in the open what they carry hidden. The worst thing they could say, the one accusation she "could not bear," is not that she killed her children but "that her children were never born and were never conceived." The novel refuses to settle it. She has borne "as many as the stars in the sky," and she has borne no one, and both are held at once, without one being allowed to win.

This is why her one refrain is a question with no floor under it. "Who killed cock robin?" she asks, and answers, "Nobody... All people killed him," he was "killed by the death that was in him from the time of his birth." She has understood the thing the whole book has been circling, that there is "something stillborn in every stream, in every flood, in every rose," that no one is "guiltless of this guilt," that every birth already carries its death. She universalizes the stillborn the way Mrs. Hogden universalized the private dream, and it makes her, too, unbearable, because she is right.

The turn for a writer is the tenderest and hardest in the set, because Esther Longtree is the maker who cannot keep what she makes. Every writer knows her as the thing carried for months that comes out unformed, the book that will not come to term, the figure conceived with the whole heart that arrives as "an eye, an ear, a long gold hair, a cobweb, nothing." She is the grief of everything that did not make it, buried and mourned and never let go, and she is the stubbornness that conceives again the next morning. She keeps the vigil no one else keeps, for the unwritten, the abandoned, the stillborn draft, and she alone lays a place for them. Late in her chapters she has passed into the purest form of the work, "knitting without needles or thread," no longer unraveling anything, making for the sake of making with the product long since given up. Set her beside Mr. Weed, who reduces the living to weights, and beside Mrs. Hogden, who dreams other people's lives out loud, and Esther is the third face of the same craft, the one who brings forth and loses and brings forth again and carries every loss in her body as bulk. She is the book's own self-portrait, a thing everlastingly pregnant, a sentence that will not end, and the novel does not rescue her, does not deliver her, does not let cock robin be born. It lets her stand in the beam of yellow light with her hand on her heart, having hope. The sentence hasn't ended. The last sentence of the novel belongs to her and it is that she will go down again and then, after a spell, she will be back.
