Chapter 11

← Chapter 10 · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 12 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Pillars: Grief Doesn't End

The Questions

Catherine’s universe “admitted no theory of loss.” What does a world built to keep loss out have to seal shut?

Vera can tell dream from reality; her mother cannot. Is the lesson that reality wins, or that the sealed dream cannot grieve?

When Raleigh says “God is nothing but an idea in the mind,” what is he doing to the last thing most people take for solid?

Is imagination Catherine’s refuge, or her prison?

Catherine’s world is built to keep loss out. Her “mirage-like calculations…admitted no theory of loss in the universe where all things were lost.” The line is exact and devastating: everything is lost, and her invention is the one place that refuses to know it. This is the sealed house given its cosmology. What the invention seals out is grief. To admit loss would be to let the weather in, and the architecture of her dream exists to keep the weather out.

Which means her sealed invention is a failure of Grief Doesn't End before it is anything else. The parade of imaginary callers, Pericles and Penelope and the penguins and the cold snobbish mountains, is company assembled so that no one need ever be missing. A world where no one is lost is a world where no one is grieved, and Catherine has chosen it precisely to be spared the grief. The book has shown this walling before; here it names the thing walled out.

Vera sets the counter-position, and it is quieter than it looks. She can tell dream from reality where her mother cannot, and it is tempting to read this as reality winning over illusion. But the real difference is not that Vera prefers facts. It is that her philosophy acknowledges and accepts loss as an inherent part of existence. She can grieve. That is what her mother’s sealed dream cannot do, and it is the distinction between them.

Around the edges the chapter keeps dissolving the solid. Raleigh’s night society holds that “man had existed before Adam” and that “God is nothing but an idea in the mind,” turning even God into a construction, an idea rather than a fact dug from the earth. And Mr. Spitzer, the rational lawyer on his “dusty legal errands,” is faulted for missing the “crowded musical evenings,” for taking the built legal world as the real one and missing what cannot be filed. Everything the settled mind treats as bedrock is shown made.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that makes this a simple contest between Vera’s realism and Catherine’s fantasy, reality good and dream bad. It is not scoring reality over imagination. It refuses, too, the reading that admires Catherine’s loss-proof universe as a triumph of imagination.

The footing is that the sealed invention’s deepest work is to refuse loss, and refusing loss is refusing to grieve. Catherine’s world admits no theory of loss because grief is exactly what it was built to keep out, and that is why it fails. Vera’s answering position is not that reality beats dream but that a life must be able to hold loss, must let the weather in. Even God is an idea in the mind, a construction like the rest; the question is never whether we build but whether we can grieve inside what we build. A world that cannot lose cannot grieve, and a life that cannot grieve is sealed.

For Reflection

What has one built, in a life, specifically so that nothing need ever be missing from it?

Is the ability to tell dream from reality worth anything if it is not the ability to grieve?

What that one takes for solid ground, God, fact, the law, would look different named as something built?
