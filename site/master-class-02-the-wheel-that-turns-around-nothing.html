The Wheel That Turns Around Nothing

← Introduction to the 2nd edition · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Brief Overview of Radical Immanence →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · The Stone Deaf Man · Vera Cartwheel

Marguerite Young told an interviewer that she thought of Miss MacIntosh as the center of a wheel, the hub, with the other characters as spokes radiating out from her, and the wheel itself endlessly expanding like a universe. That is the architect describing the blueprint. She explicitly names it and then takes it away from the reader.

Miss MacIntosh, the practical nursemaid from What Cheer, Iowa, stands at the center. Vera, Catherine, Mr. Spitzer, Cousin Hannah, Esther Longtree, all the others turn around her. Each spoke holds its distance from the others and its attachment to the middle. This is a reassuring shape. It has a center. It promises that if one travels far enough inward, past the outer characters and their orbits, one arrives at something solid, a person who is what she appears to be, who knows what time it is, who cannot be fooled.

Then Miss MacIntosh walks into the ocean, weighted with stones, in the opening pages, and is never seen again. The hub is gone almost before the wheel has finished turning once. The wig comes off. And here is the thing the novel is quietly doing from that moment forward: it is not showing a wheel that lost its center. It is showing that the center was never there to lose. Miss MacIntosh was the still point everyone believed in, the plain reliable ground, and the whole long book is the slow revelation that even she was turning all along, secretly, under her hat, the same as everyone else. The hub was the last illusion to go.

This is the motion the novel was built on. The hub-and-spoke wheel, the one Young described, collapses into a different wheel underneath it. That second wheel is older. Boethius drew it in prison while he waited to be executed: the Wheel of Fortune, which turns as it pleases, raises the low and brings down the high, and does not turn for any one person specifically. It simply turns, and people happen to be on it. The medieval world treated this not as a metaphor but as a diagram of how time actually works. Young built that diagram into the novel from the first page, and she encoded it in the narrator's name before a single chapter unfolds. Vera Cartwheel. Truth, attached to a wheel. Truth turns. It does not stay.

What Young does with the old wheel is the part worth studying, and it is where a writer can learn the most from her. In the traditional Wheel of Fortune, many people ride the rim. Fortune lifts one and drops another; the wheel is crowded with rising and falling figures, each having their turn. Young made one change, and it is the change that generates everything else. She put a single person on the wheel. Not many selves each taking a turn, but one self that is itself the turning, made of the same stuff as the wheel, multiple and contradictory and never arriving. When Vera observes that life must always have these double motions, circles within circles, wheels within wheels, orbs within orbs, she is not describing the sky. She is describing the inside of a single consciousness that contains its own revolutions and cannot resolve them into one center. Mr. Spitzer is the clearest case: always between two streams, unable to decide which way to turn, unable to stand still. He is not a man who lost his center. He is a man made of turning.

That single change is why the novel can be 1,198 pages long without going anywhere in the ordinary sense. A spoke does not travel; it rotates. The book does not build toward a climax; it accumulates revolutions. This is a structural decision with consequences, and any writer who has been told that a novel must advance, must escalate, must arrive, should sit with what Young proves here: that a book can hold a reader by turning rather than by climbing, if the turning is the subject. She built a structure whose deepest architecture is invisible on a first reading, that accumulates across the full length, and that rewards the reader who stays long enough to feel its weight. She learned to do this by reading Tom Jones thirteen times as a student, until she could see how a structure hides itself in plain sight. She spent eighteen years applying that lesson to a novel that looks nothing like Tom Jones.

I have a name for the philosophy the novel enacts by turning this way. I call it Radical Immanence: the conviction that everything which matters is here, in this world, in this turning, and not in some still point beyond it. I want to state clearly that I coined the term for what I found in Young before I knew it had a philosophical history. The word immanence carries other thinkers behind it, Spinoza and others who set the immanent against the transcendent, and when I traced that history afterward I found that Young seems to have arrived at her version on her own, the same way I arrived at the name on mine. Three independent arrivals at the same place is not a weakness in the idea. It is a small argument that the place is real, and that it was waiting in the novel to be found rather than carried in from outside.

One more honest note about what I am claiming. I am arguing that the novel enacts this philosophy, not that Young sat down intending to build it. The documents that might settle the question of her conscious intent are at the Beinecke Library; I have put the question to her official biographer. Until the archive speaks, the claim rests where it should rest, on what the book does rather than on what its author meant, and I would rather stand on firmer ground. This is itself, as it happens, one of the philosophy's own commitments: to name where you are speaking from instead of pretending to speak from nowhere.

Radical Immanence has eleven load-bearing ideas, and I will not walk them here. They have their own chapters, and readers who want the full case, or the chapter-by-chapter mapping to the novel, will find it in the companion volumes listed above. What matters here is how the wheel gathers them. The plural self is the person on the wheel, many and turning. Constructed reality is the recognition that the ground everyone stands on, including the "just the way things are" that Miss MacIntosh insists upon, was built by someone and can be asked who it serves. Grief that does not end is the wheel's emotional weather: it keeps turning even after everyone has gone home, and the losses accumulate rather than resolve. Witnessing is what Vera does, staying present with contradictions she cannot fix, which reframes her so-called passivity as the hardest and most ethical thing the book asks. Building anyway, unfinished as honest, the margins revealing the seams, the past accumulating beneath the present, language shaping what can exist, the sacred found here rather than beyond: each of these is a spoke, and each turns around the same absent center.

The eleventh idea is the one that ties the others together, and it is where Young's reinvention of the wheel finishes its work. I call it the crossing point. The traditional Wheel of Fortune is vast and impersonal; it turns over you and produces awe at its scale. Young's singular wheel produces something else. If there is one self on the wheel, and the self is the turning, then the meaningful place is not the great rim sweeping through the heavens but the exact intersection where this specific past meets this specific moment, here, in this body, now. The crossing point is not large. It is precise. It is Vera arriving not at What Cheer, Iowa, the magical center she quested toward, but in Indiana, in the ordinary Middle West, at herself, choosing to marry a stone deaf man and accepting that there is no perfect moment and no hub to reach. “Whatever is average is the dream. Whatever is hidden is the revealed.” The still point she spent the novel seeking was never out on the rim. It was the crossing point she already was.

Young gave us a wheel with a center, and then showed us the center was never there, and in doing so gave us a truer wheel, the old one, with a single turning self on it, arriving nowhere, finding the sacred in the exact and ordinary here. She could not live long enough to write the book about writing she said she would never write. But she left the lesson in the architecture, for any reader patient enough to feel the wheel turn, and for any writer willing to learn how a novel can hold together when the hub is gone.
