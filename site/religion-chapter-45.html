On Chapter 45 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Christianity and Religion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.

Allusions: Angels and Archangels

She has "wandered like some penitential palmer with sea shells on his hat," the pilgrim to the Holy Land marked by scallop shells, penance made visible, and a "Crusader's castle" sets her among the medieval holy wars.

“Is it on the mountain slope or in the valley of the shadow?”: See Psalm 23:4 in the Chapter 43 resources. See The Bible. Earlier in Chapters 6 and 43.

"There were halos where there were no moons," a light of holiness that shines from itself, not borrowed or reflected.

“The deepest sleep is dreamless.”: Socrates says that if death is a dreamless sleep, “to die is gain; for eternity is then only a single night” (Plato, Apology 40d-e, trans. Benjamin Jowett, 1871). The Mandukya Upanishad names deep sleep, where the sleeper desires nothing and dreams no dream, as the third state of the self. See Plato and Platonism and Hinduism.

"Angels rolling away the stones of tombs" recalls the empty grave of Easter and the stone rolled back.

“the infernal mountains of frozen furies”: The lowest circle of Dante’s Hell is not fire but ice, the frozen lake of Cocytus (Inferno 32-34). See Dante.

“It would be neither the anchor nor the heart”: The anchor on a gravestone is hope: “Which hope we have as an anchor of the soul, both sure and stedfast” (Hebrews 6:19). See The Bible.

“pigeons many-colored as Joseph’s coat”: “Now Israel loved Joseph more than all his children… and he made him a coat of many colours” (Genesis 37:3). See Genesis. Earlier in Chapter 23.

Around this the chapter also gathers echoes from beyond Christianity, a Roman epitaph, Carthage, a "forgotten Ramadan," a winding horn, but the mourning frame stays Christian, a burial in a "grey New England churchyard."

“the Greeks and Medes, the Persians”: “The law of the Medes and Persians, which altereth not” (Daniel 6:8). See The Bible.

"a white bird lifted its archangelic wing" lends the highest order of angels to a bird stirring in the wind, and "an old cross-beam breaking upon the waves" carries a faint echo of Noah's ark.

Her long prayer, "Forgive the cloud which knows not what it whispers," on through "I fell from star to star. My life was one long act of falling," gathers divine forgiveness, the Fall, and the downward pull of all things into one plea, a breaking body asking pardon for a secret "none can tell and live."

“I have been a wanderer ever since, wandering over the face of the earth”: Cain, who killed his brother, is sentenced: “a fugitive and a vagabond shalt thou be in the earth” (Genesis 4:12). Hannah believed she had killed her love. See Genesis.

“he who loves shall lose himself in love as in death”: “For whosoever will save his life shall lose it: and whosoever will lose his life for my sake shall find it” (Matthew 16:25; see also John 12:25). The loss of self in love is not presented as purely tragic but as an essential part of the human condition and the search for connection beyond isolation. See The Bible.
