The Tavern Landlord: The Life Made From Its Own Telling

← Jacqueline / Jackie White · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · The Tavern Landlady →

Spoiler note: This portrait is written for readers who have finished the novel. It discusses the character’s whole story, including how it ends.

Characters: Homer Capehorn · Madge Capehorn · Miss MacIntosh · Moses Hunnecker · Mr. Weed · Vera Cartwheel

The landlord is the man at the door of the whole book. The Tavern is where the bus stops, the hotel where Madge and Homer and Moses and Vera and Mr. Weed all come to rest, and it is the landlord who signs them in, carries the trunks, and hands out the rooms. He is the only figure in the set whose main work is not being made by someone else. His work is telling, and what he tells first and last and always is himself.

He is a former sailor now keeping a hotel, bald, ruddy, deaf in one ear or pretending to be, standing behind his counter in "suppressed power." His whole biography is written on his skin, "hearts and darts and one lonely anchor," a battleship behind the hairs of his chest with "Remember the Marne" under it, a woman's face in his palm that winks when he closes his hand. He wears where he has been the way Madge wore her painted sailboats and skiers, a man turned into a surface of the world he claims to have crossed. And the claim is enormous. He was a candy merchant at ten, a magician who plucked cards off people's sleeves, a soldier, a hunter, a man in Intelligence, a killer of a little black man who had swallowed a diamond. "There is no country I have not seen," he says, "except America," and "there seemed to be nothing he had not experienced." The life is plainly a tall tale, and he tells it, the book notes, "as if he were talking to himself."

What holds the tale together is that he has already survived his own death, several times. He went down on four ships, the Titanic and the Lusitania among them, and was "written up as missing in many a shipwreck," so that he stands at his counter, in his own phrase, "unofficially alive." His mother had told him he was "born to drown, not to hang," and he believes it still, waiting for the sea to finish a job it has four times failed at. He is a man narrating himself out of the graves he was reported into, which is only what everyone in this book does, except that he does it about himself, cheerfully, to a stranger, while carrying her luggage.

His trade is knots, and the trade makes him the exact opposite number of the guest signing in beside him. The other guest is a hangman, and where the hangman ties the knot that ends a man, the landlord ties "lovers' knots, knots which none shall untie until Gabriel blows his horn." He has spliced many a couple into the one knot he swears "cannot be untied," the marital knot, binding them so that "even after death should not part them." The hangman, gloomy, corrects him, "the marital knot can always be untied," which is just what the hangman is there to do. Between the two of them the whole business of the book is laid out at the front desk, one man binding people together past death and the other cutting them loose, and the reader is left to notice that these are the same rope.

Then the landlord does the thing that marks him as the book's own kind of maker. He tells Vera that the talkative, confessing hangman was not the hangman at all. The real hangman is a close-mouthed, respectable dairy farmer who ties the knot only because, as the landlord puts it, "he got paid to tie it." The one who rambled and confessed was "the hangman's shadow, who got loose after every hanging and said the things the hangman never would have said." So the landlord splits a single man into two, the silent farmer and the loosed shadow, and then, carrying the trunk up the stairs, he closes the split back up, because "as if in ocean depths, the two were one." He is a plain working man who casually performs the book's whole doctrine of the divided self, and he does it as a favor, to explain the noise from the next room.

He cannot keep persons and things and names apart, and does not try. Asked about a rustling, he says it is thirty chained turkey buzzards, then that they are canaries big as buzzards, then that they belong to an old woman, then, tapping a wardrobe trunk, "Tillie," a very young girl who will knit a baby hood, and it turns out he means one person by all of it, or none. Asked who Imadaisy is, he confesses, "Nobody." He puts Vera in a room called the Fire Escape and explains it by the bridal suite being taken by a young couple, the same explanation, word for word, he had already given to the hangman's shadow. Names, guests, rooms, buzzards, canaries, girls, nobodies, they run together in his mouth like water, and he hands them out with total confidence, because at his desk the difference between a real guest and an invented one has stopped meaning anything.

The landlord is not haunted the way the mother is, or grieving the way Miss MacIntosh is. He is cheerful, loud, and completely untroubled by the fact that his life will not bear checking, that his hangman has two bodies, that his guests may be nobodies. He is the one person in the book who has made peace with living inside a story, and he keeps the door of the place where all the other stories arrive.

The connection for a writer is the plainest in the book, which is why it comes at the threshold. Among his many trades the landlord names the truest one, ventriloquist, the man who threw his voice into empty rocking chairs, who "made the coffee cups speak with human voices" and "made birds twitter where there were no birds." That is the novel describing itself. Everything in these pages is a coffee cup made to speak and a bird made to sing where there was no bird, a sleeping stranger given a marriage, a cried name given a death, an absent chauffeur given a rap sheet, a faceless coachman given a whole conscience to steer. Young puts her method in the mouth of a bald, deaf, shipwrecked hotel-keeper because the method has no dignity to protect and needs none. A writer is a person who narrates a self that will not bear checking, who ties the living to the dead in knots meant never to come undone, who splits one person into a man and his shadow and then swears the two are one, and who stands at the door of a house where the real guests and the made-up ones sign the same register and are shown to their rooms with equal care. The landlord does all of it for a living and sleeps fine. The honest writer, this book suggests, could do worse than to be him, untroubled at the threshold, generous with the rooms, telling the tale as if talking to himself, and never once pretending that the guests he invented are worth less than the ones who walked in off the bus.
