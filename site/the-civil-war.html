Chapter 3

  • Coal mining labor history in the United States is marked by dangerous work, exploitation, and the struggle for workers' rights, driven by waves of immigration, the Civil War, and industrialization. Early laborers were diverse, including enslaved and free African Americans, and later, large numbers of immigrants from Great Britain, Ireland, and Eastern and Southern Europe were drawn to the industry by recruiters. The United Mine Workers of America (UMWA) was founded in 1890 to fight for better wages, safer conditions, and benefits, leading to significant labor conflicts and hard-won reforms like the eight-hour workday and pensions. The coal industry was marked by a series of violent conflicts and massacres, often stemming from labor disputes between striking workers (many of whom were immigrants) and mine owners who hired private security forces or leveraged local law enforcement to suppress union organizing efforts. Lattimer Massacre (September 10, 1897): In Lattimer, Pennsylvania, a sheriff's posse opened fire on a march of unarmed striking immigrant anthracite miners, mostly of Polish, Slovak, Lithuanian, and German descent. At least 19 miners were killed, and many more were wounded. The sheriff and his deputies were later tried but acquitted. Battle of Virden (October 1898): In Virden, Illinois, a gun battle erupted when the Chicago-Virden Coal Company attempted to bring in African-American strikebreakers with armed guards. Eight strikers and six guards/strikebreakers were killed. Ludlow Massacre (April 20, 1914): During a major strike in Colorado, the Colorado National Guard and guards from the Colorado Fuel & Iron Company attacked a tent colony of 1,200 striking miners and their families. The assault resulted in the deaths of approximately 19 people, including women and children, many of whom were burned to death when the camp was set on fire. Matewan Massacre (May 19, 1920): A deadly shootout occurred in Matewan, West Virginia, between Baldwin-Felts detective agency guards (hired by mine owners to evict striking workers from company-owned housing) and local residents, including the mayor and police chief, who supported the miners. Ten people died in the clash (seven detectives, two miners, and the mayor). Herrin Massacre (June 21-22, 1922): In Herrin, Illinois, a violent confrontation occurred between union miners and strikebreakers at a strip mine. After a gunfight and a subsequent surrender under the promise of safe passage, union miners murdered 20 strikebreakers and mine guards, and three union men also died. The incident is considered one of the most violent labor disputes in US history. Battle of Blair Mountain (August/September 1921): The culmination of the West Virginia Mine Wars, this was the largest armed insurrection in U.S. history since the Civil War. Thousands of union miners fought against local law enforcement and company-hired forces. Federal troops were eventually sent in by President Harding to quell the uprising, which involved the use of airplanes to drop bombs. At least 10 to 33 people were killed in the fighting. Columbine Mine Massacre (November 21, 1927): In Serene, Colorado, striking miners were met with machine-gun fire from the Colorado Rangers and guards from the Rocky Mountain Fuel Company during a strike, resulting in the deaths of six miners. These events, part of the broader "Coal Wars" (roughly 1890-1930), were pivotal in the struggle for better wages, safer working conditions, and the right to unionize in the U.S. coal industry.

  • Historically, the term "Black Republican" has had different meanings: 1) Before the Civil War, it was used as a derogatory term for abolitionist members of the Republican Party. 2) During Reconstruction the term was also used for Republicans who supported legislation that favored African Americans. 3) During and after the Civil War the term was used to refer to members of the Negro Republican Party, a faction of the Republican Party in the South that was predominantly made up of Black Americans. 4) From the 1870s to the 1960s it was associated with the "black-and-tan faction" of the Republican Party in the South, which opposed the segregationist "lily-white movement".
  • “the undertaker . . . had done an excellent sidetrade in the furniture business”: In nineteenth-century America the town cabinetmaker often made coffins as well as tables and beds, and many furniture dealers became undertakers. Embalming, the undertaker’s art “to paint the dead and make them seem alive,” spread after the Civil War, when soldiers were embalmed to be sent home and Lincoln’s embalmed body was carried across the country.

  • Sea Island cotton, a long-staple variety cultivated on coastal islands of South Carolina and Georgia, was intimately linked to the institution of slavery. Its high quality and demand made it a lucrative crop, but its production relied entirely on the forced labor of enslaved Africans. The Sea Island cotton industry thrived from the late 1700s to the late 19th century, fueled by the brutal exploitation of enslaved people. The cultivation, harvesting, and processing of Sea Island cotton were entirely dependent on the labor of enslaved Africans. The Civil War brought an end to the Sea Island cotton industry and the institution of slavery, disrupting the established economic order.

Chapter 52

  • Many young people served as soldiers in the Civil War, with estimates suggesting that around 10% of Union soldiers were under 18. Some were as young as 10 years old, and many served as musicians, messengers, or even in combat roles. While the official minimum enlistment age was 18, many boys lied about their age to join the military, and some were even encouraged by recruiters. William Black: At age 12, he was wounded in battle, making him the youngest known wounded soldier, according to Nashville.gov. Johnny Clem: He joined the Union Army at age 10 and became a drummer boy. Johnny Cook: He was 15 when he volunteered as a cannoneer at the Battle of Antietam and received the Medal of Honor for his bravery, according to the National Park Service. Young soldiers faced the same dangers and hardships as older soldiers, including combat, disease, and imprisonment.

Chapter 52

  • The 1850s and 1860s in the US were a period of intense national division and conflict, culminating in the Civil War. The central issue was slavery and its expansion, which led to political realignment, violence, and ultimately, secession and war. The decade of the 1850s saw escalating tensions surrounding slavery, with political compromises failing to resolve the issue and the nation moving closer to the brink of war. The Civil War itself, fought from 1861 to 1865, resulted in the end of slavery and Northern victory, but at a tremendous cost in human lives and national unity. Compromise of 1850 attempted to address the issue of slavery in newly acquired territories, but ultimately failed to quell the growing divide. The Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 introduced the concept of "popular sovereignty," allowing residents of territories to decide on slavery, leading to violence and conflict in Kansas ("Bleeding Kansas"). Existing political parties fractured, and new ones emerged, such as the Republican Party, dedicated to preventing the expansion of slavery. The movement to abolish slavery gained momentum, with figures like Harriet Beecher Stowe and her novel Uncle Tom's Cabin further inflaming anti-slavery sentiment. Dred Scott Decision (1857) where the Supreme Court ruled that enslaved people were not citizens and Congress could not prohibit slavery in the territories, further exacerbating tensions. Abraham Lincoln's election as president in 1860, without a single Southern electoral vote, led to the secession of several Southern states. The Civil War (1861-1865) was fought between the Union (North) and the Confederacy (South), resulting in immense loss of life and widespread destruction. Major battles like Gettysburg and Vicksburg marked turning points in the war. Emancipation Proclamation (1863) declared enslaved people in Confederate-held territory to be free. New York City draft riots (1863) discontent with the draft laws during the Civil War led to violent protests. The 13th Amendment (1865) officially abolished slavery in the United States. Reconstruction (1865-1877) after the Civil War focused on rebuilding the South and integrating formerly enslaved people into society. This era was marked by successes and failures in achieving true equality.

Chapter 52

  • The period from 1850 to 1860 in American news was dominated by the escalating tensions over slavery and the eventual outbreak of the Civil War. Newspapers played a crucial role, both reflecting and shaping public opinion on these issues. The rise of the penny press and the increasing use of correspondents, especially during the war, marked significant developments in the news industry. The debate over slavery intensified, with the Compromise of 1850, the Kansas-Nebraska Act, and the Dred Scott decision significantly impacting the political landscape and fueling sectionalism. Newspapers became powerful tools for disseminating views on these issues, often aligning themselves with specific political parties. The penny press, exemplified by newspapers like the New York Herald, gained popularity by offering affordable, mass-circulation papers with a focus on local news, crime stories, and human interest pieces. This shift in business model and content helped make newspapers a mass medium. While colonial newspapers were typically one-person operations, the 1850s saw a growth in the number of journalists and correspondents, particularly with the outbreak of the Civil War. The war itself became a major news event, with newspapers deploying reporters to cover battles and casualty lists. Newspapers were often heavily partisan, reflecting the political divisions of the time. They frequently supported specific parties or factions with editorials, illustrations, and even filled-out ballots and were actively involved in political campaigns. This contrasts with the modern ideal of journalistic independence. The telegraph, while not yet dominant, began to play a role in the speed of news dissemination, especially for major events. The development of the telegraph allowed for faster communication over long distances. Railroads carried thousands of newspaper copies across the country. The Civil War saw the rise of the war correspondent, a figure who would become a staple of journalism. These correspondents, including some who were soldiers themselves, provided eyewitness accounts of the war, often facing censorship and logistical challenges. Both sides restricted reporting and even shut down newspapers, causing tension with the press. Newspapers played a role in shaping public opinion on social issues, including the abolitionist movement. For example, Harriet Beecher Stowe's "Uncle Tom's Cabin", serialized in a newspaper, significantly influenced public perception of slavery. Western and Southern newspapers, often personal enterprises, emphasized local news, particularly about crops, land, and Native American relations. The Wide Awakes political organization, which arose in the late 1850s, relied heavily on newspapers to spread their message and organize supporters, demonstrating the power of the press in social and political mobilization. Legendary editors like Horace Greeley and James Gordon Bennett rose to prominence, shaping the landscape of American journalism.

Chapter 53

  • Archery - has been a crucial hunting method for millennia, with evidence of its use dating back to prehistoric times. Bows and arrows were also essential for warfare in many cultures throughout history. The widespread adoption of firearms, particularly flintlock weapons, by the 1800s led to a decline in the use of archery for hunting, especially in regions where firearms were readily available. However, archery did not disappear entirely. In fact, the 19th century saw a revival of archery as a sport and recreational activity, with the establishment of archery clubs and competitions in Europe and elsewhere. A notable example is the Toxophilite Society in London, founded in 1781. While firearms became more prevalent, archery continued to be used for hunting in some areas, particularly where firearms were not as accessible or by those who preferred the traditional methods. The "primitive" regions of the world continued using bows and arrows in warfare. Archery's cultural significance extended beyond its practical uses. It was often associated with spirituality, skill, and social status in various societies. The book "The Witchery of Archery" by the Thompson brothers, who took up archery after the American Civil War, is credited with inspiring a revival of the sport in the United States.

Chapter 54

  • “Indian maiden Pocahontas” Pocahontas also known as Matoaka and Rebecca Rolfe was a Native American woman belonging to the Powhatan people, notable for her association with the colonial settlement at Jamestown, Virginia. She was the daughter of Wahunsenacawh, the paramount chief of a network of tributary tribes in the Tsenacommacah (known in English as the Powhatan Confederacy), encompassing the Tidewater region of what is today the U.S. state of Virginia. Pocahontas was captured and held for ransom by English colonists during hostilities in 1613. During her captivity, she was encouraged to convert to Christianity and was baptized under the name Rebecca. She married the tobacco planter John Rolfe in April 1614 at the age of about 17 or 18, and she bore their son, Thomas Rolfe, in January 1615. In 1616, the Rolfes travelled to London, where Pocahontas was presented to English society as an example of the "civilized savage" in hopes of stimulating investment in Jamestown. On this trip, she may have met Squanto, a Patuxet man from New England. Pocahontas became a celebrity, was elegantly fêted, and attended a masque at Whitehall Palace. In 1617, the Rolfes intended to sail for Virginia, but Pocahontas died at Gravesend, Kent, England, of unknown causes, aged 20 or 21. She was buried in St George's Church, Gravesend; her grave's exact location is unknown because the church was rebuilt after being destroyed by a fire. Numerous places, landmarks, and products in the United States have been named after Pocahontas. Her story has been romanticized over the years, many aspects of which are fictional. Many of the stories told about her by the English explorer John Smith have been contested by her documented descendants. She is a subject of art, literature, and film. Many famous people have claimed to be among her descendants, including members of the First Families of Virginia, First Lady Edith Wilson, American actor Glenn Strange, and astronomer Percival Lowell.

  • Pinckney Street is in Boston, Massachusetts. Pinckney Street is a historic residential street in Boston's Beacon Hill neighborhood, known for its Federal-style architecture and its connection to a significant African American community that emerged in the area before the Civil War. One of the oldest homes on the street, the George Middleton House, was built in the late 1780s and is a testament to the street's long history. The area is notable for its role as a center for free Black residents and, later, as a place where people like Louisa May Alcott lived and wrote.

Chapter 54

  • “Civil War silver dollar” may refer to the Seated Liberty Dollar. The Civil War caused widespread hoarding of the coins due to their value. They were made of 90% silver. This led to a coin shortage in 1862. The government issued paper Demand Notes and later minted silver dollars which were stored in a vault for future use. The Seated Liberty Dollar was the design in use for U.S. silver dollars before and during the Civil War. A law in 1865 allowed the motto "In God We Trust" to be added to coins, and it was placed on the Seated Liberty dollar in 1866. The Seated Liberty dollar was the first coin struck at the Carson City Mint in Nevada, beginning in 1870, with thousands of these coins stored in Treasury vaults.

Chapter 54

  • “American soap flake king” may refer to Procter & Gamble (P&G), founded by William Procter and James Gamble. They were a pivotal force in the American soap industry and its transition to soap flakes. While British manufacturer Lever Brothers were the first to produce flakes commercially, P&G popularized the product in the U.S., particularly with its Ivory soap brand. The American Civil War provided a major boost to P&G's fortunes when the company secured contracts to supply the Union Army with soap and candles. After the war, returning soldiers created nationwide demand for P&G products. In the 1910s, P&G created Ivory Flakes, a shredded form of their popular Ivory soap. Unlike the harsh laundry soap of the era, the mild, pure Ivory Flakes were marketed for washing delicate items like baby clothes, silks, and nylons. The product's success cemented P&G's dominance in the market.

Chapter 55

  • “twin President Garfields” James Abram Garfield (November 19, 1831 – September 19, 1881) was the 20th president of the United States, serving from March 1881 until his death in September that year after being shot two months earlier. A preacher, lawyer, and Civil War general, Garfield served nine terms in the United States House of Representatives and is the only sitting member of the House to be elected president. Before his candidacy for the presidency, he had been elected to the U.S. Senate by the Ohio General Assembly, a position he declined when he became president-elect. Garfield was born into poverty in a log cabin and grew up in northeastern Ohio. After graduating from Williams College in 1856, he studied law and became an attorney. He was a preacher in the Stone– Campbell Movement and president of the Western Reserve Eclectic Institute, affiliated with the Disciples. Garfield was elected as a Republican member of the Ohio State Senate in 1859, serving until 1861. He opposed Confederate secession, was a major general in the Union Army during the American Civil War, and fought in the battles of Middle Creek, Shiloh, and Chickamauga. He was elected to Congress in 1862 to represent Ohio's 19th district. Throughout his congressional service, he firmly supported the gold standard and gained a reputation as a skilled orator. He initially agreed with Radical Republican views on Reconstruction but later favored a Moderate Republican–aligned approach to civil rights enforcement for freedmen. Garfield's aptitude for mathematics extended to his own proof of the Pythagorean theorem, which he published in 1876. At the 1880 Republican National Convention, delegates chose Garfield, who had not sought the White House, as a compromise presidential nominee on the 36th ballot. In the 1880 presidential election, he conducted a low-key front porch campaign and narrowly defeated the Democratic nominee, Winfield Scott Hancock. Garfield's accomplishments as president included his assertion of presidential authority against senatorial courtesy in executive appointments, a purge of corruption in the Post Office, and his appointment of a Supreme Court justice. He advocated for agricultural technology, an educated electorate, and civil rights for African Americans. He also proposed substantial civil service reforms, which were passed by Congress in 1883 as the Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act and signed into law by his successor, Chester A. Arthur. Garfield was a member of the intraparty "Half-Breed" faction who used the powers of the presidency to defy the powerful "Stalwart" Senator Roscoe Conkling from New York. He did this by appointing Blaine faction leader William H. Robertson to the lucrative post of Collector of the Port of New York. The ensuing political battle resulted in Robertson's confirmation and the resignations of Conkling and Thomas C. Platt from the Senate. On July 2, 1881, Charles J. Guiteau, a disappointed and delusional office seeker, shot Garfield at the Baltimore and Potomac Railroad Station in Washington. The wound was not immediately fatal, but an infection caused by his doctors' unsanitary methods in treating the wound killed Garfield on September 19. Due to his brief tenure in office, historians tend to rank Garfield as a below-average president or omit him entirely from rankings, though he has earned praise for anti-corruption and pro-civil rights stances.

  • “Canton flannels” is a warm, cotton-based fabric, named for its origin in Canton (now Guangzhou), China, around the mid-to-late 19th century. It is characterized by a twill or plain weave on one side and a fleecy, napped surface on the other.   • Boston abolitionists, active from the 1830s to the Civil War, included prominent figures like William Lloyd Garrison, Wendell Phillips, and Lewis Hayden, who formed societies like the New England Anti-Slavery Society and published the anti-slavery newspaper The Liberator. These activists, both Black and white, organized and rallied against slavery, often facing violent opposition from anti-abolitionist mobs in the city.   • "flags of the Confederacy" refers to several flags used by the Confederate States of America during the Civil War (1861-65), with the most recognizable being the Battle Flag of the Army of Northern Virginia. It was created for battlefield use to distinguish it from the Union flag and later became a symbol for the Lost Cause narrative of Southern heritage, though its use also became tied to white supremacy and racist groups. Chapter 62

  • Pledge of Allegiance - is a patriotic recited verse that promises allegiance to the flag of the United States and the republic of the United States. The first version was written in 1885 by Captain George Thatcher Balch, a Union army officer in the Civil War who later authored a book on how to teach patriotism to children in public schools. In 1892, Francis Bellamy revised Balch's verse as part of a magazine promotion surrounding the World's Columbian Exposition, which celebrated the 400th anniversary of Christopher Columbus' arrival in the Americas. Bellamy, the circulation manager for The Youth's Companion magazine, helped persuade then-president Benjamin Harrison to institute Columbus Day as a national holiday and lobbied Congress for a national school celebration of the day. The magazine sent leaflets containing part of Bellamy's Pledge of Allegiance to schools across the country and on October 21, 1892, over 10,000 children recited the verse together. Bellamy's version of the pledge is largely the same as the one formally adopted by Congress 50 years later, in 1942. The official name of The Pledge of Allegiance was adopted in 1945. The most recent alteration of its wording came on Flag Day (June 14) in 1954, when the words "under God" were added. However, Bellamy's authorship has been contested, as evidence has come out contradicting his claim. Several countries have a form of national pledge or oath, though the practice of daily, mandatory recitation in schools (as commonly associated with the US) is unusual by international standards. In many nations, oaths are typically reserved for specific occasions like citizenship ceremonies or for public officials.

Chapter 64

  • Abraham Lincoln was the 16th president of the United States, serving from 1861 until his assassination in 1865. He led the United States through the American Civil War, defeating the Confederate States and playing a major role in the abolition of slavery.

Chapter 65

  • St. Louis, Missouri, was founded by French fur traders in 1764 at a strategic location on the Mississippi River. It became the "Gateway to the West" after being purchased by the United States in the Louisiana Purchase of 1803 and was the starting point for the Lewis and Clark Expedition. The city grew into a major trade center, fueled by its river and railroad access, and hosted the 1904 World's Fair and Olympics. Key historical moments include its 1823 incorporation, its strategic importance during the Civil War, and the famous Dred Scott case at the Old Courthouse.

Chapter 65

  • The history of returning enslaved people to Africa, known as the Back-to-Africa Movement, involved efforts by groups like the American Colonization Society (ACS) in the 19th century to send freed slaves to colonies like Liberia and Sierra Leone, aiming to create homelands for Black people in Africa. While supported by some, including President Lincoln at times, it faced strong opposition from many African Americans who saw it as racist and a way to strengthen slavery, with thousands settling in Liberia before the Civil War despite harsh conditions and high mortality. Founded in 1816 by white elites, the ACS established Liberia (settled 1822) as a colony for freed U.S. slaves, modeled partly on British efforts in Sierra Leone. Paul Cuffee was an early proponent who, in 1815, transported free Black people to Sierra Leone, envisioning trade and immigration. The British settled formerly enslaved people in Sierra Leone starting in the late 1780s, a model the ACS followed.

Chapter 65

  • Ohio's history spans from its Native American roots, through European exploration and settlement (leading to statehood in 1803, though officially recognized later), its crucial role in the Civil War as a Union state with significant anti-slavery sentiment, rapid industrialization in the late 19th century fueled by cities like Cleveland and Akron, its identity as the "Mother of Presidents," and into the mid-20th century, solidifying its industrial might and diverse cultural influences from New England, New York, and the South.

Chapter 66

  • American tin history spans from 17th-century colonial tinsmiths, making decorative and functional "tinware" like pierced lanterns, to the 19th-century "Tin Can Revolution" driven by Civil War needs, which fostered mass production of inexpensive, lightweight containers for food, transforming household goods and expanding food distribution. Later, tin became crucial for electronics and architecture (tin ceilings). Cans became central to American life, enabling global food distribution and connecting the frontier with the industrialized East. Tin ceiling tiles emerged as an affordable alternative to ornate plaster, symbolizing affordable elegance in buildings. Polymer coatings improved can integrity, while the industry shifted to using more steel and eventually developed easy-open lids. Tin became vital for electronics (soldering) and alloys in cars and aircraft, while recycling and sustainable sourcing became key concerns.

  • Paupers' graves (also potter's fields) in the US were common, unmarked plots for the poor, unknown, or unclaimed, often managed by poorhouses or asylums, reflecting a societal stigma where a cheap, dignified burial was a mark of last resort, with burials sometimes in communal trenches and lacking individual headstones, a practice evolving from English poor laws and continuing through periods like the Civil War era's hardship.

  • Oliver Cromwell, Lord Protector and ruler of the English Commonwealth after the defeat and beheading of King Charles I during the English Civil War, died on 3 September 1658 of natural causes and was given a public funeral at Westminster Abbey equal to those of the monarchs who came before him. Chapter 70

  • Catherine was going to open a hotel that would admit “...Jews and Negroes. Jews and African Americans could not freely stay in hotels in the US until the passage of federal and state laws that banned discrimination in public accommodations, which was enforced with the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 was a landmark piece of legislation that explicitly prohibited racial discrimination in public places, such as hotels, and this was the first time that Jewish people were also protected from discrimination in public places. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibited discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin in public accommodations like hotels, restaurants, and theaters. In the pre-1964 era, many hotels openly discriminated against both African Americans and Jews, often barring them from the premises entirely or requiring them to stay in separate, inferior accommodations. This was particularly true in the South, where discriminatory practices were widespread, even after the Civil War.

  • "Old Dominion" is Virginia's well-known nickname, given by King Charles II of England to thank the colony for its loyalty during the English Civil War, signifying it as a cherished, longstanding territory of the crown, and it's also a nod to its status as one of the oldest English overseas dominions. Chapter 80

  • Interracial marriage in the United States has only been legal since the 1967 U.S. Supreme Court decision Loving v. Virginia that held anti-miscegenation laws were unconstitutional by the 14th Amendment established in 1868. The court's landmark decision has been commemorated and celebrated every year on the Loving Day, June 12th. The number of interracial marriages in 1967 was three percent. The first interracial marriage in what is today the United States was that of Pocahontas, who married tobacco planter John Rolfe in 1614. The first ever law prohibiting interracial marriage was passed by Maryland in 1691. The Quaker Zephaniah Kingsley married, outside the US, a black enslaved woman that he bought in Cuba. He also had three black common-law enslaved wives. He freed all four. They had to flee Florida for Haiti when it became a US territory. The prospect of black men marrying white women terrified many Americans before the Civil War. It was magnified into the greatest threat to society. The first legal black-white marriage in the United States was that of African-American professor William G. Allen and a white student, Mary King, in 1853. When their plans to marry were announced, Allen narrowly escaped being lynched. Their marriage was secret, and they left the country immediately for England, never to return. In 1957 actor Sammy Davis Jr. faced backlash for his relationship with a white woman, actress Kim Novak. In 1958, Davis briefly married a black woman, actress and dancer Loray White, to protect himself from mob violence.
