Chapter 3

  • Coal mining labor history in the United States is marked by dangerous work, exploitation, and the struggle for workers' rights, driven by waves of immigration, the Civil War, and industrialization. Early laborers were diverse, including enslaved and free African Americans, and later, large numbers of immigrants from Great Britain, Ireland, and Eastern and Southern Europe were drawn to the industry by recruiters. The United Mine Workers of America (UMWA) was founded in 1890 to fight for better wages, safer conditions, and benefits, leading to significant labor conflicts and hard-won reforms like the eight-hour workday and pensions. The coal industry was marked by a series of violent conflicts and massacres, often stemming from labor disputes between striking workers (many of whom were immigrants) and mine owners who hired private security forces or leveraged local law enforcement to suppress union organizing efforts. Lattimer Massacre (September 10, 1897): In Lattimer, Pennsylvania, a sheriff's posse opened fire on a march of unarmed striking immigrant anthracite miners, mostly of Polish, Slovak, Lithuanian, and German descent. At least 19 miners were killed, and many more were wounded. The sheriff and his deputies were later tried but acquitted. Battle of Virden (October 1898): In Virden, Illinois, a gun battle erupted when the Chicago-Virden Coal Company attempted to bring in African-American strikebreakers with armed guards. Eight strikers and six guards/strikebreakers were killed. Ludlow Massacre (April 20, 1914): During a major strike in Colorado, the Colorado National Guard and guards from the Colorado Fuel & Iron Company attacked a tent colony of 1,200 striking miners and their families. The assault resulted in the deaths of approximately 19 people, including women and children, many of whom were burned to death when the camp was set on fire. Matewan Massacre (May 19, 1920): A deadly shootout occurred in Matewan, West Virginia, between Baldwin-Felts detective agency guards (hired by mine owners to evict striking workers from company-owned housing) and local residents, including the mayor and police chief, who supported the miners. Ten people died in the clash (seven detectives, two miners, and the mayor). Herrin Massacre (June 21-22, 1922): In Herrin, Illinois, a violent confrontation occurred between union miners and strikebreakers at a strip mine. After a gunfight and a subsequent surrender under the promise of safe passage, union miners murdered 20 strikebreakers and mine guards, and three union men also died. The incident is considered one of the most violent labor disputes in US history. Battle of Blair Mountain (August/September 1921): The culmination of the West Virginia Mine Wars, this was the largest armed insurrection in U.S. history since the Civil War. Thousands of union miners fought against local law enforcement and company-hired forces. Federal troops were eventually sent in by President Harding to quell the uprising, which involved the use of airplanes to drop bombs. At least 10 to 33 people were killed in the fighting. Columbine Mine Massacre (November 21, 1927): In Serene, Colorado, striking miners were met with machine-gun fire from the Colorado Rangers and guards from the Rocky Mountain Fuel Company during a strike, resulting in the deaths of six miners. These events, part of the broader "Coal Wars" (roughly 1890-1930), were pivotal in the struggle for better wages, safer working conditions, and the right to unionize in the U.S. coal industry.

Chapter 3

  • Bush-whackers/Bushwhacking was a form of guerrilla warfare common during the American Revolutionary War, American Civil War and other conflicts.

Chapter 5

  • Historically, the term "Black Republican" has had different meanings: 1) Before the Civil War, it was used as a derogatory term for abolitionist members of the Republican Party. 2) During Reconstruction the term was also used for Republicans who supported legislation that favored African Americans. 3) During and after the Civil War the term was used to refer to members of the Negro Republican Party, a faction of the Republican Party in the South that was predominantly made up of Black Americans. 4) From the 1870s to the 1960s it was associated with the "black-and-tan faction" of the Republican Party in the South, which opposed the segregationist "lily-white movement".
  • “the undertaker . . . had done an excellent sidetrade in the furniture business”: In nineteenth-century America the town cabinetmaker often made coffins as well as tables and beds, and many furniture dealers became undertakers. Embalming, the undertaker’s art “to paint the dead and make them seem alive,” spread after the Civil War, when soldiers were embalmed to be sent home and Lincoln’s embalmed body was carried across the country.

Chapter 14

  • “the stone fleet manned by stone figures”: In 1861 the Union sank old New England whaling ships, loaded with stone, to block Charleston harbor. They were known as the Great Stone Fleet.

Chapter 35

  • “as Sherman marching to the sea”: Sherman marching to the sea was a military campaign of the American Civil War through Georgia.

Chapter 42

  • “the Civil War”: The Civil War may refer to the American Civil War (1861-1865), fought between the United States and the Confederate states, which seceded to preserve slavery.
  • “Sheridan’s March”: Sheridan’s March may join two campaigns of 1864: Sheridan’s Ride, Philip Sheridan’s gallop to rally his army at Cedar Creek, told in Thomas Buchanan Read’s poem “Sheridan’s Ride” (1864), and William Tecumseh Sherman’s March to the Sea through Georgia.
  • “the Siege of Atlanta”: The Siege of Atlanta may refer to the Battle of Atlanta during the American Civil War. Sherman took the city on September 2, 1864.

Chapter 51

  • The Grand Union Army may refer to the Grand Army of the Republic which is a fraternal organization composed of veterans of the Union Army, Navy and Marines who served in the American Civil War.

Chapter 52

  • “some who were the beardless boys”: Many young people served as soldiers in the Civil War, with estimates suggesting that around 10% of Union soldiers were under 18. Some were as young as 10 years old, and many served as musicians, messengers, or even in combat roles. While the official minimum enlistment age was 18, many boys lied about their age to join the military, and some were even encouraged by recruiters. William Black: At age 12, he was wounded in battle, making him the youngest known wounded soldier, according to Nashville.gov. Johnny Clem: He joined the Union Army at age 10 and became a drummer boy. Johnny Cook: He was 15 when he volunteered as a cannoneer at the Battle of Antietam and received the Medal of Honor for his bravery, according to the National Park Service. Young soldiers faced the same dangers and hardships as older soldiers, including combat, disease, and imprisonment.
  • The 1850s and 1860s in the US were a period of intense national division and conflict, culminating in the Civil War. The central issue was slavery and its expansion, which led to political realignment, violence, and ultimately, secession and war. The decade of the 1850s saw escalating tensions surrounding slavery, with political compromises failing to resolve the issue and the nation moving closer to the brink of war. The Civil War itself, fought from 1861 to 1865, resulted in the end of slavery and Northern victory, but at a tremendous cost in human lives and national unity. Compromise of 1850 attempted to address the issue of slavery in newly acquired territories, but ultimately failed to quell the growing divide. The Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 introduced the concept of “popular sovereignty,” allowing residents of territories to decide on slavery, leading to violence and conflict in Kansas (“Bleeding Kansas”). Existing political parties fractured, and new ones emerged, such as the Republican Party, dedicated to preventing the expansion of slavery. The movement to abolish slavery gained momentum, with figures like Harriet Beecher Stowe and her novel Uncle Tom’s Cabin further inflaming anti-slavery sentiment. Dred Scott Decision (1857) where the Supreme Court ruled that enslaved people were not citizens and Congress could not prohibit slavery in the territories, further exacerbating tensions. Abraham Lincoln’s election as president in 1860, without a single Southern electoral vote, led to the secession of several Southern states. The Civil War (1861-1865) was fought between the Union (North) and the Confederacy (South), resulting in immense loss of life and widespread destruction. Major battles like Gettysburg and Vicksburg marked turning points in the war. Emancipation Proclamation (1863) declared enslaved people in Confederate-held territory to be free. New York City draft riots (1863) discontent with the draft laws during the Civil War led to violent protests. The 13th Amendment (1865) officially abolished slavery in the United States. Reconstruction (1865-1877) after the Civil War focused on rebuilding the South and integrating formerly enslaved people into society. This era was marked by successes and failures in achieving true equality.

Chapter 53

  • “this traditional Republican”: A traditional Republican may refer to the original Republican Party that emerged in the 1850s, founded by anti-slavery activists who opposed the expansion of slavery into US territories. During the 19th century, the party was strongly associated with the abolition of slavery and the preservation of the Union.

Chapter 54

  • “a Civil War silver dollar”: “Civil War silver dollar” may refer to the Seated Liberty Dollar. The Civil War caused widespread hoarding of the coins due to their value. They were made of 90% silver. This led to a coin shortage in 1862. The government issued paper Demand Notes and later minted silver dollars which were stored in a vault for future use. The Seated Liberty Dollar was the design in use for U.S. silver dollars before and during the Civil War. A law in 1865 allowed the motto “In God We Trust” to be added to coins, and it was placed on the Seated Liberty dollar in 1866. The Seated Liberty dollar was the first coin struck at the Carson City Mint in Nevada, beginning in 1870, with thousands of these coins stored in Treasury vaults.

Chapter 57

  • “reconstructing the Civil War—the Battle of Appomattox”: The Battle of Appomattox Court House, fought in Appomattox County, Virginia, on the morning of April 9, 1865, was one of the last battles of the American Civil War. It was the final engagement of Confederate General in Chief, Robert E. Lee, and his Army of Northern Virginia before they surrendered to the Union Army of the Potomac under the Commanding General of the United States Army, Ulysses S. Grant. (Wikipedia) Lee did not surrender in a tent but in the parlor of Wilmer McLean’s house at Appomattox Court House.
  • “the flags of the Confederacy”: Refers to several flags used by the Confederate States of America during the Civil War (1861-65), with the most recognizable being the Battle Flag of the Army of Northern Virginia. It was created for battlefield use to distinguish it from the Union flag and later became a symbol for the Lost Cause narrative of Southern heritage, though its use also became tied to white supremacy and racist groups. The flag’s controversial meaning, representing both Southern heritage and a symbol of racism and slavery, has led to ongoing public debates and the removal of official displays.
  • “the flags of the Grand Union Army”: Young’s Grand Union Army is the Union army of the Civil War, whose veterans formed the Grand Army of the Republic in 1866. The Grand Union Flag, by contrast, was the first flag of the Continental Army, flown from 1775 until the Stars and Stripes was adopted in 1777.

Chapter 62

  • “his body should be draped in the American flag though he had never fought in any war”: In the United States the custom of covering a soldier’s coffin with the flag spread during the Civil War; the government still gives a burial flag to veterans.

Chapter 64

  • Abraham Lincoln was the 16th president of the United States, serving from 1861 until his assassination in 1865. He led the United States through the American Civil War, defeating the Confederate States and playing a major role in the abolition of slavery.

Chapter 65

  • St. Louis, Missouri, was founded by French fur traders in 1764 at a strategic location on the Mississippi River. It became the "Gateway to the West" after being purchased by the United States in the Louisiana Purchase of 1803 and was the starting point for the Lewis and Clark Expedition. The city grew into a major trade center, fueled by its river and railroad access, and hosted the 1904 World's Fair and Olympics. Key historical moments include its 1823 incorporation, its strategic importance during the Civil War, and the famous Dred Scott case at the Old Courthouse.

Chapter 65

  • The history of returning enslaved people to Africa, known as the Back-to-Africa Movement, involved efforts by groups like the American Colonization Society (ACS) in the 19th century to send freed slaves to colonies like Liberia and Sierra Leone, aiming to create homelands for Black people in Africa. While supported by some, including President Lincoln at times, it faced strong opposition from many African Americans who saw it as racist and a way to strengthen slavery, with thousands settling in Liberia before the Civil War despite harsh conditions and high mortality. Founded in 1816 by white elites, the ACS established Liberia (settled 1822) as a colony for freed U.S. slaves, modeled partly on British efforts in Sierra Leone. Paul Cuffee was an early proponent who, in 1815, transported free Black people to Sierra Leone, envisioning trade and immigration. The British settled formerly enslaved people in Sierra Leone starting in the late 1780s, a model the ACS followed.

Chapter 65

  • St. Louis's history spans from a French fur trading settlement in 1764, becoming a crucial Mississippi River hub, booming during the steamboat era, hosting the 1904 World's Fair, growing into a major industrial city, experiencing rapid suburbanization and population shifts post-WWII (seeing a peak then decline), and navigating racial tensions and social changes leading up to the mid-1960s, all while evolving from "Mound City" (ancient earthwork mounds of the Illini Confederacy) to a significant American metropolis.

Chapter 65

  • Ohio's history spans from its Native American roots, through European exploration and settlement (leading to statehood in 1803, though officially recognized later), its crucial role in the Civil War as a Union state with significant anti-slavery sentiment, rapid industrialization in the late 19th century fueled by cities like Cleveland and Akron, its identity as the "Mother of Presidents," and into the mid-20th century, solidifying its industrial might and diverse cultural influences from New England, New York, and the South.

Chapter 66

  • “She did hope that, before she died, the King of England would lose his crown” Attempts to overthrow the English monarchy span centuries, from medieval power struggles like the Anarchy and Wars of the Roses, to the successful republican revolution during the English Civil War (leading to Charles I's execution and the Commonwealth), the Glorious Revolution (limiting monarchical power), and the Jacobite Risings seeking Stuart restoration, all demonstrating persistent challenges to royal authority.

Chapter 66

  • American tin history spans from 17th-century colonial tinsmiths, making decorative and functional "tinware" like pierced lanterns, to the 19th-century "Tin Can Revolution" driven by Civil War needs, which fostered mass production of inexpensive, lightweight containers for food, transforming household goods and expanding food distribution. Later, tin became crucial for electronics and architecture (tin ceilings). Cans became central to American life, enabling global food distribution and connecting the frontier with the industrialized East. Tin ceiling tiles emerged as an affordable alternative to ornate plaster, symbolizing affordable elegance in buildings. Polymer coatings improved can integrity, while the industry shifted to using more steel and eventually developed easy-open lids. Tin became vital for electronics (soldering) and alloys in cars and aircraft, while recycling and sustainable sourcing became key concerns.

Chapter 66

  • Paupers' graves (also potter's fields) in the US were common, unmarked plots for the poor, unknown, or unclaimed, often managed by poorhouses or asylums, reflecting a societal stigma where a cheap, dignified burial was a mark of last resort, with burials sometimes in communal trenches and lacking individual headstones, a practice evolving from English poor laws and continuing through periods like the Civil War era's hardship.

Chapter 68

  • Oliver Cromwell, Lord Protector and ruler of the English Commonwealth after the defeat and beheading of King Charles I during the English Civil War, died on 3 September 1658 of natural causes and was given a public funeral at Westminster Abbey equal to those of the monarchs who came before him. When King Charles II was recalled from exile, his new parliament, in January 1661, ordered the disinterment of the elder Cromwell's body from Westminster Abbey, for a posthumous execution. He was left hanging until the afternoon before being cut down and beheaded.

Chapter 70

  • "going all the way from Odessa to Siberia" refers to historical movements, often forced, of people from the Black Sea port of Odessa (in modern Ukraine) to Siberia, particularly during the Russian Civil War and World War II, involving White Army remnants, ethnic Germans deported by Nazis/Soviets, or political prisoners, highlighting themes of exile, conflict, and displacement across vast distances within the Russian sphere of influence.

Chapter 70

  • Catherine was going to open a hotel that would admit “...Jews and Negroes. Jews and African Americans could not freely stay in hotels in the US until the passage of federal and state laws that banned discrimination in public accommodations, which was enforced with the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 was a landmark piece of legislation that explicitly prohibited racial discrimination in public places, such as hotels, and this was the first time that Jewish people were also protected from discrimination in public places. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibited discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin in public accommodations like hotels, restaurants, and theaters. In the pre-1964 era, many hotels openly discriminated against both African Americans and Jews, often barring them from the premises entirely or requiring them to stay in separate, inferior accommodations. This was particularly true in the South, where discriminatory practices were widespread, even after the Civil War.

Chapter 72

  • "Old Dominion" is Virginia's well-known nickname, given by King Charles II of England to thank the colony for its loyalty during the English Civil War, signifying it as a cherished, longstanding territory of the crown, and it's also a nod to its status as one of the oldest English overseas dominions. The name reflects Virginia's early colonial significance and its historical ties to the monarchy, appearing on colonial seals and adopted officially as a state slogan.

Chapter 73

  • Indiana's history spans from prehistoric Native American mounds to becoming the 19th state in 1816 and establishing itself as "The Crossroads of America" by 1937. Key milestones included significant Civil War participation, pioneering professional baseball in 1871, and becoming a manufacturing and agricultural hub.

Chapter 80

  • Interracial marriage in the United States has only been legal since the 1967 U.S. Supreme Court decision Loving v. Virginia that held anti-miscegenation laws were unconstitutional by the 14th Amendment established in 1868. The court's landmark decision has been commemorated and celebrated every year on the Loving Day, June 12th. The number of interracial marriages in 1967 was three percent. The first interracial marriage in what is today the United States was that of Pocahontas, who married tobacco planter John Rolfe in 1614. The first ever law prohibiting interracial marriage was passed by Maryland in 1691. The Quaker Zephaniah Kingsley married, outside the US, a black enslaved woman that he bought in Cuba. He also had three black common-law enslaved wives. He freed all four. They had to flee Florida for Haiti when it became a US territory. The prospect of black men marrying white women terrified many Americans before the Civil War. It was magnified into the greatest threat to society. The first legal black-white marriage in the United States was that of African-American professor William G. Allen and a white student, Mary King, in 1853. When their plans to marry were announced, Allen narrowly escaped being lynched. Their marriage was secret, and they left the country immediately for England, never to return. In 1957 actor Sammy Davis Jr. faced backlash for his relationship with a white woman, actress Kim Novak. In 1958, Davis briefly married a black woman, actress and dancer Loray White, to protect himself from mob violence.

Chapter 81

Other notable agents include WWII heroes Virginia Hall (Allied spy), Noor Inayat Khan (SOE wireless operator), and Nancy Wake ("The White Mouse"), alongside American Civil War scout Harriet Tubman.
