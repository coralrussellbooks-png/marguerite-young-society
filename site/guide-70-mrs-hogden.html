Research notes: Chapter 70

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Lisa Lunde · Miss MacIntosh · Mrs. Hogden · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Duality and Doubles

Symbols: The House · Twins and Doubles

Philosophy: Aristotle · Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Exploration and the Poles · The History of Opium

Things have quieted down at the Tavern. Vera is in her room at last, in the dark, in something approaching stillness. And so she tells you about Mrs. Hogden.

Mrs. Hogden is one of the strangest and most indelible figures in a novel full of them. She arrives in this chapter as a memory: Vera’s memory of a woman who appeared at her mother Catherine’s house years ago, burdened with packages and birdcages and Javanese temple bells and at least four coats of graduated sizes, smelling of over-ripe apples, her hair plastered and bleached into curls, her face blandly expressionless except for an aggressive chin and abnormally large, unfocused eyes. She had sold her Dutch windmill home to take up a position as Catherine’s arithmetic teacher, based on a letter of employment that Catherine denied ever writing.

Catherine did not want her. The household did not want her. Mrs. Hogden, with the serenity of a woman who has never once in her life accepted evidence that contradicted her expectations, stayed for months. Here is what you need to know before we begin: Vera tells you explicitly, partway through, that she never actually saw Mrs. Hogden. That Mrs. Hogden is “a trick of my imagination”: a substitute for the dream life she wanted, a figure her mind invented or elaborated or projected. “My mind’s betrayal of my mind,” she calls her.

So: everything that follows may be true. Everything that follows may be Vera’s dreaming. Young will not choose between these options for you. Neither should you choose for yourself. Hold both.

What Actually Happens

Mrs. Hogden (or Logden, or Fogden, or Dogden, or Bogden; the name slips) arrives at Catherine’s house in a two-door Ford, piled to the ceiling with her worldly goods. She has sold her home. She has come to be Catherine’s companion and arithmetic teacher. She has a letter of employment. Catherine has never heard of her.

Lisa Lunde, who replaced Vera as Catherine’s companion when Vera escaped, sizes Mrs. Hogden up immediately as a very dubious character, possibly a shoplifter. Catherine calls her “an impostor, a liar, a day-dreamer, a thief.” But Mrs. Hogden knows intimate details of Catherine’s inner life: details only Catherine could have shared, about whales swimming into her bedroom and the head of Alcibiades in the drainpipe. Catherine denies having shared them. She claims she has “gone to Boston to see a podiatrist,” or to the North Pole. She refuses to come out. Mrs. Hogden is installed in the deserted playroom: closed for years, filled with forgotten objects, umbrellas and old trunks and the detritus of a household that has been dreaming too long to keep things clean. She is given a hospital cot too short and too narrow. She does not complain. She has her chess set, her radio, her birdcage, her Javanese temple bells. She talks to herself and answers her own questions. She plays chess alone, crying over victories and defeats with equal feeling. She argues with radio announcers. She teaches arithmetic to no one, using wormy apples and later buttons as counters, because “two and two always making four” is, she believes, a triumph of reason.

Catherine’s revulsion grows. She cannot tolerate a rival invalid. She orders Mr. Spitzer, her lawyer, to remove Mrs. Hogden by any means necessary. He arranges a generous financial settlement. Mrs. Hogden leaves.

She leaves behind, on a small three-legged blackboard in red chalk: “All a’s are b’s,” it read, “all b’s are c’s—and therefore, dear heart, all a’s are c’s. You know me.”

The playroom is closed again. No one knows where she went. No one knows where she came from. She had no relatives, no friends anyone knew of. She vanished without trace, as she had arrived: out of nowhere, into nowhere, with the serenity of someone who has always known that the universe had no particular obligation to explain itself.

Mrs. Hogden

She is both comic and tragic, which is to say she is one of Young’s most fully achieved characters. Comic: her four graduated coats, her aggressive chin bow, her empty birdcage, her argument with the radio announcer about the stock market, her chess set with an atomizer substituting for a bishop, her claim to be descended from Daniel Boone, William the Conqueror, and Alexander Graham Bell, as well as “three signers of the Declaration of Independence.” Tragic: her Dutch windmill home sold and gone, her worldly goods in a two-door Ford, her hospital cot too short and too narrow, her chess played alone with tears, her unshakeable faith that she had been invited somewhere she was not wanted.

She is described as the “least critical human being”, not as a compliment, exactly, but as a kind of stunned acknowledgment. She does not judge. She does not resist. She adjusts, permanently, to whatever temporary situation she finds herself in, and finds within it the materials for contentment. She pulls an imaginary bellrope to summon servants who do not come. She imagines social seasons in an empty playroom. She is the mistress of the house in a house that has forgotten her name.

She is also, Young suggests through the accumulation of detail, a figure for something larger: the American self-myth, the national character at its most buoyant and most deluded. She claims the founding fathers. She insists on arithmetic (two and two making four, the simplest truth, the foundation of all others) while inhabiting a world that has made no sense for decades. She collects things from everywhere (Dutch windmill, Javanese bells, ivory back-scratcher) without integrating any of them. She is “permanently adjusted to her temporary situation.” She does not hear arguments to their close, and therefore wins them all.

She is, in her way, the Anti-Miss-MacIntosh: where Miss MacIntosh was Miss Reality, Miss Plain-and-Average and Clear-Eyed, Mrs. Hogden is Miss Optimism, Miss The-Evidence-Will-Not-Discourage-Me, Miss The-Universe-Will-Accommodate-My-Expectations. They are the novel’s two poles of response to a world that does not cooperate. Miss MacIntosh’s solution was to hold to the surface of things, the facts, the cold and clear. Her solution ultimately failed her, as we will learn. Mrs. Hogden’s solution is to simply not see the contradiction. It works, in its way. She leaves, eventually, with a generous settlement and an intact worldview.

Catherine, Confronted with Her Double

This is what makes the chapter so rich and so strange: Catherine, who lives entirely inside her own hallucinations, who has taken to her bed and left it only in imagination, who has lost her mind “several times” and considers herself not responsible for her actions, Catherine finds Mrs. Hogden intolerable. She calls her “a gross, stinking parody” of her own “distended world of shimmering, loosely woven textures.”

She is right. Mrs. Hogden is a parody of Catherine: the same disconnection from reality, the same confidence in a world that doesn’t exist, but made common, made corporeal, made loud and smelling of over-ripe apples. Catherine’s madness is refined, beautiful in its way, private, the madness of a woman with expensive sheets and an opium habit and the memory of better days. Mrs. Hogden’s madness is cheerful, baggy, layered in four coats, eating wormy apples in a playroom.

What Catherine cannot forgive is the recognition. Mrs. Hogden knows things about her (the whale in the bedroom, the head of Alcibiades in the drainpipe) that only Catherine could have shared. Which means Catherine, in some moment she does not remember or refuses to admit, reached out to Mrs. Hogden. Called to her. Invited her. The letter exists. Catherine denies it. The denial is her madness and also her dignity and also her cruelty: she invited someone in and then refused to know her. She also mistakes Mrs. Hogden, at moments, for her dead Cousin Hannah. Identity is fluid in this house. Everyone substitutes for someone else.

Lisa Lunde

Lisa Lunde is Vera’s replacement: the young companion who came to take care of Catherine when Vera escaped. She is a would-be physicist working to support her own poor and mentally ill mother; she has entered one dreamhouse in order to fund her life outside it. She is sharp, skeptical, and not taken in: she sizes Mrs. Hogden up as a dubious character within minutes and never entirely revises this assessment.

But she is also, quietly, vengeful. When Catherine blames her for Mrs. Hogden’s presence and demands she get rid of her, Lisa Lunde runs back and forth between them with meaningless messages, deliberately not interpreting either one to the other, watching the situation escalate with something that functions as satisfaction. She is the trapped rationalist, the person who sees clearly and is therefore left to manage the chaos that the dreamers create, and finds her small pleasures where she can.

She will reappear later in the novel. Remember her: the woman who wanted to be a physicist and ended up carrying messages between two invalids who refused to communicate.

The Playroom

The room Mrs. Hogden is given, and Young spends time on this, is the deserted playroom: closed for years, filled with things the household no longer uses and can’t bring itself to throw away. Old trunks, umbrellas, boxes, broken objects, the accumulated sediment of a family that has been dreaming instead of living. It is a room for things that have no place and no owner, which makes it exactly right for Mrs. Hogden.

Moonlight comes in and silvers the walls. Mrs. Hogden sits under a frieze of gargoyles. The novel notes, without elaborating, that if she sat there “long enough, she would become a gargoyle herself”: stone, permanent, grotesque, part of the architecture. It is both a joke and a precise description of what happens to people who stay too long in houses that are dreaming.

The evidence she leaves behind when she finally goes: “derelict whiskey bottles, empty coat hangers, withered apple cores, broken” eggshells, dead flies, and, Vera notes specifically, an artificial eyelash. The precise inventory of a life that was here and isn’t.

The Blackboard

She leaves behind a small three-legged blackboard, the kind a child’s teacher might use. On it, in red chalk: “All a’s are b’s,” it read, “all b’s are c’s—and therefore, dear heart, all a’s are c’s. You know me.”

The logic is valid. If all a’s are b’s, and all b’s are c’s, then yes: all a’s are c’s. This is how syllogisms work. This is the foundation of reason. Two and two make four.

But “dear heart” is not logic. “You know me” is not logic. The equation ends with a personal claim (a claim of recognition, of intimacy, of being known across the gap of unreality) that the syllogism cannot support. She is saying: I am the conclusion of a chain of reasoning you have already accepted. You know me. You have always known me.

Vera cannot refute this. Neither can we. The woman who was never there has left proof of her own logic on a three-legged blackboard in a closed room. The proof is sound. The proof is absurd. Both things are true.

Vera’s Confession

Partway through this chapter, Vera tells you that she never saw Mrs. Hogden. That the whole thing (or some of it, or most of it) is a trick of her imagination. A substitute for what she actually wanted, which was a lost companion, the other half of a self that has only ever loved its own right hand.

This is one of the novel’s most important moments of self-disclosure. Vera is not a reliable narrator, not in the sense of lying, but in the sense that her consciousness is the medium through which everything is filtered, and that medium is not neutral. It is imaginative, it is obsessive, it is organized around absence. The people in this novel are sometimes real and sometimes Vera’s projections and sometimes both. Miss MacIntosh is both. Mrs. Hogden is both.

What Vera is telling you is not that Mrs. Hogden didn’t exist. She is telling you that the distinction between “real” and “imagined” has never been, for her, the distinction that matters. “There was only one definition of reality: that it was real.” “Even the unreal things were real.” Even the imagined companion is a companion. Even Mrs. Hogden, if she waddled in and out of Vera’s dreams, was there.

What to Hold Onto

Mrs. Hogden’s four graduated coats, her birdcage, her Javanese temple bells. The physical accumulation of a person who carries everything with her because she has nowhere left to put it down.

Catherine and Mrs. Hogden as mirror images: the private dreamer and the public one, the refined madness and the corpulent one, both refusing reality, neither able to bear the sight of the other.

Lisa Lunde, running between them with meaningless messages, not translating, watching the situation with quiet satisfaction. She wanted to be a physicist. She is here instead.

The blackboard: “All a’s are b’s,” it read, “all b’s are c’s—and therefore, dear heart, all a’s are c’s. You know me.”

And Vera’s confession, which is not a confession of lying but of imagination: that Mrs. Hogden is her mind’s betrayal of her mind, a substitute for what she always wanted and never found, the companion that the self invents when no real companion appears. She is looking for Miss MacIntosh. She has been, this whole novel, looking for Miss MacIntosh. What she finds instead, sometimes, is Mrs. Hogden. The next best thing. The wrong answer that has the shape of the right one.
