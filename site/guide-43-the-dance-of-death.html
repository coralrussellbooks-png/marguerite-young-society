Chapter 43: The Dance of Death

← Chapter 42 · Guide Home · Chapter 44 →

Research notes: Chapter 43

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

Themes: Death and Mortality · Decay and Ruin · Love · The Search for Meaning

Symbols: Clothing, Hats, and Skirts

Philosophy: Freud and Jung · Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Greek Mythology · Women's Suffrage

If Chapter 42 brought you to the room where Cousin Hannah is dying, Chapter 43 keeps you there. It does not let you leave. And what it shows you, hour by hour in the long unraveling of a great life, is that dying is not peaceful and it is not dignified and it does not sort itself into the clean stages of acceptance that we would prefer it to have. Cousin Hannah is screaming. She is delirious. She is asking, in the chaos of her final consciousness, the most fundamental question available to anyone: “am I the anvil which is struck or the hammer which strikes?”

Young gives her this question at the end of everything. It is the question of agency itself, of whether a life is something that happens to you or something you do. And the answer, as Cousin Hannah has always known and the delirium now makes impossible to avoid, is that it is both. It was always both. The hammer and the anvil are the same person. The blow falls and is received simultaneously. This is what a life is.

What actually happens in Chapter 43

We remain at Cousin Hannah's deathbed. Mr. Spitzer maintains his vigil, wearing “his high silk hat and black” cape: the full formal costume of mourning, as if sartorial correctness can hold something steady in a room where everything else is dissolving. He is not sure, he tells us, whether he himself is living or dead. The boundary he has been approaching all his life has become, at this bedside, genuinely unclear.

Cousin Hannah's dying is not a drift toward peace. It is a storm. It is a dance: Young uses the medieval term danse macabre, the dance of death, where Death comes for everyone regardless of rank or accomplishment and leads them in an inescapable procession. But Cousin Hannah does not go quietly. She screams through dreamlike, surreal landscapes: deserts, mountains, clouds, ice. She speaks to Caesar, to a blacksmith, to a golden archer, to the blackamoor she calls her love.

She asks for a mirror and cannot find one. She clings to the vanity of silken skirts and great parasols fringed with gold: beauty, adornment, the things she spent her public life refusing to care about, surfacing now in the delirium as desires she had apparently always had. She is fragile as all mortal things, weeping because “a wandering snowflake touched her hand.”

The chapter closes without closing. Mr. Spitzer keeps watch. The lid (is it lowering or raising?) remains ambiguous. Death is not peace. Death is the great crucible where opposites meet. Death is “that which blew us all along.”

The hammer and the anvil

Cousin Hannah's question (“am I the anvil which is struck or the hammer which strikes?”) is Chapter 43's philosophical center, and it arrives exactly when it should: at the moment when every certainty she built her life around is dissolving.

In Isaiah 41, the craftsmen are encouraged when the hammer and the anvil work together to make an object. In ancient Greece, Hephaestus (the disabled god, the one thrown from Olympus) was the divine blacksmith, the maker of things, the one who transformed raw material into form by striking it repeatedly. The hammer creates. The anvil receives. Both are necessary. Neither the hammer alone nor the anvil alone makes anything.

Cousin Hannah spent her life as a hammer. She struck. She forged. She beat the soft metal of convention into new shapes through sheer relentless force. And now, in the delirium, she discovers that she was also always the anvil: the thing being shaped by forces larger than herself, by the history she was born into and the body she inhabited and the losses she sustained and the love she never quite allowed herself to have. She struck and was struck. She made and was made.

Young does not offer this as a diminishment. The hammer and the anvil together make the thing. Both matter. The question is not which one you are (you are both, always) but whether you can hold that knowledge at the end, when the forge is cooling and the work is done and the hands are finally still.

The blackamoor and the love she called her own

In her delirium, Cousin Hannah calls repeatedly for a blackamoor: a figure she addresses as her love. This is one of the chapter's most charged and carefully handled passages.

The term blackamoor, as Young uses it, carries the weight of the novel's long engagement with Cousin Hannah's identity as Hannah in Boston, Hamid in the East, Al Hamid. The figure she loves in the delirium is associated with the same crossing of cultural and religious borders that has defined her life: the Moors, the Islamic world she moved through, the identities she acquired in her travels and may have briefly inhabited. The blackamoor is both a specific person she loved (perhaps the secret lover, perhaps the caliph who supported her cause) and something larger: the part of the world she went toward when she went away from New England, the self she became when she left the self she was supposed to be.

To All My Darlings notes that the black moor becomes a black rooster in the chapter's imagery. The rooster crows at dawn, at the threshold between darkness and light, between death and whatever comes after. Cousin Hannah's love, in her dying hours, is also the thing that might guide her through.

Mr. Spitzer's hat, and the question of being alive

Mr. Spitzer arrives at this deathbed in “his high silk hat and black” cape, and the image is so precisely chosen that it deserves a moment.

The high silk hat was a clear symbol of authority, wealth, and prestige: the hat of capitalism, of enterprise, of Victorian and Edwardian gravitas. It represents everything Mr. Spitzer has always valued: appearance, dignity, the surface held intact. He wears it to a deathbed where the dying woman is screaming. The hat cannot help him. It cannot help her. But he puts it on anyway because it is the only armor he knows how to wear.

He is not sure, in this chapter, whether he himself is living or dead. He reflects “that the dead cast no vote—and” uses this, along with his general policy of non-competitive withdrawal from life, as a reason for his habitual abstention. He is a man so thoroughly at a second remove from experience that the question of whether he is technically alive has become genuinely open to him.

What he does, in this ambiguous state, is watch. He keeps vigil. He is present for Cousin Hannah's dying in a way he has not been present for much else in his life, and Young seems to regard this (the faithful keeping of watch, even when you cannot help, even when you are not sure you exist) as a form of love available even to the most constrained and self-evacuated people.

The dance of death

The danse macabre, the dance of death, was a medieval and Renaissance artistic tradition depicting Death as a skeletal figure leading a procession of people from all walks of life: pope and peasant, king and merchant, young and old, the great and the forgotten. Death does not care about rank. Everyone dances.

Young's invocation of this tradition for Cousin Hannah's dying is pointed. Here is a great captain, a warrior, a woman who faced greater dangers than most men knew, and Death comes for her the same way it comes for everyone: uninvited, insistent, indifferent to her accomplishments. The dance is compulsory. The music does not stop for negotiations.

And yet the dance, in Cousin Hannah's case, is her own. She does not go gently. Her dying has the quality of her living: excessive, stormy, full of voices and visions and the unfinished business of a life that was never quite finished with anything. She dances with Caesar. She dances with the blacksmith and the golden archer. She dances with the love she never quite named. She takes the hand of Death in the costume of the blackamoor and goes, not quietly, but going.

The lid: lowering or raising

Young gives Mr. Spitzer a question he cannot answer: is the lid lowering or raising?

He is watching something close, and he does not know whether it is closing a life or opening something else. The coffin lid lowering is the most legible image of finality available to the culture Young is writing in. But the chapter has been insisting, since its first pages, that death is not finality; it is transformation, it is storm, it is a great crucible where opposites meet. Death is “that which blew us all along.”

The eternalism note in To All My Darlings is relevant here: some philosophers of time argue that all moments exist equally, that the past is not gone but simply located elsewhere in the block universe, that the dead are not absent but present at a different coordinate. Cousin Hannah's dying is, in this framework, not a disappearance but a permanent fixture of what exists. The moment of her death exists as surely as the moment of her first campaign speech, her first border crossing, the first time she “played a mean game of cards” and won.

The lid. Is it lowering or raising? Mr. Spitzer does not know. Young does not tell him. The question stays open like a door that swings both ways.

Vanity at the end

One of Chapter 43's most humanizing and unexpected details is the vanity that surfaces in Cousin Hannah's delirium: the silken skirts, the great parasols fringed with gold, the desire for a mirror. She has spent decades refusing these things: refusing the feminine decorations of her era, refusing self-pity, refusing the courtly graces and intimate revelations. And here they all are, returning in the delirium like furniture she thought she'd given away and finds packed in a box at the back of the attic.

Young is not making a point about the shallowness of her convictions. She is making a point about the depth of what we suppress. The things we refuse do not disappear. They wait. The suffrage captain who had no time for silken skirts had, apparently, wanted them all along, or wanted something they represent: beauty, attention, the pleasure of being seen as a woman rather than only as a warrior. The delirium gives her back what life refused.

She “wept if a wandering snowflake touched her hand.” She was fragile as all mortal things. She always was. She simply did not allow the evidence to be entered into the record.

What to hold onto

Chapter 43 is the deathbed chapter, and it is long in the way that deaths are long, and difficult in the way that watching is difficult. What Young asks of you here is patience and presence: the same things Mr. Spitzer is managing, with his silk hat and his uncertain existence, at the side of the bed.

The hammer and the anvil. The snowflake and the wept-for hand. The blackamoor and the love she called her own. The lid that might be lowering or raising. These are what Cousin Hannah takes with her into whatever comes next, if anything comes next, if the lid is raising, if the storm she is dancing through leads somewhere rather than ending.

Young does not promise it leads somewhere. But she does not promise it doesn't. The question is the chapter. The question is the novel. The question is what we have been asking together since page one.
