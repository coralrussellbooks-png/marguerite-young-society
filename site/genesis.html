Definition from research

Chapter 2

  • Jacob’s Ladder is a ladder leading to heaven that was featured in a dream the biblical Patriarch Jacob had during his flight from his brother Esau in the Book of Genesis.

Chapter 5

  • “is the blood of Abel crying from the ground that I should live to see these bloody flowers?” - This may refer to the story of Cain and Abel from Genesis 1-4 where Abel’s blood carries a power that doesn’t let Cain settle again on the earth. The first human death could symbolize the future violence which pollutes the earth. God uses a flood later on in Genesis to purify the earth. This phrase encapsulates Vera’s anguished contemplation of human suffering’s enduring cry through history and its impact on her perception of life and the world around her.

Chapter 7

  • In the Bible, “headstone” primarily refers to the “principal stone in a foundation,” or the cornerstone, signifying strength, stability, and alignment. Specifically, it’s often associated with Jesus Christ, who is described as the cornerstone upon which believers are built. In a broader sense, headstones can also refer to memorial markers at graves, symbolizing remembrance and faith. (Psalm 118:22; Acts 4:11; 1 Peter 2:7; Genesis 28:18-22; Joshua 4:1-8).

Chapter 8

  • “Was I not made to be the king of the earth?”: Genesis 1:26 reads, “let them have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air.” This question is a poignant expression of human aspiration confronted by existential despair and the limitations imposed by mortality and circumstance. It highlights themes of lost potential, the transience of life, and the universal confrontation with death that pervades the novel.
  • “bone of my bone”: Adam says of Eve, “This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh” (Genesis 2:23).

Chapter 9

  • “Desire be to thy husband”: God tells Eve, “thy desire shall be to thy husband” (Genesis 3:16).
  • “the apple was no sweet mackintosh”: The McIntosh apple, found in Ontario in 1811, carries Miss MacIntosh’s name. Genesis never names the fruit; the apple came from the Latin pun malum, “apple” and “evil.”
  • “God, Who had divided the light from the darkness and created great whales”: “And God created great whales . . . Be fruitful, and multiply, and fill the waters in the seas” (Genesis 1:21-22).

Chapter 11

  • “a society of night devoted exclusively to atheistic propositions”: “The school of night” comes from Love’s Labour’s Lost (4.3): “Black is the badge of hell, / The hue of dungeons and the school of night.” The phrase became a name for Raleigh’s circle with Thomas Harriot and Christopher Marlowe, men accused of doubting Genesis and God. In 1594 an inquiry at Cerne Abbas examined charges of atheism against Raleigh’s circle.

Chapter 12

  • “darkness moved on the face of the deep”: “And darkness was upon the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters” (Genesis 1:2).
  • “filled with the greatest remorse”: “And it repented the Lord that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him at his heart” (Genesis 6:6).

Chapter 15

  • “darkness without face and void upon the deep”: “And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep” (Genesis 1:2).
  • “the dark angel of death with whom a prophet met upon a barren hill”: Perhaps Jacob, who wrestled the angel through the night at Peniel (Genesis 32:24), though Young’s prophet meets “the dark angel of death” upon a barren hill.
  • “Men are fallen”: The Fall of man is the Christian term for the passage of the first man and woman from innocent obedience to God to guilty disobedience.

Chapter 17

  • “even that holy angel with whom Jacob struggled at Peniel”: Genesis 32:22-32 and Hosea 12:3-5: “And Jacob was left alone; and there wrestled a man with him until the breaking of the day” (Genesis 32:24).

Chapter 18

  • “some angelic demon”: Young may be referring to the Nephilim. In Genesis 6:4, they are born when “the sons of God came in unto the daughters of men.” Later legend made them the children of fallen angels and mortal women, at once angelic and demonic.
  • “What if the whole world should turn to salt?”: Lot’s wife “became a pillar of salt” (Genesis 19:26).

Chapter 20

  • “the experimental light of the first day of crazed creation”: “And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters. And God said, Let there be light: and there was light” (Genesis 1:2-3).
  • “the names of the antediluvian patriarchs”: The antediluvian patriarchs are the individuals listed in Genesis 5 who lived before the Noachian flood: Adam, Seth, Enosh, Kenan, Mahalalel, Jared, Enoch, Methuselah, Lamech, and Noah. They represent the lineage of humanity before the Great Flood. Methuselah lived “nine hundred sixty and nine years” (Genesis 5:27).
  • “the dead tree of life”: “The tree of life also in the midst of the garden” (Genesis 2:9).

Chapter 22

  • “what God had created on Wednesday”: “And God made two great lights; the greater light to rule the day, and the lesser light to rule the night: he made the stars also” (Genesis 1:16).
  • “were created on Thursday last. I think it must have been around four o’clock in the afternoon”: “And God created great whales” (Genesis 1:21). Vera’s mock precision recalls Archbishop James Ussher, who dated the creation to the evening before October 23, 4004 BC (Annales Veteris Testamenti, 1650).
  • “by the sweat of my brow”: “In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread” (Genesis 3:19).
  • “the firmament in the midst of the waters to divide the waters from the waters”: “Let there be a firmament in the midst of the waters, and let it divide the waters from the waters” (Genesis 1:6-7).
  • “God created man on Friday, the sixth day”: “And the evening and the morning were the sixth day” (Genesis 1:31).
  • “a confusion of tongues, the very Tower of Babel”: “Therefore is the name of it called Babel; because the Lord did there confound the language of all the earth” (Genesis 11:9).
  • “Where is Mt. Ararat”: “The ark rested… upon the mountains of Ararat” (Genesis 8:4).

Chapter 23

  • “Am I my brother’s keeper?”: Genesis 4:9. Miss MacIntosh asks what Mr. Spitzer might. It resonates with his struggle with his sense of duty, guilt, and responsibility for his brother’s legacy and the lives entangled in their shared history. The question also connects to broader existential concerns about individual responsibility in a chaotic and often indifferent world. The novel explores themes of illusion, memory, and the blurred boundaries between life and death, self and other. It asks whether one is truly responsible for the fate, suffering, or salvation of another, particularly a close other like a brother, and by extension, for others in one’s life or society. It highlights the novel’s exploration of moral responsibility, the limits of that responsibility, and the intimate yet often painful ties that bind individuals to one another in a world marked by loss, deception, and uncertain reality.
  • “winter wore its coat of many colors”: Joseph’s “coat of many colours” (Genesis 37:3).

Chapter 24

  • “believed he was the spouse to Potiphar, the Egyptian captain”: Potiphar’s wife tried to seduce Joseph and then accused him (Genesis 39). The gardener believes he is the wife.

Chapter 26

  • “turning to dust and ashes”: “For dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return” (Genesis 3:19), and the burial service’s “earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust” (Book of Common Prayer, 1549).

Chapter 28

  • “walking in the cool of the evening shade”: “They heard the voice of the Lord God walking in the garden in the cool of the day” (Genesis 3:8).

Chapter 29

  • “brilliance as of the jewels of Aaron’s breastplate, colors like Japheth’s enlarged opals”: Aaron’s breastplate (Priestly breastplate) was a sacred breastplate worn by the high priest of the Israelites. Its twelve stones stood for the twelve tribes (Exodus 28:15-21).
  • “before the separation of the waters from the land, before the division of light from darkness”: “God divided the light from the darkness… and let the dry land appear” (Genesis 1:4, 9).
  • “like Jacob with his ladder of dreams”: Jacob’s Ladder is a ladder leading to heaven that was featured in a dream the biblical Patriarch Jacob had during his flight from his brother Esau in the Book of Genesis 28. The significance of the dream has been debated, but most interpretations agree that it identified Jacob with the obligations and inheritance of the people chosen by God, as understood in Abrahamic religions. It is significant as a symbol of transcendence and the complex interplay between different realms of existence, reflecting the novel’s broader themes of spiritual searching, change, and the quest for understanding beyond the physical world.

Chapter 34

  • The chapter’s thoughts about man fly in the face of the patrilineal succession where men serve as heads of almost every important social, cultural, and political institution and their name is passed down through children. These things are thought to pass on a legacy of power for men and their male heirs. In Genesis God creates divisions between light and dark, water and earth, male and female and he puts man to oversee it all and keep it in order.

Chapter 40

  • Tower of Babel in Genesis 11 is an origin myth to explain why the world’s peoples speak so many different languages.

Chapter 49

  • “But why should any man weep if the heavens wept for him?” Found in Islamic, Ancient Egyptian, and Jewish tradition, it is a metaphorical way to express profound sorrow and the impact of a loss on a cosmic scale. Moses 7, Jeremiah 8:19, Genesis 6:6, John 11:35 It reflects a profound meditation on sorrow, mourning, and the vastness of grief that transcends individual human experience. This rhetorical question suggests that if the heavens themselves are mourning a person’s fate, then the individual weeping might seem redundant or insignificant in comparison. It evokes the idea that cosmic forces or the universe acknowledge and encompass the sorrow of human lives on a scale far greater than any one person could express. This notion ties into the novel’s theme of the interconnectedness of human suffering with the natural and cosmic order. The mourning of the heavens symbolizes a universal sympathy and recognition of loss, making individual grief a part of a larger, perhaps more meaningful, cosmic process. It also hints at the shared destiny of all beings, where personal sorrow is enveloped within the universal cycles of life and death. The question serves to elevate the concept of mourning beyond the personal, suggesting that human tears are part of a grander, universal lamentation that the heavens themselves embody. It invites the reader to contemplate the scale of grief and the place of individual suffering within the vastness of existence.

Chapter 53

  • In Genesis 32:22-32 Jacob wrestles with an angel.

Chapter 53

  • “he knew the Jew who was knocked out by an angel” - It is an allusion rich with symbolic and possibly biblical meaning. Although the text does not provide explicit context for this phrase, it likely references the biblical story of Jacob wrestling with an angel, a well-known episode from the Book of Genesis 32. In that story, Jacob wrestles with a mysterious divine being (often interpreted as an angel or God himself) throughout the night and is physically and spiritually transformed by the encounter. The phrase "knocked out by an angel" suggests a powerful, overwhelming, possibly humbling or transformative experience. Within the context of chapter and the broader themes of the novel, the reference may symbolize a profound confrontation with truth, fate, or spiritual challenge. It could reflect a moment where a character is metaphorically 'knocked out' or overwhelmed by a higher power, a revelation, or an internal struggle. Given the novel’s dense and poetic style, filled with dreamlike, mythic, and spiritual imagery, this phrase serves as a metaphor for a transformative or decisive encounter that shakes the foundations of identity or belief.

Chapter 53

  • This may refer to Genesis 32 that describes Jacob wrestling with a "man" throughout the night. In the Book of Hosea, this figure is explicitly called an "angel". During the struggle, Jacob is injured, specifically having the socket of his hip put out of joint. This injury left him with a limp. Jacob is considered a foundational figure in Judaism and the patriarch from whom the nation of Israel descends.

Chapter 62

  • Haydn’s Creation or The Creation is an oratorio written by Haydn and considered to be one of his masterpieces. It is a celebration of the creation of the world as in the Book of Genesis.

Chapter 63

  • Noah is the last of the pre-flood patriarchs of the Book of Genesis. He builds an ark and takes pairs of animals in order to survive the great flood. The biblical story of Noah's Ark signifies a profound message about divine judgment, mercy, and salvation. It illustrates that God judges wickedness and sin but also provides a way for salvation for those who are righteous, like Noah, and those who have faith. The story highlights themes of God's faithfulness through the covenant of the rainbow, and in Christian theology, the ark is often seen as a foreshadowing of Christ as the ultimate source of salvation.

Chapter 64

  • Eden is the Garden of Eden described as the biblical paradise in Genesis. The Tree of Knowledge is one of two specific trees in the story of the Garden of Eden in Genesis 2–3, along with the tree of life. Alternatively, some scholars have argued that the tree of the knowledge of good and evil is just another name for the tree of life.

Chapter 65

  • In the Bible, Ham, one of Noah's three sons, had four sons: Cush, Mizraim, Phut, and Canaan, who became ancestors of peoples in Africa and the Middle East, particularly Ethiopia, Egypt, Libya, and Canaan (modern Israel/Palestine). The story in Genesis details how Ham saw his father Noah naked while drunk, leading Noah to curse Ham's son, Canaan, rather than Ham himself, a narrative known as the Curse of Ham. After the Flood, Noah became drunk and lay uncovered in his tent. Ham saw his father's nakedness and told his brothers, Shem and Japheth. Shem and Japheth, however, respectfully covered Noah without looking. Noah, upon waking, cursed Canaan (Ham's son) to be a "servant of servants" to his brothers, a passage later misused to justify slavery.

Chapter 68

  • Christian vegetarianism is the practice of abstaining from meat for faith-based reasons, focusing on themes like stewardship of creation, compassion, health, and honoring God, though the Bible permits meat-eating, with varied interpretations on animal rights and the ideal diet (some pointing to Genesis's plant-based start, others to Jesus eating fish). While not a universal command, it's seen as a way to live out Christian values. Vegetarians and especially vegans are more likely to be low in vitamin D, zinc, anemic, suffer from anxiety and depression and have eating disorders. Also not eating enough protein can make a person crave protein. These cravings have been documented with castaways surviving at sea. A vegan diet can be deficient in the following essential nutrients: Vitamin B12, Vitamin D, Omega-3 fatty acids (especially EPA and DHA), iron, calcium, zinc, iodine, and protein. Although supplements and fortified foods can be taken, these nutrients come from animal meat. Meat protein is vital for building tissues, producing hormones, and supporting immunity, offering complete essential amino acids and highly absorbable micronutrients like iron, zinc, and B vitamins, crucial for growth, muscle maintenance, cognitive function, and preventing deficiencies, especially in children, women, and the elderly.

Chapter 69

  • Joseph’s coat of many colors is from the Book of Genesis. It was a special, richly decorated robe given to him by his father Jacob, symbolizing his favoritism, which fueled his brothers' jealousy, leading them to sell Joseph into slavery and present the bloody coat to Jacob as proof he was dead. The garment, likely a regal tunic with long sleeves, signified Joseph's special status and set him apart, though it also provoked envy, highlighting themes of favoritism, betrayal, and God's guiding purpose through hardship.

Chapter 71

  • Simeon is derived from Simeon, son of Jacob and Leah, patriarch of the Tribe of Simeon. The text of Genesis 29:33 argues that the name refers to Leah's belief that God had heard that she was hated by Jacob, in the sense of not being as favored as Rachel.

Chapter 74

  • “For everything was so sad, and the sky bled in her dreams, and she had no menstrual period, and she was always pregnant. Who was innocent? Who was guilty? Her own childhood reproached her.” This section may refer to the death of Esther’s baby when she was young and how that traumatized her even now. Rape has been used as a plot device in literature and other media. It has been criticized for using rape as a metaphor that displaces the actual, violent and traumatic act of rape. It is considered misogynistic in the way female rape victims continue to be cast as collateral damage and the assault and rape of women is used as a plot point in a narrative that is fundamentally about men. Young is making this about Esther and showing how things would be different if she were a man. Young makes many references to mythology. The struggle to escape from sexual pursuit is one of the most popular motifs of Greek mythology: Persephone, Callisto, Daphne, Europa, Io, etc. Greek mythology depicts women as vulnerable to assault, able to escape only through death, or metamorphosis like Daphne who becomes a laurel tree. The consequence of fleeing sexual violence is another type of force on the body, the loss of the human form where women who survive sexual violence become pregnant, and when gods as the rapists, produce a hero child. The Chicago detective is described as raping Esther because he thinks she’s dead. Chione in Greek mythology was said to be so beautiful that she was the object of a thousand men's desire. Hermes used magic to make Chione to fall into a deep sleep and proceeded to rape her. Since Young has said Esther is the mother of us all and Mr. Spitzer as the father of us all, these could be archetypes for Adam and Eve and possibly Lilith. In On the Origin of World, part of the Nag Hammadi Library, Eve is described as being raped by archangels and bears children. In the Kabbalist myth Eve is raped by Lilith, Adam’s first wife, in the guise of Samael, Lilith’s demon lover or Satan. The Kabbalists continued the story to include Eve, through her menses, to have defiled Adam which then lead to Lilith being able to rape Adam, become impregnated and birth Lilin or demons by Adam.

Chapter 77

  • “...was natural and a mother should suffer—she would just deliver the child then in such a way that it would be born dead —and would kill the mother—but only after it had been the agony of birth.” This may refer to Genesis 3:16 as Eve and women’s punishment for being cast out of the Garden of Eden.
