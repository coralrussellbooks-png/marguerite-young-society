Chapter 5

  • “is the blood of Abel crying from the ground that I should live to see these bloody flowers?” - This may refer to the story of Cain and Abel from Genesis 1-4 where Abel’s blood carries a power that doesn’t let Cain settle again on the earth. The first human death could symbolize the future violence which pollutes the earth. God uses a flood later on in Genesis to purify the earth. This phrase encapsulates Vera’s anguished contemplation of human suffering’s enduring cry through history and its impact on her perception of life and the world around her.

In a broader sense, headstones can also refer to memorial markers at graves, symbolizing remembrance and faith. Psalm 118:22, Acts 4:11, 1 Peter 2:7, Genesis 28:18-22, Joshua 4:1-8

Genesis, where a beka of gold was given to Rebecca in a nose ring. Exodus 38:26

Chapter 22

  • “The first thrashing had its origin, I have no doubt, in heaven, and one fell.” - Genesis 50:10 It is a symbolic expression reflecting the theme of a cosmic or spiritual fall that initiates suffering and disorder in the world. This "first thrashing" can be interpreted as the original fall or disruption that originated in a heavenly or divine realm, suggesting a primordial rupture or failure that reverberates through creation. The phrase "and one fell" likely alludes to the fall of an angel or a spiritual being, commonly associated with the fall of Lucifer or a similar figure, who descended from a state of grace or divinity into a state of corruption or exile. This fall is seen as the root cause of the imperfections and suffering in the created world. Within the context of the novel, this line emphasizes the idea that the chaos, suffering, and moral struggles experienced by the characters are connected to a larger metaphysical or cosmic event that originated beyond the earth. It signifies that the troubles in human life are reflections or consequences of this primordial "fall" or "thrashing" in heaven, marking the beginning of a long history of struggle between good and evil, order and chaos. The phrase encapsulates the novel's exploration of spiritual fallenness as the origin of earthly suffering and the complex interplay between divine intention and human experience.

Chapter 22

  • Togarmah - “is a figure in the Generations of Noah in the Book of Genesis that represents the peoples known to the Hebrews. Togarmah is among the descendants of Japheth and is thought to represent some people located in Anatolia. Medieval sources claimed that Togarmah was the legendary ancestor of several ethnic groups in the Caucasus, including Armenians and Georgians.” He was believed to have 10 sons.

Chapter 23

  • “God lighted the darkness” - Genesis 1:3 It encapsulates a moment or idea of transcendence, an acknowledgment that even in the bleakest moments of human experience or the “darkness” of suffering and death there is a possibility of divine light or spiritual awakening. It is a poetic way of expressing hope, mystery, and the complex interplay of life and death that Vera contemplates as she observes her mother’s condition and the surrounding dream-like reality.

Chapter 23

  • “Am I my brother’s keeper?” - Genesis 4:9 It resonates with Mr. Spitzer’s struggle with his sense of duty, guilt, and responsibility for his brother’s legacy and the lives entangled in their shared history. The question also connects to broader existential concerns about individual responsibility in a chaotic and often indifferent world. The novel explores themes of illusion, memory, and the blurred boundaries between life and death, self and other. It asks whether one is truly responsible for the fate, suffering, or salvation of another, particularly a close other like a brother, and by extension, for others in one’s life or society. It highlights the novel’s exploration of moral responsibility, the limits of that responsibility, and the intimate yet often painful ties that bind individuals to one another in a world marked by loss, deception, and uncertain reality.

Chapter 29

  • Jacob’s Ladder is a ladder leading to heaven that was featured in a dream the biblical Patriarch Jacob had during his flight from his brother Esau in the Book of Genesis 28. It is significant as a symbol of transcendence and the complex interplay between different realms of existence, reflecting the novel’s broader themes of spiritual searching, change, and the quest for understanding beyond the physical world.

Chapter 49

  • “But why should any man weep if the heavens wept for him?” Found in Islamic, Ancient Egyptian, and Jewish tradition, it is a metaphorical way to express profound sorrow and the impact of a loss on a cosmic scale. Moses 7, Jeremiah 8:19, Genesis 6:6, John 11:35 It reflects a profound meditation on sorrow, mourning, and the vastness of grief that transcends individual human experience. This rhetorical question suggests that if the heavens themselves are mourning a person’s fate, then the individual weeping might seem redundant or insignificant in comparison. It evokes the idea that cosmic forces or the universe acknowledge and encompass the sorrow of human lives on a scale far greater than any one person could express. This notion ties into the novel’s theme of the interconnectedness of human suffering with the natural and cosmic order. The mourning of the heavens symbolizes a universal sympathy and recognition of loss, making individual grief a part of a larger, perhaps more meaningful, cosmic process. It also hints at the shared destiny of all beings, where personal sorrow is enveloped within the universal cycles of life and death. The question serves to elevate the concept of mourning beyond the personal, suggesting that human tears are part of a grander, universal lamentation that the heavens themselves embody. It invites the reader to contemplate the scale of grief and the place of individual suffering within the vastness of existence.

Chapter 53

  • “he knew the Jew who was knocked out by an angel” - It is an allusion rich with symbolic and possibly biblical meaning. Although the text does not provide explicit context for this phrase, it likely references the biblical story of Jacob wrestling with an angel, a well-known episode from the Book of Genesis 32. In that story, Jacob wrestles with a mysterious divine being (often interpreted as an angel or God himself) throughout the night and is physically and spiritually transformed by the encounter. The phrase "knocked out by an angel" suggests a powerful, overwhelming, possibly humbling or transformative experience. Within the context of chapter and the broader themes of the novel, the reference may symbolize a profound confrontation with truth, fate, or spiritual challenge. It could reflect a moment where a character is metaphorically 'knocked out' or overwhelmed by a higher power, a revelation, or an internal struggle. Given the novel’s dense and poetic style, filled with dreamlike, mythic, and spiritual imagery, this phrase serves as a metaphor for a transformative or decisive encounter that shakes the foundations of identity or belief.

Chapter 63

  • Noah is the last of the pre-flood patriarchs of the Book of Genesis. He builds an ark and takes pairs of animals in order to survive the great flood. The biblical story of Noah's Ark signifies a profound message about divine judgment, mercy, and salvation. It illustrates that God judges wickedness and sin but also provides a way for salvation for those who are righteous, like Noah, and those who have faith. The story highlights themes of God's faithfulness through the covenant of the rainbow, and in Christian theology, the ark is often seen as a foreshadowing of Christ as the ultimate source of salvation.

Chapter 64

  • Eden is the Garden of Eden described as the biblical paradise in Genesis. The Tree of Knowledge is one of two specific trees in the story of the Garden of Eden in Genesis 2–3, along with the tree of life. Alternatively, some scholars have argued that the tree of the knowledge of good and evil is just another name for the tree of life.

Chapter 65

  • In the Bible, Ham, one of Noah's three sons, had four sons: Cush, Mizraim, Phut, and Canaan, who became ancestors of peoples in Africa and the Middle East, particularly Ethiopia, Egypt, Libya, and Canaan (modern Israel/Palestine). The story in Genesis details how Ham saw his father Noah naked while drunk, leading Noah to curse Ham's son, Canaan, rather than Ham himself, a narrative known as the Curse of Ham. After the Flood, Noah became drunk and lay uncovered in his tent. Ham saw his father's nakedness and told his brothers, Shem and Japheth. Shem and Japheth, however, respectfully covered Noah without looking. Noah, upon waking, cursed Canaan (Ham's son) to be a "servant of servants" to his brothers, a passage later misused to justify slavery.

Chapter 68

  • Christian vegetarianism is the practice of abstaining from meat for faith-based reasons, focusing on themes like stewardship of creation, compassion, health, and honoring God, though the Bible permits meat-eating, with varied interpretations on animal rights and the ideal diet (some pointing to Genesis's plant-based start, others to Jesus eating fish). While not a universal command, it's seen as a way to live out Christian values. Vegetarians and especially vegans are more likely to be low in vitamin D, zinc, anemic, suffer from anxiety and depression and have eating disorders. Also not eating enough protein can make a person crave protein. These cravings have been documented with castaways surviving at sea. A vegan diet can be deficient in the following essential nutrients: Vitamin B12, Vitamin D, Omega-3 fatty acids (especially EPA and DHA), iron, calcium, zinc, iodine, and protein. Although supplements and fortified foods can be taken, these nutrients come from animal meat. Meat protein is vital for building tissues, producing hormones, and supporting immunity, offering complete essential amino acids and highly absorbable micronutrients like iron, zinc, and B vitamins, crucial for growth, muscle maintenance, cognitive function, and preventing deficiencies, especially in children, women, and the elderly.

Chapter 69

  • Joseph’s coat of many colors is from the Book of Genesis. It was a special, richly decorated robe given to him by his father Jacob, symbolizing his favoritism, which fueled his brothers' jealousy, leading them to sell Joseph into slavery and present the bloody coat to Jacob as proof he was dead. The garment, likely a regal tunic with long sleeves, signified Joseph's special status and set him apart, though it also provoked envy, highlighting themes of favoritism, betrayal, and God's guiding purpose through hardship.

Chapter 74

  • “For everything was so sad, and the sky bled in her dreams, and she had no menstrual period, and she was always pregnant. Who was innocent? Who was guilty? Her own childhood reproached her.” This section may refer to the death of Esther’s baby when she was young and how that traumatized her even now. Rape has been used as a plot device in literature and other media. It has been criticized for using rape as a metaphor that displaces the actual, violent and traumatic act of rape. It is considered misogynistic in the way female rape victims continue to be cast as collateral damage and the assault and rape of women is used as a plot point in a narrative that is fundamentally about men. Young is making this about Esther and showing how things would be different if she were a man. Young makes many references to mythology. The struggle to escape from sexual pursuit is one of the most popular motifs of Greek mythology: Persephone, Callisto, Daphne, Europa, Io, etc. Greek mythology depicts women as vulnerable to assault, able to escape only through death, or metamorphosis like Daphne who becomes a laurel tree. The consequence of fleeing sexual violence is another type of force on the body, the loss of the human form where women who survive sexual violence become pregnant, and when gods as the rapists, produce a hero child. The Chicago detective is described as raping Esther because he thinks she’s dead. Chione in Greek mythology was said to be so beautiful that she was the object of a thousand men's desire. Hermes used magic to make Chione to fall into a deep sleep and proceeded to rape her. Since Young has said Esther is the mother of us all and Mr. Spitzer as the father of us all, these could be archetypes for Adam and Eve and possibly Lilith. In On the Origin of World, part of the Nag Hammadi Library, Eve is described as being raped by archangels and bears children. In the Kabbalist myth Eve is raped by Lilith, Adam’s first wife, in the guise of Samael, Lilith’s demon lover or Satan. The Kabbalists continued the story to include Eve, through her menses, to have defiled Adam which then lead to Lilith being able to rape Adam, become impregnated and birth Lilin or demons by Adam.
