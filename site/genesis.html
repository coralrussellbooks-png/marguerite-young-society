Definition from research

Chapter 2

  • Jacob’s Ladder is a ladder leading to heaven that was featured in a dream the biblical Patriarch Jacob had during his flight from his brother Esau in the Book of Genesis.

Chapter 5

  • “is the blood of Abel crying from the ground that I should live to see these bloody flowers?” - This may refer to the story of Cain and Abel from Genesis 1-4 where Abel’s blood carries a power that doesn’t let Cain settle again on the earth. The first human death could symbolize the future violence which pollutes the earth. God uses a flood later on in Genesis to purify the earth. This phrase encapsulates Vera’s anguished contemplation of human suffering’s enduring cry through history and its impact on her perception of life and the world around her.

Chapter 7

  • In the Bible, “headstone” primarily refers to the “principal stone in a foundation,” or the cornerstone, signifying strength, stability, and alignment. Specifically, it’s often associated with Jesus Christ, who is described as the cornerstone upon which believers are built. In a broader sense, headstones can also refer to memorial markers at graves, symbolizing remembrance and faith. (Psalm 118:22; Acts 4:11; 1 Peter 2:7; Genesis 28:18-22; Joshua 4:1-8).

Chapter 8

  • “Was I not made to be the king of the earth?”: Genesis 1:26 reads, “let them have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air.” This question is a poignant expression of human aspiration confronted by existential despair and the limitations imposed by mortality and circumstance. It highlights themes of lost potential, the transience of life, and the universal confrontation with death that pervades the novel.
  • “bone of my bone”: Adam says of Eve, “This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh” (Genesis 2:23).

Chapter 9

  • “Desire be to thy husband”: God tells Eve, “thy desire shall be to thy husband” (Genesis 3:16).
  • “the apple was no sweet mackintosh”: The McIntosh apple, found in Ontario in 1811, carries Miss MacIntosh’s name. Genesis never names the fruit; the apple came from the Latin pun malum, “apple” and “evil.”
  • “God, Who had divided the light from the darkness and created great whales”: “And God created great whales . . . Be fruitful, and multiply, and fill the waters in the seas” (Genesis 1:21-22).

Chapter 11

  • “a society of night devoted exclusively to atheistic propositions”: “The school of night” comes from Love’s Labour’s Lost (4.3): “Black is the badge of hell, / The hue of dungeons and the school of night.” The phrase became a name for Raleigh’s circle with Thomas Harriot and Christopher Marlowe, men accused of doubting Genesis and God. In 1594 an inquiry at Cerne Abbas examined charges of atheism against Raleigh’s circle.

Chapter 12

  • “darkness moved on the face of the deep”: “And darkness was upon the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters” (Genesis 1:2).
  • “filled with the greatest remorse”: “And it repented the Lord that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him at his heart” (Genesis 6:6).

Chapter 15

  • “darkness without face and void upon the deep”: “And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep” (Genesis 1:2).
  • “the dark angel of death with whom a prophet met upon a barren hill”: Perhaps Jacob, who wrestled the angel through the night at Peniel (Genesis 32:24), though Young’s prophet meets “the dark angel of death” upon a barren hill.
  • “Men are fallen”: The Fall of man is the Christian term for the passage of the first man and woman from innocent obedience to God to guilty disobedience.

Chapter 17

  • “even that holy angel with whom Jacob struggled at Peniel”: Genesis 32:22-32 and Hosea 12:3-5: “And Jacob was left alone; and there wrestled a man with him until the breaking of the day” (Genesis 32:24).

Chapter 18

  • “some angelic demon”: Young may be referring to the Nephilim. In Genesis 6:4, they are born when “the sons of God came in unto the daughters of men.” Later legend made them the children of fallen angels and mortal women, at once angelic and demonic.
  • “What if the whole world should turn to salt?”: Lot’s wife “became a pillar of salt” (Genesis 19:26).

Chapter 20

  • “the experimental light of the first day of crazed creation”: “And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters. And God said, Let there be light: and there was light” (Genesis 1:2-3).
  • “the names of the antediluvian patriarchs”: The antediluvian patriarchs are the individuals listed in Genesis 5 who lived before the Noachian flood: Adam, Seth, Enosh, Kenan, Mahalalel, Jared, Enoch, Methuselah, Lamech, and Noah. They represent the lineage of humanity before the Great Flood. Methuselah lived “nine hundred sixty and nine years” (Genesis 5:27).
  • “the dead tree of life”: “The tree of life also in the midst of the garden” (Genesis 2:9).

Chapter 22

  • “what God had created on Wednesday”: “And God made two great lights; the greater light to rule the day, and the lesser light to rule the night: he made the stars also” (Genesis 1:16).
  • “were created on Thursday last. I think it must have been around four o’clock in the afternoon”: “And God created great whales” (Genesis 1:21). Vera’s mock precision recalls Archbishop James Ussher, who dated the creation to the evening before October 23, 4004 BC (Annales Veteris Testamenti, 1650).
  • “by the sweat of my brow”: “In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread” (Genesis 3:19).
  • “the firmament in the midst of the waters to divide the waters from the waters”: “Let there be a firmament in the midst of the waters, and let it divide the waters from the waters” (Genesis 1:6-7).
  • “God created man on Friday, the sixth day”: “And the evening and the morning were the sixth day” (Genesis 1:31).
  • “a confusion of tongues, the very Tower of Babel”: “Therefore is the name of it called Babel; because the Lord did there confound the language of all the earth” (Genesis 11:9).
  • “Where is Mt. Ararat”: “The ark rested… upon the mountains of Ararat” (Genesis 8:4).

Chapter 23

  • “Am I my brother’s keeper?”: Genesis 4:9. Miss MacIntosh asks what Mr. Spitzer might. It resonates with his struggle with his sense of duty, guilt, and responsibility for his brother’s legacy and the lives entangled in their shared history. The question also connects to broader existential concerns about individual responsibility in a chaotic and often indifferent world. The novel explores themes of illusion, memory, and the blurred boundaries between life and death, self and other. It asks whether one is truly responsible for the fate, suffering, or salvation of another, particularly a close other like a brother, and by extension, for others in one’s life or society. It highlights the novel’s exploration of moral responsibility, the limits of that responsibility, and the intimate yet often painful ties that bind individuals to one another in a world marked by loss, deception, and uncertain reality.
  • “winter wore its coat of many colors”: Joseph’s “coat of many colours” (Genesis 37:3).

Chapter 24

  • “believed he was the spouse to Potiphar, the Egyptian captain”: Potiphar’s wife tried to seduce Joseph and then accused him (Genesis 39). The gardener believes he is the wife.

Chapter 26

  • “turning to dust and ashes”: “For dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return” (Genesis 3:19), and the burial service’s “earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust” (Book of Common Prayer, 1549).

Chapter 28

  • “walking in the cool of the evening shade”: “They heard the voice of the Lord God walking in the garden in the cool of the day” (Genesis 3:8).

Chapter 29

  • “brilliance as of the jewels of Aaron’s breastplate, colors like Japheth’s enlarged opals”: Aaron’s breastplate (Priestly breastplate) was a sacred breastplate worn by the high priest of the Israelites. Its twelve stones stood for the twelve tribes (Exodus 28:15-21).
  • “before the separation of the waters from the land, before the division of light from darkness”: “God divided the light from the darkness… and let the dry land appear” (Genesis 1:4, 9).
  • “like Jacob with his ladder of dreams”: Jacob’s Ladder is a ladder leading to heaven that was featured in a dream the biblical Patriarch Jacob had during his flight from his brother Esau in Genesis 28:12. The significance of the dream has been debated, but most interpretations agree that it identified Jacob with the obligations and inheritance of the people chosen by God, as understood in Abrahamic religions. It is significant as a symbol of transcendence and the complex interplay between different realms of existence, reflecting the novel’s broader themes of spiritual searching, change, and the quest for understanding beyond the physical world.

Chapter 31

  • “for Joachim, though he pretended to be so true, was weak as water”: “Unstable as water, thou shalt not excel” (Genesis 49:4).

Chapter 32

  • “the seed-times and the golden seasons”: “While the earth remaineth, seedtime and harvest, and cold and heat, and summer and winter, and day and night shall not cease” (Genesis 8:22).

Chapter 33

  • “swords of fire, swords of ice”: At the gate of Eden God placed “a flaming sword which turned every way” (Genesis 3:24).

Chapter 34

  • “What was man, this poor amalgam crumbling at a touch”: The chapter’s thoughts about man fly in the face of the patrilineal succession where men serve as heads of almost every important social, cultural, and political institution and their name is passed down through children. These things are thought to pass on a legacy of power for men and their male heirs. In Genesis God creates divisions between light and dark, water and earth, male and female and he puts man to oversee it all and keep it in order.

Chapter 35

  • “an organizer of chaos”: Amanda Vajskop reads the Babylonian Enuma Elish and the Genesis creation story, in which male creators take over woman’s part in bringing forth life and women are cast as destabilizing and dangerous, a fear that patriarchy uses to keep women subordinate (Amanda Vajskop, “Finding Patterns in the Chaos: Woman as Chaos Agent in Creation Myths,” Denison Journal of Religion 5, article 7, 2005). The novel is deeply invested in themes of chaos versus order, illusion versus reality. Vera’s narrative continuously navigates between dreamlike confusion and the search for meaning or “ultimate harmony.” Miss MacIntosh represents order and common sense amid the chaos of Catherine’s opium-induced visions and the fragmented lives of other characters. The novel portrays characters with fragmented selves: Vera’s own complex consciousness, Miss MacIntosh’s evolving physical and psychological state (including her baldness and amputated breast), and the multiple personae like Cousin Hannah and Mrs. Hogden. Vera’s act of recounting, reflecting, and seeking patterns in her experiences and the lives around her is itself a search for coherence in fragmentation. The novel’s richly symbolic and layered narrative demands readers engage in deciphering meaning from a kaleidoscope of images and voices. The novel’s intense focus on female embodiment, sexuality, motherhood, loss, and survival complements critical perspectives on misogyny and sexual politics. Cousin Hannah, “an organizer of chaos,” takes up the part these myths assign to woman and turns it against the order men have made.

Chapter 36

  • “Had she not climbed the seventh crystal stairway”: The seventh crystal stairway may refer to Jacob’s Ladder. “Behold a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven” (Genesis 28:12).

Chapter 37

  • “that which was void and without form”: “And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep” (Genesis 1:2).

Chapter 38

  • “she had found a stairway to heaven”: “And he dreamed, and behold a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven: and behold the angels of God ascending and descending on it” (Genesis 28:12).

Chapter 40

  • “how should she be this human Tower of Babel”: Tower of Babel in Genesis 11 is an origin myth to explain why the world’s peoples speak so many different languages. “Therefore is the name of it called Babel; because the LORD did there confound the language of all the earth” (Genesis 11:9).
  • “the sleepy voice of Potiphar”: Potiphar is the captain of the Pharaoh’s guard who is said to have purchased Joseph as a slave in the Hebrew Bible and Quran (Genesis 39:1).
  • “the dust returning always to the dust”: “For dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return” (Genesis 3:19).

Chapter 41

  • “And he sent forth a raven, which went forth to and fro, until the waters were dried up from off the earth” (Genesis 8:7).
  • “so many door knobs which should have been apples”: The fruit of the tree of knowledge, traditionally an apple, was “a tree to be desired to make one wise” (Genesis 3:6).

Chapter 43

  • “Ur of the Chaldees glittering there”: Ur of the Chaldees or Ur Kasdim is a city mentioned in the Bible as the birthplace of Abraham. It stood in southern Mesopotamia, in what is now Iraq. Unlike the cities Mr. Spitzer imagines “buried under the desert sand and never exhumed,” Ur was dug up: the archaeologist Leonard Woolley excavated it between 1922 and 1934 and found its royal tombs. Terah “went forth with them from Ur of the Chaldees, to go into the land of Canaan” (Genesis 11:31).

Chapter 45

  • “pigeons many-colored as Joseph’s coat”: “Now Israel loved Joseph more than all his children… and he made him a coat of many colours” (Genesis 37:3).
  • “I have been a wanderer ever since, wandering over the face of the earth”: Cain, who killed his brother, is sentenced: “a fugitive and a vagabond shalt thou be in the earth” (Genesis 4:12). Hannah believed she had killed her love.

Chapter 46

  • “a ram had stepped out of the fog”: “And Abraham lifted up his eyes, and looked, and behold behind him a ram caught in a thicket by his horns” (Genesis 22:13).

Chapter 47

  • “void and without shape upon the waters of the darkness”: “And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters” (Genesis 1:2).
  • “crumbled into dust and ashes”: “Behold now, I have taken upon me to speak unto the Lord, which am but dust and ashes” (Genesis 18:27).

Chapter 48

  • “The mother of creation was this great abyss”: “Verily at the first Chaos came to be” (Hesiod, Theogony 116, trans. Hugh G. Evelyn-White, 1914), and “darkness was upon the face of the deep” (Genesis 1:2). See Hesiod’s Night in the Chapter 47 resources.
  • “the old lamplighter with his trembling ladder of dreams”: “And he dreamed, and behold a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven: and behold the angels of God ascending and descending on it” (Genesis 28:12).

Chapter 49

  • “These dying species multiplied not like the fishes of the sea”: “I will multiply thy seed as the stars of the heaven, and as the sand which is upon the sea shore” (Genesis 22:17).
  • “only the female endures”: “So God created man in his own image, in the image of God created he him; male and female created he them” (Genesis 1:27); Mr. Spitzer’s “female and female created He them” rewrites it. Women are often depicted as enduring various forms of hardship, including prolonged stress and instability, without succumbing to their circumstances. Some believe that societal expectations and responsibilities, like caregiving, can contribute to women developing a strong ability to persevere through difficulties. This concept is reflected in literature and cultural narratives, where women are often portrayed as resilient characters who face challenges with strength and fortitude. While men often dominate in shorter, more intense endurance events, women demonstrate strong performance in longer distances, particularly ultra-endurance activities. This is partly attributed to potential physiological advantages, such as efficient fat utilization and greater fatigue resistance in women during prolonged exercise. Psychological factors like strong mental resilience and consistent pacing strategies are also believed to contribute to women’s success in these events. It is important to acknowledge that the experiences of endurance and resilience differ based on individual circumstances and intersectional factors like race and culture. For instance, Black women in horror narratives are described as “Enduring Women” (Kinitra D. Brooks, “The Importance of Neglected Intersections: Race and Gender in Contemporary Zombie Texts and Theories,” African American Review 47, no. 4, 2014) who face different and more persistent threats than the traditional “Final Girl” (Carol J. Clover, Men, Women, and Chain Saws, 1992), highlighting the complexities of navigating misogynoir and societal challenges. It is crucial to recognize the importance of understanding resilience within the context of women’s lived experiences and the societal factors that influence their well-being. It underscores a contrast between the fleeting, fragile nature of life and the sustaining, enduring role of the female, whether literally through reproduction or metaphorically as a symbol of lasting strength and continuity in a world marked by change, decay, and death.

Chapter 50

  • “His breath was his spirit”: Spirit comes from the Latin spiritus, breath. “And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul” (Genesis 2:7).
  • “between the patriarch and the cloud or water bird”: “Also he sent forth a dove from him, to see if the waters were abated from off the face of the ground” (Genesis 8:8).

Chapter 53

  • “Mt. Ararat, where Noah’s Ark was supposed to have rested”: Mount Ararat is a snow-capped dormant volcano in Turkey. “And the ark rested in the seventh month, on the seventeenth day of the month, upon the mountains of Ararat” (Genesis 8:4, KJV).
  • “who fought a spurred angel until the angel lost its feathers”: In Genesis 32:22-32 Jacob wrestles with an angel.
  • This may refer to Genesis 32 that describes Jacob wrestling with a “man” throughout the night. In the Book of Hosea, this figure is explicitly called an “angel.” During the struggle, Jacob is injured, specifically having the socket of his hip put out of joint. This injury left him with a limp. Jacob is considered a foundational figure in Judaism and the patriarch from whom the nation of Israel descends. “Yea, he had power over the angel, and prevailed: he wept, and made supplication unto him” (Hosea 12:4, KJV).

Chapter 59

  • “Out of a cloud they came, and into a cloud they returned”: “For dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return” (Genesis 3:19, KJV).
  • “there was a missing rib”: “And the rib, which the LORD God had taken from man, made he a woman” (Genesis 2:22, KJV). Adam, too, was a sleeper: “the LORD God caused a deep sleep to fall upon Adam, and he slept” (Genesis 2:21, KJV).

Chapter 62

  • “greater than Haydn’s Creation”: Haydn’s Creation or The Creation is an oratorio written by Haydn and considered to be one of his masterpieces. It is a celebration of the creation of the world as in the Book of Genesis.
  • “He will be a wild man; his hand will be against every man, and every man’s hand against him” (Genesis 16:12, KJV). Melville’s outcast narrator begins, “Call me Ishmael” (Moby-Dick, 1851).

Chapter 63

  • “she preferring either the light of the Apocalypse or of Genesis”: “And God said, Let there be light: and there was light” (Genesis 1:3, KJV); “And the city had no need of the sun, neither of the moon, to shine in it: for the glory of God did lighten it” (Revelation 21:23, KJV).
  • “but some may be like Noah wanting to come up”: Noah is the last of the pre-flood patriarchs of the Book of Genesis. He builds an ark and takes pairs of animals in order to survive the great flood. The biblical story of Noah’s Ark signifies a profound message about divine judgment, mercy, and salvation. It illustrates that God judges wickedness and sin but also provides a way for salvation for those who are righteous, like Noah, and those who have faith. The story highlights themes of God’s faithfulness through the covenant of the rainbow, and in Christian theology, the ark is often seen as a foreshadowing of Christ as the ultimate source of salvation. The dove came back with “an olive leaf pluckt off” (Genesis 8:11, KJV).
  • “I suffer especially when there are rainbows”: “I do set my bow in the cloud, and it shall be for a token of a covenant between me and the earth” (Genesis 9:13, KJV), God’s promise after the flood.

Chapter 64

  • “the firmament laid upon the firmament”: “Let there be a firmament in the midst of the waters, and let it divide the waters from the waters” (Genesis 1:6, KJV).
  • “the day before God moved over the face of the waters”: “And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters” (Genesis 1:2, KJV).
  • “the macintosh being no fruit in Eden on the Tree of Knowledge”: Eden is the Garden of Eden described as the biblical paradise in Genesis. The Tree of Knowledge is one of two specific trees in the story of the Garden of Eden in Genesis 2-3, along with the tree of life. Alternatively, some scholars have argued that the tree of the knowledge of good and evil is just another name for the tree of life. Genesis never calls the forbidden fruit an apple.

Chapter 65

  • “to wrestle with the dark angel of death”: “And Jacob was left alone; and there wrestled a man with him until the breaking of the day” (Genesis 32:24, KJV).
  • “Weep not, Hagar, for thy little son”: According to the Bible, Hagar was the Egyptian slave of Sarah, Abram’s wife. Sarah had been barren for a long time and sought a way to fulfill God’s promise that Abram would be father of many nations, especially since they had grown old, so she offered Hagar to Abram to be his concubine. Hagar bore Abraham’s son, Ishmael. “What aileth thee, Hagar? fear not” (Genesis 21:17, KJV). Where the King James has “he will be a wild man” (Genesis 16:12), the Hebrew reads “a wild ass of a man,” as Mr. Bonebreaker has it; see Ishmael in the Chapter 62 resources.

Chapter 68

  • “a handful of dust”: “For dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return” (Genesis 3:19, KJV). Compare T. S. Eliot: “I will show you fear in a handful of dust” (The Waste Land, 1922).
  • “the mandrakes screaming when they are pulled up by the roots”: This folklore is based on the mandrake plant which is a hallucinogenic and narcotic. It is said that it grows from the blood of a hanged man and the tuber can resemble a human form. Forensic medicine does record erection and the discharge of semen in some deaths by hanging, and the folklore of the gallows mandrake grew from it. Juliet fears “shrieks like mandrakes’ torn out of the earth” (Romeo and Juliet 4.3), and in Genesis Rachel trades for Leah’s mandrakes to bear a child (Genesis 30:14-16).

Chapter 69

  • “as many colors as Joseph’s coat”: Joseph’s coat of many colors is from the Book of Genesis. It was a special, richly decorated robe given to him by his father Jacob, symbolizing his favoritism, which fueled his brothers’ jealousy, leading them to sell Joseph into slavery and present the bloody coat to Jacob as proof he was dead. The garment, likely a regal tunic with long sleeves, signified Joseph’s special status and set him apart, though it also provoked envy, highlighting themes of favoritism, betrayal, and God’s guiding purpose through hardship. “Now Israel loved Joseph more than all his children… and he made him a coat of many colours” (Genesis 37:3, KJV).

Chapter 71

  • “Sarah, Sarah!”: The name Sarah means “princess” or “noblewoman” in Hebrew, derived from the Hebrew word sar meaning “prince” or “ruler,” with Sarah being the feminine form, signifying a “lady of high rank” or “princess of a multitude.” It’s a significant Biblical name, given to Abraham’s wife and matriarch of the Jewish people, whose original name was Sarai (meaning “my princess”) before God changed it to Sarah. The name has deep roots in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, maintaining timeless popularity across cultures. “As for Sarai thy wife, thou shalt not call her name Sarai, but Sarah shall her name be” (Genesis 17:15, KJV).
  • “Why was Sarah laughing?”: “Therefore Sarah laughed within herself, saying, After I am waxed old shall I have pleasure, my lord being old also?” (Genesis 18:12, KJV): the old woman who laughed at the promise of a child.

Chapter 74

  • “the mark of the gavel on his forehead”: “And the LORD set a mark upon Cain” (Genesis 4:15, KJV).
  • “Her own childhood reproached her”: This section may refer to the death of Esther’s baby when she was young and how that traumatized her even now. Rape has been used as a plot device in literature and other media. It has been criticized for using rape as a metaphor that displaces the actual, violent and traumatic act of rape. It is considered misogynistic in the way female rape victims continue to be cast as collateral damage and the assault and rape of women is used as a plot point in a narrative that is fundamentally about men. Young is making this about Esther and showing how things would be different if she were a man. Young makes many references to mythology. The struggle to escape from sexual pursuit is one of the most popular motifs of Greek mythology: Persephone, Callisto, Daphne, Europa, Io, etc. Greek mythology depicts women as vulnerable to assault, able to escape only through death, or metamorphosis like Daphne who becomes a laurel tree. The consequence of fleeing sexual violence is another type of force on the body, the loss of the human form where women who survive sexual violence become pregnant, and when gods as the rapists, produce a hero child. The Chicago detective takes Esther for a corpse, then feels her pulse, finds her heartbeat, and rapes her. Chione in Greek mythology was said to be so beautiful that she was the object of a thousand men’s desire. Hermes put Chione into a deep sleep with his wand and proceeded to rape her. Since Young has said Esther is the mother of us all and Mr. Spitzer as the father of us all, these could be archetypes for Adam and Eve and possibly Lilith. In On the Origin of the World, part of the Nag Hammadi Library, the rulers of the world rape the earthly Eve, and she bears their children. In the midrash Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer, Samael, riding the serpent, seduces Eve and fathers Cain; later Kabbalah makes Lilith, Adam’s first wife, Samael’s bride. The Talmud says that in the years Adam was apart from Eve he fathered spirits, demons, and lilin (Eruvin 18b).

Chapter 76

  • “Even when the ejaculation of the male organ had fallen upon the empty air”: “And Onan knew that the seed should not be his; and it came to pass, when he went in unto his brother’s wife, that he spilled it on the ground” (Genesis 38:9, KJV). The LORD slew him for it.

Chapter 77

  • “was natural and a mother should suffer”: This may refer to Genesis 3:16 as Eve and women’s punishment for being cast out of the Garden of Eden. “In sorrow thou shalt bring forth children” (Genesis 3:16, KJV).
