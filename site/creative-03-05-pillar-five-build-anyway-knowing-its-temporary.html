Pillar: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary · Across every book

Or, Why Building Without a Blueprint is True Integrity

When there is no permanent foundation, why do we bother making anything at all? Marguerite Young shares a radical secret: the making itself is the whole point.

This far along, it is reasonable to pause here and ask a tough question: if there is no solid bedrock, no final destination, and no unshakeable foundation that holds, then what on earth is the point of building anything at all? Why write a book, start a relationship, launch a community project, or fight for a better world if everything is provisional?

Young's answer is Pillar Five, and it is the most quietly liberating thing her entire system contains: You build anyway.

The Beauty of the Loop

Young's principle is "recursive construction". The word recursive means a process that loops back into itself. It means using what you just experienced to inform what you do next, returning to your starting point over and over again, not to repeat your mistakes, but to find something brand new that you missed the first time around.

In Young's world, you do not build something once and then stand back to admire it forever. Instead, you create something, and the very act of making it reveals a truth you did not know when you started. So, you build again, incorporating what you just learned. Then that creation teaches you something else, and you loop back to adjust once more.

Under this pillar, life is not a static object: it is a process. The self is a verb, not a noun. The ultimate secret of this philosophy is that the value of your life does not live in the finished product, which is always going to be temporary and imperfect anyway. The making is the entire point.

This position argues that keeping your creations open, provisional, and under constant revision is not a failure. It is the only form of true integrity the world allows.

Why Tyrants Fear an Unfinished Structure

This brings us right back to our ultimate goal: jamming the authoritarian playbook.

Fascism and authoritarian systems big and small are obsessed with the fantasy of a permanent arrival. Dictators love to promise a flawless, permanent, final blueprint for a perfect society. They want to build structures that are frozen, where the rules can never change, the power can never be questioned, and the social hierarchy is locked in place forever.

When we embrace recursive construction, we shatter that entire trap.

We reject the frozen blueprint: By treating our communities, our rules, and our organizations as provisional projects, we ensure they can never be permanently frozen into tools of tyranny.

We build flexibility into the design: When an institution explicitly acknowledges that it is unfinished, it leaves the door wide open for ongoing revision and growth.

We normalize starting over: Authoritarian systems treat mistakes as crimes or signs of weakness. Young's pillar reminds us that looping back to correct our course is the natural, honest state of human progress.

A community that is always willing to look at its own setup, return to the beginning, and rebuild based on fresh insight is a community that a tyrant can never truly capture.

The Art of Permanent Revision: The Bauhaus

To understand what building provisionally actually looks like, it helps to look at a place where an entire school and philosophy was built on the principle that design is not about arriving at a perfect answer, but about staying in the process of asking better questions.

The Bauhaus was founded in 1919 in Weimar, Germany, as a school of art, architecture, and design. But it was not a school that had a fixed curriculum waiting to be taught. Instead, it was built on a radical principle: the process of making teaches you what you need to know. You learn by building. You revise based on what the materials tell you. You loop back and try again.

Walter Gropius, the founder, insisted that there was no such thing as a finished design waiting to be discovered. Instead, designers had to stay in an ongoing conversation between idea and material, between intention and reality. You make something, and the world tells you what needs to change. Then you build again.

The teachers at the Bauhaus, people like Paul Klee, Wassily Kandinsky, László Moholy-Nagy, and Josef Albers, were not delivering a fixed body of knowledge. They were modeling a way of thinking: stay curious, stay open, keep making, keep learning from what you make. Klee taught that you learn to draw by drawing, not by memorizing rules. Kandinsky taught that color and form teach you about themselves only when you work with them directly. The entire school was organized around the principle that understanding comes through doing, and doing reveals what you need to understand next.

This approach to design was revolutionary. It rejected the idea that there was a perfect style, a correct answer, an arrival point where you could stop learning. Instead, it insisted that the work was the thing: the constant looping back, the revision, the willingness to fail and try again. Every student who came through the Bauhaus learned not a fixed set of aesthetic rules but a way of being in relation to making: stay awake, stay responsive, let the work teach you.

The Bauhaus produced an enormous amount of work: buildings, furniture, textiles, typography, posters, paintings. And all of it carried the mark of this philosophy: nothing was fixed. Everything was provisional. Everything invited revision and reinvention. The forms looked modern and forward-looking not because they had arrived at the future, but because they refused to settle into the past. They stayed in motion.

Then fascism came to Germany. And one of the first things the Nazi regime did was close the Bauhaus down.

This was not accidental. The Nazis understood something crucial: a school that teaches you to stay in process, to question, to revise, to refuse fixed answers, is a school that produces people who cannot be controlled by a fixed ideology. A person who has learned to think by making, to revise by looking at what actually works, to refuse false certainty, is a person who will never accept a dictator's fixed blueprint for the perfect society.

The Bauhaus had to be destroyed because recursive construction is the antithesis of totalitarianism. A regime that demands frozen rules, permanent hierarchy, and the absolute refusal to revise cannot tolerate a school built on the principle that everything is provisional and under constant revision.

The closing of the Bauhaus is one of history's clearest lessons: when you see a regime attack the places where people are learning to make things provisionally, to ask their own questions, to revise and build again, you are witnessing an attack on freedom itself. Because the capacity to keep building, to keep learning from what you make, to refuse to accept someone else's fixed blueprint, is what keeps you free.

The Practice

Identify one area of your life where you are paralyzed by the need to get it perfectly "right" or "finished" on the very first try. It could be a creative project, a difficult conversation, or a new habit. It could be a relationship or a community you are trying to build. It could be a vision you have for how you want to live.

Take the pressure off by telling yourself: This is provisional. Build a messy, imperfect first version of it today, knowing that you are going to loop back later to learn from it and adjust. Give yourself permission to live as a verb, not a noun.

Notice also when external systems try to pressure you into accepting their fixed blueprints as final. Notice when you are told "that is just how things are" or "it has always been this way" or "there is no alternative." Remind yourself: someone built this. It is a construction. It can be questioned, revised, rebuilt. You are not required to accept someone else's blueprint as your only option.
