Chapter 33

On Chapter 33 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Woman in Young's Magnum Opus.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer

A Boston newspaper calls Catherine "the late Mrs. Catherine Cartwheel," "a famous beauty of the past," while she is still alive, and that error is the chapter's pillar: the boundary between life and death held open, a woman reported dead because she no longer performs youth and visibility. The mistake is society's habit of consigning women to the past once they stop conforming, and Catherine's persistence past her own obituary is a quiet insistence that she is not finished being.

Mr. Spitzer's choice not to correct the paper leaves her narrative in male hands, the "opium taker in love with her starry illusions" managed by a benevolent man who will not disturb her, and this is patriarchal control of a woman's story. Yet the chapter turns the drugged, dreamlike mode into a genuine freedom. Catherine's memories of her wedding, fragmented and defiant of chronology, her marriages "to Death" or to abstractions rather than to a man, refuse the bridal narrative and the patriarchal claim that a woman's legitimacy hinges on marriage. Her rejection of Mr. Spitzer as a suitor even while he stands present is a critical stance against male-defined relationship, and her "beautifully inaccurate" memory becomes a mode of knowing that his precision, his watch and his measured wind, cannot reach.

That contrast of her irrational "greater freedom" against his obsession with control is the gendered epistemology the novel keeps returning to, reason assigned to men and dream to women, and Young revaluing the dream. Catherine's unconsciousness offers "improbable possibility" where his rationality offers only foreclosure. The chapter closes with her conversing with a blackbird, detached from patriarchal reality and logic, and Mr. Spitzer opening the door to let the bird in, a tentative male accommodation of a female subjectivity he cannot rationalize. The boundary stays open, and her refusal to conform to the world's report of her death is the freedom the chapter finally credits.
