Intertextuality in MMMD

2. The Catalog and the Alphabet

← Previous · Intertextuality in MMMD Home · Next →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Ancient Egypt · Astronomy · Judaism

Definition from research

Almost every character in the novel makes lists, and the lists are where most of the book's references live. Young uses the catalog to flatten hierarchy and to multiply identity.

Catherine's lists are alphabetical. In chapter 9 "the P's were crowding upon her," and the P's include "Pandora's bottomless box, pyramids, peninsulas, pebbles, prologues, pterodactyls." By chapter 11 she has "passed beyond Pericles, Admiral Peary, Penelope's web, the Pleiades, the formal penguins." Myth, polar exploration, astronomy, and zoology stand in a row, ordered by nothing but their first letter. An alphabet cannot rank. That is its use to her.

Miss MacIntosh's lists run the other way, toward drill and fact: navigation, the deaths of kings, the Jewish calendar, the stations of the Exodus. In chapter 20 Vera is to "learn the Jewish calendar, Nisan which is March, Jyar which is April." She also learns "the itinerary of the children of Israel from Egypt to Canaan, the stations such as Rameses, Succoth, Etham, PiHahiroth, Marah, Elim." Miss MacIntosh's catalog aims at use. Catherine's aims at reverie. The material overlaps anyway.

Mr. Spitzer keeps the naturalist's catalog. His butterflies carry the gods inside their Latin names: "the butterfly Aphrodite, named after that goddess of beauty and love," the "butterfly Apollo," Io written on both wings (chapter 29). In the same chapter he lists the names of God: "the True Vine, the Bread of Life, the Gate, the Door," running through "the Man of Sorrows, the Old Man, the King of Glory." Then he draws the conclusion the list has been building toward: "God was many things to many people."

That sentence explains why Young loves the catalog. A list lets one thing be many things at once. The canary has nine names. God has dozens. Cousin Hannah is imagined as "Clovis or Cuthbert or Crispin or Charles the Mad" (chapter 38). Each catalog refuses the single fixed identity, which is the "Not One Self" of the radical immanence notes put into grammar.

The catalog also shapes the novel itself. Readers often find Young's lists overwhelming. The index suggests the overwhelming is the point. The lists are how characters hold a world too large to hold, and the novel's length is the length of those lists, never finished.
