  • Jehovah’s Witnesses is a millenarian (the belief by a religious, social, or political group or movement in a coming fundamental transformation of society, after which "all things will be changed") restorationist (is the belief that Christianity has been or should be restored along the lines of what is known about the apostolic early church, which restorationists see as the search for a purer and more ancient form of the religion) Christian denomination with nontrinitarian (is a form of Christianity that rejects the mainstream Christian doctrine of the Trinity: the belief that God is three distinct hypostases or persons who are coeternal, coequal, and indivisibly united in one being, or essence) beliefs distinct from mainstream Christianity. Jehovah's Witnesses are best known for their door-to-door preaching, distributing literature such as The Watchtower and Awake!, and for refusing military service, refusal to salute state symbols and blood transfusions. They prefer to use their own Bible translation, the New World Translation of the Holy Scriptures, although their literature occasionally quotes and cites other Bible translations. Adherents commonly refer to their body of beliefs as "The Truth" and consider themselves to be "in the Truth". They consider "human society" to be morally corrupt and under the influence of Satan, and most limit their social interaction with non-Witnesses. Congregational disciplinary actions include disfellowshipping, their term for formal expulsion and shunning, a last resort for what they consider serious offenses. Baptized individuals who formally leave are considered disassociated and are also shunned. Disfellowshipped and disassociated individuals may eventually be reinstated if deemed repentant.

Chapter 3

  • The Pilgrim's Progress from This World, to That Which Is to Come is a 1678 Christian allegory written by John Bunyan. It is commonly regarded as one of the most significant works of Protestant devotional literature and of wider early modern English literature. It has been translated into more than 200 languages and has never been out of print. It appeared in Dutch in 1681, in German in 1703 and in Swedish in 1727. The first North American edition was issued in 1681. It has also been cited as the first novel written in English. According to literary editor Robert McCrum, "there's no book in English, apart from the Bible, to equal Bunyan's masterpiece for the range of its readership, or its influence on writers as diverse as William Hogarth, C. S. Lewis, Nathaniel Hawthorne, Herman Melville, Charles Dickens, Louisa May Alcott, George Bernard Shaw, William Thackeray, Charlotte Bronte, Mark Twain, John Steinbeck and Enid Blyton.” The lyrics of the hymn "To be a Pilgrim" are based on the novel.

Chapter 3

  • “whether a camel shall enter through the needle’s eye or a rich man through the gates of heaven” - is a metaphor used in both the Bible and the Qur'an. It emphasizes the difficulty, or even impossibility, of a wealthy person achieving salvation through their own means. The phrase uses the image of a camel, a large animal, being unable to pass through the tiny eye of a needle to illustrate the immense challenge for a rich person to enter heaven. The saying appears in the Gospels of Matthew, Mark, and Luke, often in the context of Jesus speaking to a rich young man who is unwilling to give up his possessions to follow him. The Quran also uses this metaphor in Surah Al-A'raf (7:40), stating that those who reject God's signs will not enter Paradise until a camel passes through the eye of a needle.

The story of Lazarus's raising highlights the close relationship between Jesus and his friends, as well as Jesus's power over death. Chapter 10

  • “One who could know us and yet love us was undoubtedly the truest love this world has ever seen”: Psalm 139 begins, “O Lord, thou hast searched me, and known me.” The line suggests that the deepest and most genuine form of love is not blind or naive, but rather one that fully comprehends all aspects of a person (their strengths, weaknesses, flaws, and complexities) and loves them in spite of, or even because of, that complete knowledge. This statement highlights the theme of authentic love as acceptance and compassion for the whole person, including their imperfections and vulnerabilities. It contrasts with superficial or idealized love, which might only appreciate a partial or idealized image. The “truest love” is grounded in reality, acknowledging the full truth of the beloved. In the context of the chapter, Vera (the narrator) reflects on the nature of love and marriage, and the idea that true love involves a deep understanding and acceptance of the other person. Miss MacIntosh, who values common sense and reality, would likely agree with this view, emphasizing love based on truth rather than illusion or fantasy. The phrase captures a central insight into the nature of love as something that embraces the whole self (the light and the dark, the certain and the uncertain) and that this kind of love is the most meaningful and enduring.

  • “the road to Hell, as she had often grimly said, is very broad and very wide and is paved with bones and good intentions”: “Wide is the gate, and broad is the way, that leadeth to destruction” (Matthew 7:13). Samuel Johnson said “Hell is paved with good intentions” (Boswell’s Life of Johnson, 1775). Miss MacIntosh repeats it at the end of the chapter: “the highway to Hell is paved with more good intentions than shining cobblestones.”

  • “The Devil could quote his Scriptures, too, chapter and verse for his purpose”: “The devil can cite Scripture for his purpose” (The Merchant of Venice 1.3). In the temptation, Satan quotes Psalm 91 to Christ (Matthew 4:6).
  • “some there are who still confuse the brothers, he who fell”: Both are called the morning star: Lucifer, “son of the morning” (Isaiah 14:12), and Christ, “the bright and morning star” (Revelation 22:16).

  • “The King James version of the Bible was not to be trusted because made for the vanity of earthly kings”: The King James Version of the Bible was not the first English language version of the Bible, but the culmination of extensive translation activity (some illegal!) in the 1500s. This began with the work of William Tyndale and the printing of the first English New Testament in 1526. Following a tumultuous 75 years, James VI of Scotland became James I of England in 1603, joining the two crowns. Three main English Bibles were in use: the Bishops’ Bible (Church of England), the Geneva Bible (Protestants), and the Rheims New Testament (1582, Roman Catholic Church), causing much confusion and dissension. To settle disagreements over reforms in the Church of England and respond to pressure from the Puritans, King James in 1604 approved a new translation of the Bible, primarily because he knew that it would reinforce his image as a political and spiritual leader. He approved six companies of translators, 54 men in all, of whom 47 are known to have worked on it, to prepare the new translation, using previous English Bible translation work, and using the best Hebrew, Greek, and Latin texts and manuscripts. The completed King James Version was first published in 1611. These first editions were large pulpit Bibles (folios) to be read in churches, but were followed within a year by the printing of smaller versions (quartos) for personal use. Though slow to be adopted in the English-speaking world and to replace the more popular Geneva Bible, from the mid-1600s to the late 20th century, the King James Version was the Bible of the English-speaking church. The translators’ dedication addresses “the most high and mighty Prince James,” the kind of flattery Miss MacIntosh distrusts.

The significance of this enumeration lies in its portrayal of God/Jesus as an entity with many aspects and attributes, each reflecting different dimensions of spiritual truth and human experience. It emphasizes the complexity and paradox inherent in the divine nature: simultaneously merciful and just, immanent and transcendent, suffering and triumphant. This multiplicity mirrors the novel’s broader themes of ambiguity, multiplicity of selves, and the intertwining of life and death. The list serves to underscore the novel’s exploration of spiritual reality versus human perception. The divine is depicted not as a singular, easily definable being but as a presence that manifests in diverse and sometimes contradictory ways, much like the fragmented identities and overlapping realities experienced by the characters such as Mr. Spitzer and Vera’s mother, Catherine. This reflects the novel’s preoccupation with the elusive nature of truth, the complexity of existence, and the blurred boundaries between reality and illusion. By invoking these various titles, the text taps into a rich tradition of Christian symbolism, inviting readers to contemplate the depth of spiritual meaning behind everyday experiences and the mysterious workings of grace, suffering, and redemption. It situates the characters’ personal struggles within a cosmic framework, suggesting that their lives are part of a larger divine narrative that encompasses both the mortal and the eternal. This list also contrasts with the practical, grounded character of Miss MacIntosh, who embodies common sense and reality, as opposed to the more mystical and dreamlike experiences of other characters. The divine names remind us of the novel’s tension between the earthly and the spiritual, the seen and the unseen, the real and the illusory. The list is a poetic and theological meditation on the nature of divinity, serving as a thematic focal point that enriches the novel’s exploration of identity, faith, and the search for meaning amidst uncertainty and paradox.

Chapter 49

  • “the twelve Apostles in their long white robes and the thirteenth Apostle”: The twelve Apostles are Jesus’s apostles. Paul, called apart from the Twelve, is often named the thirteenth apostle, and Mary Magdalene was called “the apostle to the apostles.” The emperor Constantine had himself buried among memorials to the Twelve in his Church of the Holy Apostles and was called the thirteenth apostle.
  • “But why should any man weep if the heavens wept for him?”: In the Bible God grieves and Jesus weeps: “it grieved him at his heart” (Genesis 6:6); “Oh that my head were waters, and mine eyes a fountain of tears” (Jeremiah 9:1); “Jesus wept” (John 11:35). In the Book of Moses of the Latter-day Saints, “the God of heaven looked upon the residue of the people, and he wept” (Moses 7:28). The Quran says of Pharaoh’s drowned people, “And the heaven and the earth wept not for them” (44:29, trans. Marmaduke Pickthall, 1930), and the Egyptians said the Nile rose with the tears of Isis for Osiris (Pausanias, Description of Greece 10.32.18). If the heavens themselves mourn a person’s fate, the individual weeping might seem redundant or insignificant in comparison: cosmic forces acknowledge and encompass human sorrow on a scale greater than any one person could express. Mourning rises beyond the personal, and personal sorrow is enveloped in the universal cycles of life and death.

To weep for himself seems an excess to Mr. Spitzer, who is already “dead or dying to this world, this world dying,” so he turns his sorrow outward to others who do not mourn him in return: a solitary, unreciprocated mourner and witness caught between life and death.
  • “Is thy light shining, or hast thy spirit departed upon the evening wind?”: “Neither do men light a candle, and put it under a bushel, but on a candlestick… Let your light so shine before men” (Matthew 5:15-16). Mr. Spitzer asks whether the inner light, the vital spirit, still shines or has already departed upon the evening wind, the approach of darkness and death. Light is life and awareness; the spirit departing is a soul slipping toward death or a state between. The line questions whether a person still lives in spirit or has begun the journey toward the end of life.

  • “the dove of God like that which flew up through Mary’s skirts”: The Holy Spirit descends “in a bodily shape like a dove” (Luke 3:22), and in paintings of the Annunciation a dove brings the conception to Mary. See the three trinitarian minutes, above.

For every Martha bearing the burdens of the world there is a Mary who embodies grace and hope; here Mary is “that snow-white pigeon with the red-rose breast who used to come to her through miles of clouds.” The pairing holds loss, maternal suffering, spiritual yearning, and the interplay of outer and inner life.
