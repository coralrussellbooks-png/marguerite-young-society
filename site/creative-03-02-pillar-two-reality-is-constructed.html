Pillar: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed · Across every book

Or, Why Seeing the Magic Trick Protects Your Mind

We are brought up to believe that the world is divided into two distinct categories: hard reality on one side, and silly illusions or daydreams on the other. We are told that reasonable people live in the real world and leave fantasies behind.

But Marguerite Young steps in with something that changes everything: the line between reality and illusion is completely made up.

The Fish in the Water

Young argues that illusion is not a fake escape from the real world. It is the very medium we live in. She suggests that human beings live in illusions the way fish live in water. What we choose to call "consensus reality" is really just one specific illusion that enough people have agreed to treat as true.

Think about the grand ideas that shape our daily lives: money, borders, social status, laws, and political frameworks. None of these things are physical objects you can dig up out of the earth. They are stories, agreements, and constructs that we built together from the materials available to us.

For centuries, traditional thinkers have used the old story of Plato's cave to explain life. In that story, people are trapped looking at shadows on a wall (illusions), and the goal is to escape into the bright, sunlit world outside (reality). But Young says there is no sunlit world outside the cave. There is only a larger cave, more beautifully lit, where everyone has simply agreed to call their specific shadows real.

Pulling the Rug Out From the Tyrant

So, why is this realization such a massive problem for authoritarian control?

Every oppressive system and dictator depends on a giant trick: they try to "naturalize" their own setup. They want you to believe that the way things are arranged (their power, their hierarchies, their exclusions) is simply the natural order of the universe. They want you to look at their cruel rules and think, "Well, that is just how the world works."

Young's philosophy shatters this illusion.

It exposes the setup: When you understand that all reality is constructed, you realize the dictator's authority is a fragile human performance, not a law of nature.

It returns power to you: If the current reality is an invention rather than an unchangeable truth, it means the political choices keeping things this way can be challenged, dismantled, and remade.

It creates automatic immunity: As Young herself directly noted, if you truly understand how illusion and hallucination work, you will never blindly follow any leader. You immediately see right through the political magic trick.

The very first act of political resistance is the simple refusal to mistake a dominant, man-made system as a natural occurrence.

The Map That Became the Territory: Jorge Luis Borges

To understand what it actually looks like to live inside the invention, to build an entire practice around the knowledge that reality is a human construction, consider Jorge Luis Borges, who spent his career doing exactly that and drawing out its political implications with quiet precision.

Borges was an Argentine writer who worked primarily in short fiction and essays. He was also a librarian, which is not incidental. He spent decades surrounded by the accumulated records of human invention: maps, encyclopedias, histories, taxonomies, legal codes, theological systems. He understood from the inside that these structures do not describe the world. They construct it. A map is not the territory. A legal category is not a natural fact. A nation is not a geological formation. They are all human agreements, maintained by repetition and enforcement, held together by the willingness of enough people to keep treating them as real.

His fiction made this visible. He wrote about a cartographers' guild that builds a map so detailed and so precisely scaled that it covers the entire territory it represents, one to one, a full-sized map of the empire laid over the empire itself. The map eventually falls into ruin, and travelers find its tattered remnants in the desert. The story is barely two paragraphs long. It does not explain itself. It does not need to. The image alone is enough to make the point: we have mistaken our representations for reality for so long that we cannot always tell which one we are walking on.

He wrote about a fictional encyclopedia, the Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius, that describes a planet where reality is organized around idealism rather than materialism, where objects only exist when someone perceives them, where there is no concept of cause and effect, where nouns are replaced by verbs and adjectives because nothing holds still long enough to be named as a thing. The encyclopedia begins as a fiction. By the end of the story, the world it describes is bleeding into the actual world. Objects from Tlön are appearing in Argentina. The invented reality is displacing the one people took for granted. The story does not treat this as horror. It treats it as the logical outcome of how reality works: whoever controls the description controls the ground.

This is what Young means when she says that illusion is the medium we live in. Borges demonstrates it with precision. The systems we call real, the nations, the laws, the hierarchies, the categories, persist not because they are natural but because they are maintained. They require continuous enforcement, continuous agreement, continuous investment in keeping them visible and legible. The moment enough people stop agreeing, the system begins to dissolve. Not because it was fragile, but because it was always a construction, and constructions require builders.

The political implication is direct. An authoritarian system is a map that insists it is the territory. It claims that its categories are natural, its hierarchies inevitable, its power simply the way things are. It depends on you never noticing the cartographers. It depends on you walking on the map without ever looking up to check whether the territory matches.

Borges spent his career pointing at the cartographers. His fiction makes it almost impossible to finish reading and return to the assumption that the world you inhabit is the natural, neutral, only possible world. Once you have read him carefully, you carry a permanent awareness that someone drew these lines, that someone wrote this encyclopedia, that someone decided what counted as real and what did not. That awareness is not nihilism. It is clarity. And clarity, in the face of a system that depends on your confusion, is the beginning of everything.

Pulling the Rug Out From the Tyrant (Continued)

Young's own prose enacts the same refusal. Her sentences loop, circle back, resist settlement. She treats reality not as a fixed backdrop but as something continuously invented through the act of narration. When you read her carefully, you realize that what she is describing is not separate from how she is describing it. The form is the philosophy.

This brings us to a crucial point: understanding that reality is constructed is not the same as believing nothing matters or that you can wish things into being. Rather, it means understanding that the current arrangement is one specific choice among many possibilities. It means you cannot be gaslit into thinking that the way things are is the only way things could be.

When you live in this understanding, you become immune to a tyrant's greatest power: the power to convince you that your oppression is natural, inevitable, and impossible to change. Because you know the magic trick. You have seen the machinery. You understand that the construct is held together by agreement, and agreements can be withdrawn and re-made.

The Practice

Pick one thing in your daily routine that feels like an unshakeable, mandatory "reality" (like a rigid social expectation, a corporate habit, or a political narrative you see on the news). Pause and remind yourself: This is an invention. Someone built this story. Once you realize it is a constructed shadow on the wall, it loses its power to terrify or control you. You get to decide what stories are actually worth building.

Try a small experiment with language. Take a statement that feels like a fixed truth ("I am not creative," "people like us don't do that," "that's just how things are"). Now rewrite it in a way that emphasizes process and change instead of fixed identity.

Instead of "I am not creative," try "I have not yet given myself permission to create." Instead of "that's just how things are," try "that is what we have agreed to call normal, and we can agree to something different."

Notice how quickly the "reality" becomes fluid again when you change the grammar. That fluidity is where your power lives.
