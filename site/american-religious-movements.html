Chapter 1

  • Jehovah’s Witnesses is a millenarian (the belief by a religious, social, or political group or movement in a coming fundamental transformation of society, after which "all things will be changed") restorationist (is the belief that Christianity has been or should be restored along the lines of what is known about the apostolic early church, which restorationists see as the search for a purer and more ancient form of the religion) Christian denomination with nontrinitarian (is a form of Christianity that rejects the mainstream Christian doctrine of the Trinity: the belief that God is three distinct hypostases or persons who are coeternal, coequal, and indivisibly united in one being, or essence) beliefs distinct from mainstream Christianity. Jehovah's Witnesses are best known for their door-to-door preaching, distributing literature such as The Watchtower and Awake!, and for refusing military service, refusal to salute state symbols and blood transfusions. They consider the use of God's name vital for proper worship. They reject Trinitarianism, inherent immortality of the soul, and hellfire, which they consider to be unscriptural doctrines. They do not observe Christmas, Easter, birthdays or other holidays and customs they consider to have pagan origins incompatible with Christianity. They prefer to use their own Bible translation, the New World Translation of the Holy Scriptures, although their literature occasionally quotes and cites other Bible translations. Adherents commonly refer to their body of beliefs as "The Truth" and consider themselves to be "in the Truth". They consider "human society" to be morally corrupt and under the influence of Satan, and most limit their social interaction with non-Witnesses. Congregational disciplinary actions include disfellowshipping, their term for formal expulsion and shunning, a last resort for what they consider serious offenses. Baptized individuals who formally leave are considered disassociated and are also shunned. Disfellowshipped and disassociated individuals may eventually be reinstated if deemed repentant.

Chapter 3

  • Miss MacIntosh's philosophy could be compared to the Amish, Quakers, or Shakers. The Amish have a significant population in the Middle West and are known for simple living, plain dress, and reluctance to adopt many conveniences of modern technology. They educate their children up to the eighth grade and then they are apprenticed to work.

Chapter 22

  • “the Emperor Constantine’s having changed the calendar”: On March 7, 321, Constantine ordered that “on the venerable day of the Sun” magistrates and city people should rest. The Seventh-day Adventists and Seventh Day Baptists teach that Rome changed the Sabbath to Sunday, and Miss MacIntosh’s quarrel with Constantine is theirs.
  • “or even the Baptists or even the Free Methodists”: The Free Methodist Church was founded in 1860.

Chapter 45

  • Young may have taken inspiration for the character Cousin Hannah from Susan B. Anthony and her sister, Hannah. Mr. Spitzer described Cousin Hannah as “this great father of suffrage”. Susan B. Anthony’s father was the first one in the family to become interested in the women’s suffrage movement. She was interested in social reform. She was a teacher and was paid less than her male counterparts (then as now). Anthony said, “I wasn’t ready to vote, didn’t want to vote, but I did want equal pay for equal work.” The family started out as Quakers and Anthony claimed to be a Quaker but the father moved away from the order to join the Unitarian Church. They were abolitionists and temperance advocates and had the likes of Frederick Douglass speak at their house. She formed a partnership with Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucy Stone. Stanton kept a room for her at her house where she also spent most of her life working with her. Anthony and Stanton went on to write the History of Woman Suffrage. Anthony said this kind of work, "makes me feel growly all the time... No warhorse ever panted for the rush of battle more than I for outside work. I love to make history but hate to write it." Anthony did not believe in her writing skills which mostly went to Stanton. Most of her quotes come from speeches and lectures. Anthony did not live to see the ratification of the 19th Amendment or equal rights. "There have been others also just as true and devoted to the cause—I wish I could name every one—but with such women consecrating their lives, failure is impossible!" "Now, after 40 years of agitation, the idea is beginning to prevail that women were created for themselves, for their own happiness, and for the welfare of the world." Back to her religion. It is a mixed bag in that they moved to being Unitarian although proud of her Quaker roots. She was involved with the Friends of Human Progress that included “Christians, Jews, Mahammedans, and Pagans.” She tried to start her own Free church where all were welcome. She later, according to Stanton, became agnostic. "Work and worship are one with me. I can not imagine a God of the universe made happy by my getting down on my knees and calling him 'great.'" When Anthony's sister Hannah was on her deathbed, she asked Susan to talk about the great beyond, but Anthony later wrote, "I could not dash her faith with my doubts, nor could I pretend a faith I had not; so I was silent in the dread presence of death." Hannah was known for her domestic nature, but also for her full sympathy with the reforms championed by her sister. As a teen, Anthony went to parties, and she had offers of marriage when she was older, but there is no record of her ever having a serious romance. Anthony loved children, however, and helped raise the children in the Stanton household. As a young worker in the women's rights movement, Anthony expressed frustration when some of her co-workers began to marry and have children, sharply curtailing their ability to work for the understaffed movement. When Lucy Stone abandoned her pledge to stay single, Anthony's scolding remarks caused a temporary rupture in their friendship. It was a bit more than a temporary rupture. They both formed separate suffrage movements. Anthony wanted a federal constitutional amendment. Stone wanted a more gradual state-by-state approach. Anthony opposed the 15th Amendment because it did not grant everyone the right to vote. Stone supported it as a stepping stone. It was 20 years before their two organizations worked together again. Stone worked out with her husband a separate but equal marriage where Stone retained rights to her property and finances and her personal liberty. Lucy Stone wore bloomers. She also kept her name. They publicly declared their commitment to equality and rejected the idea of a wife’s legal subordination. Journalists repeatedly asked Anthony to explain why she never married. She answered one by saying, "It always happened that the men I wanted were those I could not get, and those who wanted me I wouldn't have.” To another, she answered, "I never found the man who was necessary to my happiness. I was very well as I was.” To a third she said, "I never felt I could give up my life of freedom to become a man's housekeeper. When I was young, if a girl married poor, she became a housekeeper and a drudge. If she married wealth she became a pet and a doll. Just think, had I married at twenty, I would have been a drudge or a doll for fifty-nine years. Think of it!" In a speech in 1877, Anthony predicted "an epoch of single women. If women will not accept marriage with subjugation, nor men proffer it without, there is, there can be, no alternative. The woman who will not be ruled must live without marriage."

Chapter 68

Chapter 72

  • Mormons are a religious and cultural group, the principal branch of the Latter Day Saint movement started by Joseph Smith in upstate New York during the 1820s. The largest number of members reside in Utah.

Chapter 72

  • Jehovah's Witnesses in America, initially Bible Students, grew from the late 1800s under Charles Taze Russell, faced intense persecution during the 1930s & WWII for neutrality, adopted the name in 1931, expanded significantly with organized campaigns and Gilead training, and solidified doctrines like the 1935 "great crowd" redefinition, all while building their distinctive identity through publications, conventions, and door-to-door work, establishing a strong presence despite opposition.

Chapter 79

  • Modern Spiritualism is widely considered to have begun in March 1848 in Hydesville, New York, following the claims of the Fox sisters, Margaret and Kate, who reported hearing mysterious rappings that they believed were spirit messages. The movement originated in an area of upstate New York known as the "Burned-over District," which was already a center for religious fervor and movements like Millerism and Mormonism during the Second Great Awakening. The movement quickly spread, with early adherents seeking direct communication with the spirits of the deceased. By the 1850s, it had spread across the country and internationally. In the 1850s, the movement attracted the attention of scientists who began investigating the reported supernatural occurrences. The credibility of the movement was challenged, especially in 1888 when the Fox sisters confessed to faking the original rappings. However, many believers continued their faith, and the movement adapted. Influential figures like Emma Hardinge Britten, a medium and lecturer, used the platform of Spiritualism to advocate for social causes like women's rights and temperance. Spiritualism experienced a major revival after World War I, as many people sought to connect with loved ones who had died in the war. The movement continued to develop organizations and gather followers, with communities like Cassadaga, Florida, becoming known as centers for spiritualist activities. Throughout the mid-20th century, Spiritualism persisted, adapting to new generations and social changes, and leaving a lasting impact on American religious and cultural history.

Chapter 80

  • Interracial marriage in the United States has only been legal since the 1967 U.S. Supreme Court decision Loving v. Virginia that held anti-miscegenation laws were unconstitutional by the 14th Amendment established in 1868. The court's landmark decision has been commemorated and celebrated every year on the Loving Day, June 12th. The number of interracial marriages in 1967 was three percent. The first interracial marriage in what is today the United States was that of Pocahontas, who married tobacco planter John Rolfe in 1614. The first ever law prohibiting interracial marriage was passed by Maryland in 1691. The Quaker Zephaniah Kingsley married, outside the US, a black enslaved woman that he bought in Cuba. He also had three black common-law enslaved wives. He freed all four. They had to flee Florida for Haiti when it became a US territory. The prospect of black men marrying white women terrified many Americans before the Civil War. It was magnified into the greatest threat to society. The first legal black-white marriage in the United States was that of African-American professor William G. Allen and a white student, Mary King, in 1853. When their plans to marry were announced, Allen narrowly escaped being lynched. Their marriage was secret, and they left the country immediately for England, never to return. In 1957 actor Sammy Davis Jr. faced backlash for his relationship with a white woman, actress Kim Novak. In 1958, Davis briefly married a black woman, actress and dancer Loray White, to protect himself from mob violence.
