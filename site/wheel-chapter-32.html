Research notes: Chapter 32 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 32

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

"She had been married to a sail-boat which suddenly lifted its wings and sailed away. She had been married to a porpoise and a star. She had been married to an iceberg. She had been married to a church bell. She had been married to all the fading images of this world."

The mother is not one person. She is a cascade of incompatible selves (bride, corpse, dreamer, femme fatale, prisoner, traveller) none of which resolves into a stable identity. Her marriage is not to a single man but to multiplicity itself: porpoise, star, iceberg, church bell. Each marriage is simultaneously real and impossible, which means the self that contracted these marriages is itself multiple and irresolvable.

Cousin Hannah presents the inverse problem. She appears singular (the great captain, the suffragist warrior) and yet the text quietly dismantles even her coherence. She is simultaneously:

  • The champion of women
  • Possibly the enemy of feminine beauty
  • The misogynist she cannot be
  • The deathbed confessor who recants everything
  • The visitor who may have arrived after she was already dead

The narrator does not resolve these contradictions. They are what Cousin Hannah is. The plural self here is not psychological complexity in the realist novel sense; it is ontological. These characters exist as fields of simultaneous, irresolvable states. Not One Self pillar names this not as pathology but as the structure of being.

The narrator-child, filtered through Mr. Spitzer's uncertain consciousness, adds a third layer of plurality: "Mr. Spitzer asked vaguely as if he were not himself". Even the witness is multiple. There is no stable observing position from which to count the selves.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

"My mother believed that Cousin Hannah had been the image of that past which never was, that future which might never be realized."

This single sentence is the philosophical center of the chapter. Cousin Hannah is not remembered; she is constructed as the image of a past that never existed and a future that may never arrive. She is illusion all the way down, and yet she is the most vivid presence in the text.

The chapter systematically refuses to distinguish between what happened and what was dreamed:

"my mother had visited her only in a dream when she was riding in her carriage driven by the dead black coachman"

"A winged hand had opened the door to my mother."

"She surely knew the day that Cousin Hannah died, for a riderless horse white as snow came through the window"

None of these events are marked as dream or reality. The text offers no outside from which to adjudicate. The mother's drugged perception, the dead coachman who knows the way, the riderless horse; these are presented with the same syntactic authority as the Boston mansion between two water towers. Illusion is not secondary to reality here. It is the primary material.

The cave metaphor from Pillar 2, "only a larger cave more carefully lit", finds its architectural equivalent in the mother's house: surrounded by walls, defenders, swordsmen, cupbearers, footmen, "many defenses and imbroglios of being and sunken cities with gleaming towers in clouds". Each layer of protection is another layer of constructed reality. There is no exit to unmediated truth. There is only deeper interiority.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

"For years afterward there was the mournful music of Chinese flutes and lutes."

Grief in this chapter does not arrive at resolution. It accumulates as climate. The temperature drops when Cousin Hannah comes. Icicles hang from the ceiling after her death. The snow falls throughout, not as event but as atmosphere, the permanent weather of a world where everything loved is always already departing.

Consider the temporal structure of grief here. The mother grieves:

  • The absent bridegroom who never was
  • Cousin Hannah's death, which she may have known before it happened
  • The youth of Cousin Hannah she cannot remember
  • The marriage that was doomed from the start
  • Loves that "had never been"

This last phrase is the most radical. Grief here is not for what was lost but for what never existed. The mother mourns the phantom bridegroom, the fairy prince, the cold kiss like a snowflake. Grief has become untethered from actual loss and become the climate of imagination itself: the permanent weather of a consciousness that loves what it invents and mourns what it never possessed.

"No one had awakened the sleeping lady to life and love. There was no fairy prince such as my mother dreamed of, and there was no man, and there was no cold kiss like a snowflake falling through the dark air under the glassy sky."

The tripled negation (no fairy prince, no man, no cold kiss) is not consolation. It is the full inventory of absence. Grief as permanent weather means the honest life does not resolve this into acceptance. It simply continues breathing in it.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

"He had been the only go-between, musically whispering as he had thought what to say, what not to say."

Mr. Spitzer, and through him the narrator, occupies the position of the Witness. He does not fix, conclude, or resolve. He stays in the room, musically whispering, attending to what cannot be said directly. His role is precisely not to determine whether the mother's visit to Cousin Hannah occurred before or after death; he hopes it did not occur after, but he does not know, and the text does not tell us.

The ethical stance of the Witness is enacted in the prose style itself. Young's sentences accumulate qualification upon qualification, never arriving at verdict:

"Sometimes she thought that she had ignored this uninvited wedding guest — sometimes, that she had frozen her with her remoteness, her icy chill — or again, that she had given her a sidelong glance which might signify much or nothing."

This is not indecision. This is the refusal to simplify that Pillar 4 names as the central ethical act. The narrator witnesses the mother's uncertainty without resolving it. Attention is the practice. The sentence that holds three contradictory possibilities simultaneously (ignoring, freezing, glancing) is the form that witnessing takes in this prose.

The chapter also stages the failure of witnessing. Cousin Hannah tried to witness the mother (to see her clearly, to rescue her) and failed, partly because the mother resisted being seen:

"causing my mother to dissemble her feelings when she came, to simulate an emotion of indifference she did not really feel"

The mother performs non-feeling for the one person who came to see her feeling. This is the tragedy of the chapter: the witness arrives, and the witnessed one hides.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

"She had returned with new attempts — or perhaps her life had been one long siege, for her retreat had been a skillful withdrawal, not a coward's retreat, and she had always rallied new forces, storming this castle as the sea corrodes the shore of rocks"

Cousin Hannah's entire existence is described as recursive construction. She builds, fails, withdraws, rebuilds, not toward final victory but as the structure of her life. The siege metaphor is crucial: a siege is not a single assault but a sustained, repetitive, accumulative pressure. Each attempt is provisional. Each withdrawal is preparation for return.

The prose itself performs recursive construction. Young returns obsessively to the same images (snow, horses, swords, icebergs, parasols, wedding veils) not to resolve them but to layer them. Each return deposits new meaning on top of old meaning without erasing it. The building is never finished. We are still inside the same chapter, still circling Cousin Hannah, still unable to conclude her.

The mother's marriage enacts a darker version of this pillar:

"she had married only in order that she might say there was a man in the house, and yet her husband had left her"

She builds the structure of marriage knowing it is provisional, perhaps knowing it will fail. She builds it anyway: "to defy Cousin Hannah", to assert independence, to proclaim something. The building collapses. She returns to the beginning. This is not presented as tragedy alone but as the recursive pattern of a consciousness that must construct even without foundation.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

"My mother had always wanted to die while travelling — as then she would never completely die — for every traveller dies merely by passing through a city, and none will know that landscape which he passes through in the long night. Death is that which is caused by our partial knowledge."

This is perhaps the most direct statement of Unfinished Is Honest in the entire chapter. Death is partial knowledge. Completion (full knowledge, full arrival) is death. The unfinished journey is the honest life. The ongoing sentence is integrity.

The chapter refuses to complete Cousin Hannah. We are told she died in bed, but we are also told the mother may have visited her after death, and we are told she made a deathbed confession, and we are told this confession was impossible to know. Cousin Hannah is never concluded. Her story remains an ongoing sentence.

The mother's vagueness about her own marriage (her wandering mind, her sudden changes of position) is not presented as weakness. It is the honest response to a life that cannot be concluded. The demand for a clear account of who she married, when, and what happened would be violence against her experience. The text refuses that violence.

"She was always so very vague as to who her husband was."

Vagueness here is not failure. It is Unfinished Is Honest in lived form.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

"She had fought for freedom on every unknown front, particularly for freedom from man, who had never seduced her."

Cousin Hannah inhabits the margins of every category the text invokes. She is:

  • A woman who fights like a man
  • A suffragist who opposes feminine beauty
  • A lover of women who may hate what she loves
  • A great captain who dies in bed
  • A visitor who may arrive after her own death
  • A woman left at no altar; she left the man at the church door

She is the evidence that categories are constructed. Her existence makes visible the edges of gender, heroism, love, and death. She is not the exception to these categories. She is the proof that the categories are not what they claim to be.

The mother occupies a different marginal position: the beautiful invalid, the femme fatale who attracts no one, the bride who married phantoms. She is:

"What beautiful femme fatale was my mother to be this lone lady in an empty house, talking to shadows"

The femme fatale is supposed to destroy men through her power over them. The mother has no men to destroy. She is the femme fatale at the margin of her own category: possessing the form without the content, the title without the territory. This is not irony at her expense. It is the text using her marginal position to reveal that femme fatale was always a construction, always a category imposed from outside.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

"the mournful music of Chinese flutes and lutes" persisting for years after Cousin Hannah's death.

The past does not recede in this chapter. It accumulates. The snow that fell when Cousin Hannah visited is still falling. The icicles that formed at her death are still hanging. The music she left behind is still sounding. Time in this text is not linear; it is sedimentary.

The chapter's treatment of Cousin Hannah's deathbed confession enacts this perfectly. She confesses that her great battles "had been as nothing to her, had been as mere incidents in a busy life". This confession does not erase the battles. It deposits a new layer on top of them. The battles remain in the sediment. The recantation is added above. Both are simultaneously present, pressing up through the ground.

The map of Cousin Hannah's military engagements "pinned upon a bedroom wall" is a literal image of sedimentary time: the past made visible as cartography, traceries of journeys through the Middle East and lands not on any other map, journeys to cities no one had visited, journeys of possible return. The map does not show what happened. It shows what accumulated.

the "past which never was, that future which might never be realized"

Sedimentary time here includes the sediment of the imagined past and the anticipated future. The layers beneath us include what we dreamed happened, not only what did. This is a more radical version of Pillar 8 than it might initially appear: the sediment is not only historical fact but the accumulated weight of imagination.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter is acutely aware of language as construction, most visibly in its treatment of Cousin Hannah's titles and descriptions. She is called:

  • "the greatest courier of every storm"
  • "the greatest weathervane"
  • "this great captain"
  • "the great traveller"
  • "this great challenger of Foreign Legions"

These epithets are grandiose, Homeric, slightly absurd. They name Cousin Hannah into existence as a certain kind of figure, the heroic adventurer, and the text is simultaneously serious about this construction and aware that it is construction. The epithets come from the mother's dreaming eyes, from her imagination of Cousin Hannah. Language here is not reporting a pre-existing heroism. It is constituting one.

The most explicit moment of language-as-construction comes in the marriage passages:

"She had been married to a sail-boat... to a porpoise and a star... to an iceberg... to a church bell... to all the fading images of this world."

Marriage is a legal and social language-act. By applying it to a sailboat and an iceberg, the text exposes marriage as linguistic construction: a word that creates a relationship, not a word that names one. The mother's marriages are real because she says they are. They are also impossible. Language shapes what can exist.

The narrator's own language is also marked as located. The repeated "perhaps", "it seemed", "my mother thought", "there was little doubt in my mother's mind"; these are not hedges toward objectivity. They are the text naming its own location, speaking from inside the mother's and Mr. Spitzer's subjectivity, refusing to pretend to speak from nowhere.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

"For every soul must know finally its Northwest Passage, must find that which cannot be found, the crevice narrow as a thread suddenly opening into those great abysses filled with cities of frozen pinnacles and towers."

The sacred in this chapter does not live beyond the world. It lives in the crevice, in the narrow opening that leads deeper into the immanent, into frozen pinnacles and towers that are still here, still material, still made of ice and stone. The Northwest Passage is not heaven. It is the passage through this world to its hidden interior.

The mother's bed is the site of immanent transcendence. From her bed, surrounded by cascades of lace and the floating white umbrella, she receives visions: the riderless horse, the snowflakes, the icicles, the prisms dancing. These are not departures from the material world. They are the material world intensified to the point of revelation:

"She surely knew the day that Cousin Hannah died, for a riderless horse white as snow came through the window, and the snow fell around my mother's bed. And the temperature dropped, and there were great icicles hanging from the ceiling, and the prisms danced."

This is not supernatural. It is super-natural in the original sense: nature at its highest intensity. The sacred arrives through snow and prisms and temperature, not through departure from them.

Cousin Hannah's death-bed confession participates in this same structure:

"she realized now when she was passing over to the realm of one who had overthrown great kings and had slain minotaurs — that the greatest heroes were not those who perished in the loud clash of arms"

The realm she passes into is not named as heaven. It is named through what it does: overthrow kings, slay minotaurs. It is a realm of action, not rest. The sacred is immanent even in death, pointing deeper into the world of struggle and myth rather than away from it.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

"Death is that which is caused by our partial knowledge."

The final lines of the chapter are the most direct engagement with Pillar 11. The mother wants to die while travelling because "then she would never completely die", because the traveller who passes through a city is constituted by that passage and constitutes the city's memory of passage. She wants to be a crossing point rather than a concluded self.

The entire chapter is structured as a crossing point. The narrator exists at the intersection of:

  • The mother's dreaming consciousness
  • Mr. Spitzer's uncertain mediation
  • Cousin Hannah's accumulated presence and absence
  • The reader's present moment of reading

None of these are prior to the others. They cross each other to produce the text.

Cousin Hannah herself is explicitly a crossing point between past and future:

"My mother believed that Cousin Hannah had been the image of that past which never was, that future which might never be realized."

She is where the unreal past and the possible future cross. She does not exist in time. She exists as a crossing of times.

The structural obligation of Pillar 11, "The ancestors are in the sediment. The carrying continues", is enacted in the mother's relationship to Cousin Hannah. Cousin Hannah deposited something in the mother. The mother carries it, poorly, reluctantly, through her drugged dreams and dissembled feelings. The narrator carries it further, into language. We, reading, carry it further still. The chapter does not conclude this carrying. It deposits it.
