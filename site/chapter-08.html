To All My Darlings

Chapter 8

On Chapter 8 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 7 · Home · Chapter 9 →

Summary

The bus pushes through a cloudburst with a broken windshield wiper, the road barely distinguishable from the flooded fields. It stops at a fork near a hill of old tombstones, beside Beatrice Fitzgerald’s tin house in a yard of mud and wreckage. Madge calls Beatrice a common prostitute. Homer defends her: a hard worker who lost an eye to a boy’s arrow, whose son, rumored to be old Doc’s, joined the Navy. Lost in thought of drowning, Vera cries out for Miss MacIntosh. Moses brings word that old Josh Hathaway is dead and calls it murder, whoever turned the key. Moses delivers a long, dark meditation on death and medicine, insisting that no doctor could have saved Jacqueline. The bus enters a village at night. Its houses look like coffins under a skull-like moon, and Vera wonders whether she has arrived in a land of eternal night or a dream within a dream.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a vivid description of passing through violent weather: cloudbursts, thunder, rain like a deluge, darkness without illumination, the broken windshield wiper, and the road as a river. This stormy journey symbolizes a transition, perhaps even a metaphor for the passage from life to death or from one state of existence to another. Vera reflects, “Perhaps it was the beginning of creation. Perhaps we had said farewell to earth.” This sets a tone of mystery and existential reflection, indicating a crossing into an unknown or liminal space.
  • The bus stops at a place where the road divides near a graveyard hill, with old tombstones bearing faded names. One branch leads downward to an obscure village described as a “land of promise through eternity,” where time ceases, and life is eternal, “where the children would dance, where the young would not grow old, where the old would never die.” This passage contrasts the realm of death (graveyard) with the idealized vision of eternal life, emphasizing themes of mortality, hope, and the human yearning for immortality.
  • On the other side of the road is a decrepit tin house in a flooded yard, described in grim detail: mud, refuse, broken furniture, and wreckage. Here lives Beatrice Fitzgerald, an old woman shrouded in mystery and local gossip. The dialogue about her reveals the community’s harsh judgments, mingling myth and fact: she is called a “common prostitute,” yet also described as “royal,” with “blue blood” due to her name’s prefix “Fitz-.” Her tragic story includes losing an eye and having a son who joined the Navy and travels the world but still remembers her. This passage reflects themes of social ostracism, the complexity of human identity, and the enduring bonds of family amid decay and despair.
  • Moses delivers a long, rambling, and philosophical monologue about “old Doc,” a once-practicing physician now dismissed as mad and irrelevant. He talks about the futility of medical science against death and decay, expressing skepticism about doctors, cures, and even the existence of God or heaven. His words are laced with dark humor and bitterness: “There’s no hell, and there’s no heaven, and there’s no doctor.” He wrestles with mortality, the inevitability of death, and the human desire to resist it, but ultimately acknowledges the helplessness everyone faces. This passage is a meditation on aging, illness, and the limits of human control over life and death.
  • Moses’s speech includes vivid, symbolic imagery: “Alpha and Omega, beginning and end! Trunk, bole, hulk, skeleton,” and references to biblical and mythological figures: Leviathan, Julius Caesar, Hercules, and Charon. These evoke the grand, inescapable cycle of life and death, reinforcing the theme that no one escapes mortality. The passage blends the mundane and the cosmic, illustrating the universal human condition through both everyday and mythic lenses.
  • The chapter concludes with a haunting description of arriving at a village at night, described as “spectral,” with “upright houses like coffins,” “a pale, dented moon like a skull,” and “great spaces yawning between.” The imagery evokes death, emptiness, and silence, filled only with the voices of the past and the dead. Vera questions whether this place is real or a dream, a “dream within the dream,” underscoring the elusive nature of reality and the boundary between life and death.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • The road divides at the graveyard: one branch climbs to the tombstones, the other descends to the village of “promise through eternity.” Which branch does the bus take?
  • Madge leaves the light on so a man “cannot say he did not know,” while Beatrice puts the lights out. What does each woman want to be seen, or not seen?
  • Moses rattles off strings of words: “Barmy . . . screw loose, unhinged, off his head.” What is he trying not to say?

Characters

  • Homer calls Beatrice “royal,” and Madge calls her “the oldest prostitute in all this country.” What makes each of them see her as they do?
  • Moses says of old Doc, “you can’t help giving him your hand when he gives his hand to you.” Why can’t Moses let Doc go?
  • Beatrice’s son “never forgot her” and sends postcards from the world’s great ports. What has he escaped, and what has he kept?

Connections

  • Moses repeats “the key turned by itself” and Madge’s charge of murder from Chapter 7. Why does the novel keep returning to the locked door?
  • Vera wonders whether she relives “another’s” life, as the drowning are said to do. Whose face is “pillowed upon the oozy tides of time”?
  • The chapter ends on “that dream within the dream.” How has the whole journey come to feel like one?

Reflection

  • “You can’t prove it’s murder . . . you can’t prove it’s not, so you are right, whichever way you look at it.” Can a town live by that logic?
  • “There’s no hell, and there’s no heaven, and there’s no doctor.” What is left to Moses if he believes this?
  • “To come to a village at night may be never to know it.” What do people miss when they first meet a place, or a person, in the dark?

Research: Characters

Vera Cartwheel

  • Her descriptions are richly metaphorical and poetic, often blending natural imagery with existential or mythic overtones.
  • She imparts a sense of transition or passage not just through physical space but through states of being or time.
  • She frequently distances herself from the events, perceiving them as if in a vision or dream.
  • She is grappling with realities that are elusive, fragmented, or overwhelming.
  • She contemplates mortality, memory, and identity. This reflects an engagement with themes of impermanence and the unknowability of ultimate fate.
  • While observing others on the bus, Vera remains somewhat apart, bearing witness to their stories and conflicts without fully participating.
  • Her grief and confusion suggest a loneliness or estrangement, emphasized by the eerie, almost spectral description of the village and its inhabitants.
  • Vera is positioned on the edge of the known and unknown, the living and the dead, the earthly and the eternal.
  • The stopping of the bus at a fork, one path leading to a graveyard hill and the other to a mysterious village of promise, serves as a potent symbol for her liminal condition. This reflects an internal or spiritual threshold, not merely a physical one.
  • Her tone is somber, contemplative, and at times skeptical or cynical, especially in the bus-driver’s monologue on medical science, mortality, and social decay. She absorbs this skepticism without endorsing it outright, contributing to a complex, layered narrative voice.

Moses Hunnecker (The Bus Driver)

  • An experienced, somewhat cynical man who knows the terrain well and sees the storm as routine.
  • He stops the bus at a graveyard hill, signaling a crucial moment in the journey.
  • He is skeptical of medical science and doctors, especially “old Doc,” whom he sees as out of touch and ineffective.
  • The bus-driver’s long monologue reveals his mistrust in institutions, the inevitability of death, and a complex view of life, health, and mortality.

Madge Capehorn

  • Madge condemns Beatrice Fitzgerald as “the oldest prostitute in all this country.”
  • Madge is outspoken and somewhat judgmental, suggesting that Beatrice tries to hide in darkness because of her ugliness and reputation.
  • Madge’s perspective contrasts with Homer’s more sympathetic view.

Homer Capehorn

  • Offers a more nuanced and sympathetic view of Beatrice Fitzgerald, describing her as hardworking, loyal, and even “royal” because of her name’s lineage.
  • He recounts stories of her past hardships and suggests a tragic background, including the loss of an eye and a son who joined the Navy.

Beatrice Fitzgerald

  • Described by some characters as an “old dirty thing,” “the ugliest old hag for miles,” and a “common prostitute,” highlighting the harsh judgment and stigma she endures.
  • Madge’s disdainful remarks emphasize how Beatrice is viewed through a lens of moral condemnation and social ostracism.
  • This negative perception is powerful enough that Beatrice is said to hide because she “puts out the lights” so “they won’t see her,” suggesting that she is conscious of her social invisibility and perhaps the shame imposed upon her by the community.
  • Contrasting with the harsh judgments, Homer provides a more sympathetic and respectful view of Beatrice. He notes that she “brings my mother’s laundry” and “washes the clothes so clean,” and that “no one ever worked so hard,” illustrating her role as a diligent, if marginalized, member of society.
  • The image of her pulling a little red sled over snow, with “Her face turns blue,” evokes a sense of endurance and hardship borne quietly.
  • This portrayal complicates the one-dimensional negative stereotype and suggests that beneath social condemnation lies a person who struggles to survive and maintain dignity.
  • Beatrice is noted to be “nearly blind” and losing “the other eye,” a physical manifestation of her vulnerability and decline.
  • This detail adds a layer of pathos to her character, symbolizing not only physical deterioration but also perhaps the metaphorical blindness of society toward her suffering.
  • The loss of her eye is somberly attributed to a childhood incident, where “a little boy shot at her with his bow and arrow,” an event she dismissed lightly, indicating either denial or stoic acceptance of suffering.
  • Homer refers to Beatrice as “royal,” citing her surname Fitzgerald and the idea that names beginning with “Fitz” are linked to royal bastardy (“the bar sinister”).
  • This rumor of noble blood contrasts sharply with her low social standing, suggesting a tragic fall from grace or a life marked by lost potential.
  • The community’s suspicion and gossip about her lineage add to the aura of mystery and complexity surrounding her identity.
  • Homer also speaks of a son Beatrice supposedly had with “old Doc,” a figure who “never claimed him.”
  • This son, who joined the Navy and travels the world, symbolizes a link to a life beyond the village and possibly a hope or legacy that Beatrice clings to.
  • The son’s absence and the fact that “no one ever worked so hard” to survive in the village underscore themes of abandonment and endurance.
  • The chapter uses Beatrice as a symbol of those who exist on the margins of society, invisible and judged yet enduring.
  • Her existence amidst “wreckage” and a “flooded yard oozing with mud and refuse” reflects her precarious social and material condition.
  • The bus-driver’s need to “change horses” at her house metaphorically suggests a pause or shift in the journey, highlighting Beatrice’s role as a waypoint in the narrative of this community’s life and death struggles.

Jacqueline White

  • Jacqueline is described as a “poor little Jacqueline” who evokes a poignant emotional response from the old bus-driver. The bus-driver reflects on her with a mixture of sympathy and sorrow, noting that “Death never entered her head” and calling her “kind of infatuated with life.”
  • He observes that she is “prettier now than she ever was,” and there is a hopeful anticipation that she will go “to see us all next Christmas, going to dance the light fantastic.”
  • This imagery conveys a youthful vitality and innocence, an almost naïve optimism or denial of mortality in the face of impending death.
  • Jacqueline is portrayed as vibrant and spirited, someone who clings to life and the joys it holds, despite the dark, foreboding atmosphere that envelopes much of the chapter.
  • The bus-driver’s reflection on Jacqueline contrasts with the grim and fatalistic tone that permeates his monologue about death, illness, and the futility of medical science.
  • He laments that “the best doctor couldn’t help her,” and that “Old Doc certainly couldn’t,” implying Jacqueline is suffering from an incurable condition.
  • This adds a tragic dimension to her character; she is full of life and hope, yet doomed by forces beyond her control.
  • The bus-driver’s lament underscores the helplessness felt by those who witness her decline and the inevitability of death, which no amount of medical intervention or human will can prevent.
  • Jacqueline’s brief mention is also thematically significant. Her character embodies the tension between life and death, hope and despair, youth and decay that runs throughout the chapter.
  • The contrast between her vitality and the decay and ruin described elsewhere in the chapter highlights the fragility of life and the relentless passage of time.
  • Jacqueline represents a fleeting glimpse of beauty and life amidst a landscape marked by death, desolation, and resignation.

Research: Themes

Mortality and Death

See: Death and Mortality

  • The graveyard hill, the bus-driver’s reflections, and the references to death throughout the dialogue emphasize the theme of mortality.
  • The bus-driver’s long rant on death, medical science’s limitations, and the inevitability of going “when the tide goes” frame death as an unavoidable reality.

Memory and the Passage of Time

See: Memory and the Past · Time

  • Vera reflects on the idea that drowning people relive their lives in moments before death, but here the memories seem fragmented and possibly belong to others.
  • The blending of past and present, and the uncertainty of reality and memory, permeate the narrative.

Isolation and Mystery

See: Isolation and Connection · Faith, God, and the Supernatural

  • The setting (a spectral village at night, broken fences, a flooded yard with refuse, and a mysterious woman who hides from strangers) creates a sense of isolation and secrets.
  • The village itself appears ghost-like, with empty streets, wooden geese, and flickering lights, symbolizing a place suspended between life and death.

Skepticism Toward Institutions

See: Society, Class, and Race

  • The bus-driver’s distrust of “old Doc,” politicians, and even the concept of God highlights disillusionment with traditional authorities and systems.
  • Medical science is portrayed as outmoded and helpless in the face of mortality.

Social Stigma and Reputation

See: Society, Class, and Race

  • Beatrice Fitzgerald’s character embodies themes of social judgment, stigma (being called a prostitute), and resilience.
  • The conflicting opinions of Madge and Homer reveal the complexity of social roles and the harshness of gossip.

Research: Resources

  • “where the cape of the glacier had reached”: The last ice sheets covered northern and central Indiana and left behind the flat, rich till plains. See Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest.
  • “a white carbon lamp like a moon”: Carbon-arc lamps lit streets and grounds with a harsh white light from the 1880s into the 1920s.
  • “Stonecipher, Longtree, Thistlewaite, Chesterfield, Dalton, Grebe, Thomas, Whitehill”: The graves carry names of stone, tree, thistle, and hill, as Chapter 7’s dead carried names of stone. Longtree is the surname of Esther, who appears late in the novel.
    • Stonecipher means stone, swampy creek.
    • Longtree refers to the parish they came from in England.
    • Thistlewaite means thistle meadow.
    • Chesterfield means Roman fort, open country.
    • Dalton means from the valley town.
    • Grebe means digger of ditches or graves or engraver of seals.
    • Thomas is a common name meaning twin.
    • Whitehill is a Scottish name meaning white hill.
  • “a realm of visionary hypothesis or uncertain speculation”: Representationalism, or indirect realism, is the philosophical theory that argues that the mind perceives only mental images (representations) of the external world, not the world itself. It’s opposed to realism, which holds that the mind directly grasps objects as they are in the world. In his philosophy, Kant distinguishes between intuitions (immediate, sensory representations) and concepts (general, mediate representations). He also distinguishes between appearances (things as they appear to an observer) and things-in-themselves (the world as it is independently of anyone’s perception).
  • “I got to change horses”: Stagecoaches changed horses at stations along the road. Moses’s “last stage” is also a phrase for dying.
  • “that same old one-eyed Beatrice”: Beatrice is a name derived from the French name Béatrice, which came from the Latin Beatrix, which means “she who makes happy.” The popularity of the name spread because of Dante Alighieri’s poetry about the Florentine woman Beatrice Portinari. Dante presents Beatrice as being worthy of speaking for God, making her a holy individual. Beatrice “Bice” di Folco Portinari was an Italian woman who has been commonly identified as the principal inspiration for Dante Alighieri’s Vita Nuova, and is also identified with the Beatrice who acts as his guide in the last book of his narrative poem the Divine Comedy, Paradiso, and during the conclusion of the preceding Purgatorio. Within the Comedy, Beatrice is symbolic of divine grace and theology. Young’s Beatrice, a one-eyed washerwoman called a whore, is the opposite of Dante’s guide to heaven. Yet she too lives where the road divides, the graveyard on one side and the village of “promise through eternity” on the other. See Dante.
  • “Everything that begins with Fitz is royal in this country”: There is the belief that the last name Fitzgerald and every name that begins with Fitz is from royalty except that it is a bastard line in England. This belief is because of Charles II, who named his bastard son Fitzroy and the Duke of Clarence, later William IV, whose children by the actress Dorothea Jordan were named FitzClarence. Fitz comes from the Norman French fils, “son,” so Fitzgerald means son of Gerald. The Fitzgeralds were Norman lords in Ireland, the Earls of Kildare.
  • “It’s the bar sinister”: Bar sinister is the popular and erroneous term for bend sinister. Bend sinister is a diagonal stripe on a shield said to be a sign of bastardy.
  • “That’s where they learned to toboggan, too”: Toboggan is a sled. The word comes from the Mi’kmaq, and Madge uses it as a sly euphemism.
  • “See no evil”: The three wise monkeys are carved over a stable at the Tōshō-gū shrine in Nikkō, Japan (1636).
  • “Borneo, Singapore, Japan, the China Sea, the Rock of Gibraltar”: In the 1920s and 1930s the U.S. Navy’s Asiatic Fleet patrolled the China coast and its rivers, so a sailor could truly send postcards from these ports.
    • Borneo is the third largest island in the world.
    • Singapore is a sovereign island city-state.
    • Japan is an island country in East Asia.
    • The China Sea is the East and South China Seas, the waters off the coast of China.
    • The Rock of Gibraltar is a monolithic limestone promontory located in the British territory of Gibraltar, near the southwestern tip of Europe on the Iberian Peninsula. Its name is Arabic, Jabal Tariq, for Tariq ibn Ziyad, the Muslim general who landed there in 711 to invade Spain. The Prudential Insurance Company made the Rock its emblem in 1896, which is Homer’s “life insurance ad.”
  • “was it really true that one relived, in three minutes, all one’s life as the drowning are said to do”: Life review: In 1790, British naval officer Francis Beaufort nearly drowned and reported experiencing a life review. In 1847, a letter by Beaufort recalling the incident was published in the autobiography of Sir John Barrow, 1st Baronet. Wrote Beaufort: “Thought rose after thought with a rapidity of succession that is not only indescribable, but probably inconceivable, by any one who has not been in a similar situation. The course of those thoughts I can even now in a great measure retrace... our last cruise - a former voyage, and shipwreck - my school - the progress I had made there, and the time I misspent - and even all my boyish pursuits and adventures. Thus travelling backwards, every past incident of my life seemed to glance across my recollection in retrograde succession; not, however, in mere outline, as here stated, but the picture filled up with every minute and collateral feature. In short, the whole period of my existence seemed to be placed before me in a kind of panoramic review, and each act of it seemed to be accompanied by some reflection on its cause, or its consequences; indeed, many trifling events which had been long forgotten then crowded into my imagination, and with the character of recent familiarity. The length of time that was occupied by this deluge of ideas, or rather the shortness of time into which they were condensed, I cannot now state with precision, yet certainly two minutes could not have elapsed from the moment of suffocation to that of my being hauled up.” Vera finds herself remembering not her own life but “another’s ultimate face,” and she cries out for Miss MacIntosh, who was lost in the sea.
  • “I was sinking for the first, the second, the third time”: An old belief held that a drowning person rises three times before going down for good.
  • “Where was Miss MacIntosh, my darling, now aloud I cried?”: The novel’s title may echo the old ballad “Oh My Darling, Clementine,” whose Clementine is lost beneath the water. See Music and Composers.
  • “You can’t prove it’s murder”: A logical fallacy is a flaw in reasoning that makes an argument invalid. It’s an error in logic that can weaken or even defeat the conclusion of an argument. Fallacies can be either formal (related to the structure of the argument) or informal (related to the content or premises of the argument). Knowing them helps a reader spot weak or flawed arguments, argue more persuasively by avoiding them, and weigh information more critically. Moses commits the argument from ignorance: what cannot be proved false is taken as true.
  • “Who killed the old horse? Who killed the sparrows?”: The nursery rhyme asks, “Who killed Cock Robin? / I, said the Sparrow.” See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “the influenza epidemic that struck us shortly after the Armistice”: The influenza pandemic of 1918 peaked as the war ended on November 11, 1918, and killed about 675,000 Americans. It reached the United States through Boston, and rumors blamed German agents for spreading it, hence Moses’s “German germs washed up in Boston Harbor.”
  • “Delirium tremens, mocking birds, snakes”: The shaking delirium of alcohol withdrawal often brings visions of snakes and crawling things. See Madness and Sanity.
  • “snatch me from the jaws of living death”: Tennyson’s “The Charge of the Light Brigade” (1854): “Into the jaws of Death, / Into the mouth of Hell.”
  • “me hanging by a thread”: The sword of Damocles hung over him by a single horsehair. In Chapter 5 Moses hangs on to life by “a single hair that would never break, a hair hanging between him and a star.” See the Chapter 5 resources.
  • “me trusting to a broken reed”: “Thou trustest in the staff of this broken reed, on Egypt” (Isaiah 36:6). See The Bible.
  • “going to dance the light fantastic”: John Milton’s poem L’Allegro (The Happy Man), written about 1631 and published 1645, has the lines “Come, and trip it as ye go / On the light fantastic toe.” It is a pastoral poem, which has from its first appearance been paired with the contrasting pastoral poem, Il Penseroso (The Melancholy Man), which depicts a similar day spent in contemplation and thought. L’Allegro is the poem of the happy man, and Moses gives its dance to the dying Jacqueline. See Milton.
  • “Barmy, old Doc is, screw loose, unhinged, off his head”: Moses’s strings of synonyms, mad words here and later “Trunk, bole, hulk, skeleton,” read like the lists in Roget’s Thesaurus (1852). See Madness and Sanity.
  • “Mad as a March hare”: Hares run wild in their March mating season. Lewis Carroll’s March Hare (1865) keeps the mad tea party. See Madness and Sanity.
  • “that old knight errant curing them all”: Don Quixote, the mad knight errant who rides out to help people who never asked to be rescued. See Don Quixote.
  • “A clean bill of health!”: A ship’s certificate that no disease was aboard when it left port.
  • “Galloping consumption, that churchyard cough”: Moses names Jacqueline’s tuberculosis. See the Chapter 6 resources.
  • “horse feathers”: 1920s slang for nonsense, and the title of a Marx Brothers film (1932).
  • “You got to go when the tide goes”: In Dickens’s David Copperfield, Barkis “goes out with the tide,” since, as Mr. Peggotty says, people along the coast “can’t die . . . except when the tide’s pretty nigh out.”
  • “Shallows, rocks, crossing the bar”: Tennyson’s “Crossing the Bar” again. See the Chapter 7 resources.
  • “Greatest kings have died. A turtle falling on your head”: The story goes that the playwright Aeschylus, father of tragedy, died when an eagle dropped a tortoise on his bald head. See Greek Mythology.
  • “three sheets to the wind”: Sailors’ slang for drunk. The sheets are the ropes that, let loose, leave a ship staggering.
  • “Yet you can’t help giving him your hand when he gives his hand to you”: Schopenhauer places compassion as the foundation of his ethical system, arguing that it is the only truly moral motive because it is based on a genuine concern for the well-being of others. He sees indifference as the opposite of this, something to be overcome in order to live a moral life. Compassion allows individuals to transcend their self-centeredness and act for the benefit of others, whereas indifference is a form of egoism that hinders moral conduct. Moses mocks old Doc and still pities him: “It kind of makes your heart give.” See Schopenhauer.
  • “Was old Lethe roaring, making you forget?”: Lethe means oblivion, forgetfulness, or concealment. Lethe is the river of forgetfulness in Hades, from which the dead drank to forget their lives. See Greek Mythology.
  • “How was old Charon that rowed the boat?”: Charon is the ferryman of Hades (the underworld). See the Chapter 5 resources and Greek Mythology.
  • “My age, my change of life!”: The change of life is menopause. Moses claims it for himself, as in Chapter 5 he would not be “my mother’s daughter.”
  • “Alpha and Omega, beginning and end!”: “I am Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the end” (Revelation 22:13). See The Bible.
  • “Man of straws where?”: A man of straw is a weak argument set up to be knocked down, another fallacy for Moses.
  • “bone of my bone”: Adam says of Eve, “This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh” (Genesis 2:23). See Genesis.
  • “Render unto Caesar the things that are Caesar’s!”: “Render therefore unto Caesar the things which are Caesar’s; and unto God the things that are God’s” (Matthew 22:21). Moses turns it into “Render unto Doc.” See The Bible.
  • “Canst thou draw out leviathan with a hook?”: Moses recites God’s speech on Leviathan from Job 41:1-21 nearly word for word, then adds his own line, “He roareth like a great locomotive.” See The Book of Job.
    • “Or his tongue with a cord which thou lettest down?” (Job 41:1). This phrase metaphorically questions how one might access or influence the hidden, powerful depths of existence: whether within the self, nature, or the cosmos.
  • “What happened to Julius Caesar?”: Julius Caesar was a Roman general and statesman. He was stabbed in the Senate in 44 B.C. by conspirators including Brutus, his friend, and Shakespeare gave him the words “Et tu, Brute?” See Shakespeare.
  • “Hercules, what happened to Hercules?”: Hercules is a half-god in Greek and Roman mythology famous for his strength and adventures. He died through his wife, Deianira, who gave him a shirt soaked in the centaur Nessus’s poisoned blood, believing it a love charm. Burning with it, he built his own funeral pyre, a death by a wife and by fire, like old Josh’s. See Greek Mythology.
  • “Wormwood, dust, ashes”: The burial service commits the body “earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust” (Book of Common Prayer).
  • “Hewers of wood and drawers of water!”: The Gibeonites were made servants of Israel (Joshua 9:21). See The Bible.
  • “Was I not made to be the king of the earth?”: Genesis 1:26 reads, “let them have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air.” This question is a poignant expression of human aspiration confronted by existential despair and the limitations imposed by mortality and circumstance. It highlights themes of lost potential, the transience of life, and the universal confrontation with death that pervades the novel. See Genesis.
  • “No partridges in these mountains”: David says Saul hunts him “as when one doth hunt a partridge in the mountains” (1 Samuel 26:20). See The Bible.
  • “Roosevelt in the White House?”: Moses asks whether old Doc thinks he is Roosevelt, who seemed never to leave the White House. In Chapter 5 Moses swore not to cut his hair until Roosevelt was gone. It may also refer to the New Deal and Social Security as well as other programs. See the Chapter 5 resources.
  • “Don’t believe in free silver”: William Jennings Bryan ran for president in 1896 on free silver and his “Cross of Gold” speech, and Republicans held to the gold standard.
  • “Upon a thin steeple, a fish weather vane”: The fish, ichthys in Greek, was an early Christian sign for Christ.
  • “Cyclops, men whose heads do grow beneath their shoulders”: Othello tells of “the Anthropophagi, and men whose heads / Do grow beneath their shoulders” (Othello 1.3). The Cyclops is the one-eyed giant of the Odyssey, like one-eyed Beatrice. See Shakespeare and Greek Mythology.
  • “dead souls”: This may echo Gogol’s Dead Souls (1842), whose hero buys up the names of dead serfs.
  • “that dream within the dream”: This may refer to Edgar Allan Poe’s poem “A Dream within a Dream” (1849), which says, “All that we see or seem / Is but a dream within a dream.” This dream within a dream is a recurring theme throughout the book. The idea that life is a dream goes back to Plato, Aristotle, and Descartes. The first stanza ends with that statement, and the last stanza turns it into a question: “Is all that we see or seem / But a dream within a dream?” This symbolizes the recursive, elusive quality of existence in the novel: a reality constantly refracted through multiple dreamlike states that challenge the notion of a fixed, singular truth. See Edgar Allan Poe.
