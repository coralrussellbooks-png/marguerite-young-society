Chapter 78: Little Jackass

On Chapter 78 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of A Guide to Reading Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 77 · Guide Home · Chapter 79 →

Content note: The Esther Longtree chapters (72 to 81) deal with stillbirth and pregnancy loss, rape, infanticide, racist violence including lynching, and cruelty to children. This chapter describes cruelty to a young child and a stillbirth.

We have arrived, finally, at Esther’s beginning.

Seven chapters in: the Greasy Spoon, the little shadow boxer, the stillborn as fragments, the dying fathers’ letters, the mud baby in the river, the detective who is also the crime, the exhibits A through U, the question of who killed the heavens, and now Young gives us where it all started. Not with the first pregnancy. Before that. Before Esther was born.

She was born kicking. Stubborn. Reluctant. Awkward. Her family called her Little Jackass from the beginning, which is not a nickname anyone gives a child they feel warmly toward. And before she was born, she had already been accused of murder.

This is the chapter where we learn her twin’s name. Rosemary. Born prematurely, born dead, because (her mother told her, repeatedly, laughing and crying) Esther kicked her out of the womb. Esther was a murderer before she drew her first breath. Rosemary was the perfect child who would have been obedient and delicate and beloved. Esther is the wrong one who lived.

What Actually Happens

The chapter is Esther’s childhood, rendered in the same lyrical and fragmented voice she has used throughout. She was born awkward and fighting. Her knees were “always scarred, her body always black and blue” from falling, her foot deformed from birth. Wasps and bees found her with uncanny regularity. She stole food from the icebox when her mother wasn’t looking because she was always hungry. She spent much of her childhood tied to a tree in the yard while her mother went about her small, fragile, carefully arranged life indoors.

Her father: a large, passive, sad, timid policeman whose life was a kind of prison. He patrolled the graveyard at night, not to arrest the lovers who met there, but to warn them off; he did not want to arrest them, just to prevent his own need to do so. This is the measure of his gentleness and his helplessness simultaneously. Esther feels pity and love for him. She is a chip off the old block, she thinks: she has his naturalness, his vitality, his inability to fit neatly into the world arranged for smaller, more manageable people. His lineage (partly Irish, partly Indian, and possibly with what the chapter calls a Negro streak) was known for robust fertility and births that happened without much ceremony, often in fields, women who gave birth and returned to work without stopping.

Her mother: tiny, childish, emotionally cold, a “pallid descendant of George Washington’s sister.” Everything in the house is built to her size and easily broken. She never nursed Esther at her narrow breasts, never stayed to comfort her tears. She blamed Esther for Rosemary’s death with the persistence of someone who has made her grief into an accusation and cannot separate the two anymore. Laughing and crying simultaneously, she told Esther again and again: you kicked your twin sister out of the womb. You killed a child before you were ever born.

Rosemary: the dead twin, perfect and idealized. She would have been delicate. She would have been obedient. She would have been the daughter the mother wanted. Her name means “dew of the sea”: a freshness, a cleanness, a delicacy that Esther with her crossed eyes and deformed foot and mulish head has never managed to approximate. Rosemary is what Esther should have been, and what Esther’s mother has mourned instead of loving the child she got.

Esther searches for traces of Rosemary throughout her childhood: digging in the earth under trees, finding small objects buried there (a little alphabet book, a string of beads, a doll) and interpreting shadows and rustlings as the twin she displaced. She sometimes wonders if she is the real one, or if she is the shadow: if Rosemary was the one who was supposed to live and Esther is only the ghost of the child who killed her.

The chapter closes with Esther reflecting on non-being: no heart as great as non-heart, no being as beautiful as non-being. Sometimes she feels like a shadow. Never fully found. Never fully known.

The Accusation Before Birth

Young has been building toward this for seven chapters. In Chapter 76, Esther told us she killed her twin in the womb without explaining how or whether. In Chapter 78, the explanation arrives: not from Esther’s own certainty, but from her mother’s accusation. The mother told her she kicked Rosemary out of the womb. She said this laughing and crying. She said it repeatedly. She made it the defining story of who Esther is. This is one of the novel’s most precise renderings of how abuse works: the accusation precedes consciousness. Esther was accused of murder before she was capable of intent, before she had a self to intend anything with. The guilt was installed in her before she had the cognitive apparatus to question it. By the time she was old enough to examine the accusation, it had already become the structure of her self-perception. She grew up already guilty. Already the wrong twin. Already the one who should not have lived.

The mother’s grief for Rosemary is real. Young does not deny this. But the mother has routed her grief through Esther (has made Esther the cause, the evidence, the ongoing reminder of what was lost) rather than allowing Esther to be a separate person with her own claim on the mother’s love. This is what To All My Darlings identifies as emotional abuse with devastating psychological consequences: the parent’s inability to cope with loss expressed as the child’s fault, the surviving child carrying the death of the lost one as personal guilt forever.

Rosemary

Her name carries layers. Rosemary, the herb, is associated in folklore and literary tradition with remembrance: Ophelia distributes it in Hamlet: “There’s rosemary, that’s for remembrance.” The dead twin is, from the moment of her name, the one who is to be remembered. Esther, by contrast, is the one who is present and therefore ordinary and therefore inadequate. The dead are always more perfect than the living because they cannot disappoint.

Rosemary would have been everything Esther is not: delicate where Esther is large, obedient where Esther is mulish, beloved where Esther is blamed. She is the idealized daughter constructed from the absence of a real one. Every quality the mother could not find in Esther she attributed to Rosemary, who was conveniently not alive to fail to display them.

Esther searches for her. She digs in the earth. She finds small objects buried under trees (the alphabet book, the beads, the doll) and cannot tell whether they are Rosemary’s or her own buried things or simply the earth’s ordinary accumulation. She has been looking for the twin all her life. “The little shadow boxer under her heart” is not Rosemary. But everything Esther has ever lost carries Rosemary’s shadow.

Am I the Shadow?

The chapter’s most searching question is Esther’s own: what if she is not the real one? What if Rosemary was the one who was supposed to be born, and Esther is only the ghost who survived when she shouldn’t have? What if the shadow that follows her is not Rosemary’s shadow but her own: the shadow of someone who is only a shadow?

Young has positioned this question carefully throughout Esther’s sequence. The little shadow boxer, the child still fighting under Esther’s heart, is named for a fighter who fights an opponent who is not there. A shadow boxer. If Esther herself is the shadow, then the little shadow boxer is doubly spectral: the shadow’s child, the ghost’s hope. The closing lines of the chapter amplify this: no heart as great as non-heart, no being as beautiful as non-being. This is not nihilism exactly; it is the philosophy of someone who has been told for her entire life that the being who did not exist was more valuable than the being who did. Esther has internalized it so thoroughly that she can articulate it as a general truth. Non-being is beautiful. She is the lesser version. This is what her mother taught her.

And yet; she is still there. Still stubborn. Still laughing loud enough to shake the snow from clouds. Still pregnant with the little shadow boxer. Still more hopeful now than she has ever been. The self that was told it should not have existed has persisted with a stubbornness that vindicates the name Little Jackass entirely.

Esther’s Father and the Mixed Lineage

The father is one of the chapter’s most quietly sympathetic figures. He is large and passive and slow, a timid man whose life has contracted to the size of a graveyard patrol; he walks through it at night not as a man of authority but as a man of resignation, warning lovers away not because he wants to arrest them but because he does not want to have to. His life is a kind of prison without walls.

Esther loves him. She is a chip off the old block, she says: she has his naturalness, his vitality, the quality of being larger than the spaces allotted to her. His lineage is explicitly described as mixed (partly Irish, partly Indian, and possibly with a Negro streak) which is to say his ancestry is the history of American contact zones: immigration, colonization, slavery, the enforced and coerced minglings of people who were not supposed to mingle. From this lineage comes Esther’s robustness: the women on this side of the family gave birth in fields and kept working. They did not require special arrangements or silver spoons. Their children survived.

Esther’s children have not survived. The chapter does not explain why the lineage that produced easy, robust births has produced in Esther only fragments and snowflakes and silence. Perhaps it is the mother’s side (the pallid descendant of Washington’s sister, the narrow and cold and fragile) that has taken over. Perhaps it is everything that was done to Esther before she was capable of defending herself. Perhaps the body that was accused of murder before it drew breath has been carrying that accusation ever since, in its own cellular grammar.

Little Jackass

The nickname is cruel and also accurate: accurate not because Esther is stupid or contemptible, but because the jackass is the animal of burden and stubbornness, the creature that will not move when it has decided not to move, that carries weight without complaining and without being thanked, that is used for its labor and dismissed for its nature. The mule, the grey mule that appeared in Esther’s earlier chapters as a symbol of barrenness and endurance, is related: the mule cannot reproduce, is the product of two species forced together, carries everything and passes nothing forward.

Esther was named Little Jackass by the world around her: family, schoolmates, everyone who found her awkward and large and resistant. She has been mulish in the sense of refusing to comply: refusing to explain herself to the detective, refusing to answer the dying fathers’ letters, refusing to be defined by the accusation her mother installed in her before she was born. Her stubbornness is not stupidity. It is the last form of self-possession available to someone who has had almost everything else taken.

What to Hold Onto

Rosemary: the dead twin who was perfect, who would have been obedient and delicate and beloved, who is more present in her absence than Esther has ever been in her presence.

The accusation before birth: you killed a child before you were ever born. Installed as guilt before Esther had the apparatus to question it.

Esther’s father patrolling the graveyard, warning lovers off so he doesn’t have to arrest them. A man whose gentleness has nowhere to go.

The small objects buried under the tree: the alphabet book, the beads, the doll. Esther digging for her twin in the earth, not knowing what she would do if she found her. No heart as great as non-heart. No being as beautiful as non-being. What her mother taught her. What she has almost believed.

And Esther, still there. Still kicking. Still pregnant with the little shadow boxer. Still more hopeful now than ever. The jackass who would not lie down.
