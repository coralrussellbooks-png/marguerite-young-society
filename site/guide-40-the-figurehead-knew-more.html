Chapter 40: The Figurehead Knew More

Research notes: Chapter 40

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Time

Symbols: Clocks and Watches

Philosophy: Augustine and Christian Theology · Freud and Jung · Marguerite Young on Her Craft · Phenomenology · Time, Physics, and Chaos Theory

Allusions: Ancient Egypt · Genesis · Greek Mythology · Milton · The History of Opium

Think of Chapter 40 as a great noise arriving from a great distance.

Cousin Hannah's voice opens it: “a husky baritone, ranging between bass and tenor,” as loud “as iron bells ringing in a leaden” northern sky, carrying what Young calls a Gothic cathedral weight. This is not a voice describing events. This is a voice that is itself an event. Before you understand a word it says, you understand its register: something immense and old and uncontainable is speaking.

And what it speaks is everything. Every language. The languages of the living and the dead, of the drowned Egyptian sailor, of the Saracen cavalier, of Sanskrit printed on a snowflake, of Tibetan monks and whirling dervishes and Finnish herons and Greek shepherds. Cousin Hannah has crossed so many borders in her life (geographic, political, metaphysical) that she has acquired the tongues of everyone she passed, including people who have been dead for centuries and languages that were never quite spoken at all.

Chapter 40 asks a question it will not answer directly: what would it sound like if someone had been everywhere and knew everything? It then tries to render that sound.

What actually happens in Chapter 40

Vera continues to narrate Catherine's interior: how she receives Cousin Hannah's voice, how she orders it (she cannot), how she experiences time (she does not experience it as linear), how her many selves move through the infinite house like beautiful volatile Psyches while her body lies propped on satin pillows.

Mr. Spitzer observes from his usual position, the perimeter. He notices, with his particular brand of resigned precision, that Catherine has a serene unconcern for facts. She introduces the living among the dead and the dead among the living as though the categories were purely administrative: a matter of which list you put someone on, easily revised. Mr. Spitzer finds this maddening and also, somewhere beneath the maddening, true.

Time runs strangely in Chapter 40: stranger than usual, which is saying something. The afterglow comes before the sunset. The echo arrives before the song. A step forward is a step backward. Young is not playing games with chronology here. She is describing what Catherine actually experiences: a consciousness in which cause and effect have loosened their grip on each other, in which the usual sequence of events has become a medley, an imbroglio, a Pandemonium, a Saturnalia.

The chapter closes at the water, the novel's recurring shoreline, with a vision of all places dreaming together, flowing into each other like water over mooned sand. And then its final, quietly astonishing image: the figurehead of a ship knows more than the captain. The figurehead always goes first.

The polyglot voice, and what it means

Cousin Hannah's linguistic range is one of the chapter's most deliberately excessive flourishes, and Young means every word of it.

The Tower of Babel, in Genesis, is where God scatters the human family into a confusion of mutually incomprehensible languages: the origin myth of why we cannot understand each other. Cousin Hannah reverses this. She has gathered back those scattered tongues. She speaks fourteenth-century Saracen and Provencal troubadour verse and “Sanskrit from a holy text printed on a snowflake” and “the hieroglyphic language of the drowned Egyptian sailor.” The dead languages and the languages of the dead: not just Latin or Ancient Greek, but the actual idioms of people whose words went under with them.

What does it mean that Catherine (who had no formal education, who spent her adult life horizontal) understands all of this? Young suggests it is not comprehension in any rational sense but a deeper kind of knowing, the kind that bypasses the educated mind and arrives directly in the dreaming one. Catherine understands Cousin Hannah's voice the way you understand music you've never heard: the meaning precedes the translation.

There is also something Young is saying about the reach of a certain kind of life. Cousin Hannah traveled. She fought. She crossed borders that were supposed to be closed to her. She accumulated experience the way a coastal cliff accumulates weather. By the time she died, she had absorbed so much of the world that the world's languages had become, in some sense, hers. This is not magic. It is biography carried to its extreme.

Time backward, time sideways

The afterglow came before the sunset. “The echo came before the song.”

Hold these phrases. Young is not being willfully obscure; she is giving you the phenomenology of Catherine's consciousness as precisely as she can. In a mind organized by association rather than sequence, by emotional resonance rather than clock time, the effect often arrives before the cause. You feel the grief before you know what you've lost. You hear the silence before you know the music has ended. The afterglow illuminates the moment before the sun has set, because in Catherine's interior, time has the structure of feeling rather than the structure of physics.

Einstein, as we've noted in earlier chapters, said the distinction between past, present, and future is a stubbornly persistent illusion. Young renders what it feels like to live without that illusion as a stabilizing frame. It is not peaceful. It is a medley, an imbroglio: the Miltonic word Pandemonium, which Young deploys with full awareness that Milton invented it for the capital of Hell; Saturnalia, the Roman festival when social order was deliberately inverted and all rules suspended. Catherine's experience of time is festive and infernal simultaneously. The sequence has been freed and the freedom is terrifying and beautiful.

Mr. Spitzer's serene unconcern

The phrase is almost affectionate: Catherine's serene unconcern for facts. Not frantic unconcern, not anxious unconcern. Serene. She has made her peace with the unreliability of the factual order.

Mr. Spitzer observes this the way a person observes weather they cannot change. He notes that she introduces the living among the dead as a matter of course; she assumes that certain living people have already passed to the other shore, and greets the dead as though they have simply stepped in from a long journey. This is, from one vantage, delusional. From another, it is a form of perception that takes seriously what official reality prefers to ignore: that the dead are still present in memory, still active in the way the past is always active in the present, and that the line between them is a social convention more than a metaphysical one.

Mr. Spitzer, who has been waiting his entire life to prove that he himself died long ago, has no standing to dismiss this. His entire existence is organized around a boundary, between his living self and the dead twin he never stopped mourning, that he too refuses to honor in practice. He is the rational observer who cannot leave the territory he claims to be merely observing.

Many beautiful volatile Psyches

Young's image of Catherine's inner “selves, many beautiful, volatile Psyches wandering about”) is one of the novel's most precise and generous formulations.

Psyche, in Greek mythology, is the soul itself: the mortal woman who becomes divine through love and suffering, who is given impossible tasks and completes them, who descends to the underworld and returns. She is also the human mind: the totality of the self, conscious and unconscious, Jungian and Freudian at once. To say Catherine is many Psyches is to say she is not one soul but a gathering of souls, each of which has its own history of impossible tasks and descents and hard-won returns.

And the Psyche knot, a woman's hair twisted into a coil at the nape, is one of the incidental details Young folds in, the physical memory of the mythological. Catherine's body carries, even in its horizontal stillness, the residue of every self it has housed.

Cousin Hannah's possible conversion, and final skepticism

To All My Darlings notes, almost in passing, something the chapter implies rather than states: that Cousin Hannah may have converted from Christianity to Islam during her travels, and subsequently abandoned religion altogether, possibly arriving at atheism or a radical skepticism that found no shelter in any creed.

This trajectory (from the faith she was born into, to a faith she chose, to no faith at all) is entirely consistent with the Cousin Hannah we know. She was a woman who moved through everything: countries, causes, languages, belief systems. She did not settle. She tested things by living them, and if they failed the test, she left. Her final skepticism, if that is what it was, is not cynicism. It is the earned position of someone who went further into faith's claims than most believers are willing to go, and found the territory did not hold.

Young holds this alongside Catherine's opium religion, and alongside Mr. Spitzer's inherited melancholy, without deciding which of them is right. The novel is not a theology. It is a house with many rooms, and each room has a different answer to the question of whether anyone is listening.

The figurehead knew more

Save the last image for last, because it earns its place.

A ship's figurehead is the carved figure mounted at the prow: the part that goes first, that meets the water before the hull, that faces everything the ship is sailing into. It is not the captain. It has no authority. It cannot steer. But Catherine believes the figurehead knows more than the captain does, because it always goes first.

This is Young's image for intuition, for the part of consciousness that precedes rational navigation, that meets experience before the organized mind has had time to categorize it. The figurehead is the part of us that runs ahead of our explanations, the part that has already felt the thing before the captain has been informed.

In Catherine's case, this is her entire mode of being. She is all figurehead, no captain. She meets everything first, raw, before the organizing intelligence can sort it into the proper categories of real and imagined, living and dead, past and present. The water roars at her mouth. She has been going first for as long as anyone can remember.

Whether this is a gift or a catastrophe, Young declines to say. Both, the novel suggests. Both, always.

What to hold onto

Chapter 40 is the novel moving at full velocity inside Catherine's mind, and you should let it move. Don't try to inventory every allusion or keep track of every historical figure who passes through. Let the medley be a medley. Let the Pandemonium be Pandemonium.

What to hold onto: Cousin Hannah's voice, arriving with Gothic cathedral weight, carrying the languages of everyone who ever lived and crossed a border and left a word behind. Catherine's many selves wandering the infinite house, none of them the captain, all of them the figurehead, all of them going first into the water. And Mr. Spitzer at the edge of it, tapping his “tiny diamond-headed gold walking stick” against his teeth, knowing he is watching something he cannot explain and cannot leave.

The water flows over the mooned sand. The sentinels sleep at the shifting borderlands. There is no place, but there are all places dreaming together.
