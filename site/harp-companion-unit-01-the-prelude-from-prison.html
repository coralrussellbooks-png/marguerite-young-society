Unit 1, pp. 1-16: The prelude from prison

Background for this unit: The History in Harp Song for a Radical

Young opens at the end of Debs's life, with his wartime imprisonment, then circles through the summer of 1916, when a San Francisco bombing and the death of James Whitcomb Riley fell on the same day. The unit sets up the book's method: every figure is a doorway into a larger story.

Major entries

The Espionage Act (1917) and Sedition Act (1918) (pp. 4-5)

  • What it was: The Espionage Act, passed in June 1917 after the United States entered World War I, made it a crime to interfere with the draft or military operations. The 1918 amendments, known as the Sedition Act, went further and punished "disloyal" speech about the government, the flag, or the war.

  • Why it matters: Roughly two thousand people were prosecuted under these laws, most of them Socialists, anarchists, pacifists, and members of the Industrial Workers of the World. The Supreme Court upheld the convictions in 1919, including Debs's, in cases that shaped free speech law for decades.

  • Debs's case: Debs spoke against the war at Canton, Ohio, on June 16, 1918. He was sentenced to ten years, entered prison in April 1919, and was moved from Moundsville, West Virginia, to the Atlanta Federal Penitentiary that June.

  • How Young uses it: She treats Wilson's refusal to pardon conscientious objectors as "so black a page in American history" (p. 4). The prison becomes the book's frame, what she calls "the long leap to the Ultima Thule of prison" (p. 3).

  • Further reading: Geoffrey R. Stone, Perilous Times: Free Speech in Wartime (2004).

Debs's 1920 campaign from prison (p. 5)

  • What it was: The Socialist Party nominated Debs for president while he was prisoner number 9653. Campaign buttons read "For President, Convict No. 9653." He received about 914,000 votes.

  • Why it matters: It remains the only serious presidential campaign run from a prison cell. President Warren Harding commuted his sentence, and Debs walked out on Christmas Day 1921. His citizenship was not restored in his lifetime.

  • Where the record differs: The book's chronology gives the vote as 6 percent of the total. The standard figure is about 3.4 percent. His highest share came in 1912, at about 6 percent.

  • Further reading: Ernest Freeberg, Democracy's Prisoner: Eugene V. Debs, the Great War, and the Right to Dissent (2008).

Norman Thomas (pp. 4-5)

  • Who he was: A Presbyterian minister (1884-1968) who turned pacifist during World War I and joined the Socialist Party in 1918. After Debs's death he led the party and ran for president six times, from 1928 to 1948.

  • How Young uses him: Thomas is her witness to Debs's character, the man who "would preach Debs's funeral sermon from the front porch of his home at Terre Haute" (p. 4) and who called Debs one of the world's darlings in the Emersonian sense.

  • Further reading: W. A. Swanberg, Norman Thomas: The Last Idealist (1976).

Tom Mooney, Warren Billings, and the Preparedness Day bombing (pp. 8-11)

  • What happened: On July 22, 1916, a bomb exploded at a San Francisco parade held to promote military "preparedness" for war. Ten people died and about forty were injured. Labor organizers Tom Mooney and Warren Knox Billings were convicted on testimony later shown to be perjured.

  • Who Mooney was: Born in Chicago in 1882, he grew up in Holyoke, Massachusetts, where his widowed mother sorted rags in a paper mill. He became an iron molder, a Socialist, and a labor militant. He rode on Debs's campaign train, the Red Special, in 1908.

  • Why it matters: The case became an international cause, the most famous frame-up in American labor history before Sacco and Vanzetti. Wilson himself asked California to reconsider after protests reached Russia.

  • Where the record differs: Young writes that Mooney "was to spend more than two decades on death row" (p. 10). In fact his death sentence was commuted to life in 1918. He spent the next twenty years in San Quentin until Governor Culbert Olson pardoned him in January 1939. Billings was released later that year.

  • Further reading: Richard H. Frost, The Mooney Case (1968).

James Whitcomb Riley (pp. 11-16)

  • Who he was: Indiana's most beloved poet (1849-1916), born in Greenfield. He wrote in Hoosier dialect, first under the pen name "Benjamin F. Johnson of Boone" in the Indianapolis Journal. His best-known poems include "Little Orphant Annie" and "When the Frost Is on the Punkin."

  • Connection to Debs: They were close friends and drinking companions. Riley wrote a poem about Debs praising his generosity, though he never shared Debs's politics.

  • How Young uses him: Riley died at his Lockerbie Street home in Indianapolis on the same day as the Preparedness Day bombing, July 22, 1916. Young sets the two events side by side (p. 11), the gentle poet of childhood against the violence of a nation drifting into war. He was buried at Crown Hill Cemetery.

  • Further reading: Elizabeth J. Van Allen, James Whitcomb Riley: A Life (1999).

Debs's union path: the Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen to the American Railway Union (p. 6)

  • What it was: Debs spent the 1880s building the Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen, a craft union of skilled railroad firemen. In 1893 he left it to found the American Railway Union, which took in every railroad worker regardless of craft.

  • Why it matters: This shift, from craft unions to one industrial union, is the main organizing argument in American labor history. The ARU grew to about 150,000 members in a year and was destroyed in the Pullman strike of 1894.

  • Where the record differs: Young places the firemen's brotherhood "under the aegis of the Knights of Labor" (p. 6). The two were separate organizations. The railroad brotherhoods kept their distance from the Knights.

  • Further reading: Nick Salvatore, Eugene V. Debs: Citizen and Socialist (1982).

Shorter entries

  • Horace Traubel (p. 6): Walt Whitman's secretary and literary executor, and a socialist editor. He was a friend of Debs and a living link between Debs and Whitman.

  • The Chinese exclusion question (p. 7): Congress barred Chinese laborers in 1882, the first federal law to exclude immigrants by nationality. Debs's answer, "The souls of Chinese children are yellow butterflies" (p. 7), is unverified.

  • Carl Sandburg (pp. 7-8): Before his fame as a poet he was a Socialist Party organizer in Wisconsin. He later wrote admiringly of Debs, and Young notes he went over to Wilson.

  • The Red Special (pp. 8, 10): The train the Socialist Party chartered for Debs's 1908 campaign against William Jennings Bryan and William Howard Taft.

  • The Lusitania (p. 8): The British liner sunk by a German submarine on May 7, 1915, killing 1,198 people, including 128 Americans. Young places the sinking in the North Sea, but it happened off the south coast of Ireland.

  • The International Socialist Congress at Stuttgart (p. 9): The 1907 gathering of the Second International, which debated how socialists should respond to a coming European war.

  • Colonel George Harvey and Newton D. Baker (p. 10): Harvey was a magazine editor who first promoted Wilson and then turned on him. Baker was Wilson's secretary of war, from 1916 to 1921.

  • The Haymarket affair (p. 10): The 1886 Chicago bombing after which four anarchists were hanged on weak evidence. Unit 18 covers it in full.

  • Elijah W. Halford (p. 14): Editor of the Republican Indianapolis Journal, which published Riley's early poems. He later served as private secretary to President Benjamin Harrison.

  • Benjamin Harrison (pp. 14, 16): Indiana Republican, president from 1889 to 1893. He reappears in Units 10 and 19.

  • "Pastels of Men" (pp. 12-13): Debs's late series of short memorial sketches. Young names Riley, Wendell Phillips, Robert Ingersoll, Eugene Field, and John Swinton among its subjects.

  • Eugene Field (p. 12): Chicago newspaper humorist whose column "Sharps and Flats" ran in the Chicago Morning News. He wrote the children's poem "Little Boy Blue."

  • John Swinton (pp. 12-13): New York labor journalist whose Striking for Life (1894) defended the Pullman strikers.

  • Wendell Phillips and Robert Ingersoll (p. 12): The abolitionist and the agnostic orator, both covered at length in Units 10 and 13.

  • The Dennison House (p. 16): A leading Indianapolis hotel, where Debs and Riley often met.

Allusions

  • Ultima Thule (p. 3): The ancient name for the farthest northern land, used for any remote extreme.

  • Abel and Cain (p. 3): Genesis 4. Young calls Debs the innocent Abel "branded Cain" by his accusers.

  • Little Nell and Tiny Tim (p. 7): Child characters from Dickens's The Old Curiosity Shop and A Christmas Carol.

  • Emersonian, Transcendental (p. 4): The New England philosophy of Ralph Waldo Emerson, stressing individual conscience and the divine in nature.

  • Don Quixote (p. 16): Cervantes's idealist knight. Young uses the name for both Debs and Riley.

  • Croesus (p. 15): The proverbially rich king of ancient Lydia.
