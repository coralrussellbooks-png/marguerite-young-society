The Philosophy in MMMD

Chapter 23. The Ending as Demonstration

← Previous · Philosophy of MMMD Home

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · The Stone Deaf Man · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Identity and the Self · Illusion and Reality

Allusions: Deafness and Sign Language · The History of Opium

Chapters discussed: 2, 82

Definition from research

The last chapter of Miss MacIntosh, My Darling performs everything the novel has argued. It is worth reading slowly, in the same way Chapter 2 read the monist passage, as a proposal, an accumulation, and a collapse. But this time the pattern turns in a new direction.

The Inventory

The chapter opens with a list of losses. Vera reflects on what she could have confessed to Esther Longtree: “that my mother was dead, that the opium paradise had crumbled, that the sea had swept over the dark house with its golden turrets and towers,” that “the only property which was left to me was this string of pearls I wore, that I was in mourning for three deaths, that I was in love with an older love than memory provided” (ch. 82).

This is accumulation of the familiar kind. Each system in the novel has now ended. Catherine is dead and her invented world has been swept away. Mr. Spitzer has learned which brother he was, and the answer is “the dead.” Miss MacIntosh is remembered “with her bald head upon the waves.” Everything the novel built has been loaded until it gave way.

Vera imagines telling all this to Esther and imagines Esther’s reply: “So had not everyone lost something? Think, she might say, of those who do not see, those who do not hear!” Loss itself becomes common ground.

The Final Proposal

Then Vera states the novel’s last philosophical proposal. She faces “that reality which had been a dream and which had crumbled, perhaps into reality, perhaps into a dream if nothing was real.” And she offers a thought that gathers all the trials into one: “Perhaps there is always a sense missing, that sense which would make the world real.”

This is a new and precise claim. The novel has shown that every system fails. Here it suggests why. Every person, every philosophy, is missing a sense. The deaf lack hearing. The blind lack sight. But even those with all five senses lack the one sense that would make the world fully real. No system has it, and so every system is partial. The missing sense is the novel’s name for the gap that no master key can close.

The Collapse of Illusion Itself

And then comes the turn that completes Young’s philosophy. Vera has spent the night listening to a voice she half believes she dreamed, a man singing of silence and of “the things awakening not.” She thinks it may have been imagined. Then she writes: “But I was wrong, for all was real, and he was real. He was the stone deaf man whom I saw in the dim corridor at dawn.”

For more than a thousand pages, the novel has tested every category against experience and watched it fail. Here, at the very end, the last category fails too. The category of illusion itself gives way. After all the dreams and mirages, Vera finds a real man. The novel does not return to naive realism. Miss MacIntosh’s granite road is gone for good. But neither does it rest in the easy conclusion that everything is illusion. That conclusion, too, is a system, and it too breaks under the weight of a real person standing in a corridor at dawn.

This is why Young’s philosophy is complete in the only way it can be. The method of accumulation, applied long enough, undoes even the doctrine that nothing is real. What remains is neither reality nor illusion but a person, met.

What Is Left

The stone deaf man is himself a portrait of Young’s ethics. He lost his hearing at fourteen. “It took him a long time to learn to dream. He became a painter, and he was also the artist of his life.” He “heard with his eyes.” Missing a sense, he built a life from the senses he had. An old woman on a country road told him the words that could stand as the novel’s last teaching: “life is imaginary, and this be an imaginary world. And the silence had its tongues, and love came before words were or ever will be” (ch. 82).

Vera loves him “as a mother might love a child,” “as a woman loves a man,” and “for the silences and for all the tongues of silence speaking the name of love.” She is carrying his child, and he gives the child its purpose: “our child will be our ear.” What one generation lacks, the next may supply.

Then comes the crossing point: “I do not know the night in which I conceived as I do not know the night in which I was conceived.” Vera stands between two unknowns, her origin and her child’s, and she does not need to know either.

She lets go of her last possession. “My pearls were lost,” she writes, “and never would I find my pearls in the morning light, never even look for them.” The inheritance from the opium house is scattered over the bluffs, and she does not search. After a whole novel of searching, this refusal to search is itself a form of peace.

The two lovers plan a wedding, and the reason given is disarming: “We would be married, for legality helped.” A legal marriage is a construction, and they know it. They will use it anyway, because it helps. This is living in the invention with open eyes.

The guest list follows, and it is the last of Young’s great accumulations. Wildflowers, birds, waters, clouds, moon and sun and stars, “and the old mule that climbed over the fence and Esther Longtree, for she was also our love.” In every earlier list, experience piled up until a system broke. In this list, experience piles up and nothing breaks, because there is no longer any system to break. The accumulation has become a celebration. Everything is invited.

“Esther Longtree would be me.” The narrator and the waitress, the searcher and the one who waits, the mother who will bear a child and the mother of the stillborn, become one. It is the monist vision of the first chapter, “Every heart is the other heart,” returned in a new form. Not as a doctrine seen by an omniscient eye, but as a plain statement about two women in a small town who will share a future.

The Last Words

The novel ends with a sign. Esther hangs it in the restaurant window: “Owt to luntsch. bee bak in a whale.” And then: “For she could not spell either.”

After more than a thousand pages of some of the most elaborate prose in American literature, the last words are misspelled. The sign still works. Everyone who reads it will know what it means. And the misspelling opens a second meaning. “Bee bak in a whale” is a joke on “be back in a while,” and it is also Jonah, swallowed by the great fish and returned to the world. The promise of return, which eternal recurrence could not keep, comes back as a waitress’s spelling error. It is a small, comic, human return, not the cosmic cycle of the philosophers.

The word “either” is quietly important. Esther could not spell either, just as the deaf man could not hear, just as Vera could not know the night of her conception, just as every person lacks the sense that would make the world real. Everyone is missing something. The sign goes up in the window anyway.

That is the system’s final demonstration. Every category has failed. Every master key has been tried and found wanting. And a woman who cannot spell hangs a sign that everyone can read, promising to be back.
