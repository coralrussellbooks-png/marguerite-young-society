Movement Six: What the Novel Teaches

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · The Stone Deaf Man

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Deafness and Sign Language · Music and Composers

Pillars: Pillar 1 (Not One Self) · Pillar 3 (Grief Doesn't End) · Pillar 5 (Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary) · Pillar 6 (Unfinished Is Honest)

Chapters discussed: 14, 29, 42, 49, 52, 55, 59, 62, 82

There is a figure who recurs in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling with the insistence of a motif and the weight of a philosophical position: the impossible artist. Mr. Spitzer catalogues them in a passage of extraordinary compression: "the blind train conductor, the blind signalman signalling to the blind man, the deaf musician, the deaf conductor of the mute orchestra, the blind portrait painter or he who painted portraits only in water changing its conformation as he painted, perhaps when a bird flew over head, the blind lighthouse keeper with his light shining both by day and by night, seeming a sunken star, star and its ripple, the crippled dancer dancing in the wind, the mute swan singing not even when it died." In Chapter 49 he “trusted all persons who had lost a sense,” and “They were all his loves.” This is not sentiment. It is an architectural necessity. The impossible artist is the figure through which Young builds her deepest claim about what art is for and what the novel itself is doing.

The impossible artist is not someone who overcomes a limitation. That would be an inspirational narrative, which is not what Young is writing. The impossible artist is someone who creates from inside a condition that structurally prevents the creation, and whose work is therefore purest because it cannot be executed in the ordinary way. The blind portrait painter paints in water: each portrait is completed and then immediately unmade by the movement of the medium, altered by a bird flying overhead, returned to formlessness by the current. The painting exists for the moment it is made and then it is gone, but it was made. The mute swan does not sing even when it dies: the song that is supposed to be its greatest utterance never arrives, and the silence is the utterance. The violinist without arms cannot play, which means every performance he imagines is more perfect than any performance he could give. In Chapter 52, Mr. Spitzer sees Cousin Hannah herself as “a type of all those who tried to do that which they could not do—like the violinist without arms, the footless dancer, the blind portrait painter, the mute singer.”

The most elaborated instance of the impossible artist in the novel is a man Mr. Spitzer encounters in Chapter 62 who spent twenty years as a second violinist in the Philadelphia Symphony without once touching his bow to the strings. He played beside the other musicians, moving his bow with "the utmost mathematical precision, the most delicate gestures and knowledge of music," creating "the illusion of music where no music was." He was never discovered. The great conductor complimented him, pointed to him as the perfect example. Then one day, absent-mindedly, "prompted by his instinct for self-destruction, for failure," he touched the strings. He was immediately dismissed. "Was it right that for one false note, he should be driven forth, even as an Ishmael among men—that he should beg upon the streets?" The question is not rhetorical. Young means it. Twenty years of perfect silence, of music so precisely not-played that it was indistinguishable from playing: this is the impossible artist at his most extreme. The one note that ended his career was the only note that was real.

Mr. Spitzer himself is the novel's central impossible artist: a deaf composer of unwritten music. He writes “his silent music written on his shirt cuffs” (Chapter 29), composing nocturnes, elegies, fugues “without instruments or voices” (Chapter 14), arranging the flight of birds into notes “on the imaginary scale, his scale which had no lines” (Chapter 59). He writes “a dirge for a dead clam, an evening sonata to a dead ear.” The music cannot be heard because he cannot hear it and because it has never been performed and because the instruments and voices it requires do not exist in the world of sound. It exists fully, however, in the world of the novel.

Mr. Spitzer's deafness does not prevent his composition; it transforms its nature. He has, perhaps, “only a deaf man’s memory of sound” (Chapter 42), and in Chapter 55 he plucks at “his dead ear which was the doorway to intricate chambers, his deaf ear.” He hears every sound he has ever heard, preserved intact because no new sound can displace it. The deaf composer's inner world is more densely musical than any hearing composer's, because nothing in it is competing with the external world. The impossible artist does not compensate for the missing faculty. The missing faculty becomes the source. The blind portrait painter cannot be distracted by what the face actually looks like. The violinist without arms cannot be betrayed by technique. The mute singer cannot be limited by what her voice can produce. Each impossibility strips away the interference between the inner vision and its expression, leaving only the vision.

Grief runs through the impossible artist in the form Young gives him: he is always also the survivor. These are "old cripples who had been the kings of speed, or thought they had, and that was the same thing at certain levels of debate." The footless dancer was once a dancer. The blind portrait painter once saw faces. The stone deaf man at the novel's end "could remember how he had drifted into sleep and what were those last sounds he heard—the clatter of the moon vines upon an old, rotted porch—the far baying of a dog—the long white curtains blowing in the wind." The impossible artist carries the permanent weather of what was lost inside the condition that replaced it, and the work is made from both: from the memory of the faculty and from the transformation of its absence into something else.

Here the impossible artist becomes the novel's argument about itself. Young spent eighteen years writing a 1,198-page novel in a literary culture that had no adequate framework for receiving it. The novel was too long, too dense, too uncompromising in its refusal to summarize. It was, in the terms of its own central motif, an impossible work. The violinist who never touched his strings had "succeeded even beyond his own wildest dreams" by creating the illusion of music where no music was. Young created the illusion of a conventional novel's readability, its forward movement, its character and plot, while actually building an entirely different kind of structure underneath, a philosophical architecture that becomes visible only after you have spent enough time inside it to understand what the walls are made of. The novel appears to be doing one thing and is doing another, like the Philadelphia violinist, for twenty chapters, fifty chapters, a hundred chapters, until the reader has learned to hear what is actually being played.

The impossible artist is what the novel enacts about itself: that the work most worth doing is the work that cannot quite be done by the instruments available, that the gap between the vision and the execution is not failure but the space in which the most honest art lives. Mr. Spitzer's silent music will never be performed. It is nonetheless composed. That it exists only in the novel, unheard, written “upon his shirt cuff where his brother had kept the horse scores” (Chapter 59), is not a limitation. It is the form that its truth requires.

The impossible artist is also unfinished. Mr. Spitzer's music is never completed in the world's sense, because it is never played. In Chapter 14 he is pictured at home “writing his silent music, a dirge for a dead clam, an evening sonata to a dead ear, a fugue without instruments or voices.” Young does not count the silence against him. The demand that a work be finished, performed, and heard is the demand the novel refuses, and the refusal is where its integrity lies.
