Movement Six: What the Novel Teaches

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · The Stone Deaf Man

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Deafness and Sign Language · Music and Composers

Definition from research

There is a figure who recurs in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling with the insistence of a motif and the weight of a philosophical position: the impossible artist. Mr. Spitzer catalogues them in a passage of extraordinary compression: "the blind train conductor, the blind signalman signalling to the blind man, the deaf musician, the deaf conductor of the mute orchestra, the blind portrait painter or he who painted portraits only in water changing its conformation as he painted, perhaps when a bird flew over head, the blind lighthouse keeper with his light shining both by day and by night, seeming a sunken star, star and its ripple, the crippled dancer dancing in the wind, the mute swan singing not even when it died." He is drawn to them magnetically, he says, more than to any other category of human being. This is not sentiment. It is an architectural necessity. The impossible artist is the figure through which Young builds her deepest claim about what art is for and what the novel itself is doing.

The impossible artist is not someone who overcomes a limitation. That would be an inspirational narrative, which is not what Young is writing. The impossible artist is someone who creates from inside a condition that structurally prevents the creation, and whose work is therefore most pure precisely because it cannot be executed in the ordinary way. The blind portrait painter paints in water: each portrait is completed and then immediately unmade by the movement of the medium, altered by a bird flying overhead, returned to formlessness by the current. The painting exists for the moment it is made and then it is gone, but it was made. The mute swan does not sing even when it dies: the song that is supposed to be its greatest utterance never arrives, and the silence is the utterance. The violinist without arms cannot play, which means every performance he imagines is more perfect than any performance he could give.

The most elaborated instance of the impossible artist in the novel is a man Mr. Spitzer encounters who spent twenty years as a second violinist in the Philadelphia Symphony without once touching his bow to the strings. He played beside the other musicians, moving his bow with "the utmost mathematical precision, the most delicate gestures and knowledge of music," creating "the illusion of music where no music was." He was never discovered. The great conductor complimented him, pointed to him as the perfect example. Then one day, absent-mindedly, "prompted by his instinct for self-destruction, for failure," he touched the strings. He was immediately dismissed. "Was it right that for one false note, he should be driven forth, even as an Ishmael among men—that he should beg upon the streets?" The question is not rhetorical. Young means it. Twenty years of perfect silence, of music so precisely not-played that it was indistinguishable from playing: this is the impossible artist at his most extreme. The one note that ended his career was the only note that was real.

Mr. Spitzer himself is the novel's central impossible artist: a deaf composer of unwritten music. He writes his silent music on his shirt cuff, composing nocturnes, elegies, fugues "without instruments or voices," arranging migratory birds in their patterns of flight into musical notation on an imaginary scale that has no lines. He writes "a dirge for a dead clam, an evening sonata to a dead ear." The music cannot be heard because he cannot hear it and because it has never been performed and because the instruments and voices it requires do not exist in the world of sound. It exists fully, however, in the world of the novel.

The plural self pillar illuminates what the impossible artist means architecturally. Mr. Spitzer's deafness does not prevent his composition; it transforms its nature. He hears with "a deaf man’s memory of sound," which is to say he hears every sound he has ever heard, preserved intact because no new sound can displace it. The deaf composer's inner world is more densely musical than any hearing composer's, because nothing in it is competing with the external world. The impossible artist does not compensate for the missing faculty. The missing faculty becomes the source. The blind portrait painter cannot be distracted by what the face actually looks like. The violinist without arms cannot be betrayed by technique. The mute singer cannot be limited by what her voice can produce. Each impossibility strips away the interference between the inner vision and its expression, leaving only the vision.

The grief as permanent weather pillar runs through the impossible artist in the form Young gives him: he is always also the survivor. These are "old cripples who had been the kings of speed, or thought they had, and that was the same thing at certain levels of debate." The footless dancer was once a dancer. The blind portrait painter once saw faces. The stone deaf man at the novel's end "could remember how he had drifted into sleep and what were those last sounds he heard—the clatter of the moon vines upon an old, rotted porch—the far baying of a dog—the long white curtains blowing in the wind." The impossible artist carries the permanent weather of what was lost inside the condition that replaced it, and the work is made from both: from the memory of the faculty and from the transformation of its absence into something else.

The recursive construction pillar is where the impossible artist becomes the novel's formal argument about itself. Young spent sixteen years writing a 1,198-page novel in a literary culture that had no adequate framework for receiving it. The novel was too long, too dense, too uncompromising in its refusal to summarize. It was, in the terms of its own central motif, an impossible work. The violinist who never touched his strings had "succeeded even beyond his own wildest dreams" by creating the illusion of music where no music was. Young created the illusion of a conventional novel's readability, its forward movement, its character and plot, while actually building an entirely different kind of structure underneath, a philosophical architecture that becomes visible only after you have spent enough time inside it to understand what the walls are made of. The novel appears to be doing one thing and is doing another, like the Philadelphia violinist, for twenty chapters, fifty chapters, a hundred chapters, until the reader has learned to hear what is actually being played.

Young said she asked her writing students to attend not to what a work stated but to what it enacted. The impossible artist is what the novel enacts about itself: that the work most worth doing is the work that cannot quite be done by the instruments available, that the gap between the vision and the execution is not failure but the space in which the most honest art lives. Mr. Spitzer's silent music will never be performed. It is nonetheless composed. That it exists only in the novel, unheard, written on a shirt cuff beside the horse scores his dead brother kept, is not a limitation. It is the form that its truth requires.
