Chapter 15

Summary

Still on the night of her fourteenth birthday, Vera stands by a roaring sea under a moon like a white skull and runs laughing into the surf. For the first time she judges Miss MacIntosh: her proverbs, her umbrella carried indoors, her windows thrown open in winter, her bare, cold bedroom, and above all that she “could not swim a stroke” and would be dragged down by the first great wave. Vera recalls the day’s long sermon of sayings, Death as “the bridegroom,” marriage as a lottery, the warnings to keep close to the house, and laughs at her dream of a false wedding. The sea flares into colors like the end of time, then rolls back into blackness. Once the tide carried the marble Saul out to sea and he never returned. Far off, the old blind sheep dog barks at the waves.

Key passages

From the research

  • Vera stands by the tumultuous sea on her fourteenth birthday, overwhelmed by the “immensity of life” and the idea that imperfection might weave a “higher perfection” than the flawless days she had known. The imagery of the bloodless moon “like a white, thin skull,” the “black waters,” and “waves like primal memories” evokes a sense of mystery, mortality, and awakening. This passage expresses Vera’s complex and heightened emotions about life, truth, and the unknown. It sets a tone of existential questioning and adolescent revelation.
  • Vera muses on the paradox of truth and falsehood, considering the possibility that “all false things should be true” and, conversely, that “all true things should be false,” exemplified by her thoughts about Miss MacIntosh’s truthfulness versus her “false mother” who doubts the external world. This passage delves into the elusive nature of reality and perception, a central theme in the chapter.
  • Vera critically examines Miss MacIntosh’s practical knowledge and emotional coldness, noting her inability to swim or face the “roaring tide” and her peculiar habits like carrying an umbrella indoors or keeping windows open in winter. Miss MacIntosh represents a kind of rigid, conventional wisdom and order, yet Vera perceives her as lacking a deeper understanding of life’s unpredictability and vitality. This passage contrasts youthful openness with adult caution and limitation.
  • Through Miss MacIntosh, the chapter explores themes of death, order, and the human condition. Death is described metaphorically as a “bridegroom” and an “old harpoonist,” inevitable and ever-present. Miss MacIntosh’s sayings such as “He is bare whom virtue hath not clothed,” and “The night is no man’s friend” reveal a worldview grounded in caution, morality, and acceptance of life’s hardships. Her warnings about dangers for a young girl emphasize societal fears and protective control.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s practical and sometimes cynical teachings about marriage, housekeeping, and life’s challenges highlight the limitations imposed on Vera’s future. The advice to “keep one’s eyes open” before marriage but “half shut” afterward underscores the compromises and disillusionments expected in adult life. This passage reflects societal expectations and gender roles, as well as the tension between youthful dreams and harsh realities.
  • The chapter closes with vivid, almost supernatural descriptions of the sea and sky: “the sea was like a crucible of glass,” with “colors so intense” and “myriads of sparks like fireflies.” Vera perceives a cosmic, spiritual dimension to the natural world, imagining “harpers harping on those seas of glass which man shall behold at the end of time.” This passage evokes a sense of awe, transcendence, and the fleeting nature of life, culminating in the image of the dead seagull and the engulfing black waters. It symbolizes the impermanence of existence and the eternal struggle between life and death.
  • “She could not swim a stroke… she would have been dragged down at the first blow by her rainproof, her oilskin hat, her sea boots water-logged, and so many needles and pins and safety pins.” Vera’s scorn is also a warning of how Miss MacIntosh will die.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “If all false things were true, however, then all true things should be false.” Where does Vera’s paradox leave her?
  • “If she had and had still been sane, then she would have been insane.” What does Vera think it costs to understand the deeper things?
  • “Death, she had said, is the bridegroom.” Why does Vera laugh?

Characters

  • Miss MacIntosh answers every personal question with the date of a sinking or a lost battle. What is she hiding?
  • She “never looked at herself in any mirror if she could help it,” yet keeps the blind grandmother’s triple mirror. Why?
  • She warns Vera that “ignorance is no protection.” Is she right?

Connections

  • Vera’s dream of the false wedding in Chapter 10 returns with seals and northern lights for guests. What has changed in how she sees it?
  • Miss MacIntosh cannot swim. What does this foreshadow?
  • The sea once took the marble Saul and returned “a strange marble head.” What else does the sea take and return in this novel?

Reflection

  • Is a life of proverbs a way of living or a way of not living?
  • “The heart of youth… would rather die than live in a vacuum.” Is that wisdom or recklessness?
  • What does it mean to judge for the first time someone who raised you?

Research: Characters

Vera Cartwheel

  • On the cusp of adolescence, filled with a mix of excitement and fear.
  • Seeks deeper truths and experiences beyond the mundane and controlled life offered by adults.
  • Exhibits a strong connection to nature and a rebellious attitude towards imposed limitations.
  • Reflects a philosophical mind, questioning truth, life, and death.

Miss MacIntosh

  • Represents the adult world’s order, prudence, and caution, but also coldness and emotional distance.
  • She is methodical, practical, and somewhat resigned, embodying a worldview that emphasizes survival and control over passion or mystery.
  • Her repeated warnings about danger and death show concern but also a limiting worldview.
  • Her peculiar habits suggest underlying fragility or unresolved fears.

The Old Blind Sheep Dog

  • The sheep dog’s barking is repeatedly mentioned as an ominous sign, something that “had not barked for nothing,” unlike a young dog who barks merely for pleasure. This suggests that the dog’s barking is a deliberate warning of danger lurking nearby, perhaps a threat to Vera’s innocence or safety.
  • Miss MacIntosh explicitly warns Vera to be careful because “someone who would take a young girl by the hair and drag her into the shadows where her cries should not be heard” might be near.
  • The dog’s barking seems to symbolize a protective instinct, alerting to the presence of real dangers in the night that Vera might be tempted to ignore or not fully understand.
  • The dog is described as “old” and “blind,” which adds complexity to its symbolism. Blindness here can be interpreted as a metaphor for the limitations of perception that come with age or experience.
  • While the dog cannot see, it still hears and senses danger, which may suggest that wisdom or vigilance does not solely depend on sight or youthful alertness.
  • The dog’s blindness could also represent the older generation’s partial understanding of dangers, aware but not fully able to prevent them. This aligns with Vera’s reflections on how older people, including Miss MacIntosh, fail to grasp the “heart of youth” and the deeper currents of life and death.
  • The dog’s old age and blindness also evoke mortality and the approach of death, consistent with the chapter’s pervasive meditation on death as a “bridegroom” and the many faces of death.
  • The dog barking at the night and waves can be seen as a harbinger of the inevitable, a reminder that danger and mortality are ever-present.
  • Its presence amid the tumultuous night sea imagery, the black waters, and the skull-like moon amplifies the sense of encroaching darkness and the testing ground of life and death.
  • The old blind sheep dog is isolated in the night, barking at waves and wind, much like Vera’s own sense of separation from the older generation and their “adult fantasies.”
  • The dog’s isolation parallels Vera’s feeling of being alone in her youthful quest for experience, standing against the warnings and restrictions imposed by adults like Miss MacIntosh.
  • On a narrative level, the dog’s barking serves as a tension-building device. It punctuates the chapter’s meditative and philosophical tone with a concrete, audible signal of danger. It brings the abstract themes of death, danger, and innocence into a tangible presence, grounding Vera’s lofty reflections in the physical reality of the night and the environment.

Research: Themes

Youth and Experience versus Adult Convention

See: Freedom and Escape · Order, Chaos, and Discipline

  • Vera experiences a moment of awakening, realizing the vastness and complexity of life beyond the restrictive, controlled world she has known.
  • She contrasts her youthful desire for experience and freedom with the narrow, methodical, and often fearful mindset of the adults, especially Miss MacIntosh.
  • The tension between youthful exploration and adult caution is central: Vera laughs at the warnings and rules, craving life’s fullness despite risks.

Truth and Falsehood

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Vera muses on the nature of truth, suggesting a paradox where if everything seen by adults is false, then the false must somehow be true.
  • This philosophical questioning reflects her struggle to understand reality and the reliability of those around her, especially Catherine and Miss MacIntosh, who represent different kinds of truth and falsehood.

Death and Mortality

See: Death and Mortality

  • Death is a recurring motif, discussed through Miss MacIntosh’s grim but practical philosophy.
  • Death is described as the inevitable “bridegroom” and harpoonist capturing the old and young alike, a force that is both feared and accepted.
  • Vera’s reflections on death are intertwined with natural imagery of the sea and night, symbolizing the unknown and the boundary between life and afterlife.

Nature and the Sublime

See: Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos

  • The powerful sea and the night sky dominate the narrative, filled with vivid, almost mystical descriptions.
  • The sea’s “long, melancholy roarings” and the “bloodless moon” evoke a primal, almost spiritual experience for Vera.
  • Nature is both beautiful and terrifying, a symbol of life’s immensity and mystery beyond human control.

Isolation and Alienation

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Vera feels isolated from the adults, especially Miss MacIntosh, whose coldness and detachment contrast with Vera’s passionate engagement with life.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s peculiar habits (keeping windows open in winter, carrying an umbrella indoors) symbolize a disconnect from practical reality and warmth.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

The Sea

See: The Sea and Water

  • Symbolizes life’s vastness, mystery, and power.
  • The “black, whirling waters” and “primal memories” suggest subconscious depths and timeless forces.
  • The sea’s roar and the waves breaking evoke both danger and excitement.

The Moon

See: Moon and Stars

  • Described as “bloodless,” “white,” and like a “thin skull,” symbolizing death, the passage of time, and the eerie, detached observer of human life.
  • The moon, sea, and death imagery function symbolically to explore deeper themes.

Nature’s Contrasts

See: Roses and Flowers

  • The “humped, naked dunes” with “seaweed,” “Queen Anne’s lace,” and “wild grass” represent the wildness and beauty of life.
  • The “low, stunted, blackened spruce and hemlock” suggest decay and resilience simultaneously.

The House and Its Contents

See: The House

  • The “old, sea-blackened house” with “empty paddocks,” “marble statues,” and “granite birds” reflects decay, history, and the passage of time.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s sparse bedroom symbolizes emotional barrenness and detachment.

Research: Resources

  • “bayberry and beach rose and meadows of billowing Queen Anne’s lace”: Bayberry wax made the colonists’ Christmas candles. Legend says Queen Anne pricked her finger making lace, and the one dark floret at the center of the flower is her drop of blood. See Roses and Flowers.
  • “my false mother who postulated merely as her theory the outer world”: Descartes doubted the existence of the outer world (Meditations, 1641), and Berkeley held that “to be is to be perceived” (1710). See Empiricism (Locke, Berkeley, Hume) and the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “my body gleaming with finny phosphorescence”: Late summer seas glow with bioluminescent plankton when the water is stirred. See The Sea and Water.
  • “a mustard poultice placed across my chest”: The mustard plaster was a household remedy for chest colds, hot enough to blister the skin.
  • “the dark angel of death with whom a prophet met upon a barren hill”: Jacob wrestled the angel at Peniel until the breaking of the day (Genesis 32:24). See Genesis and the Chapter 6 resources.
  • “old Benjamin Franklin had ever said about early to bed and early to rise or a bird in the hand”: “Early to bed and early to rise, makes a man healthy, wealthy, and wise” (Poor Richard’s Almanack, 1735). “A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush” is older, in John Heywood’s proverbs (1546). See American Writers and the Chapter 9 resources.
  • “old John Bunyan had said on the evils of swearing and lying and gambling”: Bunyan confessed his youthful swearing and Sabbath games in Grace Abounding (1666) and drew a whole life of sin, including the seduction of maids, in The Life and Death of Mr. Badman (1680).
  • “as crazy as loons”: The loon’s wild, laughing cry gave the phrase. See Birds.
  • “She could not swim a stroke”: Foreshadowing of Miss MacIntosh’s death in the sea. See Miss MacIntosh and the Chapter 6 resources.
  • “Men and melons, she herself had often said, are hardest to know”: An old proverb: “Men and melons are hard to know.”
  • “still water runs deep”: “Still waters run deep” is a proverb of Latin origin now commonly taken to mean that a placid exterior hides a passionate or subtle nature. Formerly it also carried the warning that silent people are dangerous, as in Suffolk’s comment on a fellow lord in Shakespeare’s Henry VI, Part 2: “Smooth runs the water where the brook is deep” (3.1). See Shakespeare.
  • “the Farmer’s Almanac”: The Old Farmer’s Almanac, published since 1792, with weather forecasts, planting tables, and proverbs. See Ecclesiastes.
  • “a crazy quilt”: A Victorian quilt of irregular scraps of silk and velvet, the craze of the 1880s.
  • “my grandmother’s mahogany dressing table with its three stained mirrors”: The blind grandmother of the garden of the blind in Chapter 12. See Eyes and Blindness and the Chapter 12 resources.
  • “the sinking of the Titanic”: The Titanic struck an iceberg and sank on April 15, 1912.
  • “one swallow does not make a summer”: Aristotle uses the saying in the Nicomachean Ethics (1.7) to discuss happiness and eudaimonia (living well or flourishing). He argues that a brief period of happiness or a single good deed doesn’t make a person truly happy or blessed. Happiness, for Aristotle, is a state of being that develops over a lifetime through virtuous activity. See Aristotle.
  • “a thin meadow is soon mowed, and winter is summer’s heir”: Many of Miss MacIntosh’s sayings are found in George Herbert’s Outlandish Proverbs (1640) and John Ray’s English Proverbs (1670).
  • “a straight line cannot be made straighter and is the shortest distance between two points”: Archimedes took it as a postulate in On the Sphere and Cylinder.
  • “Our cradle stands in the grave”: Bishop Joseph Hall was an English bishop, satirist, and moralist. His contemporaries knew him as a devotional writer, and a high-profile controversialist of the early 1640s. In church politics, he tended in fact to a middle way. See Death and Mortality.
  • “that we know not who or what we are, not even when the hour is late”: Ophelia says, “Lord, we know what we are, but know not what we may be” (Hamlet 4.5). Miss MacIntosh’s version denies even the first half. See Shakespeare.
  • “none should ever see it and live”: “There shall no man see me, and live” (Exodus 33:20). See The Bible and the Chapter 43 resources.
  • “Death loves a young maid and an old man”: Ralph Waldo Emerson’s poem “Illusions”: “Sleep is not, death is not; / Who seem to die live. / House you were born in, / Friends of your spring-time, / Old man and young maid, / Day’s toil and its guerdon, / They are all vanishing, / Fleeing to fables, / Cannot be moored.” See American Writers and Death and Mortality.
  • “Death loves a shining mark”: Edward Young, Night Thoughts (1742-45). See Death and Mortality.
    • . . .Death’s approach (if orthodox my song) Is hasten’d by the lure of Fortune’s smiles. . . Death loves a shining mark, a signal blow; A blow, which, while it executes, alarms; And startles thousands with a single fall. It encapsulates the novel’s meditation on how death interacts with life’s brilliance and beauty, serving as a reminder that mortality is universal but also drawn to what is luminous and significant
  • “there being so many doors to let out life”: “Death hath so many doors to let out life,” from The Custom of the Country (c. 1620) by John Fletcher and Philip Massinger. Webster’s Duchess has the same thought: “death hath ten thousand several doors / For men to take their exits” (The Duchess of Malfi 4.2). See Death and Mortality.
  • “is always the bridegroom”: The old image of Death and the Maiden, set to music by Schubert. See Weddings and Funerals, Death and Mortality, and the Chapter 6 resources.
  • “wedlock is padlock”: From John Ray’s English Proverbs (1670). See Weddings and Funerals.
  • “Man was but a bubble breaking on a dark shore”: Homo bulla, “man is a bubble,” Varro’s saying, gathered by Erasmus in his Adages.
  • “Man was but a shadow and a breath”: “Man is like to vanity: his days are as a shadow that passeth away” (Psalm 144:4). See The Bible.
  • “Man was but a poor, forked animal”: Lear: “unaccommodated man is no more but such a poor, bare, forked animal as thou art” (King Lear 3.4). See Shakespeare.
  • “eggs and oaths were soonest broken”: A Danish proverb.
  • “The old sheep dog barking in the night had not barked for nothing”: The proverb is “An old dog barks not in vain,” from George Herbert’s Outlandish Proverbs (1640). See The Old Blind Sheep Dog and Dogs.
  • “the fairest flower is plucked the earliest”: The Passionate Pilgrim (1599) is an anthology of 20 poems collected and published by William Jaggard that were attributed to W. Shakespeare on the title page, only five of which were considered authentically Shakespearean. One reads, “Sweet rose, fair flower, untimely pluck’d, soon vaded.” See Shakespeare and Roses and Flowers.
  • “the grasshoppers are always surprised by winter”: Aesop’s “The Ant and the Grasshopper,” and “Go to the ant, thou sluggard; consider her ways, and be wise” (Proverbs 6:6). See Fairy Tales and Folklore and The Bible.
  • “she had been a good shepherd”: “I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep” (John 10:11). See The Bible.
  • “the crooked crab which can never be taught to walk a way that is not crooked”: Aesop’s “The Crab and Its Mother,” in which the mother tells her child to walk straight and cannot do it herself. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “children should be seen but not heard”: The oldest form, from about 1450, is “A maid should be seen, and not heard.”
  • “the apples of speech were silver, but the apples of silence were golden”: “Speech is silver, silence is golden.” Its modern form most likely came from Arabic sayings as early as the ninth century. Young’s apples come from Proverbs 25:11: “A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in pictures of silver.” See The Bible.
  • “Better a castle of bones than of stones”: An old Scottish saying: better a garrison of strong men than stone walls.
  • “woman’s heart is folly and deception”: “A foolish woman is clamorous: she is simple, and knoweth nothing” (Proverbs 9:13). See The Bible.
  • “man is born to trouble as the sparks fly upward”: “Yet man is born unto trouble, as the sparks fly upward” (Job 5:7). See The Book of Job.
  • “man is a little soul carrying around a heavy corpse”: Marcus Aurelius is quoting Epictetus: “Thou art a little soul bearing about a corpse, as Epictetus used to say” (Meditations 4.41). See Stoicism.
  • “man is a substance clad in shadows”: John Sterling.
  • “Men are fallen”: The Fall of man is the Christian term for the passage of the first man and woman from innocent obedience to God to guilty disobedience. See Genesis and Augustine and Christian Theology.
  • “this life was a way of thorns”: “The way of the slothful man is as an hedge of thorns” (Proverbs 15:19). See The Bible.
  • “one should keep one’s eyes open, but after marriage, one should keep them half shut”: Poor Richard: “Keep your eyes wide open before marriage, half shut afterwards” (1738). See American Writers and Weddings and Funerals.
  • “the sheet anchor was gone”: A ship’s largest anchor, kept for emergencies, and so a last hope.
  • “harpers harping on those seas of glass”: “And I saw as it were a sea of glass mingled with fire… having the harps of God” (Revelation 15:2). See Revelation and the Apocalypse and the Chapter 14 resources.
  • “when the sky rolls back like a scroll”: “And the heaven departed as a scroll when it is rolled together” (Revelation 6:14). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “darkness without face and void upon the deep”: “And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep” (Genesis 1:2). See Genesis.
  • “for this earth was but the testing ground”: The Christian idea of life as a probation, called “a probationary state” in the Book of Mormon (Alma 12:24).
  • “the marble Saul”: Saul was a king of Israel. His reign marked the transition from tribal society to statehood. He killed himself during the battle with the Philistines to avoid capture. Having been anointed by Samuel, Saul is told of signs indicating that he has been divinely appointed. The last of these is that Saul will be met by an ecstatic group of prophets leaving a high place and playing the lyre, tambourine, and flutes. Saul encounters the ecstatic prophets and joins them. See The Bible and the Chapter 43 resources.

Vocabulary

Aphorism
A short, pointed saying that states a general truth.
Athwart
Across, from side to side; also, against.
Bayberry
A shrub of the Atlantic coast whose waxy berries were boiled to make fragrant candles.
Begetting
Fathering; producing offspring.
Bespeak
To show or indicate.
Blear
Dim and watery, as eyes are with age or weeping.
Conch
A large sea snail with a spiraled shell that can be blown like a horn. Held to the ear, it seems to hold the sound of the sea.
Corpuscular
Made of tiny particles.
Crazy quilt
A quilt pieced from irregular scraps of fabric.
Crucible
A heat-resistant pot for melting metals. Figuratively, a severe test that changes a person.
Curlicue
A fancy twist or curl.
Drouth
Drought.
Drove
A herd or flock driven or moving together.
Effaced
Erased; wiped out.
Equivocation
The use of vague or double-edged words to avoid committing oneself.
Genealogy
A record of a family’s descent.
Hackneyed
Worn out by overuse; trite.
Harpoonist
One who hurls a harpoon.
Headland
A high point of land jutting into the sea.
Laconically
Using very few words.
Myriad
A countless number.
Nimbus
A halo or glow of light.
Oilskin
Cloth made waterproof with oil; a raincoat of it.
Paddock
A small field where horses are kept.
Pagoda
An Asian tiered tower with multiple eaves, usually a Buddhist temple or shrine.
Phosphorescent
Glowing faintly in the dark.
Postulated
Assumed as true, as the basis for an argument.
Poultice
A soft, warm mass spread on cloth and laid on the body as a remedy.
Queen Anne’s lace
Wild carrot, a weed with flat white flower clusters like lace.
Shades
Ghosts; the spirits of the dead.
Sheet anchor
A ship’s largest anchor, kept for emergencies; a last hope.
Shingle
A beach of small, rounded stones.
Skulking
Lurking or moving about in a sneaking way.
Translucence
The quality of letting light pass through without being clear.
Tumultuous
Loud, wild, and disorderly.
Undertow
A current beneath the surface that pulls back out to sea.
Wayward
Willful and hard to control.
Weathervane
A turning device on a roof that shows the direction of the wind.
