Miss Havisham and the Single Wound

In Charles Dickens's Great Expectations, Miss Havisham stops all the clocks at twenty minutes to nine: the exact moment she received the letter telling her she had been jilted. Every clock in Satis House reads twenty minutes to nine. The wedding cake rots on the table. The wedding dress yellows on her body. The single wound has stopped all time in the house, and the house enforces the wound's permanence by refusing to let the clocks move. There is one moment, one betrayal, one stopped hour, and the entire architecture of Miss Havisham's existence is organized around it.

Vera's mother in the Cartwheel house has stopped clocks too. But she has not stopped them all at the same hour. "Each clock having been stopped at a different hour over that period of time when my mother, retiring from the world, had gone to bed, by slow degrees and not suddenly, as if she had hesitated a great deal, as if her decision had not been an easy one to make". This is the architecturally crucial distinction. Miss Havisham's wound is singular, precise, locatable in time. The wound in the Cartwheel house is plural, distributed, spread across a withdrawal that happened slowly, over time, without a single hour at which everything stopped. There is no moment of jilting. There is no letter. There is a long, hesitant retreat from the world, each clock stopped at the moment its room was abandoned, its hour the record of one more small departure from ordinary time.

Miss Havisham's stopped clock is an act of will: time shall not move. The Cartwheel house's stopped clocks are an accumulation: time stopped here, and then there, and then in that room, over months or years, as the opium lady went to bed and did not get up. The Cartwheel house does not refuse time. It records the shape of a withdrawal. Each stopped clock is a chapter in the history of a person becoming gradually unavailable to the world. The clocks together are a biography told in arrested hours.

The Clock That Starts Up in the Dark

Miss Havisham's clocks do not start. They are stopped and they stay stopped. They are monuments to a fixed point. The Cartwheel house is different: sometimes, in a remote part of the house, especially at night, a stopped clock would suddenly start up, wheezing or musical, seeming to make this one last effort. The stopped clocks in the Cartwheel house are not dead. They retain the potential to start. They occasionally exercise it, wheezily, in the dark, ringing the wrong hour, ringing three bells and then four bells from different corners, and then going silent again.

This is the AND Philosophy's recursive construction principle in the form of a timepiece. The stopped clock represents the arrested moment, the wound that has frozen its hour. But it is not permanently inert. It retains, somewhere in its mechanism, the capacity to move again. It does not move reliably. It does not move at the right time. When it starts up in the dark, ringing three dim bells and then four from another corner, the darkness does not lift. The clocks do not return the house to ordinary time. They mark, from their arrested positions, an unreliable and partial attempt at continuation.

Miss MacIntosh has one clock: Old Reliable, the Big Ben alarm clock she winds every day. It does not repeat the hours. A day being gone when a day was gone. It is not enchanted. It does not stop. It is set to ring at the hour for rising up and for lying down, and it will. Miss MacIntosh lives by the one clock in the house that actually keeps time, the one clock that refuses both the enchantment of arrest and the unreliable ambition of the clocks that start up in the dark. She is the only person in the house who knows what time it is, and she drowns in the September ocean while knowing exactly what time it is.

Nobody Knew What Time It Was

The Cartwheel house's stopped clocks are not only about the opium lady's withdrawal. They appear in a second context: a dreamlike passage in which all the clocks are stopped and nobody knew what time it was, the funeral wreaths were on the doors, and the voice of the turtle-dove was not heard in all that land. This is a different register of stopped time: not the biography of a private withdrawal but the apocalyptic suspension of all ordinary temporal reckoning. Golden bowl broken. Wheel broken at the cistern. These are Ecclesiastes: the end of the body, the end of its capacity to receive sensation, figured as the stopping of mechanism.

This second stopped-clock passage makes explicit what the first implies: the Cartwheel house clocks are not only psychological but cosmological. They are not only recording one woman's slow retreat from the world. They are also registering the world's slow retreat from meaning, the long accumulation of losses that makes any single clock's hour arbitrary. In the Ecclesiastes register, all clocks stop because all mechanisms fail eventually, because the golden bowl is always going to break, because the wheel will always stop at the cistern. Miss Havisham's clock stops because of a specific human cruelty. These clocks stop because that is what clocks do: they stop.

The AND Philosophy Reading: What Many Stopped Hours Do

The AND Philosophy's fifth pillar, recursive construction, is enacted by the multiplicity of stopped clocks. If Miss Havisham's single stopped clock represents the wound that cannot move past its hour, the Cartwheel house's many stopped clocks at different hours represent the plural self's relationship to time: there is no single wound, no single hour at which everything stopped, no single moment that, if you could only return to it and do it differently, would allow the clocks to start again. There are many such moments. They accumulated. The withdrawal was gradual. The record of it is distributed across the house in arrested hours that no longer correspond to each other.

The third pillar, grief as permanent weather, is also here. Miss Havisham's stopped clocks are the architecture of a grief that refuses to move. The Cartwheel house's stopped clocks are the architecture of a grief that never had a single starting point to refuse. The grief preceded any specific cause. The opium lady went to bed by slow degrees and not suddenly, as if she had hesitated a great deal. The stopped clocks record not a refusal but a hesitation extended over years until it became permanent. Grief as permanent weather is not a storm you could have prevented. It is the slow accumulation of arrested hours in all the rooms of the house, each at a different time, none of them agreeing on when it became too much.

Miss Havisham cannot leave her stopped house. She burns in it, at the end, wearing the yellowed dress, the cake gone to dust. The stopped house destroys her because it is a perfect monument to a single wound that she has refused to move past. The Cartwheel house does not destroy its inhabitant in the same way. Vera's mother lives in it for years, entertaining imaginary guests, entertaining a thousand roaring voices and then a thousand canaries blown here by the storm. The house with many stopped clocks is more livable than the house with one, because it is not organized around a single refusal. It is organized around an accumulation of small withdrawals, each at a different hour, none of them final, some of them occasionally starting up again in the dark.
