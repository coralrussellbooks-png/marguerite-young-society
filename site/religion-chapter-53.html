Chapter 53

On Chapter 53 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Christianity and Religion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 24, 2026

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Philosophy: Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres

Allusions: Astronomy

A "baffled look... as if suddenly an angel had passed" gives Mr. Spitzer the air of one brushed by the unseen and misunderstood by those around him.

“Cockerel Church, the golden cock upon the weathervane”: The Cockerel Church was the New Brick Church on Hanover Street in Boston’s North End, later the Second Church, nicknamed for the gilded rooster weathervane Shem Drowne made for it in 1721; the rooster now turns above the First Church in Cambridge. The cock became Peter’s emblem because he denied Jesus three times before the cock crew (Matthew 26:74-75), and Pope Gregory I is said to have deemed it a suitable Christian symbol. Pope Nicholas I decreed that roosters could be used on weathervanes. Protestant churches used this symbol on church steeples to distinguish them from Catholic churches.

"the roof of the Sistine Chapel, painted with angelic figures" sets his imagination among the great Christian art.

"the tombs of the Medicis," hung with "golden pawn-shop balls," blends the sacred vault with the pawnbroker's sign, itself once tied to Saint Nicholas.

He could sooner describe "Mt. Ararat, where Noah's Ark was supposed to have rested," than make himself understood.

“Death rode a stumbling nag”: In Revelation, “I looked, and behold a pale horse: and his name that sat on him was Death, and Hell followed with him” (Revelation 6:8, KJV), and the swift horse of the archer is the white horse of Revelation 6:2: “he that sat on him had a bow” (KJV). Young’s death rides a stumbling nag, an old, worn-out horse: not graceful, certain, or dignified, but awkward and uncertain in its approach. See Revelation and the Apocalypse. Earlier in Chapters 9 and 14.

“Martin Luther was a great believer in bowling”: Martin Luther, the Protestant Reformation founder, had a bowling lane built next to his house. Mr. Spitzer thought Martin Luther recommended bowling as an outlet for “...human aggression and the thirst for conquest…” Organized sports has been compared to simulated war and as a diversion for the populace.

“who fought a spurred angel until the angel lost its feathers”: In Genesis 32:22-32 Jacob wrestles with an angel. See Genesis.

His arithmetic of the heavens, "seven for the singing planets, one for the silent star, the black-out," sets seven, the number of completeness and the old music of the spheres, against a single dark, fallen star, harmony shadowed by loss.

“he knew the Jew who was knocked out by an angel”: It recalls Jacob at Peniel: “And Jacob was left alone; and there wrestled a man with him until the breaking of the day” (Genesis 32:24). Jacob was left lame and renamed Israel. Young turns the wrestling match into a boxing match. This may refer to Genesis 32 that describes Jacob wrestling with a “man” throughout the night. In the Book of Hosea, this figure is explicitly called an “angel.” During the struggle, Jacob is injured, specifically having the socket of his hip put out of joint. This injury left him with a limp. Jacob is considered a foundational figure in Judaism and the patriarch from whom the nation of Israel descends. “Yea, he had power over the angel, and prevailed: he wept, and made supplication unto him” (Hosea 12:4, KJV). See Genesis and Judaism. Earlier in Chapters 3, 6 and 15.

"the pebble embedded in Goliath's forehead" recalls David and Goliath in 1 Samuel 17, the giant felled by a single stone, here likened to the moon in a cloud.
