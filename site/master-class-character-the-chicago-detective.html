The Chicago Detective: The Looker Who Saw a Corpse

← Joe Goldberg · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · The Little Toy Salesman →

Spoiler note: This portrait is written for readers who have finished the novel. It discusses the character’s whole story, including how it ends.

Characters: Esther Longtree · Lisa Lunde · Mr. Weed · The Chicago Detective

He is the one whose whole profession is seeing, and he looks straight at the living woman and sees a dead one. Three winters running, a "Chicago detective and specialist in murder mysteries" comes to the frozen meadows hunting an escaped killer, a man who murdered his wives "because of the cracked church bells always ringing" and someone calling him yellow, and three times he fails to find the murderer and finds instead Esther Longtree lying on the cold ground with her eyes wide open, "whom he had always taken to be a corpse." He kneels to examine the body. His rimless eyeglasses drop from his nose. And by her account he "raped her mysteriously" there on the ground he took for a death scene, and went away leaving more clues than the killer he came for, "his eye-glasses, his calling card, a bone button, the frozen conception, a thread, and a single spot of brown blood."

He is the anti-witness in his coldest form, and the book has been building toward him. Mr. Weed reduces the living to weights and never meets them; Lisa Lunde sees through people and so never sees them; the detective looks with the trained, professional eye whose entire job is to see what is really there, and he mistakes a sleeping woman for a murdered one because he arrived with the case already framed. His eyes are "pale, unastonished." Nothing surprises him, which sounds like mastery and is the disease, because a man who cannot be surprised has stopped looking and started confirming. He steps over the real mystery, the living, grieving, uncatalogued woman at his feet, to keep hunting the abstract murderer who fits his file, and in doing so he becomes a criminal in a case he does not know he is in, "the beginning of another mystery."

He cannot solve the one that matters, and the book says he is the one "who could solve all murder mysteries but his own." He is also, of all Esther's fathers, the most plural, "partly wax and partly human," "always in disguise," arriving "once as a wandering locksmith, his pockets full of keys and locks, once as a soap salesman," once as himself, so that the man whose trade is fixing identity, naming the culprit, pinning the fact, is himself the least fixed and most waxen figure in the chapter. He is the official reality walking, the law that decides what counts as a crime and a victim, and he is Esther's father's kind, the policeman's kind, the law that looks and does not see. There is "nothing about her case in the law books." The keeper of the record has no category for her, so he files her as a corpse and moves on.

And here is the wound under all of it. Esther wanted him to detect her. "If he had only detected her, this could not have happened." "Something in her had yearned to be questioned by him, to be dragged forth," to be truly investigated, truly seen, made real by the cross-examination she both dreaded and craved. She would have answered nothing, and still she longed to be asked. And he was "indifferent to the mystery." The one figure whose job was to look hard at her looked past her. The subject offered herself to the witness, and the witness was bored. That is the deepest cruelty in the chapter, almost worse than the rape, that the man of attention had no attention to spare for the actual person, only for the escaped abstraction he never caught.

The turn for a writer is the sharpest indictment in the set, because the Chicago detective is the writer who believes he stands outside the material, cool and expert, detecting the truth of a scene he did not disturb. He arrives with his frame, decides the living woman is a corpse in someone else's story, and steps over the real thing to chase the plot he came for. He is "unastonished," which for a maker is fatal, the incapacity to ever be surprised by what is actually on the ground. And in the act of investigating from his imagined outside, he turns out to be inside, complicit, leaving his own evidence, fathering his own stillborn, generating the next mystery he will fail to solve. Set him beside Weed the procedural killer, and beside the hangman's certainty that the imagined thing leaves a real body. The detective is the looker whose looking is itself the violation, the writer who reduces a person to a scene, who solves everyone and cannot solve himself, "partly wax," and the wax is the thing a writer must not let harden over the looking. The book leaves him wiping his hands, walking away into the winter, still looking for the wrong body, having stepped over the one that was alive and asking to be found.
