Definition from research

Young does not treat Islam as a subject to explain so much as a colored light to write by. Islamic and "Oriental" imagery gives the novel a fluid, dreamlike backdrop for its questions about reality and illusion, freedom, and the shapes an identity can take. The imagery reaches the reader mostly through three figures: Catherine, Cousin Hannah, and, at a remove, Mr. Spitzer and Miss MacIntosh.

In Catherine's opium dreams the East is pure escape. Her room fills with "gold-turbaned Egyptians" and the "perfume of Oriental hanging gardens," and she gives orders to phantom servants as though they were flesh. Mr. Spitzer catches the same vision from outside, "moon minarets and crescents and jasper domes of desert cities." Seraglios and harems recur as images of veiled mystery and male control, and shadowy figures like the "Black Moor" and the black coachman drift between the real and the dreamed, their very gender uncertain.

For Cousin Hannah the East is a battlefield. The militant suffragette carries her war on men into "Araby" and the seraglios, riding a horse named "El-Burraq" after the Prophet's steed and going "by camel into places where no woman had ever gone before." She travels under the name "Al Hamid," fights turbaned Turks and Moroccan pirates, and casts herself as a "liberator of woman." Her best scenes turn the tables on the male gaze: disguised as an Arab king, she lifts her veil in a seraglio to show a pursuing sheik a "great, gaunt, bearded face" and drives him mad. The novel keeps unsettling the picture, letting the greatest sheik of the East turn out to be a woman, and letting a sheik beg Hannah's help because he too feels trapped by the harem, "hen-pecked" and desperate to escape. The East here is not a single patriarchy but a place where even men want out of their roles.

Miss MacIntosh will have none of it. She waves the whole exotic apparatus away as "balderdash, nonsense," measuring it against a Middle Western childhood in What Cheer, Iowa, a place with "no palm trees, no pearl divers." She prizes use and plainness over the "princely dignity" the East lends to something as ordinary as an umbrella.

So Islam in the novel is less a faith examined than a space imagined: flight for Catherine, a stage for Cousin Hannah's rebellion, nonsense to be swept aside for Miss MacIntosh. Young neither praises nor condemns the religion itself. She weaves its imagery into a larger, intensely personal story about identity, belief, and the border between the dreamed and the real.
