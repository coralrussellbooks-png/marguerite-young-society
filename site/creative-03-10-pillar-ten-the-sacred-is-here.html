Pillar: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here · Across every book

Or, Why The Sacred Is Ordinary

When life gets incredibly difficult, chaotic, or confusing, almost every major religious and philosophical tradition makes the exact same move. They tell us to look up and out. They point toward a transcendent beyond, an unshifting sky, a distant deity, or a future utopia to anchor us and make sense of our current suffering. We are trained to look past the messy, ordinary world in search of a higher, absolute truth. The thinking goes: this world is broken and painful, but somewhere else, somewhere beyond, there is something that is perfect and whole. Hold on. Endure. Transcend. That is where meaning lives.

But Marguerite Young refuses that move at every single level of her system. She brings us a message that is profoundly comforting and fiercely radical: the sacred is here, or it is absolutely nothing.

The Magic of the Ordinary

The word immanence is just a philosophical way of describing what is right here, entirely present within our actual, day-to-day experience. When you add the word radical to it, it means this idea goes all the way down to the bedrock. There is no outside escape hatch. There is no secret baseline reality hiding underneath the world. There is no external guarantee to fix things for us. What is here is what there is.

But far from being cold or empty, Young shows us that this immediate world is absolutely bursting with depth. When we attend to our surroundings with the unblinking focus of a true witness, the ordinary landscape lights up with immense significance. The bus route becomes sacred. The dresser with the forgotten wig becomes sacred. The person sitting across from you becomes sacred. Not because we have decided to treat them that way. Not because we have projected meaning onto them. But because they are here, they are real, they are irreplaceable.

To illustrate this, Young's pillar connects with the work of indigenous scientist and author Robin Wall Kimmerer. Kimmerer teaches that the natural world is not a collection of dead, objective items for us to use and extract value from. It is a vibrant community of living subjects. The plants are not resources. They are persons. The water is not a utility. It is a subject with its own agency and its own gift to offer. The soil is not an inert medium. It is alive with millions of organisms working together.

This is what it means to find the sacred in the ordinary. It means recognizing that the ordinary world is not dead matter waiting for us to impose meaning on it. It is alive. It is conscious. It is already sacred. We do not make it sacred. We simply finally notice that it always was.

Young practices this exact mindset in her writing. In her world, everything around us becomes deeply sacred: the daily bus routes, the old wig resting on a dresser, our permanent grief, and our beautifully constructed, changing lives. Meaning is not handed down to us from a distant throne. It is carefully, lovingly crafted right here by plural selves building provisional structures together. It lives in the accumulation of small acts of attention and care. It lives in the choice to keep showing up, to keep witnessing, to keep building, even though nothing is perfect and nothing will be finished.

Starving the War Gods of Their Power

So, how does finding the sacred right under our feet deliver the ultimate blow to authoritarian control?

Throughout history, tyrants and oppressive movements have almost always relied on an appeal to a grand, distant beyond to justify their violence. They command people to sacrifice their lives, look away from immediate cruelty, and march to war by invoking abstract, absolute ideals. A mythical golden age. A supreme leader. A grand national destiny. A holy cause. A thousand-year empire. A glorious future. They use a fake, elevated sky to make regular human lives feel small, disposable, and cheap.

The appeal to transcendence is the dictator's greatest weapon. When you teach people that meaning lives somewhere else, somewhere better, somewhere that requires sacrifice in the present moment, you can get them to accept almost any amount of suffering here and now. You can get them to hurt others. You can get them to destroy. All in the name of some distant goal that will supposedly make it all worth it.

Young's final pillar completely starves that strategy of its power. It insists that there is nowhere else. There is no beyond.

It eliminates the excuse for violence. When you believe the sacred is found only in the ordinary world and the living beings right in front of you, you can never be talked into destroying real people for the sake of a distant, abstract ideology. You cannot sacrifice your neighbor for the glory of the nation. You cannot accept their suffering for the promise of a better tomorrow. Because the person in front of you is not a means to an end. They are the end. They are the sacred. They are the whole point.

It breaks the leader's spell. Dictators try to set themselves up as external gods or absolute anchors. They claim to represent something beyond, something transcendent, something that justifies their power. Young’s philosophy reminds us that there are no absolute anchors outside of human relationship and human place. It exposes the leader's performance as a fragile human invention. Once you see that the emperor has no clothes, the spell is broken. You cannot be controlled by someone who is just like you, unless you agree to treat them as more than human.

It grounds true collective power. When we realize that bus routes, town halls, community gardens, and school buildings are already filled with all the significance we will ever need, we stop waiting for a political savior to arrive. We stop looking for a leader who will rescue us and deliver us to the promised land. We find our power right under our feet, in the places we already are, with the people we already know. We realize that the sacred is not something that is coming. It is something that is already here, and we are already standing in it.

When we look at each other and our ordinary world with the full attention of a witness, we realize that here is the whole of it. We are already enough, and we have everything we need to stay free.

The Whole Point Is Here: Fannie Lou Hamer

To understand what it means to locate the sacred entirely in the ordinary world, in the specific bodies and specific ground of the people immediately around you, consider Fannie Lou Hamer, who never stopped pointing downward.

Hamer was born in 1917, the twentieth child of Mississippi sharecroppers. She spent the first forty-four years of her life working other people's land in Sunflower County, one of the poorest and most violently enforced counties in the Mississippi Delta. She did not have a platform. She did not have an education the system recognized. She had the ground under her feet and the people standing on it with her, and she treated both as sacred without ever using that word.

In 1962, she went to a meeting and heard for the first time that she had the right to register to vote. She did not experience this as an abstraction. She experienced it as something that was already true, already hers, being withheld from her by a system that depended on her believing the withholding was natural. She went to the courthouse the next week. She was turned away. She went back. She was evicted from the plantation where she had worked for eighteen years. She kept going back.

What drove her was not a vision of a distant future. It was the specific faces of the specific people she lived among, whose lives were being ground down right now, on this ground, in this county, by this specific arrangement of power. She was not working toward an arrival. She was insisting that the people in front of her were already the whole point. Their lives, ordinary and difficult and irreplaceable, were already the sacred thing. There was nothing beyond them worth more.

When she testified before the Credentials Committee at the 1964 Democratic National Convention, she did not speak in the language of ideology or grand national destiny. She spoke from her body, from a specific night in a Winona, Mississippi jail where she had been beaten so severely the damage was permanent. She named what had been done to her. She named where it happened and who did it and what it felt like. She did not abstract the violence upward into a symbol. She kept it right here, in her specific flesh, in the specific ground of Mississippi, among the specific people whose names she knew.

President Johnson was so threatened by the directness of her testimony, by the way it refused to be managed or softened or pointed toward a more comfortable elsewhere, that he called an emergency press conference to pull cameras away from her. He understood, even if he could not have named it this way, that a woman speaking from that location, refusing transcendence, insisting that what was happening right here to these people right now was the whole of the moral question, was more dangerous than any abstract political argument. You cannot negotiate with someone who will not look away from what is directly in front of her.

She was offered paths out. She could have left Mississippi. She could have taken her national profile and her extraordinary gifts and built something in a larger arena. She stayed. Not out of limitation but out of commitment to the ground she was standing on, to the people whose lives were the reason she had started in the first place. The Delta was not a stepping stone. It was the sacred place. The ordinary people in it were not a constituency to be managed toward a better future. They were the future, right now.

Authoritarian systems and the plantation economy she fought her whole life both depended on the same fundamental lie: that the lives of the people in front of you are preparation for something, instruments toward something, acceptable casualties of something more important happening elsewhere. Hamer refused that lie every time she opened her mouth. She kept pointing at the people directly around her and saying: these lives. This ground. Right now. That is the whole of it.

She died in 1977, in Mississippi, having never left. The ground she is buried in is the same ground she was born on, the same ground she refused to abstract into a symbol, the same ground she spent her life insisting was already sacred. She did not transcend it. She stayed in it, fully, until she was done.

The Practice

Begin to pay attention to the moments in your day when you are tempted to look past what is actually in front of you. When you are having dinner with someone you love, notice if you are thinking about how this moment will look in memory, rather than actually tasting the food and listening to the person. When you are walking through your neighborhood, notice if you are thinking about a better place you would rather be, rather than noticing the light on the buildings and the smell of the air.

Try this: for one day, try not to improve anything. Do not plan. Do not fix. Do not try to make your life better. Just notice. Just witness. Just be present to what is there. Pay close attention to a cup of coffee, or a conversation, or the way light falls through a window. Notice how much depth becomes visible when you stop trying to transcend the present moment and actually arrive in it.

Also notice when you are being offered a false transcendence. When a system tells you to sacrifice now for a better future. When a leader promises you something beyond if you will just comply. When an ideology asks you to treat abstract ideals as more important than the real people in front of you. Pause. Remember Young. Remember that the sacred is here. Starve that appeal to a distant beyond. Keep your attention on what is real, what is present, what is right in front of you. That is where your power lives.
