Chapter 81: The Little Toy Salesman

Research notes: Chapter 81

Characters: Esther Longtree · Vera Cartwheel · Walter

Themes: Guilt, Innocence, and Justice · Love · Motherhood, Pregnancy, and Birth · Time

Symbols: Clouds and Storms · Pregnancy, Birth, and Children · Snow, Ice, and Winter · The Sea and Water

Philosophy: Freud and Jung · Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Gambling and Playing Cards · Judaism · Revelation and the Apocalypse · Suicide in History and Literature

Chapter 81 is the penultimate chapter of Esther’s sequence, and it turns outward. After ten chapters deep inside Esther’s interior life (her stillborn, her dying fathers, her mud baby, her detective, her twin, her silver spoon, her Walter) Young now introduces a figure from outside: a man who comes into the Greasy Spoon and tells Esther the story of the end of the world.

He is the little toy salesman. He is small and childlike, bandy-legged, tearless, with only three teeth and pale blue eyes and patchy hair. He drinks milk from a baby bottle. He carries rubber ducks and teddy bears and a baby bottle as his samples. His mother was a world-famous beauty and spy. His father was a windowpeeper. He was a bottle baby, never held at the breast, never given the ordinary comforts of being someone’s child.

He is the lone survivor of a great storm at sea that drowned everyone else: “the captain, the crew, the women, the children,” and in Young’s cosmic rendering, even the moon and the stars. He sat in a rubber life boat while the world ended. And now he sits at Esther’s counter, and he whispers to her that there will be a great war, and the bad men will “blow up the world some day of calm just for fun.”

Esther is considering marrying him. Not for love. For legality. She wants a legal father for the little shadow boxer. He is the best available option.

What Actually Happens

The little toy salesman comes into the Greasy Spoon. He drinks milk from his baby bottle. He flirts with Esther in a childlike way. He cannot see that she is enormously pregnant. He cannot see much of anything clearly anymore.

He tells her his story, or she reconstructs it: the chapter blurs the line between what he says and what she knows about him. His mother was a spy, beautiful and famous, the Mata Hari type. His father was a windowpeeper. He was a bottle baby from the start, a child who had no one to hold him. He sold toys: rubber ducks, teddy bears, baby bottles. He was at sea when the storm came.

The storm is described with apocalyptic imagery: “a wild, raging storm between the cracking Poles,” “the sea rising in billows to the dark heavens,” the moon and stars falling like sparks and rain of blood. The captain, old and German, goes down with the ship conducting, bright with rage and love. The passengers (conceited, snobbish, oblivious to their doom) drown. The women take the lifeboats. The men jump into the sea. Everyone drowns except the little toy salesman, who survives by accident in a rubber life boat, upheld by swollen bladders and heaving breasts of ocean, not because he wanted to live but because the sea didn’t take him.

The calm after the storm is worse than the storm. He drifts. Around him: floating debris, bloated bodies, the detritus of a world that has ended. A great white shark circles him, which he initially mistakes for a woman. And there is a dead child’s ivory-colored hand, “always uplifted from the slow, swelling waters,” wearing a gold ring like his own. The hand tugs at him. The hand clings to him. He cannot make it let go.

He loses his memory. His taste buds. His tear ducts. His reason. He washes ashore in America: the shore littered with purple oyster sponges, crab shells, jellyfish like glass, bones of dead fish, “little carcasses of other little children,” “playing cards, old junk, rubber tires, birth control devices.” America as the shore where everything washes up and nothing resolves.

He is pursued by a great white shark on the return journey. He survives. He is smaller now than he was before; he tells Esther he is getting smaller all the time. Until the world blows up, things will be this way, so he doesn’t mind too much if the world blows up. When there is a new creation, he can begin again. He whispers into her ear: there will be a great war. The bad men will “blow up the world some day of calm just for fun.” Everything will be poisoned and disappear forever. Music, stars, fireflies, everything.

Esther listens. She remains forever pregnant with her dreams. She wonders who fathered the little shadow boxer. The dead loved the dead, she thinks, and perhaps that is how the world goes on.

The Little Toy Salesman

He is a figure of compressed ironies. He sells toys (the objects of childhood, of innocence, of the uncorrupted) but he was himself never given childhood. He was a bottle baby, never held, never nursed. His mother was a spy; his father a peeping tom. He carries rubber ducks as his samples. He drinks from a baby bottle. He is, in some sense, still the baby he was never allowed to be.

He is also, in his survival, a figure of survivor’s guilt taken to its extreme. He did not want to live, or so it seems: the sea simply didn’t take him when it took everyone else. And Esther, who has presided over so much death, who has carried so many dead children, calls him a murderer for not drowning with the rest. This is the logic of guilt applied in reverse: to survive when others die is to have killed them by surviving. The little toy salesman did not drown “the captain, the crew, the women, the children.” He simply continued to exist when they did not. This is what Esther cannot forgive.

And yet she is considering marrying him. For legality. For the little shadow boxer. She will teach him how to behave, she thinks: how to have normal emotional responses, how to live a normal life. She is maternal toward him in the way she is maternal toward everyone: she sees someone who never had what they needed and wants to provide it. He is her most peculiar candidate. He is also her most available one.

The Storm as Apocalypse

Young does not write the shipwreck realistically. She writes it cosmically. The moon and stars fall. The Poles crack. The sea rises to the dark heavens hammering. The captain conducts as he goes down: “a golden flood of music more beautiful than Bach, Beethoven, or Brahms.” A black mourning band spans the sky from horizon to horizon.

This is not merely a ship sinking. It is the end of the world, rendered in miniature aboard a single vessel. And the little toy salesman sits in his rubber life boat and watches it happen and survives. He is the one person who witnesses the world ending and then washes ashore in America to report it. Young is writing during the nuclear age: the Cold War, Mutually Assured Destruction, the knowledge that the bad men could “blow up the world some day of calm just for fun.” The little toy salesman’s prophecy, whispered into Esther’s ear at the counter of the Greasy Spoon, is the period’s ambient terror made personal: everything will be poisoned and disappear forever. Music, stars, fireflies. To All My Darlings connects his description of the burning sunlight blackening his skin to a nuclear blast, and his account of the world turning into salt or smoke to the specific imagery of Hiroshima. He has already seen the end. He came back to report it.

The German captain who goes down with the ship, bright with rage and love, conducting music as the ship sinks: To All My Darlings connects this to Hans Langsdorff, the German captain who scuttled the Admiral Graf Spee in 1939 to save his crew and then died by suicide to prove he had acted with honor. And to Gustav Schroeder, the German captain who tried to bring 937 Jewish refugees from Germany to Cuba in 1939, was turned away by Cuba, Canada, and the United States, and was forced to return his passengers to Europe. Both men (captains who prioritized their passengers over orders, who faced impossible situations with what honor was available to them) are present in the captain who goes down with the ship conducting.

The Dead Child’s Hand

Throughout the long calm after the storm, one image persists: a dead child’s ivory-colored hand, “always uplifted from the slow, swelling waters,” wearing a gold ring like his own. The hand tugs at him. The hand clings to him. He cannot make it let go and he cannot look away from it. This is the chapter’s most quietly devastating image, and its resonance with Esther’s own history makes it exact. She has buried children in rivers and streams and tree stumps. She has carried children who were never born. She has pressed her thumb to still a birth cry. The dead child’s hand reaching up from the water is what she has been reaching toward and what has been reaching toward her for ten chapters. The ring the dead child wears is like his own. This is the detail that locks the image in place: the hand is recognizable, is kin, wears what he wears. The dead child could be him: the child he never was, the childhood he was never given, the self that the storm should have taken. He survived but his self did not, or not entirely. What clings to him from the water is the part of himself that was supposed to be innocent and was not protected.

America’s Shore

The shore where the little toy salesman washes up is littered: purple oyster sponges, crab shells, jellyfish like glass, bones of dead fish, “little carcasses of other little children,” “playing cards, old junk, rubber tires, birth control devices.” Young’s inventory of what America receives when the world ends and washes ashore is not flattering. It is the accumulated detritus of a civilization: the natural and the manufactured, the chance and the controlled, the bones and the prophylactics.

The birth control devices on the shore are not accidental. Young has spent ten chapters in the world before birth control was legal: Esther’s everlasting pregnancy, her stillborn, her Walter, all of it occurring in a legal and social landscape where women had no effective recourse. The devices washing up on the shore with the dead fish and the old junk and the little carcasses of children: this is where reproductive control ends up in the American accounting, thrown away, found on shores, mixed with everything else that did not get used or was used wrong or was too late.

The End of the World as Prologue

The little toy salesman’s prophecy is that there will be a great war and the bad men will blow up the world. This comes one chapter before Chapter 82, which is the last chapter of Esther’s sequence and the final chapter before the novel returns to Vera’s story. Young is positioning the apocalyptic vision here deliberately: the end of the world is not the end of Esther. She remains forever pregnant with her dreams. The little shadow boxer is still there, still fighting. The dead loved the dead, she thinks, and perhaps that is how the world goes on.

This is not optimism exactly; it is something stranger and more durable. Esther has witnessed so much death and generated so much life and lost most of what she generated. She is still here. The world may blow up on a “day of calm just for fun,” and Esther will still be at the counter of the Greasy Spoon, still swelling with the little shadow boxer, still setting out breadcrumbs for spirits, still knitting the invisible, still more hopeful now than she has ever been.

She is the survivor. Not the accidental survivor of the little toy salesman, who lived because the sea didn’t take him. Esther survives because she refuses not to.

What to Hold Onto

The little toy salesman: bottle baby, spy’s son, peeper’s boy, seller of rubber ducks, survivor of the end of the world, three teeth, tearless, getting smaller all the time.

The German captain conducting as he goes down. “A golden flood of music more beautiful than Bach, Beethoven, or Brahms.” “Bright with rage and love as if he had realized his strength, at last, his weakness.”

The dead child’s ivory-colored hand, “always uplifted from the slow, swelling waters,” wearing a gold ring like his own. The hand that clings and will not let go.

America’s shore: purple oyster sponges, rubber tires, playing cards, birth control devices, “little carcasses of other little children.” The whisper into Esther’s ear: there will be a great war. The bad men will “blow up the world some day of calm just for fun.” Everything will be poisoned and disappear forever.

And Esther, forever pregnant with her dreams. “The dead loved the dead, and” perhaps that is how the world goes on.
