Appendix B: Further Reading

← Appendix A: The Five Frameworks at a Glance · The Weather Inside · Endnotes →

Organized by framework. Each entry includes the source and a brief note on what it contributes to the framework's argument. Where a source speaks to more than one framework, it appears under the framework it most directly supports.

The Chorus Model

William James, The Principles of Psychology (1890). The foundational argument that the self is relational and multiple rather than fixed and singular. James's concept of the social self, that a person has as many social selves as there are individuals who recognize them, anticipates by a century the clinical and neuroscientific research that has been confirming it since.

Richard Schwartz, No Bad Parts (2021). The most accessible entry point into Internal Family Systems, Schwartz's clinical framework for working with the plural self. Less clinical in register than his earlier work and more useful for the general reader. The core argument: psychological health is not the achievement of unity but the development of a relationship among the parts.

Richard Schwartz, Internal Family Systems Therapy (1995). The original clinical text for readers who want the full framework and its therapeutic applications.

Dan McAdams, The Stories We Live By (1993). The narrative psychology argument that the self is not an object to be discovered but a story being constructed in real time. McAdams's research on the life narrative as the primary vehicle of identity provides the psychological grounding for understanding the plural self as a storytelling system rather than a fixed entity.

Bessel van der Kolk, The Body Keeps the Score (2014). While primarily a trauma text, van der Kolk's work on how the body holds the history of the self, and how trauma fragments rather than unifies identity, provides essential context for understanding why the plural self is not a pathology but the ordinary condition that trauma makes more visible.

The Working Story

Shelley Taylor, Positive Illusions (1989). The research foundation for the Working Story framework. Taylor's decades of work on the slightly inflated self-assessments that mentally healthy people carry almost universally remains the most rigorous scientific argument for the adaptive function of the productive illusion.

Michael White and David Epston, Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends (1990). The foundational text of narrative therapy. White and Epston's argument that the self is a story being written in real time, shaped by dominant cultural narratives, and that the work of therapy is to help the person become the author of their own narrative rather than a character in someone else's: this is the clinical grounding for the Working Story audit.

Michael White, Maps of Narrative Practice (2007). A more developed account of the narrative therapy framework, particularly useful for understanding the externalizing of the problem and the concept of unique outcomes: the moments when the person acts outside the dominant narrative that provide the material for a new story.

Jeff Greenberg, Shelley Solomon, and Tom Pyszczynski, The Worm at the Core (2015). The accessible account of terror management theory for general readers. The argument that human culture is largely organized around the management of mortality anxiety provides the deepest context for understanding why the stories we live by carry existential as well as psychological weight.

The Climate of Loss

Elisabeth Kübler-Ross, On Death and Dying (1969). Read as the origin of the stage model and the source of its misapplication rather than as a prescription. Kübler-Ross observed rather than prescribed; understanding what she actually argued is the first step toward understanding how the culture misused it.

Dennis Klass, Phyllis Silverman, and Steven Nickman, eds., Continuing Bonds: New Understandings of Grief (1996). The foundational text of continuing bonds theory. The research collected here directly challenges the detachment model and establishes the ongoing relationship with what is lost as the normal and healthy form of grief rather than the pathological one.

Pauline Boss, Ambiguous Loss (1999). The essential text for understanding the grief that has no clean object or endpoint. Boss's framework is the most useful clinical tool for the specific grief of the relationship that is still continuing, the self that was lost gradually, the loss that has no funeral.

Pauline Boss, The Myth of Closure (2021). Boss's more recent and more accessible work on why the cultural demand for closure is itself a form of harm. Useful for readers who have been told they have not grieved correctly.

Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut, The Dual Process Model of Coping with Bereavement (1999). The oscillation model, the research that establishes healthy grief as movement between loss-orientation and restoration-orientation rather than linear progression. Available as a journal article rather than a book; worth finding for readers who want the full research argument.

Joanna Macy and Molly Young Brown, Coming Back to Life (1998, revised 2014). The foundational text of the Work That Reconnects, Macy's framework for processing ecological grief and staying present to the world's losses without being destroyed by them. Essential for readers whose grief literacy has extended to the planetary scale.

Glenn Albrecht, Earth Emotions (2019). Albrecht's development of the concept of solastalgia and the broader vocabulary of ecological emotion. The most useful single text for naming the grief of the changed home landscape and the full range of emotional responses to environmental loss.

The Standing Still

Daniel Siegel, The Developing Mind (1999). The foundational neuroscience text for the window of tolerance concept. Siegel's interpersonal neurobiology provides the scientific grounding for understanding why the witness position is not a spiritual achievement but a neurological capacity that can be developed and maintained.

Daniel Siegel, Mindsight (2010). The more accessible account of Siegel's framework for general readers. The clearest explanation of how the observer self functions neurologically and why the development of self-as-context rather than self-as-content is the foundation of psychological flexibility.

Steven Hayes, Kirk Strosahl, and Kelly Wilson, Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (1999). The clinical foundation of ACT. Hayes's distinction between self-as-content and self-as-context, and the defusion practices that create distance between the self and the thought, provide the most practical clinical tools for developing the standing still capacity.

Steven Hayes, A Liberated Mind (2019). The accessible account of ACT for general readers. More useful than the clinical text for readers who want the framework without the therapeutic apparatus.

Jonathan Shay, Achilles in Vietnam (1994). The foundational text on moral injury. Shay's argument that the specific psychological damage of betrayed moral expectation is distinct from trauma and requires distinct responses is essential for understanding the standing still under sustained pressure, particularly the pressure of watching the worse thing be chosen repeatedly.

Jonathan Shay, Odysseus in America (2002). Shay's extension of the moral injury framework to the reintegration of veterans and, by extension, to anyone navigating the return from sustained moral exposure to a world that has not shared the experience.

The Reef Method

Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun, Trauma and Transformation (1995). The foundational research on post-traumatic growth. Tedeschi and Calhoun's careful documentation of the measurable development that occurs through sustained engagement with significant loss and rupture provides the scientific grounding for the reef's core claim: that the wound is the material, not only the damage.

Richard Tedeschi, Jane Shakespeare-Finch, Kanako Taku, and Lawrence Calhoun, Posttraumatic Growth: Theory, Research, and Applications (2018). The most comprehensive account of the post-traumatic growth research for readers who want the full scientific argument.

Esther Thelen and Linda Smith, A Dynamic Systems Approach to the Development of Cognition and Action (1994). The complexity theory foundation for understanding human development as iterative rather than programmatic. Technical in register but essential for readers who want the scientific grounding for the reef's argument that growth proceeds through the accumulation of iterations rather than linear progression toward a fixed endpoint.

Clare Graves, the work collected and synthesized in Don Beck and Christopher Cowan, Spiral Dynamics (1996). The developmental psychology argument that growth moves in spirals rather than lines, revisiting earlier territory from new vantage points. The most accessible account of Graves's research and its implications for understanding the nonlinear shape of a human life.

Relational Foundations: Further Reading for Chapter Eight

John Bowlby, A Secure Base (1988). The most accessible single volume of Bowlby's attachment theory for general readers. The foundational argument that the self develops in relationship and that the quality of early attachment shapes the nervous system's fundamental orientation toward other people provides the relational grounding for understanding why the interior work of the five frameworks is also always relational work.

Murray Bowen, Family Therapy in Clinical Practice (1978). The foundational text of Bowen family systems theory. Bowen's concept of differentiation, the capacity to maintain your own functioning and values while remaining in emotional contact with people who are important to you, is the most useful single clinical concept for understanding what the plural self requires of its relationships.

Peter Fonagy, Gyorgy Gergely, Elliot Jurist, and Mary Target, Affect Regulation, Mentalization, and the Development of the Self (2002). The technical foundation of mentalization theory and affect regulation research. For readers who want the full scientific argument for the difference between emotional suppression, flooding, and integration.
