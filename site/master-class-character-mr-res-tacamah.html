Mr. Res Tacamah: The Author Who Was a Bottle

← The Sea-House Servants · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Mr. Chandelier →

Characters: Lisa Lunde · Mr. Chandelier · Mr. Res Tacamah · Mrs. Hogden · Vera Cartwheel

He is a drug bottle. In the sealed house by the sea, Vera's mother, the great recluse, holds crowded imaginary evenings, and among her guests is "Mr. Res Tacamah, the drug bottle with ears to hear her ravings, her commands." He is "my mother's only permanent companion," the one who is always there when the fickle others come and go, and the book gives him the most unnerving job description in the whole household: he is "the author of so many fantasies." The bottle that produces the visions is named as the maker of them, promoted from object to gentleman, given ears to listen and a hand in every dream, "any number of forms shifting and merging into each other."

This is the mother's whole method, the constructing of reality by naming, carried to its purest point. She turns her furniture into company. A light fixture becomes a suitor; a medicine bottle becomes a man of letters. And the book, which keeps insisting that language is where the real gets built, lets her be right in her mad way, because Res Tacamah does have an existence once she has named him. He is talked about, waited for, given errands, and years later, in the Mrs. Hogden episode, he is still expected, "the delightful gentleman who intended" to share the arithmetic lessons, until Lisa Lunde shatters him at one blow with the flat true word: "Res Tacamah is nothing but a drug bottle." She is correct, and she has seen nothing, because the point about Res Tacamah is that he is a drug bottle and a permanent companion and the author of the fantasies, all at once, and the flat true word about the bottle misses the man the naming made.

He is, most quietly and most terribly, the figure who can never be met. Vera is told to find out for herself "why it was that he could never meet her." He is forever on an errand toward an inheritance that is not there, a companion with big ears who hears everything and is no one, a gentleman with no face behind the label. He is the source of the mother's whole dreamed world, and there is nothing there to shake hands with.

The turn for a writer is the most unsettling self-portrait in the set. Res Tacamah is the drug bottle named "the author of so many fantasies," which is to say he is the machinery of invention itself, personified and empty, the thing that produces the visions and takes the credit and cannot be met because there is no one there, only the substance and the naming. He has ears to hear every raving and no self behind them.

He is a life spent making fantasies. The writer's Res Tacamah is the suspicion that the author is a bottle, that the maker of the dream world is only the mechanism of the dream, big-eared and faceless, permanently present and impossible to meet. The book sets him beside Mr. Chandelier and lets the mother love them both, the fickle light and the faithful bottle, and does not tell us they are less real than the people, because it has spent its whole length refusing to say that the dreamed is less real than the true.
