Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 28

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: Not One Self · Grief Doesn't End · Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act · The Past Doesn't Go Away

The Questions

Is Mr. Spitzer a man, or the hallucination of a woman dreaming him, and does the novel treat the question as a breakdown or as the ordinary condition of a self?

What is his dead brother now: gone, or pressed into the present Mr. Spitzer keeps living?

Why does the book give its deepest attention to a passion that possesses nothing and settles nothing?

What does it mean that his knowledge of butterflies grew while he was not looking?

Earlier Not One Self showed confusion between Mr. Spitzer and his dead twin. Here it goes further. He wonders whether he exists at all apart from Catherine, whether he is her hallucination, dreamed into being by a woman in an opium sleep. The self is not merely doubled now but unsure of its own edges, uncertain where it ends. The novel does not present this as a sickness to be cured. It is the truth of what a self is, a crowd that cannot always find its own border, and the deepening from the early chapters to this one is part of how the system unfolds.

The Past Doesn't Go Away runs underneath the chapter. His knowledge of butterflies “grew during the years, even though he has not been consciously thinking about them.” The past is not behind him. It accumulates beneath him and goes on working while he sleeps. His brother, likewise, is not simply dead: Mr. Spitzer keeps thinking his brother’s thoughts, the dead pressed into the living present. Time does not pass here. It layers.

His passion is the Witness at work. It is “almost incorporeal,” an “impersonal” attention that possesses nothing. He watches the butterfly’s transformations without resolving them, and he can stay with what will not resolve: “if one were suddenly called away in the midst of these proceedings, one should never know whether it was death or birth, beginning or end.” The butterfly that seems identical to the flower, so that one cannot say which took flight, is the Witness’s proper object, attended to and not pinned. And the passion is grief-shaped, since his love for the dead brother has nowhere to land and settles instead on the incorporeal. The grief does not close. It changes form and goes on, which is Grief Doesn't End.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that treats Mr. Spitzer’s uncertainty about his own existence as a problem to be solved. It refuses, too, the pull to resolve the novel’s questions about death into a position. The novel is not shopping for a theory of the soul.

The footing it offers is quieter and firmer. The self is plural and porous, and this is ordinary rather than broken. The dead go on shaping the living. The truest attention is the Witness’s, staying with what will not resolve: the butterfly one cannot tell from the flower, the moment one cannot tell from birth or death. Attend without possessing, and let the unresolved stay unresolved. The chapter ends, as Mr. Spitzer does, without deciding, and that is the discipline, not the failure.

For Reflection

How many selves answer when one says “I”?

Which of one’s own dead are actually gone, and which are pressed into the present, still shaping the day?

What would it mean to love something the way Mr. Spitzer loves butterflies, attending closely and possessing nothing?
