Definition from research

Chapter 2

  • John Milton wrote the epic poem Paradise Lost. He was blind when he dictated it to people including his daughters.

Chapter 3

  • John Milton's Paradise Lost, where the Devil famously declares as he grapples with his new reality in Hell. He suggests that even in the depths of despair, one's mindset can determine their experience, "The mind is its own place, and in itself can make a heaven of hell, a hell of heaven". This quote highlights the power of the mind to influence one's perception of reality and experience, suggesting that our internal state can transform even the most adverse external circumstances into something positive or negative.

Chapter 7

  • “He’s rolling in the flood”: John Milton, Paradise Lost (1.324): “To adore the conqueror, who now beholds Cherub and seraph rolling in the flood.” The expression metaphorically conveys that old Doc is submerged in a kind of existential or spiritual flood: caught in the overwhelming currents of his own disordered mind or possibly in the waters of death itself. It suggests that Doc is drowning or engulfed by his circumstances, unable to escape his fate. The flood represents a chaotic, engulfing force that traps Doc, reflecting his mental confusion and the dissolution of his medical practice and life.

Chapter 8

  • “dance the light fantastic” - John Milton 1645 poem, L’Allegro (The Happy Man) is a pastoral poem which has from its first appearance been paired with the contrasting pastoral poem, Il Penseroso (The Melancholy Man) which depicts a similar day spent in contemplation and thought.

Chapter 18

  • “when the roll was called up yonder” - popular Christian hymn that speaks of being ready for the day when God's chosen ones gather in heaven. The song was written by James Milton Black in 1893 and has been translated into multiple languages and sung by various artists. The song's lyrics are often interpreted as a reflection on the concept of the Book of Life and the promise of eternal life in heaven. It encourages believers to live a life of faith and service so that they will be among those who are called up yonder. This phrase is part of a surreal and symbolic wedding/funeral scene involving Mr. Spitzer and Miss MacIntosh, where the preacher speaks of "the roll being called up yonder" as a metaphor for the end of the world or the final judgment. The scene mixes the imagery of marriage and death, reflecting the novel’s themes of transition, mortality, and the ambiguity between life and death. The phrase underscores the idea that life and death, beginnings and ends, are intertwined and that ultimately, all must face a final accounting beyond earthly existence.

Chapter 18

  • “Better go to hell with rags than to heaven with embroidery.” - may refer to a Japanese proverb that states, "Even if you're wearing rags, your heart is embroidered". The famous line "Better to reign in Hell than serve in Heaven" is from Paradise Lost by John Milton. This line, spoken by Satan, expresses his desire for power and control, even in the face of a wretched existence, rather than subservience in a place of perceived perfection. The phrase encapsulates a key aspect of Miss MacIntosh’s character and worldview: valuing the essential, the real, and the practical over the ornamental, the illusory, and the vain.

Chapter 40

  • John Milton invented the name in Paradise Lost for the capital of Hell.

Chapter 63

  • "Heaven preserve you from a beardless man. Heaven preserve you from a hairy woman." This opening line is a warning against unnatural or incomplete identities, possibly alluding to the disruption of traditional gender roles or the loss of conventional human distinctions. The "beardless man" might symbolize immaturity or failure to fulfill societal or spiritual masculinity, while the "hairy woman" could indicate a departure from expected femininity or an overwhelming, chaotic force. The invocation of "Heaven preserve you" is a religious appeal for divine protection from these unsettling or undesirable conditions. "Consider well, my dear, if you mean what you are saying, if this is your infinite desire for nothing." Miss MacIntosh challenges the listener (Vera) to reflect carefully on whether their longing for "nothing", a void, emptiness, or perhaps death or nihilism is truly what they want. It suggests a deep questioning of desire, existence, and meaning. “She clapped the air with one hand. ‘A screech owl flew by just then. Did you not see it, hear it?’” The screech owl is a traditional symbol of death, omens, or the supernatural. Its sudden appearance emphasizes the gravity and ominous tone of the warning. The rhetorical question underscores awareness and perception, suggesting that profound or unsettling truths may go unnoticed. "The sun and the moon will shine in the same sky, and yet the earth will not be lighted but will be wrapped in darkness like the night." This line juxtaposes the presence of celestial bodies (sun and moon), which symbolize light, guidance, and life, with the paradoxical image of the earth being wrapped in darkness. It could imply a world where natural order and illumination exist but fail to reach or enlighten humanity, reflecting spiritual darkness despite physical light. "All earth will be made bare like this. No birds will sing in the tree-tops ever again. There shall be bare and ruined choirs." The "bare and ruined choirs" is a direct allusion to John Milton's Sonnet 7 ("...the bare and level plains, / The ruin of the ancient cities..."), evoking desolation, decay, and the loss of harmony or divine order. The silence of birds, which often symbolize the soul or spiritual joy, suggests a profound loss of life or spirit. "The flower will perish while it is still in the seed. The tides will not return what the tides take out." These images symbolize premature death or failure to reach potential ("flower perishing in the seed") and irreversible loss ("tides will not return what the tides take out"). They evoke the idea that some losses are final and natural cycles of renewal are broken. "There will be only, my dear, the dream." The closing line suggests that after all the destruction and loss, only the "dream" remains, possibly illusion, memory, or an insubstantial reality. It can be interpreted as a reflection on the ephemeral nature of life and the dominance of subjective perception or fantasy when the physical world fails. The invocation of "Heaven" and "Heaven preserve you" draws on Judeo-Christian imagery. The allusion to "bare and ruined choirs" connects to Milton, whose work is steeped in Christian theology and themes of the Fall and apocalypse. The presence of the sun, moon, and the view of the earth wrapped in darkness resonates with biblical apocalyptic imagery, such as the darkening of the sun and moon in the Book of Revelation signaling the end times. The repeated themes of loss, desolation, and the finality of death align with Christian meditation on mortality, sin, and the hope or despair beyond life. This passage is a somber meditation on loss, identity, and the tenuous hold on reality, filled with symbolic and religious imagery that suggests a spiritual or existential crisis. Miss MacIntosh’s words warn of the dangers of embracing "nothingness" or illusion without acknowledging the profound reality of life and death.
