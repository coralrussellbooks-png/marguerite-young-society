Chapter 67

On Chapter 67 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of The Wheel and the Spokes.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 66 · Wheel and Spokes Home · Chapter 68 →

Research notes: Chapter 67 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 67

Characters: Justice O’Leary

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The narrator observes that "probably half the conversation in America occurs in this manner": meaning addressed to someone who cannot hear, someone absent, someone who exists only in the speaker's interior. The bus-driver is not one person having one conversation. He is simultaneously:

  • A working man trying to park a bus
  • A grieving interlocutor addressing an absent friend
  • A moralist delivering judgment on life and death
  • A singer, a cursing man, a weeping man

The passengers mirror this plurality. The football player dreams "of a bride his wife might never know"; he is literally two selves, the married man and the man who dreams elsewhere. The pregnant girl laughs at misery she says is "nothing new to her", which means she carries a prior self who has already metabolized this scene. The narrator, exhausted, notes their heads "zooming with stars and flies and memories of distant places": the self dispersed across geography and time simultaneously.

Not One Self here is not a psychological observation. It is the structural condition of everyone in the bus. No one is singular. No one is present in only one sense.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter's central formal device is the invisible automobile. The bus-driver wages a full, passionate, detailed argument with "an old Ford with black canvas curtains flapping" that is "invisible to me but not to the bus-driver". The narrator cannot see it. We cannot see it. The bus-driver sees it completely.

The chapter refuses to resolve this. It does not tell us the bus-driver is simply drunk and wrong. It does not confirm the car exists. The narrator notes: "Or did I merely dream of this?", implicating their own perception as equally constructed.

This is the Pillar in its most radical form: there is no sunlit world outside the cave. The bus-driver's reality and the narrator's reality are both constructions. The hearse is real. The milk truck is real. The invisible Ford may or may not be real. The chapter offers no arbiter. Reality is what is made, and different people in the same space are making different realities simultaneously.

The girl's image of the crazy house with mirrored walls is the chapter's own gloss on this pillar:

"The walls, when you got inside, were all glass, lined with mirrors like the floor and ceiling. You felt along the glass, but you did not know how to get out, which mirror was a door."

There is no exit to an unmediated real. There is only the ongoing negotiation with constructed surfaces.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The bus-driver's monologue is not simply comic. Beneath its absurdist surface runs a sustained lament. He grieves for:

  • Old Doc, who brought children into the world "when they had never had parents"
  • The rabbit-faced girlfriends whose "rabbit's womb never burst": lives unlived, pregnancies that never were
  • The one-horse shay that "ain't what she used to was": the decay of things once vital
  • The buzzards returning to Rush Creek at sundown, "the great grandfather of them all": death as cyclical, permanent, generational

The bus-driver does not move through grief toward resolution. He accumulates it. Each new complaint layers onto the last. He swings between cursing and singing, between threatening Doc and pledging to shed "every drop of his blood" for him. This is not the arc of mourning. This is grief as climate: the weather the bus-driver lives inside permanently. The pregnant girl's laughter is the same structure in a different register. She laughs at misery that is "nothing new to her". She is not beyond grief. She has learned to inhabit it. The laughter is not resolution. It is a form of permanent coexistence with loss.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator's role in this chapter is almost entirely that of witness. They do not intervene, do not correct the bus-driver, do not get off the bus, do not explain the invisible car. They observe:

"I was the witness to a strange traffic problem."

This is a direct, almost programmatic statement of the Pillar. The narrator stays in the room. They are exhausted, "my head zooming with stars and flies", and yet they continue to attend. They notice the bus-driver's beard, his red spotted handkerchief, his voice buzzing "like some great bumble bee through the fog". They notice that "pretty soon, I was sure of it — a bird would fly out of the bus-driver's beard".

This last image is crucial. The narrator is not reporting facts. They are attending so fully that the surreal becomes visible. Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act in this Pillar is not a passive recorder. It is an active ethical stance: the refusal to simplify, the willingness to stay with the scene until its stranger truth emerges.

The narrator does not conclude what the bus-driver's monologue means. They do not diagnose him as drunk, delusional, or wise. They hold all possibilities simultaneously. That holding is the ethical act.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The bus-driver's argument is structurally recursive. He makes the same appeal to Doc repeatedly, each time with new evidence, new songs, new threats, new pledges of friendship, and each time he returns to the beginning:

"Move over, Doc." "Move your old ghost buggy out of here, Doc." "So move over, Doc, will yuh." "Be a good Boy Scout, Doc, and move over."

He builds the argument, it fails, he returns to the beginning with what he has learned (nothing, or everything), and builds again. The bus never parks. The building is never finished. This is not presented as failure: the chapter does not end with the bus successfully parked. It ends mid-sentence, mid-thought, mid-dream.

The narrator's own journey enacts this. They have "walked the streets of New York until my shoe soles were as thin as paper" (they have been building something, a life, a presence in the city) and now they are returning, on a bus, to a home that is "no home". They are back at the beginning. With worn soles. Ready to build again.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter does not end. It stops. The boy is "still yawning". The bus has not parked. Old Doc has not moved. The narrator has not arrived anywhere. Jackie, whoever she is, has gone through "which door" nobody knows.

The demand for completion (for the bus to park, for the argument to resolve, for the passengers to disembark and begin their lives) is never met. And the chapter implicitly argues that this is honest. The concluded sentence would be a lie. The ongoing sentence, "The boy was still yawning", is the truth.

The girl's crazy house image reinforces this. You cannot find the door. The mirror that is a door looks like all the other mirrors. The staircase becomes a clothes chute. There is no completion available. Unfinished Is Honest here is not a resigned shrug. It is the chapter's formal argument: enacted in its own refusal to conclude.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Every figure in this chapter is marginal:

  • The pregnant girl with a bird on her hat: unmarried, it is implied, or at least displaced, traveling alone at night, arriving at no home
  • The football player dreaming of a woman who is not his wife
  • The narrator who has worn their shoe soles to paper walking a city of "paper dolls"
  • Old Doc: the grizzled country doctor who brought children into the world "when they had never had parents", now apparently dead or dying, his ghost buggy taking up space in a world that wants him to move
  • The bus-driver himself: long-haired, bearded, talking to invisible men, drinking from a bottle through Indiana night

These are not the people at the center of American life in the mid-twentieth century. They are the edges. And at the edges, the constructed nature of reality becomes most visible. The center of society tells a story of arrival, completion, home, family, resolution. These marginal figures reveal that the story is a construction. Nobody is arriving. Nobody has a home. Nobody's womb burst. The rabbit's womb never burst. The categories (home, family, life, death) are visible here as the fictions they are.

Old Doc himself is the most powerful instance. He brought life into the world. Now he is a ghost. The children he delivered shot at him with bean-shooters. The marginal figure who made the center possible is now an obstacle to be cursed and moved. His marginality is not incidental. It is evidence of how the center constructs itself by forgetting its origins.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The bus-driver's monologue is a geological cross-section of time. In a single speech he moves through:

  • The rabbit tests of past girlfriends
  • Old Doc's history of delivering children
  • The one-horse shay as emblem of a vanished era
  • The buzzards returning to Rush Creek: a cyclical, generational time
  • "Sweet Mary O’Grady,’ he was singing again, ‘down by the old mill stream": a song from another era, another self

The past does not recede for the bus-driver. It presses upward. The dead girlfriends, the unborn children, the dead doctor who will not move his ghost buggy; they are all present, all simultaneously active, all still pressing on the steering wheel.

The narrator's own sediment is visible in their exhaustion: "memories of distant places", shoe soles worn thin by a city that turned out to be "one-dimensional". The layers are there. They press up through the fatigue.

The hearse sitting next to the bus is the chapter's most literal image of this pillar. The vehicle of death is parked next to the vehicle of ordinary transit. Past and present, death and life, are parked side by side, equally real, equally obstructing passage.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The bus-driver's language is the chapter's most elaborate demonstration of this Pillar. He constructs Old Doc entirely through language. Doc has "elongated, nerveless hands". His buggy has "black canvas curtains flapping". His passengers are "heathen Chinee", then "naked birds", then "pigs", then "bastards", then "luna moths". The description shifts constantly because language is not reporting Doc; it is building him, and the building is unstable.

The narrator is acutely aware of this. They note that the city of New York seemed "one-dimensional": a city of "paper dolls". The language that built New York as a destination, a place of arrival and possibility, turned out to be flat. The paper city was a paper construction.

The girl's crazy house is also a language-construction: she builds it in words as she speaks it, and it becomes real enough that the boy responds to it, "Jackie found the door", as if it were a shared reality. Language has made a space both of them now inhabit.

The chapter's own language is self-consciously constructed. The narrator uses traffic-sign language, "steep incline," "slow turn," "crumbling gravel," "bridge ahead", to describe the logic of human relationships and sleep. The language of roads is being used to map something roads cannot map. The construction is visible. The narrator is not pretending to speak from nowhere.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter lives entirely in the ordinary and the degraded:

  • The bus-driver's beard, through which his voice buzzes "like some great bumble bee through the fog": a figure almost Biblical in its strangeness, a prophet in a Greyhound
  • The pregnant girl with the bird upon her hat: the bird as emblem of spirit, perched on the most ordinary domestic accessory, riding a night bus through Indiana
  • Old Doc's ghost buggy, a vehicle with "no head lights, no steering wheel, no motor, no license plates", a vehicle that should not be able to exist and yet is, somehow, blocking the way
  • The buzzards coming in to roost at sundown near Rush Creek: death as "the great grandfather of them all", something ancient, cyclical, present

The bus-driver's song, "I can't give you anything but love, baby", is perhaps the chapter's most concentrated image of The Sacred Is Here. It is a cheap popular song. It is sung by a drunk man draped across a steering wheel. And it is, in this context, genuinely sacred: the only honest statement available, the irreducible thing that survives when everything else (the motor, the license plates, the steering wheel, the rabbit's womb) has failed or vanished.

The grey bus, stalled in the Indiana night, surrounded by a milk truck leaking "a milkish fluid" and a hearse with "tissue-colored eyes"; this is where the sacred lives. Not in a church. Not in a resolution. Here, in this careening, exhausted, impossible scene.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is perhaps the Pillar most fully embodied by the chapter's structure.

The narrator is explicitly constituted by what was deposited before they arrived. They carry New York in their worn shoe soles. They carry "memories of distant places". They are returning to "no home", which means they are constituted by a home that no longer exists, or never existed as imagined.

The pregnant girl is the chapter's most literal Crossing Point. She carries the not-yet-born. She is the place where past and future cross. She is traveling at night, arriving at no home, laughing at misery that is nothing new to her, and she carries, under her bird-hatted head, a new life that will stand on ground she is constituting right now, in this bus, in this night.

Old Doc is the crossing point of generations. He brought children into the world who shot at him with bean-shooters. He is the sediment the living stand on, and they have forgotten this, and he will not move, and the bus-driver loves him and curses him in the same breath, because you cannot move the ground you stand on, even when it is inconvenient, even when it is dead.

The bus-driver's closing argument is the Pillar stated plainly:

"The generations of buzzards do go on, honest to God. You haven't a chance."

This is not nihilism. It is the structural truth of the Crossing Point. The generations go on. The carrying continues. You have not a chance to stop it, to step outside it, to be singular and self-originating. You are where time crosses itself. The obligation is not chosen. It is structural.
