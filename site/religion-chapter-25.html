Definition from research

The chapter turns on death and the divided self. Mr. Spitzer is both living and dead, accused of being married to the worm, and the confusion foreshadows what the book will later reveal about him.

He believes in no heaven "except as a state of mind," a private, this-worldly faith.

He carries a "stain of immortal guilt" over his brother's death and longs, without hope, for absolution.

Behind the two brothers stands Cain and Abel. The book keeps asking whether Mr. Spitzer killed Peron, lives out the dead man's desires, or is only his shadow, the surviving twin marked and set wandering.

Catherine's knowledge outstrips his. "Both living and dead," she sees everything, an omniscience the book grants her as a near-divine attribute while his is only half.

Mr. Spitzer, "a soul divided," is in no place to judge another's dreams, and the passage folds in Paul's change "in the twinkling of an eye," the sparrows that fall by God's notice, and Ezekiel's wheels within wheels, images of a providence he cannot grasp.

Kept awake between "two iron beds," his own and his dead brother's, he guards against the "thief" of the gospel warning in Matthew 24, death or dissolution coming unannounced.

When Peron fell, "a bright light shining in his eyes deceived him," light turned to delusion rather than revelation.

"clouds of eyeless witnesses and dead sparrows falling" inverts two comforts, Hebrews' watching cloud of witnesses now blind, and the sparrow God marks now simply falling, a world stripped of divine attention.
