Angels and Archangels

Definition from research

Chapter 5

  • Gabriel is an archangel first described in the Hebrew bible. The reference to Gabriel serves to mark the bus journey as not merely physical but spiritual, underscoring the novel's exploration of mortality, transcendence, and the search for meaning beyond the material world.

Chapter 7

  • “He’s rolling in the flood”: John Milton, Paradise Lost (1.324): “To adore the conqueror, who now beholds / Cherub and seraph rolling in the flood.” The expression metaphorically conveys that old Doc is submerged in a kind of existential or spiritual flood: caught in the overwhelming currents of his own disordered mind or possibly in the waters of death itself. It suggests that Doc is drowning or engulfed by his circumstances, unable to escape his fate. The flood represents a chaotic, engulfing force that traps Doc, reflecting his mental confusion and the dissolution of his medical practice and life.
  • “old angel Gabriel blowing his tin horn”: Gabriel is an archangel in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. Gabriel announces the births of John the Baptist and of Jesus (Luke 1:19, 26), fit company for a doctor of births. The Bible does not name the angel of the last trumpet; the spiritual “Blow Your Trumpet, Gabriel,” printed in Slave Songs of the United States (1867), gives it to Gabriel.

Chapter 7

Gabriel announces the births of John the Baptist and of Jesus (Luke 1:19, 26), fit company for a doctor of births. The Bible does not name the angel of the last trumpet; the spiritual “Blow Your Trumpet, Gabriel,” printed in Slave Songs of the United States (1867), gives it to Gabriel.

Chapter 10

  • St. Michael is an archangel, a spiritual warrior in the battle of good versus evil.
  • “St. Michael and of all the holy angels”: The list comes out of Milton’s Paradise Lost, which gives Hell “Gorgons and Hydras, and Chimaeras dire” (2.628) and seats Chaos as umpire with “high arbiter Chance” who “governs all” (2.907-910).
  • “the archangel trumpeting the last trump”: “For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God” (1 Thessalonians 4:16).

Chapter 16

  • “angels who restored sight to the blind and hearing to the deaf”: “Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf shall be unstopped” (Isaiah 35:5). The gardens of the blind and of the deaf were Chapters 12 and 13.

Chapter 18

  • “and Archangel”: Archangel, or Arkhangelsk, is a Russian port on the White Sea.
  • “some angelic demon”: Young may be referring to the Nephilim. In Genesis 6:4, they are born when “the sons of God came in unto the daughters of men.” Later legend made them the children of fallen angels and mortal women, at once angelic and demonic.

Chapter 19

  • “the crumbling heads of cherubim”: In the Bible, cherubim are fierce guardians, set “at the east of the garden of Eden… to keep the way of the tree of life” (Genesis 3:24). The winged child heads of decoration are the putti of Renaissance art. These have “already lost their wings.”

Chapter 21

  • “once he sat at the right hand of the throne of God and was a favorite son of Heaven”: “How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!” (Isaiah 14:12). Milton’s Satan was “of the first, / If not the first Archangel, great in power” (Paradise Lost 5.659-660).

Chapter 29

  • “as the plume of Raphael”: The plume (feather) of Raphael may refer to the archangel.

Chapter 30

  • “blowing like Gabriel’s skirts”: Gabriel, the archangel of the Annunciation (Luke 1:26). The Bible does not name the angel of the last trumpet; the spiritual “Blow Your Trumpet, Gabriel,” printed in Slave Songs of the United States (1867), gives it to Gabriel.
  • “where she had heard an archangel blowing a ram’s horn three times”: In Islamic tradition, the archangel Israfil will sound the trumpet of Judgment. The shofar is the ram’s horn of Jewish worship.

Chapter 36

  • “Had she not wintered in Archangel”: Archangel, or Arkhangelsk, is the Russian port on the White Sea, named for its Monastery of the Archangel Michael. An Archangel is an angel of high rank usually associated with the Abrahamic religions. It was Russia’s chief seaport until St. Petersburg was founded in 1703, and its harbor is icebound for about half the year.

Chapter 39

  • “should not Gabriel lose his plume”: Gabriel is the archangel who brings God’s messages, to Daniel (Daniel 8:16) and to Mary (Luke 1:26). Michael is the guardian of Israel (Daniel 12:1).

Chapter 49

  • “the great archangel Uriel”: Uriel, “God is my light,” is an archangel of Jewish and Christian tradition, named in 1 Enoch and in the Apocrypha: “the angel that was sent unto me, whose name was Uriel, gave me an answer” (2 Esdras 4:1). He represents knowledge and wisdom.

Chapter 60

  • “almost angelic wings”: Compare the cherubim of Solomon’s temple: “the wing of the one touched the one wall, and the wing of the other cherub touched the other wall; and their wings touched one another in the midst of the house” (1 Kings 6:27, KJV).

Chapter 68

  • “until Gabriel blows his horn”: The Bible does not name Gabriel as the trumpeter of Judgment Day: “For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God” (1 Thessalonians 4:16, KJV). The tradition of Gabriel’s horn came down through African American spirituals such as “Blow Your Trumpet, Gabriel.”

Chapter 70

  • “She had the almost seraphic look of an old alcoholic”: Seraphic is characteristic of a seraph, one of the seraphim, or an angelic being, regarded in traditional Christian angelology as belonging to the highest order of the ninefold celestial hierarchy, associated with light, ardor, and purity.
  • “the shivering cherub wrapped in a bath towel”: Cherubim are in the books of Ezekiel and Revelation. They are majestic beings with four faces (man, lion, ox, eagle) representing divine power, majesty, and guardianship of God’s presence. The lion face signifies strength and kinship, the ox represents service, the man wisdom, and the eagle swiftness, with their overall form symbolizing God’s all-encompassing glory and authority. A lion’s skin evokes strength, majesty, and mythic power (recalling, for example, the Nemean lion’s skin worn by Hercules). The uncertain reference “was it a bath towel or a lion’s skin?” suggests a blurring between fragility and pretension, innocence and grandeur, reality and illusion. Applied to Mrs. Hogden, who is described as fat, oddly adorned, and carrying a confusing assortment of objects, this phrase underscores her contradictory character: she tries to present herself with an air of dignity and importance (like a lion’s skin) but is also pathetic and vulnerable (like a cherub wrapped in a bath towel). It reflects the novel’s themes of illusion, identity, and the tension between appearance and reality. The phrase metaphorically captures the complexity and ambiguity of Mrs. Hogden’s presence as part farcical, part tragic, both grand and diminished which highlights her as a figure caught between opposing qualities and uncertain roles within the household and narrative.

Chapter 73

  • “a fighter in the Jewish family, beginning with those who had fought the angels”: “And Jacob was left alone; and there wrestled a man with him until the breaking of the day” (Genesis 32:24, KJV). The man renamed him Israel, “for as a prince hast thou power with God and with men, and hast prevailed” (Genesis 32:28, KJV); Hosea says “he had power over the angel, and prevailed” (Hosea 12:4, KJV). Joe’s last bout, with God on a peak above the clouds, is Jacob’s, and he is losing it.

Chapter 74

  • “Her own childhood reproached her”: This section may refer to the death of Esther’s baby when she was young and how that traumatized her even now. Rape has been used as a plot device in literature and other media. It has been criticized for using rape as a metaphor that displaces the actual, violent and traumatic act of rape. It is considered misogynistic in the way female rape victims continue to be cast as collateral damage and the assault and rape of women is used as a plot point in a narrative that is fundamentally about men. Young is making this about Esther and showing how things would be different if she were a man. Young makes many references to mythology. The struggle to escape from sexual pursuit is one of the most popular motifs of Greek mythology: Persephone, Callisto, Daphne, Europa, Io, etc. Greek mythology depicts women as vulnerable to assault, able to escape only through death, or metamorphosis like Daphne who becomes a laurel tree. The consequence of fleeing sexual violence is another type of force on the body, the loss of the human form where women who survive sexual violence become pregnant, and when gods as the rapists, produce a hero child. The Chicago detective takes Esther for a corpse, then feels her pulse, finds her heartbeat, and rapes her. Chione in Greek mythology was said to be so beautiful that she was the object of a thousand men’s desire. Hermes put Chione into a deep sleep with his wand and proceeded to rape her. Since Young has said Esther is the mother of us all and Mr. Spitzer as the father of us all, these could be archetypes for Adam and Eve and possibly Lilith. In On the Origin of the World, part of the Nag Hammadi Library, the rulers of the world rape the earthly Eve, and she bears their children. In the midrash Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer, Samael, riding the serpent, seduces Eve and fathers Cain; later Kabbalah makes Lilith, Adam’s first wife, Samael’s bride. The Talmud says that in the years Adam was apart from Eve he fathered spirits, demons, and lilin (Eruvin 18b).

Chapter 76

  • “old men like old cherubim with bald, flat heads and pot bellies and winged penises”: “The flying penis” refers to the ancient Roman and medieval symbol of the winged phallus, known as fascinus, which was used as a protective charm against evil, infertility, and the evil eye.

Chapter 82

  • “when Gabriel blew his horn”: “For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God” (1 Thessalonians 4:16, KJV); The Bible does not name the angel who sounds the last trump; the spiritual “Blow Your Trumpet, Gabriel,” printed in Slave Songs of the United States (1867), gives it to Gabriel.
