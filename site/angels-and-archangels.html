Chapter 5

  • Gabriel is an archangel first described in the Hebrew bible. The reference to Gabriel serves to mark the bus journey as not merely physical but spiritual, underscoring the novel's exploration of mortality, transcendence, and the search for meaning beyond the material world.

Chapter 7

  • “He’s rolling in the flood”: John Milton, Paradise Lost (1.324): “To adore the conqueror, who now beholds / Cherub and seraph rolling in the flood.” The expression metaphorically conveys that old Doc is submerged in a kind of existential or spiritual flood: caught in the overwhelming currents of his own disordered mind or possibly in the waters of death itself. It suggests that Doc is drowning or engulfed by his circumstances, unable to escape his fate. The flood represents a chaotic, engulfing force that traps Doc, reflecting his mental confusion and the dissolution of his medical practice and life.
  • “old angel Gabriel blowing his tin horn”: Gabriel is an archangel in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. Gabriel announces the births of John the Baptist and of Jesus (Luke 1:19, 26), fit company for a doctor of births. Tradition gave him the last trumpet.

Chapter 10

  • St. Michael is an archangel, a spiritual warrior in the battle of good versus evil.
  • “St. Michael and of all the holy angels”: The list comes out of Milton’s Paradise Lost, which gives Hell “Gorgons and Hydras, and Chimaeras dire” (2.628) and seats Chaos as umpire with “high arbiter Chance” who “governs all” (2.907-910).
  • “the archangel trumpeting the last trump”: “For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God” (1 Thessalonians 4:16).

Chapter 16

  • “angels who restored sight to the blind and hearing to the deaf”: “Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf shall be unstopped” (Isaiah 35:5). The gardens of the blind and of the deaf were Chapters 12 and 13.

Chapter 18

  • “and Archangel”: Archangel, or Arkhangelsk, is a Russian port on the White Sea.
  • “some angelic demon”: Young may be referring to the Nephilim. In Genesis 6:4, they are born when “the sons of God came in unto the daughters of men.” Later legend made them the children of fallen angels and mortal women, at once angelic and demonic.

Chapter 68

  • The history of knots is ancient, predating tools like the wheel, with evidence of rope-making and knots from 15,000-17,000 years ago for hunting, fishing, and shelter, evolving into essential technology for sailing, construction, record-keeping (like the Inca Khipu), and art (Chinese knots), and later formalized in mathematics (Knot Theory) and modern uses from surgery to climbing. Knots developed independently across cultures out of necessity, becoming fundamental to civilization's advancement and symbolic meaning. The landlord is contrasting his own experience and understanding of "knots" with that of the hangman. While the hangman knows literal knots used in hangings, the landlord speaks metaphorically of "lovers’ knots": the bonds of marriage that are meant to be unbreakable until the end of time, "until Gabriel blows his horn" (symbolizing Judgment Day), or even beyond, suggesting that even death should not part true lovers. The landlord, who runs a hotel that has endured floods and fire, is well-versed in various types of practical knots (overhand, figure-of-eight, running bowline, and more) that have specific uses in sailing and daily life. He questions the hangman’s ability to understand the "marital knot," implying that the hangman’s knowledge is limited and that he has not even mastered the basics of tying his own bow-tie properly, a task he suggests is better suited to a wife. The landlord's metaphorical description emphasizes the strength and permanence of the marital bond, contrasting it with the hangman’s grim profession and the fleeting nature of life and death. The whimsical reference to dolphins sporting over his head as his ship went down and the sinking hotel that “sinks every night” adds a layer of imagery connecting the fragility of human constructs and relationships to the natural world and its constant changes: oceans, ships, drifting continents. This passage reflects the novel’s recurring themes of impermanence, human bonds, and the interplay between reality and metaphor. It also highlights the characters' different perspectives on life, love, and death: the landlord’s practical yet poetic view versus the hangman’s somber, fatalistic outlook.

Chapter 70

  • seraphic is characteristic of a seraphim or an angelic being, regarded in traditional Christian angelology as belonging to the highest order of the ninefold celestial hierarchy, associated with light, ardor, and purity.

Chapter 70

  • “cherub wrapped in a bath towel… or a lion’s skin” Cherubim are in the books of Ezekiel and Revelation. They are majestic beings with four faces (man, lion, ox, eagle) representing divine power, majesty, and guardianship of God’s presence. The lion face signifies strength and kinship, the ox represents service, the man wisdom, and the eagle swiftness, with their overall form symbolizing God’s all-encompassing glory and authority. A lion’s skin evokes strength, majesty, and mythic power (recalling, for example, the Nemean lion’s skin worn by Hercules). The uncertain reference “was it a bath towel or a lion’s skin?” suggests a blurring between fragility and pretension, innocence and grandeur, reality and illusion. Applied to Mrs. Hogden, who is described as fat, oddly adorned, and carrying a confusing assortment of objects, this phrase underscores her contradictory character: she tries to present herself with an air of dignity and importance (like a lion’s skin) but is also pathetic and vulnerable (like a cherub wrapped in a bath towel). It reflects the novel’s themes of illusion, identity, and the tension between appearance and reality. The phrase metaphorically captures the complexity and ambiguity of Mrs. Hogden’s presence as part farcical, part tragic, both grand and diminished which highlights her as a figure caught between opposing qualities and uncertain roles within the household and narrative.

Chapter 74

  • “For everything was so sad, and the sky bled in her dreams, and she had no menstrual period, and she was always pregnant. Who was innocent? Who was guilty? Her own childhood reproached her.” This section may refer to the death of Esther’s baby when she was young and how that traumatized her even now. Rape has been used as a plot device in literature and other media. It has been criticized for using rape as a metaphor that displaces the actual, violent and traumatic act of rape. It is considered misogynistic in the way female rape victims continue to be cast as collateral damage and the assault and rape of women is used as a plot point in a narrative that is fundamentally about men. Young is making this about Esther and showing how things would be different if she were a man. Young makes many references to mythology. The struggle to escape from sexual pursuit is one of the most popular motifs of Greek mythology: Persephone, Callisto, Daphne, Europa, Io, etc. Greek mythology depicts women as vulnerable to assault, able to escape only through death, or metamorphosis like Daphne who becomes a laurel tree. The consequence of fleeing sexual violence is another type of force on the body, the loss of the human form where women who survive sexual violence become pregnant, and when gods as the rapists, produce a hero child. The Chicago detective is described as raping Esther because he thinks she’s dead. Chione in Greek mythology was said to be so beautiful that she was the object of a thousand men's desire. Hermes used magic to make Chione to fall into a deep sleep and proceeded to rape her. Since Young has said Esther is the mother of us all and Mr. Spitzer as the father of us all, these could be archetypes for Adam and Eve and possibly Lilith. In On the Origin of World, part of the Nag Hammadi Library, Eve is described as being raped by archangels and bears children. In the Kabbalist myth Eve is raped by Lilith, Adam’s first wife, in the guise of Samael, Lilith’s demon lover or Satan. The Kabbalists continued the story to include Eve, through her menses, to have defiled Adam which then lead to Lilith being able to rape Adam, become impregnated and birth Lilin or demons by Adam.

Chapter 76

  • Esther Longtree emerges in this chapter as a deeply tragic and complex figure, embodying themes of fertility, loss, sorrow, and marginalization. Her character is portrayed with a rich, symbolic density that reveals much about her internal struggles and her relationship to the world around her. Esther is described as physically large, often pregnant or swollen, with features like a long nose and crossed eyes, evoking a somewhat grotesque yet poignant image. She is burdened by repeated pregnancies, many of which end in stillbirths or miscarriages. This physical condition mirrors her emotional state: she is exhausted, confused, and isolated, caught in a cycle of hope and despair. Her body and mind are both "clouded" and "indistinct," reflecting her fragmented sense of self and history. Esther’s experience of pregnancy and motherhood is fraught with sorrow and complexity. She carries numerous "stillborn children," described almost as ghostly presences hovering around her, watching her with innocent smiles and applause, yet also causing her pain and distraction during intercourse. These spectral children symbolize her lost potential for motherhood and the deep trauma of repeated loss. She does not reject these children but claims them as hers, showing a profound maternal attachment despite the pain they bring. However, this attachment is intertwined with guilt and confusion, as she contemplates the nature of her past conceptions and births. Esther is entangled with a Chicago detective who is aggressively pursuing proof of his paternity of her children. This detective symbolizes the harsh, intrusive forces of society, law, and judgment. Esther fears his investigations, seeing them as threats that could expose her to legal and social punishment. Yet at the same time, she contemplates the possibility of the trial with a strange blend of hope and resignation, almost welcoming the exposure as a form of acknowledgment of her suffering and existence. This ambivalence highlights her isolation and the precariousness of her position as a woman burdened by reproductive tragedy and societal scrutiny. The chapter frequently returns to motifs of birth and death, conception and miscarriage, seen both literally and metaphorically in Esther’s story. She is described as knitting and unravelling a black hood, symbolizing the night and mystery surrounding her existence. Her pregnancies are "clouded," and her children are often invisible or spectral, linking her personal tragedy to broader cosmic or existential mysteries. Esther’s life is likened to an "empty coffin on the water," merging images of life and death, presence and absence. This symbolism situates her story within larger themes of loss, fate, and the unknowable forces shaping human experience. Despite her suffering, Esther exhibits a kind of wildness and defiance. She runs, she laughs, she knits, she endures. She is "wilder than people thought," living a life bounded by routine but also marked by moments of freedom and rebellion. Her laughter in the face of the detective’s threats and the looming trial shows a resilience and complexity that defy simple victimhood. Esther’s narrative is one of survival amid despair, of a woman caught between social condemnation and her own intricate inner world. Esther’s experience is deeply intertwined with natural imagery: autumn leaves, winter bees, snowflakes, mists, rivers, and stars. These elements emphasize the cosmic scale of her suffering and the universality of her predicament. Her children are likened to "snowflakes drifting in eternity" and "robed cherubim," linking personal loss to the eternal cycles of life and death. This connection to nature and the cosmos reinforces the mystical and tragic dimensions of her character. Esther Longtree is a richly drawn figure whose story encapsulates themes of loss, fertility, maternal anguish, societal judgment, and existential mystery. She is at once vulnerable and resilient, tragic and wild, caught in a liminal space between life and death, hope and despair. The chapter paints her not merely as a victim but as a complex woman grappling with profound personal and cosmic questions, embodying the novel’s broader exploration of human suffering and endurance.
