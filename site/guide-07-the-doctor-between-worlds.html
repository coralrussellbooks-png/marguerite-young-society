Chapter 7: The Doctor Between Worlds

Research notes: Chapter 7

Themes: Faith, God, and the Supernatural · The Search for Meaning

Symbols: Keys, Doors, and Locks · The House · Weddings and Funerals

Philosophy: Augustine and Christian Theology · Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Fairy Tales and Folklore · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · Shakespeare · The Bible

By now you have your bearings on this bus. You know Moses: his hair, his politics, his whiskey, his raging at the dark. You know Madge: her grief, her fury, her two faces, her clear-eyed understanding of the trap she is in. You know the landscape outside: flooded roads, fallen pillars, a midwestern darkness that goes on and on.

Chapter 7 deepens all of it. We stay on the bus, but the bus now feels less like a vehicle and more like a vessel, like something crossing between states. And Justice O’Leary, the old doctor who has been sitting quietly in the background since Chapter 1, finally takes his place at the center.

What actually happens in Chapter 7

Moses is driving through rain and flood when he spots something on the side of the road: the skeletal chassis of Old Doc’s Model T (no wheels, no engine parts, barely a car at all) faintly illuminated in the dark. Old Doc is out there, tending to his patients. Except his patients, as Moses describes them, are shades. Ghosts. People who may never have been born, or who have been dead for years.

Moses watches, rationalizes, mocks, and underneath all of it is moved. He debates whether to help the old man or leave him to his fate. He does not stop.

Then the chapter shifts to the bus interior, where Madge and Homer are talking or rather, Madge is talking and Homer is resisting. She tells stories about the people back home: the Hathaway and Tidings families, a tangle of death, suspected murder, and generational suffering that reads like a dark fairy tale from the rural Midwest. Josh Hathaway is dead. His brother Rudolph died before him. Their wife or widow, or both, in sequence, Gertrude, is accused by Madge of having killed them both. The evidence is circumstantial, the story murky, the locked door and the fire never quite explained.

And then there is Musidora, Gertrude’s sister, mentally impaired, living in poverty, sold by her mother to an old man who became her husband and the father of too many children. Musidora’s pale, moth-like children play in the graveyard because it is one of the last public spaces where they are allowed to gather.

Homer wants to believe the best. Madge knows better. And Vera witnesses all of it from her place on the bus, adding it to the archive of human suffering she has been quietly assembling since the novel began.

Old Doc and the question of what he is doing

Justice O’Leary is one of the stranger figures in a novel full of strange figures.

He was once a real doctor, a country physician who made house calls, who delivered babies, who was a figure of practical care in his community. Now he is old and his mind has gone. His Model T has no wheels. His instruments are rusted. But he keeps going out. He keeps answering calls. He keeps doing what a doctor does, even when there are no patients left who are truly alive.

Moses thinks this is madness. And in a literal sense, he is right. But Young is doing something more complicated. The old doctor’s delusion, that he is still needed, still helping, still bringing life into the world is in its way a kind of faithfulness. He cannot stop being what he was. He cannot accept that the world he served has gone.

There is a question this chapter asks without answering: Is it worse to be deluded and faithful, or clear-eyed and resigned? Moses has given up on almost everything. The old doctor has given up on nothing. Who is actually more alive?

Madge’s stories, and what they are really about

The Hathaway and Tidings families might seem like a detour, local gossip, small-town tragedy, a locked-room mystery Young drops into the middle of a chapter and never fully resolves. But look more carefully at what Madge is describing.

Gertrude married one brother, then the other. The first died mysteriously. The second died under suspicious circumstances. She is accused of murder but never charged. She drinks and her house falls apart around her.

Musidora was sold, that is the word, sold, by her own mother to an old man who became her husband. She is described as “peculiar,” which in this context is Young’s careful way of suggesting cognitive or developmental disability. She has too many children and not enough of anything else. Her pale children play in the graveyard because it is one of the few spaces that hasn’t been closed to them.

These are not unusual stories for the time and place Young is writing about. They are the stories that didn’t get told: the lives of poor rural women in the American Midwest, ground up by poverty, limited options, and a legal and social system that offered them almost no protection. Marital rape was not considered a crime in the United States until 1978, and not in all states until 1993. Women were frequently passed between men, from fathers to husbands, from one brother to another because their independence was not a category the law recognized.

Madge tells these stories with the flat, unflinching tone of someone who knows they are true because she has been watching from nearby her entire life. Homer resists them because he prefers not to know. This dynamic of the woman who sees clearly, the man who would rather not, runs through the entire chapter.

The graveyard as gathering place

One of the most haunting images in Chapter 7 is Musidora’s pale children playing in the graveyard. Young doesn’t linger on it. She just puts it there, matter-of-factly, and moves on.

But think about what it means. The graveyard is a public space, one of the few that remains. It costs nothing to enter. No one tells you to leave. The children of a poor, marginalized woman are welcome there in a way they are not welcome elsewhere.

Young is not being sentimental about this. She is being precise. The dead are more hospitable to these children than the living have been. The graveyard is where life happens, for people who have been pushed to the margins of everything else.

The locked-room mystery

Young had a minor in criminology and loved murder mysteries, and she planted one here: Josh Hathaway dead behind a locked door, a fire, circumstances that suggest murder but prove nothing. Gertrude is accused. Nothing is resolved.

This is deliberately unsatisfying, and it is meant to be. The locked-room mystery, a staple of detective fiction in which a murder seems to have been committed in circumstances that make it impossible, traditionally promises resolution. The detective figures it out. The truth comes out. Young denies you that comfort.

Life in this novel is full of locked rooms. Miss MacIntosh’s disappearance is a locked-room mystery. Catherine’s inner world is a locked room. The truth about almost every character is locked away behind a door that may never open. The Hathaway murder is Young’s way of telling you, directly: don’t expect clean answers. The evidence is always incomplete. The door stays locked.

What to watch for as you keep reading

Old Doc’s violin. He plays it in Chapter 7, a broken or barely functional instrument, a smoky lantern at his side. Music in this novel is never just music. It is a form of reaching toward something that cannot be said directly. Watch what instruments appear and who is playing them, and under what circumstances.

The names Young gives to Madge’s small-town cast. They are thick with Biblical allusion: Solomon, Bathsheba, Reuben, Gad, Manasseh. Young is layering the ordinary horror of these Midwestern lives with the weight of ancient stories: the stories of women desired and disposed of, of brothers in conflict, of families destroyed by the decisions of powerful men. The names tell you that these stories are not new. They have always been happening, everywhere, to women who were never given the chance to tell them themselves.

The jeremiad. Moses’s long, raging monologue in this chapter falls into a specific American literary tradition, the jeremiad, a lament over the moral decay of society, the nation fallen from its promise. Young knew this tradition well. Moses is not just a drunk bus driver railing at Democrats. He is speaking in a voice that goes back to the Puritan preachers, to the founding of the country, to the original American anxiety that things are not as good as they were supposed to be.

Homer’s hope. Notice how consistently Homer chooses not to know. He believes in an afterlife. He resists Madge’s grim accounts. He prefers the version of events that ends better. This is not stupidity, it is a choice, and Young is watching it the way she watches everything: carefully, without judgment, and without quite letting it off the hook.

What to hold onto

Chapter 7 is a chapter about people who keep going when they should, by all reasonable measures, have stopped. The old doctor tends to patients who aren’t there. Moses drives roads that aren’t really roads. Madge tells truths that no one wants to hear. Musidora’s children play among the headstones because it is the only space left to them.

None of these people have what they need. All of them are still moving. That is, quietly, what this chapter is about.

The bus is still going. So are you.
