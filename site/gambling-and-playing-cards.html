Definition from research

Chapter 14

  • “King and Queen, Prince and Spade”: The horses are named like playing cards, and the aces fall later in the chapter.
  • “paper-backed dream books sold by fortunetellers”: Dream books matched dreams to numbers for the policy, or numbers, game played in poor neighborhoods, as in Aunt Sally’s Policy Players’ Dream Book (1889).
  • “The playing card dropping from his sleeve each morning had nearly always been an ace”: The “ace up the sleeve” is the cardsharp’s hidden card. In most fortune-telling, the ace of spades is the death card.
  • “The club, which was trifoliate, might be transformed into the sign of the cross”: The club suit is a trefoil, and the shamrock was said to teach the Trinity.
  • “at shrouded parimutuel windows”: The parimutuel, a betting pool shared among the winners, was invented by Joseph Oller in Paris in 1867.

Chapter 18

  • “Clubs are trumps”: The cards of Chapter 14.
  • “the joker is that card which has no face but is the highest card when it is used in euchre”: The joker was added to the deck for euchre in 1860s America as the “best bower,” the highest card, just as Miss MacIntosh says.

Chapter 19

  • “the deuce of clubs, that card which signifies the test of tears”: Cartomancy reads fortunes in ordinary playing cards. Young gives the deuce of clubs its meaning herself. Clubs were trumps the night before.
  • “the Devil’s cards are marked”: The Puritans called playing cards “the Devil’s books.” The American historian of cards Mrs. John King Van Rensselaer titled her book The Devil’s Picture-Books (1890).

Chapter 20

  • “dark as the ace of spades”: The ace of spades has long been called the death card. The deuce of clubs was “the omen of death” in Chapter 19.

Chapter 28

  • “the wastrel wheels of chance, the games of loaded dice, the croupiers”: Roulette (“little wheel”), whose croupier spins it.

Chapter 29

  • “where the turf meets the surf”: The slogan of the Del Mar racetrack, opened in 1937 by Bing Crosby and Pat O’Brien. Crosby sang “Where the Turf Meets the Surf.”

Chapter 30

  • “a game of whist or loo or poker”: Whist, whose rules Edmond Hoyle published in 1742, and loo, the gambling game of Pope’s age.

Chapter 45

  • “pigeon poler” - It may refer to poker (Peron Spitzer) where it is a player who is perceived as a weak or inexperienced player, easily manipulated or exploited. Some poker players might intentionally act like a “pigeon” to deceive other players. It symbolizes a guardian or guide navigating others, in this case pigeons, through uncertain or liminal spaces, much like Mr. Spitzer’s own role as a watcher and intermediary between worlds.

Chapter 51

  • Mr. Spitzer acknowledges that he can only perceive his brother through the "mazed images of the dead" and "all the differences". This suggests that his brother's memory is fragmented and distorted, forever altered by death. The imagery of "a light upon the water where there should be no light" and "a darkening cloud" evokes a sense of unease and the disruption of natural order caused by his brother's passing. It contrasts the miraculous nature of Mr. Spitzer living through death with the image of "some crazy old gambler" throwing his last dice. This juxtaposition highlights the unpredictable and chaotic nature of life, as well as Mr. Spitzer's struggle to reconcile his orderly existence with the chaotic world of his brother. Mr. Spitzer's effort to practice law is portrayed as a futile attempt to impose order on a world that is inherently lawless. He is described as "trying to practice law where there was no law and no lawyer," highlighting the absurdity of his situation. The image of a "left-handed portrait painter in a world of right-handed portrait painters" further emphasizes his sense of being an outsider, someone who is fundamentally different from the rest of the world. Despite his efforts to "go straight," Mr. Spitzer's path seems "finally circular," suggesting that he is trapped in a cycle of repetition and unable to escape his past. This idea is reinforced by the notion that "law…had always seemed to the gambler with his illegal interests an aspect of death." Mr. Spitzer's legal practice is described as existing in a liminal space between life and death. He practices "only in the courts which were paved with the moonstones of lost cases under a firmament of lost stars," evoking a sense of decay, loss, and the ephemeral nature of human endeavors. The images of a "dead moth with its wings trembling" and a "mouse in a paper nest" further emphasize the themes of decay and disorder. The mouse, in particular, embodies the chaotic forces that undermine Mr. Spitzer's attempts to impose order. It gnaws on legal documents and musical manuscripts, "jumbling symphonies with old racing forms," and ultimately becoming "the greatest impresario" by shaking "the long handle of the Great Dipper of stars pouring out little stars." Mr. Spitzer's preference for "the seething silence to the sound, preferring mystery to exposure, preferring a cloak to nakedness" is highlighted. Despite his "great sense of order," he is increasingly drawn to the chaotic and irrational aspects of life, symbolized by the "litigious starfish" and the bees buzzing around his head.

Chapter 52

  • “Mr. Spitzer had trembled like a gambler for whom there would always be another shuffle” and the next paragraph. These two paragraphs use rich, poetic imagery and metaphor to explore themes of uncertainty, decay, memory, and intertwined fates. Mr. Spitzer is depicted as a liminal figure caught between reality and illusion, past and present, life and death, whose reflections on the suffragette reveal the elusive nature of truth and identity in the novel. The suffragette herself is a haunting presence (both fragile and magnetic, real and spectral) whose life and legacy resist simple comprehension, embodying the novel's complex interplay of reality and dream.The opening lines immediately establish a world governed by chance. Mr. Spitzer trembling "like a gambler" suggests a life where outcomes are unpredictable and determined by forces beyond individual control. This aligns with existentialist themes of the absurdity of existence and the lack of inherent meaning. The "faceless card" and "star of no known number" symbolize the limits of human knowledge. We can never fully grasp the forces shaping our lives, and there will always be elements of the unknown. This resonates with the existentialist emphasis on the limits of reason. The line "Yes, there was an external world, he knew now as it departed" is crucial. It indicates a loss of faith in an objective reality. The world is not fixed but is constantly slipping away, emphasizing the subjective experience. "He gave his belief when there was no reason for his belief" highlights a rejection of rationalism. Spitzer's faith is not based on evidence or logic but on something deeper, perhaps a Kierkegaardian "leap of faith" into the absurd. "For he reserved his credulity for the grand occasions eliciting the skepticism of others. Their skepticism was his faith" is a complex statement. It suggests that Spitzer finds meaning in what others reject. This could be interpreted as a form of contrarianism, or as a deeper understanding that truth often lies in challenging conventional wisdom.

Chapter 53

  • Louis XIV did love pool and other games and encouraged gambling at Versailles to entertain and keep the nobles close to him. His Minister of France then was Jean-Baptiste Colbert. There is no reference to him gaining the position by beating Louis XIV at pool. Gambling was banned by Louis XIV for the general populace.

Chapter 53

  • Sir Walter Raleigh was beheaded but there is no mention of him playing cards with his executioners.

Chapter 53

  • A playing card with 8 pips would be any of the 8 cards in a standard deck. Each suit (hearts, diamonds, clubs, and spades) contains an 8 card with eight corresponding suit symbols (pips) on the face.

Chapter 53

  • “acorns, hearts, bells, and leaves” are associated with German-suited playing cards, a traditional card system used in Central Europe. These suits predate the more internationally recognized French suits (hearts, diamonds, clubs, and spades). German-suited cards typically feature 32 or 36 cards with these four suits. The acorns suit is one of the oldest. Leaves is another of the original suits. Hearts are the same in both the German and French systems. Bells are a distinctive German suit. German suits are believed to have influenced the development of the French suits. For example, the French "clubs" are thought to be derived from the German "acorns". German-suited cards are still used in many parts of Central Europe.

Chapter 54

  • “cashiered from the Army or the Navy or both” Cashiering is a ritual ceremony where the individual is dismissed for some form of misbehavior. Military veterans have historically been cashiered for misconduct related to gambling debts, though not typically for the debt itself. Instead, it is the dishonorable failure to pay, fraudulent acts to acquire gambling money, or other offenses undermining military discipline that lead to dismissal or cashiering.

Chapter 54

  • “Ivory soap flakes” In 1840, the J.B. Williams Company in Glastonbury, Connecticut, manufactured soap under the name Ivorine. Williams decided to focus on its shaving soap and sold Ivorine to Procter & Gamble, which later developed Ivory. In 1879, James Norris Gamble, son of Procter & Gamble co-founder James Gamble and a trained chemist, developed an inexpensive white soap. The name Ivory was created by Harley Procter, the other founder's son, who was inspired by the quote "[a]ll thy garments smell of myrrh and aloes and cassia out of the ivory palaces", from Psalm 45 of the Bible. In September 1879, Procter & Gamble trademarked "Ivory" as the name of its new soap product. As Ivory is one of P&G's oldest products it is sometimes called "Ivory Towers", and its factory and research center in St. Bernard, Ohio, is named "Ivorydale". Ivory's first slogan, "It Floats!", was introduced in 1891. The product's other well-known slogan, "99+44⁄100% Pure", which was in use by 1895, was based on the results of an analysis by an independent laboratory that Harley Procter hired to demonstrate that Ivory was purer than the castile soap available at the time. The original Ivory bar soap was whipped with air in its production and floated in water, although P&G discontinued this version of the soap in 2023, and the new version no longer floats. According to an apocryphal story, later discounted by the company, a worker accidentally left the mixing machine on too long, and the company chose to sell the "ruined" batch because the added air did not change the basic ingredients of the soap. When appreciative letters about the new, floating soap inundated the company, P&G ordered the extended mix time as a standard procedure. However, company records indicate that the design of Ivory did not come about by accident. In 2004, over 100 years later, the P&G company archivist Ed Rider found documentation that revealed that James N. Gamble, who was a chemist, had discovered how to make the soap float and noted the result in his writings.

Chapter 54

  • “American soap flake king” may refer to Procter & Gamble (P&G), founded by William Procter and James Gamble. They were a pivotal force in the American soap industry and its transition to soap flakes. While British manufacturer Lever Brothers were the first to produce flakes commercially, P&G popularized the product in the U.S., particularly with its Ivory soap brand. The American Civil War provided a major boost to P&G's fortunes when the company secured contracts to supply the Union Army with soap and candles. After the war, returning soldiers created nationwide demand for P&G products. In the 1910s, P&G created Ivory Flakes, a shredded form of their popular Ivory soap. Unlike the harsh laundry soap of the era, the mild, pure Ivory Flakes were marketed for washing delicate items like baby clothes, silks, and nylons. The product's success cemented P&G's dominance in the market.

Chapter 55

  • “Chinatown” U.S. Chinatowns were ethnic enclaves, primarily in the West, established in the mid-19th century by immigrants drawn by the Gold Rush and railroad jobs, but were devastated by the 1882 Chinese Exclusion Act which banned further immigration and caused populations to decline. By the early 20th century, Chinatowns were predominantly male-dominated communities centered on social societies, gambling, and opium dens, but after the 1943 Magnuson Act repealed the exclusion laws, populations began to rise again, leading to changes in the established communities.

Chapter 55

  • Gambling or betting or gaming is the wagering of something of value on an event with an uncertain outcome with the intent of winning something else of value. Gambling dates back to the Paleolithic period, before written history. Financial Investments are generally not considered gambling when they meet certain criteria. Studies show that though many people participate in gambling as a form of recreation or to earn an income, gambling, like any behavior involving variation in brain chemistry, can become a behavioral addiction. Behavioral addiction can occur with all the negative consequences in a person's life minus the physical issues faced by people who compulsively engage in drug and alcohol abuse. Problem gambling has multiple symptoms. Gamblers often gamble to try to win back money they have lost, and some gamble to relieve feelings of helplessness and anxiety. Gamblers may exhibit a number of cognitive and motivational biases that distort the perceived odds of events and that influence their preferences for gambles. Addiction by Design by Natasha Dow Schull shows how the manipulation of gambling pulls players into a trancelike state in which daily worries, social demands, and even bodily awareness fade away. Once in the zone, gambling addicts play not to win but simply to keep playing, for as long as possible, even at the cost of physical and economic exhaustion. Gamblers seek to lose themselves while the gambling industry seeks profit. Schull describes how the industry designs systems to profit from the maximum amount from gamblers and manipulates human psychology.

Chapter 55

  • “playing the bones” can have several meanings, depending on the context. It could refer to using a musical instrument called the "bones", or it could be a slang term related to gambling, mob culture, or even sexual activity.

Chapter 55

  • “parimutual” is a betting system in which all bets of a particular type are placed together in a pool; taxes and the house-take, or vigorish, are deducted, and payoff odds are calculated by sharing the pool among all winning bets. In some countries it is known as the tote after the totalisator, which calculates and displays bets already made. The parimutuel system is used in gambling on horse racing, greyhound racing, jai alai, and other sporting events of relatively short duration in which participants finish in a ranked order. A modified parimutuel system is also used in some lottery games. Parimutuel betting differs from fixed-odds betting in that the final payout is not determined until the pool is closed, in fixed-odds betting, the payout is agreed at the time the bet is made. Parimutuel gambling is frequently state-regulated, and it is offered in many places where gambling is otherwise illegal. Parimutuel gambling is often also offered at off-track facilities, where players may bet on the events without actually being present to observe them in person.

Chapter 56

  • “green-vizored yachtsmen’s hats” The green-visored hat is most historically associated with accountants, gamblers, and other indoor professions that required reducing eye strain from harsh, early artificial lighting.

Chapter 59

  • The chapter delves deeply into the philosophy of chance and randomness as it relates to the characters' experiences and the larger metaphysical structure of the novel. The chapter, primarily through the perspective of Mr. Spitzer, explores the tension between order and chaos, certainty and uncertainty, and how chance governs much of human life and beyond. Mr. Spitzer reflects on the human tendency to seek order and predictability in life, yet he recognizes the profound role of chance and the limits of human control. Despite our efforts to impose logic, mathematics, or law, reality often eludes these frameworks, revealing itself as a chaotic, unpredictable flux. This is emphasized in the discussion of how Mr. Spitzer’s attempts to organize his life and memories are constantly disrupted by unforeseen events and errors beyond his control. The chapter highlights the dual nature of existence, where opposites coexist: life and death, truth and falsehood, light and shadow, order and chaos. Mr. Spitzer himself embodies this duality, being a man torn between certainty and doubt, reality and illusion, the living and the dead. The interplay of chance affects these states, suggesting randomness is not merely external but intrinsic to the self and consciousness. Chance is portrayed as a force that shapes reality, both creating and destroying. The metaphor of dice and gambling recurs as an allegory for life’s unpredictability and the illusion of control. Mr. Spitzer’s brother’s gambling life contrasts with his own cautious nature, underscoring differing responses to randomness: one embracing chance, the other attempting to manage or deny it. Chance extends beyond human affairs into cosmic and metaphysical dimensions. Mr. Spitzer contemplates the vastness of space, the birth and death of stars, and the fragmentation of existence, all governed by forces beyond human comprehension or control. The randomness inherent in the universe mirrors the unpredictability in individual lives and destinies. The chapter suggests that chance affects identity itself, with Mr. Spitzer questioning his own selfhood and existence. The fluidity of identity is likened to shifting shadows, reflections, and illusions, where personal continuity is disrupted by forces beyond control. Chance influences memory, perception, and even the boundary between self and other. While chance brings uncertainty and disorder, there is also a sense of inevitability and surrender. Mr. Spitzer’s reflections convey a tragic acceptance of randomness as a fundamental aspect of existence, where attempts to impose total order are ultimately futile. This acceptance is tinged with melancholy but also a recognition of the limited human role in the cosmic design. The metaphor of music recurs in the chapter, with chance associated with the unpredictable notes, rhythms, and silences that compose the cosmic and personal “music.” Time itself is seen as an artificial construct, with moments overlapping, fading, and recurring in a way that defies linear understanding. Chance governs the flow and interruption of this music, reflecting the random and cyclical nature of existence. It presents chance and randomness not merely as external phenomena but as essential, pervasive forces that shape reality, identity, and the cosmos. Through Mr. Spitzer’s anguished and reflective voice, chance emerges as both a creative and destructive principle that defies human attempts at mastery or prediction. The philosophy articulated here embraces ambiguity, duality, and the limits of knowledge, suggesting that randomness is inseparable from life and death, order and chaos, memory and identity. This nuanced treatment aligns with the novel’s broader themes of illusion, mortality, and the search for meaning amidst uncertainty. Chance is both a source of suffering and a condition for the possibility of life and experience, challenging the characters and readers to reconsider assumptions about control, fate, and reality itself.

Chapter 61

  • The "Barbary Coast" most commonly refers to the historical coastal region of North Africa, inhabited by Berbers, from which pirates and privateers launched raids on European shipping and coastal towns from the 16th to the 19th century. The term is also used to describe the raucous waterfront district of San Francisco during the late 19th and early 20th centuries, known for its saloons, gambling, and brothels. The North African region was notorious for its Barbary corsairs, who captured ships and people for ransom and the slave trade.

Chapter 65

  • Waddingtons in England, produced playing cards featuring Dutch windmills on the backs, often in sealed, faux-leather boxes, with designs popular in the 1950s and 1960s for their quaint, scenic appeal.

Chapter 65

  • Vera, recounts Miss MacIntosh's memories and opinions, which include several stereotypes about Chinese people: Miss MacIntosh states, "Did I know that the Chinese are a nearly hairless people, that Chinese men prefer a hairless woman with small breasts?" This perpetuates a stereotype about the physical characteristics of Chinese individuals. Miss MacIntosh mentions Chinese gambling dens where men play solitaire and are watched to prevent cheating the "invisible partner." This associates Chinese culture with gambling and implies a need for surveillance to ensure fair play. Miss MacIntosh claims to have learned solitaire in San Francisco's Chinatown, suggesting a connection between the game and Chinese culture.

Chapter 65

  • Bowling alleys evolved from ancient games, gaining indoor structure in 19th-century America, particularly among German immigrants, shifting from nine-pin to ten-pin to circumvent gambling bans, and transforming into modern entertainment venues with automatic pinsetters and widespread media appeal, especially after the 1950s, though they've always been associated with socializing and gambling. Pinsetters began as young boys and girls ("pinboys") manually resetting pins and returning balls, a common, low-paying job in early 20th-century alleys, often enduring harsh conditions. It was hard, hot work, requiring speed and agility to avoid fast-moving balls and pins, as recounted by both men and women who did the job.

Chapter 66

  • The Tong Wars were a series of violent disputes beginning in the late 19th century among rival Chinese Tong factions centered in the Chinatowns of various American cities, in particular San Francisco. Tong wars could be triggered by a variety of inter-gang grievances, from the public besmirching of another Tong's honor, to failure to make full payment for a "slave girl", to the murder of a rival Tong member. Each Tong had salaried soldiers, known as boo how doy, who fought in Chinatown alleys and streets over the control of opium, prostitution, gambling, and territory. In San Francisco's Chinatown district, the Tong Wars lasted until 1921, with the various criminal Tongs estimated between nineteen and as many as thirty at the peak of the conflict, though the actual number is uncertain, with frequent splintering and mergers between the various Tongs. While a loose alliance, consisting of the Chinatown police, Donaldina Cameron, the courts, and the Chinese community itself tried to stem the tide of the fighting Tongs, it was the 1906 San Francisco earthquake and subsequent fires caused by the earthquake that was the death knell for the Tongs in San Francisco, as it destroyed the brothels, gambling dens, and opium houses that the criminal organizations had used for the majority of their revenue. Recovery was slow for most Tongs, so they abandoned the old Chinatown.

Chapter 66

  • “It would be a natural death, not a suicide, for she was not like the other gamblers on the Stock Exchange, frenzied by losses, killing themselves by jumping from high windows.” Miss MacIntosh is describing the myth that men jumped out of office windows after the market crash in 1929.

Chapter 66

  • Ivory soap is a flagship personal care brand created by the Procter & Gamble Company. Ivory's first slogan, "It Floats!", was introduced in 1891.

Chapter 72

  • Pinochle originated in 19th-century Germany from the French game Bezique, evolving from Binokel, with its name possibly meaning "eyeglasses" (from the Queen of Spades and Jack of Diamonds combo). German immigrants brought it to America, where it became hugely popular in the late 1800s and early 1900s, especially in the Midwest, growing into a beloved game rivaling poker and bridge, featuring unique melding and trick-taking mechanics with a special 48-card deck.

Chapter 81

  • “four ugly gamblers” may refer to the "Big Four" Allied powers during World War II, who led the fight against the Axis, were the United States, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union (USSR), and China. These nations formed the core of the alliance, with China fighting Japan since 1937 and the others joining later.

Chapter 81

  • “playing cards”: Playing cards in the novel are often associated with chance, fate, and the illusions of control, as seen in scenes with gamblers. Their presence on the shore suggests America as a place where fortunes are gambled, where outcomes are subject to unpredictable forces, and where the remnants of failed risks are left behind.
