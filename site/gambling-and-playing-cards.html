Gambling and Playing Cards

Definition from research

Chapter 14

  • “King and Queen, Prince and Spade”: The horses are named like playing cards, and the aces fall later in the chapter.
  • “paper-backed dream books sold by fortunetellers”: Dream books matched dreams to numbers for the policy, or numbers, game played in poor neighborhoods, as in Aunt Sally’s Policy Players’ Dream Book (1889).
  • “The playing card dropping from his sleeve each morning had nearly always been an ace”: The “ace up the sleeve” is the cardsharp’s hidden card. In most fortune-telling, the ace of spades is the death card.
  • “The club, which was trifoliate, might be transformed into the sign of the cross”: The club suit is a trefoil, and the shamrock was said to teach the Trinity.
  • “at shrouded parimutuel windows”: The parimutuel, a betting pool shared among the winners, was invented by Joseph Oller in Paris in 1867.

Chapter 18

  • “Clubs are trumps”: The cards of Chapter 14.
  • “the joker is that card which has no face but is the highest card when it is used in euchre”: The joker was added to the deck for euchre in 1860s America as the “best bower,” the highest card, just as Miss MacIntosh says.

Chapter 19

  • “the deuce of clubs, that card which signifies the test of tears”: Cartomancy reads fortunes in ordinary playing cards. Young gives the deuce of clubs its meaning herself. Clubs were trumps the night before.
  • “the Devil’s cards are marked”: The Puritans called playing cards “the Devil’s books.” The American historian of cards Mrs. John King Van Rensselaer titled her book The Devil’s Picture-Books (1890).

Chapter 20

  • “dark as the ace of spades”: The ace of spades has long been called the death card. The deuce of clubs was “the omen of death” in Chapter 19.

Chapter 28

  • “the wastrel wheels of chance, the games of loaded dice, the croupiers”: Roulette (“little wheel”), whose croupier spins it.

Chapter 29

  • “where the turf meets the surf”: The slogan of the Del Mar racetrack, opened in 1937 by Bing Crosby and Pat O’Brien. Crosby sang “Where the Turf Meets the Surf.”

Chapter 30

  • “a game of whist or loo or poker”: Whist, whose rules Edmond Hoyle published in 1742, and loo, the gambling game of Pope’s age.

Chapter 31

  • “not one horseshoe of the red and white roses… two dice cubes in a lucky throw, seven and eleven”: Floral horseshoes and dice were set on gamblers’ graves. Seven and eleven are winning throws in craps.
  • “worth a candle’s flame in the headlong wind”: From the French proverb le jeu ne vaut pas la chandelle, “the game is not worth the candle,” from card players who paid for the candles they played by.

Chapter 35 · Cards and Games

  • The metaphor of cards and poker reflects strategy, risk, and deception.
  • Cousin Hannah is a “mean game” player; Mr. Spitzer distrusts her, believing she cheats.
  • This imagery symbolizes life’s gambits and the characters’ differing approaches to fate and challenge.

Chapter 37 · Cards and Dice

  • The snow falls “black as the ace of spades.”
  • The dead dice-throwers rattle “old bones under dimmed street lamps, and their skulls were their dice cups.”
  • Hannah is “one whose last card long ago was played.”

Chapter 42

  • “was solitary as the ace of hearts”: Hearts is the suit of love. See the ace of spades under “he who had played his last spade,” below.
  • “he who had played his last spade”: The ace of spades has long been called the death card. Peron was a gambler: in Young’s first draft “Mr. Spitzer knew that he was really Peyron the gambler.”

Chapter 45

  • “the universe was something shaken out of a dice box”: Einstein wrote to Max Born on December 4, 1926, that he was convinced God “does not throw dice.” Peron the gambler believed the opposite.

Chapter 46

  • “All was not over so long as there was one card out”: See the ace of spades and the ace of hearts in the Chapter 42 resources.

Chapter 47

  • “Should the reprobate be pure as snow”: “Though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow” (Isaiah 1:18), and “If I wash myself with snow water, and make my hands never so clean; yet shalt thou plunge me in the ditch” (Job 9:30-31). See the ace of spades in the Chapter 42 resources.

Chapter 48

  • “neither spade nor heart nor diamond nor club”: The four French suits date from fifteenth-century France. See the ace of spades and the ace of hearts in the Chapter 42 resources.

Chapter 49

  • “only an old night porter riding with her”: Pullman sleeping-car porters were almost all Black men, many of them formerly enslaved or the sons of the enslaved; passengers called every porter “George,” after George Pullman, and they worked long hours for low pay and tips until they organized the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters under A. Philip Randolph in 1925. “Black as the ace of spades” was a common racist simile; see the ace of spades in the Chapter 42 resources.

Chapter 51

  • “the great planetary wheel turning like a ferris wheel”: George Ferris built the first Ferris wheel for the Chicago World’s Fair of 1893. Fortune’s wheel is older: in Boethius, Fortune says, “I turn the wheel that spins. I delight to see the high come down and the low ascend” (The Consolation of Philosophy 2.2, trans. W. V. Cooper, 1902).
  • “Never should he see his brother’s face”: Mr. Spitzer can see his brother only “through all the mazed images of the dead, through all the differences.” He lives through death no more miraculously than “some crazy old gambler who had thrown his last dice,” and he tries “to go legal,” as biased as “a left-handed portrait painter in a world of right-handed portrait painters.” “His effort to go straight had made him crooked,” and his way is “finally circular.” He practices only “in the courts which were paved with the moonstones of lost cases under a firmament of lost stars,” and he prefers “the seething silence to the sound, preferring mystery to exposure, preferring a cloak to nakedness.”

Chapter 52

  • “Mr. Spitzer had trembled like a gambler”: This paragraph and the next use rich, poetic imagery and metaphor to explore themes of uncertainty, decay, memory, and intertwined fates. Mr. Spitzer is depicted as a liminal figure caught between reality and illusion, past and present, life and death, whose reflections on the suffragette reveal the elusive nature of truth and identity in the novel. The suffragette herself is a haunting presence (both fragile and magnetic, real and spectral) whose life and legacy resist simple comprehension, embodying the novel’s complex interplay of reality and dream. The opening lines immediately establish a world governed by chance. Mr. Spitzer trembling “like a gambler” suggests a life where outcomes are unpredictable and determined by forces beyond individual control. This aligns with existentialist themes of the absurdity of existence and the lack of inherent meaning. The “faceless card” and “star of no known number” symbolize the limits of human knowledge. No one can fully grasp the forces shaping a life, and there will always be elements of the unknown. This resonates with the existentialist emphasis on the limits of reason. The line “Yes, there was an external world, he knew now as it departed” is crucial. It indicates a loss of faith in an objective reality. The world is not fixed but is constantly slipping away, emphasizing the subjective experience. “He gave his belief when there was no reason for his belief” highlights a rejection of rationalism. Spitzer’s faith is not based on evidence or logic but on something deeper, perhaps a Kierkegaardian “leap of faith” into the absurd. “For he reserved his credulity for the grand occasions eliciting the skepticism of others. Their skepticism was his faith” is a complex statement. It suggests that Spitzer finds meaning in what others reject. This could be interpreted as a form of contrarianism, or as a deeper understanding that truth often lies in challenging conventional wisdom.

Chapter 53

  • “People were indifferent to those things which interested Mr. Spitzer”: Young drew Peron’s world from her own reading: “I’ve read a great many books on gambling, and I’ve been interested in it since early childhood, because I knew gamblers in early childhood.” Of her research she said, “Baseball, gambling, vaudeville, burlesque jokes, all this became part, as well as Moby Dick, the Bible” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).
  • “the old shell games in which the victim had no chance”: In the shell game, or thimblerig, a pea is shuffled under three shells or thimbles and the operator palms it, so the mark can never win.
  • “what was a playing card with eight pips”: A playing card with eight pips is any of the four eights in a standard deck. Each suit (hearts, diamonds, clubs, and spades) contains an 8 card with eight corresponding suit symbols (pips) on the face.
  • “what was an act of trumping”: Trump comes from triumph, the name of an old card game, which is why the musician first thinks of an angel blowing a trumpet.
  • “who was the jack of discs, who was the jack of cups”: The Latin suits of Italian and Spanish cards and of the tarot are cups, coins, swords, and batons; in tarot the coins are often drawn as discs or pentacles. A jack of cups is the knave of that suit.
  • “the bird cage, of course, has something to do with gambling”: “birdcage” typically refers to Chuck-a-Luck, a dice game played with a distinctive hourglass-shaped cage containing three dice. The game is also known as “birdcage” or “sweat rag.” Players bet on the outcome of the dice roll after the dealer flips the cage and the dice come to rest.
  • “acorns, hearts, bells, and leaves”: are associated with German-suited playing cards, a traditional card system used in Central Europe. These suits predate the more internationally recognized French suits (hearts, diamonds, clubs, and spades). German-suited cards typically feature 32 or 36 cards with these four suits. The acorns suit is one of the oldest. Leaves is another of the original suits. Hearts are the same in both the German and French systems. Bells are a distinctive German suit. German suits are believed to have influenced the development of the French suits. For example, the French “clubs” are thought to be derived from the German “acorns.” German-suited cards are still used in many parts of Central Europe.

Chapter 54

  • “a famous sportsman had been laid away”: Young told Studs Terkel that she “had such fun with the gambling part of the book, especially the gambler’s funeral, because Mr. Spitzer provided a marvelous funeral for his brother.” Terkel was reminded of Jack Teagarden singing “Gambler’s Blues,” a version of “St. James Infirmary” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).

Chapter 55

  • “a dead cue-ball artist”: See the billiards legend of Louis Fox in the Chapter 53 resources.
  • “twirled the bare-ribbed bird-cages”: In chuck-a-luck, also called birdcage, three dice tumble in an hourglass-shaped wire cage, and players bet on the numbers that come up.
  • “an old croupier, perhaps at the wrong wheel”: A croupier is the person in charge of a gaming table, gathering in and paying out money or tokens.
  • “could not and never had been able to distinguish between two spots and three spots”: The spots are the pips on dice and playing cards.
  • “In every gambler’s make-up”: Gambling or betting or gaming is the wagering of something of value on an event with an uncertain outcome with the intent of winning something else of value. Gambling dates back to the Paleolithic period, before written history. Financial Investments are generally not considered gambling when they meet certain criteria. Studies show that though many people participate in gambling as a form of recreation or to earn an income, gambling, like any behavior involving variation in brain chemistry, can become a behavioral addiction. Behavioral addiction can occur with all the negative consequences in a person’s life minus the physical issues faced by people who compulsively engage in drug and alcohol abuse. Problem gambling has multiple symptoms. Gamblers often gamble to try to win back money they have lost, and some gamble to relieve feelings of helplessness and anxiety. Gamblers may exhibit a number of cognitive and motivational biases that distort the perceived odds of events and that influence their preferences for gambles. Addiction by Design by Natasha Dow Schull shows how the manipulation of gambling pulls players into a trancelike state in which daily worries, social demands, and even bodily awareness fade away. Once in the zone, gambling addicts play not to win but simply to keep playing, for as long as possible, even at the cost of physical and economic exhaustion. Gamblers seek to lose themselves while the gambling industry seeks profit. Schull describes how the industry designs systems to profit from the maximum amount from gamblers and manipulates human psychology. Flag: after 1964, Natasha Dow Schüll’s Addiction by Design (2012).
  • “playing the bones”: Dice were first made of bone, so gamblers still call them bones; to play or roll the bones is to shoot dice.
  • “crazy as the old bank-teller or parimutuel man”: Parimutuel is a betting system in which all bets of a particular type are placed together in a pool; taxes and the house-take, or vigorish, are deducted, and payoff odds are calculated by sharing the pool among all winning bets. In some countries it is known as the tote after the totalisator, which calculates and displays bets already made. The parimutuel system is used in gambling on horse racing, greyhound racing, jai alai, and other sporting events of relatively short duration in which participants finish in a ranked order. A modified parimutuel system is also used in some lottery games. Parimutuel betting differs from fixed-odds betting in that the final payout is not determined until the pool is closed, in fixed-odds betting, the payout is agreed at the time the bet is made. Parimutuel gambling is frequently state-regulated, and it is offered in many places where gambling is otherwise illegal. Parimutuel gambling is often also offered at off-track facilities, where players may bet on the events without actually being present to observe them in person. (Wikipedia) The Paris entrepreneur Joseph Oller invented it in 1867.

Chapter 56

  • “both with only sevens and elevens”: In craps, a seven or eleven on the first roll is an automatic win, a “natural.” Peron is buried with dice that cannot lose.
  • “He had paid an old billiard hall artist to come and take one last shot”: See the billiards legend of Louis Fox in the Chapter 53 resources.

Chapter 57

  • “to bet with a penny which had heads upon both sides”: The two-headed coin is the oldest of gamblers’ tricks: whoever calls heads cannot lose.

Chapter 58

  • “the rattle of dice in an ivory box”: See playing the bones in the Chapter 55 resources.

Chapter 59

  • “played the starter music at the shining glow-worm tracks”: At American racetracks a bugler sounds the “Call to the Post” to bring the horses onto the track.
  • “the records of euchre”: Euchre is a trick-taking card game commonly played in English speaking countries. The joker was added to the American deck around the 1860s as the top trump in euchre, the “best bower.”

Chapter 61

  • The "Barbary Coast" most commonly refers to the historical coastal region of North Africa, inhabited by Berbers, from which pirates and privateers launched raids on European shipping and coastal towns from the 16th to the 19th century. The term is also used to describe the raucous waterfront district of San Francisco during the late 19th and early 20th centuries, known for its saloons, gambling, and brothels. The North African region was notorious for its Barbary corsairs, who captured ships and people for ransom and the slave trade.

Chapter 65

  • Waddingtons in England, produced playing cards featuring Dutch windmills on the backs, often in sealed, faux-leather boxes, with designs popular in the 1950s and 1960s for their quaint, scenic appeal.

Chapter 65

  • Vera, recounts Miss MacIntosh's memories and opinions, which include several stereotypes about Chinese people: Miss MacIntosh states, "Did I know that the Chinese are a nearly hairless people, that Chinese men prefer a hairless woman with small breasts?" This perpetuates a stereotype about the physical characteristics of Chinese individuals. Miss MacIntosh mentions Chinese gambling dens where men play solitaire and are watched to prevent cheating the "invisible partner." This associates Chinese culture with gambling and implies a need for surveillance to ensure fair play. Miss MacIntosh claims to have learned solitaire in San Francisco's Chinatown, suggesting a connection between the game and Chinese culture.

Chapter 65

  • Bowling alleys evolved from ancient games, gaining indoor structure in 19th-century America, particularly among German immigrants, shifting from nine-pin to ten-pin to circumvent gambling bans, and transforming into modern entertainment venues with automatic pinsetters and widespread media appeal, especially after the 1950s, though they've always been associated with socializing and gambling. Pinsetters began as young boys and girls ("pinboys") manually resetting pins and returning balls, a common, low-paying job in early 20th-century alleys, often enduring harsh conditions. It was hard, hot work, requiring speed and agility to avoid fast-moving balls and pins, as recounted by both men and women who did the job.

Chapter 66

  • The Tong Wars were a series of violent disputes beginning in the late 19th century among rival Chinese Tong factions centered in the Chinatowns of various American cities, in particular San Francisco. Tong wars could be triggered by a variety of inter-gang grievances, from the public besmirching of another Tong's honor, to failure to make full payment for a "slave girl", to the murder of a rival Tong member. Each Tong had salaried soldiers, known as boo how doy, who fought in Chinatown alleys and streets over the control of opium, prostitution, gambling, and territory. In San Francisco's Chinatown district, the Tong Wars lasted until 1921, with the various criminal Tongs estimated between nineteen and as many as thirty at the peak of the conflict, though the actual number is uncertain, with frequent splintering and mergers between the various Tongs. While a loose alliance, consisting of the Chinatown police, Donaldina Cameron, the courts, and the Chinese community itself tried to stem the tide of the fighting Tongs, it was the 1906 San Francisco earthquake and subsequent fires caused by the earthquake that was the death knell for the Tongs in San Francisco, as it destroyed the brothels, gambling dens, and opium houses that the criminal organizations had used for the majority of their revenue. Recovery was slow for most Tongs, so they abandoned the old Chinatown.

Chapter 66

  • “It would be a natural death, not a suicide, for she was not like the other gamblers on the Stock Exchange, frenzied by losses, killing themselves by jumping from high windows.” Miss MacIntosh is describing the myth that men jumped out of office windows after the market crash in 1929.

Chapter 66

  • Ivory soap is a flagship personal care brand created by the Procter & Gamble Company. Ivory's first slogan, "It Floats!", was introduced in 1891.

Chapter 72

  • Pinochle originated in 19th-century Germany from the French game Bezique, evolving from Binokel, with its name possibly meaning "eyeglasses" (from the Queen of Spades and Jack of Diamonds combo). German immigrants brought it to America, where it became hugely popular in the late 1800s and early 1900s, especially in the Midwest, growing into a beloved game rivaling poker and bridge, featuring unique melding and trick-taking mechanics with a special 48-card deck.

Chapter 81

  • “four ugly gamblers” may refer to the "Big Four" Allied powers during World War II, who led the fight against the Axis, were the United States, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union (USSR), and China. These nations formed the core of the alliance, with China fighting Japan since 1937 and the others joining later.

Chapter 81

  • “playing cards”: Playing cards in the novel are often associated with chance, fate, and the illusions of control, as seen in scenes with gamblers. Their presence on the shore suggests America as a place where fortunes are gambled, where outcomes are subject to unpredictable forces, and where the remnants of failed risks are left behind.
