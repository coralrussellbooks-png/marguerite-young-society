What It Is For and How to Build It

A Companion Document to the Constitution

The Philosophical Foundation

Education is not primarily workforce preparation. It is not even primarily civic preparation in the thin sense of knowing the three branches of government and showing up to vote. It is the cultivation of people who can stay in a room with complexity without demanding its premature resolution, who can hold more than one identity at the same time without requiring one to silence all the others, who can recognize that their understanding of the world is shaped by their particular experience without being destroyed by that recognition.

That is a different educational philosophy than anything currently operating at scale in American public education. The current system is organized around the singular acceptable student: the student who scores within the acceptable range on the acceptable measures, who progresses through the acceptable sequence at the acceptable pace, who produces the acceptable outputs in the acceptable formats. The student who exceeds or escapes these categories in any direction is managed back toward the center. The student who cannot be managed back is sorted into a remedial category and the sorting is presented as an objective finding about the student rather than a judgment made by a system built without them in mind.

Obsessions are the fuel for inquiry, research, and investigation. That is an educational philosophy. It names the thing the current system is most systematically designed to extinguish: the obsession, the particular burning interest that does not fit the sequence, that exceeds the time allotted, that insists on going deeper when the curriculum needs to move on. The student with an obsession is a management problem. A different philosophy says the obsession is the condition without which genuine learning cannot happen. The current system has it exactly backwards.

The Structural Critique

Funding and the Zip Code

American public schools are funded primarily through local property taxes. This means that the quality of the public school a child attends is determined by the wealth of the neighborhood in which they were born. This is not an accidental outcome of a neutral system. It is the intended outcome of a system designed to ensure that the children of the affluent receive better-resourced schooling than the children of everyone else, while maintaining the fiction that all children have equal access to public education.

This pattern shows itself directly here: a structure of equal opportunity, presented as the natural order, that encodes the interests of the people who built it. The zip code is not a neutral administrative category. It is a sorting mechanism. The child born in the wealthy suburb and the child born in the post-industrial city are not receiving the same public education. They are receiving educations whose resource gap reflects and reproduces the wealth gap that produced it. The system is self-perpetuating. It requires no conspiracy to maintain. It only requires the continued acceptance of property tax funding as simply how school finance works.

Federal equalization funding exists but is inadequate and subject to the political will of each Congress. A constitutional obligation to equalize resources across all public schools removes the question from political negotiation. The floor of educational resource is the same for every child regardless of where they were born. This is not redistribution in the pejorative sense. It is the honest application of the equal protection principle to the domain where inequality is most consequential and most durable.

Standardized Testing as the Singular Acceptable Student

The standardized test is where the problem shows itself most clearly in education policy. It is a construction of what intelligence and preparation look like, built from studies of a specific population and administered as though it measures something universal and objective. It does not. It measures performance on a particular kind of task, under particular conditions, by people whose prior education has prepared them for that particular kind of task under those particular conditions. The correlation between test scores and family income is not a surprising finding. It is the predictable outcome of a test that measures, in significant part, the quality of the preparation that wealth makes possible.

The consequences of the test determine access to resources: to advanced coursework, to college admission, to the credential that opens the door to the next credential. The child sorted into the remedial category by the test does not receive more support. They receive less. The sorting mechanism produces the outcome it predicts by withholding from the students who most need investment the investment that would most change their trajectory. The accumulated disadvantage is built into the structure, reproduced generation by generation, presented as the natural result of who the students are rather than as a political choice about whose children deserve resources.

Assessment that serves education rather than sorting asks different questions. Not: how does this student perform relative to the population the test was built around? But: what does this student know, what can they do, what are they building toward, and what do they need next? Portfolio assessment, project-based evaluation, demonstrated competency, teacher observation over time: these are not soft alternatives to rigorous measurement. They are more rigorous, because they measure the actual complexity of what a student knows rather than the subset of that knowledge that can be captured in a timed multiple-choice format.

The School-to-Prison Pipeline

The school-to-prison pipeline is the criminal legal system, starting in childhood. The mechanisms are well documented: zero-tolerance discipline policies that treat childhood behavior as criminal conduct, police presence in schools that routes disciplinary matters into the criminal legal system, suspension and expulsion that remove students from education and deposit them in the conditions that produce further involvement with the criminal system, special education placements that segregate and stigmatize rather than support. These mechanisms fall disproportionately on Black and brown students, on students with disabilities, on students whose behavior reflects trauma rather than defiance.

The pipeline is the nation's accumulated history of harm made visible in schools: the losses built into the foundation of this country, reproduced in the institution nominally designed to offer the next generation a different outcome. The school that responds to the traumatized child with punishment rather than support is the school that has decided in advance that this child's presence in the educational community is conditional. The condition is compliance. The threat issued to eight-year-olds: be the acceptable student or be removed. The removal is not the end of the story. It is the beginning of the next chapter in a story the system has already decided how to tell.

Restorative practices in schools, investment in counselors and social workers rather than police officers, and a trauma-informed approach to behavior that asks what happened to this child rather than what is wrong with this child: these are not soft on discipline. They are the practice of staying in the room with the difficult child rather than removing them. Bringing the full weight of attention to what is actually there rather than managing it out of sight.

The Curriculum as Curated Consensus Reality

The curriculum is not a neutral collection of the most important things to know. It is a selection made by people with the power to select, encoding their construction of what counts as knowledge, whose history matters, whose literary tradition is universal, whose scientific contributions are canonical. Feminist scholarship of the past fifty years has documented this pattern in detail: the curriculum built from studies of men and boys and presented as universal, the literary canon defined by the critics who defined it and called the definition objective, the history that centers the experiences of the people who wrote it.

This is not a problem that is solved by adding a diversity unit. Treating excluded knowledge as an add-on is its own version of the same problem: the dominant curriculum remains the curriculum, and the additions are positioned as supplements to the real thing rather than as challenges to the construction of what the real thing is. The full integration of the complete human record requires not adding to the current curriculum but rebuilding it from the ground up with the question: what do we know about how human beings have lived, created, organized themselves, resisted, built, and lost, across the full range of human experience? The answer to that question is a different curriculum than the one currently taught.

Indigenous knowledge systems, feminist scholarship on how knowledge is built and who gets to build it, the intellectual traditions of the African diaspora, the history of labor and the people who performed it, the history of what was done to the people the dominant history needed to not see: these are not supplements. They are the complete record that the current curriculum is incomplete without. The student who graduates from a public school having learned only the consensus version of human history has not received an education. They have received an incomplete picture designed to look like the whole thing.

Early Childhood

The research on early childhood education is among the most robust in all of social science. The Perry Preschool Project, which followed participants for over fifty years across their whole lives, found that high-quality early childhood education produces measurable benefits across the full life course: higher graduation rates, higher employment, lower involvement with the criminal legal system, better health outcomes, greater civic participation. The Abecedarian Project, which began working with children in infancy rather than at age three, shows similar findings with even earlier intervention. The Chicago Child-Parent Centers, which ran across a larger and more diverse urban population over many years, extended the evidence with consistent results.

The benefits are largest for children from low-income families. This is not because early childhood education matters less for affluent children. It is because affluent children are already receiving the equivalent through the resources their families can provide: the books, the conversation, the stable housing, the nutritious food, the absence of chronic stress. The public investment in early childhood education for children whose families cannot provide these conditions is the economic floor applied at the moment when the floor matters most, before the gap between children born into different circumstances has had years to compound.

Universal pre-K from age three, fully funded and fully resourced, is not an educational luxury. It is the precondition for everything the educational system claims to be trying to achieve. The child who arrives at kindergarten two years behind a peer who had access to high-quality early childhood education is not going to catch up through the normal operation of the K-12 system. The gap compounds. The investment that does not happen at three requires ten times the investment at thirteen to begin to close. You build at the beginning because the beginning is where the building matters most.

The Teacher

Any serious document about education has to address who teaches and under what conditions. The current system treats teachers the way it treats students: as people to be managed into compliance rather than trusted as professionals in their own classrooms. Scripted curricula that require teachers to follow a script regardless of what the students in front of them need. Test-prep mandates that compress the curriculum into the subset of knowledge that will be assessed. Evaluation systems that reduce the complex practice of teaching to a score derived from student test performance. Administrative hierarchies that increase while classroom resources decrease.

The teacher who is not permitted to follow their own obsessions cannot teach students to follow theirs. The teaching philosophy this document holds to is simple: bring everything you know into the room and give it completely. The full weight of attention. No holding back for a more manageable version. The teacher who is managing thirty students through a scripted curriculum toward a standardized test is not doing that. They are a production line worker whose product is test scores.

Teacher pay, in every country where it has been studied, is correlated with educational outcomes. Countries that pay teachers comparably to other professions requiring similar education and expertise attract a broader and stronger pool of candidates into the profession. The United States systematically underpays teachers, particularly at the elementary level and in the schools serving the lowest-income communities: the schools where the teaching is hardest and the support is least. This is the same pattern applied to labor policy: a judgment about whose work is valuable, presented as the market's objective assessment of what teaching is worth.

Teacher autonomy is the prerequisite for the teaching this document envisions. The teacher trusted to know their students, to follow the thread when the students pull it somewhere the curriculum did not anticipate, to spend three weeks on the thing that has caught the class's obsession rather than moving on because the sequence requires it: that teacher is doing the real work. That teacher cannot exist inside the current accountability architecture. The architecture has to change.

Universal Design and Neurodiversity

Universal design in education starts from the question: how do we build this so that every student can participate fully, rather than building it for the median student and then providing accommodations for everyone else? The accommodation model treats the standard as fixed and offers a lesser version to everyone who cannot meet it. The universal design model asks: what if we built the standard differently from the beginning, so that the modification is not necessary?

The neurodiversity framework understands cognitive difference not as deficit but as variation. The student with dyslexia is not a student with a broken reading mechanism. They are a student whose reading mechanism works differently from the one the current instructional system was designed for. The student with ADHD is not a student with deficient attention. They are a student whose attention works differently from the one the current classroom structure was designed for. The mismatch between the student and the system is a design problem, not a student problem.

Multiple means of representation: students access information through different modalities and the curriculum is built to offer multiple pathways into the same content. Multiple means of expression: students demonstrate what they know through different formats and assessment systems are built to receive multiple forms of demonstration. Multiple means of engagement: students are motivated by different things and the learning environment is built to activate different forms of interest and persistence. These are the Universal Design for Learning principles developed by researchers at CAST (the Center for Applied Special Technology), and they are the honest acknowledgment that there is no singular acceptable learner and the system built for one is failing everyone else.

Education and Historical Grief

The history of American education is inseparable from the history of American violence against specific populations. The boarding school system for indigenous children, which operated for over a century with the explicit goal of destroying indigenous languages, cultures, and family structures, is not a distant historical episode. Its effects are present in indigenous communities today in the patterns of trauma, disconnection, and loss that researchers and community members document consistently. Several federally run boarding schools still operate today, though under a changed mission. The people whose grandparents were taken are alive.

The systematic miseducation of Black children, documented from Carter G. Woodson's 1933 book "The Mis-Education of the Negro" forward, is education used as a tool of erasure: the teaching of a version of history and culture that positioned Black people as objects of history rather than its subjects, that excluded or distorted the intellectual and creative traditions of the African diaspora, that prepared Black students for the subordinate positions the economy had decided they would occupy. The curriculum integration that followed the civil rights movement was incomplete and has been progressively rolled back in many states.

The truth and restorative justice process established in Article VII of the Constitution must include an educational component: the honest reckoning with what the educational system did and did not do to specific communities, and the active reconstruction of an educational system that those communities have genuine power to shape. This is not the dominant system adding a unit on indigenous history written by people outside indigenous communities. It is indigenous communities, Black communities, the communities most directly affected by the educational system's historical violence, having genuine authority over the education of their children. That authority is not a supplement to the constitutional education right. It is its fullest expression.

What the Classroom Looks Like

The classroom is recognizable as a classroom. There are students and a teacher. There is content to be learned. There is assessment of whether the learning is happening. None of that changes. What changes is the relationship between all of those elements.

The content is the full human record, not the curated consensus version. The students bring their own histories into contact with it, which means the content is alive in the room rather than delivered from outside it. The teacher is following the obsessions rather than the sequence, which means the sequence emerges from the learning rather than being imposed on it. The assessment asks what the student knows and can do and is building toward, not how they compare to the students the test was designed to measure.

The room holds everyone. The student who reads differently, who attends differently, who processes differently, who grieves differently, who carries a history that the curriculum has previously pretended does not exist: all of them are in the room and the room is built to hold all of them. Not because inclusion is a value to be performed but because the capacity to engage with complexity cannot be cultivated in a room from which complexity has been sorted away.

The student in this classroom is learning to stay in the room with difficulty. Not to be comfortable with everything, not to agree with everyone, not to perform tolerance as a social courtesy. To genuinely engage with what is actually there, including what is uncomfortable, including what challenges their own understanding of how things are. That is the civic capacity the constitution requires. It cannot be taught in a lecture. It can only be practiced in a room where it is required.

The teacher in this classroom brings the full weight of attention to what is actually happening in the room, following the thread when it leads somewhere unexpected, trusting the process. Not managing the students toward a predetermined output but accompanying them toward something neither the teacher nor the students have fully seen yet.

The classroom built on this philosophy gives students the same gift: the time and permission to find out how much is actually in the room they are in.

The Transition

The transition from the current system to this one is not instantaneous and pretending it could be would be dishonest. The current system employs millions of people, maintains physical infrastructure across the country, carries institutional inertia of enormous weight, and is defended by interests that benefit from its current form: the testing industry, the textbook industry, the private school sector, the real estate interests that depend on the connection between school quality and property values.

The transition requires a timeline, adequate funding, and the genuine participation of the communities most affected in designing what replaces what currently exists. It requires the training and compensation of teachers for the practices this document describes. It requires the physical reconstruction of school buildings designed for the singular acceptable student into spaces that can hold everyone. It requires the development of curriculum that does not yet exist at the scale required. None of this is impossible. All of it is expensive. The expense is the investment that makes everything else possible.

The abolition of private schools requires the same honest acknowledgment of transition complexity. The endowments, the buildings, the teachers, the families who have organized their lives around private school options: all of these require a transition plan that is fair to the people caught in the change while being uncompromising about the destination. The destination is a public school system good enough that no family needs a private option and wealthy enough to make that genuine rather than aspirational.

What is written here is one pass: it finds what the previous conversations walked past, it builds on what the constitution gestured at, and it points toward a next pass that will find what this one has missed.
