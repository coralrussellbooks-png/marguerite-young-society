The Symbolism

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · The Stone Deaf Man · Vera Cartwheel

Young's symbols are not scattered decorations. They organize into a small number of families, and every family points in the same direction: toward the collapse of the boundaries the mind relies on, between life and death, reality and illusion, self and other, past and present. To catalogue the symbols one by one is to miss that they are a system, each object a different instrument playing the same refusal. But before the system can be read, it helps to know where the habit of mind that built it came from, because Young's symbolism is not only a set of images. It is a way of thinking in images, and that way of thinking was trained. She was a scholar before she was a novelist, and the novel is the output of a specific education. What follows traces that education first, from the sentence she inherited to the consciousness she inherited, and then reads the symbols as what that formation produced.

The euphuistic root

Young did her graduate study on John Lyly, whose Euphues gave the language the word euphuism: a prose built on balanced antithesis, elaborate patterning, and above all the relentless drawing of similitudes from the natural world, the beast, the stone, the star, the herb, each pressed into service as an emblem of a human truth. Lyly's prose advances by coupling opposites and by heaping analogy on analogy until the argument is carried less by statement than by accumulated image.

This is Young's method exactly, transposed from the Elizabethan drawing room to the American interior. Her sentences balance opposites the way Lyly's do, and her symbols work the way Lyly's similitudes work, as a hoard of natural and made objects, the sea, the rose, the chessman, the clock, the ship, each standing for a state of the soul. The difference is what she does with the form. Lyly's antitheses resolve into wit; the balanced opposites click shut into a point. Young takes the same balancing habit and denies it the click. Her opposites are coupled and left open, her similitudes multiplied past any single meaning, so that the euphuistic machine, built to produce clarity through pattern, is turned to produce the opposite: a patterned irresolution. The scholar of Lyly learned to think in balanced emblems and then built a novel in which the emblems refuse to balance out. That inheritance, absorbed and inverted, is the engine under everything that follows.

The formal inheritance: how the book is built

If Lyly gave Young the sentence, two English novelists gave her the shape of the book, and she studied both of them as deliberately as she studied Lyly.

The decisive one was Fielding. As a student under the Neo-Aristotelian critic Ronald S. Crane at the University of Chicago, Young read Tom Jones thirteen times, not for pleasure and not for the plot, but to map its invisible, load-bearing architecture, to see how every seemingly casual digression and peripheral character served a grand formal design the novel never announced but always enacted. What she took from Fielding, by way of Crane's exacting functional questions, was a single conviction: that a novel's structure could hide itself completely in plain sight, that the design could be total and yet invisible on a first reading, revealing itself only across the full length to a reader patient enough to feel it. She spent eighteen years applying that lesson to a book that looks nothing like Tom Jones. What reads as oceanic, undisciplined prose is, on this inheritance, a precisely engineered structure whose architecture is meant to stay submerged until the whole has been carried.

The second formal ancestor is Sterne. Tristram Shandy is where the English novel first refused to advance in a straight line, where digression became the method rather than a lapse from it, where time looped and stalled and doubled back and the promised story kept deferring itself. Young inherits the license Sterne opened: the right to build a long book that circles rather than climbs, that treats the tangent as the substance and the arrival as something it need never quite reach. From Fielding she took the buried, total design; from Sterne, the freedom to let that design express itself as circling rather than progress. Between them they account for the paradox of the novel's shape, that it can be enormous and rigorously built and still go nowhere in the ordinary sense, because a structure this deliberate does not have to travel in order to hold.

The consciousness inheritance: what the book believes, and how it repeats

The formal ancestors explain how the novel is made. Two figures from psychology and philosophy explain what it believes about the mind it renders, though one of them enters by being refused.

William James is the genuine ancestor. The stream of consciousness that critics reach for to describe Young's prose is James's phrase and James's discovery, and the deeper inheritance is his method in the Principles of Psychology: the patient, exhaustive description of mental experience as it is actually lived, before philosophy or clinical practice has sorted it into categories. James insisted that every element of the stream is equally real as an element of consciousness, regardless of whether it corresponds to anything outside the mind. The fringe, the half-formed feeling at the edge of attention, the vague and the peripheral, are as much a part of the stream as the clear focal thoughts that respectable psychology had taken as its only subject. This is the philosophical root of Young's refusal to rank. Her prose grants the dead queens the same reality as the knitting needles because James had already argued that consciousness does not, in fact, sort its contents into the real and the negligible while it is having them. The oceanic quality of her sentences, moving outward in all directions at once and carrying everything present in the mind forward together, is James's stream widened until it has no banks. His radical empiricism, in which reality is constituted by experience rather than found beneath it, is a direct ancestor of the novel's conviction that the world is made by the attention paid to it.

Stein is the contrast, and Young drew it herself. The two spent two weeks together at the University of Chicago, and when Young was later asked what Stein's influence had been, she answered: very little. This is worth taking at face value, because Young was declining precisely the kinship a careless reader would assume, and she was right to decline it. Stein is the one contemporary whose surface most resembles Young's, the insistent repetition, the continuous present, the phrase returned to and slightly varied. But the resemblance is a trap, and the two weeks that produced the disavowal are the tell. Stein repeats in order to strip a word of its accumulated associations, to scrub memory off it and make it land clean in the present, a word freed from everything it has carried. Young repeats to do the exact reverse. Her recursion thickens a phrase with everything it has already meant in the book, so that each return deposits more of the past into it rather than clearing the past away. Stein's continuous present erases history from the word; Young's circling is history refusing to leave the word. The two methods look identical on the page and are opposite in their aim, and Young's "very little" is not modesty or evasion but an accurate account of that difference. The one contemporary she most resembles is the one she most needed to distinguish herself from, and the distinction is what her repetition is for: not the erasure of the past but its accumulation, which is the emotional weather of the entire novel.

So the education resolves into a single tendency. From Lyly, a sentence that balances opposites and hoards emblems. From Fielding, a total design that hides itself. From Sterne, the freedom to circle rather than arrive. From James, a mind whose contents cannot honestly be ranked. And from Stein, by way of refusal, a repetition that gathers the past instead of dissolving it. Every one of these points the same way, toward accumulation without resolution, and the symbols are where that tendency becomes visible objects.

Symbols of the dissolving boundary: sea, water, ship

The largest family is water in all its forms, and its work is dissolution. The sea, the tides, the floods, the submerged cities are the novel's image for the unconscious and the infinite, but more precisely they are the image for the loss of edges. Water muffles sound and obliterates boundaries; it merges the separate into the unknowable whole; it is where individual identity goes to stop being individual. When the sea finally takes Catherine's house, it is not just destroying a building but performing what water always performs in the novel, the erasure of the line between one thing and another.

The ship and the voyage belong to the same family, turned toward the journey rather than the merging. Ships, sea voyages, and shipwrecks figure life as navigation through chaos toward a meaning that may not arrive; the lost ships and drowned passengers are the disappearances the whole book grieves. Water dissolves, and the ship is the fragile human attempt to cross the dissolving thing without going under. That most of the novel's ships are lost tells you how Young rates the attempt.

Symbols of decay: the house, the marble statues, the rose

A second family renders the passage of time as ruin, and it centers on the sea-blackened house, with its gilded ballrooms where no one dances, its broken statues, its ruined gardens. The house is grandeur in the act of failing, a physical and psychological space where memory and illusion and ghost all lodge, and its slow slide into ruin is the fading of a life and the dissolution of a self made visible in architecture.

The marble statues inside it sharpen the point. Broken and scattered, they are human aspiration and the idealization of beauty, and their breakage is the failure of those ideals to survive time and nature. Miss MacIntosh's disdain for them is characteristic and, in the novel's final judgment, ironic: she scorns the statues as pretension, the plain woman rejecting the dream of perfection, but she is herself a construction under a wig, an aspiration to solidity as breakable as any marble. The rose completes the family from the other side. Ethereal, ghostly, falling, the rose and its petals are beauty precisely as transience, and the "immortal rose" is the paradox the family turns on: the fleeting thing wished permanent, love and memory persisting past the death that should have ended them.

Symbols of duality held open: black and white, the chessmen

A third family stages opposition without letting it resolve, and it is the most directly euphuistic of the groups, since it is built on balanced pairs. The recurrent black and white, the black coachman, the black and white chessmen, the play of light and shadow, sets life against death, reality against illusion, knowledge against ignorance, and then declines to choose. The pairing is the meaning. Where Lyly's antitheses would resolve to a witty point, Young's stay balanced and unresolved, two colors on one board.

The chessmen make the family's argument fully. Life-sized, moving through shifting clouds and waters, they figure existence as a game of fate whose pieces are moved by forces larger than themselves, and they "move unevenly," which is the tell: the board is not the site of fair contest but of imbalance, individuals caught in a game they do not control and cannot see whole. The black-and-white board promises the clean opposition of a game with rules. The uneven motion takes the promise back.

Symbols of the unstable self and unreliable time: the coachman, Mr. Spitzer's cloak, the clocks

A fourth family attacks the two coordinates a person uses to locate the self, identity and time. The black coachman is the presiding image, driving his carriage through chaos and darkness, his face never seen, never turning to his passengers, the perfect emblem of a fate that steers without revealing itself. He is death and the soul's passage, and the novel names him specifically as Catherine's conscience, so that the unknowable driver of her life turns out to be a part of herself she cannot see the face of. The self is driven by the self and cannot look at the driver.

Mr. Spitzer's cloak does the same work on the body. The mantle that seems to grow and envelop him like a shell is identity as both shelter and prison, the shadowy, ambiguous covering of a man who cannot say whether he is himself or his brother, alive or dead. And the clocks, sundials, and watches with broken or missing numbers dismantle the other coordinate: time here is cyclical, fragmented, suspended, never a reliable line. Between the faceless driver, the enveloping cloak, and the broken clocks, the novel strips a person of every fixed point, who is steering, who am I, what time is it, and leaves the self turning without a coordinate to hold.

Symbols of communication past language: the mute's hands, silence

A last, smaller family points to what lies beyond words, and it is where the novel locates its deepest expression. The mute character's hands, performing their signs of prayer, farewell, flight, and meeting, stand for the ineffable and for the limits of language, communication that succeeds exactly where speech fails. This family runs toward the stone deaf man Vera marries, and it makes the book's quiet claim that the truest things are said in silence, by the hand, by the flower, in the register language cannot reach. In a novel so made of language, these symbols of wordless meaning are Young's admission of where words stop, which is itself a euphuist's confession, the master of patterned speech marking the border past which pattern cannot go.

The whole system

Set the families side by side and the design is plain. Water dissolves the boundary between things; the house and rose and statue dissolve the boundary between grandeur and ruin; black and white and the chessboard hold opposites open without resolving them; the coachman and cloak and clock dissolve the self and its sense of time; the mute's hands dissolve the boundary between speech and silence. Every family is a different assault on the same wall, the wall between the categories a mind uses to keep reality sorted.

And every family traces back to Young’s education. The balanced emblem is Lyly. The buried total design that only the whole reveals is Fielding. The refusal to arrive is Sterne. The unranked contents of the mind are James. The repetition that gathers the past rather than dissolving it is Stein turned inside out. Young learned to think in balanced emblems and pressed the whole inherited apparatus toward a single end none of her models intended: not to sort the world into witty oppositions or clean streams or cleaned-off words, but to show the oppositions bleeding into one another until the sorting fails.

The lesson for a writer is in the discipline underneath the density. Young's symbolism looks like profusion, image heaped on image, but it is governed, every symbol assigned to one of a few jobs and every job serving one argument, and the governance was learned. A writer drowning a page in imagery for its own sake produces only noise; Young produces a system, because each image, however lush, is doing philosophical work, and because behind each image stands a decade of study in how images can be made to carry it. The reader who traces the families rather than the individual symbols finds not a catalogue of pretty things but a single sustained proof that the lines we draw to make the world legible are lines the world does not honor, built by a writer who had studied, more closely than almost anyone, exactly how those lines get drawn.
