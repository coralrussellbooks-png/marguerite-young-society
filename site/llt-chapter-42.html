Chapter 42

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: Not One Self · Unfinished Is Honest

The Questions

Catherine reports a farewell visit to Cousin Hannah that “could never have taken place,” both women being bedridden. Why is Mr. Spitzer “obscurely grateful” for it anyway?

His census rolls always have “one person…missing,” and he decides the missing one is himself. What is a self that is always the one left off the list?

He catalogues the lost ships, sure of the longitude or the latitude but never both. What kind of knowledge can never be completed?

He likens himself to a ship carrying “that supernumerary passenger who…was his soul.” Where is the soul, if it is the hidden extra aboard?

Catherine’s carriage-journey to the dying Cousin Hannah is a memory of something that never happened. Both women were bedridden, the visit “could never have taken place in this life,” and the chapter says so plainly. Yet Mr. Spitzer is “obscurely grateful” for her “fitful accuracy,” because her impossible report gets the city lights and the jogs in the road and the last days of the suffrage captain very nearly right. This is memory as construction at its most exact and its most impossible at once: Catherine builds a visit out of nothing and the building is somehow true, truer than the fact that it could not have occurred. The book will not let the reader choose between the impossibility and the accuracy. Both stand.

Mr. Spitzer’s work is the counting of the lost, and the counting keeps producing the same remainder. He studies census rolls and graveyard registers “from which one person was always missing no matter what time or place it was,” and he concludes “doubtless that person was himself…always precariously absent.” This is the missing self again, carried over from the immortal-lost one of the last chapter and made his own: he is the person left off every list, the one the record cannot hold. Not One Self reaches its strangest form here, a self defined not by its many presences but by its permanent absence, the one always subtracted from the count.

His catalogue of sunken ships is Unfinished Is Honest turned into a life’s labor. He lists the vessels that went down and where, but “if he knew the longitude, then he did not know the latitude,” and the reports “were not always to be trusted,” and “sometimes there were no eye witnesses.” The list cannot be finished, and its unfinishability is not a failure of his diligence but the honest shape of the knowledge: the lost cannot be fully located, and a complete register of the vanished would be a lie. He is particularly drawn to the ships that “left no wreckage,” the ones that disappeared without trace, because they are the purest case of what cannot be closed.

And the soul, in this chapter, is the hidden extra aboard. Mr. Spitzer likens himself to “that ship carrying that supernumerary passenger who, darkly hidden from him, was his soul,” one more than the manifest records, unaccounted for, riding along unseen. The self is not the ship and not the listed passengers but the supernumerary one the list missed, the same missing person from the census, now sailing. “Should the lost locate the lost?” he asks, and the question is the chapter’s: a man who is himself the missing one, cataloguing the missing, looking for a soul that is the extra passenger he cannot see.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that dismisses Catherine’s impossible visit as mere delusion to be corrected, and the reading that treats Mr. Spitzer’s catalogues as a task that could, with more diligence, be completed. Neither the corrected error nor the completable list is what the chapter offers.

The footing is that memory builds what could not have happened and the building can still be true, so that Catherine’s impossible visit and its fitful accuracy both stand. The self is the one always missing from the count, the person subtracted from every census, and the soul is the supernumerary passenger the manifest cannot hold. And the register of the lost cannot be completed, the longitude known without the latitude, the ships that left no wreckage the truest case, because the lost cannot be fully located. The impossible can be accurate, and the count of the missing always misses one. Mr. Spitzer goes on listing the vanished, himself among them, and the chapter closes no ledger.

For Reflection

Can an account of something that could not have happened still be, in the ways that matter, accurate?

Is there a sense in which one is always the person missing from one’s own accounting of a life?

What knowledge in one’s life is unfinishable by its nature rather than by any failure of effort?
