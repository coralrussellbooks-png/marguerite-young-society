Research notes: Chapter 43 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 43

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

"He did not know which aspect of Cousin Hannah was dying—would hate to think all were dying, facet after facet"

This is perhaps the most explicit statement of the plural self in the entire chapter. Mr. Spitzer does not mourn a unified person. He mourns a multiplicity, and the horror is not death itself but the possibility that all the facets might die simultaneously.

Hannah herself demonstrates the plural self in her delirium. She asks:

"Am I the anvil? Am I the hammer? Am I the anvil which is struck or the hammer which strikes? Am I the flying spark? Am I the star? Am I the horseshoe? Am I the horseman or the horse?"

These are not rhetorical questions born of confusion. They are ontological statements. She cannot locate herself within a single role because she is the entire system of relations: striker and struck, container and contained, self and other. The plural self is not her dying condition; it was always her condition, only now made visible.

Mr. Spitzer mirrors this plurality. He is:

"not sure who he was, whether living or dead...not sure where he stood or where he should cast his vote"

He cannot mourn clearly because he cannot establish a stable mourning subject. The mourner and the mourned are both plural, both irresolvable. The Radical Immanence framework insists this is not pathology, and the chapter confirms it. This irresolution is simply what they are.

Hannah's dying visions further fracture identity. She speaks to emperors, blacksmiths, Moorish figures, cup-bearers, Chinese lords; each addressee representing a different relational self, a different Hannah who existed in relation to a different world. The self is not one person moving through time but a chorus of selves, some of whom are only now being heard for the first time.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

"Perhaps all things were illusion, just as he had always told himself, this crying, this crumbling, this falling away, this vanishing, this revelation of that which was no more, and there was nothing given but this dream. Perhaps this dream was not given but earned."

Young's prose does not present illusion as something to be stripped away to reveal truth beneath. Instead, illusion is primary material: the substance of which experience is made. The "dream" is not opposed to reality; it is the only available reality, and crucially, it may be earned rather than merely suffered.

This is the second cave, more carefully lit. Hannah's visions of Nineveh, Babylon, Ur of the Chaldees, crystal deserts, and hanging gardens are not symptoms of a failing mind. They are the architecture of a consciousness that has always operated in constructed space. The chapter asks:

"Was it a city or a mirage shining through clouds? Was it the future or the past?"

The question is never answered, because within the framework of Radical Immanence, it cannot be. The mirage and the city are the same thing differently lit. Reality is not what we find; it is what we make. Hannah has been making her reality all her life, and she is making it still, even now.

Mr. Spitzer's own epistemological instability confirms this. He cannot verify Hannah's past adventures:

"he had never trusted her adventures in the remote past when there still might have been a possibility of checking on them...Why should he believe in her now when there was no proof?"

But this skepticism is not a condemnation. It is a recognition that all experience operates without final proof. The chapter never suggests that verifiable reality would be preferable. It suggests instead that the constructed dream is the only dwelling available to any of us.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

"he was always mourning for one or the other"

This single line encapsulates Pillar 3 with devastating economy. Mr. Spitzer does not grieve in response to specific losses. Grief is his climate. It precedes the particular death he witnesses. It will outlast it.

The chapter refuses the arc of grief. There is no movement from sorrow toward acceptance, no catharsis, no resolution. Instead, grief accumulates:

"He had been witness to this great lady's screamings, babblings which had revealed so much of vanished life, that past which, as he had thought then, was not memory—but something almost spontaneously starting up outside of the scheme of time"

The past does not resolve into grief and grief into peace. The past starts up again, unbidden, outside of time. Grief here is not a response to loss but a permanent condition of consciousness in a world where everything loved is always already changing.

Even Hannah's dying is framed not as a discrete event but as a continuation of a process long underway:

"there was this lingering presentiment that she had died long before—perhaps long before him"

She may already have died. The death-bed scene may be a belated recognition of a loss that was always already occurring. The honest life, in Radical Immanence terms, stops pretending that grief has a beginning and an end. Young's prose performs this: the chapter has no clear temporal markers, no before and after, only the ongoing weather of mourning.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Mr. Spitzer's role in this chapter is almost entirely that of witness. He does not intervene. He does not comfort. He does not try to pull Hannah back from her visions or correct her delusions. He simply stays in the room:

"He had sat by her bedside, often consulting his watch with the broken crystal...keeping at all times the attitudes of dignity, remorse, patience which had always been his, to that extent that perhaps some people thought he was unfeeling—for someone must maintain spiritual equilibrium as this body plunged like a wild horse"

The broken crystal of the watch is a precise image for the witness's position; he measures time with an instrument that cannot accurately measure time. He keeps vigil with broken tools. And yet he stays.

The ethical weight of witnessing is made explicit:

"What good would it do for him to lose his calm?"

This is not coldness. It is the recognition that the witness's task is attention, not intervention. To flinch, to rush toward resolution, to simplify Hannah's dying into something manageable would be a betrayal of what is actually happening. The Radical Immanence framework names this as the central ethical stance, and the chapter dramatizes it at length.

Crucially, the witness himself is unreliable:

he "must also recognize that, as he had told himself at the time, she was not in her complete senses and perhaps not responsible for all she said"

"he must leave at all times a wide margin for error or doubt"

The witness does not claim perfect access. He acknowledges the limits of his witnessing. This is not a failure of the ethical stance but its fulfillment: refusal to simplify includes refusing to simplify one's own perception into certainty.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Hannah's dying is not linear. It spirals. She returns again and again to the same images (the skirt, the mirror, the Black Moor, the crystal casket, the lost love) each time from a slightly different angle, each time with slightly different urgency.

Mr. Spitzer himself operates recursively. He notes that he may be confusing Hannah's hallucinations with the opium lady's:

"Mr. Spitzer, having been present at the opium lady's many hallucinations—and, indeed, he was not sure that he had not confused them in memory with these"

He builds his understanding of this death from materials gathered at other deaths. He returns to the beginning with what he learned, but what he learned was itself provisional, possibly misremembered, possibly belonging to someone else. The construction is never finished because the materials themselves keep shifting.

The prose enacts recursive construction formally. Young's sentences loop back on themselves, qualify their own qualifications, begin again:

"Or so he had supposed. He had supposed that all would be over in a moment, that physiological death was actual death or that one would soon follow the other"

The double "supposed" performs the recursion. He builds a thought, recognizes it as a construction, and builds it again, knowing it is provisional. This is not failure. This is how it works.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter is structurally incomplete. Hannah's death is not witnessed as a concluded event. The prose does not arrive at a moment of final passing and then step back to assess. It remains inside the ongoing process, inside the unfinished sentence of dying:

"There would be only a few more flashings, gleamings."

This is as close as the chapter comes to marking an end, and even here, the language is of diminishing light rather than extinction. The demand for a completed death scene, a resolved death scene, would be, in Radical Immanence terms, a violence against experience.

Mr. Spitzer's understanding itself remains incomplete:

"It seemed to him that his understanding always came too late."

This is not presented as a personal failure but as a structural condition of consciousness. Understanding arrives after the moment it could have been useful, which means understanding is always, by definition, incomplete relative to the living moment. The ethics of incompletion means accepting this without self-reproach, continuing to understand even when understanding can no longer help.

Hannah's great cause (suffrage, women's liberation) is itself left incomplete at her death. She dies not having secured all she fought for. The chapter does not mourn this as failure:

"her cause so great that there had been little room for any other thought"

The ongoing sentence of her life's work is not concluded by her death. It continues elsewhere, in others. The unfinished is honesty.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Hannah herself is a figure of the marginal: a suffragette, a woman who rejected the roles assigned to women, who lived outside the structures of marriage and conventional femininity. Mr. Spitzer is equally marginal; neither fully living nor fully dead, neither mourner nor mourned, neither present nor absent.

The chapter's most philosophically rich moment for this pillar comes with the Black Moor:

"this Black Moor who was woman's great love might be a man and not a woman as Mr. Spitzer might have supposed"

The gender ambiguity of the Black Moor, and of Hannah's love for this figure, places the dying woman at the edge of multiple categories simultaneously. She is a woman who loved in ways that could not be named, a suffragette who cried for her skirts at the last moment, a great public figure who harbored intensely private passions. She is the evidence of the constructed nature of the categories that claimed to contain her.

Mr. Spitzer notes:

"he was not sure that he had not confused them in memory with these"

The confusion of one marginal figure's hallucinations with another's is not mere carelessness. It suggests that marginal figures share a common territory, the space outside the categories, and that their experiences, however individually specific, illuminate the same structural reality: the edges of categories are where the constructed nature of reality becomes most visible.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This pillar may be the most thoroughly worked in the entire chapter. Time in Young's prose does not flow forward. It accumulates:

"time which was as thin, as superficial as a sheath of ice upon a brook where the tinkling water ran"

Time is a surface phenomenon. Beneath it, the water runs: the accumulated past, still moving, still pressing upward. The past does not recede. It is beneath us.

Hannah's dying visions are precisely this sedimentary time made visible. She sees Tiberius, ancient armies, Nineveh, Babylon, Ur of the Chaldees, not as historical curiosities but as layers of the present, still pressing up through the ice of the immediate moment. These are not memories. They are, as Mr. Spitzer notes:

"not memory—but something almost spontaneously starting up outside of the scheme of time, whether future or past"

The past starts up. It does not stay buried. It is not behind her; it is beneath her, and at the moment of death the ice thins and it breaks through.

The image of broken mosaics is particularly precise for sedimentary time:

"From tiny bits, broken mosaics, gleamings of stone traced by the lizard's path, pieces of glass gleaming through fog, he had to put together a complex pattern he was only beginning to understand."

The mosaic is a sedimented form: pieces laid down at different times, now constituting a single surface. To read the mosaic is to read time's accumulation. Mr. Spitzer is always only beginning to understand because the sediment is always still settling.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Young's prose is itself the most powerful argument for this pillar. The language of the chapter does not attempt transparency. It draws attention constantly to its own constructedness: its repetitions, its qualifications, its inability to pin down what it describes.

Consider the recursive qualification:

"Or so Mr. Spitzer thought though perhaps, of course, he might have heard incorrectly. So many times he heard voices which others did not hear."

The language names its own location. It speaks from the position of a man with a "dead ear" a broken watch crystal a tendency to confuse one person's dreams with another's. It does not pretend to speak from nowhere.

Hannah's own language in her delirium is similarly self-aware about its constructedness:

"Oh, the whole thing's full of fallacies...you can't stretch it to that, or else the line will break. It's the merest silken thread, this cable. Why, it's a hair, dear heart, a hair of starlight reaching through a cloud and not a bridge"

She is describing the limits of language, of the thread of connection between signifier and signified, between word and world. The cable that should be a bridge is only a hair. Language reaches but does not arrive.

The chapter's obsessive use of questions, Hannah's dying speech is almost entirely interrogative, performs language's inability to conclude. Questions do not assert; they open. The language of this chapter refuses to close.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

This chapter is saturated with images of transcendence: mountains, stars, crystal palaces, distant cities, the lid of the sky lifting or lowering. But crucially, none of these images point outward to a beyond that is separate from the immanent world. They point deeper into the immanent:

"Death was the wind whistling through these shrouds, the wind blowing these snowflakes, these glittering sands, the wind causing these dead chords to tremble, these dead voices to sing, whisper, shout upon the wind. There was no death but the dying wind."

The sacred is the wind. The wind is here, in the shrouds, in the snowflakes, in the dead chords. There is no separate realm of the transcendent. Death itself is immanent; it is this wind, these snowflakes.

Mr. Spitzer's blue stone ring, "the blue stone gleaming like a lighthouse on his finger", is a perfect image of immanent transcendence. The sacred light is in the ordinary object, on the finger of a man who may or may not be alive, sitting by the bedside of a woman who may or may not be dying. The lighthouse does not point to another shore. It illuminates this darkness.

Hannah's cry for her skirt is perhaps the most surprising instance of immanent transcendence in the chapter:

"Oh, who will cover me and make me beautiful for my great lord who comes in darkness and in snow and whirlwind"

The great lord is not a deity in a separate realm. He comes in darkness and in snow and in whirlwind, in the weather of the immediate world. The sacred encounter, the transcendent love, happens here, in the storm, in the immanent world of ice and wind.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Hannah at her death-bed is precisely this crossing point. She is constituted by what was deposited before she arrived: the suffrage movement, the women who came before her, the ancient civilizations that press up through her visions, the unnamed love she lost long ago. And she is constituting the ground that others will stand on: the cause she carried, incomplete, to be carried further.

Mr. Spitzer understands this structurally:

"he had discovered many things which others had not known or had ignored or had interpreted incorrectly as to this great lady's former life and imminent death"

He is the inheritor of her sediment. He carries what she deposited. The carrying continues.

The most explicit statement of the crossing point comes in the chapter's meditation on voting, which is, after all, the political form of constituting the ground for others:

"He believed, unlike his brother, that the dead cast no vote—and so Mr. Spitzer made no choice. He had not been inside a ballot box for years."

But this is precisely wrong by the logic of Pillar 11. The dead do constitute the ground. Hannah's entire life was an act of depositing ground: fighting for the vote, for women's right to constitute the future. The obligation is structural, not chosen. The ancestors are in the sediment. And Hannah, dying, is becoming sediment: becoming the ground that those who come after will stand on without knowing her name.

Her final cries perform this:

"Death might be her life, her love, her whisper in the darkness, she whispered, for should love be extinguished by death?"

The whisper continues after the whisperer is gone. This is the crossing point: the place where what was deposited before her arrival and what she is depositing for those who come after her intersect in the single present moment of her dying. She is not moving through time. She is where time crosses itself.
