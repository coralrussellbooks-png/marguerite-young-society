Chapter 2

Chapter 2

Chapter 2

Chapter 11

  • “the proud, cold, utterly snobbish Queen Maud Mountains”: The Queen Maud Mountains rise at the head of the Ross Ice Shelf, on the south edge of the Ross Sea. Roald Amundsen found them in 1911 on his way to the South Pole and named them for Queen Maud of Norway, a daughter of King Edward VII of England. Queen Maud Land is a different region of Antarctica, claimed by Norway.
  • The Ross Sea, a deep bay of the Southern Ocean, is named for James Clark Ross, who sailed into it in 1841.
  • “Sir Walter Raleigh in his marvelous white satin suit embroidered with seed pearls”: Sir Walter Raleigh (c. 1552-1618) was an English statesman, soldier, spy, writer, poet, and explorer. He was famous at Elizabeth’s court for the pearls and jewels on his clothes, even his shoes.
  • “Admiral Peary”: Robert E. Peary claimed the North Pole in 1909 and was made a rear admiral in 1911. Matthew Henson, the Black explorer beside him, by his own account reached the spot first and got little credit for decades. In 1897 Peary brought six Inuit from Greenland to New York for the American Museum of Natural History, and four died within months. The museum staged a fake burial for the boy Minik while it kept his father Qisuk’s skeleton.

Chapter 18

  • “the rose at the North Pole is the rose immortal”: In a spiritual sense it signifies the coexistence of death and eternal life, the presence of enduring beauty and essence in a world otherwise marked by loss, coldness, and transience. It evokes a mystical, almost sacred dimension beneath the surface of harsh reality or an eternal “rose” blooming in the frozen heart of the North Pole.
  • The Compass Rose (Rose of the Winds) represents spiritual direction, awakening, and discovery. The directions of the compass represents infinite possibility, the present/past/future or the direction of your True North. There are two North Poles (magnetic and true). It signifies the challenge of navigating through the complexities of life and consciousness, where certainties dissolve, and one must find or create meaning amid flux and paradox. This theme resonates with the broader novel’s exploration of illusion versus reality, identity, and the search for truth.

Chapter 20

  • “haunted rays of Aurora Borealis”: The northern lights. The Latin name, “northern dawn,” joins Aurora, the Roman goddess of the dawn, and Boreas, the north wind, and is usually credited to Galileo (1619).

Chapter 22

  • “Where’s old Jessup Land, far to the North?”: The Arctic Jesup Land, named for Morris K. Jesup, president of the Peary Arctic Club, like Cape Morris Jesup in Greenland.
  • “Where’s Herbert Island?”: Herbert Island lies off northwest Greenland, near Thule, a walrus-hunting ground.
  • “Where is Queen Maud Mountains”: Roald Amundsen named them in 1911, on his way to the South Pole, for Queen Maud of Norway.

Chapter 23

  • “is Baffin Bay?”: Baffin Bay is located between Baffin Island and the west coast of Greenland. It is named for William Baffin, who charted it in 1616.
  • “It’s always well”: An igloo (Inuktitut iglu, “house”) is built of snow blocks.
  • “the latitude and longitude in which may be located the Austral Islands, ice-locked Baffin Bay or the Magnetic Pole”: The Austral Islands are in French Polynesia. James Clark Ross reached the North Magnetic Pole in 1831.

Chapter 24

  • “Admiral Peary was to be put into the blue room”: Admiral Robert Peary.

Chapter 28

  • “perhaps the Antarctic Queen Maud Mountains”: The Queen Maud Mountains are a major group of mountains in the Antarctic. Roald Amundsen named them in 1911 for Queen Maud of Norway, on his way to the South Pole.

Chapter 29

  • “Did it come through the strings of the golden lyre of the stars”: The constellations Lyra, Sagittarius (the archer), Argo Navis, and Centaurus. The Small Magellanic Cloud is a dwarf galaxy, named for Magellan’s crew, who described it on the first voyage around the world (1519-22).

Chapter 30

  • “disguised as an Arab king”: There were female explorers in the Middle East: Gertrude Bell (a British explorer, archaeologist, and writer) and Freya Stark who was another British explorer and writer. Other explorers: Isabella Bird, Lady Anne Blunt, Lady Hester Stanhope. Isabelle Eberhardt (1877-1904) rode the Sahara dressed as an Arab man, and Lady Hester Stanhope lived among the Arabs in men’s dress.
  • “sailing in the six months’ darkness”: The polar night, when the sun does not rise for months near the poles.

Chapter 30 · Mountains and Deserts

  • Represent challenges, isolation, and spiritual journeys.

Chapter 31 · The Desert and the Alps

  • The Desert and the Alps symbolize harsh trials, isolation, and spiritual quests. Cousin Hannah’s travels through these landscapes symbolize her restless spirit and adventurous life.

Chapter 32

  • “colder than the Weddell Sea”: The Weddell Sea is part of the Southern Ocean off Antarctica. It is named for the sealer James Weddell, who sailed into it in 1823, and its pack ice crushed Shackleton’s Endurance in 1915.
  • “Had she come from Manitoba, Hudson’s Bay, Cape Reprieve, the Laurentian Shield”: The Canadian Far North.
  • “Had she found the Northwest Passage”: Northwest Passage is the sea route between the Atlantic and Pacific oceans through the Arctic Ocean. Sir John Franklin’s expedition was lost seeking it in 1845, and Roald Amundsen first sailed through it in the Gjøa, 1903-1906.

Chapter 33

  • “great balloon ascensionists who long ago had descended to earth, dragging their shrouds and cords”: Salomon Andrée set out by balloon for the North Pole in 1897 and vanished. The bodies and diaries of his party were found on White Island in 1930.
  • “a great many formal king penguins, a great many melancholy seals”: King penguins live only in the far south, and seals in both polar seas, so “the bipolar illusion” means of both poles.

Chapter 35

  • “why so many great Antarctic explorers had left home”: Richard E. Byrd named Marie Byrd Land in Antarctica for his wife, Marie, in 1929.
  • “even like Queen Maud Mountains”: Roald Amundsen named the range for Queen Maud of Norway in November 1911, as his party climbed the Axel Heiberg Glacier toward the South Pole.

Chapter 40

  • Eskimo is Young’s word. Many Inuit and Yupik people now reject it.
  • They are named for Ferdinand Magellan, whose crew recorded them on the first circumnavigation of the world (1519-1522).
  • “Christolpho Colombo”: Christolpho Colombo or Christopher Columbus was an Italian explorer and navigator who completed four voyages across the Atlantic Ocean.
  • “balloonists who had not been sighted for years”: S. A. Andrée’s balloon expedition to the North Pole vanished in 1897. The men’s last camp was found on White Island in 1930.

Chapter 41

  • “like Balboa discovering the Pacific Ocean”: Balboa may refer to Vasco Núñez de Balboa, the Spanish explorer who crossed the Isthmus of Panama and in 1513 became the first European to see the Pacific from the Americas.

Chapter 42

  • Her captain “frozen in the snow… while writing his last log” recalls the legend of the Octavius.
  • “two ragged mountain climbers climbing Mt. Everest”: George Mallory and Andrew Irvine were last seen climbing toward the summit of Everest on June 8, 1924, and vanished. Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay reached the top in 1953.

Chapter 44

  • “mountains of the moon”: Ptolemy placed the Mountains of the Moon at the source of the Nile.

Chapter 45

  • “some distant roof- top of the world”: The Pamirs and the Tibetan plateau are called the Roof of the World.
  • “the magnetic North”: James Clark Ross reached the North Magnetic Pole in 1831. It wanders, and has moved hundreds of miles since.

Chapter 48

  • “like Stanley, find his Livingston”: Sir Henry Morton Stanley was a Welsh-American journalist and explorer of Central Africa. Sent by the New York Herald to find the missionary David Livingstone, he found him at Ujiji on Lake Tanganyika in November 1871 and reported greeting him, “Dr. Livingstone, I presume?” (How I Found Livingstone, 1872). The pages of Stanley’s diary for that meeting were torn out, and his biographer Tim Jeal doubts the words were said (Stanley: The Impossible Life of Africa’s Greatest Explorer, 2007). David Livingstone was an explorer of Africa and one of the most popular British heroes of the Victorian Era.
  • “holes in ice swallowing the sled”: Sir John Franklin’s expedition of 1845 in search of the Northwest Passage was lost when its ships, Erebus and Terror, were locked in the ice; all 129 men died.

Chapter 50

  • “perhaps the poles themselves had flown”: A geographic pole is either of the two points where Earth’s axis of rotation meets its surface, the North Pole in the Arctic Ocean and the South Pole in Antarctica. The poles wander by a few meters over a few years, partly from the Chandler wobble, a free oscillation with a period of about 433 days, but for maps they are taken as fixed, the points where the lines of longitude meet. See the reversals of the magnetic poles in the Chapter 42 resources. (Wikipedia)

Chapter 52

  • “She had slept among Laplanders”: “Laplander” is an outdated and sometimes considered offensive term for the Sami people, an indigenous group inhabiting the Arctic region of Sápmi, which includes parts of Norway, Sweden, Finland, and Russia. While “Laplander” was historically used to refer to people from the region of Lapland, it’s now more respectful to use the term Sami or the endonym their specific language group uses, such as Northern Sámi.

Chapter 54

  • “whether it was the North Pole or the South Pole”: The North Pole and the South Pole are the northernmost and southernmost points of the Earth.
  • “Captain Cook and Admiral Perry”: Captain Cook was a British captain in the British Royal Navy famous for three voyages between 1768 and 1779 in the Pacific Ocean and to Australia. Admiral Perry is probably Robert E. Peary, the Arctic explorer who claimed to have reached the North Pole in 1909 and was made a rear admiral in 1911; Commodore Matthew Perry, who opened Japan to American ships in 1854, never sailed to the ends of the earth.
  • “all four points were North upon this compass”: At the South Pole every direction is north.

Chapter 55

  • “the long streamers of Aurora Borealis”: See the northern and southern lights in the Chapter 54 resources.

Chapter 59

  • “that land where no explorer had ever set his flag”: Robert Peary claimed the North Pole in 1909, a claim still disputed, and Roald Amundsen’s party planted the Norwegian flag at the South Pole on December 14, 1911.

Chapter 61

  • “the great Arctic birds who round this globe each year, going from pole to pole”: The Arctic tern flies each year from its Arctic nesting grounds to the Antarctic and back, the longest migration of any bird.

Chapter 64

  • “that flowers once grew at the North Pole”: Fossil forests and the pollen of palms show that the Arctic was warm and green about fifty million years ago, and frozen mammoths, the elephants of the Ice Age, have been dug out of the Siberian permafrost.

Chapter 65

  • “that there is a great hole at the North Pole”: In 1818 John Cleves Symmes Jr. announced that the earth is hollow and open at the poles. In the 1880s Cyrus Teed’s Koreshan Unity in Chicago taught the reverse, that we live on the inside of a hollow earth.

Chapter 66

  • “Who was Captain Cook?”: Miss MacIntosh’s “Captain Cook” is Dr. Frederick A. Cook (1865-1940), the American who claimed to have reached the North Pole in April 1908, a year before Peary. The University of Copenhagen rejected his records in 1909. In 1923 he was convicted of mail fraud for promoting oil stocks in Texas, served his sentence at Leavenworth until his parole in 1930, and was pardoned by President Franklin Roosevelt in 1940. Young merges him with Captain James Cook, the British navigator.
  • “it was Captain Cook and not Commander Peary”: Commander Peary or Robert Peary was an American explorer and officer in the United States Navy who made several expeditions to the Arctic in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. He is best known for, in April 1909, leading an expedition that claimed to be the first to have reached the geographic North Pole. Explorer Matthew Henson, part of the expedition, is thought to have reached what they believed to be the North Pole narrowly before Peary. (Wikipedia) Peary’s backers were the Peary Arctic Club of wealthy New Yorkers, and the National Geographic Society endorsed his claim.
  • “Where is the Bering Sea?”: The Bering Sea and Strait are named for Vitus Bering (1681-1741), a Dane in Russian service who sailed them in 1728 and died on what is now Bering Island in 1741. The explorer of this chapter is a Dane too.
  • “this explorer who was wooden-legged”: Compare Peter Freuchen (1886-1957), the Danish Arctic explorer who lost his left foot to frostbite in 1926, settled in New York, and won The $64,000 Question in 1956.
  • “they would both have done better as fiction writers”: Neither claim is accepted without doubt today. Cook’s records were rejected in 1909, and Peary’s own navigation and speeds have been questioned since.
  • “Far above King William Land, at the Magnetic Pole”: King William Land, also called King William Island, is an island in the Kitikmeot Region of Nunavut, which is part of the Arctic Archipelago. (Wikipedia) Franklin’s lost expedition perished near King William Island in 1845-1848, and James Clark Ross first reached the North Magnetic Pole nearby, on the Boothia Peninsula, in 1831.
  • “the nights were six months long where they would go”: At the North Pole itself the sun sets near the September equinox and does not rise again until the March equinox, so the night there is about six months long.

  • “He was a Christopher Columbus in the world of the dead”: Christopher Columbus was an Italian explorer and navigator who completed four Spanish-based voyages across the Atlantic Ocean sponsored by the Catholic Monarchs of Spain, opening the way for the widespread European exploration and colonization of the Americas. His expeditions were the first known European contact with the Caribbean, Central America, and South America.
