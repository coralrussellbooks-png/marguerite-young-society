Chapter 11

  • “the proud, cold, utterly snobbish Queen Maud Mountains”: The Queen Maud Mountains rise at the head of the Ross Ice Shelf, on the south edge of the Ross Sea. Roald Amundsen found them in 1911 on his way to the South Pole and named them for Queen Maud of Norway, a daughter of King Edward VII of England. Queen Maud Land is a different region of Antarctica, claimed by Norway.
  • The Ross Sea, a deep bay of the Southern Ocean, is named for James Clark Ross, who sailed into it in 1841.
  • “Sir Walter Raleigh in his marvelous white satin suit embroidered with seed pearls”: Sir Walter Raleigh (c. 1552-1618) was an English statesman, soldier, spy, writer, poet, and explorer. He was famous at Elizabeth’s court for the pearls and jewels on his clothes, even his shoes.
  • “Admiral Peary”: Robert E. Peary claimed the North Pole in 1909 and was made a rear admiral in 1911. Matthew Henson, the Black explorer beside him, by his own account reached the spot first and got little credit for decades. In 1897 Peary brought six Inuit from Greenland to New York for the American Museum of Natural History, and four died within months. The museum staged a fake burial for the boy Minik while it kept his father Qisuk’s skeleton.

Chapter 50

  • A geographical pole or geographic pole is either of the two points on Earth where its axis of rotation intersects its surface. The North Pole lies in the Arctic Ocean while the South Pole is in Antarctica. Relative to Earth's surface, the geographic poles move by a few meters over periods of a few years. This is due to several factors like the Chandler wobble, a free oscillation with a period of about 433 days. As mapping requires exact and unchanging coordinates, the locations of geographical poles are taken as fixed and become the points where the body's great circles of longitude intersect.

Chapter 54

  • “Indian maiden Pocahontas” Pocahontas also known as Matoaka and Rebecca Rolfe was a Native American woman belonging to the Powhatan people, notable for her association with the colonial settlement at Jamestown, Virginia. She was the daughter of Wahunsenacawh, the paramount chief of a network of tributary tribes in the Tsenacommacah (known in English as the Powhatan Confederacy), encompassing the Tidewater region of what is today the U.S. state of Virginia. Pocahontas was captured and held for ransom by English colonists during hostilities in 1613. During her captivity, she was encouraged to convert to Christianity and was baptized under the name Rebecca. She married the tobacco planter John Rolfe in April 1614 at the age of about 17 or 18, and she bore their son, Thomas Rolfe, in January 1615. In 1616, the Rolfes travelled to London, where Pocahontas was presented to English society as an example of the "civilized savage" in hopes of stimulating investment in Jamestown. On this trip, she may have met Squanto, a Patuxet man from New England. Pocahontas became a celebrity, was elegantly fêted, and attended a masque at Whitehall Palace. In 1617, the Rolfes intended to sail for Virginia, but Pocahontas died at Gravesend, Kent, England, of unknown causes, aged 20 or 21. She was buried in St George's Church, Gravesend; her grave's exact location is unknown because the church was rebuilt after being destroyed by a fire. Numerous places, landmarks, and products in the United States have been named after Pocahontas. Her story has been romanticized over the years, many aspects of which are fictional. Many of the stories told about her by the English explorer John Smith have been contested by her documented descendants. She is a subject of art, literature, and film. Many famous people have claimed to be among her descendants, including members of the First Families of Virginia, First Lady Edith Wilson, American actor Glenn Strange, and astronomer Percival Lowell.

Chapter 55

  • “Penguin Colonization” refers to the process where penguins establish new breeding populations in areas where they haven't previously lived, often driven by environmental factors like ice retreat or the availability of suitable breeding sites and food sources. Historical colonization events, such as the post-glacial expansion of Adélie penguins in Antarctica, are documented through geological records. More recent examples include the successful colonization of artificial breakwaters by Humboldt penguins and ongoing efforts to establish new African penguin colonies to connect fragmented populations. Penguin movements, including the relocation of Emperor penguin colonies due to changing sea ice, are also monitored by scientists using satellite imagery.

  • The questions Miss MacIntosh poses about monsoons (a rainy period in South and Southeast Asia between May and September), mackerel skies (a sky filled with rows of high-altitude cirrocumulus or mid-level altocumulus clouds that form a rippling, wavy pattern, resembling the scales of a mackerel fish. This formation indicates moisture and atmospheric waves, often signaling approaching weather changes, leading to the old saying, "Mackerel sky, not twenty-four hours dry"), the Bering Strait (is a narrow waterway separating Asia (Russia) from North America (Alaska), connecting the Pacific Ocean's Bering Sea to the Arctic Ocean, famous as the site of the Ice Age land bridge (Beringia) for human migration and currently a vital wildlife corridor with a US-Russia boundary, known for cold, stormy waters and the time-bending Diomede Islands), and Hyperborean lights (refers to the poetic or mythical descriptions of the sun and continuous daylight in the legendary land of Hyperborea, a paradisiacal realm in Greek mythology) are metaphorical and educational, suggesting a vast, perhaps overwhelming world of knowledge and challenges that must be faced. It emphasizes Miss MacIntosh’s role as a stern moral guide who insists on facing life's harsh realities head-on, rejecting illusions or dreams. This passage reflects Miss MacIntosh’s pragmatic, somewhat severe worldview, emphasizing survival, knowledge, and preparation in a harsh, unforgiving world.

  • Socialism is a left-wing political, social, and economic philosophy encompassing a range of economic and social systems characterized by social ownership of the means of production, as opposed to private ownership. Vera recalls Miss MacIntosh lecturing on socialism while walking in the surf with her skirts pinned up and her black umbrella billowing above her head. This image highlights the unconventional and almost theatrical way Miss MacIntosh approached the subject. Miss MacIntosh expresses disdain for the unequal distribution of wealth, stating that in New York City, "The rich threw dollar bills out of windows, and the poor did not catch the money". She hopes for a reformed world without rich people by the time Vera grows up. Despite her disapproval of wealth, Miss MacIntosh visits the Stock Exchange, which she calls "a palace of evil". She claims she goes there to level the rich so they should all be poor. Miss MacIntosh teaches Vera "the beauty of deprivation". This suggests a belief in the value of simplicity and a rejection of materialism. Miss MacIntosh's socialist views are also connected to her own experiences of loneliness and isolation. She mentions her desire to improve the human race and her search for "simple humanity". Miss MacIntosh's character is full of contradictions. She visits the Stock Exchange to make imaginary money but saves real money for her old age. She also mentions a past relationship with an Arctic explorer, implying a desire for adventure and escape from societal norms. The Arctic explorer, whom she met at the Stock Exchange, echoes some of Miss MacIntosh's criticisms of society, viewing it as empty and artificial. He suggests that she abandon her worldly goods and join him on an everlasting trek to the North Pole.

Chapter 66

  • “Wilderness Scout” in the U.S. wasn't a formal program but emerged from early youth groups like Ernest Thompson Seton's Woodcraft Indians (1902) and Daniel Carter Beard's Sons of Daniel Boone (1905), which emphasized nature skills and American Indian lore, eventually merging into the Boy Scouts of America (BSA), which itself developed older youth programs like Senior Scouts and Explorers (1935) for advanced wilderness camping and challenging outdoor pursuits, exemplified by trips to Philmont and Canadian wilderness canoe expeditions. Wilderness scouts, frontiersmen, and explorers frequently wore fringed garments, like the "hunting frock" or buckskin jackets, because the fringe served practical purposes, shedding rain and helping the leather dry faster, in addition to being a cultural style adopted from or shared with Native Americans, making it ideal for frontier life.

Chapter 66

  • “Ah, my dear, said he, they would both have done better as fiction writers.” Polynesian voyagers, who predate the Maori people, may have reached Antarctica as early as the 7th century, long before Europeans like Cook and Peary. This evidence includes oral histories, legends like that of Hui Te Rangiora, and archaeological findings on subantarctic islands. Maori oral traditions speak of Polynesian explorers like Hui Te Rangiora, who sailed to the southern ocean in the 7th century and encountered areas of ice and a "dark, misty, foggy place". Archaeological evidence, such as artifacts from ancient ovens and middens found on subantarctic islands, suggests Polynesian presence in waters further south than previously understood. The study argues that these oral histories and traditional records, often overlooked by Western scholars, provide evidence that Polynesians were the first humans to see Antarctica. European exploration of Antarctica was not confirmed until the 19th century, with Cook and Peary's expeditions occurring much later than the proposed Maori voyages.

Chapter 66

  • "He just knew that they had something in common—their differences from others—and their amputations and their sense of having killed something young and helpless" This passage suggests that both Miss MacIntosh and the explorer share a profound sense of isolation and loss that sets them apart from ordinary people. Their "amputations" are both literal and metaphorical: Miss MacIntosh physically has an amputated breast and is bald, while the explorer has a wooden leg, symbolizing bodily loss. But more importantly, these amputations symbolize emotional or existential wounds, each carries a deep internal absence or sacrifice. The phrase "their sense of having killed something young and helpless" reflects a psychological or spiritual burden they both bear, perhaps guilt or sorrow over lost innocence, lost potential, or something vulnerable within themselves or their past. This shared feeling of having caused or endured damage to something fragile adds to their alienation and mutual understanding.
