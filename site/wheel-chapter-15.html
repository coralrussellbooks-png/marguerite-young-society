Chapter 15

On Chapter 15 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of The Wheel and the Spokes.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 14 · Wheel and Spokes Home · Chapter 16 →

Research notes: Chapter 15 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 15

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The narrator is not one person in this chapter. She is simultaneously a child afraid of mustard poultices, a philosophical consciousness grappling with the immensity of existence, a body trembling in cold water, and a mind recursively examining its own examining.

"I was filled with an almost unbearable excitement as I realized the immensity of life, that which, through its necessary imperfections, might weave a higher perfection than the faultless and restricted days such as I had known."

The self here is not unified toward a conclusion. It multiplies outward. She laughs aloud while philosophizing. She thinks of mustard poultices while standing in what feels like a cosmic moment. The sublime and the domestic do not resolve into each other; they coexist without hierarchy. This is not a narrator who has achieved integration. This is a narrator who is the unintegrated multiplicity.

The false wedding dream she recalls, "Mr. Spitzer was married to the bridegroom", introduces another self entirely, a dreaming self whose logic operates independently of the waking philosophical self. The plural self is not a metaphor here. It is structural to the narration.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The entire philosophical passage at the chapter's opening is a meditation on this pillar:

"What if everything should be false and nothing true... Then all false things should be true, I thought, as true as Miss MacIntosh who was so very truthful."

This is not nihilism. It is something more precise: the recognition that the category of "true" is itself constructed, and that Miss MacIntosh's famous truthfulness is itself a kind of construction, a performance of the real rather than access to it. The narrator does not conclude that nothing matters. She concludes that the distinction between true and false is itself unstable, and that this instability is generative rather than catastrophic.

Her mother's condition confirms this:

"my false mother who postulated merely as her theory the outer world, the blowing cherry trees beyond the surf line, the lanes where she had never walked."

The mother's world is entirely constructed, but so, the narrator begins to suspect, is everyone's. The mother is not the exception. She is the evidence made visible.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Grief is not named directly in this chapter, but it is the atmosphere in which everything occurs. The landscape itself is grief:

"the long, melancholy roarings of black waters under the near sky where, in the partings of the curtain of streaked fog, the bloodless moon was like a white, thin skull drifting without purpose over the many roofs, the dark towers, the abandoned golf course."

Note the accumulation: abandoned golf course, dark towers, bloodless moon. Nothing is neutral. Everything is already elegiac. The birthday, which should be celebratory, is instead a night of reckoning. There is no resolution offered. The chapter does not end with the narrator warmed and understood. It ends with a blind sheep dog barking at waves.

Miss MacIntosh's aphorisms about death, delivered at breakfast on the narrator's birthday, are themselves a form of grief as permanent weather:

"Death, she had said only this morning at breakfast in the grey light of my fourteenth birthday, is always the bridegroom."

This is not a dramatic announcement. It is breakfast conversation. Death has been fully domesticated into the daily climate. It does not arrive. It was always already there.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator's primary act in this chapter is sustained, unflinching attention. She does not look away from the sea, from Miss MacIntosh's contradictions, from her mother's condition, from the dead seagull:

"A dead seagull, wings folded, lay cold upon the narrow shelf of rock above the flood."

This detail is not explained or moralized. It is simply seen. Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act pillar is perhaps most purely expressed in the chapter's extended descriptions of the sea's light:

"colors so intense lighting the dark sky that they seemed outside the mortal spectrum. For one moment, it seemed that the moving waters stood still, and I could hear a sound as of harpers harping on those seas of glass which man shall behold at the end of time."

She does not rush to conclude what this means. She stays in it. She witnesses the moment of apparent transcendence and then watches it dissolve back into "rolling blackness" without forcing it to mean more than it was. The refusal to simplify is itself the ethical act.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The narrator is explicitly constructing meaning on her birthday, and she knows it. The night is framed as revelation:

"night which should be greater revelation than the sunlight which conceals so much."

But the revelation is not received. It is built, recursively, from the materials available: Miss MacIntosh's proverbs, her mother's theories, the sea's behavior, the memory of the false wedding. The narrator returns again and again to Miss MacIntosh: examining her, revising her assessment, building an understanding, then undercutting it:

"Miss MacIntosh knew less than I did, hers being, as I now thought, a deadness, a dullness, a coldness of heart."

But this conclusion is itself provisional. The narrator has not finished with Miss MacIntosh. The novel has not finished with her. The building continues. The assessment is not the final word.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Miss MacIntosh's aphorisms are the novel's great counter-example to this pillar. They are completed statements: proverbs, maxims, moral conclusions:

"a straight line cannot be made straighter and is the shortest distance between two points."

"eggs and oaths were soonest broken."

"early to bed and early to rise."

These are language that has concluded. And the narrator's entire philosophical project in this chapter is a resistance to that conclusion. She does not offer counter-aphorisms. She offers the ongoing sentence: the long, subordinate-clause-laden, recursive, self-interrupting prose that enacts incompletion as a formal principle. The ethics of incompletion is not argued. It is performed in the syntax itself.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Every significant figure in this chapter is marginal in some structural sense. The narrator's mother has refused the outer world entirely. Miss MacIntosh is:

"single and single-minded, no man looking at her a second time or perhaps even a first, her life being almost over and the battle perhaps already lost."

Mr. Spitzer appears only in a dream of a false wedding with no legitimate guests: only "the northern light beams, the stars, and droves of seals on a solitary shore in a crystal void where no hunter ever comes."

These are not peripheral characters who illuminate the central norm. There is no central norm. The marginal figures are the cast. Their marginality is not their problem. It is the novel's evidence that the categories of normal, central, and legitimate are themselves constructions. The narrator, standing alone in the ocean at night on her fourteenth birthday, is herself the marginal figure, and she is where all the meaning is happening.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This pillar is expressed with extraordinary literalness in the passage about the sea sweeping through the garden:

"Once, the marble Saul had been swept out to sea in the departure of a musical tide, but a strange marble head had been swept in and was found resting in that place. Saul had never returned."

The past does not recede. It is deposited and replaced and deposited again. The garden contains "the prow of an old ship, an empty coffin, and many playing cards": wreckage from other times, other stories, pressing up through the present landscape.

Miss MacIntosh's aphorisms are themselves sedimentary: Benjamin Franklin, John Bunyan, Ecclesiastes, Exodus, the Farmer's Almanac. They are the accumulated deposits of centuries of moral instruction, still pressing up through the breakfast table conversation on a grey birthday morning. The narrator cannot escape them. They are beneath her, constitutive of the ground she stands on.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Miss MacIntosh's language is the chapter's primary exhibit of language that pretends to speak from nowhere:

"He is bare whom virtue hath not clothed... and one swallow does not make a summer, and a thin meadow is soon mowed, and winter is summer's heir, and the night is no man's friend."

This is language performing objectivity, performing the universal, performing timeless truth. The narrator's own language is its opposite; it names its own location constantly. She speaks from this night, this birthday, this cold body, this specific confusion. Her prose does not claim the view from nowhere. It claims the view from a fourteen-year-old standing in black water in August, teeth chattering, hair wet with salt.

The contrast between these two languages is itself a philosophical argument about the construction of authority.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The chapter's most explicit transcendent moment is deliberately returned to the immanent:

"For one moment, it seemed that the moving waters stood still, and I could hear a sound as of harpers harping on those seas of glass which man shall behold at the end of time when the heavens are parted, when the sky rolls back like a scroll. The sea turned to rolling blackness again."

The apocalyptic imagery (harpers on seas of glass, the sky rolling back) is invoked and then immediately grounded. The sacred does not arrive from outside. It flickers within the ordinary sea, within the mortal spectrum, and then returns to blackness. This is not a failure of transcendence. This is transcendence correctly located: inside the immanent, not beyond it.

The dead seagull, the phosphorescent body, the jellyfish like ice on the beach; these are the sacred objects of this chapter. Not symbols pointing elsewhere. Presences fully themselves.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is perhaps the most structurally embedded pillar in the chapter. The narrator stands at the crossing point of multiple temporalities simultaneously:

  • She is fourteen, at the threshold of adulthood
  • She is constituted by Miss MacIntosh's centuries of accumulated moral sediment
  • She is constituted by her grandmother, whose dressing table and mirrors still occupy Miss MacIntosh's room
  • She is constituted by her mother's refusal of the outer world
  • She is already constituting the future; this very night, this very philosophical crisis, will become what she carries forward

"my grandmother's mahogany dressing table with its three stained mirrors reaching to the ceiling and dusty powder dishes and tarnished silver brushes and combs which had been hers when, in her blindness, she had used to stand before her crystal image thrice reflected."

The grandmother is in the sediment. She has never left. The mirrors still reflect, though she is gone. The narrator is not moving through time toward a destination. She is the place where her grandmother's blindness, her mother's theories, Miss MacIntosh's proverbs, and her own fourteenth birthday all cross simultaneously.

"Our cradle stands in the grave."

Miss MacIntosh says this as a proverb. The narrator lives it as a structural condition.
