James the Chauffeur: The Life Made From an Absence

← Doctor Justice O'Leary · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · The Black Coachman →

Characters: Jacqueline White · Madge Capehorn · Mrs. Hogden · Vera Cartwheel

James is the servant who is not there. He is the mother's chauffeur, and by the time the novel introduces him he has been gone for years, having quit without notice and driven off with her car. She keeps him anyway. When she loses patience and dismisses the whole staff, the real maids and cooks and valets, the one servant she refuses to let go is the one who already left. "The real would go with the imaginary," and she chooses the imaginary. James is the clearest case in the book of a person kept alive entirely by the mind that needs him.

The line that holds him together is short: "That James was absent made him always present." A real chauffeur can quit. An imagined one cannot, because he has already done the only leaving he will ever do, and now he is fixed at the wheel forever, exactly where she wants him. He never turns around. He never speaks. She rides behind him for years giving orders into an imaginary horn, and the whole time he answers nothing, until her commands curdle into a kind of prayer, "James, why have you not answered me through all these years?" He is pure function, a driver with no face left, carrying her on a journey that has no arrival.

The journey is to Alaska, and they never reach it. "Home, James," she says, and then asks where home is, because there is no home and no turning, only the same barber pole and the same bridge crossed again and again in a frozen city. She tells him to run the red lights and stop at the green ones, the logic of someone who has stopped believing arrival is possible and drives on anyway. This is the mother's whole condition rendered as a car ride: a woman who cannot get out, steered by a man who is not there, toward a place that recedes as fast as they approach it.

What makes James more than a symptom is that she builds him a life, and keeps building. She gives him a history, an ex-convict falsely accused and unjustly sentenced, a man who broke rocks on a state road and dreams of open country and can drop a seagull on the wing. Then she gives him the most extraordinary detail of all. He is planning to kill her. When they reach Alaska he means to shoot her where no one will hear, and to shoot her dead father first for good measure. She knows this, rides with it, and cannot call for help. She has invented not just a companion but her own assassin, installed him at the wheel, and cannot look away from him or make herself get out of the car. The witnessing this book asks for, the refusal to flinch from what is actually in the room, turns literal here. The thing in the room is the driver she made to kill her, and she stays.

He has no fixed self, which is the other thing that marks him as wholly built. For the mother he is at once the killer at the wheel and a servant of impossible courtesy, the one who, when her pearl string breaks and twelve pearls scatter, returns thirteen, and the more she loses the more he brings back, "infinities of pearls." When Mrs. Hogden takes over the house and starts giving James orders of her own, he changes character on command, becoming "the soul of honor," and the novel shrugs that "he deviated from his true character" but calls it "an unimportant difference." He is whatever the dreamer requires. He is a terror and a saint in the same week, because there is no man underneath to contradict either version. He is one of the imaginary servants "in no position to question her reality."

He does not even get to be the center of the delusion he anchors. After all the mileage and the murder plot and the pearls, the book sets him aside in a single sentence: "It had not been her chauffeur upon whom her life depended, however." That turned out to be the canary. James, so carefully built, is demoted in a breath, and later accounts cannot even keep his story straight, so that the man who never reaches Alaska is also, elsewhere, said to be driving a taxi in Nome, having finally gone north to shoot his moose. Both are held at once. A made man does not have to be consistent.

The reason James matters past his own chapters is that he shows where Vera learned her trade. The daughter builds Madge out of a sleeping face, and Madge builds Jacqueline out of a cried name, but before either of them the mother lay in her bed and built James out of an absence, an ex-employee who robbed her turned into the most faithful companion of her life. The novel keeps making the same claim through different mouths, that the figures a mind cannot stop seeing are the ones it makes, and that the making is not a failure of contact but the form contact takes. James is the household version of the whole book. A real chauffeur will drive the mother's coffin to the grave, anonymous, barely described, a clam-digger at the wheel of a rickety Packard. The invented one got the biography, the injustice, the loaded gun, the infinities of pearls. In this novel that is the ordinary order of things. The made thing outlives and outshines the real, because the made thing is the one someone needed.

The turn for a writer is the freeing one. Invention runs freest exactly where there is nothing to check it against, and James is built on the emptiest, and so the most open, ground in the house, a man who had already left. An absence cannot talk back. A real chauffeur could be given almost nothing, because a real chauffeur would quit and prove the dreamer wrong, but the one who was already gone could be handed a rap sheet, a wronged past, a loaded gun, a plan to shoot her dead in Alaska, and "infinities of pearls," and none of it could be refuted, because there was no man left to do the refuting. That is where a writer builds most lavishly, on the blank a person leaves behind. And James teaches the harder second thing, that detail is not the same as weight. He gets the fullest biography of any invented figure in the book and is then set aside in a single line, "It had not been her chauffeur upon whom her life depended, however." A writer can pour a whole life into a figure and find the load resting somewhere unremarked, on a canary in a cage, and the danger is in mistaking the sheer amount of invention for its importance. The made thing does not even have to hold still to be real. James drives north and also, elsewhere, drives a taxi in Nome having finally gone to shoot his moose, and the book keeps both at once, because a figure a mind cannot stop seeing lives by being needed, not by being consistent.
