Summary

Vera takes a last long look at Miss MacIntosh asleep: the scar, the missing eyebrows and lashes, a green ribbon looped over a bust of Martin Luther. She falls back asleep. When she wakes, Miss MacIntosh stands fully dressed, her red wig shining, in unusually good humor, and the room is tidy as if nothing happened. She says Vera has been sick and scolds her for dancing without a partner. She holds up the birthday dress Mr. Spitzer sent, declares it cannot be mended, and sends it to the ash bin.

Key passages

From the research

  • This opening passage is crucial because it establishes Vera's conscious perception of their companion (later identified as Miss MacIntosh) in her usual, seemingly strong and formidable state. The language ("shining armor," "scaled vizor," "rugged individualist," "triumphant") creates an image of someone powerful, decisive, and perhaps intimidating, contrasting sharply with the vulnerability revealed later. It sets up the central theme of appearance versus reality and the layers of disguise or perception that Vera navigates. The detail about the evening walk and the old dog grounds this potentially abstract description in a concrete, if slightly surreal, daily ritual.
  • The physical reality of the companion's baldness is explicitly revealed to Vera upon waking is a pivotal moment. Vera contrasts this "palpable disguise of the average man" (or perhaps, woman) with a sense of hidden "dead and vanished glory." The image of the "great baldness as of the moon sunken" is striking and suggests something vast, desolate, and perhaps beyond reach. Crucially, Vera interprets this physical revelation not as a simple lack, but as something that reveals a deeper truth about reality: that strength is intertwined with fragility, illusion, and disorder. This passage shifts the narrative from external observation to deep internal reflection on the nature of identity, love, and perception.
  • Vera's complex motivation is articulated after witnessing Miss MacIntosh's vulnerability. The "good intention" is to maintain a facade of normalcy for her sake, even if it requires Vera to "deceive her" about their own profound realization. Vera seems to want to protect her from the implications of the "glory" or "chaos" they perceived, wishing instead for her to see herself as ordinary, stable, and authentic. This reveals Vera's protective instinct but also highlights the inherent tension in their relationship and the burden of Vera's newfound knowledge. It introduces the theme of deliberate self-deception or mutual deception as a way to cope with difficult truths.
  • While seemingly a digression, it reinforces the chapter's themes of appearance vs. reality, disguise, and the difficulty of discerning truth. The companion's sayings about the Devil emphasize that evil can be deceptive, appearing in attractive forms ("charming," "handsome," "fine dancer," "God's ape"). This mirrors Vera's experience of seeing Miss MacIntosh's seemingly strong exterior peeled away to reveal a hidden reality, suggesting that what appears "normal" or even "good" might conceal something else entirely. It adds a layer of theological or philosophical depth to the personal revelation, implying that the struggle to understand her is part of a larger human struggle to understand complex, hidden realities. It also gives voice to Miss MacIntosh's own worldview.
  • This extended description is incredibly powerful and visceral. It moves beyond the initial shock of baldness to reveal a body marked by age, illness, and labor (the missing breast, scar, gnarled hands/feet). The imagery is both starkly realistic ("mouth open and toothless") and metaphoric ("Alpine frozen slopes," "wintry field," "polished marble," "barnacles"). The repetition of "pity" underscores Vera's overwhelming emotional response. The description emphasizes vulnerability, loss, and a stripping away ("denudement") of layers both physical and perhaps metaphysical. The comparison of this reality to something "less substantial than the wildest, saddest dream" suggests a profound disorientation for Vera, blurring the lines between waking life and nightmare, and making the revealed reality seem almost unbelievable despite its physical presence.
  • After the intense, detailed description of the vulnerable, sleeping figure, Miss MacIntosh is abruptly presented as the familiar, formidable "Miss MacIntosh," fully dressed and appearing exactly as she did the previous evening, complete with her shining red hair. Vera notes their lack of astonishment initially, highlighting how quickly the mind can revert to familiar perceptions, even after a profound revelation. However, Vera is astonished to find themselves in Miss MacIntosh's bed, introducing a new element of mystery and suggesting that the morning's events (or at least Vera's being there to witness them) are out of the ordinary. This passage reinstates the "disguise" or preferred appearance and raises questions about which reality is dominant or "true" – the vulnerable sleeping figure or the robust, familiar Miss MacIntosh.
  • This dialogue shifts the focus away from Miss MacIntosh's physical state to Vera's past and apparent recent illness or misbehavior. Miss MacIntosh's tone is parental ("sick and unusually stubborn," "mild reproach"), emphasizing their caregiver-dependent relationship. The mention of the "first birthday party" and the torn dress introduces a specific past event, possibly symbolic of a loss of innocence or a failed attempt at celebration. Most significantly, the passage presents a clear discrepancy: Vera sees no tear in the dress, but Miss MacIntosh insists it is irreparably damaged and must be discarded. This highlights a potential unreliability in the narrative, either on the part of Miss MacIntosh (perhaps deliberately misrepresenting the dress's condition) or Vera (perhaps unable to see the damage, literal or symbolic). The torn dress becomes a symbol of something broken or lost that cannot be fixed, but the disagreement about its state complicates its meaning and adds to the chapter's layers of uncertain reality. Miss MacIntosh's repetition of the "road to Hell" saying connects this conversation back to the earlier philosophical digression, grounding it in her practical, somewhat fatalistic worldview.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Vera Cartwheel

  • She is introspective, empathetic, and deeply affected by Miss MacIntosh’s state.
  • She wrestles with her feelings of pity and the desire to shield Miss MacIntosh from harsh truths.
  • She reflects on love, the nature of identity, and the meaning of suffering, indicating a philosophical and emotional depth.

Miss MacIntosh

  • Portrayed as a strong yet vulnerable figure, marked physically by baldness, scars, and loss, which symbolize deeper suffering.
  • Despite her condition, she maintains a robust and kind demeanor, showing resilience. She is depicted with vivid details like her red hair, green ribbon, and practical attire.
  • She embodies the tension between appearance and inner reality, strength and vulnerability.

Research: Themes

Love as Eternal and Unchanging

See: Death and Mortality · Love

  • Vera emphasizes that true love remains constant despite changing circumstances. Love "does not ebb, does not wane," symbolizing an ideal of permanence in an impermanent world.
  • This eternal love contrasts with the physical and emotional struggles depicted, underscoring a spiritual or emotional steadfastness.

Appearance vs. Reality

See: Illusion and Reality

  • There is a tension between outward appearances and inner realities. Vera observes Miss MacIntosh’s bald head, a stark and raw image that strips away pretense and illusion.
  • The "palpable disguise of the average man" and the "inarticulate mediocrity" hint at the masks people wear to hide deeper truths.
  • Vera’s intention to let Miss MacIntosh believe she is "a usual being" shows a desire to protect her from harsh truths.

Suffering and Pity

  • The chapter conveys deep pity for Miss MacIntosh’s condition, symbolized by her baldness and the physical aftermath of illness or trauma (e.g., the missing breast, scar, and hairlessness).
  • The imagery of "valley of dry bones" and "the road to Hell... paved with bones and good intentions" suggests the harshness of life and the prevalence of suffering masked by well-meaning actions.

The Devil and Deception

See: Faith, God, and the Supernatural

  • The narrative includes reflections on evil’s many forms, warning against being deceived by charming appearances ("The Devil does not always wear horns").
  • The Devil is described metaphorically as "God’s ape," emphasizing the complexity of good and evil and the danger of superficial judgments.

Research: Resources

  • The King James Version of the Bible was not the first English language version of the Bible, but the culmination of extensive translation activity (some illegal!) in the 1500s. This began with the work of William Tyndale and the printing of the first English New Testament in 1526. Following a tumultuous 75 years, King James I came to power in 1603, unifying a divided England. Three main English Bibles were in use: the Bishops’ Bible (Church of England), the Geneva Bible (Protestants), and the Rheims New Testament (Roman Catholic Church), causing much confusion and dissension. To settle disagreements over reforms in the Church of England and respond to pressure from the Puritans, King James in 1604 approved a new translation of the Bible, primarily because he knew that it would reinforce his image as a political and spiritual leader. He appointed six committees, totaling 54 scholars, to prepare the new translation, using previous English Bible translation work, and using the best Hebrew, Greek, and Latin texts and manuscripts. The completed King James Version was first published in 1611. These first editions were large pulpit Bibles (folios) to be read in churches, but were followed within a year by the printing of smaller versions (quartos) for personal use. Though slow to be adopted in the English-speaking world and to replace the more popular Geneva Bible, from the mid-1600s to the late 20th century, the King James Version was the Bible of the English-speaking church. Though many recent modern translations have gained in popularity at the expense of the KJV, it remains the standard of measure for all new translations, and it still stands as the outstanding masterpiece of the English language.
  • The King James Only movement (also known as King James Onlyism or KJV Onlyism) asserts that the King James Version (KJV) of the Bible is superior to all other English translations of the Bible. Adherents of the movement, mostly certain Conservative Anabaptist, traditionalist Anglo-Catholic, Conservative Holiness Methodist and Independent Baptist churches, believe that this text has been providentially preserved as a perfect translation of the Bible into English. Followers of the movement assert that modern English Bible translations are corrupt, based on a distrust of the Alexandrian text-type or the critical texts of Nestle-Aland, and Westcott-Hort, sources for the majority of twentieth- and twenty-first-century translations. Instead, they prefer the Textus Receptus (which is mainly based on the Byzantine text-type, with some influences from other text-types). Some factions argue that the King James translation itself was divinely inspired. Others prefer the KJV simply because it is in the public domain in most countries (with the United Kingdom being a notable exception), which allows them to freely copy any amount of the translation without worrying about royalties or copyright.
  • Miss MacIntosh vividly expresses sexism through her character by highlighting her pragmatic, no-nonsense attitude toward the limitations imposed on women, her rejection of illusions, and her critical view of the societal roles prescribed to women. Miss MacIntosh's insistence on practicality and self-reliance reflects a critique of traditional sexism by underscoring how women are often expected to conform to narrow roles, suppressing their individuality and desires. Miss MacIntosh’s harshness can be read as internalized sexism, reflecting how women may police themselves and others to survive within a patriarchal system that offers them limited agency. This portrayal critiques the constraints sexism imposes on women’s lives, identities, and dreams by grounding Miss MacIntosh as a figure of practical wisdom and resilience who nonetheless embodies the burdens of gendered limitations. It sums up how Miss MacIntosh has internalized sexism where she takes sexist behaviors and attitudes about women and projects them onto herself and Vera in the form of fear, envy, jealousy, suspicion, resentment, rage, anxiety, lack of self-esteem and confidence. Miss MacIntosh’s greatest fear is that Vera will become and get to do everything society expects a ‘normal’ woman to be and do. Something Miss MacIntosh will never achieve.
