Chapter 21

Summary

Vera promises herself that tomorrow she will look only at the red hair “like shining armor” and go on as before: “Daylight should cover us.” Her “good intention” is to deceive Miss MacIntosh into believing herself “but a usual being in the light.” Yet she remembers Miss MacIntosh’s own warnings, that the road to Hell “is paved with bones and good intentions,” that the Devil “does not always wear horns” and can quote Scripture. Pity wakes in her: “It was only the beginning of pity. It was not the end.” Vera takes a last long look at the sleeping body, the scar, the stalk of the cut-away breast, the wad of cloth in the brassiere, the green ribbon looped over the bust of Martin Luther. Then she falls asleep. When she wakes, Miss MacIntosh stands at the foot of the bed, fully dressed, her red hair shining, in remarkably good humor. She says Vera has been very sick, scolds her for dancing at “your first birthday party, even though no guest came,” and holds up the party dress Mr. Spitzer gave her. Vera can see no tear in it. Miss MacIntosh declares it cannot be mended: “Into the ash bin it goes.”

Key passages

From the research

  • This opening passage is crucial because it establishes Vera’s conscious perception of Miss MacIntosh in her usual, seemingly strong and formidable state. The language (“shining armor,” “scaled vizor,” “rugged individualist,” “triumphant”) creates an image of someone powerful, decisive, and perhaps intimidating, contrasting sharply with the vulnerability revealed later. It sets up the central theme of appearance versus reality and the layers of disguise or perception that Vera navigates. The detail about the evening walk and the old dog grounds this potentially abstract description in a concrete, if slightly surreal, daily ritual.
  • The revelation of Miss MacIntosh’s baldness is a pivotal moment. Vera contrasts this “palpable disguise of the average man” with a sense of hidden “dead and vanished glory.” The image of the “great baldness as of the moon sunken” is striking and suggests something vast, desolate, and perhaps beyond reach. Crucially, Vera interprets this physical revelation not as a simple lack, but as something that reveals a deeper truth about reality: that strength is intertwined with fragility, illusion, and disorder. This passage shifts the narrative from external observation to deep internal reflection on the nature of identity, love, and perception.
  • Vera’s complex motivation is articulated after witnessing Miss MacIntosh’s vulnerability. The “good intention” is to maintain a facade of normalcy for her sake, even if it requires Vera to “deceive her” about her own profound realization. Vera seems to want to protect her from the implications of the “glory” or “chaos” she perceived, wishing instead for her to see herself as ordinary, stable, and authentic. This reveals Vera’s protective instinct but also highlights the inherent tension in their relationship and the burden of Vera’s newfound knowledge. It introduces the theme of deliberate self-deception or mutual deception as a way to cope with difficult truths.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s sayings about the Devil, while seemingly a digression, reinforce the chapter’s themes of appearance versus reality, disguise, and the difficulty of discerning truth. They emphasize that evil can be deceptive, appearing in attractive forms (“charming,” “handsome,” “fine dancer,” “God’s ape”). This mirrors Vera’s experience of seeing Miss MacIntosh’s seemingly strong exterior peeled away to reveal a hidden reality, suggesting that what appears “normal” or even “good” might conceal something else entirely. It adds a layer of theological or philosophical depth to the personal revelation, implying that the struggle to understand her is part of a larger human struggle to understand complex, hidden realities. It also gives voice to Miss MacIntosh’s own worldview.
  • This extended description is incredibly powerful and visceral. It moves beyond the initial shock of baldness to reveal a body marked by age, illness, and labor (the missing breast, scar, gnarled hands/feet). The imagery is both starkly realistic (“mouth open and toothless”) and metaphoric (“Alpine frozen slopes,” “wintry field,” “polished marble,” “barnacles”). The repetition of “pity” underscores Vera’s overwhelming emotional response. The description emphasizes vulnerability, loss, and a stripping away (“denudement”) of layers both physical and perhaps metaphysical. The comparison of this reality to something “less substantial than the wildest, saddest dream” suggests a profound disorientation for Vera, blurring the lines between waking life and nightmare, and making the revealed reality seem almost unbelievable despite its physical presence.
  • After the intense, detailed description of the vulnerable, sleeping figure, Miss MacIntosh is abruptly presented as the familiar, formidable “Miss MacIntosh,” fully dressed and appearing exactly as she did the previous evening, complete with her shining red hair. Vera notes her lack of astonishment, highlighting how quickly the mind can revert to familiar perceptions, even after a profound revelation. Vera notes, without astonishment, that she is in Miss MacIntosh’s brass bed and not her own, “something which had never happened before,” and does not ask how. This passage reinstates the “disguise” or preferred appearance and raises questions about which reality is dominant or “true”: the vulnerable sleeping figure or the robust, familiar Miss MacIntosh.
  • This dialogue shifts the focus away from Miss MacIntosh’s physical state to Vera’s apparent illness of the night before. Miss MacIntosh’s tone is parental (“sick and unusually stubborn,” “mild reproach”), emphasizing their caregiver-dependent relationship. The mention of the “first birthday party” and the torn dress introduces a specific past event, possibly symbolic of a loss of innocence or a failed attempt at celebration. Most significantly, the passage presents a clear discrepancy: Vera sees no tear in the dress, but Miss MacIntosh insists it is irreparably damaged and must be discarded. This highlights a potential unreliability in the narrative, either on the part of Miss MacIntosh (perhaps deliberately misrepresenting the dress’s condition) or Vera (perhaps unable to see the damage, literal or symbolic). The torn dress becomes a symbol of something broken or lost that cannot be fixed, but the disagreement about its state complicates its meaning and adds to the chapter’s layers of uncertain reality. Miss MacIntosh’s repetition of the “road to Hell” saying connects this conversation back to the earlier philosophical digression, grounding it in her practical, somewhat fatalistic worldview.
  • “It was only the beginning of pity. It was not the end.” Vera’s pity starts here and keeps deepening through the novel.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “Daylight should cover us.” What does Vera expect daylight to do?
  • “The pity, pity, pity of nothing, of something, for nothing is always something.” What is the “something”?
  • Vera “could not see a hole in it,” yet Miss MacIntosh says the dress “cannot be mended.” What is torn?

Characters

  • Miss MacIntosh says Vera “has been very sick.” Whose story of the night is this?
  • She is in “a remarkably good humor.” Why now?
  • Vera’s “good intention” is to deceive her. What does Vera know about good intentions, from Miss MacIntosh herself?

Connections

  • In Chapter 9, Miss MacIntosh disliked the dress’s low neck and bare arms, and Mr. Spitzer promised “Fair as a bride I should be.” Why does she throw it away now?
  • “That love is love which… does not alter” echoes Sonnet 116, as in Chapter 18. What is Vera promising?
  • The Devil “might appear as the angel transfixed by light.” Where has Vera already met an angel of light (Chapter 17)?

Reflection

  • Is it kinder to pretend not to know?
  • “Evil has many forms, and the good has only one.” Is that true?
  • What does a person throw away when she throws away a dress?

Research: Characters

Vera Cartwheel

  • She is introspective, empathetic, and deeply affected by Miss MacIntosh’s state.
  • She wrestles with her feelings of pity and the desire to shield Miss MacIntosh from harsh truths.
  • She reflects on love, the nature of identity, and the meaning of suffering, indicating a philosophical and emotional depth.
  • Her “good intention” is “that I should deceive her into accepting herself as but a usual being in the light.”

Miss MacIntosh

  • Portrayed as a strong yet vulnerable figure, marked physically by baldness, scars, and loss, which symbolize deeper suffering.
  • Despite her condition, she maintains a robust and kind demeanor, showing resilience. She is depicted with vivid details like her red hair, green ribbon, and practical attire.
  • She embodies the tension between appearance and inner reality, strength and vulnerability.
  • Her sayings on the Devil: he “does not always wear horns,” can “quote his Scriptures,” and is “God’s ape.”

Research: Themes

Love as Eternal and Unchanging

See: Love · Shakespeare

  • “That love is love which… does not alter with the changing circumstance” echoes Sonnet 116, as in Chapter 18.
  • Vera emphasizes that true love remains constant despite changing circumstances. Love “does not ebb, does not wane,” symbolizing an ideal of permanence in an impermanent world.
  • This eternal love contrasts with the physical and emotional struggles depicted, underscoring a spiritual or emotional steadfastness.

Appearance versus Reality

See: Illusion and Reality

  • There is a tension between outward appearances and inner realities. Vera observes Miss MacIntosh’s bald head, a stark and raw image that strips away pretense and illusion.
  • The “palpable disguise of the average man” and the “inarticulate mediocrity” hint at the masks people wear to hide deeper truths.
  • Vera’s intention to let Miss MacIntosh believe she is “a usual being” shows a desire to protect her from harsh truths.

Suffering and Pity

See: Death and Mortality

  • The chapter conveys deep pity for Miss MacIntosh’s condition, symbolized by her baldness and the physical aftermath of illness or trauma (e.g., the missing breast, scar, and hairlessness).
  • The imagery of “valley of dry bones” and “the road to Hell... paved with bones and good intentions” suggests the harshness of life and the prevalence of suffering masked by well-meaning actions.

The Devil and Deception

See: Faith, God, and the Supernatural

  • The narrative includes reflections on evil’s many forms, warning against being deceived by charming appearances (“The Devil does not always wear horns”).
  • The Devil is described metaphorically as “God’s ape,” emphasizing the complexity of good and evil and the danger of superficial judgments.

Research: Resources

  • “the scaled vizor of my rugged individualist”: Herbert Hoover praised “the American system of rugged individualism” in his campaign speech of October 22, 1928.
  • “the old, blind sheep dog running ahead of us on three legs”: The dog still “guarding the pasture which was no more against the thief who would not come.” See The Old Blind Sheep Dog and Dogs.
  • “that love is love which is love forevermore and unchanging, which does not alter with the changing circumstance”: “Love is not love / Which alters when it alteration finds” (Sonnet 116), as in Chapter 18. See Shakespeare and the Chapter 18 resources.
  • “this great head which was the secret pearl”: The merchant “who, when he had found one pearl of great price, went and sold all that he had, and bought it” (Matthew 13:46). See The Bible and Pearls and Jewels.
  • “one who had not built her house upon shifting sands”: “A foolish man, which built his house upon the sand” (Matthew 7:26). See The Bible.
  • “evil has many forms, and the good has only one”: Aristotle writes that “it is possible to fail in many ways… while to succeed is possible only in one way,” and quotes the line “men are good in but one way, but bad in many” (Nicomachean Ethics 2.6). See Aristotle.
  • “the road to Hell, as she had often grimly said, is very broad and very wide and is paved with bones and good intentions”: “Wide is the gate, and broad is the way, that leadeth to destruction” (Matthew 7:13). Samuel Johnson said “Hell is paved with good intentions” (Boswell’s Life of Johnson, 1775). Miss MacIntosh repeats it at the end of the chapter: “the highway to Hell is paved with more good intentions than shining cobblestones.” See The Bible.
  • “life is this valley of dry bones”: “The hand of the Lord… set me down in the midst of the valley which was full of bones… Can these bones live?” (Ezekiel 37:1-3). See The Bible.
  • “The Devil does not always wear horns”: In Goethe’s Faust (1808), Mephistopheles tells the witch that the devil has given up his horns, tail, and claws to keep up with the times. See Angels, Devils, and Myth.
  • “fine feathers which make fine birds”: An old proverb, in print by the sixteenth century.
  • “the Devil wore a red coat and a pair of green breeches”: Folklore often makes the Devil a gentleman and a dancer. In Burns’s “Tam o’ Shanter” (1791), “auld Nick” plays the pipes for the witches’ dance. See Angels, Devils, and Myth.
  • “The Devil could quote his Scriptures, too, chapter and verse for his purpose”: “The devil can cite Scripture for his purpose” (The Merchant of Venice 1.3). In the temptation, Satan quotes Psalm 91 to Christ (Matthew 4:6). See Shakespeare and The Bible.
  • “once he sat at the right hand of the throne of God and was a favorite son of Heaven”: “How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!” (Isaiah 14:12). Milton’s Satan was once among the highest of the angels (Paradise Lost, Book 5). See Milton and Angels and Archangels.
  • “some there are who still confuse the brothers, he who fell”: Both are called the morning star: Lucifer, “son of the morning” (Isaiah 14:12), and Christ, “the bright and morning star” (Revelation 22:16). See The Bible.
  • “The Devil, she had said when opening her black umbrella in the sunlight, is God’s ape”: The devil as God’s ape, simia Dei, who mocks God by imitating Him, is an old Christian saying, often traced to the Church Fathers and used by Luther. See Augustine and Christian Theology and Angels, Devils, and Myth.
  • “better the Devil you know than the Devil you will never know”: The proverb is “Better the devil you know than the devil you don’t.” Miss MacIntosh makes the unknown devil one you will “never know.”
  • “He might appear as the angel transfixed by light or as the angel of darkness”: “For Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light” (2 Corinthians 11:14), as in Chapter 17. See The Bible and the Chapter 17 resources.
  • “The King James version of the Bible was not to be trusted because made for the vanity of earthly kings”: The King James Version of the Bible was not the first English language version of the Bible, but the culmination of extensive translation activity (some illegal!) in the 1500s. This began with the work of William Tyndale and the printing of the first English New Testament in 1526. Following a tumultuous 75 years, James VI of Scotland became James I of England in 1603, joining the two crowns. Three main English Bibles were in use: the Bishops’ Bible (Church of England), the Geneva Bible (Protestants), and the Rheims New Testament (Roman Catholic Church), causing much confusion and dissension. To settle disagreements over reforms in the Church of England and respond to pressure from the Puritans, King James in 1604 approved a new translation of the Bible, primarily because he knew that it would reinforce his image as a political and spiritual leader. He appointed six committees, totaling 54 scholars, to prepare the new translation, using previous English Bible translation work, and using the best Hebrew, Greek, and Latin texts and manuscripts. The completed King James Version was first published in 1611. These first editions were large pulpit Bibles (folios) to be read in churches, but were followed within a year by the printing of smaller versions (quartos) for personal use. Though slow to be adopted in the English-speaking world and to replace the more popular Geneva Bible, from the mid-1600s to the late 20th century, the King James Version was the Bible of the English-speaking church. The translators’ dedication addresses “the most high and mighty Prince James,” the kind of flattery Miss MacIntosh distrusts. See The Bible.
  • “A man who is a pig is still a man”: Circe turns Odysseus’s men into swine, but “their minds were as before” (Odyssey 10). See Homer’s Epics and Greek Mythology.
  • “the darkness and the whirlwind”: “Then the Lord answered Job out of the whirlwind” (Job 38:1). See The Book of Job.
  • “the tissues like a baby’s fist”: Here the text has “a baby’s fist.” Chapter 19 has “no bigger than a baby’s.” See the Chapter 19 resources.
  • “a brassiere with a wad of cloth which had been Concealed”: The “breast pad” Vera imagined in Chapter 20. After a mastectomy, women padded the brassiere with cloth. Young’s capital “Concealed” is in the text. See Women, Gender, and Marriage and the Chapter 20 resources.
  • “her Irish green hair ribbon looped over the bust of Martin Luther”: The bust of Chapter 19. See the Chapter 19 resources.
  • “Dancing is never to be encouraged by this old lady”: Miss MacIntosh vividly expresses sexism through her character by highlighting her pragmatic, no-nonsense attitude toward the limitations imposed on women, her rejection of illusions, and her critical view of the societal roles prescribed to women. Miss MacIntosh’s insistence on practicality and self-reliance reflects a critique of traditional sexism by underscoring how women are often expected to conform to narrow roles, suppressing their individuality and desires. Miss MacIntosh’s harshness can be read as internalized sexism, reflecting how women may police themselves and others to survive within a patriarchal system that offers them limited agency. This portrayal critiques the constraints sexism imposes on women’s lives, identities, and dreams by grounding Miss MacIntosh as a figure of practical wisdom and resilience who nonetheless embodies the burdens of gendered limitations. It sums up how Miss MacIntosh has internalized sexism where she takes sexist behaviors and attitudes about women and projects them onto herself and Vera in the form of fear, envy, jealousy, suspicion, resentment, rage, anxiety, lack of self-esteem and confidence. Miss MacIntosh’s greatest fear is that Vera will become and get to do everything society expects a ‘normal’ woman to be and do, something Miss MacIntosh will never achieve. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “as when Mr. Spitzer had taken it out of its long white box”: The dress of Chapter 9. Mr. Spitzer promised, “Fair as a bride I should be,” and Miss MacIntosh complained of its low neck and lack of sleeves. See Joachim Spitzer, Weddings and Funerals, and the Chapter 9 resources.
