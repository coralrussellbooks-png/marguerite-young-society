Research notes: Chapter 22 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 22

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The chapter opens with Vera in a state of bodily fragmentation that she cannot explain: "There were scratches on my arms and legs as if I had walked in my sleep through patches of spiked brambles... My breasts ached. My knees were black and blue. My lips were dry, cracked and scaling". She is a self that has been somewhere she has no memory of. The body has acted independently of consciousness. This is not metaphor; it is a structural demonstration that the self is not unified, not sovereign over its own history.

Miss MacIntosh, meanwhile, presents as aggressively singular: the most insistently one-person character imaginable, with her flat Middle Western certainty, her routines, her umbrella. Yet the chapter quietly dismantles this. Her voice is described as "haunted by another's sad, imploring voice which I could almost recognize". The woman who insists most loudly on the singularity and reliability of the self is herself inhabited by another voice she cannot name or suppress. The pillar holds: the self is multiple even when, especially when, it performs unity.

Mr. Spitzer further fractures the chapter's selfhood. He exists as twin, as double, as sometimes "seen double, standing in two places at once". His dead brother Peron haunts every reference to him, so that it becomes genuinely impossible to determine which Mr. Spitzer is meant at any given moment. The chapter does not resolve this. It lets the doubling stand as ontological fact.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Miss MacIntosh's entire philosophical project is the defense of the real against the illusory. She is "far from self-pity... who lived not by illusions or memories or dreams and who never would". She insists the phantom is only "a spar or an empty fish barrel or a shad-bush moving with the leeward tide". She is the novel's designated realist.

But the chapter systematically shows that her realism is itself a construction, and a fragile one. The breakfast table, which she does not notice, is laid with "vermilion and silver and gold... a hammered silver bowl of white roses glistening with coarse water-drops": luxury she refuses to perceive because she has already decided she lives in What Cheer, Iowa, with broken crockery and smoky lamps. She sees "only the old place at What Cheer... She could not get used to anything else". Her "reality" is a remembered image imposed on the present. Her realism is revealed as nostalgia performing itself as fact.

Vera's mother represents the opposite extreme: a woman for whom "a dish of apples" becomes "the portrait of a lady" and "a golden harp" becomes "a fan". But crucially, the chapter does not privilege Miss MacIntosh's perception over the mother's. Both are constructions. The line between illusion and reality is, as the pillar states, itself a construction. Young places the two women as poles of the same problem, not as error and correction.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

This is perhaps the pillar most deeply embedded in the chapter's texture. Vera's grief is not named, not located, not caused by any identifiable event. It is simply the climate of the morning: "my spirits were drooping with a nameless disappointment". It does not arrive; it is already there. It does not move toward resolution; it accumulates through the chapter's long, winding sentences.

Miss MacIntosh's grief is more buried but more devastating when it surfaces. Her speech about knitting hoods for orphans breaks open suddenly: "we shall have no baby of our own, none to lay its head on this cold and empty breast. How handsome it might be with its curls, its three-inch hair, its closed eyes and long eyelashes. We shall save ourselves this trouble. We are not married, my dear. No man has asked for my hand". This erupts from a woman who has spent the entire chapter performing cheerful practicality. The grief has not been processed or resolved. It has been sealed beneath the surface, and it presses up through the cracks of her own discourse.

The chapter's final extended passage about sunken ships ("so many ships going down in sight of shore", bells ringing through water, the captain's table still set) is grief rendered as landscape. The coast itself is a graveyard. The weather of loss is literal weather here.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Vera is the chapter's witness. She does not intervene, does not fix, does not conclude. She watches Miss MacIntosh with an attention that is simultaneously irritated and profound. She notices the "brick red top of her head, the redness as of the rooster's comb", the "ivory castanets, her false teeth", the knife and fork crossed accidentally in the sign of the cross. These details are not offered as symbols to be decoded. They are offered as things seen, fully, without flinching.

The witnessing is most acute when Vera observes Miss MacIntosh's face losing its individuality: "Her face, it seemed to me then, was hardly personal and almost featureless, drained of all color, a face which might be anybody's or nobody's". This is not a failure of attention but its deepest achievement: the witness sees through the surface of the performed self to the void beneath it, without rushing to fill that void with meaning.

Vera's refusal to simply answer Miss MacIntosh's relentless questions is also a form of witnessing. She holds the space of not-knowing open. Her question, "Did you throw away my party dress for a punishment?", is an act of witness against evasion, forcing the unsaid into the room.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The chapter's structure is itself recursive. Miss MacIntosh reads the newspaper, which contains the same news as yesterday's newspaper. She asks the same questions she has always asked. The creation narrative (what God made on Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday) is rehearsed again, as it has been rehearsed every morning. "There is no news today that was not yesterday", she finally says, and this is not cynicism but ontological description.

Young's sentences enact recursion formally. The long catalog of newspaper items, "a Brazilian rebellion or a needle-workers' strike or a new garment union or the obsolescence of schooners... the tonnage of water falling each day over Niagara Falls... a great drouth in the sheep-raising lands of Western Alberta, a fall in sea island cotton", circles back through similar catalogs later in the chapter. The reader is returned, again and again, to the same material, but each return carries more sediment. We are building, as the pillar says, knowing the building is provisional. The chapter does not progress in a linear sense. It deepens.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Miss MacIntosh's theology of God's failure is a direct articulation of this pillar: "The first was to have begun what He could not finish. The second was not to have finished what He had begun". God's incompletion is not condemned; it is simply stated as the condition of existence. And the response to it is not to demand completion but to work on both Sunday and Saturday, "observing no period of rest", not because completion is achievable but because the ongoing work is what there is.

The chapter itself formally enacts incompletion. Young's sentences do not conclude so much as they accumulate and pause. The chapter does not arrive at a revelation. It ends mid-process, as all the chapters do, the sentence still running. The demand for a conclusion, for the chapter to mean something resolvable, is, as the pillar states, a violence against the experience the prose is tracking.

The five hundred salt shakers "not one of which would work" is a quietly devastating image of this: an abundance of objects designed for a specific function, none of them functional. The house is full of beautiful incompletions. This is not presented as tragedy. It is presented as the texture of the place.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Miss MacIntosh is herself the marginal figure made central. She is a plain-faced, red-headed, flat-footed, unmarried, childless, Middle Western woman with a broken nose and false teeth, reading a wet newspaper at a table set with Haviland china she refuses to see. She occupies the exact center of the chapter and the novel, and she is the figure through whom the constructed nature of every category (gender, class, beauty, sanity, reality) becomes most visible.

Her bald head, revealed later in the novel but already suggested here in the "brick red top" that Vera stares at with "utmost fascination", is the ultimate marginal image: the woman who performs femininity most insistently is the woman most radically stripped of its conventional markers. Her baldness is the evidence beneath the wig, the constructed nature of the self made literal and physical.

The drowned man who turns out to be a store-window dummy is another marginal figure: the human and the manufactured object occupying the same category, indistinguishable. "My mother had thought he was very lifelike, much more like Mr. Spitzer than Mr. Spitzer". The dummy is more real than the man. The edge of the category of personhood is where the construction shows.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The chapter is architecturally sedimentary. The house contains one hundred and forty silver services, forty silver soup tureens, four hundred finger bowls: the accumulated deposits of a family's history, unused, pressing upward through the present. The coast is a graveyard of ships, "buried in the oozy deep, yet not deeply buried", their masts and spars still surfacing. The past does not recede. It rises.

Miss MacIntosh's What Cheer is not behind her; it is beneath her, shaping everything she sees and refuses to see. The "old-fashioned celluloid collars and paper collars, too, the kind her old father had worn in old What Cheer" surface in the middle of a catalog of current newspaper prices. The dead father's collar appears between the price of shirts and a recipe for gooseberry tart. The sediment presses up without announcement.

The creation narrative, rehearsed each morning, is itself a sedimentary practice. Each morning, the first day is relived. Time does not move forward; it deposits. "The longer the orbit, the longer the planetary year, and the longer it would take to circle time", but Miss MacIntosh doubts this, insisting that "time was circled in a single week". She is describing sedimentary time: the week as the complete deposit, repeated.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Young's prose is the most direct enactment of this pillar in the chapter. The newspaper language (financial, sporting, meteorological, political) is shown to be a specific construction of reality, one that Miss MacIntosh uses as both shield and weapon. She holds the newspaper "to shield her eyes" literally. The language of current events is a barrier against the present moment.

Miss MacIntosh's nautical language, "Heave ho, my hearties!... All hands to the ropes!... Spread the sheets to the wind!", is explicitly identified as construction: "she had never been to sea". She speaks a language that has no referent in her actual experience. The language is performing a self (the competent, weathered sailor) that does not exist. Young makes this visible by immediately noting the bathing suit with its voluminous skirt and the black umbrella and the fear of crabs.

The Latin proverb, "Homo hominy lupus", is dropped into the breakfast conversation with the note "I know no Greek, less Latin, less Hebrew". Miss MacIntosh uses a language she does not possess, borrowed from a tradition she has not inherited, to say something she believes to be simply true. Language shapes what can be seen: she can only access the idea of human predation through the borrowed authority of Latin.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter does not point outward. It is embedded in the ordinary objects of the breakfast table, the sea, the newspaper. The candelabras that "had once been used as altar pieces upon an altar to the dead, perhaps in some ancient Eastern church" now sit on a breakfast table where Miss MacIntosh reads about Norwegian shipping. The sacred has migrated into the domestic without losing its charge.

The sea itself is the chapter's primary site of immanent transcendence. It is described as "an astral light as of some other planet", but it is still the sea, still this specific sea, still the water that licks at Miss MacIntosh's feet in her long striped stockings. The transcendent is not elsewhere. It is in the foam line, in the tidal pool, in the comb jellyfish coming in on the glassy tide.

Miss MacIntosh's own body carries this quality. Her false teeth, her broken nose, her knotted veins; she describes them as the outer form of a soul that might itself be "battered... impaired by much hard usage". The sacred is in the damaged body, in the worn clothes that will "probably outlast her old, worn body if the holes were patched". Immanent transcendence lives in the patched hole, not in the perfected garment.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is the pillar that organizes the chapter's deepest structure. Vera is explicitly positioned as the place where multiple times cross. She is the child of an opium-dreaming mother and a dead father, educated by a woman from a vanished Iowa town, living in a house full of the accumulated objects of generations she never knew, on a coast littered with the wreckage of centuries of ships. She did not choose any of this. It was deposited before she arrived.

The creation narrative that Miss MacIntosh rehearses each morning is the most explicit statement of this: Vera must memorize what God made on each day of the week, must know "the genealogies of the Biblical shepherds", must carry the weight of a cosmological history she had no part in making. The obligation is not chosen. It is structural.

The chapter's final movement, Miss MacIntosh's long defiant speech about giving the Emperor Constantine "a whack with her black umbrella" for having changed the calendar, is a crossing point image. Constantine's decision in the fourth century is still pressing up through the schedule of a morning in coastal New England. The ancestors are in the sediment. The calendar is still wrong. The carrying continues.

Vera's question, "I shall dance again, even with my own shadow, Miss MacIntosh. You cannot stop me", is the crossing point speaking. She is constituted by everything that has been deposited in her, and she is constituting the ground for something not yet born. The dance with the shadow is the dance with the self's own accumulated sediment, with the plural, irresolvable, grief-weighted, beautiful weight of what she is made of.
