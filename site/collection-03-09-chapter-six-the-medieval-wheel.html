Chapter Six: The Medieval Wheel

Part of Radical Immanence Collection, on Radical Immanence, the philosophy Coral Russell draws from the work of Marguerite Young (1908 to 1995).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter Five: Boethius in Prison · Radical Immanence Collection · Chapter Seven: Young's Horizontal Lady →

The illuminated manuscripts of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries are full of wheels. They appear in the margins of psalters, in the frontispieces of philosophical texts, in the decorative programs of cathedral windows and choir stalls. The Wheel of Fortune is one of the most common images in medieval visual culture, which means it is one of the most common images in the visual culture of the period that produced the philosophical and theological framework that Western thought has been working within and against ever since.

The standard iconographic program shows four figures at the four cardinal points of the wheel. At the top: regnabo, I shall reign, a figure rising with crown in hand. At the right: regno, I reign, a figure enthroned at the peak. At the bottom: regnavi, I have reigned, a figure falling or fallen. At the left: sum sine regno, I am without a kingdom, a figure prostrate at the nadir. The wheel turns clockwise. The figures rise and fall. Fortune, often depicted as a blindfolded woman turning the wheel's crank, is indifferent to their fate.

This is the standard reading, and it is the reading that has made the Wheel of Fortune a cautionary image for two thousand years: don't cling to what the wheel can take. Don't mistake the top for a permanent position. The wheel turns.

But look at the figures more carefully.

The four figures are not, in the most philosophically interesting manuscripts, four different people. They are the same person at four orientations. The same face, the same body, the same crown, appearing four times around the wheel. What changes between the four appearances is not identity but orientation: the figure at the top is horizontal, spread wide in the lateral accumulation of power. The figure on the right is beginning to rotate. The figure at the bottom is vertical, oriented along the line that runs perpendicular to the horizontal of power. The figure on the left is rotating back toward the horizontal.

The wheel is not a description of the instability of fortune. It is a description of the rotation between two axes.

The horizontal axis is the axis of accumulation, of the lateral spread of power and relation and the plural self in its widest extension. The vertical axis is the axis of transmission, of the line running from what was given to what will be required, of the crossing point where the self discovers what it is made of and what it is making.

Every full life involves both rotations. You spend time on the horizontal axis, spread wide in the present moment, building and accumulating and relating and witnessing in the lateral dimension. You spend time on the vertical axis, oriented along the transmission line, reading the sediment beneath you and attending to the not-yet-formed ground above you. The rotation between them is not punishment or reward. It is the description of what it is to be a full person across a full time.

The figure at the bottom of the wheel, the figure without a kingdom, is not the figure who has failed. It is the figure who is most fully oriented along the vertical axis, most fully in contact with the transmission line, most fully at the crossing point. It is the figure who has been stripped of the horizontal accumulation and left standing on the vertical line, feeling the weight of what is beneath and the weight of what is above, holding the crossing without the consolation of lateral spread.

This is the figure Boethius became in the cell. This is the figure that the ten pillars have been preparing us to recognize. This is the figure the eleventh pillar names: the one who is at the crossing point, who has stopped moving along the horizontal axis long enough to feel the vertical line running through them, who has discovered that the crossing point does not move and that the self at the crossing point is not less than the self at the top of the wheel. It is differently oriented. It is more fully itself.

The wheel holds. That is the epilogue's claim, and it is this chapter's ground. The wheel holds because the crossing point holds. The crossing point holds because it is not on the rim. It is the axis. It is what the rotation is around.
