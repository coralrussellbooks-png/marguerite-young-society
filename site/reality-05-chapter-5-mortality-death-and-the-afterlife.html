Reality and Illusion in MMMD

Chapter 5: Mortality, Death, and the Afterlife

← Chapter 4: Identity, Selfhood, and Otherness · Exploring Reality and Illusion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling · Chapter 6: The Search for Meaning and the Ethical Life →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Titus Bonebreaker · Mrs. Hogden · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

If illusion is primary, as this book has argued from the start, then death looks like the one exception. Money can be an agreement and a self can be a crowd, but a stopped heart is not held in place by repetition. Death seems to be the bedrock the framework says is missing, the one fact that no dream can soften.

Young’s answer is strange and precise. Death is real in her novel, but no one in it ever sees one directly. Vera’s father is lost on a face of the Jungfrau and never found. Peron falls through a window on the fourteenth floor, and no one knows whether he jumped. Of Cousin Hannah, “it was difficult to know on what date of the calendar her death should be marked.” The canary dies again and again behind Catherine’s back. And the death at the center of the book, Miss MacIntosh’s, has no body and “no eye witnesses.” Death is certain, but it always reaches the living secondhand, as an absence, a rumor, or a pile of things left behind. Absence is exactly where illusion does its work. This chapter follows what Young’s characters build in the places where the dead used to be.

A Death No One Saw

Vera tells of Miss MacIntosh’s end twice. The first account, near the beginning of the novel, is a question: “It seemed that she had walked into the sea. The waves had carried her away. What proof was there?” There were her clothes on the beach, her Pilgrim’s Progress soaked and marked by shells, “her broken black umbrella beating like a bird’s wounded wing at the edge of the sea,” and “all her old properties left behind her, nothing taken, nothing missing, nothing claimed.”

The second account, near the end, comes after Vera knows about the wig and the corset, and it tells what those properties were. On the lonely beach, “her black umbrella, Mr. Bonebreaker’s umbrella, was turned inside out like a trumpet,” “her red wig was found on the back of a shriveled horseshoe crab,” and “her old Admiral Dewey corset was found draped over a rock with its fasteners gleaming like eyes.” Miss MacIntosh walked into the ocean “with her head shining, bald as a lost planet, with her hands crossed over two major wounds, the breast, the side.”

Read beside the earlier chapters, the scene is almost unbearably exact. Everything that made Miss MacIntosh the Rock of Ages is left on the sand: the hair, the corset, the umbrella. What walks into the water is the woman those things had held together, bare-headed and wounded. Her constructed self outlives her. It lies on the beach to be found, catalogued, and remembered, while the person beneath it goes where no one can follow. The one thing she carries with her is a pair of black gloves she had mended long ago “for a sad occasion which she could not foresee.”

Then Vera adds the line that gives the novel its title: “At last, however, she was given to something. My poor darling.” The woman who had given herself to nothing but duty and common sense is finally given over to the sea. With no witness and no body, the death stays open, and Vera keeps it open. “She had only disappeared, I had always said.” Chapter 2 follows the search that grows out of that refusal. What matters here is that the refusal is not simple denial. The evidence itself is a costume. Vera has proof that the made Miss MacIntosh died on the beach. She has no proof about the real one.

The Deathless Bedroom

Catherine has solved death in the simplest way possible, by refusing the line between the living and the dead. She believes herself “both living and dead, this animated and bejeweled corpse.” For her, “death was this dream’s immeasurable extension.” No one in her room is dead, because no one there is alive in the ordinary sense. Her lost husband, her dead cousins, and the kings and drowned sailors all arrive as guests and all stay.

Vera inherited this logic before she learned to question it. As a child she concluded that if a dreamer’s dream “existed and continued and merged with the dreams of others,” then death “might be proved... to be nothing but an old humbug, an imposture, an imposition bearing no grain of truth. The truth was only in the erroneous dream, the bridge between life and death.”

Miss MacIntosh answered with the most humane lesson she ever gave. “When nothing had happened, I was supposed to admit, quite simply, that nothing had happened, that not a leaf had fallen,” for “neither I nor any other mortal was God, and omniscience had not been given to us, fortunately, so that we could not see everything or we should all most certainly lose our minds.” This is not the flat denial it first sounds like. It is a teaching about the limits of sight, and she calls those limits a mercy. Catherine wants eyes that could see everywhere, as Chapter 3 showed. Miss MacIntosh gives thanks that people cannot. What lies beyond death is “God’s murky business and not ours.” If there was any heaven at all, “it should be bare and plain and unfurnished.”

The two women offer two afterlives, one crowded and one empty. Neither can be checked. Both are built. The difference, again, is candor: Catherine furnishes the beyond to escape the present, while Miss MacIntosh leaves it bare so that she can keep her attention here.

The Canary’s Funeral

Between these two positions sits the novel’s smallest and most telling object. Catherine’s canary “was all that had ever stood between her and tides of death, as she would used to say, and so long as this bird lived, she could not die. If ever this little bird went, then she must go, too, searching through all the realms of death with an empty bird cage and a package of bird seed in her hands.”

The bird dies many times. Each time Mr. Spitzer replaces it, warning the servants that Catherine “must not see the dreadful transaction, the plain deceit, the canary’s funeral in the wave-washed garden under the dripping trees, or something worse might happen.” The life she trusts as single and continuous is a line of substitutes, and her own life depends on the deception staying hidden.

This is Pillar Two at its most tender. The canary is not a fact but an agreement, kept up by repetition exactly as the framework describes. Mr. Spitzer holds a private theory that makes the arrangement almost natural to him: “anything which was worth happening at all was worth happening twice,” because “nothing which happened once was real.” The canary is real because it keeps happening. Whether the deception is love or a trap, the novel refuses to decide, and the refusal is honest. It is both. Mr. Spitzer keeps Catherine alive, and he keeps her from ever grieving.

There is a quiet echo that Chapter 4 set up. When Mrs. Hogden arrives at the door years later, she carries “an empty bird cage” and “a package of bird seed.” The image Catherine once imagined for her own journey into death, the searcher with the empty cage, walks into her house on a stranger. Young does not comment on it. She lets the prop reappear, as though the house itself were rehearsing the ending Catherine has put off for so long.

Never Born and Yet Died

Esther Longtree works as a waitress at the small-town hotel where Vera’s bus journey ends. She has been pregnant, or says she has, for years, due, as the hotel’s own account puts it, “every moon.” She claims to have “already given birth to as many as the autumn leaves,” and she “could never sleep at night without hearing the little voices of the little stillborn.” The town mocks her. People say “that she was never pregnant or everlastingly pregnant which was the same thing.”

The novel never settles whether her children existed. Esther herself admits that “nothing ever happened here, nothing much that other eyes could see.” She says she gave birth “as easily as the snow falls from the sky, and it had melted.” Her pregnancies may be real losses, or imagined ones, or grief that has taken the shape of a body. Young leaves every possibility open.

What is not in doubt is the grief. Whether or not the children lived, Esther hears their voices every night. Her body swells and aches with a loss that nobody else can see or count. The line Miss MacIntosh reads aloud from the obituary columns fits her exactly: “Here’s one who was never born and yet has died.” So does the bus driver’s boast in the book’s opening chapters: “I never saw life come, but I have seen life go.” Esther is his reverse. She claims to see life come constantly, and no one else ever sees it.

The framework has a principle for Esther, Grief Doesn't End: grief is not a stage to pass through on the way to recovery but permanent weather, and refusing to perform a tidy healing is a way of staying truthful about what was lost. By any ordinary measure Esther is deluded. By this measure she may be the most honest mourner in the book. She will not let her losses be erased simply because no one else can see them.

The Brother Who Died

Chapter 2 portrays Mr. Spitzer and Chapter 7 gives him a full meditation. His place in this chapter is as the man for whom death will not stay on one side of the grave. He is not sure which twin died. “Had he not always lamented that it was he who had died so suddenly he had not known what had happened? For death was the heart of life. But was life, by extension, the heart of death, he asked?”

His uncertainty makes him the novel’s great attendant at deathbeds. At Cousin Hannah’s bedside the narrator notes that “there was no one so expert as he in conducting a dying soul on its last journey,” and there he arrives at the thought that “each who dies must die twice”: once in the body, and once in the life that was never lived. In the framework’s terms, Mr. Spitzer is the Witness, the one who stays in the room with what is painful and complicated without rushing to fix it or wrap it up. He cannot save anyone. He cannot even be sure he is alive. But he stays.

“One Dies”: Heidegger and the Novel’s Quarrel with Him

Martin Heidegger’s account of mortality in Being and Time helps name what these characters are doing. He argued that everyday life hides death behind the impersonal phrase “one dies,” so that death becomes something that happens to people in general and never quite to oneself. He called that anonymous public self das Man, “the they,” and said it offers a steady tranquilization about death. Against this he set an authentic relation to one’s own death: anticipating it as one’s “ownmost” possibility, which no one else can take on, and which cuts a person off from all relations to others.

Miss MacIntosh’s morning ritual fits the first half of this almost too well. She scans the obituaries “brightly” and says, “There is nothing to grieve over. Here’s a man who has died far from home. Here’s one who has died intestate.” Death arrives as a list of strangers, which is the voice of “one dies.” Her own death, however, fits the second half. She walks into the sea alone, with no witnesses, taking only her gloves. In the end she did not flee the one death that was hers.

Where the novel quarrels with Heidegger is on solitude. For him death is non-relational: each person dies alone and faces it alone. Young’s deaths are relational through and through. Mr. Spitzer cannot tell his own death from Peron’s. Catherine’s life is tied to a bird. Esther’s losses live on in her body. Vera’s grief is the shape of her whole life. In Young no one dies only their own death. Every death is carried by someone else, and often distorted, prolonged, or kept alive by the carrying. Heidegger’s authentic individual facing death alone is exactly the figure Vera’s line calls “the one illusion.”

A brief word about Buddhism belongs here too, because the novel so often seems to echo it. Buddhist teaching holds that all things are impermanent (anicca) and that there is no fixed self (anatta), and much in Young agrees with both. The difference is where the two lead. Buddhism names these truths so that a person can be freed from suffering. Young names them and offers no release. Her characters do not wake from the dream. They keep living inside it, grieving, substituting, and searching.

Grief as Weather

The novel’s final position on death is neither Catherine’s deathless room nor Miss MacIntosh’s bare heaven. It is closer to the framework’s principle of The Sacred Is Here: meaning is here, in the actual world underfoot, not in a promised beyond. The afterlife that Young can vouch for is the one the living carry. It lies in Vera’s search, in Mr. Spitzer’s replacement canaries, in Esther’s sleepless nights, and in the corset on the rock that someone has to find and remember.

That afterlife is made, like everything else in the book. It is kept up by repetition, sustained by illusion, and vulnerable to being seen through. It is also the only one anyone in the novel actually meets. Vera never receives a body to bury, so she never receives the ending that burial promises. The novel does not treat this as a failure she must get over. It treats it as the truth about loss. The dead do not stay on their side of the line, because the line was always drawn by the living, and it is the living who keep crossing it.

Reflection Questions

  1. Miss MacIntosh leaves her wig, corset, and umbrella on the beach and walks into the sea without them. What does it mean that her constructed self is what survives her?

  2. Mr. Spitzer keeps replacing the canary so that Catherine will not die. Is a deception that keeps someone alive an act of love, a trap, or both?

  3. Miss MacIntosh calls it fortunate that “omniscience had not been given to us.” Is there mercy in not being able to see everything, especially about death?

  4. Esther Longtree grieves children no one else can confirm existed. Does grief need proof to be real?

  5. Heidegger says each person dies their own death alone. Young’s characters carry each other’s deaths. Which picture of mortality feels closer to lived experience?

  6. If the only afterlife the novel can vouch for is the one the living carry, what responsibilities come with carrying it?
