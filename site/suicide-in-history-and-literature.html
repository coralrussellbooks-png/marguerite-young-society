Chapter 3

  • God Moves in a Mysterious Way is a Christian hymn, written in 1773 by William Cowper from England. It was a poem entitled Light Shining out of Darkness. The poem is the likely source for the phrase "God moves in mysterious ways", although the first line of the poem actually runs "God moves in a mysterious way.” The poem, the last hymn text that Cowper wrote, was written following his attempted suicide while living at Olney.

Chapter 3

  • The author Virginia Woolf who committed suicide by filling her pockets with stones and walking into the River Ouse in March 1941 only several years before Marguerite Young started writing this novel.

Chapter 4

  • “she had jumped from Brooklyn Bridge”: The first man known to jump from the Brooklyn Bridge, Robert Odlum, died doing it in 1885, two years after the bridge opened, and many have taken their lives there since. Hart Crane, whose long poem The Bridge celebrates it, drowned himself in 1932 by leaping from a ship in the Gulf of Mexico.

Chapter 6

  • “Death was birth, and birth was death”: Schopenhauer’s philosophy views death not as a final annihilation, but as the negation of the individual “will-to-live,” a fundamental force driving existence and causing suffering. He believed that life is inherently a state of suffering, a constant cycle of wanting and never truly being satisfied. Death, for him, is the end of this striving, the cessation of the individual will and a return to the underlying, impersonal Will. He argued that the fear of death is irrational, stemming from the attachment to the will-to-live. Although acknowledging the desire to escape suffering, Schopenhauer rejected suicide as a philosophical solution. He argued that suicide, like the will-to-live, is an act of will, preventing the potential for a more profound liberation through the negation of the will in death. Schopenhauer saw individuality as an illusion, a temporary manifestation of the underlying Will.
  • “She was Juliet, and I was Romeo”: In the play Romeo kisses Juliet, believing her dead, and drinks poison before she wakes from the sleeping potion.

Chapter 20

  • “that we shall not die like Saul or Solomon or Sennacherib in the midst of our corruption”: Saul fell on his own sword (1 Samuel 31:4). Solomon “slept with his fathers” (1 Kings 11:43) after his wives “turned away his heart after other gods” (1 Kings 11:4). Sennacherib was killed by his sons while he worshipped in the house of his god Nisroch (2 Kings 19:37).

Chapter 25

  • “this perpetual and unconsummated suicide”: The will-to-live by Schopenhauer could be used to rationalize why Mr. Spitzer did what he did and how Jock, the Black Coachman, Joachim and Peron are all the same person. Mr. Spitzer’s actions (his tireless searches, his care for others, and his internal conflicts) are driven by this fundamental will, compelling him to persist despite loss and disintegration. The multiplicity of characters such as Jock, the Black Coachman, Joachim, Peron, and Mr. Spitzer symbolically represents different manifestations of this singular will struggling within one being, blurring the lines between self and other, life and death, reality and illusion. Schopenhauer’s term is Wille zum Leben, from The World as Will and Representation (1818). “In psychology, the will to live is the drive for self-preservation, usually coupled with expectations for future improvement in one’s state in life. The will to live is an important concept when attempting to understand and comprehend why we do what we do in order to stay alive, and for as long as we can.” (Wikipedia)
  • “What death was this which was no death?”: The philosophy of death explores the meaning of death, our relationship to it, and the various ways it impacts human existence and understanding. It delves into questions about what happens after death, the nature of consciousness, and the ethical implications of death, including suicide. The Nature of Death: Dualism (the soul continues to exist after death, potentially in a separate realm or afterlife), Materialism (death is the permanent cessation of all bodily functions, including consciousness), Annihilationism (death is the end of all conscious experience, and nothing exists after death). Philosophical Perspectives on Death: Stoicism (death is not something to be feared, as it is a natural part of life and a release from suffering), Existentialism (thinkers like Kierkegaard and Heidegger, in Being and Time, 1927, emphasized the importance of confronting our mortality to understand our own existence and the meaning of life), Nietzsche (proposed that individuals have the freedom to define their own death, suggesting it could be a conscious choice rather than an inevitable fate: “Die at the right time,” in “On Free Death,” Thus Spoke Zarathustra, 1883). Ethical Considerations: Suicide (philosophy engages with questions about the moral permissibility of suicide, including whether it’s ever justifiable).
  • “Peron had rushed impetuously ahead, and he had fallen through a glass window on the fourteenth floor”: Peron’s death, left between suicide and accident: “this intention made to seem this irrational accident.”

Chapter 26

  • “the attitude of a perpetual grief”: This may refer to the philosophy of Arthur Schopenhauer, who believed that death is a natural part of life and a return to a state of non-existence that existed before birth. He thought that individual consciousness is impermanent and that the life of an individual is meaningless. His key ideas are will-to-live (the driving force behind our attachment to life and our fear of death), knowledge (shows the meaninglessness of life and the worthlessness of living), suicide (pointless because it affirms the will-to-live, though it destroys the individual), palingenesis (the will, not the individual mind or soul, is reborn in new beings), redemption (that death is an opportunity for redemption through the negation of the will). The portrayal of Mr. Spitzer’s lifelong sorrow, his recognition of life as a series of losses and unfulfilled desires, and his contemplation of the illusory nature of reality all echo Schopenhauerian themes. The passage reflects a worldview where grief and suffering are continuous and intrinsic to life. His fullest statement is in The World as Will and Representation, vol. 2 (1844), chapter 41, “On Death and Its Relation to the Indestructibility of Our Inner Nature.”
  • “he had lost his life, or he had gained his life through death”: “He that findeth his life shall lose it: and he that loseth his life for my sake shall find it” (Matthew 10:39).

Chapter 49

  • “to all those beauties which never should have been expressed” then he would be “threatened with the Federal pen”. It has been customary even with non-violent actions of protest or resistance that the federal government starts their prison term charges at 35 years. In at least one instance this led to Aaron Swartz committing suicide. It refers metaphorically to the intangible, elusive, and perhaps forbidden or hidden aspects of life, art, or existence: those subtle, delicate, or ineffable qualities that are difficult to fully reveal or communicate. These "beauties" could symbolize hidden truths, deep emotions, or mysterious experiences that the characters or the narrator encounters, which are beyond ordinary perception or societal acceptance. When it says that he would be “threatened with the Federal pen,” this means that if he were to openly express or expose these hidden truths or forbidden beauties, he would face serious consequences from legal or governmental authorities. The “Federal pen” symbolizes the power of law enforcement or government censorship that could punish or suppress such revelations. Metaphorically, it conveys the idea that revealing certain profound or secret realities could be dangerous or forbidden, with threats of punishment looming over those who dare to disclose them.

  • The thirteenth Apostle may refer to Judas of Iscariot until after his suicide when lots were drawn and Matthais was chosen in Acts 2:1-4. Others consider Mary of Magdalene the thirteenth apostle.

Chapter 51

  • Mr. Spitzer's reflections here reveal a deep uncertainty about identity, reality, and the nature of knowledge. The opening line, "Perhaps all definitions failed where each was each and each was the other," immediately sets the stage for a rejection of clear-cut, definitive statements. This aligns with Popper's emphasis on the provisional nature of knowledge. There are no absolute truths, only conjectures that have not yet been falsified. Mr. Spitzer sees himself as "a man of many parts, many branches, a man of many faded horizons." This suggests a complex and multifaceted identity, one that defies simple categorization. This resonates with Popper's idea that knowledge is never complete, and our understanding of the world is always evolving. Mr. Spitzer's inclination to trust the past more than the future ("he trusted the past more than he trusted the future") could be interpreted as a reliance on established ideas or traditions. However, the paragraph also suggests that this trust stems from a sense of uncertainty about the present and the future, rather than a firm belief in the past's inherent truth. Popper would caution against blindly accepting any authority, including the authority of the past. The paragraph is permeated with images of deception and illusion: "Perhaps he was the decoy and not the man, for his face was wooden, and he had been deceived by many facades." This highlights the difficulty of distinguishing between appearance and reality, a theme that is central to both Young's novel and Popper's philosophy. Mr. Spitzer's recognition of "the impossibility of committing suicide, for he had died merely by living, by continuing from moment to moment" can be interpreted as an acknowledgement of the ongoing process of change and transformation that characterizes human existence. Even in the face of despair, there is a kind of persistence or continuity that defies simple categorization. Mr. Spitzer's reflections reveal a deep skepticism about the possibility of attaining a complete or definitive understanding of himself or the world around him. There is a constant questioning and re-evaluation of beliefs and assumptions. Mr. Spitzer recognizes that his perceptions may be unreliable and that he may be easily deceived. This suggests a willingness to challenge his own assumptions and to remain open to the possibility that his beliefs may be wrong. Mr. Spitzer's tendency to trust the past more than the future could be interpreted as a reluctance to embrace new ideas or to challenge established traditions. In the book, Open Society, Popper warned of the dangers of idealizing the past and assuming there is some perfect past that society can return to and make everything better. For example, the 1950s were perfect and if we could just go back to that time. Or the Dark Enlightenment bros wanting to go back even further to The Gilded Age and its perceived “perfection”.

Chapter 51

  • At the top it says that Peron walked off the top of the building and not through the window like earlier. Which brought to mind the 1929 stock market crash and the stories about how large numbers of investors and brokers jumped from buildings. Which isn’t exactly true… The idea of a widespread wave of suicides by jumping from buildings is largely considered an urban myth, amplified by sensationalized media and dark humor of the time. There were certainly suicides and suicide attempts in the wake of the crash, some of them involving jumping from buildings or other heights. While not immediately after the crash, the suicide rate in the US did increase as the Great Depression unfolded, from 17.0 per 100,000 people in 1929 to 21.3 in 1932. Winston Churchill, then a British reporter, witnessed a man fall from a building in New York City around the time of the crash and reported on it, potentially contributing to the myth. However, it was later discovered the man's death occurred hours before the market's plunge and wasn't directly related to the crash. Some individuals, including a brokerage firm clerk and a member of the New York Mercantile Exchange, did jump to their deaths in Manhattan's financial district in the weeks following the crash. While jumping received more attention, other suicide methods, such as gassing, shooting, poisoning, and burning, were also used by individuals struggling with financial ruin after the crash. Although Miss MacIntosh pays a visit to Wall Street and invests “imaginary money” it is not mentioned if Peron or Joachim invested in Wall Street.

Chapter 62

  • “He knew a clarinetist who had committed suicide because he had suffered” - This paragraph uses the story of the clarinetist to illustrate themes of alienation, the quest for perfection, the impact of internal fears, and the possibility of transcendence through art or death. Chapter 68

  • Suicide is a leading cause of death during pregnancy and the postpartum period, often linked to underlying mental health conditions. Mental health conditions, including postpartum depression and psychosis, can lead to increased risk of maternal suicide. While not directly a cause of suicide, the inability to control the number of children a woman has can be a major stressor that contributes to or worsens mental health conditions, which in turn can increase the risk of suicide.

Chapter 68

  • The woman whose jail cell was furnished with “satin hangings and a German music box with a horn and a Yale pennant” may refer to Ilse Koch, the notorious Nazi war criminal known as "The Witch of Buchenwald". Ilse Koch was the wife of Karl Koch, the commandant of the Buchenwald concentration camp. She was known for her exceptional sadism and cruelty towards prisoners. After World War II, she was arrested by American authorities and stood trial for war crimes. Ilse Koch did not personally have a Yale pennant or a Yale tattoo; rather, it was a piece of tattooed human skin featuring a Yale-related design that was allegedly found at the Buchenwald concentration camp and attributed to her collection. Koch was infamous for reportedly selecting prisoners with unique or artistic tattoos to be murdered so their skin could be harvested for decorative household objects, such as lampshades, book bindings, and gloves. Testimonies and post-liberation reports described a piece of tanned skin among the camp's "pathology specimens" that featured a Yale University pennant or similar collegiate crest. It belonged to an unknown prisoner who had likely been a student or traveler before the war. Despite widespread media coverage of these "human skin" artifacts, prosecutors in her 1947 and 1951 trials were unable to conclusively prove that Koch herself ordered the creation of these items or possessed them. General Lucius D. Clay later reduced her initial life sentence partly due to a lack of physical evidence linking her directly to the "lampshades". She was eventually sentenced to life imprisonment and died by suicide in prison in 1967.

Chapter 70

  • “droves of peaceful whales committing suicide upon a lonely beach” Whale beachings were common natural events, often involving sick, old, or disoriented individuals, or sometimes large groups, with historical records showing strandings in places like Nantucket and New Zealand long before modern naval sonar, though documentation was less complete than today; events like a 1927 false killer whale mass stranding in Scotland demonstrate significant incidents, with causes typically attributed to disease, injury, or navigational errors, rather than solely modern human factors.

Chapter 81

  • “the German captain went down with the ship” Captain Hans Langsdorff is the most famous German captain who went down with his ship metaphorically in WW2. After ordering his ship, the pocket battleship Admiral Graf Spee, to be scuttled in Montevideo on December 17, 1939, to save his crew, he died by suicide on December 20, 1939, in Buenos Aires. Hans Langsdorff chose to take his own life to prove that he acted with honor, ensuring his crew's safety after the Graf Spee was trapped by British cruisers. He is often cited for his moral courage and for prioritizing the lives of his crew over the strict, often suicidal, orders of the Nazi regime.

Chapter 81

  • “the little rubber salesman, a victim of the Jews” may refer to Arthur Miller's play Death of a Salesman. The play features a character, Willy Loman, who is a failing salesman. While Arthur Miller was Jewish, the play itself is often considered a universal American tragedy about the failure of the American Dream, and Willy's Jewish identity is a long-debated topic among literary critics. Some productions and analyses have framed Willy as a Jewish character whose assimilation makes him vulnerable to societal pressures. The rubber pipe that Willy uses to commit suicide is a key symbol in the play. This is also a representation of the character's antisemitism, prejudiced and paranoid worldview within the fictional narrative.
