Definition from research

Chapter 1

  • “because of her suicide”: This stark fact grounds the previous symbols in tragedy and finality. The suicide adds emotional weight and explains why these relics are scattered and why the search is urgent and poignant: there is a desperate need for understanding or closure.

Chapter 3

  • God Moves in a Mysterious Way is a Christian hymn, written in 1773 by William Cowper from England. It was a poem entitled Light Shining out of Darkness. The poem is the likely source for the phrase "God moves in mysterious ways", although the first line of the poem actually runs "God moves in a mysterious way.” The poem, the last hymn text that Cowper wrote, was written following his attempted suicide while living at Olney.

Chapter 3

  • The author Virginia Woolf who committed suicide by filling her pockets with stones and walking into the River Ouse in March 1941 only several years before Marguerite Young started writing this novel.

Chapter 4

  • “she had jumped from Brooklyn Bridge”: The first man known to jump from the Brooklyn Bridge, Robert Odlum, died doing it in 1885, two years after the bridge opened, and many have taken their lives there since. Hart Crane, whose long poem The Bridge celebrates it, drowned himself in 1932 by leaping from a ship in the Gulf of Mexico.

Chapter 6

  • “Death was birth, and birth was death”: Schopenhauer’s philosophy views death not as a final annihilation, but as the negation of the individual “will-to-live,” a fundamental force driving existence and causing suffering. He believed that life is inherently a state of suffering, a constant cycle of wanting and never truly being satisfied. Death, for him, is the end of this striving, the cessation of the individual will and a return to the underlying, impersonal Will. He argued that the fear of death is irrational, stemming from the attachment to the will-to-live. Although acknowledging the desire to escape suffering, Schopenhauer rejected suicide as a philosophical solution. He argued that suicide, like the will-to-live, is an act of will, preventing the potential for a more profound liberation through the negation of the will in death. Schopenhauer saw individuality as an illusion, a temporary manifestation of the underlying Will.
  • “She was Juliet, and I was Romeo”: In the play Romeo kisses Juliet, believing her dead, and drinks poison before she wakes from the sleeping potion.

Chapter 20

  • “that we shall not die like Saul or Solomon or Sennacherib in the midst of our corruption”: Saul fell on his own sword (1 Samuel 31:4). Solomon “slept with his fathers” (1 Kings 11:43) after his wives “turned away his heart after other gods” (1 Kings 11:4). Sennacherib was killed by his sons while he worshipped in the house of his god Nisroch (2 Kings 19:37).

Chapter 25

  • “this perpetual and unconsummated suicide”: The will-to-live by Schopenhauer could be used to rationalize why Mr. Spitzer did what he did and how Jock, the Black Coachman, Joachim and Peron are all the same person. Mr. Spitzer’s actions (his tireless searches, his care for others, and his internal conflicts) are driven by this fundamental will, compelling him to persist despite loss and disintegration. The multiplicity of characters such as Jock, the Black Coachman, Joachim, Peron, and Mr. Spitzer symbolically represents different manifestations of this singular will struggling within one being, blurring the lines between self and other, life and death, reality and illusion. Schopenhauer’s term is Wille zum Leben, from The World as Will and Representation (1818). “In psychology, the will to live is the drive for self-preservation, usually coupled with expectations for future improvement in one’s state in life. The will to live is an important concept when attempting to understand and comprehend why we do what we do in order to stay alive, and for as long as we can.” (Wikipedia)
  • “What death was this which was no death?”: The philosophy of death explores the meaning of death, our relationship to it, and the various ways it impacts human existence and understanding. It delves into questions about what happens after death, the nature of consciousness, and the ethical implications of death, including suicide. The Nature of Death: Dualism (the soul continues to exist after death, potentially in a separate realm or afterlife), Materialism (death is the permanent cessation of all bodily functions, including consciousness), Annihilationism (death is the end of all conscious experience, and nothing exists after death). Philosophical Perspectives on Death: Stoicism (death is not something to be feared, as it is a natural part of life and a release from suffering), Existentialism (thinkers like Kierkegaard and Heidegger, in Being and Time, 1927, emphasized the importance of confronting our mortality to understand our own existence and the meaning of life), Nietzsche (proposed that individuals have the freedom to define their own death, suggesting it could be a conscious choice rather than an inevitable fate: “Die at the right time,” in “On Free Death,” Thus Spoke Zarathustra, 1883). Ethical Considerations: Suicide (philosophy engages with questions about the moral permissibility of suicide, including whether it’s ever justifiable).
  • “Peron had rushed impetuously ahead, and he had fallen through a glass window on the fourteenth floor”: Peron’s death, left between suicide and accident: “this intention made to seem this irrational accident.”

Chapter 25 · Life and Death, Liminality

  • Mr. Spitzer exists in a state between life and death, embodying the ambiguity of human existence.
  • The chapter explores death as both a physical end and a metaphysical continuum.
  • The interplay between living and dead, memory and oblivion, is central.

Chapter 26

  • “the attitude of a perpetual grief”: This may refer to the philosophy of Arthur Schopenhauer, who believed that death is a natural part of life and a return to a state of non-existence that existed before birth. He thought that individual consciousness is impermanent and that the life of an individual is meaningless. His key ideas are will-to-live (the driving force behind our attachment to life and our fear of death), knowledge (shows the meaninglessness of life and the worthlessness of living), suicide (pointless because it affirms the will-to-live, though it destroys the individual), palingenesis (the will, not the individual mind or soul, is reborn in new beings), redemption (that death is an opportunity for redemption through the negation of the will). The portrayal of Mr. Spitzer’s lifelong sorrow, his recognition of life as a series of losses and unfulfilled desires, and his contemplation of the illusory nature of reality all echo Schopenhauerian themes. The passage reflects a worldview where grief and suffering are continuous and intrinsic to life. His fullest statement is in The World as Will and Representation, vol. 2 (1844), chapter 41, “On Death and Its Relation to the Indestructibility of Our Inner Nature.”
  • “he had lost his life, or he had gained his life through death”: “He that findeth his life shall lose it: and he that loseth his life for my sake shall find it” (Matthew 10:39).

Chapter 37

  • “a cross upon a crossroad”: In England suicides were buried at crossroads, sometimes with a stake through the body, until the Burial of Suicide Act of 1823 ended the practice.

Chapter 49

  • “would have been threatened with the Federal pen”: The Federal pen is the federal penitentiary. For “giving a voice to all those beauties which never should have been expressed,” the old pigeon lobbyist would be accused of anarchy and sedition. In 2013 the programmer and activist Aaron Swartz, facing federal charges that carried up to thirty-five years in prison for downloading academic articles, died by suicide. The “beauties” are the elusive, perhaps forbidden, aspects of life and art, hidden truths and deep emotions beyond ordinary perception or social acceptance; to expose them would bring punishment from legal or governmental authority, the power of law enforcement or censorship to suppress such revelations.

Chapter 51

  • “perhaps had walked off the roof like his poor brother”: Here Peron walked off the roof of the building and not through the window as earlier, which brings to mind the 1929 stock market crash and the stories of investors and brokers jumping from buildings. The wave of such suicides is largely an urban myth, amplified by sensational newspapers and the dark humor of the time; John Kenneth Galbraith called it a legend (The Great Crash, 1929, 1955). There were suicides: a brokerage firm clerk and a member of the New York Mercantile Exchange jumped to their deaths in the financial district in the weeks after the crash, others died by gas, gun, or poison, and the suicide rate rose as the Depression deepened, from 17.0 per 100,000 people in 1929 to 21.3 in 1932. Winston Churchill, in New York at the time, wrote of a man who fell from a building near his hotel (Wikipedia). Although Miss MacIntosh pays a visit to Wall Street and invests “imaginary money,” it is not mentioned if Peron or Joachim invested in Wall Street.

Chapter 54

  • “the greatest Lady Macbeth”: Lady MacBeth is a leading character in Shakespeare’s play, MacBeth. Lady Macbeth goads her husband into committing regicide, after which she becomes queen of Scotland. After Macbeth becomes a murderous tyrant, she is driven to madness by guilt over their crimes, and commits suicide offstage.

Chapter 59 · On Suicide and Immortality

  • Suicide is contemplated as a potentially creative act, a desire to transcend mortality rather than a mere escape.
  • The relationship between fear of death and the will to live is explored deeply.

Chapter 62

  • “He knew a clarinetist who had committed suicide”: This paragraph uses the story of the clarinetist to illustrate themes of alienation, the quest for perfection, the impact of internal fears, and the possibility of transcendence through art or death. The kaleidoscopic imagery and the layering of real and symbolic witnesses enrich the text’s meditation on existence, while the clarinetist’s tragic end embodies the human longing for harmony and the fear of being “out of tune” in the grand symphony of life.

Chapter 64

  • “I walked into the traffic flow”: Miss MacIntosh “...wandered. I wandered on and on. I broke my nose. I lost my breast. I had no place. I walked into the traffic flow.” Walking into the traffic flow may have been an accident but it also seems to be her first attempt at suicide.
  • “a pair of old black gloves”: Young had Miss MacIntosh walk into the sea in black gloves “which she had carefully darned the night before.” Unsure what they meant, she asked a psychiatrist friend, who explained “all the meanings of the word black and gloves in relation to psychology and guilt” (Marguerite Young, interview with Charles Ruas, WBAI radio, New York). Two or three years after she wrote the scene, a man killed himself off Long Island: “He wore only a pair of black gloves,” which made her feel “I had struck something in reality there, too” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).

Chapter 65

  • “she walked into the roaring ocean on a cold September morning”: Miss MacIntosh died or disappeared sometime in September. Vera tells it here, before Miss MacIntosh has finished her story.

Chapter 66

  • “It would be a natural death, not a suicide”: Miss MacIntosh is describing the myth that men jumped out of office windows after the market crash in 1929. John Kenneth Galbraith found that the suicide wave of 1929 was a legend; suicides in New York in the weeks after the crash were fewer than the year before (The Great Crash, 1929, 1955).
  • “her corset draped over a rock in a salt pool”: See her walk into the sea in the Chapter 65 resources.

Chapter 68

  • “she feared she had conceived again”: The Comstock Act of 1873, named for the anti-vice crusader Anthony Comstock, made it a crime to mail contraceptives or information about them. This was challenged by Margaret Sanger in 1914. The Supreme Court protected contraception for married couples in Griswold v. Connecticut (1965) and for the unmarried in Eisenstadt v. Baird (1972).

Chapter 70

  • “droves of peaceful whales committing suicide upon a lonely beach”: Whale beachings were common natural events, often involving sick, old, or disoriented individuals, or sometimes large groups, with historical records showing strandings in places like Nantucket and New Zealand long before modern naval sonar, though documentation was less complete than today; events like a 1927 false killer whale mass stranding in Scotland demonstrate significant incidents, with causes typically attributed to disease, injury, or navigational errors, rather than solely modern human factors.

Chapter 81

  • “the German captain went down with the ship” Captain Hans Langsdorff is the most famous German captain who went down with his ship metaphorically in WW2. After ordering his ship, the pocket battleship Admiral Graf Spee, to be scuttled in Montevideo on December 17, 1939, to save his crew, he died by suicide on December 20, 1939, in Buenos Aires. Hans Langsdorff chose to take his own life to prove that he acted with honor, ensuring his crew's safety after the Graf Spee was trapped by British cruisers. He is often cited for his moral courage and for prioritizing the lives of his crew over the strict, often suicidal, orders of the Nazi regime.

Chapter 81

  • “the little rubber salesman, a victim of the Jews” may refer to Arthur Miller's play Death of a Salesman. The play features a character, Willy Loman, who is a failing salesman. While Arthur Miller was Jewish, the play itself is often considered a universal American tragedy about the failure of the American Dream, and Willy's Jewish identity is a long-debated topic among literary critics. Some productions and analyses have framed Willy as a Jewish character whose assimilation makes him vulnerable to societal pressures. The rubber pipe that Willy uses to commit suicide is a key symbol in the play. This is also a representation of the character's antisemitism, prejudiced and paranoid worldview within the fictional narrative.
