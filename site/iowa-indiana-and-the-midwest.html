Definition from research

Chapter 1

  • “the depths of southern Indiana, a state as yet unknown to me”: Young was born in Indianapolis and lived for years in New Harmony, in the far southwestern corner of the state, where she wrote Angel in the Forest. Vera, raised by the New England sea, enters Young’s own home state as a stranger.

Chapter 3

  • Anabaptist groups are established in Iowa (Amish, Mennonites, Old Order River Brethren).

Chapter 3

  • The Protestant Work Ethic, the Calvinist Work Ethic, or the Puritan Work Ethic is a work ethic concept in theology, sociology, economics and history that emphasizes that hard work, discipline, and frugality are a result of a person’s subscription to the values espoused by the Protestant faith, particularly Calvinism. The phrase was initially coined in 1904-1905 by Max Weber in his book The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism. Weber asserted that Protestant ethics and values along with the Calvinist doctrine of asceticism and predestination gave birth to capitalism. The novel is supposed to examine the culture of the Middle Westerner in the USA. It is defined by the Census Bureau as Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Michigan, Minnesota, Missouri, Nebraska, North Dakota, Ohio, South Dakota, and Wisconsin.

Chapter 3

  • Miss MacIntosh's philosophy could be compared to the Amish, Quakers, or Shakers. The Amish have a significant population in the Middle West and are known for simple living, plain dress, and reluctance to adopt many conveniences of modern technology. They educate their children up to the eighth grade and then they are apprenticed to work.

Chapter 3

  • "Middle Westerner" encapsulates Miss MacIntosh's essential nature as a pragmatic, strong, and no-nonsense woman, deeply rooted in the values and environment of the American Midwest. The term "Middle Westerner" emerged in the late 19th century, solidifying a regional identity for the area between the American East and West. This region, often called the "Midwest," evolved through westward expansion and the development of transportation networks, particularly railroads. It became a distinct area with its own cultural and economic characteristics, influenced by both rural and urban landscapes. The Midwest, initially known as the "Northwest," played a key role in westward expansion following the Louisiana Purchase. As the frontier moved further west, the term "Middle West" gained traction to define the area between the Northwest (Great Lakes region) and the Southwest (Oklahoma and Texas). The development of railroads significantly shaped the Midwest's growth, facilitating trade and connecting disparate areas. A literary movement in the late 19th century focused on realistic depictions of Midwestern life, particularly in small towns and rural areas. The Midwest has been a significant force in American history, contributing to movements like the Granger movement (The Granger movement, also known as the Patrons of Husbandry, was a 19th-century agrarian movement that aimed to address the economic hardships faced by farmers, particularly those related to railroad and grain elevator monopolies. Founded in 1867 by Oliver Hudson Kelley, the movement grew rapidly in the 1870s, fueled by economic instability and the perceived exploitation of farmers by powerful industries.), the Progressive movement (The Progressive Movement was a period of widespread social and political reform in the United States, spanning roughly from the 1890s to the 1920s. It aimed to address the negative consequences of rapid industrialization, urbanization, and political corruption, seeking to improve society and government through various reforms.), and the development of industries and architecture. The Midwest uniquely combines urban industrial power with the conservatism of its rural hinterland. The Midwest continues to be studied and defined, with journals like the Middle West Review exploring its identity, history, and culture. The Midwest typically includes the following 12 states: Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Michigan, Minnesota, Missouri, Nebraska, North Dakota, Ohio, South Dakota, and Wisconsin.

Chapter 3

  • In an interview Young said she had no idea there was a real town by the name of What Cheer, Iowa. She thought she had made it up.

Chapter 4

  • “the promised land of which I had read in catalogues of wild flowers and seed catalogues”: The Promised Land is the land God promised Israel, “a land flowing with milk and honey” (Exodus 3:8). Seed catalogues such as W. Atlee Burpee’s, begun in 1876, brought pictures of gardens and orchards to farm families across the country.

Chapter 5

  • “His hair had grown three inches since he left Persia at sunset”: Persia is a real small town in western Iowa. Midwestern towns often borrowed faraway names, such as Peru, Lebanon, Cairo, Moscow, and Athens.

Chapter 6

  • “though she had worked in a pottery”: Madge had left the town to work in a pottery or potter town that produced ceramics. In the Midwest through Ohio and Indiana, there was a variety of pottery and stoneware clays with common items made such as washbowls, jugs, and red roof tiles. East Liverpool, Ohio, which called itself the Crockery City of the World, is an example of a “potter town.”
  • “She was Abraham Lincoln’s sweetheart and Lincoln’s mother afterwards”: Ann Rutledge, Lincoln’s legendary sweetheart, died young in 1835. His mother, Nancy Hanks Lincoln, died of milk sickness in Indiana in 1818.
  • “it was not the malaria poisoning the air”: Malaria, “bad air,” or ague, plagued the marshy Midwest in the nineteenth century until drainage and quinine drove it out. It was carried by mosquitoes, not the air.

Chapter 7

  • Edward A Hannegan was a politician from Indiana. He murdered his brother-in-law in a drunken rage and was acquitted. He later developed a morphine habit as he continued in politics.
  • “It must have been that wounded bank-robber”: John Dillinger, from Mooresville, Indiana, robbed banks across the Midwest in 1933-1934. He had his wounds treated, and his face altered, by doctors who kept quiet.
  • “go over the river to the mines and be the paymaster, or he might dig coal in the pits”: Southwestern Indiana’s coal fields employed thousands of miners, who wore carbide lamps on their caps.
  • “The better element wanted to close down on old Josh anyhow”: Indiana’s county-option law of 1908 let voters close saloons county by county, and the state went dry in 1918, before national Prohibition.
  • “It’s awful the way these common people reproduce themselves”: Eugenics is an immoral and pseudoscientific theory that claims it is possible to perfect people and groups through genetics and the scientific laws of inheritance. Eugenicists used an incorrect and prejudiced understanding of the work of Charles Darwin and Gregor Mendel to support the idea of “racial improvement.” Women judged “feeble-minded,” like Musidora, were the chief targets of the sterilization laws, Indiana’s first among them (1907), and Madge asks why “the county” could not “put her away.”

Chapter 8

  • “where the cape of the glacier had reached”: The last ice sheets covered northern and central Indiana and left behind the flat, rich till plains.

Chapter 9

  • “What Cheer, Iowa”: A real town in Keokuk County. Its name is said to come from Roger Williams’s greeting to the Narragansett, “What cheer, netop?”
  • “a loving couple need not ratify its bond by marriage”: Common-law marriage, by living together as husband and wife, is still recognized in Iowa.

Chapter 13

  • “farewell to What Cheer”: What Cheer, Iowa, Miss MacIntosh’s hometown.

Chapter 18

  • “Oh, where’s What Cheer, Iowa”: Miss MacIntosh’s hometown.

Chapter 22

  • “cabbage roses such as grew, if not in What Cheer, Iowa”: Miss MacIntosh’s hometown.

Chapter 45

  • Twelve Mile is an unincorporated community in Cass County, Indiana, near Young’s own Indiana country.

Chapter 54

  • “the old Fishback Building”: The Fishback Building may refer to the Fishback Company Building which was located on McCarty and Delaware streets in Indianapolis, Indiana. Frank Fishback started his coffee roasting business in the late 1880s and, by the 1910s, also sold Virginia Sweet brand foods including coffee and pancake flour. The Fishback Company closed during the 1930s. Young grew up in Indianapolis.

Chapter 55

  • “the old Fishback Building”: “old Fishback Building” may refer to the building in Indianapolis it was the start of Frank Fishback’s coffee roasting business in the late 1880s and by the 1910s it also sold Virginia Sweet brand foods. The company closed in the 1930s. The Fishback family is a family in America that can be traced back to John Fishback, an immigrant from Germany in 1714 who settled in Virginia with Governor Spottswood’s colony. Extensive genealogical records, including the 1914 book Genealogy of the Fishback Family in America, document the family’s history and its descendants’ migration across the United States, with significant populations historically in Kentucky and other states. See the Fishback Building in the Chapter 54 resources.

Chapter 65

  • “called by many the Windy City”: Chicago is known as the “Windy City” due to a combination of factors, primarily a 19th-century political jab, rather than just its weather. The primary theory suggests the name was coined by journalists, particularly from New York and Cincinnati, to mock Chicago’s boastful politicians and promoters. During the competition to host the 1893 World’s Columbian Exposition, a reporter for the New York Sun, Charles A. Dana, reportedly editorialized that the city’s advocates were “full of hot air.” The derogatory term was meant to suggest that Chicagoans were all talk and bluster. The term appeared in print as early as 1876 in the Cincinnati Enquirer during a heated baseball and economic rivalry between the two cities. The Cincinnati papers used the term in a double-edged way, referring both to a recent tornado and to Chicago’s verbose (or “windy”) speakers. Despite its initial use as an insult, the nickname became popular and was eventually embraced by Chicagoans as a badge of honor, reflective of the city’s dynamic and resilient spirit. Chicago’s location on the shores of Lake Michigan creates distinct wind patterns, especially along the waterfront, due to temperature differences between the land and the water. A cold, harsh winter wind in Chicago is often referred to as “The Hawk” or “Mr. Wind.”
  • “Mr. Bonebreaker was born in Missouri, in the Ozark foothills”: The Missouri Ozarks begin with Native American tribes like the Osage, followed by French claim and Spanish jurisdiction. Significant white settlement started in the 1800s, driven by settlers from the Appalachian region, and the area was a major destination during the Trail of Tears. Throughout the 19th century, industries like lead mining and farming flourished.

Chapter 66

  • “She would not invest in zippers”: Miss MacIntosh would not invest in zippers because in What Cheer they were considered a modern invention and against the will of God. This kind of restriction sounds similar to the Amish and Mennonites who have lived in Iowa for over 160 years. The zipper began as Whitcomb Judson’s “clasp locker” (1893) and Gideon Sundback’s “hookless fastener” (1913); B. F. Goodrich named it the zipper in 1923.

Chapter 68

  • “The Tavern, built in 1827 by God’s servants who were believers in the lamb”: The town is New Harmony, Indiana, the setting of Angel in the Forest (1945). “Very astute readers of people who have been there know that it is the same town to which Vera arrives. I left out all utopian references. It’s the same town exactly” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983). The bus “stops before an old hotel where there is a tattooed landlord. And my heroine meets her first encounter with rural reality as a hangman who tells of his excellent fruit orchards. These characters appear in both books” (Marguerite Young, talk on the reissue of Angel in the Forest, 1966). George Rapp’s Harmony Society founded the town in 1814 and sold it to Robert Owen in 1825.

Chapter 71

  • “the Wabash country”: The Wabash runs across Indiana to the Ohio. Paul Dresser’s “On the Banks of the Wabash, Far Away” (1897) became the state song in 1913.
  • “The lieutenant governor of Indiana combed here”: The position of Lieutenant Governor emerged as Indiana became a state, with early officials setting precedents. Ashbel P. Willard served as Lt. Governor (1853-1857) before being elected Governor in 1856, showing a direct path to the top office. Like the governorship, the Lt. Governor’s office saw shifts between Democratic and Republican figures throughout the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The Lt. Governor presides over the Senate and assumes the governorship if the elected governor is unable to serve.

Chapter 72

  • “the Hoosier Amateur Star-Gazers”: “Hoosier” refers to the U.S. state of Indiana. It is the official nickname for residents of Indiana, used since the mid-19th century, and serves as the state’s official demonym (the name for a person from a specific place).

Chapter 73

  • “an Indiana waitress said to be everlastingly pregnant”: Indiana’s history spans from prehistoric Native American mounds to becoming the 19th state in 1816 and establishing itself as “The Crossroads of America” by 1937. Key milestones included significant Civil War participation, pioneering professional baseball in 1871, and becoming a manufacturing and agricultural hub.

Chapter 75

  • “perhaps clear to the Gulf of Mexico”: From Indiana the Wabash runs to the Ohio, the Ohio to the Mississippi, and the Mississippi to the Gulf of Mexico.

Chapter 76

  • “She had heard of a girl who was made pregnant by the police in a jail”: Indiana opened the first separate prison for women in the United States in 1873, after the Quaker reformers Rhoda Coffin and Sarah J. Smith exposed the sexual abuse of women prisoners by male guards at the state prison.

Chapter 79

  • Artificial insemination is the deliberate introduction of sperm into a female's cervix or uterine cavity for the purpose of achieving a pregnancy through in vivo fertilization by means other than sexual intercourse or in vitro fertilisation. It is a fertility treatment for humans, and is common practice in animal breeding. The first recorded case of artificial insemination was John Hunter in 1790, who helped impregnate a linen draper's wife. The first reported case of artificial insemination by a donor occurred in 1884 when William H. Pancoast, a professor in Philadelphia, took sperm from a handsome student in his class to inseminate an anesthetized woman without her knowledge. The incident was not published until twenty-five years later in a medical journal. The sperm bank was developed in Iowa starting in the 1950s in research conducted by University of Iowa medical school researchers Jerome Sherman and Raymond Bunge. In the United Kingdom, the British obstetrician Mary Barton founded one of the first fertility clinics to offer donor insemination in the 1930s. Mary Barton and her first husband were medical missionaries in pre-partition India, then still ruled by Britain. She witnessed the way in which women would be punished or even killed for being childless. At the time, it was taboo to suggest that it might be the husband, and not the wife, who was infertile - not only on the Indian subcontinent but also in the United Kingdom. Barton understood that both men and women could be infertile and in 1943 was the lead author on an article for the British Medical Journal. It was signed by many of the researchers active in the field in Great Britain at that time. With her second husband, Bertold Wiesner, Mary Barton successfully inseminated an estimated 1500 women, the majority with sperm provided from Wiesner, some 100-200 from neuroscientist Derek Richter as well as an unknown number from as yet unidentified donors. It is estimated that Wiesner is the biological father of around 600 children born following these procedures, although some believe this figure could be as high as 1000. When Barton and Wiesner published their paper on the procedure it was publicly condemned by the Pope who called it a sin, and the Archbishop of Canterbury who called for the British parliament to make human artificial insemination illegal. Although it was not criminalized it was not legalized either and therefore the status of artificial insemination was ambiguous. Consequently, the activities of Barton and Wiesner at the fertility clinic were conducted in secrecy and all inseminated women were instructed to tell nobody about it.
