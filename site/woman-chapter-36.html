Chapter 36

On Chapter 36 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Woman in Young's Magnum Opus.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 35 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 37 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

The chapter is about a message that cannot get through, and transmission is its pillar. Mr. Spitzer accuses Cousin Hannah of having "never loved any living being," and his own life registers as meaningless repetition, a "counterfeit" self living at "second remove," while Catherine keeps trying to summon the dead suffragist back. The disconnected telephone, the illegible letters, the messages Catherine would send "by mourning dove" or "winged Mercury" or a eunuch who "never would leave home," are the failure of a feminist legacy to cross the boundary between the dead and the living and the failure of the record to carry a woman's voice at all, the two being one failure. Catherine's "logic of madness" is the alternative epistemology the rational man cannot follow, and her persistence in calling to Hannah is feminist consciousness reaching across a divide the culture has left unbridged.

The chapter's central reversal is that Hannah grows after death while Mr. Spitzer depletes. She becomes a "wraith-like mist of fading flowers," her militant legacy expanding into the air even as the male witness who survives her shrinks, and the aggrandizement of the dead feminist against the diminishment of the living patriarchal man is Young's quiet verdict on which of them the future belongs to.

Mr. Spitzer's role as an "obstacle" between the mother and her dead love is the patriarchal interference that stands between women and the transmission of their own history, keeping Catherine isolated so the message stays undelivered.

The suffrage captain's imagined conquests carry the reach the record denies her. She travels "to the most distant places," "from polestar to polestar," and storms Al Sirat and the paradise reserved for men except as "black-eyed houris," "taking heaven by storm," so that even the afterlife's gender exclusions are breached. Catherine's questioning of Hannah's gender after death, whether she is man or woman, is the plural, unfixed selfhood the novel keeps returning to, the individual dissolving into something the categories cannot bind. The chapter closes on the infant who does not know its mother's face, an "old man crying in the storm," the cycle of women's oppression and endurance folded into one image, and the message still trying to reach across.
