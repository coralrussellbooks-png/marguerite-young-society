Chapter 72 · The pearl-like raindrops that never fall

  • Reflect suspended time and unfulfilled potential.
  • Esther Longtree may symbolize the sexual revolution in the United States by considering her character’s embodiment of themes such as fertility, sexuality, societal repression, and the struggle for female autonomy.
  • Esther is depicted as "everlastingly pregnant," carrying multiple stillborn children, representing an ongoing, unresolved fertility and reproductive issue. She is physically swollen, with symptoms of pregnancy (morning sickness, evening sickness, swollen breasts) but no viable birth occurs. This paradox of constant pregnancy without fulfillment can symbolize the tension in the 1960s between traditional expectations of women centered on motherhood and the emerging desire for sexual freedom and reproductive control. Esther’s "everlasting pregnancy" may reflect the societal burden placed on women to reproduce and the frustration when that role is unfulfilled or controlled by external forces like sterile fathers or societal norms.
  • Esther is described as robustly alive and free in some ways like her hair that flows loose "at whatever cost to sanitation," and she tosses it regularly as if to preserve her freedom. This image evokes a woman resisting social constraints, symbolic of the sexual liberation movement where women began to assert control over their own bodies and sexuality. Yet, she is also trapped in a "womb" that is both a restaurant and a grave, a liminal space between life and death, fertility and barrenness, reflecting the societal repression of female sexuality and the stigma around pregnancy out of wedlock or outside accepted norms.
  • The numerous stillborn children Esther carries (hidden under leaves, buds, snow, rivers) represent unrealized potential, lost possibilities, and the silencing of female sexual and reproductive expression. The fact that the fathers are "sterile" or "impotent" men, often traveling salesmen or transient figures, can symbolize the failure of patriarchal structures to support or recognize women's full sexual agency. The stillborns haunt Esther, much like the repressed desires and consequences of sexual control haunt society during the sexual revolution.
  • Esther is simultaneously described as "brilliant but dumb," "ugly outside... but beautiful inside," "experienced and sought after" yet also an "old maid" misunderstood by her own parents. This duality reflects the conflicted social status of women during this time who were beginning to break free from traditional roles but still faced judgment, misunderstanding, and marginalization. Esther’s education and cultural refinement contrast with her physical and social marginalization, symbolizing the new woman’s struggle to reconcile intellect, sexuality, and societal acceptance.
  • Esther’s "immortal medical case" status, with the old doctor predicting she will "be pregnant until the stars had fallen," suggests the ongoing, unresolved nature of women’s sexual liberation. The "everlasting pregnancy" can metaphorically represent the period of social transformation as an era pregnant with possibilities but fraught with struggle, loss, and hope. Her hopefulness despite repeated disappointments mirrors the spirit of the sexual revolution, which challenged existing norms and sought new forms of expression and liberation.
  • Her affinity with natural and mythical elements (wild mares pregnant by the wind, the great skull, the starry heavens, the moon, and the stars) links Esther to a broader symbolic framework of fertility, creation, and cosmic forces. This elevates her personal reproductive tragedy to a universal level, reflecting the countercultural embrace of naturalism, mysticism, and a redefinition of human sexuality as part of a cosmic order or liberation from mechanistic, repressive traditions.
  • Esther Longtree symbolizes the complexities and contradictions of the sexual revolution in America: the tension between fertility and barrenness, freedom and repression, hope and despair, tradition and change. Her character embodies the social anxieties about female sexuality, the quest for autonomy, and the redefinition of womanhood during a period of profound cultural upheaval.
  • Her story encapsulates the bodily, social, and legal struggles over reproduction, economic survival, and patriarchal oppression that shaped the conditions the women’s rights movement sought to change through activism.

Chapter 52

  • Mr. Spitzer's meticulous accounting and listing is directly tied to his struggle with his identity, particularly his relationship with his deceased brother. He seeks a perfect balance, a mirrored reflection where everything in his life corresponds to his brother's, suggesting a desire to understand himself through this dualistic lens. The fear of "no differing counterpart" speaks to the existential anxiety of lacking a defined self, relying on another for definition. This resonates with philosophical concepts of identity formation, the "self" versus the "other," and the influence of external relationships on self-perception. Thinkers like Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir explored similar themes in existentialism. Spitzer's obsessive listing and indexing of even the most mundane objects (pearl buttons, rusted nail heads, etc.) reflects a deep-seated need to impose order on a chaotic world. He's trying to find patterns, connections, and a "common denominator" to make sense of existence. This aligns with the philosophical pursuit of metaphysics: trying to understand the fundamental nature of reality. The futility of Spitzer's efforts mirrors the inherent difficulties in finding absolute order or meaning in a world often perceived as absurd or chaotic (a key theme in existentialism and absurdism). The questions about the sail ("Was the sail unwinding the same as the winding sail?") touch on the philosophical problem of identity over time. Does something remain the same if its state changes? This is further complicated by the cyclical nature of the tides and the wagon wheels, implying a sense of eternal return or repetition. This connects to Heraclitus's famous saying, "No man ever steps in the same river twice," emphasizing the constant flux and change in the universe. The paragraph also hints at Nietzsche's concept of eternal recurrence. Spitzer's attempts to capture reality through lists and measurements are ultimately limited. His fear that "all the weight, as he had often complained, was on this weightless star" alludes to the disparity between the tangible and intangible, the concrete and abstract. He can list the physical properties of objects, but he cannot capture their essence or meaning. This is related to epistemology, the study of knowledge and its limitations. The paragraph suggests the impossibility of fully knowing or representing reality, as there will always be something missing or lost in translation. The description of Spitzer's actions creating "division as in division there was creation," suggests that in every act of creation, there is also an act of destruction. This mirrors the belief that every action has an equal and opposite reaction. This is connected to the concept of the "Tao" which states that everything is composed of two opposites, and that without the other, it cannot exist.

Chapter 55

  • “mother-of-pearl Venus comb” is a species of large predatory sea snail, a marine gastropod mollusk in the family Muricidae, the rock snails or murex snails. The shell of this Indo-Pacific species has a very long siphonal canal and numerous spines. Perfect specimens of the shell are not easily found because of the fragility of the numerous long spines.

Chapter 58

  • Pearl-buttoned spats were a popular, elegant fashion accessory for both men and women from the late 19th century to the 1920s. Originally designed to protect footwear from dirt on unpaved roads, they became a symbol of social status and high fashion, especially during the Victorian and Edwardian eras. The trend began to fade in the late 1920s with the rise of more casual styles and shorter hemlines, which made intricate footwear and decorative buttons more visible.

Chapter 63

  • King Canute or Cnut was king of England, Norway, and Denmark. Miss MacIntosh invokes King Canute, who commanded the tides to stop, to illustrate the futility of "waste our lives in the vain expectation of that which might never happen". Canute, who despite being king, could not control the natural world, serves as a reminder that one must not dream of "what was not, of what would never be". He represents the limitations of human power against the forces of nature and the folly of arrogance. Catherine also mentions King Canute when she is lost in her opium-induced dreams. This further connects him with the realm of illusion and the rejection of reality that Miss MacIntosh so vehemently opposes. The story notes her mention of the "drowned pearl-diver rising to the surface, the griffin with folded wings, the centaur with its long-lashed eyelids closed," alongside her comment that James, the chauffeur, could be depended upon. Miss MacIntosh is said to be able to "do away with bodiless phantoms and monsters, with imaginary nightingales or turrets of ivory or turrets of gold or turrets of brass". King Canute, as used by Miss MacIntosh, directly contrasts with Catherine's "opium paradise." He embodies the futility of attempting to control the uncontrollable, while Vera's mother indulges in dreams that defy reality. Miss MacIntosh emphasizes that the "opium paradise was never God's way of life". The references to King Canute, particularly by Miss MacIntosh, serve as a critique of those who dwell in idealistic or unrealistic notions. He is a symbol of misplaced faith in one's ability to alter the fundamental realities of the world.

Chapter 71

  • The pearls are repeatedly connected to Vera's mother and her legacy. They represent the "lost estate" of her mother, something precious yet intangible that Vera inherits and wears, symbolizing the remnants of the past and the enduring connection to her mother's life and identity. The pearls are described as "real pearls which... would bring no more than cultivated pearls" in the market, suggesting a contrast between their material and symbolic value. The pearls also symbolize continuity amid loss and the passing of time. Vera’s mother believed that the pearls could not be lost or stolen, and that their number paradoxically increased with loss, implying an infinite or eternal quality beyond physical reality. This suggests a theme of immortality or persistence beyond death, where loss in one sense begets gain in another "the more pearls there were" despite losses in life. The novel often blurs the line between dream and reality, and pearls, like many other images, are part of this interplay. Pearls are associated with the ephemeral and the eternal, with illusions and truths mingling. They appear alongside motifs of opium dreams, ghosts, and phantoms, symbolizing the fragile and shifting nature of existence and memory. The pearls are linked to the theme of identity, as something that persists yet changes, much like Vera’s mother herself, who is simultaneously present and absent, real and dreamlike. The pearls become a metaphor for what remains of a person’s essence when all else has faded or been lost. The imagery of pearls dripping or cascading like tears, linked to the mother’s lifeless but radiant hands, suggests mourning and the cycle of life and death. Pearls are connected to sorrow ("the pity, pity, pity of nothing, of something"), highlighting their role as symbols of grief and the preciousness of what is gone.

Chapter 82

  • Vera's loss of her pearls symbolizes the shedding of material possessions and the pursuit of deeper meaning. Her relationship with the stone deaf man offers a path towards redemption, as she finds love and connection in unexpected circumstances.
