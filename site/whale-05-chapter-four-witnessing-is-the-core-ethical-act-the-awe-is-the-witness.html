Chapter Four. Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act: The Awe Is the Witness Recognizing the Witness

← Chapter Three. Grief Doesn't End: The Earth Took Them Comple · Be Bak in a Whale · Chapter Five. Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary: The End →

Pillar: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act · Across every book

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

I started chasing the philosophy in the novel.

Not because I had a philosophy background. I didn’t. Philosophy had been noise to me, occasional sound bites rising above the static, nothing that cohered into a system I could use. But something in Young’s novel kept pulling at me and I knew, the way you know things before you can prove them, that the pulling was philosophical. That the novel had embedded in it a way of thinking about the world that was not incidental to the fiction but structural to it, woven into the sentences and the images and the formal decisions so completely that you could not extract the philosophy without destroying the novel and you could not fully read the novel without the philosophy.

So I bought the books. I gathered the resources. I went out of my depth and I knew I was out of my depth and I stayed anyway because the alternative was to stop following the threads, and stopping felt like a betrayal of what the novel was asking of me.

What I didn’t know yet was that what I was chasing was the fourth pillar. That I was already doing what Young’s philosophy names as the only honest ethical stance available in a world built the way this world is built.

I was witnessing.

⁂

The fourth pillar of Young’s philosophy is this: the only honest ethical stance available in a world where the self is plural and reality is constructed and grief is permanent is the witness. The one who stays in the room with the contradiction and does not flinch and does not conclude. The one who brings the full weight of attention to what is actually there without the distortions of projection or the desire to make the thing more manageable than it actually is.

This sounds passive. It is not.

The witness is the most demanding ethical stance Young’s system makes available, and it is demanding precisely because it refuses the satisfactions that make ethical action feel complete. The hero acts and the problem is solved and the story ends. The witness stays and the problem remains and the story keeps going and the witness keeps staying. There is no arrival. There is only the quality of attention brought to the continuing.

Young builds this into the novel’s form before she builds it into its content. The 1,198 pages are not excess. They are the time required to witness properly. You cannot witness in summary. You cannot witness at a distance. Witnessing requires duration and proximity and the willingness to keep looking after the looking has become difficult, which it becomes almost immediately in a novel that is committed to not looking away.

Young spent eighteen years in the room with this novel. That is not a metaphor for dedication. It is the literal enactment of the fourth pillar. She stayed. She kept following the threads. She kept giving the novel more than any conventional measure of sufficiency would have required because the witness does not calculate the minimum attention necessary. The witness gives the full attention the thing deserves, which is always more than anyone expected, which is why the novel is the length it is.

⁂

The novel itself is an act of witnessing.

Young’s narrator does not judge Catherine Cartwheel. She does not diagnose her, does not position her opium world as inferior to the world outside it, does not offer the reader the comfort of a clinical or moral frame that would explain Catherine and thereby contain her. She witnesses Catherine. She gives Catherine’s world the full weight of her attention and renders it with the same density and care she gives everything else and she does not conclude. This is Young’s formal ethics made visible in the prose before the reader has the language to name what they are seeing.

And Young does not judge Miss MacIntosh either. This is the harder thing to see and the more important one. Miss MacIntosh is the character who failed to witness. She drew the boundary of her attention at the wig and refused to see what was underneath it, refused to allow the subordinate self its visibility, threatened annihilation when the visibility was forced on her. She is the novel’s clearest example of what happens when the witness stops witnessing: when the quality of attention becomes selective, when some things are seen and others are decided in advance not to be.

And yet Young does not condemn her. She gives Miss MacIntosh the same fullness of attention she gives everyone else. She witnesses the woman who failed to witness with the same care and density and refusal to conclude that she brings to Vera and Catherine and Mr. Spitzer and Esther. The witness does not exclude the people who have refused to witness. The witness stays in the room with the contradiction of that too.

That is the most demanding thing Young asks of the reader: not to follow the philosophy, not to understand the system, to bring to the novel the same quality of attention Young brought to writing it. To stay in the room with all of it, the beautiful and the compromised and the failed and the grieving and the threatened, and not look away and not conclude.

⁂

I was in awe of what Young had packed into the novel.

The awe came from recognizing an encyclopedic mind at work. Young had studied philosophy and came close to her PhD before deciding it took too much time away from her novel, and you can feel that in every page: she knew Descartes and Hume and James and Whitehead and she had thought through them and past them and built a system that their work pointed toward but could not complete because their form could not hold what the system required. She packed all of that into the fiction not as a performance of knowledge but because the knowledge was structural. Because the novel could not do what it needed to do without it.

I knew when I felt the awe that it had to mean something. Something big. Something important. And I hoped, something original, because no other book had made me feel this way, and the feeling was the kind that belongs to genuine discovery rather than recognition of something already known.

So I did the work. I built A Guide to Reading Miss MacIntosh, My Darling. I built AND: The Philosophy of Marguerite Young. I named the five pillars and placed Young in conversation with the thinkers her work had anticipated and surpassed. And at the end of all of it, after all the work, my instinct was not, I have solved this. It was: I’ll put it out there and hope someone smarter will come along. Maybe someone with a philosophy degree who would know more about it than I ever could and would want to give it the same attention. I hoped it was at least original enough for that.

I want to stay with that for a moment because it is the most important thing I have said in this book and I said it without recognizing what it was.

That is the witness stance taken to its fullest expression. Not the awe, though the awe was necessary. Not the work, though the work was necessary. The release. The understanding that you do your portion with complete attention and then you put it out there and you trust that the attention you brought will call forth more attention. That the work is larger than any single person’s capacity to complete it. That completion is not the point. The quality of attention brought to the continuing is the point.

Young did the same thing. She put the novel out there in 1965 and it was filed and ignored and occasionally praised and largely unread and she kept going. Harp Song for a Radical reached 2,400 pages of manuscript at the time of her death. She did not stop witnessing because the witnessing was not being recognized at the scale it deserved. She stayed in the room. She kept following the threads. She put it out there and trusted the carrying.

The Earth carries what we put out there. That is the thread from Chapter One and Chapter Three arriving here as the crab, the rabbits, the unmarked grave, and now this. The work you do with full attention and release into the world’s keeping. The Earth carries it. The witness carries it. The next reader who comes along and pulls the thread carries it. The carrying is not yours to complete. It is yours to begin.

⁂

Simone Weil argued that the foundation of ethics is not duty or utility or virtue but attention. The capacity to truly see the thing in front of you, to give it the full weight of your regard without the distortions of self-interest or the desire to make it into something more manageable than it actually is. For Weil, attention is an act of love. Not a feeling but a practice. The practice of keeping your eyes open when everything in you wants to look away or simplify or conclude.

Young arrives at the same place through fiction rather than philosophy. The witness in Young is practicing Weil’s attention whether Young named it that way or not. The 1,198 pages are 1,198 pages of practiced attention: keeping the eyes open, refusing the simplifications that would make the novel shorter and the ethics easier, staying in the room with what is actually there.

The difference between them is instructive. Weil’s attention operates between persons as you attend to the other, the one in front of you, the one whose suffering makes a claim on your regard. Young’s witnessing operates at every scale simultaneously. You witness yourself, your own plurality, your own grief, your own constructed reality. You witness the other. You witness the system that shapes what is possible for both of you. And you witness the natural world that carries what neither of you can hold alone.

Young’s witnessing is Weil’s attention made systemic: attention not as an individual ethical act but as a philosophical stance, a way of being in the world that shapes everything the self does and makes and builds and releases.

⁂

The characters who fail in Young’s novel are not the ones who suffer. Suffering is not failure in Young. The characters who fail are the ones who stop witnessing. Who replaces genuine attention with a frame that makes the thing in front of them manageable at the cost of its actual complexity.

Miss MacIntosh fails this way. She witnessed the solid world with tremendous attention and refused to witness anything else. Her pragmatism was a witnessing of one reality and a deliberate not-witnessing of everything that reality couldn’t contain. The wig was the boundary of her attention. What was underneath it she had decided in advance not to see. And deciding in advance is the failure: not the wig, not the baldness, not the false teeth or the one breast, but the threat issued to protect the boundary. The - I will kill you that stands behind every selective attention and enforces it.

Mr. Spitzer fails differently. He fails from the outside, from the institutional side: the man who arrives with the apparatus of reason and law and social legitimacy and still cannot see what Catherine and Cousin Hannah already know. His failure is not selective attention but the specific blindness of the person whose construction of reality is so dominant and so unexamined that it cannot perceive what falls outside its frame. He is not threatening. He is simply unable to witness what the women in the room are witnessing because his frame was built to exclude it.

Together they triangulate the failure. Miss MacIntosh wigs herself into certainty and threatens annihilation when the certainty is disturbed. Mr. Spitzer cannot find certainty even with the full apparatus of institutional reason behind him. One builds the frame too tight and enforces it with violence. The other cannot build a frame adequate to what is actually there. Between them is the witness: the one who sees without the frame, who stays in the room without the protection of the wig or the institution, who bears the full weight of what is actually present.

That figure is Vera. Imperfect, grieving, plural, unable to arrive at the destination she has been traveling toward for 1,198 pages. And yet witnessing. Staying on the bus. Keeping the journey going. Bringing the full weight of her attention to everything she passes through without the protection of the wig or the institution or the stage model of grief that would tell her the loss is processed and the forward motion can resume.

⁂

I want to say something about what it cost to witness Young’s novel across five readings without the validation of a tradition that had decided the novel was worth witnessing.

The critical tradition filed Young away. The academic apparatus had decided she was a difficult woman writer who didn’t quite fit, and that filed away things don’t get reconsidered easily, and so the attention that the novel deserved and demanded had not been given. The reading community that would have recognized what Young had built did not have a visible center. There was and is no institution behind the work I was doing. No publisher, no academic affiliation, no peer review, no conference, no tenure case that required someone to engage seriously with Young’s philosophy. Just the novel and the threads and the feeling that the threads led somewhere important.

The witness does not require institutional validation to continue witnessing. This is the political dimension of the fourth pillar that the tradition has never fully recognized because the tradition is itself the institution. It rewards the witnesses it has already decided are worth rewarding and ignores the ones working outside its frame.

Young was outside the frame. I was outside the frame. The work we were both doing, each in our own time and form, was the work of the witness who stays in the room because the room contains something true, not because the staying will be recognized.

I publish exclusively on Substack and in free PDFs because the philosophy this book is built on does not permit me to do otherwise. Serious ideas belong to everyone. The witness who charges admission to the witnessing has already compromised the attention. You cannot give the full weight of your regard to something and simultaneously manage the economics of who gets to receive it. Young understood this. She gave the novel everything. She held nothing back for a more manageable version.

The awe I felt reading her was the awe of recognizing that quality of attention in someone else’s work. The recognition that something had been given everything and held nothing back. That the encyclopedic mind behind the novel had stayed in the room for eighteen years and given the room everything it had.

The awe is the witness recognizing the witness.

⁂

There is one more thing that needs to be said about the witness before we move to the fifth pillar, and it is the thing that connects all four pillars into a coherent ethical stance rather than a collection of philosophical positions.

The witness can only do what the witness does because of the first three pillars. You cannot witness from a unified self because the unified self is already managing what it will and will not see, already enforcing the boundary of its attention, already prepared to threaten what threatens it. The witness requires the plural self: the self that has stopped demanding that one of its selves kill the others into silence, that can hold the wig and the baldness simultaneously without resolving them into a judgment.

You cannot witness from inside the belief that consensus reality is the only reality because the belief has already decided what is worth seeing. The witness requires the understanding that all reality is constructed, that the frame you are looking through is a frame and not a window, that what falls outside your frame is not therefore less real.

And you cannot witness without the capacity to stay in the permanent weather of grief. The witness sees loss. Constantly. The things that are being lost while they are still being lost. The beauty that is inseparable from the grief of the natural world that Mr. Spitzer embodies. The losses built into the foundation of the collective life that the political system demands be called resolved. The small losses, the rabbits, the unmarked grave, the stick that the Earth took along with everything else. The witness does not look away from any of it. And that requires the third pillar: the understanding that the looking will not resolve, that the grief is the climate, that the honest life is the one that stays in the weather.

The witness is what the first three pillars make possible. It is the ethical stance that follows honestly from the philosophical ground. If the self is plural and reality is constructed and grief is permanent, then the only honest response is to witness: to stay in the room with all of it, to bring the full weight of attention to what is actually there, to not look away and not conclude and not replace the difficulty with a consolation that would require pretending the difficulty isn’t there.

Young stayed in the room for eighteen years.

I have been in the room since 2019.

The room is larger than either of us. It contains the novel and the philosophy and the reading guide and this book and everything that will come after this book when someone smarter pulls the thread further. The room is the Earth that carries what we put into it. The room is the witness that outlasts any individual witness.

Stay in the room.

Keep looking.

Do not conclude.

Put it out there and trust the carrying.
