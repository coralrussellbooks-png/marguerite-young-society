Research notes: Chapter 16 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 16

Characters: Falada · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

Vera Cartwheel is never singular in this chapter. She is simultaneously a fourteen-year-old girl, a bareback circus rider "wearing a plume, a pair of pink tights," a headless rider, an orphan whispering secrets, and a child who does not know what she thinks "until I heard myself speaking the words".

This last detail is crucial. The self is not prior to its own expression. It discovers itself in the act of speaking, which means there is no unified interior self waiting to be expressed. There is only the ongoing, surprising production of selfhood through language and action.

"I often did not know what I thought until I heard myself speaking the words. I was frightened when sometimes, though whispering, I heard my voice shouting among the stars."

The fright here is the fright of plurality, of discovering that you are larger, louder, and less controlled than you believed. The self overflows its own container. This is not psychological instability presented as a problem to be solved. It is presented as the condition of being alive and conscious.

The rider and the horse further split the self. By the chapter's end, Falada "seemed to be my head, my intelligence. I was the headless rider". Vera distributes her own cognition into the animal beneath her. The self is not located in the body or the mind; it is dispersed across the relationship between them.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The entire landscape of this chapter refuses the distinction between real and constructed. The house contains "ghostly players like swans running back and forth in the moonlight" on tennis courts: are these real players in white, or apparitions? The text does not adjudicate. The moon's reflection becomes a polo ball that Falada can see before Vera can. The horse perceives a reality the human cannot access.

The most philosophically dense passage on this pillar concerns the mother:

"She might think we were phantoms, of course, and then we would be safe. If we were phantoms, she would not be afraid... But if we were real, she might die of shock."

This is a stunning inversion of the usual ontological hierarchy. Being real is the dangerous condition. Being phantom is the safe one. The mother, submerged in her dreams, has constructed a world in which the imaginary is habitable and the real is lethal. Young does not mock this. She presents it as a coherent epistemological position: one that Vera must navigate carefully and respectfully.

The house itself is the architectural embodiment of illusion as primary. It is "a water castle" on dry land, containing gondolas, dolphins, a phoenix, a gold piano upheld by cherubim. These are not decorations. They are competing realities layered on top of each other, none more authoritative than any other.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Grief in this chapter is not announced. It is the climate through which everything moves.

The pony Falada is old, wind-broken, diseased, his mane "withered," his heart rasping "like a door opening on rusted hinges". He is beautiful and dying simultaneously. Vera's love for him is inseparable from his deterioration. The joy of the ride and the approaching loss are not sequential; they are concurrent. This is grief as permanent weather: the cold and the celebration occupying the same air.

The house is similarly structured. Everything in it is a monument to what has already been lost: "old wheels and carriage bodies," "broken torsos and marble heads lying in the grass," "faded marquees and dragging nets", the tomb of a musician "who had gone to join the world of music", a marble boy whose tomb may never have had an occupant. Loss is not past tense here. It is the present tense of the environment.

Even Vera's orphanhood, "I had no mother and was an orphan, how I had never known my father", is whispered as a secret to the horse, not as a wound seeking healing but as a fact of the self, deposited like sediment, carried forward.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrative voice of this chapter is one of sustained, unflinching attention. Young does not rush past the dying horse toward the drama of Miss MacIntosh's revelation. She stays, for pages, with the foam on Falada's sides, the dust mop feathers of his mane, the cold of his nose against the warmth of his sweating sides.

This is the ethics of the witness made structural. The prose refuses to simplify, refuses to accelerate toward conclusion. Every object in the house receives full attention:

"We passed the empty tomb of some old musician who had gone to join the world of music, the tomb which was a gold grand piano upheld by cherubim pallbearers with folded wings wading through a musical fountain."

The sentence does not pause to evaluate whether this is beautiful or grotesque, real or symbolic. It witnesses. The cherubim are pallbearers. The fountain is musical. The tomb is a piano. These contradictions are held open, not resolved.

The chapter's final movement, toward Miss MacIntosh's room, is itself an act of witnessing. Vera chooses to go to the one person who "would know that we were real". The ethical imperative is not comfort or validation but confirmation of reality: someone willing to see what is actually there.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The ride itself is circular. They ride "around and around on a path that made a circle like an eye through the garden of the blind". The structure of the chapter mirrors this: it begins with exultant pride and returns, through the interior of the house, to the threshold of knowledge, Miss MacIntosh's door, where something will be revealed that affirms "some older shock which had been forgotten".

This is recursive construction precisely: you return to the beginning with what you have learned, and the return reveals that you already knew, at some level, what you are now discovering. The building of the self is never finished. The ride never truly ends; it becomes the chapter, becomes the novel, becomes the narrator's life.

The pony's pedigree extends this recursion across time. Falada is descended from the horse that inspired the cabriole, the Chippendale furniture, the carousel. His bloodline is a recursive loop through human aesthetic history: the horse that became furniture that became the horse again in the merry-go-round. Nothing is original. Everything returns.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter ends mid-recognition:

"She had greatly changed since last I had seen her, and she was unbelievably not herself, and yet—how was it that I recognized her instantly as if she were something I had always dimly known, and the shock of this knowledge was but the affirmation of some older shock which had been forgotten and laid over by the autumn leaves, the snow falling, the first buds of spring?"

The sentence does not complete its own revelation. It opens onto the next page, the next chapter, the ongoing sentence of the novel. This is not a cliffhanger in the commercial sense. It is an ethical commitment to incompletion: a refusal to conclude what has not concluded, to resolve what is still in the process of being known.

The novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is famously 1,198 pages long, and this structural incompletion is not accidental. Young is making the argument, through form, that the demand for resolution is a violence against experience. The ongoing sentence is the honest one.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Every figure in this chapter exists at the edge of a category. Falada is barely a horse: "almost only an idea of a horse". The mother is barely present: submerged in dreams, entertaining imaginary guests, neither fully conscious nor unconscious. Miss MacIntosh, when we reach her, is "unbelievably not herself", which implies she has a self, but that self has become unstable, marginal to its own identity.

Vera herself is marginal: an orphan, a child without parents, a girl riding a horse through a house at night, occupying no stable social category. She is neither the dreaming mother nor the plain nurse. She is the figure who moves between them, which is to say she is the figure who makes the constructed nature of both categories visible.

The house's contents are similarly marginal objects: things that were once functional (gondola, piano, furniture) now existing as monuments, tombs, or decorations. They are at the edge of their own categories: the piano is a tomb, the tomb is a piano. The marginal object reveals that all categories are constructions.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This pillar is perhaps the most visually explicit in the chapter. The house is literally a sediment of historical time:

"We passed the sleeping marble lions which had once guarded the palace of a Venetian doge who long ago had joined the world of water."

Layer upon layer: Venetian doge, marble lions, American estate, moonlit night, fourteen-year-old girl. The past does not recede. It accumulates. The doge is gone but his lions are here, pressing up through the present.

The furniture pedigree (Falada's ancestor inspiring the cabriole, Queen Anne furniture, Chippendale, "whole forests" of it, still present "in the best Boston houses") is an explicit theory of sedimentary time. The horse from an Arabic fairy tale became the furniture in an English queen's court became the chairs in a Boston drawing room. The past is not behind us. It is beneath us, holding us up.

The silver comb and brush that Vera uses on Falada "tarnished by age and the dews of the grave, had belonged to a beautiful dead lady now sleeping in her marble tomb". Vera grooms the horse with the instruments of a dead woman's vanity. She is living inside someone else's past, using it as her present. The sediment is not metaphorical. It is material.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter is acutely aware of the constructive power of naming. Mr. Spitzer's meditation on Falada's name is the most explicit statement of this pillar in the text:

"It seemed to him that the name of a horse was important as the name of a man, and he never had held with those who believe that a rose by any other name would smell as sweet. What was in a name? All things were in their names, Mr. Spitzer believed."

This is a direct refutation of the Shakespearean position. Language is not a neutral label applied to a pre-existing reality. The name constitutes the thing. Falada is not merely called Falada; he is Falada, the horse from the Arabic fairy tale whose severed head spoke oracles above the city gates. The name carries the mythology. The mythology shapes what the horse is and what his death will mean.

Vera's own experience of language as construction appears in the passage about not knowing what she thinks until she speaks it. Language is not the vehicle for pre-existing thought. It is the construction site where thought comes into being. And the result is sometimes terrifying: "I was frightened when sometimes, though whispering, I heard my voice shouting among the stars". Language exceeds its speaker. It speaks from somewhere the speaker did not intend.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter is entirely immanent: located in the dying pony, the cold nose, the diseased mane, the rasping heart. Not in heaven, not in an afterlife, not in a symbol pointing beyond itself, but here, in the body of an old horse in a moonlit garden.

"I lay on Falada's back with my arms around his bony, pulsing neck with its long, straining tendons and my face against his diseased, withered mane with its dust mop feathers."

The transcendence is in the contact. The bony neck, the straining tendons, the diseased mane; these are not obstacles to the sacred. They are the sacred. The deterioration is not something to be transcended. It is where the sacred lives.

The house's interior offers a similar immanent transcendence: the phoenix "rising from those cold ashes of the dream", the musical fountain, the crystal beads "each crystal gleaming with interior fire as if water were the coffin of fire". The sacred is inside the material: fire inside water, music inside stone, life inside the dying horse. It does not point outward. It points deeper in.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The chapter's final image crystallizes this pillar completely. Vera, riding Falada through the house, is not a self moving through space and time. She is the point where multiple times cross: her own childhood, her mother's dreaming, Miss MacIntosh's plain reality, Falada's dying body, the Venetian doge's marble lions, the dead lady's silver comb, the Arabic fairy tale's speaking horse head, the Chippendale forests of Queen Anne's England.

She did not choose any of this. She was born into it. The house was already there. The sediment was already laid. The pony was already old. The name Falada already carried its mythology.

And yet she is also constituting the ground for what comes next. She is riding toward the revelation of Miss MacIntosh's secret: a revelation that will, we understand, change her forever, deposit a new layer in the sediment of her self, become part of what the not-yet-known future Vera will stand on.

"the shock of this knowledge was but the affirmation of some older shock which had been forgotten and laid over by the autumn leaves, the snow falling, the first buds of spring"

The crossing point is described explicitly here: the new shock affirms the old shock. The present confirms what was already deposited in the past. The self is not moving forward in time. The self is where time crosses itself, where the old shock and the new shock meet and recognize each other.
