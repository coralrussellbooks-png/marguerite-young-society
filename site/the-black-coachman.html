In the research: Chapter 12, Chapter 14

Definition from research

Chapter 12

  • The grandmother argued with “her voluble black coachman” about the clouds, and touched the stone faces only once she was blind.

Chapter 14

  • A symbolic and somewhat mythical figure associated with the family’s carriage and horses.
  • Represents continuity, memory, and the link between life and death.
  • Catherine’s wish was for the black coachman to be buried with the carriage and horses so that she might continue to “go driving” even after death.
  • His actual fate and whereabouts are uncertain, and this uncertainty fuels much of Catherine’s anguish.
  • Catherine has also defined the Black Coachman as her conscience.
  • “but what was a name?”: “What’s in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet” (Romeo and Juliet 2.2). Catherine says it of a servant whose name no one kept.
  • “The black coachman had had no wages for years”: The history of domestic work in the United States is deeply intertwined with the exploitation of Black women, men, and immigrant laborers through systemic racism, limited economic opportunities, and discriminatory labor practices. Enslaved Black women performed essential, unpaid domestic labor before emancipation, with conditions often leading to sexual abuse and the work continuing in a similar vein after abolition. For both Black and immigrant workers, this path was one of few available options, resulting in low pay, long hours, and a lack of protections, while modern policies continue to perpetuate a cycle of vulnerability.
  • “the black coachman with the white, rolling eyes”: The “white, rolling eyes” are the stock caricature of minstrel shows and early films, which made Black servants figures of fear and comedy.
  • “what had become of the color line?”: W. E. B. Du Bois: “The problem of the twentieth century is the problem of the color-line” (The Souls of Black Folk, 1903). Catherine has just turned the coachman white and the horses black.
  • Catherine insists the burial was “the coachman’s idea, not hers,” that “money had meant nothing to him,” though he had no wages for years, the old excuses of those who kept Black servants unpaid.
  • She cannot remember his name, only his “white, rolling eyes.”
