Chapter Eight: The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth · Across every book

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Madge Capehorn · Miss MacIntosh · Moses Hunnecker · Vera Cartwheel

The seventh pillar is where the philosophical system encounters its own population.

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling by Marguerite Young is populated with characters who exist at the edges of the categories that social and political life uses to organize its population. Miss MacIntosh herself, bald and one-breasted beneath the wig, is a woman whose body refuses the categories of feminine normality. Catherine, Vera's mother, is a woman whose consciousness refuses the categories of sanity and productivity. Esther Longtree, the cross-eyed waitress whose body swells with pregnancies that do not survive, is a woman whose fertility refuses to produce the outcomes that fertility is supposed to produce. Madge Capehorn, the pregnant girl on the Grey Goose bus driven by Moses Hunnecker, is a woman in transit between the categories of girl and mother, belonging fully to neither. The men who mourn without being permitted to mourn are men whose grief refuses the categories of masculine endurance.

These are not marginal figures in the sense of being incidental. They are the center of Young's philosophical attention, because the margins are where the categories show their seams, where the constructed nature of the social real becomes most visible, where the plural self cannot be forced into the singular mold without the forcing becoming undeniable. The marginal is not the exception in Young's system. It is the evidence.

Hannah Arendt's political philosophy, developed across The Human Condition and The Origins of Totalitarianism, offers the most rigorous available framework for understanding why the marginal is the evidence rather than the exception. Arendt argues that the political realm is not the realm of agreement but the realm of plurality. Political life, properly understood, is the life of beings who are fundamentally different from each other, who cannot be reduced to a common type or a common interest, and who nevertheless share a world. The space of appearance, Arendt's term for the political space, is the space in which plural beings appear to each other in their difference, not in their sameness.

Arendt's analysis of totalitarianism is directly relevant here. Totalitarian movements, she argues, destroy political life not primarily through violence, though they use violence, but through the destruction of plurality. They demand that every person become the same kind of person, that difference be eliminated, that the space of appearance be replaced by the uniform surface of ideological agreement. The destruction of plurality is the destruction of the political realm itself.

Young's novel enacts this analysis. The demand for the real Miss MacIntosh beneath the wig, the demand for the true self beneath the plurality, is a totalitarian demand in Arendt's sense. It destroys the space in which the plural self can appear honestly. It replaces the full complexity of the person with the simplified version that the demand can process. The community that holds difference without erasure is the community that refuses this demand.

The passengers on Moses Hunnecker's Grey Goose bus are not a community of shared identity. They are a collection of plural selves in transit, each carrying their own permanent grief and their own constructed reality, each appearing to the others in the partial visibility that the bus's shared space allows. What holds them together is not agreement. It is the shared condition of being in transit, of moving through a world that does not resolve, of carrying what they carry without the possibility of putting it down. The community of the bus is the community of witnesses to each other's plurality, not the community of people who have found their common ground.

Mikhail Bakhtin's theory of the dialogic offers a complementary framework. Bakhtin argues that meaning is not produced by a single voice speaking a single truth. It is produced in the encounter between voices, in the dialogue that occurs when different perspectives meet and respond to each other. Miss MacIntosh, My Darling by Marguerite Young is dialogic in Bakhtin's sense, but it pushes the principle further than Bakhtin's framework anticipates. The voices in Young's novel are not only the voices of different characters. They are the voices of different selves within a single character, the plural self in dialogue with its own plurality. Vera's voice is in dialogue with the Vera who was a child with Miss MacIntosh, and the Vera who is reconstructing that childhood from the Grey Goose bus, and the Vera who cannot be sure which of her memories are memories and which are the constructions of grief.

This is the seventh pillar's image: the Grey Goose bus as the model of community. Not the community that has arrived at its destination and agreed on what it found there. The community that is still in motion, still carrying its grief, still holding the difference of its members without demanding that they simplify.
