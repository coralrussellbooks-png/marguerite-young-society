Definition from research

Chapter 1

  • “passing like ships in the night”: The phrase comes from Henry Wadsworth Longfellow’s Tales of a Wayside Inn: “Ships that pass in the night, and speak each other in passing, / Only a signal shown and a distant voice in the darkness.” Young extends it to “ships manned by dead helmsmen, by ghostly crews.”

Chapter 2

  • New England spinsters like Susan B. Anthony and Louisa May Alcott, were famous. Spinsters in general were celebrated for their “uncompromising morality”. Spinster was an honorary term for a woman who earned money spinning wool. They were regarded as “highly moral and fully womanly creatures'' and many documented in their journals that they “remained unmarried not because of individual shortcomings but because they didn’t find the one ‘who could be all things to the heart”.

Chapter 3

  • The Pilgrim's Progress from This World, to That Which Is to Come is a 1678 Christian allegory written by John Bunyan. It is commonly regarded as one of the most significant works of Protestant devotional literature and of wider early modern English literature. It has been translated into more than 200 languages and has never been out of print. It appeared in Dutch in 1681, in German in 1703 and in Swedish in 1727. The first North American edition was issued in 1681. It has also been cited as the first novel written in English. According to literary editor Robert McCrum, "there's no book in English, apart from the Bible, to equal Bunyan's masterpiece for the range of its readership, or its influence on writers as diverse as William Hogarth, C. S. Lewis, Nathaniel Hawthorne, Herman Melville, Charles Dickens, Louisa May Alcott, George Bernard Shaw, William Thackeray, Charlotte Bronte, Mark Twain, John Steinbeck and Enid Blyton.” The lyrics of the hymn "To be a Pilgrim" are based on the novel.

Chapter 3

  • “be proved, and the truth was better than the lie, even should the lie seem truer than the truth, every lie containing a seed of truth in it, no doubt, but ours was the freedom to make a choice between the truth and the lie and the truth and the truth, to do what was good and right, leaving no uncertainty.” - The passage underscores that while lies may be seductive and intertwined with truth, it is the conscious choice to pursue what is good and true that defines human freedom and moral integrity. Many cultures and thinkers have expressed the belief that, despite the potential pain or discomfort of truth, it ultimately offers a more beneficial and sustainable foundation than deception. A common African proverb states, "The bitter truth is better than a sweet lie". This highlights the idea that even if the truth is difficult to hear, it is ultimately more valuable than a comforting falsehood. Writers and thinkers like Tolstoy have emphasized the importance of truth for moral clarity and authentic relationships. They argue that while lies might offer temporary comfort, they ultimately lead to unsustainable situations and undermine trust. In contrast to the idea of truth being better, the concept of the "big lie," popularized by Adolf Hitler, suggests that people can be persuaded to believe an enormous falsehood if it is presented with sufficient audacity and repeated often enough. This highlights the potential power of lies, even if they are ultimately destructive. Mark Twain famously noted that "a lie can travel halfway around the world while the truth is putting on its shoes". This emphasizes the speed and ease with which falsehoods can spread, compared to the effort often required to establish and disseminate truth. This phrase, attributed to Mark Twain as well, highlights how statistics can be manipulated to create misleading narratives. It underscores the potential for even seemingly factual information to be used to deceive. Jung’s work strongly emphasizes the importance of truth, particularly self-truth, for psychological well-being and individuation. He believed that facing one's shadow and confronting one's inner truths, even if difficult, is crucial for personal growth and wholeness. Lying, especially to oneself, can hinder this process and lead to psychological stagnation.

Chapter 7

  • Red Mayhew may refer to Richard Mayhew who served in the U.S. Marines during World War II, being among the first Black cadets accepted in 1942. He later shared the Congressional Gold Medal given to the Montford Point Marines (2012). Traveled and studied in Europe after his military service, visiting museums in Paris, Amsterdam, and Germany. Moved to New York in 1947 and began his formal art education. He studied at: Columbia University, The Brooklyn Museum School of Art, Pratt Institute, The Art Students League (though he wasn’t officially enrolled). Developed his skills under the guidance of American painters Edwin Dickinson and Reuben Tam. Interacted with influential artists at the Cedar Tavern, a famous New York hangout for Abstract Expressionists like Jackson Pollock, Willem de Kooning, and Franz Kline.
  • “the door of the coat closet where old Josh was locked in was locked from the outside”: Young loved murder mysteries and had a minor in criminology so she may have included the “locked-room mystery” when Madge gossips about Josh’s death. A locked-room murder is committed in circumstances under which it appeared impossible for the perpetrator to enter the crime scene, commit the crime, and leave undetected. The crime in question typically involves a situation whereby an intruder could not have left; for example the original literal “locked room”: a murder victim found in a windowless room locked from the inside at the time of discovery. Following other conventions of classic detective fiction, the reader is normally presented with the puzzle and all of the clues, and is encouraged to solve the mystery before the solution is revealed in a dramatic climax. The prima facie impression from a locked room crime is that the perpetrator is a dangerous, supernatural entity capable of defying the laws of nature by walking through walls or vanishing into thin air. The need for a rational explanation for the crime is what drives the protagonist to look beyond these appearances and solve the puzzle. Poe’s “The Murders in the Rue Morgue” (1841) is the first locked-room mystery, and John Dickson Carr’s The Hollow Man (1935) gives a famous “locked-room lecture.”
  • “the headless horsemen riding with their torches”: Washington Irving’s “The Legend of Sleepy Hollow” (1820).

Chapter 15

  • “Death loves a young maid and an old man.” - Ralph Waldo Emerson, “Sleep is not, death is not; Who seem to die Live. House you were born in, Friends of your spring-time, old man and young maid, Day's toil and it's guerdon, They are all vanishing, Fleeing to fables, Cannot be moored” It encapsulates the novel’s meditation on the inevitability of death across the full spectrum of human experience, highlighting both the fragility and the shared fate of all life.

Chapter 33

  • A printer's devil was a young apprentice in a printing establishment who performed a number of tasks, such as mixing tubs of ink and fetching type. Notable writers including Ambrose Bierce, Benjamin Franklin, Walt Whitman, and Mark Twain served as printer's devils in their youth.

Chapter 41

  • “immortal beauty” is part of Emerson’s Nature essay. The idea is that beauty is not just a sensory experience but a reflection of the spiritual and universal order of the universe. It is to be found in the interconnectedness of nature, the inherent goodness of the natural world, and the human ability to perceive and experience it. Beauty is not just about physical appearances but inhabits spiritual and moral qualities. Emerson emphasizes the role of the intellect in understanding the underlying harmony and beauty of nature. Emerson encouraged people to reconnect with nature and art. It captures the idea of a timeless, enduring essence of beauty and spirit that survives beyond physical life, reflecting the novel’s exploration of mortality, identity, and transcendence.

Chapter 54

  • Pinckney Street is in Boston, Massachusetts. Pinckney Street is a historic residential street in Boston's Beacon Hill neighborhood, known for its Federal-style architecture and its connection to a significant African American community that emerged in the area before the Civil War. One of the oldest homes on the street, the George Middleton House, was built in the late 1780s and is a testament to the street's long history. The area is notable for its role as a center for free Black residents and, later, as a place where people like Louisa May Alcott lived and wrote.

Chapter 61

  • Camden is a city in Camden County, in the U.S. state of New Jersey. It is part of the Delaware Valley metropolitan region. The city was incorporated on February 13, 1828. Walt Whitman spent his final years in Camden, where he died in 1892. He is buried in a tomb at Harleigh Cemetery in Camden.
