Intertextuality in MMMD

9. The Common Underworld

← Previous · Intertextuality in MMMD Home · Next →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

Allusions: Angels and Archangels · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest

Definition from research

Two references pass through more mouths, across more classes, than almost any others: Charon the ferryman and Gabriel's horn.

The bus-driver jokes about the dead with "not a cent with which to pay old Charon when they crossed the river in a leaking boat" (chapter 5). Mr. Spitzer carries a coin "in his purse for old Charon when he should make his last passage" (chapter 53). Catherine's house opens onto "the Stygian river flowing through a bedroom" (chapter 3). Cousin Hannah's travels pass "Charon's empty boat drifting on the midnight tide" (chapter 30). In the final chapter the shore itself is "the coast of Charon" (chapter 82).

Gabriel's horn travels the same way. The bus-driver will not leave his water hole "not even if Gabriel should blow his horn" (chapter 5). The doctor's circle speaks of "old angel Gabriel blowing his tin horn" (chapter 7). A stranger in chapter 68 ties "knots which none shall untie until Gabriel blows his horn." Mr. Spitzer imagines the dead "sleeping like this no doubt when Gabriel blew his horn" (chapter 82).

This is the strategy of the shared vocabulary. A Boston lawyer and an Iowa bus-driver hold almost nothing in common, yet they share one picture of death: the ferry, the fare, the final trumpet. The pagan ferryman and the Christian archangel sit side by side without conflict, because neither is held as doctrine. They are common speech, the way ordinary Americans talk about the end.

The strategy also shapes the novel's frame. The book begins on a bus crossing a river country in fog, driven by a man who jokes about Charon's fare. The reader is on the ferry from the first page. Young never says so. She lets the shared references do the work, which is how intertextuality operates throughout the novel: through what everyone already knows, spoken by people who do not know they are quoting.
