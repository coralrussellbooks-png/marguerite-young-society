Research notes: Chapter 10 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 10

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Solomon the Turtle

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The chapter opens with the narrator unable to visualize her own marriage, "neither my lover nor his rough, frost-bearded cheek", and immediately displaces the self outward into speculation about other weddings, other brides, other bridegrooms. The self cannot hold its own image and so proliferates into others.

Mr. Spitzer is the chapter's most explicit embodiment of the plural self. He sleeps in twin beds, his own and his dead brother's, and is "sometimes disturbed by the baffled feeling that he was two bodies sleeping in two separate beds, that there were two heads on two striped pillows". He does not resolve this. He whistles in the dark and migrates from bed to bed. The plural self is not diagnosed here; it is simply inhabited.

The dream wedding then performs the ultimate dissolution: Mr. Spitzer becomes the bride, Miss MacIntosh becomes the bridegroom, the preacher cannot determine "who was woman and who was man and who was beast and who was bird". The narrator, observing, notes: "I could see quite clearly though I was not myself at all". The witness is present but the self behind the witness has dissolved. This is not presented as crisis. It is presented as the condition of perception itself.

The narrator's post-dream reflection compounds this: she feels she has wronged Miss MacIntosh through her own dreaming, as though the boundary between the dreaming self and its objects is permeable, as though imagining someone is already an act performed upon them. The self bleeds outward. Its multiplicity is not contained within the skull.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter's central formal gesture is the dream that cannot be distinguished from waking. The narrator says: "I must have been dreaming when the wedding march started up" and then immediately: "it must be the continuation of my dream, as then I reasoned". The hedge, as then I reasoned, is crucial. It locates the reasoning inside the dream, which means the reasoning cannot adjudicate between dream and waking. The instrument of verification is itself contaminated.

When the narrator wakes, she performs a kind of inventory of the unreal: "The stars had not really fallen from the sky, and Mr. Spitzer had not married Miss MacIntosh, and I had not been the bridesmaid". But the triple negation reads less like reassurance than like a list of things that happened in a register that counts. The denial is too elaborate. It enacts the very persistence of what it denies.

The preacher's sermon deepens this. He moves without transition from wedding rites to burial rites, "the preacher had changed, even without the flicker of an eyelid, from the wedding rites to the rites for the burial of the dead", and "no one seemed amazed." The absence of amazement is the tell. In a world where illusion is primary, the collapse of categorical distinction between wedding and funeral is not a rupture. It is the baseline.

The cave here is not Plato's. There is no philosopher who has seen the sun. The preacher himself is lost: "May God forgive us, for we know not who or where we are". The larger cave is not more carefully lit. It is lit by snowflakes and a single missing candle, "There was no candle lighted", until suddenly the stars illuminate everything, at which point the narrator sees that the error was not the preacher's and not hers, but the dream's own, "the error of the dream which had begotten nothing and no marriage". Illusion generates its own internal error. There is no outside position from which to correct it.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The chapter's weather is literal and structural. The shapes of the dream wedding are "blurred by everlasting snow and ice as if the surface of the earth were some enormous, frosted wedding cake under the dark, blowing heavens". The frosted wedding cake is a collapsed image (celebration and burial, sweetness and cold, the festive and the permanent) and the word everlasting does the most work. This is not a storm that passes. It is the climate.

Mr. Spitzer is grief's primary figure. He has been waiting for his dead brother for the entirety of his existence. He wears clothes "of the style he had worn when his brother was alive". His clothes are a form of temporal arrest: grief worn on the body, grief as costume, grief as the refusal to let time move him forward. He told the narrator's mother that he "would be married to no one now but his brother worm who was under the ground". His grief has become his primary relationship. It is not a stage he is passing through. It is his permanent address.

The preacher's sermon accumulates grief without resolving it. The litany of the lost, "Where, my dearest heart, is Issachar? Where is Nepthalin? Where is Reuben who begot a horde? Where is Manasses? Where is Kansas? Where is Iowa?", is not a lament moving toward consolation. It is a list that keeps growing. Kansas and Iowa alongside the biblical patriarchs: the sacred and the ordinary are equally lost, equally unlocatable. Grief does not discriminate between the holy and the mundane. It takes everything.

The narrator's post-dream meditation on Miss MacIntosh touches grief obliquely but precisely: "What would it be like to be old and unloved? It was hard enough to be young and unloved as I was, to waste one's sweetness in the desert air, but at least, the future was mine, as then I thought, and she had no future". The phrase as then I thought performs the same function as as then I reasoned earlier; it marks the thought as already suspect, already past, already the kind of certainty that will not hold. The grief here is anticipatory: grief for Miss MacIntosh's unlived life, grief for the narrator's own unloved present, grief that accumulates across both of them in a single paragraph.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator's position throughout is that of the witness who cannot fully see. She says: "Though I could clearly hear, I could not clearly see, for the shapes were blurred by everlasting snow and ice". This is the precise condition of the Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act pillar: present, attentive, unable to resolve. She does not leave. She does not stop looking. She stays in the room, or the dream, and reports what partial vision yields.

The ethical weight of witnessing appears most clearly in the narrator's guilt about her dream. She has seen Miss MacIntosh in an undignified position (as the bridegroom with a waxed mustache, as the object of the preacher's confused ceremony) and she feels this as a violation: "What had I done to Miss MacIntosh, and how must I blush when I saw her next, for suppose she guessed my forbidden thoughts, and they were written on my face as plain as day!". The witness is not neutral. Seeing is already an act. The act of imagination is already a form of doing. The narrator understands that attention is not passive.

The preacher is a dark mirror of the Witness. He sees everything and nothing. He addresses the congregation as "Dearly beloved" while being unable to identify the parties before him. He speaks with total authority and total confusion simultaneously. He is the anti-Witness: present, verbose, and fundamentally refusing to see what is actually in front of him. His failure throws the narrator's partial, honest, blurred witnessing into relief.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The chapter is structurally recursive. The narrator dreams a wedding, wakes, reflects on the dream, imagines Miss MacIntosh's future, circles back to Miss MacIntosh's stated opinions about marriage, circles back to the narrator's own loneliness, arrives at the impulse to go see Miss MacIntosh, and is stopped by the locked door. Nothing is concluded. The thought returns to its beginning with what it has learned, which is not resolution but a slightly different orientation toward the same unresolved material.

The preacher's sermon is itself a recursive construction. It begins as a wedding ceremony, becomes a funeral, incorporates elements of both simultaneously, and ends with "Amen, amen, amen. Strike up the music! On with the dance". The amen and the dance instruction are not contradictions resolved. They are contradictions held in the same breath, the building never finished, the ceremony that contains its own ending and beginning simultaneously.

Mr. Spitzer's migration between beds is a nightly recursion. He does not choose one bed and stay. He returns, night after night, to the problem of his brother's absence, building the same incomplete structure of grief and presence, knowing it is provisional, returning to the beginning.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The wedding that is also a funeral and also neither is the chapter's central image of the unfinished as honesty. The preacher's ceremony does not conclude. Miss MacIntosh "did not reply" when addressed. The marriage does not take place. The narrator reflects afterward: "there had been neither the wedding nor the funeral". The double negation is not a failure of the dream. It is the dream's most honest statement: that the categories themselves (wedding, funeral, beginning, ending) are constructions that the experience refuses to honor.

The birthday cake that is never cut is a small, domestic enactment of the same principle. The celebratory form exists. The completion of the celebration does not occur. The narrator is hungry. The incompletion is not symbolic only; it is physical, embodied, felt as an ache.

Miss MacIntosh's refusal of marriage is framed, by the narrator, as a kind of ethical position: she "had no illusions" and was "so clear as to her necessary limitations". The narrator reads Miss MacIntosh's self-assessment as a form of integrity: the refusal to complete a story (courtship, marriage, happiness) that the facts do not support. Miss MacIntosh is, in this reading, the practitioner of Unfinished Is Honest. She will not pretend to a conclusion the evidence does not warrant.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Every figure in this chapter is marginal. Mr. Spitzer is the surviving brother, the one who was not preferred, the one who inhabits his own life as a kind of remainder. Miss MacIntosh is the old maid, the woman past the zenith, the one "no man in his senses would think of looking at twice". The narrator is the young, unloved, hungry girl who cannot visualize her own future.

The preacher's inability to distinguish gender, asking Mr. Spitzer if he will take "this man to be your lawful wedded husband" and asking Miss MacIntosh if she will take "this woman", is not merely comic. It is the ceremony's revelation of its own categorical instability. The institution of marriage depends on the categories of man and woman being clear and fixed. The preacher's confusion does not corrupt the ceremony. It reveals what the ceremony was always doing: imposing clarity on what is fundamentally unclear.

The wedding guests are: the narrator (who is not herself), the turtle Solomon, the red geranium, and an old empty shoe. This is the most precise image in the chapter. The marginal witnesses to a marginal event are themselves objects at the edge of personhood: a reptile, a plant, a discarded object. They are the evidence that the category of witness is as constructed as the category of bride.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The chapter's temporal structure is layered rather than linear. The narrator moves through: present wakefulness, dream, waking reflection on the dream, memory of Miss MacIntosh's statements, anticipation of Miss MacIntosh's future, memory of Miss MacIntosh's recent complaints, projection of tomorrow's reformed behavior, and return to the present locked door. These are not sequential. They press upward simultaneously.

Mr. Spitzer's dead brother is the chapter's most explicit figure of sedimentary time. The brother is under the ground (literally, in the earth) but he presses upward through every detail of Mr. Spitzer's existence: the twin beds, the old-fashioned clothes, the rejection of all future relationships. The dead brother is not in the past. He is in the sediment beneath Mr. Spitzer's present, still constituting it.

The preacher's geographical litany performs sedimentary time: "He has gone from Newport to Mystic, from Wellfleet to Buzzards Bay, from Boston to Boston. He has gone to Gloucester". The accumulation of place names (each a layer of movement, of history, of loss) does not arrive anywhere. Boston to Boston: the journey that returns to its origin having changed nothing except that it has happened. The sediment of travel without destination.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The preacher's language is the chapter's most sustained meditation on language as construction. His ceremony begins with the formula of the wedding rite, "Dearly beloved," the preacher said, "according to the words of the old text", and immediately begins to deviate. The deviation is not random. It is the old text's suppressed content surfacing: the wedding rite contains a funeral because marriage is an institution that acknowledges mortality ("till Death do us part"), and the preacher simply lets that suppressed content rise.

His pronouns collapse: he addresses Mr. Spitzer as the one who will take "this man" as husband, addresses Miss MacIntosh as the one who will take "this woman" as wife. The pronoun system that organizes the ceremony, that makes the ceremony legible as a ceremony, fails. When the pronouns fail, the ceremony fails. Language was doing more structural work than the participants realized.

The narrator's own language is marked by its own location. The repeated "as then I thought" and "as then I reasoned" are a narrator speaking from a later position, flagging her own past certainties as provisional. The language names its own temporal location. It refuses to speak from nowhere.

The preacher's list of what marriage requires the renunciation of, "all earthly goods, titles, claims, demands, estates, effects, all corporeal and incorporeal hereditaments, spheres of influence, feelings of neglect, golden crowns and thrones, heirlooms, appurtenances", is language enacting its own excess. The legal formula proliferates past usefulness into absurdity, and in that proliferation reveals that the legal formula was always a construction, always an attempt to enumerate what cannot be fully enumerated.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter lives entirely within the ordinary and the broken. The preacher invokes God, St. Michael, all the holy angels, the Day of Judgment, golden thrones, jewels, stars burning like lamps, the sea turning to glass, and all of this transcendent apparatus is delivered in the same breath as the information that "a mighty hurricane is blowing in from the Canary Islands or Hebrides this night of nights". The transcendent and the meteorological are grammatically equivalent. Neither is more real than the other.

The moment of genuine illumination in the chapter is not divine. It is stellar and sudden: "Then suddenly, in perfect silence, the manifold skies were illuminated, pointed star by pointed star". The illumination comes from within the natural world, from the immanent sky, and what it reveals is not truth but correction of error, not revelation of the divine but clarification of the dream's own internal mistake. The sacred is here, doing the work of perception, not transcendence.

Miss MacIntosh's silver dollar watch and waxed mustache, the ordinary objects of her ordinary life, are what she carries into the ceremony. She brings no veil, no flowers. Her sacredness, if she has any, is in the silver dollar, the watch, the umbrella. The immanent objects are her regalia.

The narrator's first feeling of pity for Miss MacIntosh is described as "the first embryonic emotion": a biological metaphor for a moral development. The transcendence here is entirely interior, entirely developmental, entirely located in the body of the narrator's feeling. Poor old soul: the word soul appears once, casually, in a phrase that is almost dismissive, and in that casualness it does more work than the preacher's entire invocation of the divine.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The chapter's final image is the locked door. The narrator wants to go to Miss MacIntosh. She is stopped. She is where two impulses cross: the impulse toward connection and the law of the other's privacy. She is constituted by what has been deposited before her (her mother's opium dreams, Mr. Spitzer's grief, Miss MacIntosh's discipline, the whole sediment of the house) and she is constituting the ground of her own future understanding, the maturity she promises herself for tomorrow. The structural position of the narrator throughout is the Crossing Point made explicit. She is the child of a mother lost to illusion and a father absent from the text. She is being formed by Miss MacIntosh, who is herself formed by a history the narrator does not fully know. The narrator's dream does not belong only to her; it contains Mr. Spitzer's grief, Miss MacIntosh's self-assessment, the preacher's confusion, the turtle Solomon. She is where all of this crosses.

The preacher's sermon contains the most precise statement of the Crossing Point: "As it was in the beginning, is now, and ever shall be, world without end". This is the liturgical formula for continuity, for the crossing of past and present and future in a single moment. The preacher delivers it in the middle of a ceremony that cannot determine whether it is a wedding or a funeral, which means the formula applies equally to both: the beginning and the end are the same structure, and you are where they cross.

The narrator's final thought before the locked door is about "all those wall-eyed phantoms, necromancers, shades, whirling dervishes who had no place" and "the old rapists lurking in the grey or purple sea fog": the threats that are both real and unreal, specific and vague. She is warned against wandering. She stays. The staying is not safety. It is the structural position of the one who is constituted by what came before and is constituting what comes after, unable to move forward, unable to return, holding the crossing point in the dark.
