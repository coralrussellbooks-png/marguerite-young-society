The Poem and Its Ending

Browning was Young's first master. At about eighteen she read all of his poems, The Ring and the Book among them, with her teacher Katharine Merrill Graydon in Indianapolis, who, Young said, had known him in London (The American Audio Prose Library Interview, 1983). She took her creed from him: “I believe with Browning that the poet is a reporter” (The Ruas Interview on Her Life and Miss MacIntosh). Her first prose book began as “a series of 60 ballads in a Robert Browning manner” (The Ruas and Schwartz Interview on Angel in the Forest), and at the end of her life, bedridden, she was writing dramatic monologues “modeled on Browning's monologues” (Susan Neville, “Where the Landscape Moved Like Waves”). At a conference in 1988, after her own reading, what she wanted to talk about was Browning, whose well-thumbed book, she said, had nurtured her (Marguerite Young, Our Darling, Part 2). When Childe Roland blows his horn in her novel, he comes from the poet she loved longest.

Robert Browning's 'Childe Roland to the Dark Tower Came' (1855) is a poem about a knight who has been searching for the Dark Tower his entire life. He travels through a landscape of horror and failure: blighted plains, a starved horse, a river where he fears to set his foot upon a dead man's cheek, a field that seems designed to break the spirit of anyone who crosses it. Every knight who attempted this quest before him has failed. Their faces appear to him at the end like witnesses to his arrival. He reaches the tower. He sets his slug-horn to his lips. He blows. The poem ends.

He does not enter. He does not win or lose in any conventional sense. He arrives at the object of his entire quest and the poem stops at the moment of announcement: I am here. The ultimate fate is ambiguous. What matters architecturally is the formal structure: the quest that ends not in conquest or defeat but in the act of arriving and announcing oneself to something that will not answer. The horn blows. No other sound has ever been heard there. The tower does not open. The poem ends.

This is the formal model underlying the novel's treatment of its central impossible rescue: Cousin Hannah attempting to pull Catherine out of her dark tower.

The Dark Tower in the Novel

The dark tower appears eleven times in the novel, in nine chapters, distributed across multiple characters and situations. It is never the same tower twice. It is always the same condition.

Vera lives in a dark tower. In Chapter 3 she dreams of “the dark prince charming who should carry me away and rescue me from this dark tower.” This is the fairy-tale register: the princess in the tower waiting for the rescuer. “But Miss MacIntosh knew not of this nor of anything else which had never happened.” The tower as imprisonment in fantasy, in the self's elaborate dreaming, guarded by a nursemaid who insists on the plain world outside. Catherine lives in a dark tower. Her bedroom is the novel's most consistent dark tower, where Cousin Hannah's tales bring “many Bengal lancers to dazzle my poor mother in her dark tower” (Chapter 30), where she entertains a thousand visitors and cannot be reached by anyone who arrives in the flesh. Cousin Hannah “had wanted to carry my mother away from this dark tower, to rescue her from opium dreams of the dead love” (Chapter 31). The tower here is not imprisonment by an external captor. It is a structure Catherine has built and furnished and inhabited by choice. It is the most comfortable and well-populated tower in the novel. It is also completely inaccessible.

The husband is lost near a dark tower. As Vera tells it in Chapter 30, Catherine's “poor husband” was “lost while walking between two frozen mountain peaks,” and she asks Cousin Hannah, the mountain climber, to “search for him in dark valleys, search for him where Childe Roland had blown his horn before a dark tower and where no other sound had ever been heard.” The lost husband is Childe Roland: the knight who reached the tower and was never heard from again. Catherine is waiting for him to return from the tower, not knowing whether he entered, whether he conquered, whether he blew his horn and was taken, whether he is still somewhere inside the walls.

The dead cry at dark towers. After Cousin Hannah's death, Mr. Spitzer imagines her still searching in Chapter 47: “Perhaps she still must wander, crying at many gates, knocking at many doors, arousing many watchmen in their dark towers, forever crying for the image of her love who had gone before her into the darkness.” The tower multiplies in death: every gate is now a dark tower, every door a place where the dead cry and the watchmen do not answer.

Cousin Hannah as Childe Roland

Mr. Spitzer composes Cousin Hannah's epitaph in Chapter 31: “Here lies the greatest captain who fought against all nations and rescued many ladies from their dark towers and cruel lords but could not rescue herself.” The formulation is exact. She rescued many ladies from their dark towers. Plural. She was a rescuer of prisoners from towers as her life's work. And she could not rescue herself, which means she too was in a dark tower of some kind, though the novel leaves the nature of hers implicit in the military language: “She perished in a domestic war. She died in her own old tent bed set in a windy place with no aide-de-camp to see her go.”

The journey to Catherine's tower has the structure of Browning's poem throughout. Cousin Hannah travels through the failed landscape of the suffrage movement: the long winter marches, the desert treks, the battles against Turkish sultans and African kings, the journeys into forbidden Mecca. She passes, as Roland passes, through a territory littered with the evidence of previous failures: “those great ladies she had tried to carry away from monstrous loves, mirrors, clouds, doors” (Chapter 43). She arrives at Catherine's tower. She sets herself to the task. She blows her horn. No other sound has ever been heard there. The tower does not open. The poem ends.

Cousin Hannah is dead before the novel begins. She reached the tower. She announced herself. She is still announcing herself: her detonations of rage are still shaking the house: “The lace curtains billowed through the vast rooms, and the glass prisms shook with the memory of that wrath which would never spend itself” (Chapter 31). She blew her horn and the poem ended and the ringing is still in the walls.

The Tower That Cannot Be Entered

Browning's Dark Tower does not open when Roland blows his horn because the poem does not grant the tower an interior. The quest leads to arrival at an opaque exterior. What is inside the tower is outside the poem's knowledge. The tower's power is that no one can get inside it: it is the destination that can be reached but not entered, the object of the entire quest that refuses to resolve the quest by being openable.

Catherine's tower is the same structure. It can be visited from outside: many evenings Mr. Spitzer “would sit in the great wing chair which slightly rocked as if he were coming in on a wave and would continue whispering, whispering melodiously to himself” (Chapter 55). The visitors come in their hundreds, the imaginary ones. But no one enters the tower's actual interior: Catherine's inner life, the nature of her love for her dead husband, the content of the grief that has organized the house around her bed. These remain opaque throughout the novel. Vera witnesses from outside. Mr. Spitzer witnesses from outside. The reader witnesses from outside. The tower is described at immense length. It is never entered.

The witness cannot enter the tower. The witness can only blow the horn: announce their presence, offer their attention, record what the exterior looks like. Catherine is not withholding from the witnesses. She is simply in a place that cannot be entered from outside. The love that organized her life is not available for inspection. It is inside the tower. The tower is what it is. The horn rings against the walls and no other sound has ever been heard there and the poem ends and the ringing continues.

The Radical Immanence Reading

The dark tower thread is Pillar 2 (Reality Is Constructed) in its most concrete architectural form. The tower is where illusion lives: the opium dreams, the imaginary visitors, the dead husband who is always about to return from somewhere in the Alps. It is not a lesser reality than the world outside the tower. For Catherine, it is the primary reality. The world outside the tower, the world Cousin Hannah is trying to pull her toward, is the secondary reality: the place where time passes and people age and the drugs run out and the clocks do not stop.

Childe Roland blows his horn at the boundary between the two realities. He has arrived from the outside world with all its blighted landscapes and failed knights. He has arrived at the place where the quest was always pointing. He announces himself at the threshold. The tower does not open because the threshold cannot be crossed. The two realities are not convertible. You cannot rescue someone from a primary reality by pulling them into a secondary one. You can only stand at the boundary and blow your horn and let the ringing carry through the walls and know that it is heard, even if no other sound comes back, even if the poem ends there, even if the ringing is still in the prisms decades later, still shaking the house.

The ending is also Pillar 6 (Unfinished Is Honest). Browning stops at the threshold: “Dauntless the slug-horn to my lips I set, / And blew” (Robert Browning, “Childe Roland to the Dark Tower Came,” 1855, stanza XXXIV). He does not say what is inside the tower or whether anyone answers, and he does not pretend to know. Young keeps the same honesty at Catherine's door. Vera can tell Catherine's visions, but she cannot bring Catherine out of them, and the novel does not pretend she can.
