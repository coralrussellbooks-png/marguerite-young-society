Chapter 4

  • “she would go to Boise or Reno, where she had gone to get her divorces”: In 1931 Nevada cut the residency required for a divorce to six weeks, and Idaho soon matched it. Reno became the divorce capital of America, and its “divorce ranches” housed women waiting out their weeks.

Chapter 6 · Marriage and Gender Roles

  • Marriage is depicted as a hollow, oppressive institution.
  • The double standard of fidelity and suffering between men and women is critiqued.
  • Madge’s role as a wife and mother is tragic rather than fulfilling.

Chapter 9 · Marriage and Gender Roles

  • Marriage is portrayed as the “normal state” and a natural institution sanctioned by God and nature.
  • Miss MacIntosh emphasizes the importance of preparation for marriage and warns against sexual dangers.
  • Vera reflects on the mystery of marriage, love, and the unknown nature of her future husband.

Chapter 10 · Marriage and Identity

  • The confusion and inversion of roles in the dream wedding hint at fluidity in gender and societal roles.
  • Marriage is portrayed ambiguously, blending union and separation, life and death.
  • The preacher’s confusing vows suggest the complexity and sometimes absurdity of social rituals.

Chapter 16

  • “The fierce, exultant pride that was mine”: In “What Is It with Women and Horses?” (Intelligent Horsemanship, 2019), Dr. Rosie Jones-McVey, drawing on her PhD research among British horsewomen, writes: “In Victorian novels, as well as in the more modern genre of pony-girl books, it is often aboard a horse that girls find their wings, often proving some snot-nosed boy wrong in the process, or enabling them to escape various romantic dramas and find solace in the barn. Just like the pony-girl books, many of my participants’ life stories entwined horseyness with coming-of-age stories. Horses were the means through which many women first acted in a truly independent way, often pursuing equestrian endeavours despite/without their parents, or else, proudly taking up adult-like responsibilities in being the teacher, pilot, and carer of their ponies, rather than the one being looked after and told what to do.” She argues that horses give many women a space for independence and self-assertion that their everyday lives do not.

Chapter 17

  • “this old man who had only one breast”: Halsted’s radical mastectomy (1894), which removed the breast and the muscles beneath it, was the standard treatment for breast cancer for most of the twentieth century. Chapter 19 confirms the loss. In Chapter 10, Vera imagined “claws upon her forehead like the claws of a lobster,” and cancer takes its name from the crab.

Chapter 17 · Power and Vulnerability

  • Miss MacIntosh’s physical power contrasts with her ghostly, fragile appearance.
  • Vera, though young and vulnerable, refuses to be defeated quietly.
  • The battle becomes a metaphor for the fight between opposing forces within or external threats.

Chapter 18

  • “Would that my lover had been a man!”: Vera’s realizations this night seem to be described again as a sexual act, something very intimate and physical.
  • “The left breast is already gone”: Miss MacIntosh talks about losing her left breast. William Halsted performed his first radical mastectomy in New York in 1882 and described the operation in 1894. There is no clear record of how earlier women survived the operation, or for how long.
  • “A good husband makes a good wife”: Miss MacIntosh in this case is taking on the role of the groom in molding Vera to be the bride.
  • “Where were her red jersey bloomers?”: Amelia Bloomer’s reform dress.
  • “a man’s bedroom was a man’s castle”: “A man’s house is his castle” (Sir Edward Coke, 1628).
  • “I needed a father to guide me”: Miss MacIntosh believes Vera should have had a father and this might refer to a husband since in earlier marriages a daughter is passed from her father to her husband as her guardian.

Chapter 19

  • “her red jersey bloomers”: The bloomers Miss MacIntosh asked for at dawn in Chapter 18.
  • “one breast had been amputated”: The Halsted operation of Chapters 17 and 18.

Chapter 19 · Physical and Emotional Vulnerability

  • The amputated breast and the varying skin colors symbolize physical trauma and emotional sensitivity.

Chapter 20

  • “There was no crowning glory of woman’s hair”: “If a woman have long hair, it is a glory to her: for her hair is given her for a covering” (1 Corinthians 11:15).

Chapter 20 · Missing Breast

  • The missing breast is a stark physical symbol of loss and incompleteness, described as “like a door knob,” emphasizing its unnatural absence.

Chapter 21

  • “a brassiere with a wad of cloth which had been Concealed”: The “breast pad” Vera imagined in Chapter 20. After a mastectomy, women padded the brassiere with cloth. Young’s capital “Concealed” is in the text.
  • “Dancing is never to be encouraged by this old lady”: Miss MacIntosh vividly expresses sexism through her character by highlighting her pragmatic, no-nonsense attitude toward the limitations imposed on women, her rejection of illusions, and her critical view of the societal roles prescribed to women. Miss MacIntosh’s insistence on practicality and self-reliance reflects a critique of traditional sexism by underscoring how women are often expected to conform to narrow roles, suppressing their individuality and desires. Miss MacIntosh’s harshness can be read as internalized sexism, reflecting how women may police themselves and others to survive within a patriarchal system that offers them limited agency. This portrayal critiques the constraints sexism imposes on women’s lives, identities, and dreams by grounding Miss MacIntosh as a figure of practical wisdom and resilience who nonetheless embodies the burdens of gendered limitations. It sums up how Miss MacIntosh has internalized sexism where she takes sexist behaviors and attitudes about women and projects them onto herself and Vera in the form of fear, envy, jealousy, suspicion, resentment, rage, anxiety, lack of self-esteem and confidence. Miss MacIntosh’s greatest fear is that Vera will become and get to do everything society expects a ‘normal’ woman to be and do, something Miss MacIntosh will never achieve.

Chapter 22

  • “a needle-workers’ strike or a new garment union”: In the “Uprising of the 20,000” (1909), shirtwaist makers in New York, most of them young immigrant women, struck for months. The International Ladies’ Garment Workers’ Union was founded in 1900.
  • “every woman’s privilege and duty”: Women’s unpaid labor is the work that women do without financial compensation, such as cooking, cleaning, and caring for children. It’s often undervalued and underappreciated. Globally, women spend 2.5 times as many hours on unpaid care work as men (UN Women, “Care work keeps the world running”). Unpaid labor limits women’s time for paid work and can lead to longer hours. Unpaid labor can have negative emotional, mental, and physical health impacts on women.
  • “the best baseball player in little old What Cheer”: Women have been playing baseball as long as men have. Their long connection with the game began in the 1860s and has continued through the efforts of individual pioneers like Amanda Clement, Jackie Mitchell, Toni Stone, Maria Pepe, and Ila Borders (American Girls Baseball, “The History of Women in Baseball”). The All-American Girls Professional Baseball League played from 1943 to 1954.
  • “shirts going in job lots, slightly damaged by fire and flood”: May refer to the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire in 1911 in New York City which killed 146 workers because a door to one stairway was locked, and the fire escape collapsed. Firefighters’ ladders couldn’t reach the upper floors. Workers, mostly all women, jumped to their deaths. The tragedy led to New York’s Factory Investigating Commission and its safety laws (1911-15).

Chapter 23

  • “of marriage, the sacred union”: Women’s rights in the US were restricted during marriage: married women were legally subordinate to their husbands; they could not own property, keep their own wages, or enter into contracts; they had no rights to their children. Under coverture, “the husband and wife are one person in law” (Blackstone, 1765). New York’s Married Women’s Property Act came in 1848.
  • “an ugly woman was safe in her husband’s arms”: Only women are called ugly, because the feminine beauty ideal in every country holds that beauty is a woman’s most important asset.
  • “a man had married a man, and a woman had married a woman, and no one had been the wiser”: Womanless marriages were staged as community events and fundraisers. It is a “most unique and attractive entertainment,” the Houston Post reported on May 27, 1917. “Some of the most prominent men in the city will be in it. It is filled with fun from beginning to end and every city or town in which it has been given there have been record breaking audiences, even standing room being at a premium.” In nineteenth-century New England, two women living together as a couple were said to be in a “Boston marriage.”

Chapter 26

  • “There had been heirs who had been heiresses, heiresses who had been heirs”: Women’s property rights have expanded over time in the United States, but they have been restricted by gender stereotypes and laws. Under the English common law of coverture, a married woman’s property passed to her husband. In 1718, Pennsylvania’s Feme Sole Traders Act let the wives of absent mariners run businesses in their own names. In 1848, New York passed the Married Women’s Property Act, which allowed women to keep their property when they married. By the end of the Civil War, 29 states had passed some version of a Married Women’s Property Act, yet as late as 1887 a third of the states still did not let married women control their own earnings. (Wikipedia) Enslaved women could own nothing, not even themselves, and free women of color were often shut out of these rights in practice. Gender stereotypes and ideologies have historically been barriers to women’s property rights.
  • “held it against him that he did not rescue her, carry her away as Peron might have done”: Catherine is expecting a man to save her, which is the damsel in distress. This is a narrative device in which one or more men must rescue a woman who has been kidnapped or placed in other peril. The “damsel” is often portrayed as beautiful, popular, and of high social status; she is usually depicted as a princess in works with fantasy or fairy tale settings. Kinship, love, lust or a combination of those motivate the male protagonist to initiate the narrative. Critics have linked the helplessness of these women to societal views that women as a group need to be taken care of by men and treated nicely. Throughout the history of the trope, the role of the woman as the victim in need of a male savior has remained constant, but her attackers have changed to suit the tastes and collective fears of the period: “monsters, mad scientists, Nazis, hippies, bikers, aliens…” (Wikipedia)

Chapter 30

  • “never worn the white plume of cowardice”: This is a reference to the White Feather Campaign of World War I, begun in August 1914 by Admiral Charles Fitzgerald, in which women in Britain handed white feathers to men in civilian dress to shame them into enlisting before conscription came in 1916. The symbol came from cockfighting, where a white feather in a gamecock’s tail was taken as a sign of poor breeding and a poor fighter. It is the subject of A. E. W. Mason’s 1902 novel The Four Feathers, whose hero receives white feathers when he resigns his commission before his regiment goes to war. (Wikipedia) Henry of Navarre’s white plume, by contrast, was a sign of courage: “Rally to my white plume” (Ivry, 1590).
  • “She had been a great archer in Persia, a great dueler in France, an expert with sword and gun in Rumania”: Cousin Hannah takes up sports and roles reserved for men in her time: archer, dueler, mountain climber, yachtsman, explorer. She is a suffragette and adventurer who defies conventional female roles through her militant activism and boundary-crossing journeys, a contrast to the passive, isolated femininity of other characters, and her vitality counterbalances the decay and dream states that afflict them.

Chapter 30 · The Feminine and the Skirt

  • Repeated references to skirts, including the loss of a skirt as symbolic sorrow and redemption.
  • Cousin Hannah’s life is linked metaphorically to skirts, suggesting themes of femininity, identity, and transformation.
  • “Cousin Hannah’s entire history, it seemed now, had been a history of skirts.”

Chapter 31

  • “Death snatched away her beautiful bride”: Young’s other source for Cousin Hannah was a story told by Charles Studin, a founder of the NAACP, about a suffragist “battle-axe” who fought men all her life, “one of the original bloomer girls,” yet for forty years brought home sealed trunks that proved at her death to hold “a complete bridal trousseau” each. “She had a dream too” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). In another telling the lawyer found “fifty wedding gowns,” and Young added that she “had read the histories of mountain climbers, of suffragette captains, of travelers to the Middle East” (Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs, “A Conversation with Marguerite Young,” Review of Contemporary Fiction 23, no. 1, Spring 2003 (1989 original)).

Chapter 32

  • “the misogynist, the woman hater”: A misogynist is a person who dislikes, despises, or is strongly prejudiced against women. Female misogynists (internalized sexism/oppression) are characterized by exalting the feminine ideal, favoring traditional gender roles, looking down on women who are not feminine, self-loathing, and a belief that she is superior to other women or in constant competition with them. There is a three-tiered system for patriarchy. Patriarchy is the system of social organization that favors and empowers men. Sexism is the code of conduct or rules that are established for women. Misogyny is the policing or enforcement of these rules on women (Kate Manne, Down Girl: The Logic of Misogyny, Oxford University Press, 2017). Thus women can be misogynist if they are helping to police or enforce what women can and cannot do. Looking at all of this it can be determined that no, Cousin Hannah was not misogynist for wanting women to have independence or the right to vote.
  • “She had left him at the church door with a white bouquet in his hands”: Young told Studs Terkel that when Cousin Hannah “abandons the bridegroom at the church door,” “she goes to a numerologist who advises her as to how she should spell her name in the future and adds the hyphen just at the time when she dramatically becomes a hyphenated personality” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). The hyphen is the one in Freemount-Snowden.

Chapter 34

  • “Was not marriage, as that great suffrage captain had always said, the death of a woman’s soul”: Psychology has researched the ways marriage changes husband and wives. In previous chapters, the differences for women in marriage have been discussed.

Chapter 34 · Marriage and Loss

  • Catherine’s reflections on her marriage are fragmented and surreal. She imagines multiple versions of her wedding (married in white, black, at a funeral, by a dog, or not at all) signifying confusion, loss, and the elusive nature of commitment and love.
  • Marriage is portrayed as a metaphorical death of the soul, supported by Cousin Hannah’s cynical view that marriage leads to sacrifice and the wasting of a woman’s talents.

Chapter 34 · Female Strength and Defiance

  • Cousin Hannah embodies resilience, independence, and courage. She is portrayed as a warrior-like figure, unyielding and untamed, contrasting with Catherine’s vulnerability.
  • Cousin Hannah’s disdain for men and her distrust of Catherine’s whimsical nature highlight different approaches to womanhood and survival.

Chapter 35

  • “the triumph of woman over man, even of beauty over strength”: Joan Smith’s Misogynies discusses the pervasive nature of hatred and discrimination against women in society, exploring cultural, social, and psychological roots of misogyny (Joan Smith, Misogynies: Reflections on Myths and Malice, Faber and Faber, 1989). The novel vividly portrays the suffering, marginalization, and complex interior lives of women like Catherine, Esther Longtree, and Mrs. Hogden, who each embody different facets of women’s oppression and resilience. The presence of misogyny is implicit in their isolation, the betrayals they endure, and their struggles to assert identity and selfhood. Miss MacIntosh’s pragmatic, no-nonsense character challenges the illusions and fantasies that trap women like Catherine, reflecting resistance to the roles imposed by a misogynistic society. Her disdain for frivolity and self-pity contrasts with the more vulnerable or victimized female figures, highlighting the varied responses to misogyny. Smith emphasizes how misogyny permeates both public and private spheres, a theme echoed in the novel’s blending of intimate psychological struggles with broader social critiques, such as the suffrage movement represented by Cousin Hannah and the economic hardships faced by characters like Esther Longtree.
  • “She had wished to overthrow man, both as to the marital state”: The suffrage movement did not oppose marriage. The anti-suffrage movement opposed suffrage because it feared that women would be “masculinized” and that this in turn would “feminize” men. Under coverture, after marriage the man and woman became one legal unit that the man controlled. Women in many cases were not allowed to own property, sign contracts, earn a salary, or enter education without their husbands’ permission.
  • “paternal domination, the regnancy of the past and of the future”: Kate Millett’s Sexual Politics is a foundational feminist text analyzing the ways patriarchal power operates through sexual and social relationships, emphasizing how gender roles and power imbalances shape personal and political dynamics (Kate Millett, Sexual Politics, Doubleday, 1970). The novel portrays complex gender dynamics, especially through Vera’s mother Catherine and Miss MacIntosh. Catherine inhabits a world of illusion and decay, surrounded by hallucinations and dominated by male absences and betrayals. Miss MacIntosh, by contrast, embodies a practical, no-nonsense counterpoint to Catherine’s dreamlike passivity, and she strongly critiques illusions and instability. This tension reflects the gendered power struggles Millett describes, though in a symbolic and psychological mode. Cousin Hannah, the suffragette figure in the novel, represents a militant, radical feminist presence, dedicated to overthrowing patriarchal domination, which aligns with Millett’s critique of male control over women’s bodies and lives. Cousin Hannah’s battles for women’s freedom and her rejection of traditional feminine roles resonate with Millett’s political feminism. The novel’s exploration of pregnancies, stillbirths, and female suffering (notably through Esther Longtree’s story) touches on the control of women’s sexuality and reproduction, key concerns in Sexual Politics.
  • “an organizer of chaos”: Amanda Vajskop reads the Babylonian Enuma Elish and the Genesis creation story, in which male creators take over woman’s part in bringing forth life and women are cast as destabilizing and dangerous, a fear that patriarchy uses to keep women subordinate (Amanda Vajskop, “Finding Patterns in the Chaos: Woman as Chaos Agent in Creation Myths,” Denison Journal of Religion 5, article 7, 2005). Vera’s narrative continuously navigates between dreamlike confusion and the search for meaning or “ultimate harmony.” Miss MacIntosh represents order and common sense amid the chaos of Catherine’s opium-induced visions and the fragmented lives of other characters. The novel portrays characters with fragmented selves: Vera’s own complex consciousness, Miss MacIntosh’s evolving physical and psychological state (including her baldness and amputated breast), and the multiple personae like Cousin Hannah and Mrs. Hogden. The novel’s intense focus on female embodiment, sexuality, motherhood, loss, and survival complements critical perspectives on misogyny and sexual politics. Cousin Hannah, “an organizer of chaos,” takes up the part these myths assign to woman and turns it against the order men have made.

Chapter 35 · Freedom and Emancipation

  • Cousin Hannah embodies the fight for women’s rights and freedom from traditional roles.
  • Her life is a metaphor for breaking boundaries: social, geographic, and existential.
  • The suffrage movement is portrayed as her husband, her master, and her true love.

Chapter 36

  • “Was she become a man”: In Virginia Woolf’s Orlando (1928), the hero wakes as a woman: “Orlando had become a woman—there is no denying it.”

Chapter 36 · The Role of Women and Feminine Power

  • Cousin Hannah represents a powerful, rebellious feminine figure who challenges societal and cosmic orders.
  • There is tension between masculine and feminine realms, e.g., Catherine’s picture of a Moslem Paradise where women are absent.
  • Catherine’s contemplation of womanhood and power in death questions traditional gender roles and metaphysical status.
  • “For if a woman should be in heaven, it should not be heaven long, my mother thought.”

Chapter 37

  • “as man became woman with her heaving breasts”: Gender as a social construct refers to the socially created roles, behaviors, expressions, and identities associated with being a woman, man, boy, or girl. It’s not inherently determined by biology, but rather shaped by cultural norms, societal expectations, and historical contexts. This means gender is not fixed and can vary across cultures and time. In short, it is not a biological reality: it is socially created, varies across cultures, is dynamic and fluid, and is distinct from biological sex. Philosophical thought on gender goes back to ancient Greece, where Pythagoras’s table of opposites set male against female (Aristotle, Metaphysics 1.5) and Plato argued that women could be guardians (Republic 5). Later came John Locke, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Judith Sargent Murray (“On the Equality of the Sexes,” 1790), and Mary Wollstonecraft (A Vindication of the Rights of Woman, 1792). The chapter and novel present gender as a fluid and changeable construct rather than a rigid, innate fact, using metaphorical and dreamlike language to question fixed dichotomies between male and female, self and other, life and death.

Chapter 37 · Feminine Power and Resistance

  • Cousin Hannah, a suffrage captain, symbolizes strength, resistance, and an indomitable spirit.
  • Her death and burial contrast with her bold life, and her story reflects the struggle for identity and agency beyond traditional roles.
  • Cousin Hannah’s adventures in exotic lands and her defiance of powerful men underscore themes of female empowerment and autonomy.

Chapter 39

  • “those hidden from history”: History is the study and the documentation of the past. Events before the invention of writing systems are considered prehistory. History is an umbrella term comprising past events as well as the memory, discovery, collection, organization, presentation, and interpretation of these events. Historians seek knowledge of the past using historical sources such as written documents, oral accounts, art and material artifacts, and ecological markers (Wikipedia). Many historians believe that the production of history is embedded with bias because events and known facts in history can be interpreted in a variety of ways. Constantin Fasolt suggested that history is linked to politics by the practice of silence itself (Constantin Fasolt, The Limits of History, University of Chicago Press, 2004). According to Michel-Rolph Trouillot, the historical process is rooted in the archives, therefore silences, or parts of history that are forgotten, may be an intentional part of a narrative strategy that dictates how areas of history are remembered (Michel-Rolph Trouillot, Silencing the Past: Power and the Production of History, Beacon Press, 1995). Historical omissions can occur in many ways and can have a profound effect on historical records. Information can also purposely be excluded or left out accidentally. Gerda Lerner, a twentieth century historian who focused much of her work on historical omissions involving women and their accomplishments, explained the negative impact that these omissions had on minority groups. Her books Black Women in White America: A Documentary History (Pantheon Books, 1972), The Majority Finds Its Past: Placing Women in History (Oxford University Press, 1979), and Why History Matters: Life and Thought (Oxford University Press, 1997) have been used to examine women’s history. Women’s history is the study of the role that women have played in history and the methods required to do so. It includes the study of the history of the growth of women’s rights throughout recorded history, personal achievement over a period of time, the examination of individuals and groups of women of historical significance, and the effect that historical events have had on women (Wikipedia). Howard Zinn, in A People’s History of the United States (Harper and Row, 1980), presented what he considered to be a different side of history from the more traditional, fundamentally nationalist glorification of the country. Zinn portrays a side of American history that can largely be seen as the exploitation and manipulation of the majority by rigged systems that hugely favor a small aggregate of elite rulers from across the orthodox political parties. Zinn inspired Roxanne Dunbar-Ortiz to write An Indigenous Peoples’ History of the United States (Beacon Press, 2014). It is the third of a series of six ReVisioning books which reconstruct and reinterpret U.S. history from marginalized peoples’ perspectives.
  • “Equalities, which had always been innate”: Gender fluid philosophy challenges the traditional binary view of gender, suggesting that gender is not fixed but rather a spectrum or a dynamic process that can change over time. This fluidity can apply to both gender identity and gender expression, meaning how a person presents themselves to the world. Simone de Beauvoir in, The Second Sex, was foundational in distinguishing between biological sex and social gender, laying the groundwork for later explorations of gender as a social construct. The novel embraces gender fluid philosophy primarily through its poetic and symbolic depiction of identity as unstable, multiple, and mutable. Characters traverse and blur gender boundaries; metaphors of transformation underscore the ephemeral nature of identity; and the narrative challenges fixed binaries by portraying a world where selves, including gendered selves, are in constant flux. This fluidity is not articulated in explicit gender theory terms but is deeply woven into the novel’s exploration of selfhood, embodiment, and the interplay between reality and illusion.
  • “a blushing coward who would leave woman to fend for herself”: Research about masculinity expanded in the 1970s onward. Julián Marías wrote in his book Metaphysical Anthropology (1971) that man’s condition is insecurity, neediness, ignorance, indecision and helplessness. However, males are seen as having the main quality of strength. If he doesn’t then he feels he is inferior and must make up for this lack. Male strength may be physical, but can also consist in cunning, intelligence, prestige, experience, authority, aplomb, capacity of expression, aptitude for revealing or feigning those faculties. The paradox according to Marias is that man is ignorant, weak and threatened which leads to man’s pretension that he is the source of knowledge, strength, power and security. Joseph Pleck examined masculinity by doing a meta analysis of the male role starting with the 1930s and found it to consist of numerous instances of blatant misrepresentation of data, twisted reinterpretations of disconfirming results, misogyny, homophobia, and class bias (Joseph H. Pleck, The Myth of Masculinity, MIT Press, 1981). Alan G. Miller examined the myth of masculinity in patriarchal society as men are supposed to be competitive, diligent, disciplined, level-headed, strong, virile, controlled, successful (Alan G. Miller, “The Myth of Masculinity: A Panel,” American Journal of Psychoanalysis 33, no. 1, 1973, 65-67). Women, if considered at all, were presumed to be the opposite if for no reason other than no one ever asked them. Thomas J. Gerschick and Adam S. Miller later studied how men with physical disabilities come to terms with these ideals (Thomas J. Gerschick and Adam S. Miller, “Coming to Terms: Masculinity and Physical Disability,” in Men’s Health and Illness: Gender, Power, and the Body, ed. Donald Sabo and David Frederick Gordon, Sage, 1995). Young disparages the stereotypical feminine virtues as well.

Chapter 40

  • “her furies against archaeologists with pick and spade”: Feminist archaeology employs a feminist perspective in interpreting past societies. It often focuses on gender, but also considers gender in tandem with other factors, such as sexuality, race, or class. Feminist archaeology has critiqued the uncritical application of modern, Western norms and values to past societies. It is additionally concerned with increasing the representation of women in the discipline of archaeology, and reducing a masculine bias within the field. In archaeology this includes: focusing on presumed male roles such as hunter, warrior, chief, and farmers; under-analyzing activities considered to be in the female domain by western tradition; interpreting data from the perspective of middle-age, middle-class, western white men (Margaret W. Conkey and Janet D. Spector, “Archaeology and the Study of Gender,” Advances in Archaeological Method and Theory 7, 1984).

Chapter 42

  • “Epithalamiums sadder still”: The epithalamium, from the Greek for “at the bridal chamber,” was sung outside the bride’s door. Edmund Spenser’s Epithalamion (1595) celebrates his own wedding. Mr. Spitzer finds such songs “sadder still” than elegies.
  • “camp-followers”: Women followed armies as cooks, laundresses, and nurses. Mary Ludwig Hays, remembered as “Molly Pitcher,” is said to have taken her husband’s place at a cannon at Monmouth in 1778.
  • “it was the gentleman who left the bride at the church door”: A jilted woman could sue for breach of promise. Indiana, Young’s home state, became the first to abolish such suits in 1935, by a law sponsored by Roberta West Nicholson, and other states followed with “heart balm” laws.

Chapter 43

  • “Is that my love, my skirt moving through clouds”: Skirt has long been slang for a woman, as in “a bit of skirt.” The skirt Hannah lost “so long ago” may be both the garment she gave up for the cause and the woman she lost. See Young’s account of the bridal trousseaus under “a secret woman, a bride” in the Chapter 42 resources.

Chapter 44

  • “splitting her apart into the lover and the loved”: In Plato’s Symposium, Aristophanes tells how the first humans were split in two, so that each half seeks its other: “The women who are a section of the woman do not care for men, but have female attachments” (Symposium 191e, trans. Benjamin Jowett, 1871).
  • “this old maid”: In Henry James’s The Bostonians (1886), the Boston suffragist Olive Chancellor loves the young speaker Verena Tarrant. Two women who made a home together came to be called a Boston marriage.

Chapter 45

  • “her acolyte, her beautiful girl”: See Aristophanes’s myth in the Symposium and The Bostonians in the Chapter 44 resources.
  • “We were two lone women. There was no man.”: This is Hannah’s confession, and the secret Mr. Spitzer will keep. See the bridal trousseaus Young described to Studs Terkel under “a secret woman, a bride” in the Chapter 42 resources.

Chapter 47

  • “Was she the bridegroom? Was she the bride?”: See Aristophanes’s myth in the Symposium in the Chapter 44 resources and “a secret woman, a bride” in the Chapter 42 resources.

Chapter 49

  • “only the female endures”: “So God created man in his own image, in the image of God created he him; male and female created he them” (Genesis 1:27); Mr. Spitzer’s “female and female created He them” rewrites it. Women are often depicted as enduring various forms of hardship, including prolonged stress and instability, without succumbing to their circumstances. Some believe that societal expectations and responsibilities, like caregiving, can contribute to women developing a strong ability to persevere through difficulties. This concept is reflected in literature and cultural narratives, where women are often portrayed as resilient characters who face challenges with strength and fortitude. While men often dominate in shorter, more intense endurance events, women demonstrate strong performance in longer distances, particularly ultra-endurance activities. This is partly attributed to potential physiological advantages, such as efficient fat utilization and greater fatigue resistance in women during prolonged exercise. Psychological factors like strong mental resilience and consistent pacing strategies are also believed to contribute to women’s success in these events. It is important to acknowledge that the experiences of endurance and resilience differ based on individual circumstances and intersectional factors like race and culture. For instance, Black women in horror narratives are described as “Enduring Women” (Kinitra D. Brooks, “The Importance of Neglected Intersections: Race and Gender in Contemporary Zombie Texts and Theories,” African American Review 47, no. 4, 2014) who face different and more persistent threats than the traditional “Final Girl” (Carol J. Clover, Men, Women, and Chain Saws, 1992), highlighting the complexities of navigating misogynoir and societal challenges. It is crucial to recognize the importance of understanding resilience within the context of women’s lived experiences and the societal factors that influence their well-being. It underscores a contrast between the fleeting, fragile nature of life and the sustaining, enduring role of the female, whether literally through reproduction or metaphorically as a symbol of lasting strength and continuity in a world marked by change, decay, and death.

Chapter 52 · Women, Gender, and Marriage

  • Cousin Hannah fought “for the overthrow of man” and urged all ladies to “divest themselves of excess luggage,” yet her forty trunks hold only wedding gowns. Mr. Spitzer cannot tell whether she dreamed of being the bridegroom or the bride, and he decides the world need not know.

Chapter 63

  • “this, the missing breast which gave her the pain”: The Edwin Smith Papyrus, an Egyptian surgical text of about 1600 BC copying older sources, describes bulging tumors of the breast and says there is no treatment.
  • In 1811 the novelist Frances Burney had a mastectomy in Paris without anesthesia and wrote her sister the most famous account of it (1812).
  • “perhaps a man had married a man, and perhaps a woman had married a woman”: Compare the “Boston marriage,” the nineteenth-century name for two women who made a home together, as in Henry James’s The Bostonians (1886); and the life of Murray Hall, a New York Tammany politician who lived as a man for decades, married twice, and was found at death in 1901 to have been assigned female at birth.
  • “the antiquated structure of Brooklyn Bridge”: The Brooklyn Bridge opened in 1883, then the longest suspension bridge in the world; Emily Warren Roebling saw it through after her husband, its engineer, was disabled by caisson disease.
  • “Long hair is short wisdom”: An old German proverb said of women, “Lange Haare, kurzer Sinn,” long hair, short sense; the bald Miss MacIntosh turns it back.
  • “Where’s my hair, that woman’s glory?”: “But if a woman have long hair, it is a glory to her: for her hair is given her for a covering” (1 Corinthians 11:15, KJV).
  • “My elder brothers called me George”: Compare George Sand (1804-1876) and George Eliot (1819-1880), women who wrote as George.

Chapter 63 · The Wig and Baldness

  • Miss MacIntosh’s wig symbolizes the facade of social norms and appearances, which she ultimately reveals as a protective but deceptive covering. Young described the wig as the illusion itself, “the dream by which she lived” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965), and the hair it replaces as “the symbol of the dream and of the feminine in life that she never had” (Marguerite Young, interview with Duncan MacDonald, Observation Point, 1965).
  • Her baldness, exposed in a moment of vulnerability, represents raw truth, mortality, and the stripping away of illusions.

Chapter 64

  • “Was it a woman or a man?”: Vera asks without assuming, and Miss MacIntosh answers that there was no man, only “a beautiful dancing girl.” Her one love was a woman.
  • “she ran away and became a strip-tease dancer”: Burlesque striptease flourished in the 1920s and 1930s, until New York refused to renew the burlesque theaters’ licenses in 1937 under Mayor La Guardia.
  • “two years at the University of Iowa”: The University of Iowa admitted women from its first classes in 1855, among the first public universities in the country to do so.
  • “She should walk in narrow ways with her head covered”: “But every woman that prayeth or prophesieth with her head uncovered dishonoureth her head: for that is even all one as if she were shaven” (1 Corinthians 11:5, KJV). Paul’s rule fell hardest on a girl born bald.
  • “She had certainly learned to live with no protection”: Miss MacIntosh states that she learned to live without protection. As a woman this “protection” is a many layered thing especially in the past. Unless she was able to move from under her father’s/family’s care to a husband’s care then she was left with nothing in a very literal sense.

Chapter 65

  • “and she hath no breasts”: “We have a little sister, and she hath no breasts: what shall we do for our sister in the day when she shall be spoken for? If she be a wall, we will build upon her a palace of silver” (Song of Solomon 8:8-9, KJV). Mr. Bonebreaker makes the palace a tower.
  • “the great, loud-ticking watch, luminous and waterproof”: Luminous dials were painted with radium paint from the 1910s. The young women who painted them, the Radium Girls, were poisoned by pointing their brushes between their lips; in Ottawa, Illinois, the Radium Dial Company’s painters won their case in 1938, and their fight helped establish workers’ right to compensation for occupational disease.

Chapter 65 · The Body and Physical Affliction

  • The physical condition of Miss MacIntosh (her baldness, lost breast, and scars) are central to the narrative.
  • They symbolize both human frailty and inner strength.
  • Her mending of black gloves carries guilt. Young’s psychiatrist friend explained “all the meanings of the word black and gloves in relation to psychology and guilt” (Marguerite Young, interview with Charles Ruas, WBAI radio, New York), and she told Studs Terkel, “I finally understood why it was again a symbol of guilt” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).

Chapter 66

  • “in the shadow of Brooklyn Bridge”: The Brooklyn Bridge opened in 1883. When its engineer, Washington Roebling, was crippled by caisson disease, his wife Emily Warren Roebling carried his instructions to the site for years and learned the engineering herself; she was the first to cross the finished bridge.
  • “the seed pods, her ovaries, had been removed”: Removing the ovaries is an oophorectomy, not a hysterectomy. In 1872 the Georgia surgeon Robert Battey began removing healthy ovaries to treat menstrual pain, “hysteria,” and “insanity,” and by the 1890s thousands of women in America and Europe, many of them young, had undergone the operation.
  • “But Mr. Bonebreaker had belonged to that old religion”: This passage critiques patriarchal traditions by illustrating the extreme expectations placed on women to be subservient to men, using vivid, almost grotesque imagery of kneeling and hair used as a towel for washing men’s feet. “that old religion” and its description here refers to patriarchy of which Christianity is a part and instances of the changes from a matrilineal society and traditions to a patrilineal one are in the Bible (Marilyn French, From Eve to Dawn: A History of Women in the World, 2002). Patriarchy entitles men to admiration, sex, consent, and domestic labor from women. Men also receive better medical care and pain management and overall focus of health science for their bodies. Men also are able to practice bodily control which is often and routinely denied women. Misogyny and sexism go hand in hand with patriarchy although it is possible for men to practice one or the other and both need not be present at the same time. Sexism is the theoretical and ideological branch where beliefs, ideas and assumptions are used to rationalize and naturalize patriarchal norms and expectations. Misogyny is the “law enforcement” branch of patriarchy which polices and enforces gendered norms and expectations for girls and women (Kate Manne, Down Girl, 2017, and Entitled, 2020). Gerda Lerner documented men’s withholding knowledge and power from women through denying education for centuries (The Creation of Feminist Consciousness, 1993). Young upends many of these gendered norms in the novel.
  • “she had her own seat there, for she had appropriated it”: In 1870 Victoria Woodhull and her sister Tennessee Claflin opened the first brokerage on Wall Street owned by women. No woman held a seat on the New York Stock Exchange until Muriel Siebert bought one in December 1967.

Chapter 67

  • “the pregnant girl with the bird upon her hat”: Hats trimmed with whole birds were the fashion around 1900. In 1896 two Boston women, Harriet Hemenway and Minna Hall, founded the Massachusetts Audubon Society to stop the slaughter of birds for the plumage trade, and the federal Lacey Act of 1900 and the Migratory Bird Treaty of 1918 followed.
  • “I can’t give you anything but love, baby”: I Can’t Give You Anything But Love, Baby is an American song from 1928. The idea behind the song came during a stroll Fields and McHugh were taking one evening down Fifth Avenue. They saw a young couple window-shopping at Tiffany’s. McHugh and Fields understood that the couple could not afford to buy jewelry from Tiffany’s. They heard the man say, “Gee, honey, I’d like to get you a sparkler like that, but right now, I can’t give you nothin’ but love!” Within an hour McHugh and Fields came up with the song. The lyricist was Dorothy Fields, one of the first women to succeed as a Broadway and Hollywood songwriter, with Jimmy McHugh; the song was introduced in the all-Black revue Blackbirds of 1928.
  • “How many rabbit tests old Doc had run”: In the Friedman test (1931), a woman’s urine was injected into a female rabbit, whose ovaries were examined a few days later for signs of pregnancy. The rabbit was killed either way, so “the rabbit died” was never the sign.
  • “Sweet Mary O’Grady”: Moses sings Sweet Mary O’Grady but the famous song is called Sweet Rosie O’Grady. “Sweet Rosie O’Grady” (1896) was written by Maude Nugent, a vaudeville singer, and “down by the old mill stream where I first met you” is from Tell Taylor’s “Down by the Old Mill Stream” (1910); Moses runs the two together.

Chapter 68

  • “I had no children except a foetus who had turned to stone”: A lithopedion, or stone child, is a fetus that dies outside the womb and calcifies, sometimes carried for decades. The most famous was found in Madame Colombe Chatri of Sens in 1582, twenty-eight years after her pregnancy.
  • “Four times I have been on ships that went down”: Compare Violet Jessop, a stewardess for the White Star Line, who survived the Olympic’s collision in 1911, the Titanic in 1912, and the sinking of the Britannic in 1916, and was called Miss Unsinkable.
  • “The Christian Science Monitor”: The Christian Science Monitor commonly known as The Monitor, is a nonprofit news organization that publishes daily articles in electronic format as well as a weekly print edition. It was founded in 1908 as a daily newspaper by Mary Baker Eddy, the founder of the Church of Christ, Scientist. (Wikipedia) Mary Baker Eddy was the founder of Christian Science.
  • “she feared she had conceived again”: The Comstock Act of 1873, named for the anti-vice crusader Anthony Comstock, made it a crime to mail contraceptives or information about them. This was challenged by Margaret Sanger in 1914. The Supreme Court protected contraception for married couples in Griswold v. Connecticut (1965) and for the unmarried in Eisenstadt v. Baird (1972).
  • In Southern Horrors: Lynch Law in All Its Phases (1892), Ida B. Wells showed that the charge of rape or of relations with a white woman was the usual pretext for lynching Black men, and was usually false.
  • “He had never hanged a woman”: Mary Surratt, hanged in 1865 for her part in the plot to kill Lincoln, was the first woman executed by the federal government.

Chapter 69

  • “the landlord’s wife who had been a circus fat lady”: The “fat lady” in the U.S. circus was a prominent sideshow attraction from the 19th to mid-20th centuries, embodying spectacle, curiosity, and sometimes exploitation, with famous figures like Lottie Whitlock, billed as the original “Fat Lady,” and later stars like Alice from Dallas and Celesta Geyer (Dolly Dimples) who gained fame and fortune before some dramatically lost weight, highlighting societal views on size and health. These performers, part of the “freak show” tradition, used their size to build successful careers, often achieving celebrity status within the circus world, though facing exploitation and objectification.
  • “For God knew he was no abortionist”: By 1900 abortion was a crime in every state. In 1930 abortion was the recorded cause of death of nearly 2,700 American women, almost one in five maternal deaths.
  • “Mayo, Mayo!”: Mayo Clinic originated with the medical practice of William Worrall Mayo, M.D. who immigrated to the United States in 1846. He had two sons. The Mayo brothers were financially successful and professionally prominent. The Mayo Clinic is still in the US and has opened branches in several countries. The clinic grew from St. Marys Hospital in Rochester, Minnesota, which Mother Alfred Moes and her Sisters of St. Francis built after the tornado of 1883 and opened in 1889, with the Mayos as its doctors.

Chapter 70

  • “such as the old man who stole nothing but egg-beaters”: Kleptomania was named in 1816, and in the nineteenth century it was diagnosed above all in middle-class women caught taking goods from the new department stores, while poor shoplifters were simply called thieves.
  • Pioneering female physicists like Marie Curie, Lise Meitner, Irène Joliot-Curie, Maria Goeppert Mayer, and Chien-Shiung Wu made foundational discoveries in radioactivity, nuclear fission, and particle physics, often overcoming significant gender bias to contribute to breakthroughs like the first self-sustaining nuclear reaction and proving parity violation, though some, like Wu, were notably excluded from Nobel recognition for their experimental work. Marie Curie (1867-1934) was the first woman to win a Nobel Prize (Physics, 1903) and first person to win in two different sciences (Chemistry, 1911) for radioactivity research and discovering polonium and radium. Lise Meitner (1878-1968) was the co-discoverer of nuclear fission, though often overlooked, she was a central figure in understanding nuclear processes. Irène Joliot-Curie (1897-1956) was the daughter of Marie Curie, she won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry (1935) for synthesizing new radioactive elements. Maria Goeppert Mayer (1906-1972) was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1963 for her nuclear shell model, a major achievement in nuclear physics. Chien-Shiung Wu (1912-1997) conducted the crucial “Wu Experiment” that proved parity violation in particle physics, a discovery that earned two men the Nobel Prize in 1957 while she was excluded. Leona Woods Marshall Libby (1919-1986) was the only woman on the team that achieved the first self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction (Chicago Pile-1) in 1942. Marietta Blau (1894-1970) was a pioneer in using nuclear emulsions to study particle tracks, nominated multiple times for the Nobel Prize. Katharine Burr Blodgett (1898-1979) was the first woman to earn a PhD in physics from Cambridge (1926) and a key researcher at General Electric. Many faced discrimination as women and Jews, hindering their academic and professional advancement. Women like Wu and Blau made crucial experimental contributions that challenged theoretical physics. Despite groundbreaking work, several, including Wu and Meitner, were denied Nobel recognition.
  • “She was descended from three signers of the Declaration of Independence”: Lineage societies such as the Daughters of the American Revolution (1890) made descent from the founders a mark of status. In 1939 the DAR barred the Black contralto Marian Anderson from singing at its Constitution Hall, and Eleanor Roosevelt resigned from it in protest.
  • “the Irish Catholic maid”: These young women (ages 15 to 24) were a cornerstone of U.S. domestic service from the mid-19th to early 20th centuries, arriving as young, single immigrants seeking economic opportunity after the Irish Famine, providing vital labor in cities, and using their wages to support families back home while facing discrimination but gaining a foothold in American society, often serving as a pathway to education and other professions for their children. In 1871 Harper’s Bazaar published an article named “Bridget” or “Biddy.” Biddy’s refusal to follow orders and her frequent bouts of insubordination compromised the tranquillity and moral calm that the nineteenth-century home and the creation of a domestic space were supposed to promise. An article in Harper’s Monthly noted to its mainly male audience that the result of Biddy’s presence was that ‘the master of the house returns from the cares and vexations of his day’s business, seeking repose in his home, but finds only disquiet’.13 It was the responsibility of Anglo-American women to devise solutions on how to put Biddy in her place, in order to preserve the sanctity of the home.

Chapter 71

  • “in no state of the union was it legal to hang a woman far gone in pregnancy”: The old common law, from England, forbade executing a pregnant woman until after the birth; a condemned woman could “plead the belly.”
  • “the child strangled by the umbilical cord”: Vera pictured Marie hanging and her unborn baby hanging by its umbilical cord. Pregnant Black women were among the many victims of lynching during the era of racial terror in the United States. While the exact number is not known, the case of Mary Turner is one of the most widely documented instances of this particularly brutal violence. In May 1918, a white farmer named Hampton Smith was killed by one of his Black employees, Sidney Johnson, in Brooks County, Georgia. An enraged white mob began a week-long rampage, killing several Black people, including Mary Turner’s husband, Hayes Turner. After her husband was lynched, a grieving Mary Turner, who was eight months pregnant, publicly denounced the murder and threatened to have the mob members arrested. Her statement enraged the mob, which turned its fury on her. On May 19, 1918, a mob of hundreds of people captured Turner and dragged her to Folsom Bridge over the Little River. There, they tied her ankles, hung her upside down from a tree, and set her on fire. While she was still alive, a man cut open her abdomen with a knife, causing her unborn baby to fall to the ground. A mob member then stomped the baby to death. Turner’s body was then riddled with hundreds of bullets. No one was ever held accountable for the murders of Mary Turner and her unborn child. The grotesque nature of her lynching revealed the level of dehumanization and impunity with which Black women were treated during this period. Her case was widely publicized by the NAACP and became a symbol in the anti-lynching movement. The lynching of Black women is a less-documented aspect of racial terror, but historians have uncovered evidence that it was not uncommon. The violence against Black women, especially pregnant women like Mary Turner, served as a tool of white supremacy to terrorize the Black community and suppress any form of dissent. The sexualized and gendered violence inflicted upon women’s bodies was also used to communicate power and control. Many instances of women being lynched were underreported or not documented at all. The details of Mary Turner’s lynching only became widely known because of the investigation by Walter F. White of the NAACP and the subsequent national anti-lynching campaign. The murder of Mary Turner remains a potent and horrific symbol of the racial and gender-based violence that was inflicted upon Black women during the era of racial terror.
  • “Mary Pickford, with her long yellow curls”: Mary Pickford or Gladys Marie Smith, was a Canadian-American stage and screen actress and producer with a career that spanned five decades. A pioneer in the US film industry, she co-founded Pickford-Fairbanks Studios and United Artists, and was one of the 36 founders of the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences. She was called America’s Sweetheart and The Girl with the Curls. (Wikipedia)
  • “What shall I do if my husband leaves me?”: Advice columns for women, such as Dorothy Dix’s, begun in 1896 and read by millions, answered such letters for half a century.
  • “Queen Wilhelmina of Holland was an excellent housekeeper”: Queen Wilhelmina of Holland may refer to Wilhelmina Helena Pauline Maria who was the Queen of the Netherlands from 1890 until her abdication in 1948. She reigned for nearly 58 years, longer than any other Dutch monarch. (Wikipedia)
  • “The President’s Daughter”: There are two books by this name. One published by Nan Britton in 1927 is a memoir, detailing her affair with President Warren G. Harding. The second is Clotel; or, The President’s Daughter (1853) by William Wells Brown, about the enslaved daughters of Thomas Jefferson, the first novel published by an African American.
  • “The Catholics don’t practice birth control because they want to overcome us”: In the encyclical Casti Connubii (1930), Pope Pius XI forbade Catholics to use contraception. See the Comstock Act in the Chapter 68 resources.
  • “They want an American Pope”: Vatican conspiracy theories are conspiracy theories that concern the Pope or the Roman Catholic Church. A majority of the theories allege that the Church and its representatives are secretly controlling secular society with a Satanic agenda for global domination. In the 1920s the Ku Klux Klan in Indiana enrolled perhaps a quarter of a million members, preaching against Catholics, Jews, and Black Americans and holding the state government until its leader, D. C. Stephenson, was convicted in 1925 of the rape and murder of Madge Oberholtzer.
  • “We don’t want mulattoes”: Indiana forbade marriage between white and Black people from its first years until 1965; the Supreme Court struck down the last such laws in Loving v. Virginia (1967).

Chapter 72

  • “kewpie dolls which I had first thought were children”: Rose O’Neill drew her Kewpies for the Ladies’ Home Journal in 1909, and the dolls were a craze from 1912; she grew rich on them and drew posters for women’s suffrage.
  • Wild ponies have run on Assateague Island, off Virginia and Maryland, for centuries; legend makes them survivors of a Spanish shipwreck. Marguerite Henry’s Misty of Chincoteague (1947) made them famous.
  • “had never had to take a stitch”: “The stitch” may refer to a procedure called an episiotomy, also known as perineotomy and is a surgical incision of the perineum and the posterior vaginal wall generally done by a midwife or obstetrician. It was in the 50s in the US called the “husband stitch” because it was believed to return a woman’s vaginal opening to its original size. The routine is no longer recommended for vaginal births except in emergencies. Despite this, it is one of the most common surgical procedures specific to women.
  • “for God loves a silent woman”: “A silent and loving woman is a gift of the Lord” (Ecclesiasticus 26:14, in the Apocrypha of the King James Bible); “Let the woman learn in silence with all subjection” (1 Timothy 2:11, KJV).
  • “She had never eaten ergot”: Ergot is a fungus that in the 1930s companies marketed to women whose pregnancy was not progressing since abortion, drugs that aborted a fetus and birth control devices were illegal. The FTC deemed the pills unsafe and ineffective and stopped their sale. In 1774 it is documented that midwives used ergot to ease delivery of a baby. In 1808 the New York doctor John Stearns published his use of ergot, “pulvis parturiens,” to hasten labor.

Chapter 73

  • “the birth control devices littering the bare ground”: Before 1965, birth control in the US relied on barriers, withdrawal, and, later, hormonal methods, with access heavily restricted by the Comstock Act of 1873. Common methods included condoms (vulcanized rubber by 1839), diaphragms, spermicides, and dangerous, ineffective douches marketed as “feminine hygiene.” The first oral contraceptive pill was approved in 1960.
  • “old Phoebe’s statue”: Old Phoebe was a washwoman, and the Phoebe of the Bible was a servant of the church: “I commend unto you Phebe our sister, which is a servant of the church which is at Cenchrea” (Romans 16:1, KJV; the King James spells her name Phebe). The Greek word translated “servant” is diakonos, deacon, and she is the only woman so named in the Bible; Paul trusted her to carry his letter to Rome. Where modern translations of 1 Timothy 3:11 speak of women deacons, the King James Version has “Even so must their wives be grave, not slanderers, sober, faithful in all things.” In Greek mythology Phoebe was a Titaness, grandmother of Apollo and Artemis; the name means bright.
  • “the old Chicago detective was trying to find the proof”: England’s Act to Prevent the Destroying and Murthering of Bastard Children (1624) presumed that an unmarried mother who hid the death of her newborn had murdered it, unless she could prove “by one witness at the least” that the child was born dead; the penalty was death. It stood until 1803. In Massachusetts Hannah Piggen was reportedly the last woman hanged for concealing a birth, in 1785 (Wikipedia). Concealment of birth is still a crime in many states.

Chapter 74

  • “All the women would flock to the courthouse in droves”: Andrea Dworkin wrote the book Right Wing Women that examined how women are exploited by right wing, conservative politics. They answer women’s fears about their place in the world with offers of chivalrous protection within the domain and under the control of men through patriarchal structures such as the male head of household. It reassures women that they do not need to change the status quo, but accept it, and take whatever access to power is given, almost exclusively along gender defined norms. When these women see other women flaunting these gender norms, they bond with their fathers, husbands or father-figures to form a power base through misogyny, the enforcement branch of patriarchy, to bring the status quo back in force. Right-Wing Women was published in 1983.
  • “a man who had killed several wives”: In Perrault’s Bluebeard (1697) a husband murders wife after wife. Chicago had its own: Johann Hoch, the “Stockyard Bluebeard,” married at least 55 women between 1890 and 1905, poisoning many with arsenic, and was hanged in Chicago in 1906.
  • “Where was her lost girlhood?”: On the one hand this can be symbolic and on the other men do perpetrate the majority of the violence against women and other men probably due to men and boys being socialized to accept masculine stereotypes that reinforce violence toward others and themselves. The most dangerous time for a woman is when she is trying to leave an abusive relationship. Gendered thinking about women is that they are supposed to be mysterious and cultivate feminine aspects to be appealing to men. This is sexist in that one would not apply the same statement to men as it is applied to women. Women are not mysterious or impossible to understand but rather there is so little actual data collected and used scientifically, socially or historically about women. It is understandable in this context that men can feel that way since they never hear anything different than the baseline of men applied to everything.
  • “a woman abortionist who enjoyed police protection”: Abortion was a crime in Illinois from 1867 until 1973. In Chicago, the historian Leslie J. Reagan found, the state prosecuted chiefly abortionists, most often after a woman had died, and police questioned dying women about their abortionists and lovers to obtain “dying declarations.” Reagan, When Abortion Was a Crime (1997).
  • “she had done much lying but no laying”: Young seems to at least in part use Esther to explore the sexual double standard where men who have many sexual experiences are considered studs, while women who have many sexual experiences are considered sluts. A double standard is the application of different sets of principles for situations that are, in principle, the same. Research has found that casual sexual activity is regarded as more acceptable for men than for women. Double standards between men and women can potentially exist with regards to: dating, cohabitation, virginity, marriage/remarriage, sexual abuse/assault/harassment, domestic violence and singleness.
  • “A man could have gone off whistling after he had done something wrong”: He “could have bragged about this in a railroad station, how many bastards he had had.”
  • “this rape of a sightless child, for this was only one of many”: Esther lay with “her sightless brown eyes staring fixedly at the grey, hovering winter sky.” She calls herself a sightless child, and this rape “only one of many.”
  • “She guessed it had all been over too soon”: Young may be referring to rape culture in which rape is pervasive and normalized due to societal attitudes about gender and sexuality. Behaviors commonly associated with rape culture include victim blaming, slut-shaming, sexual objectification, trivializing rape, denial of widespread rape, refusing to acknowledge the harm caused by sexual violence, or some combination of these. Men and women who are victims of sexual violence may not report the assault because of shame, the stigma, not being believed and the criminal justice system. Traumatic memories since there is a heightened emotional response often are remembered as specific details about the event as opposed to false memories. Research shows that forgetting, not thinking about, and even misremembering an assault may be both necessary and adaptive to help survivors cope with their reality.

Chapter 74 · Gender and Identity

  • The text contrasts Esther’s experiences with societal expectations of men and women, highlighting gendered double standards, especially concerning sexuality, motherhood, and blame.

Chapter 75

  • “it might be a benign tumor, this growth”: In pseudocyesis, false or phantom pregnancy, a woman believes she is pregnant and her body follows: the menses stop, the belly swells, she feels the child move. Mary Tudor, Queen of England, believed herself pregnant in 1554 and 1555; London rang its bells for a prince who was never born.

Chapter 76

  • “to bring life into this sad, mistaken world”: This is a deeply personal, philosophical, and biological question. Despite widespread anxiety about environmental issues, political instability, and suffering, many women continue to choose motherhood. Many believe that creating new life is an act of defiance against despair, bringing love, joy, and potential for positive change into the world. They may believe they can raise children who will contribute to fixing the world’s mistakes, acting as agents of change. Humans are biologically programmed to reproduce, with hormonal and physiological influences often reinforcing the desire to have children. This desire is frequently described as a powerful, instinctual urge. Having children is often seen as a way to find purpose, share love, and create a deeper, lasting connection with a partner. Women are recognized for their immense resilience and capacity to act as “backbones of communities,” even in difficult circumstances, by nurturing the next generation. Some argue that while the world contains suffering, it also holds immense beauty and joy, and that experiencing life, including its challenges, is worthwhile. Despite the trend toward smaller families, significant social, religious, and familial pressures still exist, urging women to conform to the traditional expectation of having children. For many, the desire to experience motherhood and the unique bond with a child outweighs the perceived risks of the world.
  • “no rubber corporation or Wall Street corporation”: Esther means she never used a condom. Rubber condoms followed Goodyear’s vulcanization of rubber (1844); thinner latex condoms came in the 1920s. Under the Comstock Act of 1873 mailing contraceptives or information about them was a federal crime.
  • “She had heard of a girl who was made pregnant by the police in a jail”: Indiana opened the first separate prison for women in the United States in 1873, after the Quaker reformers Rhoda Coffin and Sarah J. Smith exposed the sexual abuse of women prisoners by male guards at the state prison.
  • “She was always knitting and unravelling the black hood”: Penelope, waiting twenty years for Odysseus, wove a shroud for his father by day and unravelled it by night to put off her suitors (Odyssey, Book 2).
  • “the school trustees who would now be the jury had not fired her on false charges”: Esther once taught school. Rural school boards hired and fired teachers at will, and a woman teacher could be dismissed for marrying, for paint and powder, or for gossip.

Chapter 76 · The Feminine and the Unseen

  • The narrative explores the unseen aspects of womanhood (miscarriage, stillbirth, and the emotional landscape tied to pregnancy and motherhood) often ignored or silenced in society.

Chapter 77

  • “If the jurymen came to her bedroom”: Young reminds us that the jury will consist of men. Mississippi, in 1968, was the last state to let women serve on juries, and in Taylor v. Louisiana (1975) the Supreme Court ruled that jury pools excluding women violated the Sixth Amendment.
  • “she would be the doll, the scarlet woman”: “And the woman was arrayed in purple and scarlet colour… MYSTERY, BABYLON THE GREAT, THE MOTHER OF HARLOTS” (Revelation 17:4-5, KJV).
  • “Many an old maid has dreamed thus of being raped”: The idea of women dreaming or fantasizing about being raped comes from a long history supported by male dominated institutions where women are trained to be rape victims which is still being challenged today. The rape fantasy in women is man-made. Women use the rape fantasy to find their sexuality within the context of male sexuality or power drive.
  • “the bathtub drownings of the brides”: In England George Joseph Smith married and drowned three brides in their baths for their money, the “Brides in the Bath” murders of 1912 to 1914; he was hanged in 1915.

Chapter 78

  • “The children were wise by comparison with their fathers”: The novel portrays children, especially characters like Esther Longtree and Musidora’s children, as burdened by premature or inappropriate knowledge of sexual realities. This knowledge is often linked to trauma, loss, and the failure of adult protection. Esther Longtree is depicted as a woman who has been “always pregnant,” with many stillborn children and a pervasive sense of loss, loneliness, and burden. She is surrounded by the imagery of children who have suffered or died, and these children represent both literal and symbolic figures of innocence lost and corrupted sexuality. The children are often shown as victims of adult failures, such as unloving or absent parents, societal neglect, and the consequences of adult promiscuity or irresponsibility. They are described as “wise” beyond their years, aware of harsh truths that should be hidden from them, and sometimes as reproachful presences haunting their mothers or the community. Vera, and other characters, like Madge, observe that these children are burdened with knowledge and experiences they cannot comprehend or control, symbolizing a premature confrontation with adult realities. This is reflected in phrases like children “playing at hanging themselves,” or being “mysterious shrouded presences” who cause confusion and suffering. The presence of sexually aware children also ties into the motif of pregnancy and birth, which in the novel is often depicted as fraught with mystery, loss, and sometimes death. The characters’ struggles with conception, stillbirths, and motherhood are interwoven with a cultural critique of the consequences of repressed or mismanaged sexuality. The motif symbolizes the erosion of childhood innocence in a world where adult hypocrisies, failures, and social decay impinge on the young. It reflects a cultural anxiety about the premature sexualization of children and the failure of society to protect them. These children embody the consequences of broken families, absent or irresponsible parents, and a society that neglects its vulnerable members. Their knowledge of sex is not empowering but burdensome, linked to trauma rather than understanding. The novel critiques the social conditions that produce such outcomes: poverty, neglect, and moral confusion. The children represent lost potential and fragmented identities, paralleling the novel’s themes of multiplicity and fractured selves. Their awareness of sex and its consequences contrasts with their powerlessness and the mysteries surrounding birth, life, and death. The motif also connects with the novel’s larger exploration of dreams, illusions, and altered states of consciousness. The children’s premature knowledge is part of a distorted reality, where what is known and unknown, real and unreal, is blurred. Their sexual awareness is part of the complex interplay between truth and illusion that defines the novel’s atmosphere. By highlighting the plight of children burdened with adult knowledge, the novel implicitly critiques the social hypocrisies surrounding sex, marriage, and reproduction. It questions the societal failure to provide clear, honest guidance and protection, instead leaving children to navigate complex, often harmful realities alone.

Chapter 79

  • “Her parents had never consummated their marriage but had only dreamed”: Purity culture is a movement emphasizing abstinence until heterosexual marriage, strict gender roles, and modesty, often creating intense sexual shame and anxiety. It uses rituals like purity balls, rings, and pledges to control sexual expression, placing the burden of sexual responsibility on women and girls to prevent male lust. Many raised in this culture report anxiety, fear, and difficulties with intimacy, even after marrying. There is a growing movement of former adherents who are deconstructing these teachings and addressing the trauma caused by them. Its influence persists through different ministries and a continued focus on “biblical womanhood.” Purity culture, with its rings and pledges, arose in the 1990s.
  • “this old concubine walking between her parents”: Concubinage in the U.S. was primarily defined by the sexual exploitation of enslaved Black women by white owners, alongside localized systems like plaçage in Louisiana. These coerced, non-marital relationships were driven by power imbalances, racial prohibitions on marriage (anti-miscegenation laws), and the economic value of offspring.
  • “something old, something new, something blue”: “Something old, something new, something borrowed, something blue,” the old wedding rhyme; the mother leaves out the borrowed.

Chapter 80

  • Esther fits the pattern later called neonaticide: a young, unmarried mother who does not recognize or denies her pregnancy, gives birth alone, and kills the newborn in panic within the first day. The psychiatrist Phillip Resnick named it in 1970.

Chapter 81

  • “She had been a world spy”: Mata Hari is the most famous female spy in history, known as a WWI-era Dutch exotic dancer accused of passing secrets. Other notable agents include WWII heroes Virginia Hall (Allied spy), Noor Inayat Khan (SOE wireless operator), and Nancy Wake (“The White Mouse”), alongside American Civil War scout Harriet Tubman.
