Women, Gender, and Marriage

Part of To All My Darlings, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).

Definition from research

Chapter 4

  • “she would go to Boise or Reno, where she had gone to get her divorces”: In 1931 Nevada cut the residency required for a divorce to six weeks, and Idaho soon matched it. Reno became the divorce capital of America, and its “divorce ranches” housed women waiting out their weeks.

Chapter 6 · Marriage and Gender Roles

  • Marriage is depicted as a hollow, oppressive institution.
  • The double standard of fidelity and suffering between men and women is critiqued.
  • Madge’s role as a wife and mother is tragic rather than fulfilling.

Chapter 9 · Marriage and Gender Roles

  • Marriage is portrayed as the “normal state” and a natural institution sanctioned by God and nature.
  • Miss MacIntosh emphasizes the importance of preparation for marriage and warns against sexual dangers.
  • Vera reflects on the mystery of marriage, love, and the unknown nature of her future husband.

Chapter 10 · Marriage and Identity

  • The confusion and inversion of roles in the dream wedding hint at fluidity in gender and societal roles.
  • Marriage is portrayed ambiguously, blending union and separation, life and death.
  • The preacher’s confusing vows suggest the complexity and sometimes absurdity of social rituals.

Chapter 16

  • “The fierce, exultant pride that was mine”: In “What Is It with Women and Horses?” (Intelligent Horsemanship, 2019), Dr. Rosie Jones-McVey, drawing on her PhD research among British horsewomen, writes: “In Victorian novels, as well as in the more modern genre of pony-girl books, it is often aboard a horse that girls find their wings, often proving some snot-nosed boy wrong in the process, or enabling them to escape various romantic dramas and find solace in the barn. Just like the pony-girl books, many of my participants’ life stories entwined horseyness with coming-of-age stories. Horses were the means through which many women first acted in a truly independent way, often pursuing equestrian endeavours despite/without their parents, or else, proudly taking up adult-like responsibilities in being the teacher, pilot, and carer of their ponies, rather than the one being looked after and told what to do.” She argues that horses give many women a space for independence and self-assertion that their everyday lives do not.

Chapter 17

  • “this old man who had only one breast”: Halsted’s radical mastectomy (1894), which removed the breast and the muscles beneath it, was the standard treatment for breast cancer for most of the twentieth century. Chapter 19 confirms the loss. In Chapter 10, Vera imagined “claws upon her forehead like the claws of a lobster,” and cancer takes its name from the crab.

Chapter 17 · Power and Vulnerability

  • Miss MacIntosh’s physical power contrasts with her ghostly, fragile appearance.
  • Vera, though young and vulnerable, refuses to be defeated quietly.
  • The battle becomes a metaphor for the fight between opposing forces within or external threats.

Chapter 18

  • “Would that my lover had been a man!”: Vera’s realizations this night seem to be described again as a sexual act, something very intimate and physical.
  • “The left breast is already gone”: Miss MacIntosh talks about losing her left breast. William Halsted performed his first radical mastectomy in New York in 1882 and described the operation in 1894. There is no clear record of how earlier women survived the operation, or for how long.
  • “A good husband makes a good wife”: Miss MacIntosh in this case is taking on the role of the groom in molding Vera to be the bride.
  • “Where were her red jersey bloomers?”: Amelia Bloomer’s reform dress.
  • “a man’s bedroom was a man’s castle”: “A man’s house is his castle” (Sir Edward Coke, 1628).
  • “I needed a father to guide me”: Miss MacIntosh believes Vera should have had a father and this might refer to a husband since in earlier marriages a daughter is passed from her father to her husband as her guardian.

Chapter 19

  • “her red jersey bloomers”: The bloomers Miss MacIntosh asked for at dawn in Chapter 18.
  • “one breast had been amputated”: The Halsted operation of Chapters 17 and 18.

Chapter 19 · Physical and Emotional Vulnerability

  • The amputated breast and the varying skin colors symbolize physical trauma and emotional sensitivity.

Chapter 20

  • “There was no crowning glory of woman’s hair”: “If a woman have long hair, it is a glory to her: for her hair is given her for a covering” (1 Corinthians 11:15).

Chapter 20 · Missing Breast

  • The missing breast is a stark physical symbol of loss and incompleteness, described as “like a door knob,” emphasizing its unnatural absence.

Chapter 21

  • “a brassiere with a wad of cloth which had been Concealed”: The “breast pad” Vera imagined in Chapter 20. After a mastectomy, women padded the brassiere with cloth. Young’s capital “Concealed” is in the text.
  • “Dancing is never to be encouraged by this old lady”: Miss MacIntosh vividly expresses sexism through her character by highlighting her pragmatic, no-nonsense attitude toward the limitations imposed on women, her rejection of illusions, and her critical view of the societal roles prescribed to women. Miss MacIntosh’s insistence on practicality and self-reliance reflects a critique of traditional sexism by underscoring how women are often expected to conform to narrow roles, suppressing their individuality and desires. Miss MacIntosh’s harshness can be read as internalized sexism, reflecting how women may police themselves and others to survive within a patriarchal system that offers them limited agency. This portrayal critiques the constraints sexism imposes on women’s lives, identities, and dreams by grounding Miss MacIntosh as a figure of practical wisdom and resilience who nonetheless embodies the burdens of gendered limitations. It sums up how Miss MacIntosh has internalized sexism where she takes sexist behaviors and attitudes about women and projects them onto herself and Vera in the form of fear, envy, jealousy, suspicion, resentment, rage, anxiety, lack of self-esteem and confidence. Miss MacIntosh’s greatest fear is that Vera will become and get to do everything society expects a ‘normal’ woman to be and do, something Miss MacIntosh will never achieve.

Chapter 22

  • “a needle-workers’ strike or a new garment union”: In the “Uprising of the 20,000” (1909), shirtwaist makers in New York, most of them young immigrant women, struck for months. The International Ladies’ Garment Workers’ Union was founded in 1900.
  • “every woman’s privilege and duty”: Women’s unpaid labor is the work that women do without financial compensation, such as cooking, cleaning, and caring for children. It’s often undervalued and underappreciated. Women do more than twice as much unpaid work as men. Women spend 2.8 more hours than men on unpaid care and domestic work globally. In the US, women do an average 4.5 hours of unpaid labor daily, while men do 2.8 hours. Unpaid labor limits women’s time for paid work and can lead to longer hours. Unpaid labor can have negative emotional, mental, and physical health impacts on women.
  • “the best baseball player in little old What Cheer”: Women have been playing baseball almost as long as men have. Their long connection with the game began in the 1860s and has continued through the efforts of individual pioneers like Amanda Clement, Jackie Mitchell, Toni Stone, Maria Pepe, and Ila Borders. The All-American Girls Professional Baseball League played from 1943 to 1954.
  • “shirts going in job lots, slightly damaged by fire and flood”: May refer to the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire in 1911 in New York City which killed 146 workers because a door to one stairway was locked, and the fire escape collapsed. Firefighters’ ladders couldn’t reach the upper floors. Workers, mostly all women, jumped to their deaths. The tragedy led to New York’s Factory Investigating Commission and its safety laws (1911-15).

Chapter 23

  • “of marriage, the sacred union”: Women’s rights in the US were restricted during marriage: married women were legally subordinate to their husbands; they could not own property, keep their own wages, or enter into contracts; they had no rights to their children. Under coverture, “the husband and wife are one person in law” (Blackstone, 1765). New York’s Married Women’s Property Act came in 1848.
  • “an ugly woman was safe in her husband’s arms”: Only women are called ugly, because the feminine beauty ideal in every country holds that beauty is a woman’s most important asset.
  • “a man had married a man, and a woman had married a woman, and no one had been the wiser”: Womanless marriages were staged as community events and fundraisers. It is a “most unique and attractive entertainment,” the Houston Post reported on May 27, 1917. “Some of the most prominent men in the city will be in it. It is filled with fun from beginning to end and every city or town in which it has been given there have been record breaking audiences, even standing room being at a premium.” In nineteenth-century New England, two women living together as a couple were said to be in a “Boston marriage.”

Chapter 26

  • “There had been heirs who had been heiresses, heiresses who had been heirs”: Women’s property rights have expanded over time in the United States, but they have been restricted by gender stereotypes and laws. Under the English common law of coverture, a married woman’s property passed to her husband. In 1718, Pennsylvania’s Feme Sole Traders Act let the wives of absent mariners run businesses in their own names. In 1848, New York passed the Married Women’s Property Act, which allowed women to keep their property when they married. Other states passed similar laws in the 1850s. By 1900, most states recognized women’s property rights. Enslaved women could own nothing, not even themselves, and free women of color were often shut out of these rights in practice. Gender stereotypes and ideologies have historically been barriers to women’s property rights.
  • “held it against him that he did not rescue her, carry her away as Peron might have done”: Catherine is expecting a man to save her, which is the damsel in distress. This is a narrative device in which one or more men must rescue a woman who has been kidnapped or placed in other peril. The “damsel” is often portrayed as beautiful, popular, and of high social status; she is usually depicted as a princess in works with fantasy or fairy tale settings. Kinship, love, lust or a combination of those motivate the male protagonist to initiate the narrative. Critics have linked the helplessness of these women to societal views that women as a group need to be taken care of by men and treated nicely. Throughout the history of the trope, the role of the woman as the victim in need of a male savior has remained constant, but her attackers have changed to suit the tastes and collective fears of the period: “monsters, mad scientists, Nazis, hippies, bikers, aliens…” (Wikipedia)

Chapter 30

  • “never worn the white plume of cowardice”: This is a reference to the White Feather Campaign during World War I where women in Britain gave white feathers to men they perceived as shirking their duty to enlist in the war effort, symbolizing cowardice. The white feather symbolism borrowed the idea of a white feather in a game bird’s tail being a sign of inferior breeding. It became a symbol of cowardice and a way to shame men who did not volunteer for service. Women would publicly give white feathers to men in civilian dress, implying they were cowards for not enlisting. The campaign was a powerful tool in the propaganda efforts of the time, aiming to mobilize men into the war effort before conscription was introduced in Britain. The white feather campaign also had an impact on the understanding of masculinity and gender roles during the war. It is the subject of A. E. W. Mason’s 1902 novel The Four Feathers, whose hero receives white feathers when he resigns his commission before his regiment goes to war. It symbolizes the visible sign of choosing the easy, cowardly path rather than the difficult path of bravery and virtue. Henry of Navarre’s white plume, by contrast, was a sign of courage: “Rally to my white plume” (Ivry, 1590).
  • “She had been a great archer in Persia, a great dueler in France, an expert with sword and gun in Rumania”: Young’s inclusion of women engaged in various sports and non-traditional roles for their time carries significant thematic weight. This imagery reflects the novel’s broader exploration of gender roles, identity, and societal expectations, particularly highlighting the contrast between conventional femininity and more liberated or unconventional expressions of womanhood. These women vividly illustrate an active, robust female presence engaged in physical and public life, which was unconventional at the time. This challenges the era’s typical expectations of women’s domestic confinement and passive social roles. The active, sporty women serve as a symbol of independence and agency, affirming a woman’s right to self-determination and physical presence in the world. The references to sporty and athletic women suggest an ideal of physical health, vitality, and engagement with life, counterbalancing the decay, passivity, and dream states that afflict other characters. By incorporating women in roles and activities traditionally reserved for men, Young subtly reflects the social transformations occurring in the early 20th century, including the women’s suffrage movement and the gradual expansion of women’s rights and public roles. This is echoed in the figure of Cousin Hannah, a suffragette and adventurer who defies conventional female roles through her militant activism and boundary-crossing journeys, contrasting with the passive, isolated femininity of other characters. It also emphasizes the complexity of female identity. These external symbols of activity and engagement contrast with internal struggles over self-perception, societal acceptance, and the tension between appearance and reality, which are central themes in the novel. Young’s references to women in various athletic and unconventional roles serve to critique restrictive gender norms, celebrate female strength and autonomy, and reflect the complex, often contradictory realities of womanhood. They help to illuminate the novel’s central concerns with reality versus illusion, freedom versus confinement, and the evolving social landscape for women during the period.

Chapter 30 · The Feminine and the Skirt

  • Repeated references to skirts, including the loss of a skirt as symbolic sorrow and redemption.
  • Cousin Hannah’s life is linked metaphorically to skirts, suggesting themes of femininity, identity, and transformation.
  • “Cousin Hannah’s entire history, it seemed now, had been a history of skirts.”

Chapter 31

  • “Death snatched away her beautiful bride”: Young’s other source for Cousin Hannah was a story told by Charles Studin, a founder of the NAACP, about a suffragist “battle-axe” who fought men all her life, “one of the original bloomer girls,” yet for forty years brought home sealed trunks that proved at her death to hold “a complete bridal trousseau” each. “She had a dream too” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). In another telling the lawyer found “fifty wedding gowns,” and Young added that she “had read the histories of mountain climbers, of suffragette captains, of travelers to the Middle East” (Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs, “A Conversation with Marguerite Young,” Review of Contemporary Fiction 23, no. 1, Spring 2003 (1989 original)).

Chapter 32

  • “the misogynist, the woman hater”: A misogynist is a person who dislikes, despises, or is strongly prejudiced against women. Female misogynists (internalized sexism/oppression) are characterized by exalting the feminine ideal, favoring traditional gender roles, looking down on women who are not feminine, self-loathing, and a belief that she is superior to other women or in constant competition with them. There is a three-tiered system for patriarchy. Patriarchy is the system of social organization that favors and empowers men. Sexism is the code of conduct or rules that are established for women. Misogyny is the policing or enforcement of these rules on women (Kate Manne, Down Girl: The Logic of Misogyny, Oxford University Press, 2017). Thus women can be misogynist if they are helping to police or enforce what women can and cannot do. Looking at all of this it can be determined that no, Cousin Hannah was not misogynist for wanting women to have independence or the right to vote.
  • “She had left him at the church door with a white bouquet in his hands”: Young told Studs Terkel that when Cousin Hannah “abandons the bridegroom at the church door,” “she goes to a numerologist who advises her as to how she should spell her name in the future and adds the hyphen just at the time when she dramatically becomes a hyphenated personality” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). The hyphen is the one in Freemount-Snowden.

Chapter 34

  • “Was not marriage, as that great suffrage captain had always said, the death of a woman’s soul”: Psychology has researched the ways marriage changes husband and wives. In previous chapters, the differences for women in marriage have been discussed.

Chapter 34 · Marriage and Loss

  • Catherine’s reflections on her marriage are fragmented and surreal. She imagines multiple versions of her wedding (married in white, black, at a funeral, by a dog, or not at all) signifying confusion, loss, and the elusive nature of commitment and love.
  • Marriage is portrayed as a metaphorical death of the soul, supported by Cousin Hannah’s cynical view that marriage leads to sacrifice and the wasting of a woman’s talents.

Chapter 34 · Female Strength and Defiance

  • Cousin Hannah embodies resilience, independence, and courage. She is portrayed as a warrior-like figure, unyielding and untamed, contrasting with Catherine’s vulnerability.
  • Cousin Hannah’s disdain for men and her distrust of Catherine’s whimsical nature highlight different approaches to womanhood and survival.

Chapter 35

  • “the triumph of woman over man, even of beauty over strength”: Joan Smith’s Misogynies discusses the pervasive nature of hatred and discrimination against women in society, exploring cultural, social, and psychological roots of misogyny (Joan Smith, Misogynies: Reflections on Myths and Malice, Faber and Faber, 1989). The novel vividly portrays the suffering, marginalization, and complex interior lives of women like Catherine, Esther Longtree, and Mrs. Hogden, who each embody different facets of women’s oppression and resilience. The presence of misogyny is implicit in their isolation, the betrayals they endure, and their struggles to assert identity and selfhood. Miss MacIntosh’s pragmatic, no-nonsense character challenges the illusions and fantasies that trap women like Catherine, reflecting resistance to the roles imposed by a misogynistic society. Her disdain for frivolity and self-pity contrasts with the more vulnerable or victimized female figures, highlighting the varied responses to misogyny. Smith emphasizes how misogyny permeates both public and private spheres, a theme echoed in the novel’s blending of intimate psychological struggles with broader social critiques, such as the suffrage movement represented by Cousin Hannah and the economic hardships faced by characters like Esther Longtree.
  • “She had wished to overthrow man, both as to the marital state”: The suffrage movement did not oppose marriage. The anti-suffrage movement opposed suffrage because it feared that women would be “masculinized” and that this in turn would “feminize” men. Under coverture, after marriage the man and woman became one legal unit that the man controlled. Women in many cases were not allowed to own property, sign contracts, earn a salary, or enter education without their husbands’ permission.
  • “paternal domination, the regnancy of the past and of the future”: Kate Millett’s Sexual Politics is a foundational feminist text analyzing the ways patriarchal power operates through sexual and social relationships, emphasizing how gender roles and power imbalances shape personal and political dynamics (Kate Millett, Sexual Politics, Doubleday, 1970). The novel portrays complex gender dynamics, especially through Vera’s mother Catherine and Miss MacIntosh. Catherine inhabits a world of illusion and decay, surrounded by hallucinations and dominated by male absences and betrayals. Miss MacIntosh, by contrast, embodies a practical, no-nonsense counterpoint to Catherine’s dreamlike passivity, and she strongly critiques illusions and instability. This tension reflects the gendered power struggles Millett describes, though in a symbolic and psychological mode. Cousin Hannah, the suffragette figure in the novel, represents a militant, radical feminist presence, dedicated to overthrowing patriarchal domination, which aligns with Millett’s critique of male control over women’s bodies and lives. Cousin Hannah’s battles for women’s freedom and her rejection of traditional feminine roles resonate with Millett’s political feminism. The novel’s exploration of pregnancies, stillbirths, and female suffering (notably through Esther Longtree’s story) touches on the control of women’s sexuality and reproduction, key concerns in Sexual Politics. The novel’s treatment of female bodies as sites of pain, mystery, and societal expectation parallels Millett’s analysis of the political control exerted through sexuality.
  • “an organizer of chaos”: Amanda Vajskop reads the Babylonian Enuma Elish and the Genesis creation story, in which male creators take over woman’s part in bringing forth life and women are cast as destabilizing and dangerous, a fear that patriarchy uses to keep women subordinate (Amanda Vajskop, “Finding Patterns in the Chaos: Woman as Chaos Agent in Creation Myths,” Denison Journal of Religion 5, article 7, 2005). The novel is deeply invested in themes of chaos versus order, illusion versus reality. Vera’s narrative continuously navigates between dreamlike confusion and the search for meaning or “ultimate harmony.” Miss MacIntosh represents order and common sense amid the chaos of Catherine’s opium-induced visions and the fragmented lives of other characters. The novel portrays characters with fragmented selves: Vera’s own complex consciousness, Miss MacIntosh’s evolving physical and psychological state (including her baldness and amputated breast), and the multiple personae like Cousin Hannah and Mrs. Hogden. Vera’s act of recounting, reflecting, and seeking patterns in her experiences and the lives around her is itself a search for coherence in fragmentation. The novel’s richly symbolic and layered narrative demands readers engage in deciphering meaning from a kaleidoscope of images and voices. The novel’s intense focus on female embodiment, sexuality, motherhood, loss, and survival complements critical perspectives on misogyny and sexual politics. Cousin Hannah, “an organizer of chaos,” takes up the part these myths assign to woman and turns it against the order men have made.

Chapter 35 · Freedom and Emancipation

  • Cousin Hannah embodies the fight for women’s rights and freedom from traditional roles.
  • Her life is a metaphor for breaking boundaries: social, geographic, and existential.
  • The suffrage movement is portrayed as her husband, her master, and her true love.

Chapter 36

  • “Was she become a man”: In Virginia Woolf’s Orlando (1928), the hero wakes as a woman: “Orlando had become a woman—there is no denying it.”

Chapter 36 · The Role of Women and Feminine Power

  • Cousin Hannah represents a powerful, rebellious feminine figure who challenges societal and cosmic orders.
  • There is tension between masculine and feminine realms, e.g., Catherine’s picture of a Moslem Paradise where women are absent.
  • Catherine’s contemplation of womanhood and power in death questions traditional gender roles and metaphysical status.
  • “For if a woman should be in heaven, it should not be heaven long, my mother thought.”

Chapter 37

  • “as man became woman with her heaving breasts”: Gender as a social construct refers to the socially created roles, behaviors, expressions, and identities associated with being a woman, man, boy, or girl. It’s not inherently determined by biology, but rather shaped by cultural norms, societal expectations, and historical contexts. This means gender is not fixed and can vary across cultures and time. In short, it is not a biological reality: it is socially created, varies across cultures, is dynamic and fluid, and is distinct from biological sex. Philosophical thought on gender goes back to ancient Greece, where Pythagoras’s table of opposites set male against female (Aristotle, Metaphysics 1.5) and Plato argued that women could be guardians (Republic 5). Later came John Locke, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Judith Sargent Murray (“On the Equality of the Sexes,” 1790), and Mary Wollstonecraft (A Vindication of the Rights of Woman, 1792). The chapter and novel present gender as a fluid and changeable construct rather than a rigid, innate fact, using metaphorical and dreamlike language to question fixed dichotomies between male and female, self and other, life and death.

Chapter 37 · Feminine Power and Resistance

  • Cousin Hannah, a suffrage captain, symbolizes strength, resistance, and an indomitable spirit.
  • Her death and burial contrast with her bold life, and her story reflects the struggle for identity and agency beyond traditional roles.
  • Cousin Hannah’s adventures in exotic lands and her defiance of powerful men underscore themes of female empowerment and autonomy.

Chapter 39

  • “those hidden from history”: History is the study and the documentation of the past. Events before the invention of writing systems are considered prehistory. History is an umbrella term comprising past events as well as the memory, discovery, collection, organization, presentation, and interpretation of these events. Historians seek knowledge of the past using historical sources such as written documents, oral accounts, art and material artifacts, and ecological markers (Wikipedia). Many historians believe that the production of history is embedded with bias because events and known facts in history can be interpreted in a variety of ways. Constantin Fasolt suggested that history is linked to politics by the practice of silence itself (Constantin Fasolt, The Limits of History, University of Chicago Press, 2004). According to Michel-Rolph Trouillot, the historical process is rooted in the archives, therefore silences, or parts of history that are forgotten, may be an intentional part of a narrative strategy that dictates how areas of history are remembered (Michel-Rolph Trouillot, Silencing the Past: Power and the Production of History, Beacon Press, 1995). Historical omissions can occur in many ways and can have a profound effect on historical records. Information can also purposely be excluded or left out accidentally. Gerda Lerner, a twentieth century historian who focused much of her work on historical omissions involving women and their accomplishments, explained the negative impact that these omissions had on minority groups. Her books Black Women in White America: A Documentary History (Pantheon Books, 1972), The Majority Finds Its Past: Placing Women in History (Oxford University Press, 1979), and Why History Matters: Life and Thought (Oxford University Press, 1997) have been used to examine women’s history. Women’s history is the study of the role that women have played in history and the methods required to do so. It includes the study of the history of the growth of women’s rights throughout recorded history, personal achievement over a period of time, the examination of individuals and groups of women of historical significance, and the effect that historical events have had on women (Wikipedia). Howard Zinn, in A People’s History of the United States (Harper and Row, 1980), presented what he considered to be a different side of history from the more traditional, fundamentally nationalist glorification of the country. Zinn portrays a side of American history that can largely be seen as the exploitation and manipulation of the majority by rigged systems that hugely favor a small aggregate of elite rulers from across the orthodox political parties. Zinn inspired Roxanne Dunbar-Ortiz to write An Indigenous Peoples’ History of the United States (Beacon Press, 2014). It is the third of a series of six ReVisioning books which reconstruct and reinterpret U.S. history from marginalized peoples’ perspectives. Human history is portrayed as something that may be largely imaginary, transient, and unknowable. The narrative suggests that human beings often live with fragmentary selves and multiple identities, making it difficult to grasp a complete or unified history of any individual. The chapter emphasizes that history and memory are not straightforward but are clouded by illusions, dreams, and shifting perceptions. The past is described as being as uncertain as the future, with no total way of knowing what truly happened or what meaning to assign to events. The novel also explores the idea that death and life, truth and falsehood, and memory and oblivion are intertwined in ways that defy simple historical narratives. The chapter touches on the limits of human understanding, the impermanence of life, and the inevitability of change and loss. It conveys a sense of melancholy about the elusiveness of truth and the continual presence of mystery within what is recorded as history. Human history is a multifaceted and elusive phenomenon, highlighting the difficulty of fully comprehending the lives and experiences of individuals, even remarkable ones.
  • “Equalities, which had always been innate”: Gender fluid philosophy challenges the traditional binary view of gender, suggesting that gender is not fixed but rather a spectrum or a dynamic process that can change over time. This fluidity can apply to both gender identity and gender expression, meaning how a person presents themselves to the world. Simone de Beauvoir in, The Second Sex, was foundational in distinguishing between biological sex and social gender, laying the groundwork for later explorations of gender as a social construct. The novel embraces gender fluid philosophy primarily through its poetic and symbolic depiction of identity as unstable, multiple, and mutable. Characters traverse and blur gender boundaries; metaphors of transformation underscore the ephemeral nature of identity; and the narrative challenges fixed binaries by portraying a world where selves, including gendered selves, are in constant flux. This fluidity is not articulated in explicit gender theory terms but is deeply woven into the novel’s exploration of selfhood, embodiment, and the interplay between reality and illusion.
  • “a blushing coward who would leave woman to fend for herself”: Research about masculinity expanded in the 1970s onward. Julián Marías wrote in his book Metaphysical Anthropology (1971) that man’s condition is insecurity, neediness, ignorance, indecision and helplessness. However, males are seen as having the main quality of strength. If he doesn’t then he feels he is inferior and must make up for this lack. Male strength may be physical, but can also consist in cunning, intelligence, prestige, experience, authority, aplomb, capacity of expression, aptitude for revealing or feigning those faculties. The paradox according to Marias is that man is ignorant, weak and threatened which leads to man’s pretension that he is the source of knowledge, strength, power and security. Joseph Pleck examined masculinity by doing a meta analysis of the male role starting with the 1930s and found it to consist of numerous instances of blatant misrepresentation of data, twisted reinterpretations of disconfirming results, misogyny, homophobia, and class bias (Joseph H. Pleck, The Myth of Masculinity, MIT Press, 1981). Alan G. Miller examined the myth of masculinity in patriarchal society as men are supposed to be competitive, diligent, disciplined, level-headed, strong, virile, controlled, successful (Alan G. Miller, “The Myth of Masculinity: A Panel,” American Journal of Psychoanalysis 33, no. 1, 1973, 65-67). Women, if considered at all, were presumed to be the opposite if for no reason other than no one ever asked them. Thomas J. Gerschick and Adam S. Miller later studied how men with physical disabilities come to terms with these ideals (Thomas J. Gerschick and Adam S. Miller, “Coming to Terms: Masculinity and Physical Disability,” in Men’s Health and Illness: Gender, Power, and the Body, ed. Donald Sabo and David Frederick Gordon, Sage, 1995). Young disparages the stereotypical feminine virtues as well. Young presents masculinity and femininity as fluid, multifaceted, and deeply intertwined with themes of identity, power, loss, and illusion. Both are shown as complex, sometimes contradictory forces shaping the characters’ experiences in a shifting, often surreal world. The novel critiques rigid gender norms and reveals the porous boundaries between male and female, reality and dream, life and death.

Chapter 74 · Gender and Identity

  • The text contrasts Esther’s experiences with societal expectations of men and women, highlighting gendered double standards, especially concerning sexuality, motherhood, and blame.

Chapter 76 · The Feminine and the Unseen

  • The narrative explores the unseen aspects of womanhood (miscarriage, stillbirth, and the emotional landscape tied to pregnancy and motherhood) often ignored or silenced in society.
