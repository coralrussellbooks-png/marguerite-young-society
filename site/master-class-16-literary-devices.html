A note on method

What follows groups Young's devices into families rather than cataloguing them one by one, because she never uses them one by one. A single sentence will run alliteration through a chiasmus while a phrase repeats at its head and its tail, and to pull those apart into separate entries is to describe a chord by naming its notes in different rooms. Each family below leads with a passage rather than a definition, because the passage is the argument; the naming comes after. Quotations are cited by chapter, since the novel has appeared in more than one edition and page numbers would depend on what edition.

Repetition

Of a bull frog's voice coming out of the dark, Young writes that it croaked "too deep, too deep" (ch. 61), and the doubling is the whole of her method in miniature. The phrase does not describe depth. It sinks, the second "too deep" carrying the first down with it, so that the reader arrives at the bottom of the sound before arriving at its sense. This is what repetition does everywhere in the novel, and it is worth treating the several named devices, anaphora, epistrophe, epanalepsis, anadiplosis, refrain, as one family, because Young runs them together and they all serve a single end. Her repetition does not clear a word for reuse. It thickens the word with everything it has already carried.

That end is the exact opposite of the one repetition usually serves in modern prose, and the distinction is the key to reading her. As the discussion of her education noted, the contemporary whose surface most resembles hers is Stein, and Young declined the kinship for a precise reason. Stein repeats to strip a word of its associations and make it land clean in the present. Young repeats to make the past accumulate in the word until it can barely be lifted. Every device in this family is a way of refusing to let something go.

Anaphora, the repeated head, is her instrument for a mind circling a question it cannot answer. Catherine, staring down the golden archer, asks "Am I the anvil? Am I the hammer?... Am I the horseshoe? Am I the horseman or the horse?" (ch. 43). The repeated "Am I" does not build toward an answer; it multiplies the identities the questioner might be until the self dissolves into the asking. The same head-repetition drives Mr. Spitzer through space in a passage where every clause begins "He passed," and by the end he "passed the same place twice, or else the place had moved" (ch. 54), the anaphora itself enacting the loop it describes, so that the reader cannot tell forward motion from return any better than he can.

Anadiplosis, the tail that becomes the next head, is where the family shows its deepest logic, because it makes the sentence physically circle. "For years collapsed into a moment of sighing. Or a moment of sighing was extended to many years" (ch. 54): the second sentence takes the first apart and reassembles it backward, "years" and "a moment of sighing" trading places across the period, so that time itself is shown to be reversible at the level of the clause. Young does the same with Spitzer's music, where "the unconscious music" becomes "the conscious music," and then, in the next breath, "the conscious music became the unconscious" (ch. 55). The chain does not advance. It hands each term back to its opposite, which is the novel's whole picture of a mind that cannot keep its states sorted.

Set against these circling figures is the flat declaration that they are not accidents of style but law. "Anything which was worth happening at all was worth happening twice" (ch. 62), Spitzer reflects, and the sentence is the family's charter. In a novel governed by that principle, nothing occurs once. Events double, the dead return, the pearls that are lost come back more numerous than before until, as Catherine explains, "the more pearls there were. It was infinity" (ch. 71). That is repetition's strangest turn here: it is not loss but increase. The thing repeated does not wear thin with each return. It gathers.

What makes the technique bearable across so many pages, rather than merely insistent, is that the doubling carries the book's grief. "Everything seemed to repeat and double before his eyes, even in this sad world so like the next that it was perhaps the next" (ch. 51). The repeated world and the world to come blur into each other because, for a man who has lost his twin, every present thing is haunted by its double, and the prose that keeps returning to the same phrase is performing the same refusal to let the dead be gone that Spitzer performs with his two watches and Catherine with her crowded bedroom. The refrain, in this novel, is grief's own grammar: the line that will not stop coming back because the person it mourns will not. It is no accident that the flat statement underwriting the whole family, that the living and the dead are not to be sharply divided (ch. 2), is also the statement underwriting the book's treatment of death. Repetition is how a sentence keeps the dead in the room.

So the several devices resolve into one gesture. Anaphora circles a question, anadiplosis circles a clause, the refrain circles the whole book, and each does it by returning rather than advancing, by gathering the past into the present phrase rather than clearing it away. A reader who wants to feel why the novel is as long as it is, and why its length is not a flaw, need only watch what its repetitions do: they do not move the book forward, because the book's deepest conviction is that nothing that mattered ever fully passes, and a prose faithful to that conviction cannot, in good conscience, stop coming back.

Sound

Young sets the whole family's terms in a single clause, calling her own prose "weavings of the threads of sound upon the loom of time" (ch. 58). That is not a description of a scene; it is a description of what her sentences are made of. Sound in this novel is not decoration laid over meaning but the material the meaning is woven from, and the several devices usually kept apart, alliteration, assonance, onomatopoeia, cacophony, and the book's peculiar musicality, are better read as one loom, because Young works them all at once and toward one end. That end is the novel's deepest paradox about hearing: that the truest sound in the book is silence, and the truest music is the music no one can hear.

The soft devices come first, and they do the work of the trance. In "whispering presentiment of rain in a distant street" (ch. 58) the repeated breathy consonants and the long vowels running under them slow the line to the pace of the thing described, so that the sentence does not report a hush but produces one. This is the hypnotic register that carries a reader through a book of this length, the alliteration and assonance functioning less as ornament than as tempo, a way of making the prose move at the speed of reverie rather than the speed of information. When Young writes of "fading ripples begetting fading ripples, merged whispers and movements" (ch. 62), the sounds enact their own sense: the phrase fades as it repeats, each soft cluster begetting the next exactly as the ripples do, so that the reader hears the dying-away the words name.

Against this hush she sets the noise, and the noise is where the family turns harsh on purpose. The novel is full of "buzzings, hummings, hammerings, ripplings of sounds" (ch. 61), and of "the demented sea shells singing to themselves" and "the shriekings of the silken skies" (ch. 56), clusters of grinding, clashing consonants that break the trance the soft passages induce. This cacophony is not a lapse from the music; it is the music's necessary opposite, the discord that the fragile order of the prose is always holding at bay. Young even personifies it, giving the racket a keeper: a "sound effects man" (ch. 54) who imitates the chaos of the world, foghorns and slamming doors, so that the noise has an author the way the silence does. Between the whispering and the shrieking the novel maps its whole acoustic range, and the range is the same as its thematic one, the fragile made thing and the chaos it is built against.

Then comes the turn that makes Sound more than a matter of ear, and it is the reason to treat the family as a unit rather than a checklist. The book is crowded with the deaf and the mute, and its central musician, Mr. Spitzer, hears his music only as "a crystal droning, buzzing in his dead ear, his deaf ear" (ch. 55), a sound that is not sound, arriving through the organ that cannot receive it. Young pushes this until the paradox states itself outright: "what was sound but the dream of communication in worlds where none should speak?" (ch. 62). Sound, in that formulation, is not the real thing but the dream of it, and silence is where the real communication lives. The novel's highest musical value is therefore an oxymoron it returns to like a refrain, "the music of the perfect silence" (ch. 14), the music beyond the reach of the falling stars, audible to no living ear and truer for it. The stone deaf man Vera finally marries is the figure this whole family has been building toward: the one who hears with his eyes, for whom all the world's noise is silent, and who is offered, without irony, as the book's deepest listener.

So the loom's two threads, the hush and the din, are woven toward silence rather than sound. The onomatopoeia that seems at first to be Young reveling in noise, the trolley bell that goes "Ding, dong, ding" (ch. 54) long after the last trolley has passed, turns out to be haunted: a bell ringing where there is no bell, sound persisting past its source exactly as the dead persist past their lives. Every device in the family finally points the same way. The soft sounds lull, the harsh sounds jar, the imitated sounds haunt, and all of them are measured against a silence the novel holds to be more musical than any of them, because in a book where the truest things are said by the mute and the truest music is heard by the deaf, sound itself is only the dream of a communication that silence, in the end, keeps better.

Opposition and reversal

The front of this book gave two chapters to the novel's largest refusal: the Dialectics chapter, on how Young invokes the machinery of thesis and antithesis and then withholds the synthesis it promises, and the Paradox chapter, on the two-things-at-once condition the book is left holding once that synthesis fails. Those chapters argue the theme across the whole length, because the refusal to resolve is a thing you can only feel over 1,198 pages. This family does the opposite. It watches the same refusal happen inside a single sentence, in the grammar, before any theme has been stated, and its whole claim is that the philosophy the chapters describe was never laid over the prose but built into its smallest joints. Antithesis, chiasmus, and oxymoron are the theme worked at the scale of the clause.

Antithesis is the balance the novel refuses to tip. When Young writes that Mr. Spitzer "was this soul divided, for he was this twin, and one was dead, and one lived" (ch. 25), the mechanism to watch is not the meaning but the missing word. The natural sentence would join those halves with "but," ranking one over the other, one the true brother and one the shadow. Young joins them with "and," and the two clauses sit at equal grammatical weight, neither subordinated, so that the sentence itself declines to say which twin is the real one. The refusal the Dialectics chapter traces across the book is here performed in the choice of a single conjunction. She does it again with Catherine, who "was both living and dead" (ch. 25): the word "both" is doing the antithesis's whole work, holding two states the mind wants to sort into one register and forbidding the sort. What a reader feels in these lines is not an idea about unresolved opposites but the small grammatical fact of an opposition that the sentence structurally will not resolve.

Chiasmus is the same refusal made to circle. Its mechanism is the mirror: a clause reverses its own order so that its end throws its beginning back, and the sentence arrives where it started. When the world "was a mad man's dream" (ch. 22) recurs across the novel, the surrounding sentences keep folding their terms back to front, past becoming present becoming past, until the line has no forward direction left in it. The device does something no theme-statement can do: it makes the reader's eye travel out and back within a single sentence and end at the beginning, so that the book's claim about time's non-progression is not asserted but enacted in the act of reading the clause. The chapters can tell you the novel does not advance. Chiasmus makes a sentence not advance while you watch.

Oxymoron is the collision compressed until it fits in a breath, and it is where the family does its most distinct work, because it operates faster than thought. "Wakeful sleep, of sleeping wakefulness" (ch. 4): the two words are pressed so close that the mind cannot hold them apart long enough to reject the contradiction, and the fusion registers before the objection can form. This is precisely what a sentence can do and a chapter cannot. The Paradox chapter can describe a state in which sleep and waking are one condition; the oxymoron makes you experience the two meanings refusing to merge in real time, in the half-second it takes to read two words. When Young writes that "there should be nothing certain but uncertainty" (ch. 2), the paradox is not a theme she is introducing, it is a torque she puts on a single sentence, the statement undoing its own claim to statement even as it makes it. The mechanism is the point: the contradiction is delivered too fast to be argued with, which is exactly why it lands as felt rather than proposed.

Juxtaposition is the family's device turned outward from the clause to the paragraph, the same balancing carried up one scale. Where antithesis holds two terms in one sentence, juxtaposition sets two images side by side and, again, withholds the connective that would rank them: the half-eaten breakfast apple beside the phantoms thickening in the air, the plain fact laid next to the impossible one with no "but" to tell you which to believe. It is the bridge between this family and the whole book's method, the point at which the sentence-level refusal becomes the structural one the Dialectics chapter describes.

Figuration

Young's densest single image is a definition of her whole figurative method: a world that is, she writes, "the spectre of the spectre, the dream of the dream" (ch. 49). Look at what the figure does rather than what it says. It offers a thing, then the copy of the thing, and removes any original the copy could have been made from, so that the metaphor has no ground floor. This is the trait that unifies the devices usually separated here, metaphor, simile, analogy, personification, anthropomorphism, allegory. In ordinary prose a figure borrows the solidity of the literal to illuminate the figurative: the real bridge lends its realness to the metaphor. In Young the direction reverses. Her figures do not stand on a literal ground; they dissolve it, so that the comparison and the thing compared turn out to be equally provisional.

Metaphor and simile, in her hands, tend to unmake the solid rather than fix it. When Mr. Spitzer becomes "a poor puppet dancing on its strings, but who held the spindle?" (ch. 56), the simile does the expected work of rendering his helplessness, and then the question dismantles the whole figure by asking after a puppeteer the novel refuses to supply. The comparison opens onto an absence. The same reversal runs through the marble statue "sculptured by man dreaming that he was other than he was" (ch. 1): the figure of solid stone is immediately undercut by the dream inside it, so that the most literal object in the sentence, carved marble, is revealed to rest on a fantasy. Young's figures characteristically point at the drawn thing and then at the hand that drew it, and find the hand uncertain.

Personification and anthropomorphism are the same gesture turned on the inanimate world, and they should be read together because Young makes no working distinction between them. She grants agency to objects, forces, and abstractions indiscriminately, and always to the same end: the erasure of the line between what is alive and what is not. The earth "swelled, heaved like a woman in child labor" (ch. 56); the sea lies "paved with human faces and luna moths and glow-worms" (ch. 2). These are not decorative animations of a dead landscape. They are the figurative arm of the novel's deepest claim, that the boundary between the living and the dead, the animate and the inanimate, is one the book does not honor. When the furniture dreams and the tides obey and the statues open their eyes, personification is doing philosophical work: it is enacting, at the level of the individual image, the dissolution that the whole novel argues.

Allegory sits above these as the same principle organized at book scale, the house as psyche, the journey as quest, the black coachman as conscience, but it works the way the small figures do, refusing to let its vehicle settle into a stable one-to-one key. The coachman is death and conscience and fate and simply a coachman, and the allegory will not resolve to any single reading, exactly as the metaphors will not resolve to any single ground. What holds the whole family together, from the two-word "dream of the dream" to the book-long allegory of the wheel, is this consistent refusal of the literal anchor. Young's figuration does not illuminate a solid world by comparison. It is the instrument by which she shows the world was never solid, each figure a small demonstration that the thing compared and the thing it is compared to are made of the same dissolving material.

Amplification and excess

Young sends Cousin Hannah up "Alps exceeding Alps, perhaps the mountains of illusion" (ch. 46), and the phrase names the engine of her whole rhetoric of excess: not a mountain but a mountain beyond the mountain, magnitude that will not stop at any summit. Hyperbole and amplification are the same device at two scales here, the single exaggerated stroke and the sustained accumulation, and both serve the novel's conviction that ordinary proportion is a lie about a world that is actually boundless.

Hyperbole inflates the particular past the point where realism could hold it. Cousin Hannah becomes "the boldest mountain-climber ever known among members of the weaker sex" (ch. 46); the great house acquires more drawing rooms than tideless years. The exaggeration is not decorative overstatement but a claim about scale: that these lives and losses are genuinely too large for the measured sentence, and that a prose faithful to them must break proportion. Amplification is the same impulse extended, the piling of "many drifting mirrors, many cascading stars, stars rushing like streams, tides of faceless memory" (ch. 39), where the repeated "many" and the cascading clauses refuse to arrive at a countable total. The novel does not amplify to emphasize a point already made; it amplifies because its subject, memory, grief, the dead, is inexhaustible by nature, and a finite sentence would misrepresent it. Excess, in this book, is a form of honesty about magnitude. Where a spare prose would imply that experience can be captured in proportion, Young's overflow insists that it cannot, and the surplus that seems like extravagance is the sentence admitting it will never be equal to what it carries.

Understatement and negation

Set against all that overflow is a countervailing family that works by saying less, and Miss MacIntosh is its voice: "Fine words butter no parsnips. Words do not harness the winds. Words are as nothing" (ch. 63). Litotes, apophasis, antanagoge, antiphrasis, and euphemism are the devices of saying-by-not-saying, and in a novel this saturated with excess they do something the excess cannot, they mark the places where the book distrusts its own eloquence, and where the deepest things are the things withheld.

Litotes and its blunt cousins belong mostly to Miss MacIntosh, whose plain negations puncture the surrounding lyricism. Her verdict that the law "was the practice of liars" (ch. 14) is understatement wearing bluntness: it says less than a full denunciation and lands harder for the restraint. The device characterizes her, the flat Middle Western negative set against Catherine's cascades, but it also does thematic work, because her understatement is the book's own skepticism about whether any of its beautiful language reaches the real.

Apophasis and euphemism carry the heavier freight, because they point at what cannot be said directly. The narrator's description of Miss MacIntosh as "nobody's fool, the mirror to no one" (ch. 22) defines her by negation, by what she is not and reflects for no one, and the whole novel's method of approaching its central mysteries, death, identity, the dead brother, is this apophatic circling, naming a truth by refusing to state it. Euphemism does the same for mortality: death arrives as "the music of the perfect silence," as sleep, as a veil, never as the blunt fact, and the softening is not evasion but the book's recognition that the plain word would falsify a thing it holds to be unspeakable. Where the amplification family insists that some things are too large for proportion, this family insists that some things are too deep for statement, and the two families are the novel's two honesties, the overflow and the withholding, the excess that admits it cannot capture enough and the reticence that admits it should not try to say everything.

Syntax and disruption

Young's most famous sentences run without brakes: "Brooklyn Bridge like a purple lace glove reaching through the darkness and Times Square lighted with a thousand lights and Roman fountains splashing in lonely court yards" (ch. 2). Asyndeton and its relatives, the missing conjunctions, the broken clauses, the inverted order, the endless parallel, are how the novel builds and breaks the sentence itself to match a consciousness that does not proceed in orderly steps.

Asyndeton is the rush: images strung with "and" after "and," or with no connective at all, so that the reader is carried through a cascade without the pauses that ranking and subordination would impose. This is the sentence-level form of the novel's refusal to sort, the same refusal the opposition family traced in the conjunction, now extended across a whole breathless period until the images pile faster than judgment can process them. Against this flood Young sets anacoluthon, the sentence that breaks its own structure mid-flight, swerving from one grammar to another: "Sound was life, echo of echo. Death was silence, though it was also figured like a state of mind" (ch. 55), where the thought turns and re-turns and never completes the shape it started. The broken syntax is not carelessness; it is the accurate transcription of a mind losing and refinding its footing, the grammar fracturing exactly where the consciousness fractures. Parallelism and anastrophe complete the family, the one imposing a mirror-order that the content keeps disrupting, the other inverting normal sequence so that the sentence arrives strangely, out of its expected order, the way memory arrives. Taken together these devices make the sentence a working model of the mind the novel renders: accumulating without sorting, breaking without warning, ordered and disordered in the same clause, so that reading the syntax is the closest a reader comes to inhabiting the consciousness from inside.

Structure and time

The larger architecture of the novel is built, above all, to unsettle time, and its governing image is a house where "each clock having been stopped at a different hour" (ch. 12). Foreshadowing, flashback, motif, archetype, and the recurring symbolic image are the devices that operate at this structural scale, and they all serve the book's refusal of linear sequence, the conviction that the past does not lie behind the present but presses up through it.

Foreshadowing here is unusually total, because in a book of reversals nearly everything is a seed for something later. The secret canary substitution, "this substitution of the living for the dead, another canary always in the house, a spare" (ch. 2), planted early and paid off late, is characteristic: the novel seeds its ends in its beginnings so thoroughly that, as the foreshadowing material rightly observes, a second reading becomes a different book, the reader now seeing every early image as the announcement of a reversal to come. Flashback, correspondingly, refuses to stay in the past, because the novel's memory is not retrospective but present-tense, the recalled scene arriving with the same weight and immediacy as the framing one, so that past and present cease to be separable layers.

Motif and recurring image are the threads that bind this non-linear structure, and they behave like the repetition family at book scale: the black coachman, "upon whom all things depended" (ch. 14), the sea, the clocks, the wheel, returning across hundreds of pages, each recurrence gathering the weight of its previous appearances rather than merely reminding. Archetype gives these recurrences their mythic reach, the Dreamer and the Realist, the Quest, the House as psyche, lifting the particular characters toward the universal patterns the Characters-as-Representations material traces. And under all of it runs the novel's treatment of time as stopped, cyclical, and simultaneous, the clocks each frozen at a different hour and starting up wheezing in the dark. What these structural devices share with the sentence-level ones is the single conviction the whole book is built on: that sequence is a fiction the novel will not honor, that the dead coexist with the living and the past with the present, and that a form faithful to this cannot advance in a line but must, like its own stopped clocks, keep the hours all at once.
