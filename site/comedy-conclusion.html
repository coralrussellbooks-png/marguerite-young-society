Definition from research

Taken together, the comic passages are not a series of interruptions in a sad book. They are how the book thinks.

The most consistent thing the comedy does is refuse comfort. Young's jokes tend to implicate rather than soothe. The white detective who abandons a murder the moment blackbirds enter it, the toy salesman whose grotesque babble curdles into paranoid, hateful raving, these are figures the reader laughs at and then recoils from, and the recoil is part of the design. The humor keeps pointing at the thing a gentler book would look away from, and it will not let the reader off with a chuckle.

It also protects the novel from its own lushness. Young's prose runs toward incantation, toward the beautiful and the elegiac, and left unchecked it could tip into unbroken lament. The comedy is the counterweight. A misspelled sign, a mule's bray, a man tipping his hat to the drowning, these keep the grief honest by keeping it in company with the ridiculous, so that no sorrow in the book is ever quite allowed to admire itself.

There is a deeper reason the comedy belongs. The novel's whole argument is that reality is a shared fiction, that the self is not one stable thing, that dream and fact bleed into each other, that nothing stays. Comedy is the natural language of exactly those ideas. Mistaken identity, impersonation, the person who cannot tell the living from the dead, the impossible administered as routine, these are jokes, and they are also the book's philosophy acted out in front of the reader. When Mr. Spitzer confesses that his whole life has been "this joke, almost a celestial joke," he is describing the mechanism of the entire novel.

The comedy is also where the meaning lives, and it lives at the margins rather than the center. The figures who carry the book's deepest feeling are its grotesques and outsiders, the cross-eyed waitress, the hangman, the deaf man, the toy salesman, the bald nursemaid. Young plants her truth in precisely the people a tidier novel would use as comic relief, and by making them absurd and sacred at once she refuses to let the reader choose between the two responses.

That refusal is finally what the whole comic method insists on. The book closes not on a drowned world or a dead child but on a spelling error, "Owt to luntsch, bee bak in a whale," and on the flat aside "For she could not spell either." After more than a thousand pages of loss, Young signs off with a pun, and the choice is not a retreat from seriousness. It is the last line of the argument. In this novel the comic and the elegiac were never two separate things holding each other at bay. They were always the same attention, turned with equal steadiness on a world that is at once ridiculous and beloved.
