In the research: Chapter 2, Chapter 4, Chapter 5, Chapter 8, Chapter 18

Definition from research

Chapter 2

  • Edgar Allen Poe wrote the poem, The Raven, as a narrative, without intentional allegory or didacticism. The main theme of the poem is one of undying devotion. The scholar experiences a perverse conflict between desire to forget and desire to remember. He seems to get some pleasure from focusing on loss. He assumes that the word "Nevermore" is the raven's "only stock and store", and, yet, he continues to ask it questions, knowing what the answer will be. His questions, then, are purposely self-deprecating and further incite his feelings of loss. Poe leaves it unclear whether the raven actually knows what it is saying or whether it really intends to cause a reaction in the poem's narrator. The scholar begins as "weak and weary", becomes regretful and grief-stricken, before passing into a frenzy and, finally, madness. Christopher F. S. Maligec suggests the poem is a type of elegiac paraclausithyron, an ancient Greek and Roman poetic form consisting of the lament of an excluded, locked-out lover at the sealed door of his beloved.

Chapter 4

  • “the silver, the golden, never the leaden bells”: Bells in this chapter symbolize the presence of death and the funeral rites permeating the house, the relentless passage of time and the cycle of life and death, a link between the material and spiritual worlds, calling attention to the unseen and the spectral, the deep, underlying melancholy that shadows the characters' lives and the house itself. Edgar Allan Poe’s poem “The Bells” (1849) moves through the same stages of life, from silver sleigh bells and golden wedding bells to brazen alarm bells and the iron bells of the funeral.

Chapter 5

  • “He had been told of people buried alive and crying in their graves”: While there appears to be an overestimation of the number of people buried alive accidentally in this chapter, in 1885 a victim was found inside his coffin with much of his hair pulled out. The fear was widespread in the nineteenth century. Poe’s story “The Premature Burial” (1844) turns on it, and inventors patented “safety coffins” with bells, air tubes, and flags so that anyone who woke underground could signal.

Chapter 8

  • “that dream within the dream”: This may refer to Edgar Allan Poe’s poem “A Dream within a Dream” (1849), which says, “All that we see or seem / Is but a dream within a dream.” This dream within a dream is a recurring theme throughout the book. The idea that life is a dream goes back to Plato, Aristotle, and Descartes. The first stanza ends with that statement, and the last stanza turns it into a question: “Is all that we see or seem / But a dream within a dream?” This symbolizes the recursive, elusive quality of existence in the novel: a reality constantly refracted through multiple dreamlike states that challenge the notion of a fixed, singular truth.

Chapter 18

  • Edgar Allan Poe's "A Dream Within a Dream" explores the cyclical nature of dreams and the illusion of reality, further reinforcing the idea of life as a dream.
