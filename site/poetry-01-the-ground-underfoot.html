Exploring Young's Poetry

The Ground Underfoot

Exploring Young's Poetry · Radical Immanence Explained →

The question that started this book was a small one. A reader at Involutions of the Seashell, on Substack, asked whether Marguerite Young’s poetry carried any of the themes of her novel, Miss MacIntosh, My Darling. The honest first answer was uncertainty. The poems had been read, but not with confidence, and poetry is a harder country to feel sure of than prose and there was little certainty back then to feel sure about even that. So the book began as an attempt to answer one reader’s fair question: do the poems connect to the novel?

They do. That answer held, poem after poem, and it was worth making. But in the making, the question turned out to be too small for what the poems were doing. Young said as much herself. She held that all her writing came from a single set of preoccupations, ones she had been turning over since she was nine years old. If that is so, then the poems do not connect to Miss MacIntosh, My Darling the way a stream connects to a river, running into it and vanishing. They connect the way four windows connect. Each opens on to the same room from a different wall.

This second edition follows that correction. It stops treating the poems as a doorway into one novel and starts treating them as one full statement, among four, of a single vision that runs under everything Young wrote. The vision needs a name, and the name this series uses is Radical Immanence. Both of its words are the kind that can hide more than they show, so both are worth going in depth with.
