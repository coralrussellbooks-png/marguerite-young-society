Definition from research

Mr. Spitzer carries more references than anyone else in the novel, and they come in two shapes: pairs and the afterlife. His brother's house hangs with "the twin George Washingtons fording the twin Delawares" and "the twin President Garfields" (chapter 55). He confuses "Captain Cook and Admiral Perry" (chapter 54). He cannot tell one imaginary Napoleon from another. A man who lost his twin sees doubles everywhere.

His afterlife is mapped in detail. He carries a coin "in his purse for old Charon when he should make his last passage" (chapter 53). He wonders when "he should come to the last infernal circle of this Divine Comedy of life and death" (chapter 29). His mind moves between the upper and lower worlds, for "Mr. Spitzer's was an escalator psychology" (chapter 54). He hopes that "eternal recurrence" might be "the one law of life" (chapter 42), which would bring his brother back.

Mr. Spitzer is also the novel's naturalist. Myth survives in him as the Latin names of butterflies, such as "the butterfly Aphrodite, named after that goddess of beauty and love" (chapter 29). His catalogs reach even to the names of God: "God was the Narrow Way, the Rose, the Thorn. God was many things to many people" (chapter 29). He knows "a whole Decameron of sports" (chapter 53). His learning is vast and gentle, and it never quite reaches a conclusion.
