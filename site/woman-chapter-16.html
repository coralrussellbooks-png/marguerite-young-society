Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 16

← Chapter 15 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 17 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Falada · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Vera rides Falada, her old black pony, through the ruined garden on her birthday, and the ride locates her mind outside her body. Falada carries the name of the speaking horse's head from the fairy tale, and Vera says the horse "seemed to be my head, my intelligence," which makes her "the headless rider." This is a girl's exhilaration and freedom, and it is the detachable self, the intelligence set loose from the body and carried by an animal, the head motif the book has run since the head-hunter wanted Miss MacIntosh's and the villagers grew heads beneath their shoulders. The self here is distributed between rider and horse, plural before Vera has even dismounted.

She dreams herself into other bodies as she rides, a comedian with a mustache, a bareback rider in pink tights, and the mustache is the crossing point tucked into a daydream, gender tried on and set down. Falada goes "winged" over church steeples, the ordinary pony transcending the solid world, real and dream as one country.

The ride ends at the tomb-like house, its marble courts, its "garden of the blind," its silence over still water, a place where erasure and the dead are the weather. Vera passes Catherine asleep among sea shells and "little fish sleepers" and goes instead to Miss MacIntosh's door. The web on the doorknob, the creak, the moonbeams, and then recognition with an "older shock" underneath it: the changed woman is already waiting, and the chapter ends a breath before the book shows what the change is.
