Miss Georgia MacIntosh: The Sensible Woman in the Wig

← Vera Cartwheel · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Jock Cartwheel →

Spoiler note: This portrait is written for readers who have finished the novel. It discusses the character’s whole story, including how it ends.

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

You meet her as the correction. She is hired. Into the great sea-blackened house where the mother dreams her life away on opium, someone sends a plain, sensible nursemaid to raise the child the dreamer will not, and that is Miss MacIntosh, arriving with her black umbrella and her Pilgrim's Progress and her rule that there is a place for everything because being the correction, the answer, the solid ground the others are measured against, is the role she plays and the role the novel is built to take away from her.

The facts of her are all plain, and the plainness is the point. She is Georgia MacIntosh of What Cheer, Iowa, the middle child of a poor but honest family, daughter of a little shoemaker who also built coffins for other people. There was no room for her at home, no nest. She worked her way through two years at the University of Iowa waiting tables and washing and ironing the shirts of the men students, and learned little more than she already knew from the Farmers' Almanac, how to plow stony ground, how to protect cherry buds from frost, how to break ice for water. No young man ever looked at her twice. Beauty was not hers. She has a broken nose, a whale-boned corset, rimless glasses, no paint on her face, and red hair the color of an August sunset. Her religion is truth to nature and nothing else. She wears blinders on purpose. Once, walking with Vera, she covers the girl's eyes rather than let her carry away the memory of sleigh bells hung in a blossoming cherry tree, because such confusion, she says, is not the mirror of truth. Sleigh bells belong in a world of snow. There is a place for everything.

Ask the wrong question and she is simply the sane one, the standard, the character with no mystery because she is the thing mystery is measured against. Ask the right one and she is the deepest problem in the book. The question Radical Immanence puts to her is not why is she so plain, but what becomes of a person who insists that reality is single, given, found, and never made? Every other figure in the novel lives as though reality is something you build and move into. Miss MacIntosh alone denies it. She is the one who insists the real is simply there, the same for everyone, waiting to be reported and not composed. The whole book is arranged to answer what happens to such a person, and the answer is a wig.

Read one way, she is the cure. She is brought in as Catherine's correction, the discipline set against the dream, the daylight sent into the opium house, the sensible woman against the addict. She is the title character, the one the whole book is named for and aimed at, the visible center everyone watches. Marguerite Young called her the hub. She is reality itself, walking around in sensible shoes, there to save the child from the mother's madness.

Then, at midnight, Vera sees her bald. The red hair is gone, the whole great dome shorn and shining and sweating, the body flat and shorn beneath it, and Vera, horrified, screams "Miss MacIntosh, you are a man!" The wig is a wig. The solidity is a costume. The one person in the house who policed the real, turns out to be the most disguised soul in it, the bald pretender who had been pulling the wool over everyone's eyes for years. It would be easy to file this as an unmasking, the sensible woman exposed as a fraud, and to feel the cheap superiority of having caught her. The novel refuses to be read that way in a single stroke. The child, who might have recoiled, says instead that Miss MacIntosh was more real now than she had ever been. The disguise coming off does not make her false. It makes her true.

That is the reversal the whole book turns on, and it is the same discovery Catherine's story makes from the other side. Catherine built a dream world out of opium and the illustrious dead and moved into it for good. Miss MacIntosh built a sensible world out of a wig and a corset and a book of maxims and moved into that. Both are constructed realities. Both are a self assembled to bear a life that could not otherwise be borne, a poor plain unloved woman making, out of discipline and false hair, a person solid enough that a whole household could lean on her and call her the ground. The only difference between the two women is that Catherine knew hers was a dream and Miss MacIntosh insisted hers was the given world. That insistence is the one thing she is wrong about, and it is the one thing she cannot let go of, because it is load-bearing. To admit the wig is a wig is to admit that her reality was made, like everyone else's, and that admission is the single truth her whole life was built to keep out.

This is why she is sympathetic and wrong at once, and why the novel loves her exactly where it corrects her. Her construction is the bravest kind, a self hammered together by a woman who had nothing, no beauty, no love, no room at home, and held up steady enough to raise another woman's child. Her error is not a lie she told the world. It is the one confession she could not make to herself. And she cannot survive being seen without the disguise. She walks into the loud sea and leaves everything in wild confusion, her galoshes thrown back water-logged by the tide, her Pilgrim's Progress swollen with salt, she who believed there was a place for everything. The woman who preached truth to nature drowns herself rather than be witnessed as she is. She could not live inside the single given reality she preached, because it was never there, and the moment the child saw through the wig, the ground went out from under her too.

Her disappearance is where the whole book begins. Vera's bus is aimed at her, the long search for the lost love who may or may not be dead. The body is never found, which is why Mr. Spitzer, keeping faith with the missing as he keeps faith with all the dead, will not sign her into the grave. Until the body is found, he says, we cannot assume that she is dead. So the woman who insisted the real was a matter of found facts becomes the one fact that can never be found, the missing corpus delicti, the case that stays open forever. Even her ending refuses to be single and given. She spent her life reporting a world of settled facts and left the book a world in which the central fact, her own death, cannot be settled at all.

The lesson for a writer is in how Young builds the character who is wrong without turning her into a fool or a villain. Miss MacIntosh is the most beloved person in the house, the one the child would die or run away for, and the novel never argues against her. It simply springs the wig and lets the reader feel, in the same instant, the horror and the tenderness, and then, worse and better than either, the recognition that this brave assembled woman was doing all along the very thing she denied anyone could do, making a reality to stand up in. You refute a character most completely not by defeating her in an argument but by revealing that she and her opposite were performing the same act the whole time. The sensible woman and the dreamer were both building somewhere to live. One of them just refused, to the end, to call it a building.
