Chapter 67: Move Over, Doc

← Chapter 66 · Guide Home · Chapter 68 →

Research notes: Chapter 67

Characters: Homer Capehorn · Justice O’Leary · Lorena · Madge Capehorn · Miss MacIntosh · Moses Hunnecker · Mr. Titus Bonebreaker · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Love

Symbols: Pregnancy, Birth, and Children · The Bus and the Journey

Philosophy: Augustine and Christian Theology · Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Exploration and the Poles · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest

We are back on the bus.

After four chapters in the deep past of Miss MacIntosh's life (her baldness, Lorena, Mr. Bonebreaker, the Arctic Explorer, the last month, the clothes on the beach) Young returns us to the present tense of the novel: Vera on a bus in the grey American Midwest, searching for What Cheer, Iowa. Moses Hunnecker is still driving. Madge and Homer are still aboard. The bus has arrived in Indiana, in front of a tavern.

And Moses cannot park.

What actually happens

The bus pulls up to a tavern in Indiana and stops. Moses wants to park. He cannot park because, he insists, an old Ford belonging to someone he calls "old Doc" is blocking the space. The passengers cannot get off the bus. Moses addresses old Doc directly, at length, cursing, pleading, reasoning, and threatening, while the passengers rock in their seats with something he calls "infinite weariness." Madge laughs breathlessly. Homer daydreams about a bride. Vera sits and watches and feels tired and dreamlike.

Old Doc does not move. He may not exist. The bus does not park. Nobody gets off.

That is the entirety of Chapter 67's external event. It is also, in its way, everything.

Who is old Doc?

Moses talks to him as if he is there: an unseen presence in an old ghost buggy, a Ford without essential parts, blocking the space. Children, Moses says, shoot at him with bean-shooters. He is dead from the shoulders down. He is the undertaker in a hearse, or perhaps he is the doctor who became an undertaker, which is to say the best doctor of all.

Vera briefly contemplates this: old Doc as undertaker, death wearing a medical coat. Young leaves it open, as she always does. Old Doc might be a real person Moses knows, a former local doctor who parks badly and ignores horns. He might be a ghost. He might be death itself, parked immovably in the one spot the living need to reach.

What Moses wants, very simply, is for death to move over. To give way. To let the bus get where it's going.

Death does not move over.

Moses and the rules of traffic

Moses's frustration erupts into a lecture about traffic order: chalk lines, detour signs, slow-turn signals, the basic courtesies that keep movement possible. He is a man who believes in rules, even as he drives erratically, drinks openly, and calls on angels from behind the wheel. He believes the road should work by logic. If everyone followed the chalk lines, the traffic would flow, the buses would park, the living could get off and get on with it.

But old Doc's ghost buggy doesn't follow chalk lines. The past doesn't consult detour signs. Death is not interested in yield requirements.

This is Moses's essential predicament, and in a way it is everyone's on this bus: the laws of order and forward motion keep failing to account for what is actually blocking the road.

Madge on the ferris wheel

Madge describes their situation with a precision that cuts through Moses's noise: it is like being stalled at the top of a ferris wheel in the sky, she says. Like being trapped in a crazy house with mirrored walls and no doors.

Madge is pregnant, unhappy, returning to a place she doesn't want to return to, married to a man sleeping peacefully beside her. She knows about being stalled. She knows about mirrored walls. Her laughter (high and thin, breathless) is the laughter of someone who has given up expecting things to move.

Homer, meanwhile, daydreams about a bride. He fears his wife might never know the bride he envisions, which may simply mean he daydreams of a woman who is not Madge, or may mean something more obscure, a future self no one can reach from here. Homer is always asleep or dreaming when the important things happen. He is the person on the bus least troubled by old Doc's ghost buggy, which is the same as saying he is the person least troubled by death.

"I can't give you anything but love, baby"

Moses, draped over the steering wheel, sings this softly to himself: a 1928 song born from a couple window-shopping at Tiffany's, a man saying to his beloved: I can't buy you the sparkler. I can only give you this. Moses is a man of grandiose faith and spectacular failure, driving a bus that can't park, addressing a ghost who won't move, singing a tender song about the limits of what love can offer.

Young chooses her songs as carefully as she chooses her names. This one fits Moses exactly: all the feeling, none of the resources.

Back to the beginning

Young makes a structural move here. We have just come through four chapters of Miss MacIntosh's death: the accumulation of her life told in the last month she had, ending with clothes on a beach and a dog barking at the sea. That is the novel's emotional climax, in many ways. It is what Vera's journey is moving toward and away from simultaneously.

And now: Moses. A tavern in Indiana. A ghost buggy blocking the space.

Young returns us to the bus not to deflate what came before, but to remind us that the novel has always been both things at once: the vast interior tragedy and the small comic frustration, the philosophical abyss and the man shouting at a parking space. The bus keeps moving even when it can't park. Vera keeps searching even after the beach. The wheel turns. Moses drives on.

What to hold onto

Moses Hunnecker, slumped over the steering wheel of a bus that cannot park, singing softly to a ghost who will not move, while his passengers rock in infinite weariness and the Indiana night settles in around them. The chalk lines don't account for this. Neither does anything else. And yet the bus will move again, eventually, and they will get where they are going.

Or they won't. But Moses will keep driving regardless.
