Characters: Esther Longtree

“Her parents had never consummated their marriage but had only dreamed”: Purity culture is a movement emphasizing abstinence until heterosexual marriage, strict gender roles, and modesty, often creating intense sexual shame and anxiety. It uses rituals like purity balls, rings, and pledges to control sexual expression, placing the burden of sexual responsibility on women and girls to prevent male lust. Many raised in this culture report anxiety, fear, and difficulties with intimacy, even after marrying. There is a growing movement of former adherents who are deconstructing these teachings and addressing the trauma caused by them. Its influence persists through different ministries and a continued focus on “biblical womanhood.” Purity culture, with its rings and pledges, arose in the 1990s. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.

“an old spiritualist medium”: Modern Spiritualism is widely considered to have begun in March 1848 in Hydesville, New York, following the claims of the Fox sisters, Margaret and Kate, who reported hearing mysterious rappings that they believed were spirit messages. The movement originated in an area of upstate New York known as the “Burned-over District,” which was already a center for religious fervor and movements like Millerism and Mormonism during the Second Great Awakening. The movement quickly spread, with early adherents seeking direct communication with the spirits of the deceased. By the 1850s, it had spread across the country and internationally. In the 1850s, the movement attracted the attention of scientists who began investigating the reported supernatural occurrences. The credibility of the movement was challenged, especially in 1888 when the Fox sisters confessed to faking the original rappings. However, many believers continued their faith, and the movement adapted. Influential figures like Emma Hardinge Britten, a medium and lecturer, used the platform of Spiritualism to advocate for social causes like women’s rights and temperance. Spiritualism experienced a major revival after World War I, as many people sought to connect with loved ones who had died in the war. The movement continued to develop organizations and gather followers, with communities like Cassadaga, Florida, becoming known as centers for spiritualist activities. Throughout the mid-20th century, Spiritualism persisted, adapting to new generations and social changes, and leaving a lasting impact on American religious and cultural history. See Spiritualism and the Occult and American Religious Movements. Spiritualism is the metaphysical school of thought opposing physicalism and also is the category of all spiritual beliefs from ancient to modern. They believe that spirit mediums are gifted to carry on such communication, but that anyone may become a medium through study and practice. They believe that spirits are capable of growth and perfection, progressing through higher spheres or planes, and that the afterlife is not a static state, but one in which spirits evolve. The two beliefs, that contact with spirits is possible, and that spirits may dwell on a higher plane, lead to a third belief, that spirits can provide knowledge about moral and ethical issues, as well as about God and the afterlife. Many believers speak of spirit guides or specific spirits, often contacted, and relied upon for worldly and spiritual guidance. According to Spiritualists, anyone may receive spirit messages, but formal communication sessions are held by mediums, who claim thereby to receive information about the afterlife. Spiritualism developed and reached its peak growth in membership from the 1840s to the 1920s, especially in English-speaking countries. By 1897, spiritualism was said to have more than eight million followers in the United States and Europe, mostly drawn from the middle and upper classes.

"her own was the virgin birth," Esther says, "but she was Jesus Christ or anybody holy," and yet "popular and vulgar... dropping stillborn," the sacred claim collapsing into her earthy, grieving life.

"A dove of God... impregnated Mary," so "Christ was born and fatherless in the barnyard," retells the Annunciation, the Spirit as a dove, in her own rough, homely terms.

“that Christ was the youngest brother”: The Gospels make Jesus the eldest: “Is not this the carpenter, the son of Mary, the brother of James, and Joses, and of Juda, and Simon? and are not his sisters here with us?” (Mark 6:3, KJV). See The Bible. Earlier in Chapter 3.

“This third person, this dark woman”: A “dark woman” who assisted with births may refer to the following: 1) the Biblical midwife Shiphrah, one of the “Hebrew midwives” of Exodus 1:15, though some readers have taken her and Puah for Egyptians. Shiphrah, along with her colleague Puah, defied the Pharaoh’s order to kill newborn Hebrew males. 2) Biddy Mason, who was born into slavery, was a midwife and advocate for other enslaved people in 19th-century California. After gaining her freedom, she became a successful entrepreneur and philanthropist. 3) Margaret Charles Smith was an African American midwife in Alabama who delivered over 3,000 babies during a career spanning over 30 years, often in rural areas with limited healthcare access. 4) Mary Francis Hill Coley was a midwife from Georgia who delivered over 3,000 babies during her career. Her work was featured in the 1952 documentary, All My Babies. 5) Susie Carey was a third-generation midwife featured in a photograph at the National Museum of African American History and Culture. 6) Sarah Hudson Grant was a Black Southern midwife whose work is remembered today. See Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery, Society, Class, and Race, Judaism, and Ancient Egypt.

“in the twinkling of an eye”: See the Chapter 72 resources. “In a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trump” (1 Corinthians 15:52, KJV). See The Bible. Earlier in Chapters 37, 58 and 72.

“the other woman carrying candle, book, bell, funeral wreath”: The phrase “bell, book, and candle” refers to a Latin Christian method of excommunication by anathema, imposed on a person who had committed an exceptionally grievous sin. Evidently introduced by Pope Zachary around the middle of the 8th century, the rite was once used by the Latin Church. This form of excommunication was inflicted on Robert II of France by Pope Gregory V for his marriage to Bertha of Burgundy in the year 996, because Bertha was his second cousin. He was later reconciled with the Church after negotiations with Gregory’s successor Pope Silvester II. (Wikipedia) See Augustine and Christian Theology.

“of ladders to heaven”: “And he dreamed, and behold a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven” (Genesis 28:12, KJV). See Genesis. Earlier in Chapters 14, 29, 36, 38, 48 and 52.

“the grey hen crowing on the bed-post”: A crowing hen was an omen of ill luck: “A whistling woman and a crowing hen / Are neither fit for God nor men.” See Fairy Tales and Folklore and Birds.

“Her innocency had been spoiled by men, women, children”: “I will wash mine hands in innocency” (Psalm 26:6, KJV); innocency is the King James word. See The Bible.
