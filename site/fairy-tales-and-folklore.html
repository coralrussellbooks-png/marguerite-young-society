Chapter 2

  • The Headless Horseman has appeared in folklore around the world since the Middle Ages.

Chapter 3

  • “Like the princess, I would let down my hair, and he should climb it as if it were a rope.” - may refer to Rapunzel like folklore and fairytales. Many scholars have interpreted "Maiden in the Tower" stories, which Rapunzel is a part of, as a metaphor for the protection of young women from premarital relationships by overzealous guardians. Scholars have drawn comparisons of the confinement of Rapunzel in her tower to that of a convent, where women's lives were highly controlled and they lived in exclusion from outsiders. Scholars have also noted the strong theme of love conquering all in the story, as the lovers are united after years of searching in all versions after Persinette and are ultimately happily reunited as a family. The "Maiden in the Tower" archetype has drawn comparisons to a possible lost matriarchal myth connected to the sacred marriage between the prince and the maiden and the rivalry between the maiden, representing life and spring, and the crone, representing death and winter.

Chapter 6

  • “her little shoes which danced and danced without her”: In “The Red Shoes” (1845) by Hans Christian Andersen a vain girl is condemned to dance even after she dies. The red shoes represent Madge’s precarious balance between appearance and reality, innocence and corruption, freedom and constraint. They are a vivid symbol of her tragic, conflicted condition as she rides the bus toward an uncertain fate. In the tale the executioner cuts off Karen’s feet, and the red shoes dance away with them, much as Jacqueline dances “on one foot.”
  • “Tell them Papa’s gone a-hunting the rabbits”: Rabbits can symbolize good luck, prosperity, and abundance. The symbol of rabbits in the chapter encompasses innocence, vulnerability, lost youth, and the pervasive presence of death that shadows life in the novel. The nursery rhyme runs, “Bye, baby Bunting, / Daddy’s gone a-hunting, / Gone to get a rabbit skin / To wrap the baby Bunting in.”
  • “leading the dance in your little glass shoes”: Cinderella’s glass slipper comes from Charles Perrault’s tale (1697), and Madge travels in “glass-heeled shoes.”
  • “this sleeping beauty who never dreamed of her”: In Perrault’s and the Grimms’ tale a kiss wakes the sleeping princess. Here the bridegroom sleeps, and Madge says later, “Your kiss will not restore her beauty.”
  • “Ding, dong, bell”: Ariel’s song for a drowned father in The Tempest: “Full fathom five thy father lies . . . Ding-dong, bell.” Madge’s father drowned. The nursery rhyme “Ding, dong, bell, / Pussy’s in the well” also ends in drowning.
  • “Everyone pays the piper”: In the tale of the Pied Piper of Hamelin, the town that would not pay the piper lost its children.
  • “Jack Frost who paints the window pane white”: The nursery rhyme runs, “Jack be nimble, Jack be quick, / Jack jump over the candlestick.” Madge’s string of Jacks, Jack Robinson, Jack Frost, little Jack Fleet, Jack White, Jack-in-the-pulpit, and later Homer’s jack-in-the-box, all circle back to Jackie.
  • “Death is an old hunter. Wait until you hear his winding horn”: In the legend of the Wild Hunt a ghostly huntsman rides through the winter sky with baying hounds, and to hear him meant death.

Chapter 7

  • “The stork knoweth her times”: “Yea, the stork in the heaven knoweth her appointed times” (Jeremiah 8:7). It symbolizes the natural, inevitable rhythms of life and birth that persist despite human resistance or denial, and it resonates with the novel’s broader themes concerning the tension between illusion and reality, the passage of time, and the cycles of life and death. Hans Christian Andersen’s “The Storks” (1838) helped spread the story that storks bring babies, a fit verse for a doctor of births.
  • “somebody saying it looked like the phoenix”: The phoenix burns itself and rises from its own ashes.
  • “Her children played ring-around-a-rosy”: The rhyme was first printed in 1881. The story that it describes the plague is a twentieth-century invention.
  • “Little pitchers have big ears”: The proverb is in John Heywood’s collection of 1546.

Chapter 8

  • “Who killed the old horse? Who killed the sparrows?”: The nursery rhyme asks, “Who killed Cock Robin? / I, said the Sparrow.”

Chapter 10

  • Lucifer “who was the brightest and is fallen farthest” is the name of different figures in folklore associated with the planet Venus. “How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!” (Isaiah 14:12).
  • “live happily ever after as in an old fairy tale”: The fairy-tale ending, “and they lived happily ever after.”
  • “Her face was certainly not her fortune”: The nursery rhyme’s milkmaid answers, “My face is my fortune, sir, she said.”

Chapter 11

  • “Rimski-Korsakov who never came except in Mr. Spitzer’s absence”: Rimski-Korsakov was a prominent Russian composer, a member of the Mighty Handful, five composers who drew on Russian folk music, and a significant figure in shaping Russian classical music. He is known for his operas, orchestral pieces like Scheherazade (1888), named for the bride who tells a tale night after night to stay alive, and Capriccio Espagnol. He sailed around the world as a young naval officer.
  • “the sister ravens, the brother doves”: The Grimms’ “The Seven Ravens” and Giambattista Basile’s “The Seven Doves” (1634-36) both tell of brothers changed into birds and a sister who saves them. Young makes the ravens sisters.
  • “a wild swan absent, one wild swan more”: In Andersen’s “The Wild Swans” (1838), eleven brothers are turned into swans, and their sister weaves nettle shirts to free them. The last shirt is unfinished, and the youngest is left with a swan’s wing.

Chapter 12

  • “twelve white swans like brothers or suitors bewitched”: Andersen’s eleven brothers turned into swans, and the bewitched swan maidens of Tchaikovsky’s Swan Lake (1877).
  • “headless horsemen ride about in purple fog”: Washington Irving’s “The Legend of Sleepy Hollow” (1820).
  • “her face was not, it was simply not her fortune”: The nursery rhyme’s milkmaid: “My face is my fortune, sir, she said.”

Chapter 15

  • “the grasshoppers are always surprised by winter”: Aesop’s “The Ant and the Grasshopper,” and “Go to the ant, thou sluggard; consider her ways, and be wise” (Proverbs 6:6).
  • “the crooked crab which can never be taught to walk a way that is not crooked”: Aesop’s “The Crab and Its Mother,” in which the mother tells her child to walk straight and cannot do it herself.

Chapter 16

  • “riding my black pony, Falada”: In the Brothers Grimm fairy tale “The Goose Girl” (1815), a princess is sent off to marry with her magical horse Falada, who can talk, and a waiting maid. The maid forces the princess to change places with her and, once she is the bride, has the prince kill Falada so he cannot tell. The princess has the horse’s head nailed under the dark gateway, and each morning it speaks to her as she passes: “Alas, young Queen, how ill you fare! / If this your tender mother knew, / Her heart would surely break in two.” In the end the king learns the truth and the false bride is punished. Falada means “spoken” in Portuguese.
  • Mr. Spitzer calls it “an old Arabic fairy tale which had its parallels in many countries, in many ages,” and tells of the head that “had spoken oracles and parables to the people passing through the gates.”
  • “Miss MacIntosh was an old witch who kept me enchained in a dark tower”: Rapunzel, locked in a tower by a witch.
  • “I was the headless rider”: “The Legend of Sleepy Hollow” again.
  • “there would be a ding and a dong and a bell through all the rooms”: The nursery rhyme “Ding, dong, bell, / Pussy’s in the well,” and Ariel’s song in The Tempest.

Chapter 17

  • “the mountains shall never meet with the mountains”: “Friends may meet, but mountains never greet,” an old proverb.
  • “you shall praise the bridge that carried you”: “Praise the bridge that carries you over,” an English proverb.

Chapter 18

  • “one who combs her long gold hair upon the rock”: Heine’s Lorelei (1824), the Rhine siren who combs her golden hair on the rock and draws boatmen to their deaths. Clemens Brentano first told of her in a ballad of 1801. The body “scaled with silver scales” that follows is the mermaid’s.
  • “hold with the hounds while she ran with the hares”: John Heywood’s proverb (1546).
  • “the crooked way of the crab”: Aesop’s crab, who cannot walk straight.

Chapter 19

  • “beauty is as beauty does, and beauty is more than skin deep”: Miss MacIntosh bends two proverbs. “Handsome is that handsome does” is Mrs. Primrose’s in Goldsmith’s The Vicar of Wakefield (1766). “Beauty is but skin deep” goes back to Sir Thomas Overbury’s poem “A Wife” (1613): “All the carnall beautie of my wife, / Is but skinne-deepe.”

Chapter 20

  • “Had some old crow plucked each hair away?”: In Aesop’s fable, a middle-aged man had two sweethearts. The young one plucked out his grey hairs, the old one his dark ones, until he was bald.

Chapter 22

  • “the Islands of the Blessed or the Islands of the Dead”: Arnold Böcklin’s painting Isle of the Dead (1880) inspired Rachmaninoff’s tone poem (1909).
  • “An old dog barks at nothing”: This turns around the proverb of Chapter 15, “An old dog barks not in vain.”
  • “Where is the Giants’ Causeway?”: The Giants’ Causeway is an area of basalt columns in Northern Ireland. In legend, Finn McCool (Fingal) built it, and it is said to run under the sea to Fingal’s Cave.

Chapter 23

  • “We make mountains of molehills”: In print by 1548 (Nicholas Udall).
  • “had always brought gold in their mouths”: The German proverb “Morgenstunde hat Gold im Munde,” “the morning hour has gold in its mouth.”
  • “a new broom sweeps clean”: John Heywood’s proverb (1546).

Chapter 26

  • “each having nine lives”: “A woman hath nine lives like a cat” (John Heywood, 1546).
  • “and headless horsemen”: Washington Irving’s “The Legend of Sleepy Hollow” (1820).
  • “held it against him that he did not rescue her, carry her away as Peron might have done”: Catherine is expecting a man to save her, which is the damsel in distress. This is a narrative device in which one or more men must rescue a woman who has been kidnapped or placed in other peril. The “damsel” is often portrayed as beautiful, popular, and of high social status; she is usually depicted as a princess in works with fantasy or fairy tale settings. Kinship, love, lust or a combination of those motivate the male protagonist to initiate the narrative. Critics have linked the helplessness of these women to societal views that women as a group need to be taken care of by men and treated nicely. Throughout the history of the trope, the role of the woman as the victim in need of a male savior has remained constant, but her attackers have changed to suit the tastes and collective fears of the period: “monsters, mad scientists, Nazis, hippies, bikers, aliens…” (Wikipedia)

Chapter 27

  • “storks’ nests on the roof-tops”: In northern Europe a stork’s nest on the roof brought luck, and storks brought babies. Hans Christian Andersen’s “The Storks” (1838).

Chapter 28

  • “the imaginary sea bishops with mitre-shaped heads and vestments of silver scales”: The Sea Bishop, a fish-man in a bishop’s mitre, was said to have been caught off Poland in 1531. It is pictured in Conrad Gesner’s Historia animalium (1558).
  • “the mermaids feeding their infants at their breasts”: Mermaids nursing their young.

Chapter 28 · The Enchanted Dream World

  • Filled with fantastical figures and surreal imagery (e.g., snow owls with human faces, headless horsemen, mermaids).
  • Symbolizes the blurred line between reality and illusion, life and death, sanity and madness.

Chapter 29

  • “According to the Greek, the butterfly was psyche”: Butterflies feature prominently in mythology across various cultures. They often symbolize the soul, death, rebirth and transformation and, to some, good luck and messengers from the spirit world. Psyche, in Greek mythology a goddess of the soul, is often depicted with butterfly wings, symbolizing her connection to the soul and transformation. They symbolize the complex interplay of life, death, transformation, and the soul’s journey, embodying the novel’s central motifs of illusion, fleeting beauty, and the search for meaning beyond the physical world. They are a poetic representation of the transient yet enduring nature of existence as seen through Mr. Spitzer’s meditative perspective.
  • “the bed of the sleeping beauty who would sleep until she was awakened by the kiss of a fairy prince”: Perrault’s “La Belle au bois dormant” (1697) and the Grimms’ “Briar Rose” (1812).
  • “and all the dogs of creation would bark at him”: The nursery rhyme “Hark, hark, the dogs do bark, / The beggars are coming to town; / Some in rags, and some in tags.”

Chapter 30

  • “Cousin Hannah Freemount-Snowden, the jolly old soul”: “Old King Cole / Was a merry old soul” (nursery rhyme, in print by 1708).

Chapter 31

  • “forbidden cities of brides, those who were held in thralldom by a cruel bridegroom”: It recalls Perrault’s “Bluebeard” (1697), whose dead brides hang in the forbidden room.

Chapter 32

  • “No one had awakened the sleeping lady to life and love”: This characterization of Catherine resembles the folktale of Sleeping Beauty which is a classic fairy tale about a princess who is cursed to sleep for a hundred years by an evil fairy, to be awakened by a handsome prince at the end of that time. The good fairy, realizing that the princess would be frightened if alone when she awakens, uses her wand to put every living person and animal in the palace asleep, to awaken when the princess does. Early origins and versions of the tale involve rape, cannibalism and Sleeping Beauty giving birth to children while she is asleep (similar to noncanonical books, as in the Gnostic Hypostasis of the Archons, in which the rulers of the world rape Eve). Catherine embodies the Sleeping Beauty archetype as a woman caught in a protracted, enchanted sleep between life and death, isolated in a dreamlike world filled with phantoms, awaiting an uncertain awakening. Her physical stillness, the canary of Chapter 2, the grand but empty house, and the thematic concerns with time, death, and unfulfilled love strongly evoke the folktale’s motifs and deepen the novel’s exploration of illusion versus reality. Through Catherine, Young reimagines the Sleeping Beauty myth, using it to probe themes of mortality, memory, and the elusive boundary between waking life and dreams.
  • “as the robber bridegroom carries the swooning bride”: In the Grimms’ “The Robber Bridegroom” (1812), a bride discovers that her bridegroom murders young women. Eudora Welty retold it as a novel, The Robber Bridegroom (1942).
  • “weird huntsmen and barking dogs”: The Wild Hunt of European folklore, a ghostly troop of huntsmen and hounds riding the storm at night.
  • “her wedding gown had been torn to shreds by brambles”: In the Grimms’ “Little Briar Rose,” a hedge of thorns grows around the sleeping castle, and princes who try to pass die caught in it.
  • “this uninvited wedding guest”: In the Grimms’ “Little Briar Rose” (1812), the thirteenth wise woman, not invited to the feast, curses the child. Coleridge’s Ancient Mariner also stops a Wedding-Guest at the door.

Chapter 34

  • “had eloped with the robber bridegroom”: The Robber Bridegroom is a novella by Eudora Welty which used the Cupid and Psyche myth.

Chapter 36

  • “then all the skies might fall upon his head”: Celtic envoys told Alexander the Great that they feared nothing except that the sky should fall on them (Arrian, Anabasis 1.4). In the folktale of Henny Penny, or Chicken Little, an acorn on the head convinces a hen that the sky is falling.
  • “Should she send a slow messenger to overtake the swift”: In Aesop’s fable the slow tortoise overtakes the swift hare, who stops to sleep.

Chapter 38

  • “the Prince Charming to awaken this Sleeping Beauty”: In Perrault’s “Sleeping Beauty” (1697) the whole household sleeps for a hundred years with the princess, until the prince comes.

Chapter 39

  • “the porcelain drug bottle with very large ears”: Tacamahac, or tacamahaca, is an aromatic resin once sold by apothecaries, and Mr. Res Tacamah may take his name from it. The “glowering genii” recall the Arabian Nights tale of the fisherman who uncorks a brass bottle from the sea and lets out a jinni.

Chapter 41

  • “their skeleton grooms sleeping”: When the princess pricks her finger, “The horses, too, went to sleep in the stable, the dogs in the yard, the pigeons upon the roof, the flies on the wall” (Brothers Grimm, “Little Briar-Rose,” 1812, trans. Margaret Hunt, 1884). See the Sleeping Beauty in the Chapter 29 resources.
  • “growing old over night”: If Catherine really woke up she would rapidly age and die, as in old tales and novels. Oisín, returning from Tír na nÓg after three hundred years, touches the ground and becomes an old man. Ayesha in Haggard’s She (1887) withers in a moment (see the Chapter 6 resources). In James Hilton’s Lost Horizon (1933), Lo-Tsen leaves Shangri-La and reaches the outside world an ancient woman. Washington Irving’s Rip Van Winkle (1819) wakes after twenty years (see the Chapter 36 resources). The rapid aging can be a way to explore mortality and the inevitable passage of time.

Chapter 42

  • “never by robin redbreast covering him with the autumn leaf”: “Call for the robin-red-breast and the wren, / Since o’er shady groves they hover, / And with leaves and flowers do cover / The friendless bodies of unburied men” (John Webster, The White Devil 5.4, 1612). In the ballad of the Babes in the Wood, robins cover the dead children with leaves.
  • “the strength of this snow maiden”: In the Russian tale of Snegurochka, the Snow Maiden, she melts when love enters her heart (Alexander Ostrovsky’s play, 1873; Rimsky-Korsakov’s opera, 1882).

Chapter 43

  • “my wild swan girl”: In folklore the swan maiden sheds her feathered robe to become a woman, and whoever steals the robe can keep her. Tchaikovsky’s Swan Lake (1877) gives the story its best-known form.

Chapter 44

  • “snowdrops delicate as a wraith of mist”: The snowdrop flowers through the snow at Candlemas and stands for hope, but an old English belief held that a snowdrop brought into the house foretold a death (Roy Vickery, Oxford Dictionary of Plant-Lore, 1995).

Chapter 45

  • “this sleeping beauty whom all ladies become”: See Grimm’s “Little Briar-Rose” in the Chapter 41 resources.
  • “a virgin’s blood staining the snow”: In Grimm’s “Little Snow-White,” the queen pricks her finger and wishes for “a child as white as snow, as red as blood, and as black as the wood of the window-frame” (trans. Margaret Hunt, 1884). Hannah’s girl is “white as the snow upon the mountains,” and “Black was my true love’s heart though white was her skin.”

Chapter 46

  • “he had been followed by men in the grey cities”: Like the Pied Piper, the musician is followed first by beasts and then by men.

Chapter 47

  • “red as her blood, white as the snow”: See Grimm’s “Little Snow-White” in the Chapter 45 resources.
  • “He heard the singing of a dead Troubadour”: See Jaufré Rudel and the troubadours in the Chapter 40 resources and the Troubadour’s love for the dead love in the Chapter 44 resources. The lady “never awakening from her enchanted sleep” recalls Sleeping Beauty.

Chapter 48

  • “forty keys with which to open forty trunks”: In Perrault’s “Bluebeard” (1697), a husband gives his wife the keys to every room, forbids one small key, and keeps the bodies of his former wives behind its door. Here the keys belong to the wife, who fought for those “imprisoned by their cruel husbands and lords.”

Chapter 52

  • “a thieving robin redbreast such as accompanies lone travellers”: The robin redbreast of this line is the European robin, which follows gardeners and travellers for the insects they turn up; the American robin is a larger thrush named after it. A children’s poem and a legend explain that the robin’s breast was reddened by fanning the fire to keep the infant Jesus warm in the manger. In the old ballad The Children in the Wood (1595), robins cover the two lost children with leaves when they die in the forest. Many cultures have different meanings for robins, from death to the coming of spring to messengers of the spirits.
  • It may refer to The Frog Prince; or, Iron Henry, a German fairy tale collected by the Brothers Grimm and published in 1812 in Grimm’s Fairy Tales. In the forest, a selfish princess accidentally drops her golden ball into a well. A frog offers to retrieve it in exchange for her friendship. She agrees but goes back on her word after getting the ball back and runs to her castle. The next day, she is eating with her father when the frog knocks on the door and requests to be let in. The king tells his daughter that she must keep her promise and she reluctantly obeys. The frog sits next to her and eats from her plate, then desires to sleep in the princess’s bed. She is disgusted at the idea of sleeping with the frog, but her father angrily chastises her for loathing someone who helped her in a time of need. She picks up the frog and places him in the corner of her bedroom, but he hops up to her bed and demands to sleep as comfortably as the princess. Furious, she throws the frog against the wall, but as he falls to the floor he has transformed into a handsome prince. He explains that he was cursed by a wicked witch and the spell could only be broken with the princess’s help. The next day, the two go to the prince’s kingdom where they will be married. In modern versions, the transformation is triggered by the princess kissing the frog (a motif that apparently first appeared in English translations). In other early versions, it was sufficient for the frog to sleep for three nights on the princess’ pillow. The frog prince also has a loyal servant named Henry who had three iron bands affixed around his heart to prevent it from breaking from sadness when his master was cursed. When the frog prince reverts to his human form, Henry’s overwhelming happiness causes the bands to break, freeing his heart from its bonds. (Wikipedia)
  • Both are in folklore and literature. The Alp is a supernatural being, often likened to a demon, vampire, or incubus. It can be a spirit summoned by a witch or an evil person or can be a human or animal that transforms into an Alp during the night. Known for shapeshifting, sometimes appearing as a cat, pig, dog, snake, or even a small white butterfly. It wears a magical hat which grants powers like invisibility. The Alp may also possess an “evil eye” that causes illness and misfortune. Primarily known for attacking sleeping victims, causing nightmares and difficulty breathing, similar to sleep paralysis. They may also drink blood or milk from their victims. Can be warded off by placing a broomstick under a pillow, iron horseshoes on the bedpost, or shoes with toes pointing towards the door. Trapping them indoors by plugging holes and keeping a light on at night are also mentioned as protective measures. A White Witch is generally considered a benevolent or sympathetic magic practitioner, focusing on healing and/or helping others. In C.S. Lewis’s “The Chronicles of Narnia,” specifically “The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe,” Jadis, the White Witch, is the main antagonist, a powerful and malevolent sorceress who usurped the throne of Narnia. She is not human but rumored to be half-Jinn and half-Giantess. Described as tall with skin “white like snow or paper or icing-sugar,” with a very red mouth. She has a long golden wand and crown and rides a sleigh pulled by reindeer. She has powers like creating an endless winter, turning people to stone, and enchanting food. Jadis enforces a tyrannical rule over Narnia, casting a spell of eternal winter. She manipulates and tempts, famously using enchanted Turkish Delights to lure Edmund. She seeks to prevent the prophecy of her downfall and is ultimately defeated by Aslan. Often interpreted as representing evil, temptation, and anti-Christian leadership, in contrast to Aslan, who symbolizes goodness and Christ. She also embodies a false god, not as powerful as Aslan.
  • “a lost bride who had played a game of hide-and-seek with the bridegroom”: The old romance is “The Mistletoe Bough,” a legend claimed by several English country houses and best known from Thomas Haynes Bayly’s ballad of the 1830s: on her wedding day a bride hides in an old oak chest during a game of hide-and-seek, the lid locks shut, and her skeleton in its wedding dress is found years later. Samuel Rogers told the same tale of the Italian bride Ginevra in his poem Italy (1822).

Chapter 59

  • “the barkings of red-eyed dogs accompanying dead souls to Hades”: In northern English folklore the Gabriel Hounds, or Gabble Ratchets, were a spectral pack heard yelping overhead at night, and their cry foretold a death; the sound was probably wild geese in passage.
  • “the revolving castles of the dead, the glass castles, the spiral castles”: In the Welsh poem Preiddeu Annwfn (The Spoils of Annwn, Book of Taliesin, early fourteenth century), Arthur sails with three shiploads of men to the fortresses of the otherworld, among them a Glass Fortress, and only seven come back. Caer Sidi, the otherworld fortress of another Taliesin poem, has been translated “Revolving Castle.”

Chapter 68

  • “...a man experiences, at the instant his neck is broken and for an instant afterward, a convulsion, an agony of life. A ghostly progeny is always shot forth into the empty air. Ten thousand sons and daughters fall like a cobweb into everlasting nothingness, nothingness forever. Or else his seed takes root, and his children spring up around the scaffold—flower-faced children winged like birds and rooted in earth, the mandrakes screaming when they are pulled up by the roots, for they are the children of the hanged man, and they grow like the little weeds. That was why the hangman thought of hanging as a marriage ceremony with death.” This folklore is based on the mandrake plant which is a hallucinogenic and narcotic. It is said that it grows from the blood of a hanged man and the tuber can resemble a human form. It is considered a myth that men ejaculate when they are executed.

Chapter 71

  • The Three Bears is a 19th-century British fairy tale of which three versions exist. The original version of the tale tells of a not-so-polite old woman who enters the forest home of three bachelor bears while they are away. She sits in their chairs, eats some of their porridge, sits down on one of their chairs and breaks it, and sleeps in one of their beds. When the bears return and discover her, she wakes up, jumps out of the window, and is never seen again. The second version replaced the old woman with a little girl named Goldilocks, and the third and by far best-known version replaced the original bear trio with Papa Bear, Mama Bear, and Baby Bear. What was originally a frightening oral tale became a cozy family story with only a hint of menace.

Chapter 71

  • Little Tom Thumb is a character of English folklore. Tom Thumb may have been a real person born around 1519, as there is a grave purporting to be his. It is set into the floor adjacent to the font of the main chapel in Holy Trinity Church at Tattershall, Lincolnshire, UK. On the tombstone it reads that T. Thumb was 101 years old. He died in 1620 and the grave measures just sixteen inches in length.

Chapter 75

  • A red geranium may refer to Saint Robert’s geranium (wild geranium, red robin, and old maid’s nightcap). The folklore around this flower is that it could fulfill a couple’s wish for children (the legend of the stork delivering the babies). The herb was worn as an amulet or placed under the bed, to signal to the spirits a desire for a child.

Chapter 76

  • Little Tom Thumb may refer to Tom Thumb, a character in English folklore. The History of Tom Thumb was published in 1621 and was the first fairy tale printed in English. Tom Thumb may have been a real person born around 1519, as there is a grave purporting to be his. The grave measures just 16 inches in length.
