Chapter 3

  • “Like the princess, I would let down my hair, and he should climb it as if it were a rope.” - may refer to Rapunzel like folklore and fairytales. Many scholars have interpreted "Maiden in the Tower" stories, which Rapunzel is a part of, as a metaphor for the protection of young women from premarital relationships by overzealous guardians. Scholars have drawn comparisons of the confinement of Rapunzel in her tower to that of a convent, where women's lives were highly controlled and they lived in exclusion from outsiders. Scholars have also noted the strong theme of love conquering all in the story, as the lovers are united after years of searching in all versions after Persinette and are ultimately happily reunited as a family. The "Maiden in the Tower" archetype has drawn comparisons to a possible lost matriarchal myth connected to the sacred marriage between the prince and the maiden and the rivalry between the maiden, representing life and spring, and the crone, representing death and winter.

Chapter 6

  • “her little shoes which danced and danced without her”: In “The Red Shoes” (1845) by Hans Christian Andersen a vain girl is condemned to dance even after she dies. The red shoes represent Madge’s precarious balance between appearance and reality, innocence and corruption, freedom and constraint. They are a vivid symbol of her tragic, conflicted condition as she rides the bus toward an uncertain fate. In the tale the executioner cuts off Karen’s feet, and the red shoes dance away with them, much as Jacqueline dances “on one foot.”
  • “Tell them Papa’s gone a-hunting the rabbits”: Rabbits can symbolize good luck, prosperity, and abundance. The symbol of rabbits in the chapter encompasses innocence, vulnerability, lost youth, and the pervasive presence of death that shadows life in the novel. The nursery rhyme runs, “Bye, baby Bunting, / Daddy’s gone a-hunting, / Gone to get a rabbit skin / To wrap the baby Bunting in.”
  • “leading the dance in your little glass shoes”: Cinderella’s glass slipper comes from Charles Perrault’s tale (1697), and Madge travels in “glass-heeled shoes.”
  • “this sleeping beauty who never dreamed of her”: In Perrault’s and the Grimms’ tale a kiss wakes the sleeping princess. Here the bridegroom sleeps, and Madge says later, “Your kiss will not restore her beauty.”
  • “Ding, dong, bell”: Ariel’s song for a drowned father in The Tempest: “Full fathom five thy father lies . . . Ding-dong, bell.” Madge’s father drowned. The nursery rhyme “Ding, dong, bell, / Pussy’s in the well” also ends in drowning.
  • “Everyone pays the piper”: In the tale of the Pied Piper of Hamelin, the town that would not pay the piper lost its children.
  • “Jack Frost who paints the window pane white”: The nursery rhyme runs, “Jack be nimble, Jack be quick, / Jack jump over the candlestick.” Madge’s string of Jacks, Jack Robinson, Jack Frost, little Jack Fleet, Jack White, Jack-in-the-pulpit, and later Homer’s jack-in-the-box, all circle back to Jackie.
  • “Death is an old hunter. Wait until you hear his winding horn”: In the legend of the Wild Hunt a ghostly huntsman rides through the winter sky with baying hounds, and to hear him meant death.

Chapter 22

  • Islands of the Dead may refer to a concept from pre-Christian Europe of an island to the west where souls went after death. It is reported as being part of Celtic belief by several Roman historians, and evidence for this belief is also found in Welsh folklore. It also existed in ancient Germanic traditions where the British Isles were sometimes depicted as the isles to the west that the dead inhabited.

Chapter 26

  • Catherine is expecting a man to save her, which is the damsel in distress. This is a narrative device in which one or more men must rescue a woman who has been kidnapped or placed in other peril. The "damsel" is often portrayed as beautiful, popular, and of high social status; she is usually depicted as a princess in works with fantasy or fairy tale settings. Kinship, love, lust or a combination of those motivate the male protagonist to initiate the narrative. Critics have linked the helplessness of these women to societal views that women as a group need to be taken care of by men and treated nicely. Throughout the history of the trope, the role of the woman as the victim in need of a male savior has remained constant, but her attackers have changed to suit the tastes and collective fears of the period: "monsters, mad scientists, Nazis, hippies, bikers, aliens..."

Chapter 29

  • Moths have a rich history in folklore and mythology and are often associated with death, the afterlife, and even messengers from the spirit world as well as good fortune and cleansing. Black moths can symbolize mortality, divinity, and universal secrets. White moths can symbolize death or ill wishes. Brown moths symbolize the need for a purification and the slow, unseen destruction of things. Moths in Matthew 6:19-21 are something that destroys treasures on earth so treasures in heaven should be created instead. Moths serve as a complex metaphor for transformation, the interplay of light and shadow, life and death, and the elusive boundaries of identity and existence. They embody the novel’s themes of impermanence, mystery, and the search for meaning beyond the visible world.

Chapter 32

  • This characterization of Catherine resembles the folktale of Sleeping Beauty which is a classic fairy tale about a princess who is cursed to sleep for a hundred years by an evil fairy, to be awakened by a handsome prince at the end of that time. The good fairy, realizing that the princess would be frightened if alone when she awakens, uses her wand to put every living person and animal in the palace asleep, to awaken when the princess does. Early origins and versions of the tale involve rape, incest, cannibalism and Sleeping Beauty giving birth to children while she is asleep (similar to non-canical books that depict Eve being raped by angels and giving birth). Catherine embodies the Sleeping Beauty archetype as a woman caught in a protracted, enchanted sleep between life and death, isolated in a dreamlike world filled with phantoms, awaiting an uncertain awakening. Her physical stillness, the symbolic presence of the bird, the grand but empty house, and the thematic concerns with time, death, and unfulfilled love strongly evoke the folktale’s motifs and deepen the novel’s exploration of illusion versus reality. Through Catherine, Young reimagines the Sleeping Beauty myth, using it to probe themes of mortality, memory, and the elusive boundary between waking life and dreams.

Chapter 52

  • “that beauty which would never be awakened either by the kiss of a frog or by its croak.” - It metaphorically expresses a beauty or a love that cannot be revived or brought to life by conventional or magical means. This alludes to the fairy tale motif of a frog's kiss awakening a princess, but here it is negated, suggesting a beauty that is lost, unreachable, or impervious to such enchantments. The addition of "or by its croak" extends the metaphor, implying that even the frog's natural call, a symbol of life and vitality, cannot arouse or restore this beauty. The beauty in question is thus something that remains dormant or dead, beyond the power of transformation or resurrection. Within the novel's broader themes, this likely refers to a character that is tied up with sorrow, loss, and the inevitable passage of time and death. It underscores the novel’s ongoing exploration of the fragility of life, the elusiveness of love, and the impermanence of physical beauty.

Chapter 52

  • It may refer to The Frog Prince; or, Iron Henry, a German fairy tale collected by the Brothers Grimm and published in 1812 in Grimm's Fairy Tales. In the forest, a selfish princess accidentally drops her golden ball into a well. A frog offers to retrieve it in exchange for her friendship. She agrees but goes back on her word after getting the ball back and runs to her castle. The next day, she is eating with her father when the frog knocks on the door and requests to be let in. The king tells his daughter that she must keep her promise and she reluctantly obeys. The frog sits next to her and eats from her plate, then desires to sleep in the princess's bed. She is disgusted at the idea of sleeping with the frog, but her father angrily chastises her for loathing someone who helped her in a time of need. She picks up the frog and places him in the corner of her bedroom, but he hops up to her bed and demands to sleep as comfortably as the princess. Furious, she throws the frog against the wall, but as he falls to the floor he has transformed into a handsome prince. He explains that he was cursed by a wicked witch and the spell could only be broken with the princess's help. The next day, the two go to the prince's kingdom where they will be married. In modern versions, the transformation is triggered by the princess kissing the frog (a motif that apparently first appeared in English translations). In other early versions, it was sufficient for the frog to sleep for three nights on the princess' pillow. The frog prince also has a loyal servant named Henry who had three iron bands affixed around his heart to prevent it from breaking from sadness when his master was cursed. When the frog prince reverts to his human form, Henry's overwhelming happiness causes the bands to break, freeing his heart from its bonds.

Chapter 56

  • “bright red fishermen’s hats” The iconic bright red fisherman's hat, most often a knitted beanie, has roots in 19th-century diving, Scandinavian folklore, and resistance movements. Its fame was cemented by legendary oceanographer Jacques Cousteau, who wore it as a tribute to early maritime tradition. The modern red beanie tradition traces back to oyster divers in the town of Whitstable, UK, in the 1820s. Early "hard hat" divers wore heavy metal helmets that contained a pressurized air valve, which was operated by bumping one's head against it. A red wool cap was worn to prevent painful bruising from the valve. The red also made the caps visible in the dimly lit water. As diving technology advanced, divers continued to wear the red cap out of respect for the pioneers of their profession. Another thread of the red beanie's history connects to Norwegian folklore and wartime defiance. In Norwegian fairy tales, the working-class hero Askeladden (the "Ash Lad") is often depicted wearing a red knit cap with a pom-pom, symbolizing adventure and rebellion. During the Nazi occupation of Norway, the "nissebeanie," a traditional red hat, became a silent symbol of patriotic resistance and Norwegian heritage. The German occupation forces considered the red hat such a threat that they eventually outlawed it. The red cap became famous globally due to the French oceanographer Jacques Cousteau, who proudly wore it on his televised adventures aboard the Calypso. Cousteau wore the red cap as a conscious tribute to the early diving pioneers. His highly popular TV series, The Undersea World of Jacques Cousteau, solidified the red beanie as a worldwide symbol of exploration and adventure in the 1960s.

Chapter 71

  • The Three Bears is a 19th-century British fairy tale of which three versions exist. The original version of the tale tells of a not-so-polite old woman who enters the forest home of three bachelor bears while they are away. She sits in their chairs, eats some of their porridge, sits down on one of their chairs and breaks it, and sleeps in one of their beds. When the bears return and discover her, she wakes up, jumps out of the window, and is never seen again. The second version replaced the old woman with a little girl named Goldilocks, and the third and by far best-known version replaced the original bear trio with Papa Bear, Mama Bear, and Baby Bear. What was originally a frightening oral tale became a cozy family story with only a hint of menace.

Chapter 71

  • Little Tom Thumb is a character of English folklore. Tom Thumb may have been a real person born around 1519, as there is a grave purporting to be his. It is set into the floor adjacent to the font of the main chapel in Holy Trinity Church at Tattershall, Lincolnshire, UK. On the tombstone it reads that T. Thumb was 101 years old. He died in 1620 and the grave measures just sixteen inches in length.
