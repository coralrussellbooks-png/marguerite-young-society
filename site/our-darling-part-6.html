Marguerite Young, Our Darling, Part 6: What It Reveals About Young and Her Work

← Part 5 · Marguerite Young, Our Darling: What It Reveals, Part by Part · Part 7 →

Source: Marguerite Young, Our Darling: Tributes and Essays, edited by Miriam Fuchs (Dalkey Archive Press, 1994). Part 6 completes Susan Strehle's essay on women's time, then gives two of the book's most theoretical pieces: Miriam Fuchs's “Liquescence as Form” and Steven Shaviro's “Exorbitance and Death.” Together they make the collection's strongest case for why Miss MacIntosh, My Darling matters and why the academy has resisted it. Only material that illuminates Young and her work is kept below; quoted phrases from the book are marked with quotation marks.

Time without history

Strehle finishes by showing how thoroughly the novel dissolves historical time. Vera's quest is deliberately diffuse; she vanishes for hundreds of pages, and the long bus ride yields none of the adventures a traditional journey promises, because Young's world rests on the premise that “every heart is the other heart,” so hero and villain, self and other, cannot be cleanly opposed. The patriarchal counter-figure is the gospel salesman Bonebreaker, who speaks for linear striving and consulting his watch so that “every minute counted,” and who flees, struck speechless, when Miss MacIntosh reveals her baldness and her missing breast. Miss MacIntosh instead teaches Vera an ahistorical sense of time, in which death is not a culmination but an ongoing, echoing loss. Strehle sets Young apart from her male contemporaries in the postmodern epic, Pynchon, Barth, Coover, and DeLillo, who pack their books with the elections, wars, and news of the century. Young does the reverse: her coastal starting point and her Indiana ending go unnamed, and history is drained away so that the book can be told entirely from within what Kristeva calls women's time.

Liquescence as form

Fuchs builds her essay from the novel's sheer physical scale, which she takes from Bernard Bergonzi's review: eighteen years of work, some 3,449 pages in typescript, three and a half pounds in the hand. She turns that into her governing metaphor. Just as oil stirred into water divides into suspended particles before rising again, Vera narrates by mixing her recollected figures into small peculiar particles, Miss MacIntosh's bald head, Esther's swollen womb, Mr. Spitzer's double vision, and restirring them whenever the mixture thickens. In this liquid world objects and identities transform without stable boundaries: Catherine insists her lawyer Joachim is an umbrella stand, a pear becomes a glove becomes a basket of peaches, and borders are no more than “the thinnest line thinner than the thinnest thread.” There is almost no clearly delineated action; Vera's quest is openly illogical, “seeking for [Miss MacIntosh] in all those places where she was not,” pursuing a woman already dead and dissolved into ocean and objects. Fuchs reads this as Young's refusal to let the text be reduced to what can be understood and controlled, an embrace of what Alice Jardine calls the “categorically unrepresentable.” When plot does resurface, late and abruptly, through mad Dr. O'Leary, then Esther Longtree's hundred-and-sixty-five pages, then the stone-deaf man arriving eight pages from the end, it only skews the structure further: when Young uses plot, Fuchs argues, she does so to negate its legitimacy. A footnote records a striking fact: John Gardner was Young's pupil at Iowa and wrote his first two novels in her class, and she helped him find his narrative style; he called her novel extraordinary for breadth of mind and verbal genius.

Feminine writing and the dispersal of patriarchy

Both Fuchs and Strehle place the novel as a rare achieved example of the “feminine writing” that Hélène Cixous theorized: prose that wrecks partitions and refuses closure, that in Cixous's phrase “starts on all sides at once,” twenty or thirty times over, rather than driving from origin to conclusion. Fuchs argues that Esther Longtree's endless, possibly imaginary pregnancy is the perfect figure for a book that will not move toward climax or birth, and that the whole novel evokes a matriarchal world of mothers, would-be mothers, and daughters in which patriarchal law is dispersed and diluted, its fathers unnameable, its hangman mocked by children. Even the ending refuses to close: Vera reports in the conditional that Esther “would hang” her lunch sign, an act that has not yet happened and may not, suspending the book between action and inaction. Crucially, Fuchs stresses that for Young these feminine qualities, sympathy, nurture, patience, cross gender lines; they surface most movingly in Mr. Spitzer, the aging lawyer trying to comprehend the collapse of the old hierarchies and slowly yielding to the richness of female sentience.

Exorbitance, and why the academy resists it

Shaviro's essay is the boldest defense in the book. He describes entering the novel without guideposts, overwhelmed by its page-long sentences and infinite variations, so that the reader's disorientation mirrors Vera's own. He calls it a “delirious phantasmagoria of memories,” an epic lament of loss whose space of absence is nonetheless “infinitely full rather than infinitely empty.” His central claim is that the book induces a pre-interpretive delirium in the reader, a passion, and that this is exactly why it sits poorly in the academy. Academic criticism is built for modernist abstruseness, self-reflexivity, and formal intricacy, all of which Young supplies, but it cannot handle passion that threatens the critic's distance and mastery. The novel is, Shaviro insists, “as carefully and deliberately crafted as any great twentieth-century work,” yet it is anti-formalist and not metafictional; rather than folding back on itself it “expends itself to infinity.” This “ex-orbitance” marks a mutation in English-language modernism. Where Joyce, Pound, and Nabokov generate scholarly industries devoted to mastery, Young creates a different audience, a community of readers bound by a shared experience rather than a fraternity of scholars, and she feels none of the elitist male modernist compulsion to impose order on chaos, subverting instead the very dualisms, subject and object, male and female, individual and society, on which that modernism feeds. He notes, tellingly, that the book contains almost no dialogue, only multiple monologues, because dialogue would imply a reconciliation this radically pluralistic world does not allow.

A celebration of ordinary life

Shaviro closes on the novel's philosophy of the real, which he reads as its deepest achievement. Young grants every experience its own reality: a delusion or a hallucination is an actual event, and in Vera's words “even the unreal things were real,” including the ignored, the rejected, and the lost. The real is embedded in the imaginary rather than opposed to it, every actuality produced by a movement of desire, and the fact that all desires are disappointed does not cancel this but confirms it, since the unreality of failure is itself perfectly real. Death saturates the book to the point of obsession, yet even death is not conclusive; there is no final harmony and no return to silence, only an open, forever-varied passage. Shaviro's last point is the one worth keeping: for all its extravagance, Young's vision is not transcendent or otherworldly. What the novel offers is a vivid perception and a celebration of something available to everyone, at every moment, in ordinary life.
