Research notes: Chapter 36 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 36

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The chapter opens with the problem of identity as its central crisis. Mr. Spitzer is described as "the counterfeit of himself — this face which passed for his face and yet was not his identical face". He exists in permanent tension with his dead twin brother, so that the question of which self is present is never answerable. He is not one person who has lost another; he is a self that was always already divided, constituted by its double.

My mother compounds this. She cannot distinguish between Cousin Hannah as she was and Cousin Hannah as she imagined her: between the real woman and the dream woman. The text asks: "Was she become a man — bold as fire, puissant, invulnerable to any attack — or was she woman, a beautiful lady in that other sphere". The question is not rhetorical. It is ontological. Cousin Hannah's self is plural across gender, geography, life, and death simultaneously.

Most strikingly, the narrator's mother is herself described as potentially already dead: "my mother having already died, as she believed, she being herself this pale lady with the wild dishevelment of hair". The self here is not a stable subject moving through events. It is multiple, simultaneous, and irresolvable: precisely as the first pillar demands. The self is not broken. It was always this way. The text refuses to mourn the lost unity because no such unity ever existed.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The second pillar finds its most explicit statement in the accusation Mr. Spitzer levels at my mother: "it seemed to him that she had never distinguished between imagination and memory, the thinnest line sometimes dividing them, a line thinner than the thinnest thread of starlight or moth spittle hanging in a cloud".

But the text does not validate Mr. Spitzer's accusation as correct. He himself walks on "clouded waters or clouds", hears church bells a minute late, sees traffic signals after they have changed. His claim to be the responsible citizen who makes "precise judgments" is immediately undermined by his own perceptual displacement from reality. Both figures inhabit constructed realities. The difference is that my mother knows hers is constructed, or at least does not pretend otherwise, while Mr. Spitzer clings to the fiction of precision even as his broken watch and dead ear betray him.

The cave is not replaced by sunlight. There is only, as the second pillar states, "a larger cave more carefully lit." Mr. Spitzer's cave is lit by punctuality, by the wrist watch, by the cane, by funeral clothes: all props of a reality-construction he fears abandoning: "if he should forget to wear his wrist watch or carry his cane, then all the skies might fall upon his head". His conservatism is not wisdom. It is terror of the constructedness becoming visible.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

This pillar is perhaps the most thoroughly embodied in the chapter. Grief here is not an event or a stage. It is the atmosphere of the entire text. The house has "its air of perpetual mourning, its gardens of broken lilies in steaming fogs, its dim, unvisited rooms, shrouded furniture and half-strung harps and table set for the dead".

Note the phrase "table set for the dead." This is not grief that has arrived at a meal and then cleared the table. The table remains set. Grief is not a visitor who has come and gone. It is the permanent arrangement of the furniture.

Mr. Spitzer has worn "the sombre black which he recalled he had worn ever since his brother's death"; he cannot even remember a time before grief. It is not that he is still grieving. It is that grief has become the baseline condition of his existence, the climate in which all other weather occurs.

My mother weeps "over the deaths of persons she had never known, never loved until they were no longer the citizens of this world". This is grief that has become so structurally embedded that it no longer requires a personal object. She grieves preemptively, universally. Grief has become her mode of perception. The honest life, as the third pillar states, stops pretending otherwise, and this text never pretends otherwise for a single sentence.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrative voice of this chapter is itself an act of witnessing in the most radical sense. The narrator does not explain, conclude, or resolve. The narrator stays in the room (the room of my mother's consciousness, of Mr. Spitzer's displacement, of Cousin Hannah's posthumous wandering) and reports with exhausting, unblinking attention.

Consider the description of Cousin Hannah's dying body as witnessed by Mr. Spitzer: "the dark air scarcely streaked by beams of the fading sunlight, the emaciated face, the shrivelled mummy features, the grey forelock, the hands like bird talons clutching at nothing". He does not look away. He does not offer consolation. He "murmured diffident, distant remarks not calculated to disturb her peace", which is itself a form of witnessing that refuses to impose meaning on what it sees.

The narrator similarly refuses to simplify my mother's obsession with Cousin Hannah. It would be easy to diagnose her as delusional, to conclude that she is simply mad. The text refuses this conclusion. It witnesses the full complexity of her relationship to the dead (its jealousy ("her cheeks twitching with jealousy" ), its love, its political admiration, its grief) without reducing it to pathology. Attention is the practice. The refusal to simplify is the act.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Mr. Spitzer's existence is described as "this routine of daily continuing life" that is "utterly without validity, an imitation of life", and yet he continues it. He builds the routine knowing it is hollow. He returns each day to the same gestures (the wrist watch, the cane, the funeral clothes) knowing they are provisional constructions that could collapse at any moment.

My mother's project of evoking Cousin Hannah is equally recursive. She has been trying to contact her for years. She has not yet sent the letters she has written to herself. She changes her mind "just at the moment of crucial decision". She "was always very brilliant at building a road to a conclusion she would not reach". This is not failure of will. It is recursive construction in its purest form: the building continues, the building is never finished, and the returning to the beginning with what was learned is itself the structure of her consciousness.

The fifth pillar says: "The building is never finished. That is not failure. That is how it works." The text embodies this by never arriving. It circles Cousin Hannah, circles Mr. Spitzer's grief, circles my mother's isolation, and the circling is the meaning, not a detour around it.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The prose style itself is the argument here. These are sentences that do not end so much as exhaust themselves temporarily, sentences that add clause upon clause, qualification upon qualification, "perhaps" upon "perhaps." Consider: "a line thinner than the thinnest thread of starlight or moth spittle hanging in a cloud, weaving a moth pearl or swelling to a moon or a universe, perhaps an invisible line". The sentence keeps going because the thought cannot be completed without falsifying it.

The demand for completion is, as the sixth pillar states, "its own violence against experience." Mr. Spitzer's attempt to be precise, to make "precise judgments which should be important, larger than the immediate moment", is shown to be a kind of violence against the actual texture of reality, which is imprecise, water-logged, retarded in its perceptions. His precision is a lie he tells himself. My mother's incompletion is more honest.

Cousin Hannah's life is described as one of permanent incompletion: "It was impossible to know how she could have been engaged in so many activities without at some time contradicting herself". She is not criticized for this. She is celebrated. The contradictions are the evidence of a life fully lived in the ethics of incompletion.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Every figure in this chapter exists at the margins of legible categories. Mr. Spitzer is the surviving twin who considers himself dead; he occupies the margin between life and death, between self and other, between the man he is and "that man he might never be, neither himself nor his twin brother, perhaps that man who should have been born alone as he must die alone". He is the evidence of the constructed nature of identity.

Cousin Hannah is described as having been "in grey New England a warring old maid urging ladies to arise and rebel and who had been in Araby a great desert king"; she crosses gender, crosses geography, crosses the boundary between the living and the dead. She is simultaneously spinster and king, suffragette and desert warrior. She is not the exception to the categories. She is the proof that the categories are constructions.

My mother herself occupies the margin between the living and the dead, between the present and the past, between sanity and what Mr. Spitzer calls "the logic of madness". She has not left the house in so many years that she has "forgotten the time of her journey". She exists at the edge of social legibility, and it is precisely at that edge that the constructed nature of the normal world becomes most visible. The marginal person is the evidence.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This pillar is perhaps the most structurally important for understanding the chapter's relationship to time. The text does not move forward. It accumulates. The past does not recede; it presses upward through every sentence.

Mr. Spitzer noted "always that an old landmark had disappeared, perhaps miraculously in a city loving its dead". Boston itself is described as "a city not distinguishing between past and present or present and future, remembering every breath, every whisper, every dream as if it were indelible, remembering even the drunken whaler sleeping in a doorway, the dead fisherman sleeping in his scallop upon the tide". The city is sedimentary time made architectural. It cannot let the past recede because the past is its foundation.

My mother "did not always know when her last journey was — perhaps it would be in the future". Past and future are not sequential but sedimentary: layered, pressing against each other, unable to be separated. Cousin Hannah's death has not moved into the past. It continues to accumulate beneath the present, pressing "upon her now with an ever increasing insistence, demanding her undivided attention as if they were immediate and not already resolved". The dead are not behind us. They are beneath us, still pressing up.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The text is extraordinarily self-conscious about the constructedness of its own language. Consider the problem of addressing the letter to Cousin Hannah: "Should my mother address her letter to Madame or Monsieur — to what street, what house, what city". The question of address is not merely practical. It is ontological. Language cannot locate Cousin Hannah because language's categories (Madame or Monsieur, this street or that) are constructions that cannot contain the reality of a person who has crossed all categorical boundaries.

Mr. Spitzer's relationship to language is one of permanent discrepancy: "he lived at this second remove from reality", hearing music "only when the music had ceased", seeing traffic signals "always a little late". Language and perception arrive after the event they are meant to name. This is not merely Mr. Spitzer's personal disability. It is the condition of language itself; it always arrives after the fact, always names what has already moved on.

My mother's letters to herself are "illegible or written in wavy lines like the sea's surface in a calm or written in hieroglyphs she could not read". She cannot read her own language. This is not madness. This is the honest recognition that language does not transparently convey the self to itself. It shapes what can be seen. It names its own location, and her location is one of radical uncertainty, which is why her language is unreadable even to herself.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

This chapter refuses transcendence in the conventional sense; there is no heaven that resolves the grief, no afterlife that answers the questions about Cousin Hannah's whereabouts. Every attempt to locate the sacred in a beyond is immediately undermined. The Moslem paradise is described as a place where "there should be no woman", and therefore a place Cousin Hannah would immediately transform into something else, invading it as she invaded every other institution.

But the sacred is everywhere in the immanent texture of the prose. It lives in "the friction of pebbles troubled by roaring surf", in "moth spittle hanging in a cloud, weaving a moth pearl", in "a leaf falling on another star", in the "music of friction which was the music of creation, of creation's dissonance". The sacred is not above the ordinary world. It is the ordinary world perceived with sufficient attention and without the falsifying demand for resolution.

The grey hen "laying the naked egg of reality in the long, whispering grass of a dream", excluded from paradise, is a perfect image of immanent transcendence. The sacred egg is the naked egg of reality. It is laid in the grass of the dream. It is not golden. It is not in heaven. It is here, in the whispering grass, in the immanent world, pointing deeper into itself rather than outward to a beyond.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The final pillar finds its most complete expression in the chapter's treatment of time, inheritance, and obligation. The narrator is constituted by what was deposited before she arrived, by Mr. Spitzer's grief, by Cousin Hannah's adventures, by my mother's obsessions. She did not choose this inheritance. It is structural.

The chapter ends with an image of radical temporal crossing: "The infant should be an old man crying in the storm, plucking at his beard". The not-yet-born and the already-ancient are collapsed into a single figure. This is not confusion. This is the crossing point: the place where time crosses itself, where what was deposited before we arrived and what we are constituting for those who come after us are simultaneously present.

Cousin Hannah herself is described as the crossing point of multiple historical moments ("the leader of the light-footed, moon-breasted brigade", the suffrage captain, the desert king, the Crusader) she carries all of these simultaneously, and her death does not resolve them but adds new layers: "Death had not diminished her. She seemed to have added new dimensions and taken on new ways". The ancestors are in the sediment. The carrying continues, not as choice but as structure.
