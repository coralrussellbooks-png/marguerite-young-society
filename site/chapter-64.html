Summary

Solomon the turtle is missing. Bald and unguarded, Miss MacIntosh tells Vera her life story: childhood baldness, wigs made of stolen horsehair, boys trying to hook her wig with fishing lines, a phrenologist who admired her skull, a Gaelic prince among her ancestors. As a matron of orphans she cared for a forger, who later wrote to her from Alcatraz, and for Lorena, a dancing girl she loved. Vera sees her as she cannot see herself and begins to compose an elegy. Their last month together begins.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with Vera noticing Solomon’s absence and feeling a newfound appreciation for his presence, symbolizing the theme of unnoticed value and loss.
  • Miss MacIntosh reveals she loved only once, a beautiful dancing girl named Lorena, whose life was marked by escape and tragedy. This passage adds layers to Miss MacIntosh’s character, showing her vulnerability and past connections to those society might reject.
  • A significant portion of the chapter is devoted to Miss MacIntosh’s lifelong baldness. She explains that she was born bald, wore a toupee as a child, and has never had hair, making her different and marginalized. Her baldness symbolizes loneliness, alienation, and the human condition of exposure and vulnerability.
  • The story of the barber who could do nothing for her bald head illustrates the hopelessness of her condition and her acceptance of it as an intrinsic part of her identity. The barber’s eloquence about his inability to grow even a single hair underscores the finality of her condition.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s resourcefulness and desperation are shown through her making a wig from stolen horses’ hairs, symbolizing her attempt to mask her difference and blend in, yet also emphasizing her isolation and the futility of this attempt.
  • The chapter describes how boys tried to hook her wig with fishing lines and how she was afraid of many things, including animals and people, which highlights her deep-seated fear and social alienation from a young age.
  • Miss MacIntosh claims descent from a Gaelic prince who was also bald and lost his crown, presenting a tragic nobility that contrasts with her humble, marginalized existence. She rejects the idle rich and social pretensions, affirming her identity rooted in practicality and resilience.
  • Throughout the chapter, there are poetic meditations on mortality, the ephemeral nature of beauty and life, and the essential loneliness of the human condition. Miss MacIntosh’s baldness becomes a metaphor for universal mourning and the stripping away of illusions.
  • Near the end, she is portrayed as a firm, solid woman who, despite her internal dissolution, stands for stability and common sense in a shifting world, symbolized by her black umbrella and practical nature.
  • Miss MacIntosh challenges established religious narratives (e.g., Emperor Constantine’s changes to the calendar) and extols the humble macintosh (raincoat) as a symbol of practical, unpretentious survival, contrasting with grand but empty imperial and religious traditions.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Miss MacIntosh

  • The central figure of the chapter, an orphanage matron who has suffered lifelong baldness.
  • She is portrayed as a strong yet vulnerable woman, bearing the weight of loneliness and social alienation.
  • Her baldness is both a physical condition and a metaphor for her isolation and difference from others.
  • Miss MacIntosh evolves from a figure shrouded in mystery to a deeply human character marked by resilience and vulnerability.
  • Her storytelling reveals layers of her personality, from pride and courage to sadness and regret.

Vera Cartwheel

  • A person who cares deeply for Miss MacIntosh, showing compassion and love.
  • Through their eyes, we see the complexity of Miss MacIntosh’s character and her struggles.
  • Vera’s growing understanding and affection for Miss MacIntosh deepen the reader’s empathy.

Solomon (pet turtle)

  • Mentioned briefly as a lethargic creature absent at the beginning, symbolizing a kind of comfort or presence that is missing.

Lorena

  • Lorena is depicted as a beautiful dancing girl, dark and fat, with long hair described like a "midnight curtain concealing the star until the star was revealed." She first encountered Miss MacIntosh when the latter was working in an orphanage.
  • Lorena laughed at Miss MacIntosh and then ran away, eventually becoming a strip-tease dancer. Years later, Lorena was paroled to Miss MacIntosh’s care, but she ran away again, and Miss MacIntosh could not pursue her due to her role as parole officer.
  • Lorena symbolizes a more flamboyant, elusive, and perhaps tragic aspect of femininity and life experience, contrasting sharply with the sober, practical, and grounded Miss MacIntosh. While Miss MacIntosh is associated with order, responsibility, and a no-nonsense approach to life, Lorena embodies escape, performance, and the allure of the spotlight.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s relationship with Lorena is tinged with affection and regret with they were “very dear friends” for a time, but Lorena’s life took a path of repeated flight and apparent self-destruction, as she “slept with many men” and aged into baldness, a fate that mirrors Miss MacIntosh’s own physical barrenness and loneliness.
  • Miss MacIntosh reflects on Lorena’s invitation to join her in the strip-tease, suggesting Lorena believed Miss MacIntosh would have made a sensational artist in that world. This highlights the tension between Lorena’s world of public display and Miss MacIntosh’s private, concealed existence (especially with her baldness and wig).
  • Lorena’s life is marked by a search for light and visibility, “danc[ing] in the spotlight,” while Miss MacIntosh sought the shadow, the hidden, and the protective fog.
  • Lorena enriches the narrative by providing a foil to Miss MacIntosh’s character by showing a different trajectory of womanhood, freedom, and loss. Lorena’s story also deepens the themes of love, disappointment, and identity, as Miss MacIntosh mourns not only her own solitude but also the loss of a connection that might have been different if circumstances had allowed.
  • The mention of Lorena evokes a poignant sense of fragmented relationships and missed possibilities in the world the novel inhabits.

A forger

  • The forger is a minor but symbolically significant figure mentioned in the context of Miss MacIntosh’s past connections with orphans under her care. The forger is one of only two orphans with whom Miss MacIntosh maintained contact after they left the orphanage, the other being Lorena, the dancing girl.
  • He was incarcerated at Alcatraz, a notorious prison, and despite his criminal status, he maintained a connection with Miss MacIntosh by sending her beautifully decorated Christmas cards every year. These cards showcased his skill in penmanship, as he was able to imitate signatures ranging from Abraham Lincoln to William Jennings Bryan with remarkable artistry. His ability to produce such forgeries suggests a complex character capable of both criminality and refined craftsmanship.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s relationship with the forger reflects her enduring, if fraught, loyalty and care for those she once supervised. It also illustrates the theme of lost or broken lives touched by her, emphasizing her role as a maternal figure to children who ultimately faced harsh fates. The forger’s death marks the end of one such connection, underscoring the transient and often tragic nature of Miss MacIntosh’s attachments.
  • Symbolically, the forger embodies the blurred lines between authenticity and imitation, truth and falsehood, mirroring Miss MacIntosh’s own struggles with identity, appearance (notably her baldness and wigs), and the facades people maintain. His skill in forgery contrasts with her straightforward, no-nonsense character, highlighting the tension between illusions and reality pervasive in the novel.

  • Orphan asylums in the U.S. grew rapidly from the 1830s to the early 1900s, driven by immigration, poverty, and epidemics, providing refuge for thousands, often run by religious groups and separating children from harsh almshouses. While intended to protect children, they often housed many whose parents were alive but impoverished, and faced criticism for overcrowding and neglect, leading to reforms emphasizing family-based care and smaller, suburban institutions by the mid-20th century, shifting away from large, congregate settings. Women played a foundational role in US orphan asylums, from elite founders like Isabella Graham establishing institutions in the early 19th century to Catholic nuns and working-class women like Margaret Haughery providing daily care, all while navigating societal expectations, with some women encouraged by Jewish orphanages even becoming college-educated teachers or nurses, shaping the moral and practical care of orphaned children within these developing welfare systems. Racial prejudice persisted thought, with Black trustees only being hired much later.
  • The history of U.S. schools for the blind began in 1829 with the Perkins School for the Blind, followed by New York (1831) and Philadelphia (1832), establishing early residential models inspired by Europe, with pioneers like Samuel Gridley Howe developing embossed reading systems before Braille became standard. State support grew with Ohio (1837), leading to widespread institutions, while innovations like the first Braille press (Missouri, 1865) and advocacy by figures like Helen Keller drove expansion, culminating in federal support (APH, 1879) and eventual integration efforts (mainstreaming in public schools by the 1970s).

This phrase conveys the preacher’s meditation on the fleeting nature of worldly things and human endeavors under the sun.   • "the barber pole which was the sign of the blood-letter with his bucket of leeches" refers to the historical origin of the barber pole symbol. Traditionally, the barber pole (a red and white striped pole) symbolized the barber's role not only in cutting hair but also in bloodletting, a common medical treatment in earlier centuries. Bloodletters were practitioners who used leeches or other means to draw blood from patients to treat various ailments. The description sets a vivid scene in the barber shop Miss MacIntosh visited, linking the place to these archaic and somewhat grim practices, which underscores the hopelessness Miss MacIntosh felt when she sought help for her baldness there and was told nothing could be done.

  • "Old Baldy" is a deeply symbolic figure, represented most directly by Miss MacIntosh herself, who is bald from childhood and wears a wig made of stolen horsehair. Her baldness is not just a physical condition but a metaphor for loneliness, loss, endurance, and resilience through hardship. She is portrayed as a practical, no-nonsense woman, grounded in reality, and her baldness is a lifelong affliction that shapes her identity and experience. Miss MacIntosh relates that she is descended from a Gaelic prince who was lost at sea and was bald like she is. This ancestral figure, "old Baldy," is described as having lost his golden crown, his hair, and ultimately being cast up on the bleak shores of Scotland. The prince who lost his crown symbolizes a fallen royalty, a lineage that has endured loss and exile. This connection to a lost Gaelic prince imbues Miss MacIntosh’s baldness with historical and mythical resonance, linking her personal suffering to a broader narrative of displacement and faded nobility. Historically, "Old Baldy" could be interpreted as an archetype of the dispossessed or fallen ruler, a figure whose physical baldness symbolizes the stripping away of power, status, and protection like the "golden crown floating on the waters" representing lost authority and vanished grandeur. This image reflects themes of mourning, survival, and the harsh realities beneath romantic or royal illusions. Miss MacIntosh’s identification with this "Old Baldy" connects her personal baldness and hardship to a lineage of strength and resilience, despite the apparent desolation and loneliness that baldness symbolizes in the novel.
