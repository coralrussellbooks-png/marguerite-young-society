To All My Darlings

Chapter 64

On Chapter 64 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 12, 2026

← Chapter 63 · Home · Chapter 65 →

Summary

Solomon the turtle is gone; Miss MacIntosh has put him out of doors, and for the first time she calls Vera “darling.” She tells Vera she loved only once, a dark dancing girl named Lorena who laughed at her in the orphanage, became a fan dancer and stripper, was paroled to her care, and ran away again. For days afterward she keeps taking her wig off and putting it back. She tells her history: born bald in What Cheer, Iowa, the daughter of a poor shoemaker and coffin-maker; a doll’s toupee at three and a wig of hair stolen from the plow horses; boys fishing for her wig from the orchard boughs; a traveling phrenologist who gave her bald head the prize; a baptism in an icy river where the whole congregation saw her bare head; years in orphanages and a school for the blind; a forger at Alcatraz who sent her Christmas cards; a Chicago barber who could not grow one hair for a million dollars. Vera, who already knows that Miss MacIntosh will be committed to the deep, loves her more now than before. Their last month together has begun.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with Vera noticing Solomon’s absence and feeling a newfound appreciation for his presence, symbolizing the theme of unnoticed value and loss.
  • Miss MacIntosh reveals she loved only once, a beautiful dancing girl named Lorena, whose life was marked by escape and tragedy. This passage adds layers to Miss MacIntosh’s character, showing her vulnerability and past connections to those society might reject.
  • A significant portion of the chapter is devoted to Miss MacIntosh’s lifelong baldness. She explains that she was born bald, wore a toupee as a child, and has never had hair, making her different and marginalized. Her baldness symbolizes loneliness, alienation, and the human condition of exposure and vulnerability.
  • The story of the barber who could do nothing for her bald head illustrates the hopelessness of her condition and her acceptance of it as an intrinsic part of her identity. The barber’s eloquence about his inability to grow even a single hair underscores the finality of her condition.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s resourcefulness and desperation are shown through her making a wig from stolen horses’ hairs, symbolizing her attempt to mask her difference and blend in, yet also emphasizing her isolation and the futility of this attempt.
  • The chapter describes how boys tried to hook her wig with fishing lines and how she was afraid of many things, including animals and people, which highlights her deep-seated fear and social alienation from a young age.
  • Miss MacIntosh claims descent from a Gaelic prince who was also bald and lost his crown, presenting a tragic nobility that contrasts with her humble, marginalized existence. She rejects the idle rich and social pretensions, affirming her identity rooted in practicality and resilience.
  • Throughout the chapter, there are poetic meditations on mortality, the ephemeral nature of beauty and life, and the essential loneliness of the human condition. Miss MacIntosh’s baldness becomes a metaphor for universal mourning and the stripping away of illusions.
  • Near the end, she is portrayed as a firm, solid woman who, despite her internal dissolution, stands for stability and common sense in a shifting world, symbolized by her black umbrella and practical nature.
  • Miss MacIntosh challenges established religious narratives (e.g., Emperor Constantine’s changes to the calendar) and extols the humble macintosh, both the apple and the raincoat, as a symbol of practical, unpretentious survival, contrasting with grand but empty imperial and religious traditions.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • Miss MacIntosh calls her “Darling,” and Vera writes, “Darling! I sank on my knees, faintly.” Why does one word undo her?
  • “I have always been bald, ever since I was a child.” How does this change what Vera thought she knew?
  • The phrenologist finds “the most beautiful brain box he had ever seen in the world of the living.” Why does Young give the bald child the prize?

Characters

  • Vera asks, “Was it a woman or a man?” What does it mean that Miss MacIntosh’s one love was Lorena?
  • “She sought the light as I have sought the shadow.” How are Lorena and Miss MacIntosh opposites, and how are they alike?
  • The forger sent his cards “to exhibit the beauty of his penmanship.” Why did Miss MacIntosh keep faith with him?

Connections

  • Miss MacIntosh says, “I walked into the traffic flow.” How does this prepare for her walk into the sea?
  • Miss MacIntosh would ask Constantine “why he had changed reality.” Why does the emperor keep returning from Chapter 63?
  • “Before her body she committed to the deep.” How does Vera’s foreknowledge change the reading of this last month?

Reflection

  • Perhaps “a hair was important only because she had none.” Is that true of most things people long for?
  • “No one wants to be different.” Is it true?
  • “Perhaps she illustrated the difficulty of living with the illusion which may be one’s self.” What illusions does every self live with?

Research: Characters

Miss MacIntosh

  • The central figure of the chapter, an orphanage matron who has suffered lifelong baldness.
  • She is portrayed as a strong yet vulnerable woman, bearing the weight of loneliness and social alienation.
  • Her baldness is both a physical condition and a metaphor for her isolation and difference from others.
  • Miss MacIntosh evolves from a figure shrouded in mystery to a deeply human character marked by resilience and vulnerability.
  • Her storytelling reveals layers of her personality, from pride and courage to sadness and regret.

Vera Cartwheel

  • A person who cares deeply for Miss MacIntosh, showing compassion and love.
  • Through her eyes the reader sees the complexity of Miss MacIntosh’s character and her struggles.
  • Vera’s growing understanding and affection for Miss MacIntosh deepen the reader’s empathy.

Solomon (pet turtle)

  • Mentioned briefly as a lethargic creature absent at the beginning, symbolizing a kind of comfort or presence that is missing.

Lorena

  • Lorena is depicted as a beautiful dancing girl, dark and fat, with long hair described like a “midnight curtain concealing the star until the star was revealed.” She first encountered Miss MacIntosh when the latter was working in an orphanage.
  • Lorena laughed at Miss MacIntosh and then ran away, eventually becoming a strip-tease dancer. Years later, Lorena was paroled to Miss MacIntosh’s care, but she ran away again, and Miss MacIntosh could not pursue her due to her role as parole officer.
  • Lorena symbolizes a more flamboyant, elusive, and perhaps tragic aspect of femininity and life experience, contrasting sharply with the sober, practical, and grounded Miss MacIntosh. While Miss MacIntosh is associated with order, responsibility, and a no-nonsense approach to life, Lorena embodies escape, performance, and the allure of the spotlight.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s relationship with Lorena is tinged with affection and regret: they were “very dear friends” for a time, but Lorena’s life took a path of repeated flight and apparent self-destruction, as, Miss MacIntosh imagines, she “slept with many men” and aged into baldness, a fate that mirrors Miss MacIntosh’s own physical barrenness and loneliness.
  • Miss MacIntosh reflects on Lorena’s invitation to join her in the strip-tease, suggesting Lorena believed Miss MacIntosh would have made a sensational artist in that world. This highlights the tension between Lorena’s world of public display and Miss MacIntosh’s private, concealed existence (especially with her baldness and wig).
  • Lorena’s life is marked by a search for light and visibility, “danc[ing] in the spotlight,” while Miss MacIntosh sought the shadow, the hidden, and the protective fog.
  • Lorena enriches the narrative by providing a foil to Miss MacIntosh’s character by showing a different trajectory of womanhood, freedom, and loss. Lorena’s story also deepens the themes of love, disappointment, and identity, as Miss MacIntosh mourns not only her own solitude but also the loss of a connection that might have been different if circumstances had allowed.
  • The mention of Lorena evokes a poignant sense of fragmented relationships and missed possibilities in the world the novel inhabits.

A forger

  • The forger is a minor but symbolically significant figure mentioned in the context of Miss MacIntosh’s past connections with orphans under her care. The forger is one of only two orphans with whom Miss MacIntosh maintained contact after they left the orphanage, the other being Lorena, the dancing girl.
  • He was incarcerated at Alcatraz, a notorious prison, and despite his criminal status, he maintained a connection with Miss MacIntosh by sending her beautifully decorated Christmas cards every year. These cards showcased his skill in penmanship, as he was able to imitate signatures ranging from Abraham Lincoln to William Jennings Bryan with remarkable artistry. His ability to produce such forgeries suggests a complex character capable of both criminality and refined craftsmanship.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s relationship with the forger reflects her enduring, if fraught, loyalty and care for those she once supervised.
  • Symbolically, the forger embodies the blurred lines between authenticity and imitation, truth and falsehood, mirroring Miss MacIntosh’s own struggles with identity, appearance (notably her baldness and wigs), and the facades people maintain. His skill in forgery contrasts with her straightforward, no-nonsense character, highlighting the tension between illusions and reality pervasive in the novel.

Research: Themes

Loneliness and Isolation

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Miss MacIntosh’s baldness sets her apart physically and socially, causing her to feel like an outsider.
  • Her wandering through life, changing jobs, and lack of lasting relationships underline a profound sense of isolation.

Identity and Difference

See: Identity and the Self

  • Baldness symbolizes not only her physical appearance but also her difference and the struggle to be accepted.
  • Her attempts to wear wigs and create a semblance of normalcy reflect the universal human desire to fit in.

Resilience and Endurance

See: Fear and Courage

  • Despite her hardships, Miss MacIntosh exhibits strength and dignity.
  • She fights against despair and maintains a firm stance in the face of adversity, symbolized by her unwavering grip on her black umbrella.

Mortality and the Human Condition

See: Death and Mortality

  • The narrative meditates on the inevitability of death and the transient nature of beauty and life.
  • Baldness becomes a metaphor for universal human vulnerability and the “essential loneliness” each person must face.

Love and Loss

See: Love · Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • Miss MacIntosh’s reflections on Lorena and her own life reveal deep emotional wounds.
  • The absence of romantic fulfillment and the loss of connections highlight themes of regret and yearning.

Reality versus Illusion

See: Illusion and Reality

  • The chapter contrasts the harsh realities of Miss MacIntosh’s life with her dreams and the illusions she once held.
  • Vera’s love and understanding challenge the idea of superficial appearances.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Baldness

See: Hair, Wig, and Baldness

  • Represents vulnerability, difference, and mourning. It is a physical manifestation of Miss MacIntosh’s inner loneliness and social alienation.

Wig

See: Hair, Wig, and Baldness

  • The wig symbolizes the mask people wear to hide their true selves and conform to societal expectations. Miss MacIntosh’s frequent removal and replacement of the wig depict her struggle with identity. Young described the wig as the illusion itself, “the dream by which she lived” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).

Black Umbrella

See: The Umbrella

  • Shields her bald head and skin from the light and casts a shadow that keeps others away. Young said it “always casts a shadow around her and forbids intimacy” (Marguerite Young, interview, WNYC radio, New York).

Fog and Shadows

See: Fog and Mist · Shadows

  • Reflect confusion, concealment, the unknown, and the liminal space between reality and dreams.

Sea and Water Imagery

See: The Sea and Water

  • Symbolize life’s constant movement, change, and the inevitability of death.

Research: Resources

  • “Solomon is fifty years old”: Box turtles commonly live more than fifty years, and some pass a hundred. See Solomon the Turtle.
  • “Darling, you will never miss him”: This is the first time Miss MacIntosh calls Vera “darling,” and Vera sinks to her knees. The word gives the novel its title.
  • “Was it a woman or a man?”: Vera asks without assuming, and Miss MacIntosh answers that there was no man, only “a beautiful dancing girl.” Her one love was a woman. See Lorena, Love, and Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “she ran away and became a strip-tease dancer”: Burlesque striptease flourished in the 1920s and 1930s, until New York refused to renew the burlesque theaters’ licenses in 1937 under Mayor La Guardia. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “I walked into the traffic flow”: Miss MacIntosh “...wandered. I wandered on and on. I broke my nose. I lost my breast. I had no place. I walked into the traffic flow.” Walking into the traffic flow may have been an accident but it also seems to be her first attempt at suicide. See Suicide in History and Literature.
  • “She was a fan dancer”: See Sally Rand’s fan dance in the Chapter 61 resources.
  • “that flowers once grew at the North Pole”: Fossil forests and the pollen of palms show that the Arctic was warm and green about fifty million years ago, and frozen mammoths, the elephants of the Ice Age, have been dug out of the Siberian permafrost. See Exploration and the Poles.
  • “it’s cold enough in What Cheer, Iowa”: What Cheer is a real coal-mining town in Keokuk County, Iowa. Its name is said to come from Roger Williams’s greeting from the Narragansett, “What cheer, netop?” (1636). Later Vera and Miss MacIntosh cry “what cheer!” in the face of all adversity.
  • “a poor little shoe-maker”: The proverb says the shoemaker’s children go barefoot; Miss MacIntosh adds that the coffin-maker’s child had no coffin.
  • “two years at the University of Iowa”: The University of Iowa admitted women from its first classes in 1855, among the first public universities in the country to do so. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “no more than was in the old Farmers’ Almanac”: The Farmers’ Almanac has been published since 1818, with its weather forecasts, planting tables, and household advice. See Poor Richard’s Almanack in the Chapter 63 resources.
  • “had rolled like the rolling stone which gathers no moss”: “A rolling stone gathers no moss” goes back to the Latin of Publilius Syrus in the first century BC.
  • “had taught bowling in a bowling alley, knocking down ninepins”: Legend says that when states banned ninepins for gambling in the 1830s and 1840s, players added a tenth pin and invented tenpins.
  • “had been a matron in various orphan asylums”: Orphan asylums in the U.S. grew rapidly from the 1830s to the early 1900s, driven by immigration, poverty, and epidemics, providing refuge for thousands, often run by religious groups and separating children from harsh almshouses. While intended to protect children, they often housed many whose parents were alive but impoverished, and faced criticism for overcrowding and neglect, leading to reforms emphasizing family-based care and smaller, suburban institutions by the mid-20th century, shifting away from large, congregate settings. Women played a foundational role in US orphan asylums, from elite founders like Isabella Graham establishing institutions in the early 19th century to Catholic nuns and working-class women like Margaret Haughery providing daily care, all while navigating societal expectations, with some women encouraged by Jewish orphanages even becoming college-educated teachers or nurses, shaping the moral and practical care of orphaned children within these developing welfare systems. Racial prejudice persisted, though, with Black trustees only being hired much later.
  • “had worked in a school for the blind”: The history of U.S. schools for the blind began in 1829 with the Perkins School for the Blind, followed by New York (1831) and Philadelphia (1832), establishing early residential models inspired by Europe, with pioneers like Samuel Gridley Howe developing embossed reading systems before Braille became standard. State support grew with Ohio (1837), leading to widespread institutions, while innovations like the first Braille press (Missouri, 1865) and advocacy by figures like Helen Keller drove expansion, culminating in federal support (APH, 1879) and eventual integration efforts (mainstreaming in public schools by the 1970s).
  • “a pale narcissus break through rock”: Vera imagines a “pale narcissus” breaking through rock, life and beauty in the barrenness of Miss MacIntosh’s baldness. See On the pale narcissus. See also Narcissus and Echo in the Chapter 58 resources. See Greek Mythology.
  • “May I never lose my umbrella, for my umbrella is my friend”: The umbrella and old plaid mackintosh shelter Miss MacIntosh from reality rather than from fantasy. Young called the umbrella “a kind of canopy between her and reality” and compared it to the umbrella of Robinson Crusoe, “a Calvinistic version of the Fall of Man” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See The Umbrella.
    • Crusoe makes himself an umbrella of skins on his island (Daniel Defoe, Robinson Crusoe, 1719).
  • “Vanity, vanity, all is vanity, sayeth the poet”: Ecclesiastes 1:2-11 “Vanity of vanities, saith the Preacher, vanity of vanities; all is vanity.” This phrase conveys the preacher’s meditation on the fleeting nature of worldly things and human endeavors under the sun. It expresses the idea that much of what people strive for (wealth, wisdom, pleasure) is ultimately empty or futile because it does not provide lasting meaning or satisfaction. It is used by Miss MacIntosh to reflect on the futility and emptiness she perceives in life, especially in relation to her own condition and experiences. The phrase captures a sense of existential weariness and the recognition that many pursuits and concerns, such as appearances, social acceptance, or even personal desires, are ultimately transient and meaningless. Miss MacIntosh calls the Preacher a poet. See On Ecclesiastes. See the Preacher in the Chapter 63 resources. See Ecclesiastes.
  • “a forger at Alcatraz”: Alcatraz, in San Francisco Bay, was a federal prison from 1934 to 1963. See On the forger at Alcatraz.
    • Abraham Lincoln was the 16th president of the United States, serving from 1861 until his assassination in 1865. He led the United States through the American Civil War, defeating the Confederate States and playing a major role in the abolition of slavery. See The Civil War.
    • William Jennings Bryan was an American lawyer, orator, and politician. He was a dominant force in the Democratic Party, running three times as the party’s nominee for President of the United States in the 1896, 1900, and 1908 elections. His “Cross of Gold” speech won him the nomination in 1896.
  • “as seas might give up their dead”: See Revelation 20:13 in the Chapter 61 resources.
  • “A barber in Chicago told me he could do nothing”: Young asked her beauty operator whether a bald woman had ever come in. One had, he said: “This lady came in, lifted her wig, and there was this head as bald as a billiard ball. And she said, what can you do for me? And he said, I couldn’t grow one hair on your head for a million dollars. And if I could, I wouldn’t need a million dollars.” Young said, “I have Miss McIntosh go to a Chicago barber and have this exact scene in the book” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See Miss MacIntosh and Hair, Wig, and Baldness.
  • “the barber pole which was the sign of the blood-letter”: The phrase refers to the historical origin of the barber pole symbol. Traditionally, the barber pole (a red and white striped pole) symbolized the barber’s role not only in cutting hair but also in bloodletting, a common medical treatment in earlier centuries. Bloodletters were practitioners who used leeches or other means to draw blood from patients to treat various ailments. The description sets a vivid scene in the barber shop Miss MacIntosh visited, linking the place to these archaic and somewhat grim practices, which underscores the hopelessness Miss MacIntosh felt when she sought help for her baldness there and was told nothing could be done. The red stripe stood for blood and the white for bandages; barbers and surgeons were one guild in England until 1745. See On the barber pole.
  • “Bald I came into this world, and bald I will go out of it”: “Naked came I out of my mother’s womb, and naked shall I return thither” (Job 1:21, KJV). See The Book of Job and Hair, Wig, and Baldness.
  • “the gold of Croesus”: Croesus was the king of Lydia and known for his wealth. When Croesus asked Solon who was the happiest of men, Solon told him to call no man happy until he was dead (Herodotus, Histories, Book 1).
  • “a barber of Seville”: The Barber of Seville is considered one of the greatest masterpieces of comedy in music known as an opera buffa. Rossini’s opera (1816) is based on Beaumarchais’s play (1775). See Music and Composers.
  • “the firmament laid upon the firmament”: “Let there be a firmament in the midst of the waters, and let it divide the waters from the waters” (Genesis 1:6, KJV). See Genesis.
  • “Before her body she committed to the deep”: “We therefore commit his body to the deep” (Book of Common Prayer, burial at sea). Vera, writing afterward, already knows how Miss MacIntosh will die. See Weddings and Funerals and The Sea and Water.
  • “this was the universal baldness and sign of mourning”: In the Bible, shaving the head is a sign of mourning: “Make thee bald, and poll thee for thy delicate children; enlarge thy baldness as the eagle” (Micah 1:16, KJV). See The Bible, Hair, Wig, and Baldness, and Grief, Loss, and Mourning.
  • “Whose were these bones?”: “Son of man, can these bones live?” (Ezekiel 37:3, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “The flesh as grass fading before my eyes”: “All flesh is grass, and all the goodliness thereof is as the flower of the field” (Isaiah 40:6, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “a pair of old black gloves”: Young had Miss MacIntosh walk into the sea in black gloves “which she had carefully darned the night before.” Unsure what they meant, she asked a psychiatrist friend, who explained “all the meanings of the word black and gloves in relation to psychology and guilt” (Marguerite Young, interview with Charles Ruas, WBAI radio, New York). Two or three years after she wrote the scene, a man killed himself off Long Island: “He wore only a pair of black gloves,” which made her feel “I had struck something in reality there, too” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See Miss MacIntosh and Suicide in History and Literature.
  • “the outgoing of the morning and the outgoing of the evening”: “Thou makest the outgoings of the morning and evening to rejoice” (Psalm 65:8, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “even upon the wings of the wind”: “Who walketh upon the wings of the wind” (Psalm 104:3, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “not those of commission, only those of omission”: Compare the General Confession of the Book of Common Prayer: “We have left undone those things which we ought to have done; And we have done those things which we ought not to have done.”
  • “Whom God hath created, let God not put away”: Compare the marriage service: “What therefore God hath joined together, let not man put asunder” (Matthew 19:6, KJV). See The Bible and Weddings and Funerals.
  • “she was this old Baldy even then”: “Old Baldy” is Miss MacIntosh herself, bald from childhood and wearing a wig. She traces her line to a bald Gaelic prince lost at sea, his “golden crown floating on the waters,” cast up on the shores of Scotland, so her baldness carries a history of fallen royalty, exile, and endurance.
  • “the journeyman phrenologist who was a great designer of octagon houses”: A phrenologist is someone who uses phrenology to study the head. It is a pseudoscience that uses the measurements of the skull to predict mental traits. See On phrenology.
    • The phrenologist sounds like Orson Squire Fowler, America’s most famous phrenologist, who also preached the octagon house in A Home for All (1848).
    • The phrenologist believes males are smarter than females. In the US gender disparity impacts students in different ways. Girls outperform boys in reading and writing in nearly every U.S. school district. By eighth grade, girls are almost a full grade level ahead of boys in reading. Boys may be more likely to have learning disabilities or be influenced by masculine norms that de-prioritize reading. Boys are more likely to experience behavioral problems and receive disciplinary action in school, including suspensions. This affects their learning outcomes and can contribute to a widening achievement gap. While gender gaps in math have narrowed, boys in affluent, educated, and predominantly white districts still tend to outperform girls. In poorer, more diverse areas, girls sometimes outperform boys. Girls are more likely than boys to graduate from high school on time. In 2021, the on-time graduation rate for girls was 88.4%, compared to 81.9% for boys. Women have outnumbered men in higher education since the 1980s. A 2024 analysis found that among recent high school graduates, women enrolled in college at a rate 9 percentage points higher than men. Enrollment growth for women at four-year universities continues to rise, while male enrollment has stagnated. Women earn a majority of associate’s, bachelor’s, and master’s degrees. As of 2024, 40.1% of women had completed four or more years of college, compared to 37.1% of men. This reverses a historical pattern where men once earned a larger share of all degrees. Women are more likely to complete their degrees. Data from the National Student Clearinghouse shows that across all school types, women graduate at a rate of 67% compared to 60% for men. At four-year institutions, the six-year completion rate is 7 percentage points higher for women. Women disproportionately carry student loan debt. They are more likely than men to take out student loans and graduate with an average of $7,000 more debt. The gender gap in education is most pronounced in STEM fields and careers. Women are severely underrepresented in engineering and computer science. In 2018, women earned just 22% of bachelor’s degrees in engineering and 19% in computer science. Conversely, women are overrepresented in health-related STEM fields like biological sciences and psychology. Women comprise only 34% of the U.S. STEM workforce. This varies widely by field, with women accounting for just 16% of engineers in 2023. A 2023 Gallup survey found that male Gen Z youth are significantly more interested in STEM careers than their female counterparts. Girls report less exposure to technical STEM concepts, such as computer programming and electrical circuits, in school. Female Gen Z students are nearly 20 percentage points more likely than males to say they are not interested in a STEM career because they don’t think they would be good at it. Gender stereotypes can influence how girls and boys perceive their own abilities, particularly in STEM. Academic success, especially in reading, is sometimes discouraged by traditional norms of masculinity. A student’s socioeconomic status intersects with gender. For example, the male college enrollment gap is wider for Black and Hispanic men. Financial constraints also affect college-bound women more, with a Pew Research survey finding women more likely than men to say they couldn’t afford a four-year degree. Girls tend to develop more effective self-regulated learning skills, which include planning and organization. This contributes to their greater academic success. Gender biases among educators can influence how they perceive and treat students, sometimes underestimating girls’ math abilities. The lower educational attainment of men has consequences for their economic mobility. For women, educational gains in fields like STEM do not always translate to comparable pay, and they face a persistent wage gap within the STEM workforce. The underrepresentation of women in high-earning STEM fields and the lagging achievement of boys in school represent an underutilization of talent that can hinder economic growth. The segregation of fields of study by gender perpetuates occupational segregation in the workforce, reinforcing traditional gender roles and limiting opportunities. (The school statistics are after 1964.)

In the United States, Clara Barton founded the American Red Cross in 1881, establishing its presence and leading its early efforts in disaster relief and aiding the U.S. military.

  • “was she not a MacIntosh”: MacIntosh is a Scottish name whose motto is “Touch not the cat bot a glove,” that is, without a glove. The Mackintoshes belong to Clan Chattan, the clan of the cat. See On the MacIntosh motto.
    • The House of Stuart (Stewart) was a royal house of Scotland, Ireland, England and Great Britain.
  • “those who allowed others to carry their umbrellas for them”: See Jonas Hanway and the umbrella in the Chapter 63 resources. See The Umbrella.
  • “here in this democracy where death must level all”: See Shirley’s “Death the Leveller” in the Chapter 60 resources. See Death and Mortality.
  • “their perfidious opium paradise”: See The History of Opium and Catherine Cartwheel.
  • “she having refused the hand of the true King of Sweden”: King of Sweden refers to Mr. Bonebreaker and his belief that he was descended from royalty. See Mr. Titus Bonebreaker, the Gospel salesman in Chapter 65 whose grandmother told him he was the true King of Sweden.
  • “her red bloomer leg”: Red bloomers, named after reformer Amelia Bloomer, emerged in the 1850s as a radical symbol of women’s dress reform for health and freedom, featuring loose trousers under a short skirt for mobility, but faced public scorn as “unwomanly” and threatening gender norms, leading many suffragists to adopt less controversial symbols like a red shawl, though bloomers persisted as gym/cycling wear. See Women’s Suffrage.
  • “her striped sock unravelling”: Striped socks were often used to denote servants, jesters, prostitutes, or prisoners, marking them as outsiders during the medieval period. The stigma faded as sports emerged in the 19th century; baseball teams like the Baltimore Orioles wore striped stockings for team identity. Stripes appeared in children’s wear in the Victorian Era, often associated with whimsical characters like fairies, creating a paradox with their earlier negative connotations.
  • “if she should meet with Mr. Res Tacamah”: See Mr. Res Tacamah.
  • “if she should meet with Mr. Chandelier and his floating prisms”: See Mr. Chandelier.
  • “We would not see the phantom driver driving a phantom carriage”: See The Black Coachman.
  • “by making old God rest on Sunday, the first day of the week”: “But the seventh day is the sabbath of the LORD thy God” (Exodus 20:10, KJV). See Constantine’s Sunday law in the Chapter 63 resources.
    • The Church of Rome is the Roman Catholic Church.
  • “the macintosh that grew on the rosy boughs of the old Iowa”: The McIntosh apple came from a seedling John McIntosh found on his farm in Dundela, Ontario, in 1811, not from Iowa. See the Macintosh raincoat in the Chapter 63 resources.
  • “from sea to shining sea”: “And crown thy good with brotherhood / From sea to shining sea!” (Katharine Lee Bates, “America the Beautiful,” 1895).
  • “the macintosh being no fruit in Eden on the Tree of Knowledge”: Eden is the Garden of Eden described as the biblical paradise in Genesis. The Tree of Knowledge is one of two specific trees in the story of the Garden of Eden in Genesis 2-3, along with the tree of life. Alternatively, some scholars have argued that the tree of the knowledge of good and evil is just another name for the tree of life. Genesis never calls the forbidden fruit an apple. See Genesis.
    • Babylon was the capital city of the ancient Babylonian Empire in the Mesopotamian area in antiquity.
    • The Egyptian Book of the Dead is an ancient Egyptian funerary text generally written on papyrus and used from the beginning of the New Kingdom (around 1550 BCE) to around 50 BCE. See Ancient Egypt.
  • “the macintosh being not those golden apples falling upward”: Golden apples belong to Greek myth: the apples of the Hesperides that Heracles fetched, the apples Atalanta stooped for, and the apple of discord Paris gave to Aphrodite. See Greek Mythology.
    • The golden apples of myth symbolize lofty ideals or corruptions, while the macintosh apple represents the practical, real-world legacy of Miss MacIntosh’s forefathers, rooted in honest labor and the necessities of life. This contrast underscores Miss MacIntosh’s grounded, no-nonsense character and her emphasis on reality over illusion or fantasy.

Combers

Constitutional

Denuded

Disdain

Firmament

Foghorn

Frugal

Gauze

Knobbly and twisted, as old trees or hands.

Incarceration
Imprisonment.
Lethargic

Giving off light.
Lute

Mackintosh

Mandarin

Mantle

Mauve

Mosaic
A picture or pattern made by setting small pieces of colored stone, glass, or tile together.
