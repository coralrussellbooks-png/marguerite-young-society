Chapter 62: The Will and the Water Music

Research notes: Chapter 62

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh

Themes: Faith, God, and the Supernatural · Music, Sound, and Silence

Symbols: Fire · Music and Instruments · Snow, Ice, and Winter · The Sea and Water

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Astronomy · Deafness and Sign Language · Suicide in History and Literature

Chapter 62 is, among other things, the chapter in which Mr. Spitzer writes his last will and testament. It is not a conventional will. It is one of the most beautiful documents in the novel.

What actually happens

Mr. Spitzer, still moving through his interior life with the slow deliberateness we have come to expect, does three things in this chapter: he dreams of a river that transforms from fire to ice; he rewrites his will, leaving his music to the world; and he learns the full story of the old violinist from the library steps, the man from Chapter 58 who played an imaginary violin with an imaginary bow.

He also contemplates a clarinetist he once knew who died by suicide, leaving a note that said he was going to join a greater orchestra.

The river of fire and ice

The chapter opens in a dream, or something like one: Mr. Spitzer rowing down a river that shifts from molten fire to frozen ice, surrounded by walruses and seals wearing diamond necklaces, seagulls transforming into musicians mid-flight, a great water orchestra assembled on the banks. The imagery is extravagant and dreamlike, but its logic is consistent: the river is always the same river, just in different states. Fire and ice are the same element transformed. The orchestra assembles from birds.

Mr. Spitzer wonders, at the opening of this chapter, whether he has ever truly slept. This is not insomnia; it is something more fundamental: the suspicion that perfect rest, like perfect music, is always just beyond reach, always slightly unattained. He has been pursuing sleep the way he has been pursuing the right notes, and neither has fully arrived.

The will

The will is extraordinary. Mr. Spitzer bequeaths his music (all of it, the compositions that exist only in his head and on scraps of paper and on his shirt cuffs) to the world, to be played on water. "Even to indifferent ears," he writes, "and even to deaf ears and even to dead ears and to sea conches." He cancels his subscriptions. He leaves instructions about eggs and milk, small domestic rituals elevated into symbolic gestures.

What is he doing? He is an artist accepting that his work may never be widely heard, and leaving it anyway. He is giving his music to the world even as he acknowledges that the world may not notice. The gift is unconditional. This is what the will says, in its legal and poetic and slightly mad form: I give all of this to everyone, including those who cannot hear it.

A legal term appears here: mortmain, from the French for "dead hand", the legal concept of property held in perpetuity by an institution, a dead hand continuing to shape the living world. Mr. Spitzer's will is an act of mortmain in the best sense: his silent music reaching forward, past his death, into whatever ears might eventually receive it.

The old violinist's story

Here, in Chapter 62, we learn what we didn't know in Chapter 58: the old violinist on the library steps was not simply a street musician. He was a second violinist with the Philadelphia Orchestra. He had played, without ever touching his strings, for years. His silent bowing was not eccentricity; it was his entire technique. He had found a way to perform invisibly within the orchestra, contributing nothing physical to the sound, but present, bowing, part of the whole.

Then, one night, he accidentally produced a false note, a single audible sound, and was dismissed.

Read that again. He was dismissed not for failing to play, but for accidentally succeeding. The moment he made actual music, the illusion was broken, and the orchestra no longer had a place for him. Young is making an argument here about the nature of performance, visibility, and institutional belonging: some artists are tolerated only as long as they remain inaudible. The moment they sound, they are expelled.

Mr. Spitzer recognizes in this man's silent bowing what he had intuited in Chapter 58: this was perhaps the best music, the truest music, the music that exists only in gesture and attention and the imagination of whoever is watching.

The clarinetist who joined a greater orchestra

Mr. Spitzer knew a clarinetist who committed suicide because he felt perpetually out of tune. This was not a technical problem; everyone who heard him assured him he was perfectly in tune. But he could not reconcile what he heard inside himself with what the world reflected back. The dissonance was internal and total and could not be argued away.

He left a note saying he was going to join a greater orchestra.

Mr. Spitzer takes this seriously. He does not dismiss it as metaphor or delusion. He considers it as a genuine possibility: that somewhere beyond this life there is an orchestra that includes all the lost music, all the unplayed compositions, all the clarinetists who were never quite in tune with the world they inhabited. His own music, which no one has ever played or evoked, might find its place there.

"My God! I thought I was in Philadelphia."

Near the end of the chapter, Mr. Spitzer looks up, disoriented, and realizes he is not where he thought he was. The city has shifted on him again. His grasp of place is unstable (he drifts between cities, between states, between realms) and this moment of confusion is both comic and heartbreaking. He has been walking through his interior world so completely that the exterior world has rearranged itself around him without his noticing.

This is not mere absentmindedness. It is the logical consequence of being the kind of person Mr. Spitzer is: someone for whom the music in his head is more real, more present, more insistent than the street he is standing on.

The music that no one has ever played

The chapter keeps circling back to this fact: Mr. Spitzer's music has never been played. Not by anyone. Not even, perhaps, by him, since he is deaf, or nearly so, and his compositions exist as patterns on paper and in memory, as the trajectories of migratory birds and billiard balls, as elegies with names like Music for Polar Bear and Butterfly and Iceberg Music and Music for the Southern Cross.

And yet he keeps writing. He is not discouraged by his failures. The music is real to him, and it will be real in his will, bequeathed to the sea conches who will receive it without judgment. This is, quietly, one of the most radical arguments in the novel: that the unheard work is still work, that the unplayed music is still music, that creating in obscurity is not the same as creating in vain.

What to hold onto

Mr. Spitzer sitting down to write his will, leaving everything (the water music, the bird-flight compositions, the impossibly complex elegies) to deaf ears, dead ears, sea conches, and anyone else who might happen to be listening. The dead hand reaching forward. The music given away before it was ever received.
