Pillar: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away · Across every book

Or, Why the Past Is Right Beneath Your Feet

We are trained to think of time as a giant, one-way conveyor belt. The future is ahead of us, the present is where we are standing, and the past is a distant land trailing out behind us. When bad things happen in history, or even in our own personal lives, people love to say: well, that is in the past now, or we need to look forward, not backward. We treat history like a book where the previous chapters are closed for good. Move on. Do not look back. The past has nothing to do with the present. This is the story we tell ourselves, and we tell it over and over, as if repetition could make it true.

But Marguerite Young looks at the clock and tells us that linear time is an illusion. The past does not recede. It does not float away into irrelevance. In her system, the past sediments. It accumulates right underneath our feet, layer upon layer, heavy and present.

The Geology of Our Minds

To understand this pillar, Young invites us to look at the earth itself. Think about a geological cross-section of rock layers beneath a riverbed. You have layers from millions of years ago, layers from ice ages, layers from recent floods. You have compressed shells from ancient seas. You have the ash from volcanic eruptions that happened long before humans walked the earth. None of those past eras have magically vanished into thin air. They are all physically present right now, stacked on top of one another, heavily pressing down on the surface we walk on today.

The weight of those layers shapes everything about the present moment. The river flows the way it does because of the rock layers beneath it. The soil composition in one place differs from another because of what sedimented there millions of years ago. The mineral content of the groundwater, the stability of the bedrock, the likelihood of earthquakes or floods in any given area: all of this is determined by the sedimentary layers beneath our feet. You cannot understand the present landscape without understanding its geological history.

Young argues that time works exactly like geology. The past does not float away. It layers up. Every joy you have experienced, every historical event, and every collective trauma is still right here, forming the invisible substrate of our current reality. The joy of your childhood does not disappear; it sediments into who you are now. The trauma that happened to your grandparents does not vanish; it shapes the nervous system you inherited. The slavery that built wealth for certain families does not fade into history; it compounds into the economic inequality we see today. The land theft that displaced indigenous peoples does not resolve itself; it shapes the property arrangements, the water rights, and the resource access that create the landscape of our present moment.

To bring this point home, Young's ideas connect with the philosopher Walter Benjamin and his famous image of the angel of history. Benjamin describes a painting by the artist Paul Klee, and in this painting, there is an angel. The angel is being pushed backward by a violent wind, a storm that is blowing it forward into the future. But the angel is not looking ahead toward the future. It is facing backward, looking at the past. And what does the angel see? Not a chain of linear events. Instead, it sees one single, massive catastrophe that keeps piling wreckage upon wreckage right at its feet. The past is always right there, loudly demanding our full attention. It is not behind us. It is beneath us, accumulated and pressing.

Benjamin wrote this in 1940, as the Nazi regime was destroying Europe. He was trying to make sense of how this catastrophe was possible, how humanity could perpetrate such violence. The answer, he suggested, is that the catastrophe was not some aberration. It was sedimentary. It had layers upon layers beneath it: centuries of colonialism, of slavery, of dehumanization, of the treatment of certain people as disposable. All of that sediment had accumulated, and the genocide was its eruption. You cannot understand the present catastrophe without understanding what sedimented beneath it.

Why Tyrants Want You to Forget the Sediment

So, how does shifting from a straight-line view of time to a layered view of time help us resist authoritarian power?

Authoritarian movements and dominant political systems absolutely love the straight-line progress narrative. They want you to believe that the past is a separate country that has nothing to do with today. That was then, this is now. We have progressed beyond those problems. That was a different era. This ideology allows them to cover up their tracks. It tries to convince you that the current setup of society is natural, fair, and neutral. It was not created by anyone. It just is.

When we embrace sedimentary time, we bust this narrative. We expose what was hidden beneath the straight-line story.

We expose the roots of wealth and power. We realize that things like the wealth extracted from centuries of enslaved labor are not just past events to be glossed over in a textbook. They are active, heavy layers in the current sediment, directly shaping today's economic and property arrangements. The fact that certain families own most of the land in certain regions, that certain cities have concentrations of wealth while others are disinvested: none of this is accidental or natural. It is sedimentary. It accumulated through specific historical choices that were made by specific people, and those choices are still shaping the landscape today.

We stop falling for fake progress. When a politician says we have moved past that era, a sedimentary mindset reminds us that you cannot just paint over a deep geological layer. The wreckage is still in the soil. The sediment is still pressing down. Building a just society requires attending to what is in the substrate rather than pretending the ground is neutral.

We protect collective memory. Authoritarians want a blank slate so they can rewrite history to fit their propaganda. They want you to believe that the regime is building something completely new, something unprecedented, something that has never been done before. This requires erasing all the layers that came before. Remembering that the past is always present beneath us prevents our collective memory from being erased. We refuse the blank slate. We insist on the layers.

Understanding time as a layered structure removes the mask from historical injustice. It shows us that the struggles of the past are not over. They are simply the ground we are standing on right now. Our job is not to move on from them. Our job is to understand them, to feel their weight, to acknowledge the people who suffered under those layers, and to take responsibility for the ground we are forming for those who come after.

The Archive and the Uncovered Ground: What We Found in the Swamp

To understand what sedimentary time means in practice, consider what happened when archaeologists and historians began to seriously study the Great Dismal Swamp: a vast wetland that spans the border between Virginia and North Carolina.

For centuries, this swamp had been treated as a barrier, a wasteland, a place to be drained and developed and turned into profitable land. But when historians began to look closely at what was actually in the swamp's sediment, they discovered something that the straight-line progress narrative had erased: the swamp had been a place of refuge for enslaved people. For over two hundred years, people fleeing slavery had found hideouts in the swamp's hidden channels and islands. They had built communities. They had maintained their dignity and their freedom in a place that the system wanted to treat as empty, uninhabitable wasteland.

The ground itself had been carrying that history. The swamp was not a blank slate waiting to be developed. It was a palimpsest of resistance and refuge, of struggle and survival. But the straight-line progress narrative had erased it. It had been treated as empty, as nothing, as worthless.

When archaeologists began to dig, to look at the sediment carefully, the layers began to tell their story. They found pottery fragments. They found the remains of structures. They found evidence of long-term habitation. They found archaeological proof that people had been living on that ground with intention and dignity, even though the official historical record had simply erased them.

This is what sedimentary time does. It refuses the erasure. It says: even though the system wanted to forget this, even though it was not written in the official history books, the ground itself carries the memory. The sediment knows. And when we pay close attention to the sediment, we recover what was taken from us.

A straight-line progress narrative would say: the past is past, slavery is over, we should move on. A sedimentary approach says: slavery is not over. Its effects are still in the economic structure, still in the laws, still in the trauma passed down through generations, still in the land arrangements, still in who owns what and who has access to what. The swamp's history of refuge is not a quaint historical detail. It is information we need right now to understand who we are and what ground we are standing on.

The Practice

Pick a physical place in your town or city. It could be an old building, a park, a street name, or even your own neighborhood. Do a little mental digging. What are the historical layers beneath it? Who lived there before? What built the economy of that space? Who benefited from those arrangements? What sediment is pressing down on the ground you are standing on?

Remind yourself that those older layers have not disappeared. They are actively pressing on the present moment. They are in the water table. They are in the property arrangements. They are in who has access to resources. They are in the inherited trauma and the inherited wealth. Practice standing on the ground with an awareness of everything it carries. Notice what changes when you stop treating the past as behind you and start treating it as beneath you.

Also apply this to your own body and your own history. The childhood you lived is not safely behind you. It is sedimentary in your nervous system. The losses you have suffered are not past events to move on from. They are compressed into your capacity to love and to be cautious. The family patterns, the cultural inheritances, the traumas passed down through generations: all of this is sediment. When you pay attention to it, when you refuse the straight-line progress narrative and acknowledge the layers, something shifts. You stop blaming yourself for your reactions. You start understanding them as sedimentary. And you start asking: what kind of sediment am I creating for those who come after me?
