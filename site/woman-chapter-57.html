Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 57

← Chapter 56 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 58 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer

A tailor's bill drives this chapter, and the debt that will not close is its pillar. Mr. Spitzer owes for the black funeral suit, and the accounting of funeral expenses, the honor and debt and legacy, is the masculine order of ledgers, and the tailor who made the suit, a male "figure of creation and transformation," stands for patriarchy's power to cut and shape a self from the outside. That the tailor may never have existed, or is dead, is the collapse of the very certainty the ledger depended on, the real and the dream running together so that the man who owes cannot find the man he owes.

The letters Mr. Spitzer opens carry the chapter's women. "Old Floradora girls" with "matted horse-hair wigs" and "eyeballs distended with bella donna" are the marginalized femininity of performance and aging and decay, entertainers whose value faded when their youth did, and the horse-hair wigs are the self as mask again, the detachable covering the book keeps returning to since Miss MacIntosh's own. Spitzer, "enclosed in a sea shell" while Peron went free, is male confinement inside societal expectation, and his "burning point" against the tailor's insult to his integrity is the social reputation and masculine pride the book keeps naming so it can watch them dissolve.

The search for the tailor takes him through a shifting dreamlike city of "contradictory signs and illusions," and the navigation of masks and false facades is real and dream as one country, a man looking for a stable identity and a fixed street and finding neither. He cannot retrace his steps, cannot map his own history, and seeing his reflection "like the pigeon's watery murmur" is the fragile, unstable self-recognition the book gives everyone who looks for a single self and finds a rippling one. The city directories and maps that hold no tailor and no street are the record failing to keep a life, women's absence from the archive and the book's larger erasure the same failure, and the thought of hiring an "Indian guide" pulls colonial and racial dispossession into the same lost ground.

Obituary and society news set men's public achievements beside women's social appearances, and the "famous beauty" who abandoned society and is presumed dead is the boundary between life and death held open on a woman reported gone because she stopped being seen. The old librarian, comically inept with a string of spectacles "one for every kind of distance" and always the wrong pair, is a female authority made outdated and ineffective, and the library's Bedlam, the people talking to themselves, the "living and dead men debating the soul's existence," is the fractured modern world with its patriarchal order coming undone. The Confederate veteran insisting on a bullet from Appomattox that could not be there is historical myth-making the book meets with a "sympathetic nod as if a high wind were blowing," and the chapter closes on clouds as moths shifting into skulls and birds and roses, a rose named for Spitzer "proud that he was faithful," red and white grafted on one bush, transformation and multiplicity flowering where the fixed self was supposed to stand.
