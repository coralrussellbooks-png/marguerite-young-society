Part 1 covers the front matter, the Publisher's Note, and the Stories section through the first two pages of "Old James," so that story breaks off mid-scene.

Front matter and the Publisher's Note

The book is the 1994 Dalkey Archive edition, assembled by Steven Moore while Dalkey was preparing its reissue of Miss MacIntosh, My Darling. His note lays out the editorial logic plainly. There are three sections (stories, essays, reviews), each arranged roughly chronologically. Four pieces were previously unpublished, one story and three essays, and because Young could not recall when she wrote them, the story went first in its section and the essays toward the end of theirs. Surviving typescripts were used to check published versions, which let Moore restore a paragraph of "An Afternoon with Marianne Moore" that Vogue's editors had cut. He credits Martha J. Sattler's bibliography as the thing that made the collection possible.

The contents page shows how wide her short prose ranged: Midwestern place essays, a visit with Marianne Moore, Mari Sandoz, Edward Young's Night Thoughts, Coventry Patmore, a memorial speech for Mark Van Doren, and reviews touching Caruso, Joseph Smith, Anaïs Nin, and a book on the "Endless House." The mix of mysticism, Americana, and literary portraiture is already visible before a single story starts.

"My Grandmother's Foot" (previously unpublished)

This is the piece most recognizably in the voice of Miss MacIntosh. The narrator admits the grandmother is "a construct of my imagination," then insists in the same breath that everything "is based on factual, uncolored reports and can be sworn to." That double claim, invention and testimony held together, is the story's engine. Young's sentences keep correcting and extending themselves ("or, more specifically"), and the family is built out of doublings: the father's luck being "the exact reverse" of his character, the mother facing "not one but two sinister separations," the elephant "multiplying by bifurcation," and the mother herself "merely doubled like the bifurcating elephant."

The mother, drugged and kept "like a Sleeping Beauty in a glass cage," neither dead nor alive, reads as an early sketch of the opium-dreaming mother in the novel. Against the parents, who love "inanimate objects, even a fence post, as substitutes for reality," the narrator sets the Jewish grandmother as "the great sanity of vision, the capacity for merely human life."

The plot is comic and grotesque in equal parts. The grandfather is torn apart in a pogrom and "scattered all over the corn fields," with no way to be reassembled on Resurrection Day. The grandmother's amputated foot is buried "in a Fourteenth Street shoe box," and when the rabbis exhume it to reunite it with her body, they find "a baby's hand, so small and blue." The father then searches a hospital's "morgue of missing members" and picks a foot with a bunion, possibly the wrong one. The repeated phrase "miscarriage of justice" carries a quiet second meaning once the baby's hand appears.

The ending turns the whole joke toward war: "When I see whole cities dissolving under the impact of high explosives, I do think of my grandmother's foot." The question "Should we really devote ourselves to the great business of scattering each other?" makes the story a meditation on bodily wholeness against mass violence, and on Jewish history as both suffering and the source of an unbreakable gaiety. The final image, "A lost foot," becomes something the narrator and "humanity itself" are searching for.

"The Dead Women" (American Prefaces, 1943)

This one is spare and realist, written in close third on Leda, a pregnant young woman who prepares bodies for her father-in-law's funeral business. It moves through three months, each with its own corpse.

In October it is an old country woman. Leda decides against rouge so that "God might recognize her," and so she "might know herself, with the kinks in the bones of her back." In November it is a young woman who died after a wild night, and here the prose slips into present tense as Leda imagines the saloon, the nickel in the slot, the dance going on. This time Leda does use rouge, "as if there would be a dance tonight, after all." In December the body is an old man found frozen in a shed with his feet in cold ashes, and the title's promise of women breaks. The third "dead woman" seems to be Leda herself, or what she carries. She has already said she "felt like two women, herself and someone else."

The father-in-law is loud, jovial, and relentlessly about birth: the Guernsey's twins "black as midnight," the teasing "I hope you don't fool Tom that way," the story of Tom crawling into coffins as a baby. His repeated question, "Have you felt it kicking yet?", gets deflected in October and November. In December no one asks, and the story ends: "But she knew. But she was sure now." Leda walks "heavily toward the stairway, passing alone under the sprig of green holly," the same spot where he always kisses her. The dripping faucet and the Christmas party with no gifts sharpen the sense that she is keeping time alone. The ending never says outright what she knows, but everything points toward a death inside her that the cheerful household cannot hear.

Feet recur here too: the crippled daughter "who has only one foot" bringing asters on a paper heart, and the father-in-law limping from a calf stepping on him. Read alongside the first story, the motif links the two across what seems a long gap in time and style.

"Old James" (opening only)

What appears sets up an Indianapolis stockbroker during the Depression and his secretary, Miss Greenwood, who enjoys his inattention because "in that fact lay her only freedom." She draws Greek faces in her shorthand book and sings hymns over the typewriter. James spends his days on the phone telling anyone who will listen about hollyhocks supposedly descended from Anne Hathaway's garden, the number of cows in Indiana, and old farm boundaries from when Sixteenth Street "was known as Tinker." Visitors ask whether he is "Expecting to go somewhere," which already hints at a man stalled on the edge of some departure. The local-history cataloguing looks forward to the Indiana material in the essays.

Across part 1

The ordering Moore chose puts the mature, digressive, comic-mystical voice first and the plainer 1943 story second, so part 1 almost runs backward through her development. What holds steady is the concern with the body as something that must be kept whole, the closeness of birth and death, women whose inner lives go unnoticed by the people around them, and a humor that sits right next to the macabre without canceling it.
