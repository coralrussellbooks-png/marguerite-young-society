Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 25

← Chapter 24 · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 26 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh

Pillars: Not One Self · Grief Doesn't End · The Past Doesn't Go Away

The Questions

Catherine accuses Mr. Spitzer of being dead, of being in several places at once, of being his own brother. How much of him is built by her looking at him?

He is “a soul divided,” thinking his dead brother’s thoughts. Where is the line between mourning someone and being them?

If he cannot tell whether he is “the right man in the wrong world, or the wrong man in the wrong world,” what is left to stand on?

What is a grief the book calls “perpetual and unconsummated suicide”?

Mr. Spitzer is being built here, and built by Catherine’s looking. Inside her sealed dream she sees him shift positions, occupy several places at once, cross the line into death, and her perceptions do not merely misread him; they make him. He begins to define himself by what she sees, uncertain whether he is “the right man in the wrong world, or the wrong man in the wrong world.” Not One Self, which began as his doubling with his dead brother, now takes in this too: a self is not only many from inside but shaped from outside, constructed in the gaze of another, and Catherine’s dreaming gaze is constructing him.

His grief has become identity. He is “a soul divided,” and the division runs through his dead twin: he thinks his brother’s thoughts, lives his brother’s life, cannot find where mourning ends and being begins. The book gives this its hardest name, a “perpetual and unconsummated suicide,” a dying-with that never completes and never releases. This is Grief Doesn't End past even the walled and the open into something stranger, grief that has stopped being an emotion he has and become the material he is made of. He does not carry the loss. He is partly assembled out of it.

Time in him is fully unmoored. He lives in the future and the past, his sense of time “like Catherine’s opium illusions,” the “deathless dead” moving through his present as freely as the living. The Past Doesn't Go Away, which pressed the past into the present as a weight, has in Mr. Spitzer become the medium he swims in, no floor of now to stand on, the dead as present as anyone and the schedule long since abandoned. He is the book’s furthest reach into what a self looks like when time stops being a line.

And the chapter sits inside “mortal chaos,” “meaningless fluctuations,” a “principle of nothing but error repeating error.” It sounds like the book giving up, and it is not. This is what the open condition looks like from inside a person who has lost every seal at once, no fixed self, no fixed time, no fixed world, only the fluctuation. The chaos is not a failure of meaning but the raw state before any construction is imposed, and Mr. Spitzer is living in it without the shelter of Catherine’s dream or Miss MacIntosh’s facts. He is unsealed almost all the way down, and the almost is his suffering.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes its “mortal chaos” as the book’s nihilism, a confession that nothing means anything, and the reading that takes Mr. Spitzer’s division as a clinical breakdown to be diagnosed. Neither the despair nor the diagnosis meets what the chapter shows.

The footing is that the self is built from outside as well as from within, and Catherine’s dreaming gaze is constructing Mr. Spitzer even as his grief for his brother constructs him from inside. His grief has stopped being an emotion and become his material, a “perpetual and unconsummated suicide.” Time in him is no longer a line but a medium. And the “mortal chaos” is not the loss of meaning but the raw unsealed condition before any construction is imposed, which he lives in without shelter. A self is constructed from both sides, and grief can become the stuff one is made of. Mr. Spitzer is left in the fluctuation, unsealed and unsheltered, and the chapter does not rescue him into any fixed thing.

For Reflection

How much of who one is has been built by the way certain people look at one?

Where is the line between mourning someone and becoming them?

What would it be like to lose every seal at once, no fixed self, time, or world, and only the fluctuation left?
