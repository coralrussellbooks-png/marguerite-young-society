Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Allusions: The Bible

Definition from research

"God pity us. God pity the small fishes and the great whales beached on this beach" is a plea for mercy over all creation, least and greatest alike, the fish and the whale stranded out of their element, an image of spiritual desolation with Jonah behind it.

"wash our sins away and leave us white as snow" is Psalm 51:7, David's prayer for cleansing.

"Our mothers and our fathers never knew us" leans on Matthew 10, where following Christ splits families apart.

"This earth is but the poor testing ground for the king rail with the bleeding throat," on through the broken crab and the dead clam to "all oxen are our brothers," repeats the trial-of-earth theme and widens it to every suffering creature, closing on a Franciscan kinship in which the ox is a brother.

The threat that "if ever I told what I had seen, she would kill me" draws on the commandment against killing and on Paul's glass seen darkly (1 Corinthians 13:12), a secret half-glimpsed and forbidden.

"Where is Tobias Smollett?" glances at the Book of Tobit, the deuterocanonical tale of Tobit, Sarah, and young Tobias guided by the angel Raphael.

"There is no God" is the flat denial of Psalm 14:1, "the fool hath said in his heart, there is no God," spoken here as Miss MacIntosh's bleakest turn.

The vision of "eyes filled... with light which came from no reflection," of a "forehead without brows or hairline" and a "viewless summit," is the first veiled sight of Miss MacIntosh's baldness, her head made a cloud-topped mountain, the biblical site of divine encounter.

"God loves you! God loves the wind, the empty places" scatters divine love across the unseen and the barren, the wind that in Scripture is also the Spirit.

"Heaven's nets are woven very wide" recalls the parable of the dragnet in Matthew 13, the net that gathers all and sorts them at the end.

Two lines, "this life was all a dream, and there was nothing" and "in another dream, we die," collapse life and death alike into dreaming and strip away the Christian afterlife, leaving dream and void where doctrine promised judgment and eternity.

"God Himself... does not know God, and the night is long which has no ending" turns even God's self-knowledge into a question and stretches the dark toward a night without end.

The string of proverbs, "Better go to hell with rags than to heaven with embroidery," "the best are often the worst," "we are all our fallen brothers," reworks the gospel reversals, the last shall be first, and the doctrine of the Fall into Miss MacIntosh's hard, humble wisdom.

"that authority which deserts the dying king" glances at kingship and abandonment, Christ's own cry of forsakenness not far behind it.

"robbing Peter to pay Paul," an idiom born of the two Roman basilicas, lands Miss MacIntosh's grand talk back among household money troubles.

"he is unworthy to live who lives only for himself" carries the gospel ethic of care for the orphan and the stranger (James 1) and the call to lose one's life for others (Mark 8), set here against the question of Sabbath rest.

"my father had fallen," "that secret was my sin," "my heart was the heart of the night" gather the language of the Fall, of sin, and of spiritual darkness around Vera's own guilt and her longing for a father to guide her.
