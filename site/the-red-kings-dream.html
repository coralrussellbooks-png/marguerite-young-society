Movement Five: The Traditions the Novel Absorbs

Themes: Dreams and Imagination

Allusions: Alice in Wonderland

Pillars: Pillar 1 (Not One Self) · Pillar 2 (Reality Is Constructed) · Pillar 8 (The Past Doesn't Go Away)

Chapters discussed: 1, 13, 25, 26, 37, 41, 48, 50, 51, 54, 56, 58, 69

Ah, and if the dreamer was dead, how should he know—for did not the dream live?

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, Chapter 56

A Book That Never Says Her Name

Alice never appears in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling. Neither does Lewis Carroll, the Oxford mathematician who wrote Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865) and Through the Looking-Glass (1871). But Young puts her sources into her own words, and To All My Darlings finds Carroll in at least fifteen chapters: in the chessmen, the rabbits, the doors too small to pass through, the trial that comes before the crime, the dreamer who may be dreamed. The Cartwheel house is, among other things, a looking-glass house.

It makes sense that it should be. Carroll's two books are about a girl who falls into a world where the rules of the waking world do not hold, and who keeps trying, politely, to make sense of it. That is Vera's childhood exactly.

The Life-Sized Chessmen

In Through the Looking-Glass, Alice steps into a countryside laid out like a chessboard and becomes a pawn in a game played with living pieces. The Cartwheel garden has its own. In Chapter 1, among the broken statues, stand “the life-sized chessmen seeming to move against the moving clouds that moved above the moving waters, the sea light lighting their wooden eyes, the seagulls perched like drifts of snow upon their heads.” In Chapter 13, as Vera looks out on her mother's imaginary garden party, the board itself moves: “a chessboard of which the squares moved and changed positions in the mathematical wind, the life-sized chessmen seemed, as they emerged from corruscated mist, their rounded eyeballs lighted by the reflection of the absent sea, to be moving, too, as if played by invisible players who could perceive an order in multiple confusions of movements and sounds, a secret plan.”

Carroll's game has a plan; Alice is moving toward the eighth square, where she will be crowned. Young's chessmen move “as if” someone were playing them, and nobody in the novel is sure there is a player. The same Miss MacIntosh who would not allow chess in the house, as Flipping the Cave tells, lived beside a garden full of chessmen as tall as she was.

Too Big for the Door

In Wonderland, Alice drinks and eats and changes size, now too large for the little door into the garden, now too small to reach the key. She feels herself “shutting up like a telescope.” Young gives that trouble to the people in the house. In Chapter 41, Catherine “was fixated for years on the idea that she had swallowed the blue divan with the golden lion hooves and wings and triple golden crowns, so that she could not get through the door, wide though it was, wide enough to admit a longhaired star.” And: “She complained for years that she was the door through which she passed or could not pass.”

In Chapter 48, Mr. Spitzer, trying to find his way out of the house, asks Alice's question: “Did the house expand—or was it shrinking? Was it small as a pea pod?” Later in the chapter, he wonders how he has come to be where he is: “Perhaps he had passed through a keyhole.”

Catherine's visitors behave like the White Rabbit, the first creature Alice follows, “a White Rabbit with pink eyes.” In Chapter 41, in Catherine's dream of the snow, there are “the rabbits wearing lace veils and silver petticoats, walking upright,” and “She saw their pink eyes in the dying light. Sometimes a great rabbit came and talked to her of Caesar’s campaign and the War of the Roses.” The White Rabbit drops his fan as he runs. In the same chapter, Catherine's guests leave things behind too: “Once there was a purple feather fan which one of these imaginary guests had surely left, proving that, though there had been no one, his visit had been real, tangible, self-evident.”

Nonsense Rules

Wonderland has its own laws, and they make a terrible kind of sense. At the trial, the King of Hearts reads out “Rule Forty-two. All persons more than a mile high to leave the court.” In Chapter 25, Catherine has a rule of her own: “that all the people who were more than three miles tall should be put out of the house, as she would say, for fear they should bump their heads into Mr. Chandelier with his candle lights like dim stars in some dim, glassy heaven.”

After the Caucus-race, where everyone runs in circles and no one can say who won, the Dodo rules: “Everybody has won, and all must have prizes.” In Chapter 26, Mr. Spitzer gives the same verdict in the language of his dead brother, the gambler: “All souls should be equal when all were dead, as Mr. Spitzer would say, and all horses should come in first.” In Carroll it is a joke. In Young it is also a hope.

Mad Hatters and March Hares

Carroll's Hatter and March Hare keep a tea party going forever because time has stopped at six o'clock. Young borrows their madness. In Chapter 54, people take Mr. Spitzer for a madman, “as Peron had been mad as a mad hatter.” In Chapter 69, the old doctor in the room next to Vera's is Dr. Justice O'Leary: “He was as mad as a March hare. He said so.” And in Chapter 58, among the many things Mr. Spitzer imagines a man might become with a change of stick, he “might have been a croquet player knocking his ball through the last wicket,” a game that in Wonderland is played with flamingos for mallets.

The Trial Before the Crime

The White Queen in Through the Looking-Glass lives backward. Of the King's Messenger she says: “He's in prison now, being punished: and the trial doesn't even begin till next Wednesday: and of course the crime comes last of all.” When Alice objects that she can't remember things before they happen, the Queen replies, “It's a poor sort of memory that only works backwards.”

Mr. Spitzer lives the same way. In Chapter 48, nothing can surprise him “unless he had somehow lived through it before or had a premonition which had warned him, much like the effect preceding the cause, even like the whisper preceding the voice or the sob preceding the whisper.” In Chapter 51, the whole world runs backward for him: “And thus there was no end but that which had transpired before the end—the withering of the flower before the blooming of the flower—the falling of the snow before the falling of the snow—the wind blowing upon the towers and porches before the wind had ever blown—the song ending before the song began.”

Humpty Dumpty explains to Alice how some of Carroll's made-up words work: “You see it's like a portmanteau—there are two meanings packed up into one word.” In Chapter 48, Mr. Spitzer, who cannot tell whether he is himself or his twin, gives the word a home: “Perhaps he was the portmanteau personality carrying another soul within his soul.”

Who Is Dreaming

The deepest Carroll in the novel is the Red King. In Through the Looking-Glass, Alice finds him asleep under a tree, and Tweedledee asks her what he is dreaming about. “Why, about you!” Tweedledee exclaimed. “And if he left off dreaming about you, where do you suppose you'd be?” “Where I am now, of course,” said Alice. “Not you!” Tweedledee retorted contemptuously. “You'd be nowhere. Why, you're only a sort of thing in his dream!” “If that there King was to wake,” added Tweedledum, “you'd go out—bang!—just like a candle!”

Young's people ask the same thing about themselves. In Chapter 37, Catherine argues that the world is “nothing real except this mistaken dream which knew not its end. For if it ended, there would be no dream. But who was the dreamer? What face, what mask did he wear?” In Chapter 50, Mr. Spitzer wonders whether he lives “by that dream which might be this extension beyond his death, dream dreamed by another dreamer.” And in Chapter 56 he turns Tweedledum's threat around: “Ah, and if the dreamer was dead, how should he know—for did not the dream live?” For Carroll, if the dreamer wakes, the dreamed one goes out like a candle. For Young, even if the dreamer dies, the dream may go on living.

Carroll ends Through the Looking-Glass on the question his whole book has been asking. Alice says to her kitten, “Now, Kitty, let's consider who it was that dreamed it all,” and the kitten will not answer. The last line of the chapter turns to the reader: “Which do you think it was?” The poem that closes the book ends, “Life, what is it but a dream?” The Butterfly's Dream follows the same question back to Zhuangzi. Young's novel never gives an answer either. It keeps the question open for 1,198 pages.

The Radical Immanence Reading

In the language of Radical Immanence, the Red King's dream is Pillar 2 (Reality Is Constructed): the waking world may be someone's dream, and the dream is still where everyone has to live. Mr. Spitzer's “portmanteau personality carrying another soul within his soul” is Pillar 1 (Not One Self). And the White Queen's memory that works both ways, the flower withering before it blooms, is Pillar 8 (The Past Doesn't Go Away): in this house, the past and the future are never safely behind or ahead.
