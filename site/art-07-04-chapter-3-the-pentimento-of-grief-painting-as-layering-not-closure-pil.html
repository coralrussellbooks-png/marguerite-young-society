I. The Philosophy: The Myth of the Clean Surface

Painting culture loves closure. Resolve the composition, seal it, varnish it, frame it, done. Wellness culture sells the same story about grief: move through the stages, process it, get over it, and come back to work cheerful and productive. Radical Immanence calls this a comforting lie. Grief does not clear out. It accumulates. Each loss does not replace the last one. It lies down on top of it, layer over layer, until you are carrying a thick weight of everything you have lost. You never finish grieving. You learn to carry it.

Painters already have a word for this, and it is honest. Pentimento is the ghost of an earlier mark rising back through the surface, the arm the painter moved, the face they painted out, showing through years later as the top layers turn clear. The old decision does not leave. It waits underneath and comes back up. That is not a defect in the picture. That is how a painted surface actually holds time.

This changes the physics of the surface. Traditional work prizes the clean finish, where every false start, every buried drawing, every scraped-back passage is hidden under an even coat. But hiding your mistakes is a kind of dishonesty. The world does not resolve. People do not arrive at a settled end. Grief does not finish. So an unfinished, scraped, openly reworked surface is not a failure. The painting that lets its pentimenti show, its buried marks, its old edges, its stains worked back in, is more honest than any sealed picture that pretends nothing ever broke.

II. Deep Journal Prompts

The Archive of the Unfinished. Find an old painting you abandoned because you could not figure out how to resolve it. Sit with it for ten minutes. Write a love letter to its incompleteness. Why is this stalled, raw thing more true to your actual life than it would be if you forced a neat finish onto it just to be done?

The Core Sample. On the left side of a page, list your real losses: not only deaths, but lost years, abandoned versions of yourself, broken ties. On the right side, trace how each loss shows up in your hands. Which one pulls you toward heavy dark grounds, toward slow glazing, toward passages you leave raw and open, even when you are not trying to paint about it?

III. Studio Exercises

The Painting That Is Never Done

Start a serious, layered painting under one unbreakable rule: it will never be finished.

Phase 1. The build. Spend three sessions building something dense and careful. Layer real skill into it. Make parts of it genuinely beautiful.

Phase 2. The weekly scrape. Every week, no matter how good it looks, take a scraper, a rag and solvent, or a loaded brush and destroy or bury at least a fifth of what is there. Scrape a passage back to the ground. Paint out a part you loved. Let the removal be as honest as the making.

Phase 3. The layered return. Work new marks directly into the scraped and buried places. Let the old edges and half-erased drawing show through the new growth. Let the wound stay visible under the repair.

The studio rule. Photograph it each week so you keep the record, but never let it settle into a sealed, varnished state. It is a process, not a monument.

IV. Cross-Medium Interference

The Visible Repair as Record

Keep a small second panel beside the main work. Every week, when you scrape into the big painting, make one loud, obvious repair on the small panel in a color that does not match, so the mend shouts. Over weeks the small panel becomes a map of every cut you made, a record of the work refusing to stay whole. Keep it pinned beside the main piece as proof that the breaking and the mending are the same practice.
