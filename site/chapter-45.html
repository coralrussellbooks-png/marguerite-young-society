To All My Darlings

Chapter 45

On Chapter 45 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 11, 2026

← Chapter 44 · Home · Chapter 46 →

Summary

Reading by candlelight the letters strangers wrote to Cousin Hannah, asking about lost loves, dead brides, and vanished bridegrooms, Mr. Spitzer marvels that one who never loved should have been taken for an authority on love. From her last ravings he pieces together her secret. She had climbed the snowbound mountains with “her acolyte, her beautiful girl,” who went back over the peak to find her skirt and never returned. Hannah believed she had let go the girl’s hand and killed her love, and that guilt drove all her suffrage battles, yet at the end she cries that it was the girl who let go: “We were two lone women. There was no man.” She dies asking forgiveness, a sea shell roaring at her feet, and is buried in a grey New England churchyard. Mr. Spitzer resolves to keep her secret and wonders what image could mark her grave, perhaps a milestone reading “Twelve Miles.” He mourns how quickly the world forgets, thinking of the pigeons of Boston and Venice and the vanished passenger pigeons, and he is no longer sure whether he is Joachim or Peron.

Key passages

From the research

  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates the paradox that Cousin Hannah, devoted to the cause of suffrage and seemingly a great authority on love, had never truly experienced love in the conventional sense. He muses that “if one knew all hearts, one should know none,” and that love is essentially an unreachable image, a vision that, if grasped, leads to its death. Cousin Hannah’s life is described as a “double life, perhaps a triple life,” full of ambivalence, contradictions, and secrecy. This had left Mr. Spitzer disappointed and uncertain about her true nature and feelings.
  • The imagery of cold mountains, snow, clouds, and icy landscapes recurs throughout the chapter as metaphors for the suffrage captain’s inner life and death. She is depicted as “sleeping under waters and clouds,” her head “wrapped in veils and clouds and pillowed on stone,” symbolizing her isolation and the inscrutability of her existence. The harshness and coldness of these natural elements reflect the emotional barrenness and the “heart of darkness and mystery” that Mr. Spitzer perceives.
  • The novel frequently explores the intertwined relationship between love and death. Cousin Hannah’s dying words and hallucinations evoke a sense of loss, betrayal, and eternal searching for a “dead love,” a love that was “killed” by her own actions or fate. Passages where she cries out about losing her love, wandering through mountains and deserts, and questioning her own identity (“Was I always falling?”) emphasize the tragic dimension of her life and the universal human experience of love’s fragility and mortality.
  • Mr. Spitzer is portrayed as a melancholic witness to this woman’s life and death, struggling to reconcile the public image of the suffrage captain with the hidden truths he uncovers. He reflects on the fleeting nature of memory and fame, lamenting that she will be forgotten or remembered only for her public deeds, while her “secret heart” remains obscured. His meditations on pigeons as symbols of souls and memory, and his sorrow at man’s loss of remembrance, highlight the theme of oblivion and the limits of legacy.
  • In the final passages, Cousin Hannah’s delirious but poignant utterances reveal her inner torment and longing. She speaks of betrayal, lost love, and the breaking of a bond that “never was,” wandering through cosmic and earthly landscapes in search of forgiveness and reunion. Her invocation of celestial and natural symbols (the moon, stars, snowflakes, sea shells) creates a dreamlike atmosphere where the boundaries between reality and vision blur. Mr. Spitzer hears and tries to interpret these “last remarks,” recognizing the deep sorrow and ambiguity they contain.
  • Throughout, there are philosophical interludes where Mr. Spitzer reflects on the nature of existence, suggesting that life and death are continuous, that identity dissolves into “waters and clouds,” and that memory is fragile and transient. He considers death as a form of love and vice versa, and entertains the idea that perhaps only the smallest, most delicate perceptions like butterflies, snowflakes, or ripples of water are free to escape fate. These reflections frame Cousin Hannah’s story within a broader existential context.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “For if one knew all hearts, one should know none.” What does Mr. Spitzer mean?
  • “Love’s image was that which was never reached by love.” Why would reaching love end it?
  • “I did not let go your hand. It was you who let go my hand.” Why does Hannah’s story change at the end?

Characters

  • Hannah calls herself the one “who killed my love” and calls her girl the “traitor, breaking that bond which never was.” Which is the truth?
  • Why does Mr. Spitzer want Hannah remembered for “her public deeds” and not for “her secret heart”?
  • Mr. Spitzer whispers his name, “daring not to say whether he was Peron who had died young or Joachim who was old.” Why does he not know?

Connections

  • In Chapter 44 the girl turned back for her skirt. Here Hannah asks, “Does my skirt walk by itself… Or is my skirt my sail?” How has the skirt changed?
  • Hannah dies with a sea shell at her feet, and Mr. Spitzer listens to sea shells on lonely beaches. What do the shells carry?
  • Mr. Spitzer would bury Hannah in the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier, “for she was unknown.” How does this connect with his own fear of being forgotten?

Reflection

  • “Shall love be infinitely postponed? If infinitely postponed, then it is love.” Is love that is never fulfilled the purest?
  • Is it kinder to keep a person’s secret after death, or to tell it?
  • “Nothing frightened him so much as man’s loss of memory.” What is owed to the forgotten?

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • A reflective figure, “this old Joachim who, the light fading from his eyes, trembled at the thought of any kind of love whatever.”
  • He has witnessed the suffragette’s life and death.
  • He is a skeptic yet compassionate observer, wrestling with doubt about love, death, and memory.
  • His perspective is melancholic and filled with symbolic musings.

Cousin Hannah

  • A heroic figure involved in suffrage, labor strikes, and adventurous exploits.
  • Her private life was a mystery, marked by lost love, guilt, and spiritual wandering.
  • She died with a sea shell at her feet, symbolizing her connection to the sea and the unknown.
  • Her lost love was “her acolyte, her beautiful girl”: “We were two lone women. There was no man.”
  • “Her name was hyphenated like her life.”
  • She is described metaphorically as a “sleeping beauty,” a “dead star,” and a “great betrayer of woman,” reflecting her complex legacy.

Research: Themes

Love and Its Ambivalence

See: Love

  • Love is portrayed as an unattainable ideal.
  • Cousin Hannah’s love life is marked by loss, guilt, and a yearning for a final answer that never comes.
  • Cousin Hannah’s lamentations at her deathbed emphasize her betrayal of love (whether by herself or by fate) and her eternal search for her lost love.

Memory, Identity, and the Passing of Time

See: Memory and the Past · Time · Identity and the Self

  • Mr. Spitzer struggles with memory’s frailty, likening it to fleeting natural phenomena.
  • The chapter meditates on the dissolution of identity.
  • Cousin Hannah’s secret life and true self remain ambiguous, veiled in clouds and mystery, reflecting the difficulty of truly knowing another person, especially after death.

Death and Transition

See: Death and Mortality

  • The narrative frequently returns to images of death as a form of journey or transformation.
  • Death is intertwined with love; “Death is love, and love is death.”
  • Cousin Hannah’s death is surrounded by symbolic natural elements which serve as metaphors for her passing and the mystery surrounding it.

Public Versus Private Life

See: Society, Class, and Race

  • Cousin Hannah was a public hero, a suffragette with noble deeds and a fiery spirit, yet her private life was marked by confusion, betrayal, and unfulfilled love.
  • Mr. Spitzer reflects on how public memory tends to forget the complexity of individuals, often reducing them to symbols or monuments.
  • Cousin Hannah had a “double life,” perhaps even a “triple life,” that no one truly understood.

Mysticism and Symbolism

See: Faith, God, and the Supernatural

  • The chapter is rich with symbolic imagery: mountains, clouds, snow, stars, fireflies, sea shells, the moon, and butterflies.
  • These symbols often blur the boundary between reality and hallucination, reflecting Mr. Spitzer’s and Cousin Hannah’s fractured consciousness.
  • The narrative suggests the possibility of existence beyond physical death, through memory, spirit, or cosmic forces.

  • “this great father of suffrage”: Young named her own sources for Cousin Hannah (see the Chapter 30 resources and the Chapter 31 resources), and Susan B. Anthony and her sister, Hannah, offer a historical comparison. Mr. Spitzer described Cousin Hannah as “this great father of suffrage.” Susan B. Anthony’s father was the first one in the family to become interested in the women’s suffrage movement. She was interested in social reform. She was a teacher and was paid less than her male counterparts (then as now). Anthony said, “I wasn’t ready to vote, didn’t want to vote, but I did want equal pay for equal work.” The family started out as Quakers and Anthony claimed to be a Quaker but the father moved away from the order to join the Unitarian Church. They were abolitionists and temperance advocates and had the likes of Frederick Douglass speak at their house. She formed a partnership with Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucy Stone. Stanton kept a room for her at her house where she also spent most of her life working with her. Anthony and Stanton went on to write the History of Woman Suffrage. Anthony said this kind of work, “makes me feel growly all the time... No warhorse ever panted for the rush of battle more than I for outside work. I love to make history but hate to write it.” Anthony did not believe in her writing skills which mostly went to Stanton. Most of her quotes come from speeches and lectures. Anthony did not live to see the ratification of the 19th Amendment or equal rights. “There have been others also just as true and devoted to the cause—I wish I could name every one—but with such women consecrating their lives, failure is impossible!” “Now, after 40 years of agitation, the idea is beginning to prevail that women were created for themselves, for their own happiness, and for the welfare of the world.” Back to her religion. It is a mixed bag in that they moved to being Unitarian although proud of her Quaker roots. She was involved with the Friends of Human Progress that included “Christians, Jews, Mahammedans, and Pagans.” She tried to start her own Free church where all were welcome. She later, according to Stanton, became agnostic. “Work and worship are one with me. I can not imagine a God of the universe made happy by my getting down on my knees and calling him ‘great.’” When Anthony’s sister Hannah was on her deathbed, she asked Susan to talk about the great beyond, but Anthony later wrote, “I could not dash her faith with my doubts, nor could I pretend a faith I had not; so I was silent in the dread presence of death.” Hannah was known for her domestic nature, but also for her full sympathy with the reforms championed by her sister. As a teen, Anthony went to parties, and she had offers of marriage when she was older, but there is no record of her ever having a serious romance. Anthony loved children, however, and helped raise the children in the Stanton household. As a young worker in the women’s rights movement, Anthony expressed frustration when some of her co-workers began to marry and have children, sharply curtailing their ability to work for the understaffed movement (Wikipedia). When Lucy Stone abandoned her pledge to stay single, Anthony’s scolding remarks caused a temporary rupture in their friendship. It was a bit more than a temporary rupture. They both formed separate suffrage movements. Anthony wanted a federal constitutional amendment. Stone wanted a more gradual state-by-state approach. Anthony opposed the 15th Amendment because it did not grant everyone the right to vote. Stone supported it as a stepping stone. It was 20 years before their two organizations worked together again. Stone worked out with her husband a separate but equal marriage where Stone retained rights to her property and finances and her personal liberty. Lucy Stone wore bloomers. She also kept her name. They publicly declared their commitment to equality and rejected the idea of a wife’s legal subordination. Journalists repeatedly asked Anthony to explain why she never married. She answered one by saying, “It always happened that the men I wanted were those I could not get, and those who wanted me I wouldn’t have.” To another, she answered, “I never found the man who was necessary to my happiness. I was very well as I was.” To a third she said, “I never felt I could give up my life of freedom to become a man’s housekeeper. When I was young, if a girl married poor, she became a housekeeper and a drudge. If she married wealth she became a pet and a doll. Just think, had I married at twenty, I would have been a drudge or a doll for fifty-nine years. Think of it!” In a speech in 1877, Anthony predicted “an epoch of single women. If women will not accept marriage with subjugation, nor men proffer it without, there is, there can be, no alternative. The woman who will not be ruled must live without marriage.” See Women’s Suffrage and American Religious Movements.

  • “Ah, traitor moon!”: “O, swear not by the moon, th’ inconstant moon, / That monthly changes in her circled orb” (Romeo and Juliet 2.2). See Shakespeare and Moon and Stars.
  • “Who is that dwarf, a turret on his head”: May refer to Nietzsche where the dwarf represents self-doubt and the limitations of conventional thought. Zarathustra’s journey involves overcoming the dwarf, symbolizing the overcoming of self-doubt and the embrace of his philosophy, including the concept of eternal recurrence. Young gives him the rose of consciousness. According to Thomas Reid sensory information, particularly in relation to movement, contributes to our subjective experience of the world. This framework suggests that consciousness might be closely tied to our ability to interact with and act upon the world. On sensation and perception, Thomas Reid saw sensation as a primitive feeling or awareness, while perception was the process of interpreting these feelings and associating them with external objects. The ability to distinguish between sensation and perception is linked to consciousness (Thomas Reid, Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man, 1785). The turret is typically a small tower, often ornamental, that juts out from a building. It is typically found on castles, large houses, etc. See Nietzsche.
  • “no trumpeter swan could accompany her”: The trumpeter swan was nearly gone but not extinct. In 1932 only 69 were counted in the United States outside Alaska, and the Red Rock Lakes refuge was set aside for them in 1935. See Birds.
  • “Shall love be infinitely postponed?”: may refer to Hegel. To deny something is to determine it, and to determine something is to negate it. Spinoza’s principle is “every determination is negation” (Spinoza, letter 50 to Jarig Jelles, 1674). Hegel takes this one step further by suggesting that negation itself is a process of creation, and is even the essence of positive being. By denying a certain trait or characteristic, one is implicitly acknowledging the possibility of its opposite or the existence of a different reality. For Hegel, this power of the negative, or the act of negation, is what drives progress and development. Denying a particular concept allows for the possibility of a new one to emerge, incorporating both the original concept and its negation. This is often explained through the concept of the negation of the negation, where a denial of a denial leads to a new synthesis that transcends both the original thesis and the antithesis. From an existentialist perspective denying a predefined purpose or essence allows for the possibility of individuals to create their own meaning and existence. See Kant and Hegel, Spinoza, and Existentialism.
    • Keats’s lover on the urn can never kiss, and so: “She cannot fade, though thou hast not thy bliss, / For ever wilt thou love, and she be fair!” (John Keats, “Ode on a Grecian Urn,” 1819). See Romantic Poets.
  • “Forgive the cloud which knows not what it whispers”: “Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do” (Luke 23:34). See The Bible.
  • “I aspired to greater heights than most, so I fell a greater distance”: Here Cousin Hannah grapples with their desires, failures, and the often painful gap between expectation and outcome, highlighting the complexity of human experience and the poignant cost of reaching beyond oneself.
    • Icarus flew too near the sun and fell into the sea (Ovid, Metamorphoses 8), and “How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!” (Isaiah 14:12). See Greek Mythology and The Bible.
  • “Oh, nightmare, were you my only love?”: The mare of nightmare is a goblin that sits on the sleeper’s chest, as in Henry Fuseli’s painting The Nightmare (1781).
  • “Forgive, too, the self-contradiction of life and death, for both were contained within us.”: “Do I contradict myself? / Very well then I contradict myself, / (I am large, I contain multitudes.)” (Walt Whitman, “Song of Myself,” 51). See American Writers.
  • “Forgive the secret none can tell”: It is considered a classic riddle and the common answer is death. In a metaphorical sense it may refer to a profound or life-altering experience that can’t be adequately described in words. Or a secret that if revealed would lead to someone’s demise or ruin.
  • “His music was made almost entirely of lost chords.”: See Adelaide Procter’s “The Lost Chord” in the Chapter 25 resources. See Music and Composers.
  • “all the chessmen were suddenly swept over-board”: See the Rubáiyát’s “Chequer-board of Nights and Days / Where Destiny with Men for Pieces plays” in the Chapter 13 resources.
  • “We were two lone women. There was no man.”: This is Hannah’s confession, and the secret Mr. Spitzer will keep. See the bridal trousseaus Young described to Studs Terkel under “a secret woman, a bride” in the Chapter 42 resources. See Women, Gender, and Marriage and Marguerite Young on Her Craft.
  • “Death is love, and love is death”: It is a profound paradox. Life, with its drive to continue, is defined by its end, and everything mortal is part of the natural cycle of birth, death, and decay. The loss of a loved one can deepen the capacity for love and understanding. Love asks for a death of the self, of the ego, as one puts the needs of another first, and sacrificial love puts itself in harm’s way. After death, love endures through memory and legacy. Some religions and philosophies offer an afterlife, reincarnation, or love as a force that conquers death, and some works suggest that the intensity of love can be destructive, a “death” of the self. Isolde’s dying song in Wagner’s Tristan und Isolde (1865) is known as the Liebestod, love-death.
    • “For love is strong as death” (Song of Solomon 8:6). See the Liebestod of Tristan und Isolde in the Chapter 9 resources. See The Bible.
  • “I have been a wanderer ever since, wandering over the face of the earth”: Cain, who killed his brother, is sentenced: “a fugitive and a vagabond shalt thou be in the earth” (Genesis 4:12). Hannah believed she had killed her love. See Genesis.
  • “ancient Samarkand”: Samarkand is currently in Uzbekistan and is one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in Central Asia. It is on the Silk Road, an ancient trade route from China to the Mediterranean.
  • “he who loves shall lose himself in love as in death”: “For whosoever will save his life shall lose it: and whosoever will lose his life for my sake shall find it” (Matthew 16:25; see also John 12:25). The loss of self in love is not presented as purely tragic but as an essential part of the human condition and the search for connection beyond isolation. See The Bible.
  • “Perhaps body and soul were one.”: Spinoza held that mind and body are one and the same thing, conceived now under the attribute of thought, now under the attribute of extension (Ethics 2, proposition 21, scholium). See Spinoza.
  • “sailing with the neap tide”: See the neap tide in the Chapter 43 resources.

Vocabulary

Abyss
A bottomless pit or chasm.
Acolyte
An attendant or follower; in church, one who assists the priest.
Alarum
An old word for alarm, a call to arms.
Ambivalence
Mixed or contradictory feelings.
Ambivalently
With mixed or contradictory feelings or meanings.
Amorphous
Without a clear shape or form.
Aplomb
Self-assurance, poise.
Ascertain
To find out for certain.
Astral
Having to do with the stars.
Athwart
Across, from side to side; also, against.
Bedouin
A nomadic desert Arab.
Bellwether
The lead sheep of a flock, which wears a bell.
Billows
Great waves.
Blackamoor
An old term for a Black African or any dark-skinned person, common in European art and in decorative figures of Black servants. It is now considered offensive.
Caisson
A two-wheeled cart for ammunition, used at military funerals to carry the coffin.
Cannonade
A long bombardment by cannon.
Casket
A small, often ornate box for jewels or valuables. In American usage, also a coffin, so Young’s “casket containing no jewel” carries both meanings.
Cavalier
A horseman or gallant gentleman; also offhand and disdainful.
Chagrin
Distress or annoyance at having failed or been humiliated.
Chaplet
A wreath or string of beads worn on the head.
Cherubim
Winged angelic beings. In art they are often shown as chubby winged children. The singular is cherub.
Chivalry
The code of the medieval knight; courtesy and protection shown by a man toward a woman.
Citadel
A fortress, usually on high ground, that protects a city.
Conch
A large sea snail with a spiraled shell that can be blown like a horn. Held to the ear, it seems to hold the sound of the sea.
Cosmography
A description or map of the whole universe, both heavens and earth.
Crusader
A soldier in the Crusades, the Christian military campaigns to take the Holy Land, fought between 1096 and 1291.
Crystal-gazer
A fortune-teller who looks into a crystal ball to see the future.
Crystal-hunter
A prospector in the Alps who climbs into rock clefts to find quartz crystals.
Defile
A narrow pass between mountains.
Diffused
Spread out widely.
Dirge
A slow song of mourning.
Drove
A herd or flock driven or moving together.
Dubious
Doubtful.
Effigy
A sculpture or model of a person, especially one lying on a tomb.
Empearled
Adorned with pearls, or having a pearly sheen.
Enigma
A mystery or puzzle; a person or thing that is hard to understand.
Epithalamium
A song or poem celebrating a marriage.
Equivocation
The use of vague or double-edged words to avoid committing oneself.
Escutcheon
The shield bearing a coat of arms.
Eunuch
A man who has been castrated. Eunuchs were employed for centuries to guard harems and to serve as officials in royal courts in the Middle East, China, and Byzantium. See The eunuch (Ch 35).
Figurehead
A carved figure, often a woman, mounted on the bow of a sailing ship. See On the figurehead and intuition (Ch 40).
Firmament
The sky or the heavens, imagined as a solid dome overhead, as in Genesis 1.
Fitful
Starting and stopping; irregular.
Flounced
Trimmed with flounces, wide strips of gathered or pleated fabric sewn to a skirt or dress.
Fluted
Marked with long rounded grooves, like a column.
Gargoyle
A carved grotesque figure on a building, often serving as a waterspout.
Glacial
Icy; like a glacier.
Gondolier
The boatman who rows a Venetian gondola, standing at the stern with a single oar.
Hallucination
Something seen or heard that is not there.
Halo
A ring of light around the sun or moon, caused by ice crystals high in thin clouds. In art, a ring of light around the head of a saint.
Harebell
A wildflower with nodding blue bell-shaped flowers.
Headland
A high point of land jutting into the sea.
Helm
The wheel or tiller that steers a ship.
Helmsman
The person who steers a ship.
Heterogeneous
Made of unlike parts; mixed.
Ice floe
A sheet of floating ice.
Inarticulate
Unable to express oneself clearly.
Indeterminate
Not clearly fixed or defined.
Infinitesimal
Extremely small.
Innumerable
Too many to count.
Inscrutable
Impossible to understand or interpret.
Inscrutably
In a way that cannot be understood.
Intaglio
A design cut into the surface of a gem or stone.
Lute
A plucked string instrument with a rounded back shaped like half a pear, popular in Europe from the Middle Ages through the 1700s.
Mandarin
A high-ranking official of imperial China.
Medusa
A jellyfish in its free-swimming, bell-shaped stage, named for the Gorgon of Greek myth whose hair was snakes.
Minaret
A tall, slender tower of a mosque, from which the call to prayer is given.
Mirage
An optical illusion, common in deserts, in which hot air bends light so that distant water or cities seem to appear.
Mother-of-pearl
The shining, rainbow-colored lining of some shells, used for inlay.
Myriad
A countless number.
Neap tide
The tide with the smallest difference between high and low water, twice a month at the quarter moons.
Nether
Lower; under.
Oblivion
The state of being forgotten, or of being unaware of everything.
Opalescent
Shimmering with changing colors, like an opal.
Palmer
A medieval pilgrim who brought back a palm frond from the Holy Land.
Persian
A person of Persia, now Iran.
Philatelist
A stamp collector.
Pinnacle
The highest point; a tall, pointed mountain peak or the slender spire on a building.
Precarious
Unstable and likely to fall or fail.
Promontory
A high point of land jutting out into the sea.
Ptarmigan
A grouse of cold mountains and the far north that turns white in winter.
Punctilio
A fine point of etiquette; exactness in form.
Refraction
The bending of light as it passes from one medium to another.
Retrospect
A looking back on the past.
Roseate
Rose-colored. Also hopeful or overly optimistic.
Sceptre
A staff carried by a ruler as a sign of power. Also spelled scepter.
Scimitar
A curved Eastern sword.
Sentinel
A guard or watchman.
Sepulchre
A tomb cut in rock or built of stone.
Seraglio
The women’s quarters of a Muslim palace; a harem.
Shah
The title of the king of Persia, now Iran.
Sheik
An Arab chief or elder.
Shot silk
Silk woven with threads of two colors, so it changes color in the light.
Sonorous
Deep and full in sound.
Spar
A pole used as a ship’s mast, boom, or yard. Spar is also the name for certain shiny, easily split crystalline minerals, such as Iceland spar.
Spectre
A ghost.
Speculative
Thoughtful and wondering; based on guesswork.
Spindrift
Spray blown from the tops of waves.

Vortex
A whirlpool or whirlwind.
Wayfarer
A traveler, especially on foot.
Winding sheet
The cloth a corpse is wrapped in for burial.
Windjammer
A large square-rigged sailing ship.
