Summary

Reading Hannah's faded letters by candlelight, Mr. Spitzer vows to keep her great secret until long after her death. He sees that her public and private lives cannot be fitted into one portrait. He recalls her asking whether she was always falling, and her sense that she aspired higher and so fell farther. He considers what her grave should say, and how public memory turns the greatest lives into monuments and loses the secret heart.

Key passages

From the research

  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates the paradox that Cousin Hannah, devoted to the cause of suffrage and seemingly a great authority on love, had never truly experienced love in the conventional sense. He muses that "if one knew all hearts, one should know none," and that love is essentially an unreachable image, a vision that, if grasped, leads to its death. Cousin Hannah’s life is described as a "double life, perhaps a triple life," full of ambivalence, contradictions, and secrecy. This had left Mr. Spitzer disappointed and uncertain about her true nature and feelings.
  • The imagery of cold mountains, snow, clouds, and icy landscapes recurs throughout the chapter as metaphors for the suffrage captain’s inner life and death. She is depicted as "sleeping under waters and clouds," her head "wrapped in veils and clouds and pillowed on stone," symbolizing her isolation and the inscrutability of her existence. The harshness and coldness of these natural elements reflect the emotional barrenness and the "heart of darkness and mystery" that Mr. Spitzer perceives.
  • The novel frequently explores the intertwined relationship between love and death. Cousin Hannah’s dying words and hallucinations evoke a sense of loss, betrayal, and eternal searching for a “dead love,” a love that was "killed" by her own actions or fate. Passages where she cries out about losing her love, wandering through mountains and deserts, and questioning her own identity ("Was I always falling?") emphasize the tragic dimension of her life and the universal human experience of love’s fragility and mortality.
  • Mr. Spitzer is portrayed as a melancholic witness to this woman’s life and death, struggling to reconcile the public image of the suffrage captain with the hidden truths he uncovers. He reflects on the fleeting nature of memory and fame, lamenting that she will be forgotten or remembered only for her public deeds, while her "secret heart" remains obscured. His meditations on pigeons as symbols of souls and memory, and his sorrow at man’s loss of remembrance, highlight the theme of oblivion and the limits of legacy.
  • In the final passages, Cousin Hannah’s delirious but poignant utterances reveal her inner torment and longing. She speaks of betrayal, lost love, and the breaking of a bond that "never was," wandering through cosmic and earthly landscapes in search of forgiveness and reunion. Her invocation of celestial and natural symbols (the moon, stars, snowflakes, sea shells) creates a dreamlike atmosphere where the boundaries between reality and vision blur. Mr. Spitzer hears and tries to interpret these "last remarks," recognizing the deep sorrow and ambiguity they contain.
  • Throughout, there are philosophical interludes where Mr. Spitzer reflects on the nature of existence, suggesting that life and death are continuous, that identity dissolves into "waters and clouds," and that memory is fragile and transient. He considers death as a form of love and vice versa, and entertains the idea that perhaps only the smallest, most delicate perceptions like butterflies, snowflakes, or ripples of water are free to escape fate. These reflections frame Cousin Hannahs’s story within a broader existential context.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • A reflective figure.
  • He has witnessed the suffragette's life and death.
  • He is a skeptic yet compassionate observer, wrestling with doubt about love, death, and memory.
  • His perspective is melancholic and filled with symbolic musings.

Cousin Hannah

  • A heroic figure involved in suffrage, labor strikes, and adventurous exploits.
  • Her private life was a mystery, marked by lost love, guilt, and spiritual wandering.
  • She died with a sea shell at her feet, symbolizing her connection to the sea and the unknown.
  • She is described metaphorically as a "sleeping beauty," a "dead star," and a "great betrayer of woman," reflecting her complex legacy.

Research: Themes

Love and Its Ambivalence

See: Love

  • Love is portrayed as an unattainable ideal.
  • Cousin Hannah’s love life is marked by loss, guilt, and a yearning for a final answer that never comes.
  • Cousin Hannah’s lamentations at her deathbed emphasize her betrayal of love (whether by herself or by fate) and her eternal search for her lost love.

Memory, Identity, and the Passing of Time

See: Memory and the Past · Time · Identity and the Self

  • Mr. Spitzer struggles with memory’s frailty, likening it to fleeting natural phenomena.
  • The chapter meditates on the dissolution of identity.
  • Cousin Hannah’s secret life and true self remain ambiguous, veiled in clouds and mystery, reflecting the difficulty of truly knowing another person, especially after death.

Death and Transition

See: Death and Mortality

  • The narrative frequently returns to images of death as a form of journey or transformation.
  • Death is intertwined with love; “Death is love, and love is death.”
  • Cousin Hannah’s death is surrounded by symbolic natural elements which serve as metaphors for her passing and the mystery surrounding it.

Public vs. Private Life

See: Society, Class, and Race

  • Cousin Hannah was a public hero, a suffragette with noble deeds and a fiery spirit, yet her private life was marked by confusion, betrayal, and unfulfilled love.
  • Mr. Spitzer reflects on how public memory tends to forget the complexity of individuals, often reducing them to symbols or monuments.
  • Cousin Hannah had a "double life," perhaps even a "triple life," that no one truly understood.

Mysticism and Symbolism

See: Faith, God, and the Supernatural

  • The chapter is rich with symbolic imagery: mountains, clouds, snow, stars, fireflies, sea shells, the moon, and butterflies.
  • These symbols often blur the boundary between reality and hallucination, reflecting Mr. Spitzer’s and Cousin Hannah’s fractured consciousness.
  • The narrative suggests the possibility of existence beyond physical death, through memory, spirit, or cosmic forces.

Research: Resources

  • Young may have taken inspiration for the character Cousin Hannah from Susan B. Anthony and her sister, Hannah. Mr. Spitzer described Cousin Hannah as “this great father of suffrage”. Susan B. Anthony’s father was the first one in the family to become interested in the women’s suffrage movement. She was interested in social reform. She was a teacher and was paid less than her male counterparts (then as now). Anthony said, “I wasn’t ready to vote, didn’t want to vote, but I did want equal pay for equal work.” The family started out as Quakers and Anthony claimed to be a Quaker but the father moved away from the order to join the Unitarian Church. They were abolitionists and temperance advocates and had the likes of Frederick Douglass speak at their house. She formed a partnership with Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucy Stone. Stanton kept a room for her at her house where she also spent most of her life working with her. Anthony and Stanton went on to write the History of Woman Suffrage. Anthony said this kind of work, "makes me feel growly all the time... No warhorse ever panted for the rush of battle more than I for outside work. I love to make history but hate to write it." Anthony did not believe in her writing skills which mostly went to Stanton. Most of her quotes come from speeches and lectures. Anthony did not live to see the ratification of the 19th Amendment or equal rights. "There have been others also just as true and devoted to the cause—I wish I could name every one—but with such women consecrating their lives, failure is impossible!" "Now, after 40 years of agitation, the idea is beginning to prevail that women were created for themselves, for their own happiness, and for the welfare of the world." Back to her religion. It is a mixed bag in that they moved to being Unitarian although proud of her Quaker roots. She was involved with the Friends of Human Progress that included “Christians, Jews, Mahammedans, and Pagans.” She tried to start her own Free church where all were welcome. She later, according to Stanton, became agnostic. "Work and worship are one with me. I can not imagine a God of the universe made happy by my getting down on my knees and calling him 'great.'" When Anthony's sister Hannah was on her deathbed, she asked Susan to talk about the great beyond, but Anthony later wrote, "I could not dash her faith with my doubts, nor could I pretend a faith I had not; so I was silent in the dread presence of death." Hannah was known for her domestic nature, but also for her full sympathy with the reforms championed by her sister. As a teen, Anthony went to parties, and she had offers of marriage when she was older, but there is no record of her ever having a serious romance. Anthony loved children, however, and helped raise the children in the Stanton household. As a young worker in the women's rights movement, Anthony expressed frustration when some of her co-workers began to marry and have children, sharply curtailing their ability to work for the understaffed movement. When Lucy Stone abandoned her pledge to stay single, Anthony's scolding remarks caused a temporary rupture in their friendship. It was a bit more than a temporary rupture. They both formed separate suffrage movements. Anthony wanted a federal constitutional amendment. Stone wanted a more gradual state-by-state approach. Anthony opposed the 15th Amendment because it did not grant everyone the right to vote. Stone supported it as a stepping stone. It was 20 years before their two organizations worked together again. Stone worked out with her husband a separate but equal marriage where Stone retained rights to her property and finances and her personal liberty. Lucy Stone wore bloomers. She also kept her name. They publicly declared their commitment to equality and rejected the idea of a wife’s legal subordination. Journalists repeatedly asked Anthony to explain why she never married. She answered one by saying, "It always happened that the men I wanted were those I could not get, and those who wanted me I wouldn't have.” To another, she answered, "I never found the man who was necessary to my happiness. I was very well as I was.” To a third she said, "I never felt I could give up my life of freedom to become a man's housekeeper. When I was young, if a girl married poor, she became a housekeeper and a drudge. If she married wealth she became a pet and a doll. Just think, had I married at twenty, I would have been a drudge or a doll for fifty-nine years. Think of it!" In a speech in 1877, Anthony predicted "an epoch of single women. If women will not accept marriage with subjugation, nor men proffer it without, there is, there can be, no alternative. The woman who will not be ruled must live without marriage."
  • Twelve Miles - is what Mr. Spitzer thinks about putting on Cousin Hannah’s gravestone. There are some famous 12 Mile Markers in the US. There are also some in unincorporated communities in Indiana. It can also refer to a territorial waters limit. Twelve Miles can be understood as a symbolic measure of travel through life and time, marking a stage or a point that carries personal or spiritual significance in the midst of complexity and ambiguity. It is less a literal distance and more an emblematic representation of passage, progress, or endurance on the journey of life and memory in the novel's broader themes of time, existence, and spiritual quest.
  • “mountaineers lost long ago stared out from behind great crystal walls.” There are many dead bodies weighing hundreds of pounds that have not been carried off of Mount Everest and other mountains, plus there is an enormous amount of trash that is left at the campsites. The phrase conveys the persistence of memory and the haunting presence of the past, while emphasizing the fragile, elusive nature of existence in the novel’s dreamlike world.
  • “pigeon poler” - It may refer to poker (Peron Spitzer) where it is a player who is perceived as a weak or inexperienced player, easily manipulated or exploited. Some poker players might intentionally act like a “pigeon” to deceive other players. It symbolizes a guardian or guide navigating others, in this case pigeons, through uncertain or liminal spaces, much like Mr. Spitzer’s own role as a watcher and intermediary between worlds.
  • “he was become his brother.” may be a full confession by Mr. Spitzer that he is not Joachim but Peron/Jock. It encapsulates the novel’s exploration of identity, loss, and the haunting persistence of the past within the present self.
  • “She knew her Carthage.” may refer to Dido, the legendary founder and queen of Carthage. Dido, whose name also appears as Elissa, is a figure from Phoenician and Roman history and mythology. She is known for founding the city of Carthage and for her tragic love story with the Trojan hero Aeneas. In Virgil’s Aeneid, Dido is depicted as a wise and ambitious leader who built Carthage into a prosperous city, according to some accounts. It means she was no stranger to hardship, defeat, and the costs of struggle, embodying the resilience and tragic grandeur associated with such historical and symbolic ruin.
  • “Who is that dwarf, a turret on his head, a rose at his collar? It is the rose of consciousness.” May refer to Nietzsche where the dwarf represents self-doubt and the limitations of conventional thought. Zarathustra’s journey involves overcoming the dwarf, symbolizing the overcoming of self-doubt and the embrace of his philosophy, including the concept of eternal recurrence. Young gives him the rose of consciousness. According to Thomas Reid sensory information, particularly in relation to movement, contributes to our subjective experience of the world. This framework suggests that consciousness might be closely tied to our ability to interact with and act upon the world. Sensation vs Perception - Thomas Reid (which I’ve mentioned before) saw sensation as a primitive feeling or awareness, while perception was the process of interpreting these feelings and associating them with external objects. The ability to distinguish between sensation and perception is linked to consciousness. The turret is typically a small tower, often ornamental, that juts out from a building. It is typically found on castles, large houses, etc. The passage poetically captures consciousness as a guarded, precious, and living presence within the self, one that both defines identity and is shaped by the ongoing flux of life and perception.
  • “Shall love be infinitely postponed? If infinitely postponed, then it is love.” may refer to Hegel. To deny something is to determine it, and to determine something is to negate it. Spinoza’s principle is “every determination is negation”. Hegel takes this one step further by suggesting that negation itself is a process of creation, and is even the essence of positive being. By denying a certain trait or characteristic, one is implicitly acknowledging the possibility of its opposite or the existence of a different reality. For Hegel, this power of the negative, or the act of negation, is what drives progress and development. Denying a particular concept allows for the possibility of a new one to emerge, incorporating both the original concept and its negation. This is often explained through the concept of the negation of the negation, where a denial of a denial leads to a new synthesis that transcends both the original thesis and the antithesis. From an existentialist perspective denying a predefined purpose or essence allows for the possibility of individuals to create their own meaning and existence. The line speaks to love as an infinite emotional state, one that transcends immediate gratification and endures through time, absence, and even death, resonating with the novel's larger themes of longing, loss, and the elusive nature of human connection.
  • “Forgive the cloud which knows not what it whispers” Luke 23:34 This line fits with the novel’s broader motifs of illusion, memory, loss, and the elusive nature of reality, where characters grapple with fragmented perceptions of existence and the blending of dream and waking life.
  • “I aspired to greater heights than most, so I fell a greater distance.” - This line occurs in a chapter where the narrator, Vera, and other characters grapple with their desires, failures, and the often painful gap between expectation and outcome, highlighting the complexity of human experience and the poignant cost of reaching beyond oneself.
  • “Forgive the secret none can tell. None can tell and live.” It is considered a classic riddle and the common answer is death. In a metaphorical sense it may refer to a profound or life-altering experience that can’t be adequately described in words. Or a secret that if revealed would lead to someone’s demise or ruin. The phrase captures the tension between the desire to disclose deep, painful truths and the necessity of silence for survival. It also suggests that some aspects of human experience, perhaps related to loss, guilt, or forbidden knowledge are too dangerous or profound to be openly shared. This interpretation aligns with the novel’s exploration of memory, secrecy, and the weight of unspoken suffering.

Anthony. In that interview she also said Esther is the mother of us all and Mr. Spitzer is the father of us all.
  • “Death is love, and love is death…” It is a profound paradox since life, with its inherent drive for continuation, is ultimately defined by its end, death. Every living thing eventually dies making it the ultimate and unavoidable end. We experience life as a continuous present, however, everything is mortal. part of a natural cycle of birth, death, and decay. Even experience with death, the loss of a loved one, can have a profound effect on a person’s life. It can deepen one’s capacity for love and understanding. The death of self can involve the death of the ego or self-centeredness as one prioritizes the needs and well-being of another. Sacrificial love where one willingly puts themselves in harms way to protect another. The death of the old self allows for the emergence of a new, more compassionate and empathetic self. Even after death, love can endure through memories, experiences and the impact of a loved one to continue to live on. Love can be a source of strength and comfort. The love between two people can leave a lasting legacy. Contemplating death can highlight the preciousness of life and the importance of love. Experiences of loss can foster deeper empathy and appreciation for loved ones. Death can serve as a reminder to live fully, cherish relationships, and express love while we have the chance. The certainty of death raises questions about the meaning of life. Highlighting the complex relationship between individual life and the larger, overarching biological cycle. Some religions and philosophies offer concepts of an afterlife, reincarnation, or the idea that life continues through the legacy of one’s actions. Or love is seen as a force that can conquer death and offer a path through it. Some say that true love is immortal. Some works suggest that the intensity of love can be destructive, leading to a kind of “death” of the self or relationship. Others suggest that embracing the reality of death can lead to a more meaningful and authentic life. It encapsulates the novel's exploration of the cyclical, intertwined nature of these fundamental human experiences, suggesting that love and death are inseparable and that understanding one requires embracing the other. This paradox also underlines the novel's broader themes of illusion, memory, and the elusive nature of reality and identity. The phrase means that love and death are deeply connected realities; love inevitably involves loss and mortality, and death can be viewed as an extension or transformation of love, a theme that permeates the novel’s existential and metaphysical reflections.
  • “he who loves shall lose himself in love as in death” - Mathew 16:25 and John 12:25 The phrase aligns with the novel’s motifs of merging opposites (life and death, self and other, reality and illusion) and reflects the emotional and metaphysical struggles of the characters. The loss of self in love is not presented as purely tragic but as an essential part of the human condition and the search for connection beyond isolation.
