On Artificial Insemination

Young’s silver spoon is not a literal description of any medical procedure, but it sits in the historical context of early artificial insemination, which To All My Darlings documents. The first recorded case of artificial insemination was John Hunter in 1790. The first reported case using donor sperm occurred in 1884, when William Pancoast took sperm from a handsome medical student and inseminated an anesthetized woman without her knowledge or consent: a case not published until twenty-five years later. The first fertility clinic offering donor insemination in Britain was founded in the 1930s by Mary Barton, who had witnessed in colonial India how women were punished or killed for childlessness while the male partner’s infertility went unaddressed. Barton and her husband Bertold Wiesner inseminated an estimated 1500 women, with Wiesner himself fathering perhaps 600 children through the clinic, all in secrecy because the procedure was publicly condemned by both the Pope and the Archbishop of Canterbury. In vitro fertilization did not succeed in humans until 1977. Young is writing at the exact moment when the concept of medically assisted conception was real but controversial and largely secret. The silver spoon is her folkloric rendering of something that was, in the world she was writing in, happening in clinics behind closed doors, without the knowledge of the women involved, condemned by every major religious authority.
