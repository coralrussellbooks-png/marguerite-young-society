Taoism and Zhuangzi

Part of To All My Darlings, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

In the research: Chapter 2, Chapter 18, Chapter 28, Chapter 40, Chapter 49, Chapter 52

Definition from research

Chapter 2

  • “My mother pretended that the real was the dream, that the dream was the real”: The Chinese philosopher Zhuangzi dreamed he was a butterfly and, on waking, could not tell whether he was Zhuangzi who had dreamed of being a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming he was Zhuangzi. In Pedro Calderón de la Barca’s play Life Is a Dream (1635), Prince Segismundo concludes that “all life is a dream, and dreams are dreams.”

Chapter 18

  • Young may also be drawing from the Chinese philosophy of Yin and Yang which is a concept of dualism, describing how obviously opposite or contrary forces may actually be complementary, interconnected, and interdependent in the natural world, and how they may give rise to each other as they interrelate to one another.
  • “Heaven’s nets are woven very wide”: The Tao Te Ching, chapter 73, in one common translation: “Heaven’s net is vast; though its meshes are wide, nothing slips through.” Miss MacIntosh finishes the thought, “but none shall escape them.” Indra’s Net appears in the Atharva Veda, and Huayan Buddhism made it the image of a world where each jewel in an endless net reflects every other. It means that the net of divine grace or judgment is extensive and all-encompassing, offering no easy escape from one’s spiritual destiny. It underscores themes of universality, inevitability, and the encompassing nature of divine authority in the novel.

Chapter 28

  • “the butterfly perching upon the flower had seemed as identical to the flower as twin to twin”: Zhuangzi (c. 300 BC) dreamed he was a butterfly, and woke not knowing “whether he was Zhuangzi who had dreamed he was a butterfly, or a butterfly dreaming he was Zhuangzi.”

Chapter 40

  • In philosophy there is the question of dreams by Zhuangzi, Plato, and Aristotle. Descartes asked and tried to answer the following question “How can I know that I am not now dreaming?” which seems to be the same question Catherine is preoccupied with. Descartes' answer was that we cannot trust our senses in waking life without invoking a benevolent God who would not deceive us. Catherine takes this one step further at the end of the last chapter and asks, “What if God is dreaming?” See the Philosophy of Dreaming. In psychology the question “Is Life a Dream?” is answered with the coherence of the waking world does give evidence that it is not just a figment of our imagination. However, the objects that we perceive as permanent are in reality through the science of quantum physics mostly made up of empty space. In a sense people do live in a dream world that is created by the mind. When people are awake, their minds conform their dreaming to a reliable set of patterns, which they assume to be determined by a reality that exists independently of their experience of it, though they have no way of knowing that reality except through the complex ways in which it affects their dream. There is also reality that is experienced and affected by psychedelic drugs and other altered states of consciousness. The chapter weaves together the philosophical inquiries of Zhuangzi, Plato, Aristotle, and Descartes on dreams to underscore the complexity and mystery of human consciousness and the elusive quest for truth amid illusions.

Chapter 49

  • Zhuangzi's "Butterfly Dream" It is a famous philosophical anecdote about a dream where the philosopher Zhuangzi transforms into a butterfly and then questions whether he is now a man who dreamed of being a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming he is a man. This story explores themes of self, reality, and the fluidity of existence within Daoist philosophy. Zhuangzi recounts a dream where he is a butterfly, flitting around happily and unaware of his human identity. He wakes up and realizes he is Zhuangzi, but is then filled with doubt about whether his current state is the true reality or just another dream. The core question arises: Is he the man who dreamed he was a butterfly, or is he a butterfly dreaming he is a man?

Chapter 52

  • Mr. Spitzer's meticulous accounting and listing is directly tied to his struggle with his identity, particularly his relationship with his deceased brother. He seeks a perfect balance, a mirrored reflection where everything in his life corresponds to his brother's, suggesting a desire to understand himself through this dualistic lens. The fear of "no differing counterpart" speaks to the existential anxiety of lacking a defined self, relying on another for definition. This resonates with philosophical concepts of identity formation, the "self" versus the "other," and the influence of external relationships on self-perception. Thinkers like Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir explored similar themes in existentialism. Spitzer's obsessive listing and indexing of even the most mundane objects (pearl buttons, rusted nail heads, etc.) reflects a deep-seated need to impose order on a chaotic world. He's trying to find patterns, connections, and a "common denominator" to make sense of existence. This aligns with the philosophical pursuit of metaphysics: trying to understand the fundamental nature of reality. The futility of Spitzer's efforts mirrors the inherent difficulties in finding absolute order or meaning in a world often perceived as absurd or chaotic (a key theme in existentialism and absurdism). The questions about the sail ("Was the sail unwinding the same as the winding sail?") touch on the philosophical problem of identity over time. Does something remain the same if its state changes? This is further complicated by the cyclical nature of the tides and the wagon wheels, implying a sense of eternal return or repetition. This connects to Heraclitus's famous saying, "No man ever steps in the same river twice," emphasizing the constant flux and change in the universe. The paragraph also hints at Nietzsche's concept of eternal recurrence. Spitzer's attempts to capture reality through lists and measurements are ultimately limited. His fear that "all the weight, as he had often complained, was on this weightless star" alludes to the disparity between the tangible and intangible, the concrete and abstract. He can list the physical properties of objects, but he cannot capture their essence or meaning. This is related to epistemology, the study of knowledge and its limitations. The paragraph suggests the impossibility of fully knowing or representing reality, as there will always be something missing or lost in translation. The description of Spitzer's actions creating "division as in division there was creation," suggests that in every act of creation, there is also an act of destruction. This mirrors the belief that every action has an equal and opposite reaction. This is connected to the concept of the "Tao" which states that everything is composed of two opposites, and that without the other, it cannot exist.
