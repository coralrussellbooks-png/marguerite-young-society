Chapter 61

On Chapter 61 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Woman in Young's Magnum Opus.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Musidora Tidings

A frog does the mute's talking in this chapter, and voicelessness is the pillar it carries. The mute cannot speak and keeps a frog trained to croak words in his place, everyday cautions like "Watch out for the pickpockets," and the rented croak is the historical silencing of women and the book's erasure through a severed sense arriving as one thing, a man with no voice of his own who must borrow one from an animal. When the frog dies the silence closes over him by exactly the sound it loses, and the book keeps him at the threshold the way it keeps all its erased, present and unaccounted for, the almost-seen and almost-heard figure who runs from Musidora through the black coachman and Martha into a body that cannot speak at all.

The frog's death and its long burial give the chapter its grief, and the grief is carried in gesture because it cannot be carried in words. The mute's hands, "white as lilies," sign division and prayer and meeting and parting and flight, a whole language "like fish fins waving through water," and the sign language is the alternative expression the book keeps crediting to the silenced, plural selfhood spoken with the body when the throat is shut. The wild flora around the grave, its fertility and its natural cycles, set life against the mute's stopped voice, and Mr. Spitzer mourning beside him without fully understanding him is the male witness half-comprehending a grief he did not make, the same half-understanding he brought to Cousin Hannah's deathbed.

Mr. Spitzer envies the mute his silence, "free from the discords, alarms, irritating buzzings" of sound, and the envy is the book naming silence as a refuge and not only a wound. Yet he grieves that the mute has "missed music and law and conceptions of justice," and the two feelings together are the double bind the book keeps finding, the silenced person spared the noise of the world and shut out of its institutions in the same stroke, refuge and exclusion arriving as one condition.

The mute walks "like a traveller in a foreign country," present in a hearing world and cut off from it, invisibility and erasure worked into a man who cannot be reached by the sound around him. And Mr. Spitzer, the "mournful musician" whose compositions no one hears, finds in the mute his "ideal audience," the listener who cannot listen, so that the music that goes unheard meets the ear that cannot hear it and the book's two great absences fold into one scene. The chapter ends on silence as "greater than sound," the unspoken carrying more than the spoken, and it leaves the mute among the many in an eternal silence that is loss and peace at once, refusing to make the reader choose between them.
