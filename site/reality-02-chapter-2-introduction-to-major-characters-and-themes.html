Miss MacIntosh: Character and Philosophy

Miss MacIntosh is the novel’s anchor in the tangible world, the fixed point against which Catherine’s opium dreams and Vera’s wandering imagination are measured. She is Vera’s nursemaid, her moral guide, and for much of the book the one person Vera can name as ordinary. Her whole way of being is a standing argument for reality over illusion, and the novel’s deepest irony is that this argument rests on a concealment of her own.

Her appearance states her philosophy before she speaks. She is plain and robust, held upright by a whale-boned corset laced tight, dressed in durable, old-fashioned clothes made to last “for this life” rather than for show. She wears a whaler’s hat and a patched mackintosh, carries a black umbrella that serves as both sword and shield against weather and illusion alike, and refuses silks, furs, and “cold sapphires” as vanities that hide poverty and distract from the living world. Her marcelled red hair fits “close like a helmet.” That detail matters, because the hair is not hers. Beneath it she is bald, without eyebrows or lashes, and she has lost a breast, over which she wears her watch. The plainest, most solid figure in the book is also its most carefully constructed.

Her creed is common sense, which she calls “the finest sense,” and “truth to nature, nothing else.” She is, by her own estimate, “the last person who would ever be taken in by what existed nowhere but in the dreaming mind.” She insists that no one should dream of what is not and will never be. Against Catherine’s shifting phantoms she sets order, industry, and usefulness: work at hand, physical exercise as “the best tonic,” self-control over wildness, one finished task preferred to a hundred begun. She holds that life is hard and “intended to be hard,” that “the best bed was a bed of rock,” and that dignity lies in accepting deprivation without self-pity. She is frugal to the point of saving candles and soap coupons against a rainy day, and she trains Vera for the life she believes a woman is owed: sewing with large, straight basting stitches that pull out clean, cooking, housekeeping, arithmetic, geography, navigation, and marriage, which she calls the only normal state for a woman.

In Catherine’s house she is “the Rock of Ages in a land where all things crumbled,” untouched by the hallucinatory air the others breathe. She has “never seen these phantoms thickening” around them. She teaches Vera “just where the dream ended, where the reality began,” and this is the office Vera resents most and needs most. To the child, Miss MacIntosh is at first “the cross I had to bear,” an interference with her contraband inner life, a woman whose practical knowledge seems small beside the ocean Vera watches for “a vision more secure.”

The relationship turns on a single revelation. At fourteen Vera discovers the baldness, the missing lashes and brow, the amputated breast, and behind them a hidden life: a singular love for a dancing girl named Lorena, a broken engagement to Mr. Bonebreaker, years of loneliness and disappointment. Vera’s first response is cruel triumph, the shout that Miss MacIntosh is a man, as though the bare skull confirmed her own visions. What follows runs deeper: pity, then tenderness, then love. She comes to love the woman for her imperfection precisely because “I could not love perfection.” The champion of the real is shown to have sustained, all along, an illusion of her own solidity, and this does not diminish her. It makes her human, and it collapses the clean wall between Catherine’s dreaming and Miss MacIntosh’s daylight on which Vera had tried to stand.

The fuller reading of the baldness as an unmasking, and its place in the novel’s architecture of doubling, is developed in Part Two of Chapter 7 and is not repeated here.

Catherine Cartwheel: The Opium World

Catherine Helena Cartwheel, Vera’s mother, is the novel’s fullest embodiment of illusion as a chosen world. She lies in a prolonged opium coma that is less a loss of consciousness than a retreat into a richer and more governable one. Having found the waking world unsatisfactory, she withdraws by slow degrees into “watchful, wide-eyed wakefulness,” her eyes shining even as she sleeps, regulated by opium and sedatives into a permanent twilight that keeps madness at bay.

Her bedroom is “as filled with visitors as the Grand Central Station,” filled with guests who are not there: Mr. Chandelier, the black coachman, dead kings and queens, faded movie stars, drowned sailors, centaurs, figures drawn without distinction from history, myth, and the grave. She receives them as real, and in her ordering the living and the dead sit together without contradiction, since she makes “no great distinction” between the two. She believes she died in youth and lives on as two selves at once, a corpse upon its marble sarcophagus and a luminous mind that leaves the body to travel. For her the terms have simply exchanged places. She “pretended that the real was the dream, that the dream was the real,” and reality is “that which was unreal and was clothed with the dream of reality.”

The dream is not passive. It is her one remaining form of control over a world that failed her, and she treats it as a private sovereignty needing no permission: her dreams “needed carry no credentials other than that they were her dreams.” Within it she manipulates time, place, and identity, and even reverses cause and effect, so that the echo can precede the song and the death precede the life. Her husband, lost years before on a face of the Jungfrau and never found, survives here among her other visitors. Her body lies among satin pillows while her mind wanders, the two so estranged that she becomes, in the novel’s phrase, an animated, bejeweled corpse.

Two objects fix her condition. The stopped clocks throughout the house, each halted at a different hour of her long withdrawal, hold all the moments of her retreat present at once, none giving way to the next. The little yellow canary is the thread she believes ties her to life: so long as it lives, she cannot die. She does not know that it has died many times, and that Mr. Spitzer has each time replaced it, so that the single continuous life she trusts is in fact a succession of substitutes. The canary is her whole situation in miniature, a fragile life sustained by a tender deception, one thing believed where there are secretly many.

Against Miss MacIntosh’s daylight, Catherine is the seduction and the danger of the inward world: expansive, limitless, and finally isolating. Her dreams protect her from a reality she could not bear and at the same time wall her off from her daughter and from any life that is shared. She is the background of illusion from which Vera spends the novel trying to flee, and the measure of how much such a flight can cost.

Mr. Spitzer: The Divided Self

Mr. Spitzer, Catherine’s lawyer and faithful attendant, carries the novel’s central proposition in his own body: that “the individual is the one illusion.” He is the surviving half of a pair of identical twins. His brother Peron, the reckless gambler and spendthrift, is dead, and Mr. Spitzer, the cautious lawyer and silent musician, lives on defined entirely by his difference from a man who is no longer there.

He keeps twin iron bedsteads, his own and his brother’s, and is troubled at night by the sense that he is two bodies in two beds, migrating between them, unable to fix himself to a single self. He pays Peron’s gambling debts with each new day, thinks his brother’s thoughts, and remembers him so completely that he seems to be living Peron’s life and dying his death while looking back on himself as one already dead. Catherine names the condition in legal terms he cannot refute: the case of Spitzer versus Spitzer, in which he must appear as both the accuser and the defense, and she taunts him with the possibility that the wrong brother lies in the grave. He attended, the novel says, “Mr. Spitzer’s funeral,” and the line will not resolve which Spitzer was buried and which remained.

His perception matches his divided being. He lives at a second remove from the present, hearing music only after it has ceased and reading traffic signals a moment late, so that the world always reaches him already past. His own music has gone silent. It falls, as the novel puts it, “on deaf ears, silent ears, dead ears,” a fugue without instruments meant for the brother who cannot hear it. Peron died falling through a glass window on the fourteenth floor, by accident or by design, and Mr. Spitzer once walked through a glass wall himself and felt nothing until the fragments were found behind his ears. The two fates reduce to whether the floor continued on the other side. On that arbitrary fact rests his whole identity as the brother who survived, which leaves him nowhere to stand, right man in the wrong world if he is himself, and the wrong man in the wrong world if he is not.

He is the book’s most sustained study of grief as a permanent condition rather than a stage, of a self that cannot be separated from the ones it has lost. The full development of that study, and its place in the novel’s architecture of doubling, is carried by Parts Two and Three of Chapter 7.

Madge and Homer Capehorn: Innocence on the Bus

The novel does not open in the house of dreams. It opens on a decrepit bus crossing the fog-bound Middle West. The first people Vera studies closely are two strangers: Madge Capehorn, a pregnant girl, and Homer, the boy she has married. Before the reader meets a single phantom, Young sets down two living people who already carry the book’s themes in their bodies.

Madge is dressed for a life she does not have. In the sentence Young gives her, “cheap cameos of an enormous size and pieces of colored glass” pile up with bracelets, ankle chains, straw flowers, velvet butterflies, and dime-store pearls until her clothing becomes an unsorted inventory. (Chapter 7 discusses that sentence and what it does to the reader.) Her coat will not close over her pregnancy. The finery is less vanity than defense, a painted surface over a girl who is frightened, poor, and young. That makes her an early, cruder cousin of Miss MacIntosh’s wig: a constructed self worn in public because the plain one will not do.

Beneath the decoration, Madge is already splitting. She feels herself “breaking into two, herself and the other, this face unknown,” and when she paints her face she paints “two mouths” and “two hearts.” Pregnancy doubles her literally, but marriage doubles her in a stranger way. Before he married Madge, Homer loved a dying girl named Jacqueline, and Jacqueline rides along with them, “an old ghost coming between them.” Homer calls Madge by the other girl’s name in his sleep. Madge finds it “harder every minute” to tell one person from another, and she names her situation “this marriage of three.” She cannot fight this rival, who is either dying or dead. The living wife is left competing with an absence.

Homer is the book’s first picture of innocence, and innocence here is less a virtue than a kind of sleep. He is a boy with a wife and a coming child and no idea what either will ask of him. He sleeps through much of the ride. When he tries to reason his way out of the confusion over Madge and Jacqueline, the reasoning undoes him. He starts from the plain logic that two bodies cannot hold the same place, and he ends by asking, “I am I, or am I? Am I? Are you? Is she?” His attempt to prove that selves are separate is what breaks his sense of having one.

Together the Capehorns introduce three notes the rest of the novel will sustain. Innocence is Homer, asleep on a bus toward a future he cannot name. Loss is Jacqueline, the absent third who shapes the marriage more than either living partner. Despair is Madge, young and pregnant and dressed in glass, already sensing that the child and the life she has entered may not hold together.

None of it is exotic, and that is the point. Before Young shows us an opium bed crowded with centaurs, she shows us a poor girl and a sleeping boy. She lets us see that the line between a self and its ghosts gives way in ordinary lives first.

Vera’s Search: The Mother in the Bed and the Nurse in the Sea

Vera has spent her life between two poles. Behind her is her mother, lying in the house by the sea. Ahead of her, somewhere, is Miss MacIntosh, who walked into the ocean and was never found.

The portrait of Catherine above describes the coma as Catherine lives it, a sovereign world of guests and stopped clocks. For her daughter, the coma is something else: a mother who is always there and never reachable. Catherine makes “no great distinction” between the living and the dead, which means she makes little distinction between Vera and any of her phantoms. A child raised at that bedside learns early that someone can be present without being reachable.

Vera calls her mother’s world the “background of illusion,” and the phrase is exact. It is not the thing she looks at. It is the thing everything else is seen against, the ground she has spent her life trying to step out from.

Miss MacIntosh was the way out. When she vanished, Vera lost the one person who had taught her where the dream ended, and the loss did not stay in the past. “Long nights, searching for one who was dead,” Vera wanders the harbor cities, and then “Long years, drifting without other purpose.” She refuses the word death, insisting “That she had only disappeared, I had always said,” and the refusal keeps the search alive. Only after losing Miss MacIntosh does Vera understand what she had been: “the central heart, the heart of all hearts, the face of all faces.”

The search is shaped by the illusion it is fleeing. Vera looks for a real woman with the dreaming mind her mother gave her, so Miss MacIntosh keeps appearing where she is not. In one substitution, Mrs. Hogden, the nurse who came after, slides into Miss MacIntosh’s place in Vera’s mind “like the moon appearing where there had been the sun in the sky.” The woman who taught Vera to separate dream from waking now lives mostly in dream. That is the trap, and it is also the book’s honest picture of grief: once the person who grounded a life is gone, that person can be reached only by imagining them.

This is Pillar Two at the scale of a single life. Vera sets out to leave illusion behind by finding the one solid person she knew. But she carries Catherine’s inheritance with her, and the woman she seeks, as Chapter 7 shows, was built of illusion too. The bus to What Cheer is not a road out of the dream. It is the dream, travelling.

Vera’s Opening Voice: The Quest and Its Tone

From its first pages the novel announces itself as a search. Vera names what she is looking for in the largest terms available: life, love, and “truth that does not fail.” She wants an “ultimate harmony,” a pattern beneath the noise that would make the losses add up to something.

What she keeps finding is repetition. Her early reflections return to “the dream of chaos repeating chaos,” and to the suspicion that truth may prove to be “but another illusion.” The quest is announced and undermined in the same breath, and that doubleness sets the book’s existential tone. Vera does not stop searching once she suspects the answer may not exist. She keeps going because the search is the only form of hope open to her, and the novel treats that persistence without irony.

Her account of why she has come shows how the search feels from inside. It begins plainly, “I had come because of my own heart’s need for an answer.” Then it multiplies into a long chain of reasons: other souls, the dead, a chance remark on a city street, a dead girl’s letters, a deaf musician, a blind man reaching for his cup. (Chapter 7 reads this passage as incantation.) No reason outranks another, and they do not build to a decision. They pile up into a weight. This is how grief explains itself: not as one loss but as everything at once.

The landscape answers to the voice. As the bus crosses the flooded Indiana flats, Vera tells us there is “no landscape but the soul’s,” and the outer world dissolves into mist and oceanic grey. The opening does not present reality as a fixed stage where a search takes place. Reality is already being reshaped by the one who searches, which is Pillar Two stated as a scene.

Out of this comes the sentence the book turns on: “Every heart is the other heart. Every soul is the other soul. Every face is the other face. The individual is the one illusion.” In context it is not a doctrine Vera has reached. It is a suspicion she carries onto the bus. If it is true, her search for one particular woman is also a search for everyone she has lost, and finding Miss MacIntosh would mean finding something no single person can be.

Young sets the stakes that high on purpose. The opening’s tone is grave, searching, and oddly tender. It is the voice of someone who expects to fail and has decided the looking still matters.

Reflection Questions

  1. Miss MacIntosh’s appearance states her philosophy before she speaks. What does it mean that the appearance was partly built?

  2. Vera meets the baldness first with cruel triumph and later with love. What has to change in a person for that shift to happen?

  3. The canary is one life believed where there are secretly many. Where else do people rely on a continuity that is really a series of replacements?

  4. Mr. Spitzer’s identity rests on whether a floor continued past a glass wall. How much of any identity rests on accident?

  5. Why might Young open with a poor girl and a sleeping boy on a bus instead of with the house of dreams?

  6. Vera suspects that “the individual is the one illusion” before she can prove it. What would it mean to search for one lost person as though searching for everyone?
