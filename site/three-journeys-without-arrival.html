The Structure of the Problem

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling contains three simultaneous pilgrimage structures: Dante's Divine Comedy, John Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress, and the novel's own bus journey across the American interior. Each is a journey from one state of being toward another. None of them arrive. Instead of noting their parallel existence separately, what is the architectural relationship between them: how they work against each other, how Young uses their contradictions as structural load-bearing joints, and what it means that Pilgrim's Progress is explicitly read backward in this novel.

The preface calls the novel a picaresque, a novel of the road, a journey or voyage of the human spirit in its search for reality in a world of illusion and nightmare. This framing invokes genre conventions that every one of the three journey structures then subverts. The journey does not educate the traveler. The road does not end at the Celestial City, the Empyrean, or any destination the traveler chose. The bus runs through hell and high water and keeps driving.

Pilgrims Progress: Read Backward

Miss MacIntosh carries Pilgrim's Progress throughout the novel. It is her book, her portable authority, the book she holds against illusion and opium and Vera's dreaming. She is herself the novel's Pilgrim: plain, directed, immune to the Slough of Despond's seductions, committed to the narrow path. When she walks into the ocean, her copy washes up on the beach, waterlogged, marked with the impressions of sea shells. The book that mapped the journey to the Celestial City ends at the bottom of the September shore.

But before this, in Chapter 3, Vera describes reading the book aloud to Miss MacIntosh on the beach: sometimes I would begin at the beginning, reading straight to the end or skipping only a few passages, and sometimes I would begin at the end, reading straight to the beginning, working from redemption backward, perhaps skipping the same passages, and she would never seem to notice. Miss MacIntosh does not notice because she is listening to the tides, not to Vera's voice. The book is being read in both directions. Miss MacIntosh does not correct the reversal.

A Pilgrim's Progress read from redemption backward is a different story entirely. It begins at the Celestial City and moves toward the City of Destruction. It begins with arrival and moves toward the original condition of sin and loss. This is not a parody. It is the AND Philosophy's structural logic applied to a pilgrimage book: both directions are true simultaneously. The journey toward salvation and the journey away from it are the same journey read by different lights. Miss MacIntosh, who embodies the Pilgrim's directional certainty in her waking life, carries a book that demonstrates, in the hands of the child she is supposed to be guiding, that the direction itself is undecidable.

The Slough of Despond appears three times in the novel, each time as a description of the actual world rather than as an obstacle to be crossed. This world was a harsh place, a Slough of Despond, and we were put here to be tested, yet though all should fail the test. The qualification is the key: yet though all should fail. In Bunyan, the Slough is a temporary obstacle. In Young's novel, it is the permanent condition of being alive, and the test is one everyone fails. Miss MacIntosh's practical wisdom cannot redeem this. Her copy of the book drowns with her.

The Divine Comedy: Escalator Psychology

Dante's Divine Comedy organizes the afterlife as a vertical structure: Inferno at the bottom, Purgatorio in the middle, Paradiso at the top. The journey has direction: downward into Hell to understand sin, then upward through Purgatory and Paradise toward God. It is the most architecturally purposeful journey in Western literature. Virgil guides Dante through Hell; Beatrice guides him upward through Paradise. The guide changes as the journey changes. The destination is the Empyrean: the realm of pure light beyond all the spheres.

Mr. Spitzer has what the novel calls an escalator psychology. He continually went up and down, passing through seas of clouds, though the upper world was under, and the under world was upper, and everything was probably reversed, trees rooted in the sky, birds flying under the water, and there were streets under the earth. He moves through Inferno and Paradiso without distinguishing them reliably, without arriving at either, without a Virgil who knows the way or a Beatrice who represents a destination worth reaching. He is not sure whether the place he is in is Paradiso if this was Paradiso. He adds the qualification because he cannot be certain.

The novel places Dante as a named visitor in Vera's mother's opium bedroom, listed alongside revolutionary Diderot and Harold Harefoot and Christolpho Colombo. Dante is one of the distinguished dead who pass through the opium lady's room on their nocturnal rounds. He has arrived at the wrong address. The journey that ends in the Empyrean has deposited its author in an opium addict's bedroom in a New England seaside house, one arrival among many undistinguished arrivals, as if the destination of any journey depends entirely on who is doing the dreaming.

The other Dante structural marker is Cocytus: the frozen lake at the bottom of Hell, the ninth circle, where the worst sinners are encased in ice. In the novel, Cocytus appears as the name on a water-warped map in Mr. Spitzer's desk drawer, alongside cancelled postage stamps of exploded monarchies and old coins of lost empires. The map of wandering Cocytus: not the fixed geography of Dante's Hell but a wandering one, subject to the same drift as the stars it is mapped beside. The underworld has come loose from its location. It wanders. This is what happens to a journey structure when the destination is no longer fixed.

Dante's Beatrice is also present. The doctor's room contains an old steel engraving of Dante wooing Beatrice. The word wooing is Young's: Dante's love for Beatrice in the Comedy is not courtship but something closer to theological aspiration. She represents divine grace and the ascent of the soul. The word wooing domesticates this, returns it to ordinary romantic pursuit, puts it on the wall of a country doctor's bedroom between medical charts and embryology texts. The guide to Paradise has been reduced to an engraving between the mattress indentations. This is the novel's treatment of the Beatrice principle: the figure who should draw us upward is available only as an image in a dead man's room.

The Bus: Charon's Ferry Driving Through Hell

The novel's framing journey is Vera's bus ride across the American interior in search of Miss MacIntosh. The bus driver is drunk, asleep at the wheel, somehow still on the road. He drives through hell and high water. His passengers cannot pay Charon when they cross the river in a leaking boat. The explicit Charon reference appears in his monologue, but the identification is structural throughout: Charon ferries souls across the Styx; the bus driver ferries passengers across a flooded interior that does not distinguish between the living and the dead.

The bus driver keeps driving even when he knows he is in his four-postered bed under a heavy blanket. He is simultaneously on the road and in bed, driving and dreaming, steering the wheel in both worlds. This is the novel's definition of the guide: someone who cannot stop navigating even when the navigation has come loose from any actual geography. His hands remain on the wheel. The road turns where the road turns. No destination governs this.

The passengers are characterized as dead passengers wrapped in their shrouds. The dead human pigeon passengers whirr with winnowing wings in Mr. Spitzer's vision. Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John appear as passengers, the four evangelists riding the bus across a dead interior country that is nobody's promised land. The bus is going somewhere. It has always been going somewhere. Nobody on it knows where that is, and the driver is asleep.

The American interior through which the bus moves is itself a failed version of the pilgrimage landscape. In Pilgrim's Progress, the landscape through which Christian travels is allegorically charged: every location is a test or a temptation or a station on the journey. The Valley of the Shadow of Death is a real valley, but it is also the condition of the soul confronting its own mortality. Young's American landscape refuses this allegorical function. The flooded Iowa roads are just flooded roads. The bus cannot find its way because the landscape provides no allegorical direction. This is the American version of the pilgrimage: the Celestial City replaced by What Cheer, Iowa, which offers no celestial content and very little cheer.

Three Guides Who Cannot Guide

Each journey structure has a guide, and each guide fails in a distinct way that is architecturally meaningful for the AND Philosophy.

Miss MacIntosh is Bunyan's Pilgrim become a guide. She knows the path and walks it plainly. She is forthright, the one form not concealed, the one person given to no excess of imagination. She fails not through ignorance or corruption but through the constitutive impossibility of the Pilgrim's role in a world where the Slough of Despond is not a temporary obstacle but the permanent condition of being alive. She cannot guide Vera to a Celestial City that does not exist as a destination. She can only model the walking. And then she walks into the ocean.

Mr. Spitzer has no guide. The escalator goes up and down without a Virgil or a Beatrice. He is a soul divided, for he was this twin, and one was dead, and one lived. The confusion reigning like order in his great heart. Without a guide to distinguish Inferno from Paradiso, he cannot organize his vertical movement into a journey. He moves. He does not arrive. He is always in transit through regions he cannot name with confidence.

The bus driver guides without knowing where he is going, without knowing if he is awake, without a map that corresponds to the actual roads. He is the most honest guide in the novel: he does not pretend to know the destination. He drives. The bus moves. That is all he can offer, and it is not nothing. It is exactly what the novel offers its reader: movement without arrival, the full swelling of the wave, and the requirement to bring back to the shore the wave which carried you.

The AND Philosophy Reading: What Three Failed Journeys Do

The three journey structures do not cancel each other out. They do not produce relativism, the conclusion that since all journeys fail no journey matters. The AND Philosophy's fourth pillar, recursive construction, is demonstrated by the three journeys running simultaneously: each one builds on the others' failures. Pilgrim's Progress read backward reveals that the direction of salvation is a choice, not a fact. Dante's Comedy turned into an escalator reveals that vertical organization of experience is a projection, not a discovery. The bus journey reveals that the American landscape will not cooperate with allegorical aspiration.

Together they enact the AND Philosophy's premise that illusion is primary and constitutive. The journey is not a lie. Bunyan's Pilgrim, Dante's souls, Vera's bus ride: these are all genuine attempts to organize experience into something with direction. The organization is real. The arrival is not. The act of walking toward something, of driving through the flooded dark, of writing silent music in a dead man's town house: these are the actions the novel presents as worthy even without destination. The grief as permanent weather pillar applies to journey structure: you do not stop traveling because the Celestial City is not there. You travel because you are a traveler, because your hands are on the wheel, because the road turns where the road turns, and the old carriage horses remember.

Miss MacIntosh's copy of Pilgrim's Progress washes up on the beach, marked by sea shells, waterlogged. It has arrived somewhere. The sea has returned it. It is not intact. But it is still legible, still there, still carrying the impressions of the water it has traveled through. This is what the novel's three journeys produce: not arrival, but the work of the journey itself, marked by everything it passed through, offered back to whoever finds it on the shore.
