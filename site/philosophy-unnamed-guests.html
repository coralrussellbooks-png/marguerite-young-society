Chapter 2. The Unnamed Guests

Part of The Philosophy in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated November 18, 2026

← Previous · Philosophy of MMMD Home · Next →

Pillars: Pillar 1 (Not One Self) · Pillar 2 (Reality Is Constructed) · Pillar 10 (The Sacred Is Here)

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Philosophy: Buddhism · Epicureanism · Heraclitus and Flux · Hinduism · Schopenhauer · Taoism and Zhuangzi · William James and Pragmatism

Chapters discussed: 5, 13, 18, 29, 38, 41, 43, 45, 48, 49, 51, 52

The novel names only a handful of philosophers. Many more are in it without their names. Young took an image or a saying from a thinker, put it in her own words, and gave it to a character to live inside, as she did with Nietzsche’s abyss. The guests in this chapter never sign the register. They come from Greece and Germany, and from China and India too, and the novel gives each of them as much room as Plato’s boat.

Heraclitus and the River

Heraclitus, the Greek philosopher of change, is remembered for saying that no one can step into the same river twice (Plato, Cratylus 402a). Young’s river runs through the whole novel. “That same stream which a child could wade in was that same river which a man could drown in” (ch. 5). Much later, “all things flowed into all things like water flowing into water” (ch. 52). In Catherine’s garden, as Vera sees it, the life-sized chessmen move “as if played by invisible players who could perceive an order in multiple confusions of movements and sounds, a secret plan” (ch. 13), close to his saying that time is a child playing draughts. The Deep Architecture follows him further in The Garden, the Stream, and the Blind God.

Epicurus and the Garden

The same garden holds Epicurus, whose followers met in a garden and taught that the good life is a quiet pleasure, and Anatole France, whose The Garden of Epicurus (1894) Young knew. In Catherine’s version, “life was only this, a perpetual garden party omitting the tragic spirit” (ch. 13). The garden keeps its pleasures by leaving out grief, and the rest of the novel puts the grief back.

Zhuangzi and the Butterfly

Zhuangzi, a Chinese philosopher of the fourth century BCE and one of the founders of Taoism, told of dreaming he was a butterfly. When he woke, he wrote, “Now I do not know whether I was then a man dreaming I was a butterfly, or whether I am now a butterfly, dreaming I am a man” (translated by Herbert A. Giles, 1889, chapter 2). The question comes to Mr. Spitzer almost in Zhuangzi’s form: “Did he so much as know whether he was a man or a butterfly, a lost psyche drifting down the wind?” (ch. 38). For him the butterfly question is also the twin question. He sees “a butterfly pirouetting in a cloud, butterfly which had seemed one butterfly but was always two butterflies conjoining over the suddenly shining wave, one with its image” (ch. 49). The Deep Architecture’s The Butterfly’s Dream gives the full trail.

The Net of Heaven

The Tao Te Ching, the old Chinese book of the Way, says in chapter 73, “The meshes of the net of Heaven are large; far apart, but letting nothing escape” (translated by James Legge, 1891). On the night Vera sees her bald, Miss MacIntosh says it in her own way: “Heaven’s nets are woven very wide,” and then, “but none shall escape them” (ch. 18). The realist who believed in nothing invisible speaks the oldest Chinese wisdom without knowing it.

The Upanishads

The Isha Upanishad, one of the old Hindu teachings on the self and the world, says of the one reality, “It moves and It moves not. It is far and also It is near. It is within and also It is without all this” (verse 5, translated by Swami Paramananda, 1919). Of Catherine, motionless in her bed, the novel says: “She was swifter than her own mind, swifter than her mercurial thoughts. She was far, yet she was near. She was within, yet she was outside” (ch. 41). The opium dreamer is given the language of the oldest Indian philosophy.

The Other Shore

The Buddha taught that freedom is a crossing to “the other shore.” Dying, Cousin Hannah cries out, “A light, a light! I see a light upon the other shore,” and Mr. Spitzer, beside her, has already told himself where he will be: “For he would be upon the other shore” (ch. 43). Nirvana sits in his list of imagined heavens, beside Valhalla and the happy hunting grounds (ch. 29).

Leibniz and the Pebble

Gottfried Leibniz, the German philosopher who died in 1716, pictured the world as made of tiny units he called monads, each one “a perpetual living mirror of the universe” (Monadology, section 56, translated by Robert Latta, 1898). In the novel, as Mr. Spitzer hears bells ringing as if all music might be heard at once, “A great city was enclosed inside a pebble” (ch. 49). Two chapters later he gives a voice “even to a city roaring inside a pebble” (ch. 51). Each small thing holds the whole.

Schopenhauer and the Music of the Will

Arthur Schopenhauer held that behind the world we see is a single blind striving he called the will, and that music, alone among the arts, speaks that will directly (The World as Will and Representation, 1818). Mr. Spitzer imagines “the beautiful music of the spheres, filling all space with its sound as of the human will” (ch. 38), music his dead brother heard and he cannot. The Deep Architecture’s The World as Will reads Mr. Spitzer through Schopenhauer.

William James and the Stream

Young named William James’s The Principles of Psychology (1890) as a book she studied “as if it were a Bible” (American Audio Prose Library interview, 1983). James wrote that consciousness “is nothing jointed; it flows” (chapter 9). Young keeps the stream and breaks its flow. Mr. Spitzer finds that “Consciousness was ever that which was sporadic, fluttering, not constant even in this life, just as sustained attention was impossible” (ch. 48).

Boethius and the Wheel

Boethius, writing in prison before his execution in 524, gave the Middle Ages the wheel of Fortune, which lifts people up and drops them without regard to merit. The family at the center of the novel is named Cartwheel, and Mr. Spitzer wonders whether “perhaps the smallest things were indeterminate, that only they escaped this wheel of fortune” (ch. 45). The Character Hubs page holds “All the Darlings,” a map of the novel’s people drawn on a Boethian diagram.

None of these guests is named, and none is refuted by argument. Like the philosophers who are named, each is given a room and a life to live in, and each idea is tested by what happens to the person carrying it. The next chapter shows how to watch that testing happen inside a single sentence.
