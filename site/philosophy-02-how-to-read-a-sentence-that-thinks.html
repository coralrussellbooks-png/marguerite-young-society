The Philosophy in MMMD

Chapter 2. How to Read a Sentence That Thinks

← Previous · Philosophy of MMMD Home · Next →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Vera Cartwheel

Philosophy: Hinduism · Plato and Platonism · Spinoza

Allusions: Greek Mythology

Definition from research

Readers new to Miss MacIntosh, My Darling often describe the prose as a flood. The sentences are long, the images pile on one another, and the thread of an idea seems to vanish under ornament. The usual advice is to surrender and let the language wash over. That advice is half right. The prose does need surrender, but it also rewards a slower kind of attention, because Young’s sentences are not decoration laid over an argument. They are the argument. Each long passage tends to move through three stages: a proposal, an accumulation, and a collapse. Once a reader learns to see those stages, the flood becomes a demonstration.

The clearest place to learn this is the first chapter, where Vera Cartwheel, the narrator, rides a bus through fog into the American interior. Something she sees by the road starts a chain of thought that runs for more than a page. It is worth walking through slowly.

The Setting

The passage begins with an image: “a tall man with a child perched on his lean shoulders, a double-headed man, staring at nothingness or beyond it.” Before any idea is stated, the body itself has been doubled. A man with a child on his shoulders is two people who look, from a distance, like one person with two heads. The plural self, which will become one of the novel’s great themes, arrives first as a trick of fog.

Then the landscape disappears. “There was now no landscape. There was now no landscape but the soul’s, and that is the inexactitude, the ever shifting and the distant.” This is the ground being cleared. Outer reality has been swallowed by mist, and what remains is inner reality, which Young immediately describes as inexact and shifting. The stage is set for a philosophical claim, and it is set on unstable footing.

Vera then states the problem her whole life has posed: “All my life I had been reaching for the tangible, and it had evaded me, much like the myth of Tantalus, much as if the tangible itself were an illusion.” Tantalus stood in water that drained away whenever he bent to drink. Vera’s version of the curse is epistemological. Solid reality recedes whenever she reaches for it.

The Proposal

Now the great proposal comes, and it comes as a question. “What was the organization of illusion, of memory? Who knew even his own divided heart? Who knew all hearts as his own?” The questions lead into a long, rising sentence that asks whether, “in the vision of some omniscient eye,” there would be “a web of spidery logic establishing the most secret relationships, deep calling to deep, illuminations of the eternal darkness, recognitions in the night world of voyager dreams, all barriers dissolving, all souls as one and united?”

This is the doctrine philosophers call monism, the belief that all apparent separateness is finally one reality. It has a long history, from the Upanishads through Plotinus to Spinoza, the same Spinoza who sleeps in Catherine’s green room. Notice how carefully Young frames it. The unity is visible only to “some omniscient eye.” It is held together by “a web of spidery logic,” a phrase that makes the web both beautiful and fragile, and faintly sinister. And the whole vision is a question, not an assertion.

Then the question hardens into four short declarations, the shortest sentences in the passage. “Every heart is the other heart. Every soul is the other soul. Every face is the other face. The individual is the one illusion.”

Here the prose changes speed. After a sentence that has run on for dozens of clauses, these four land like hammer blows. This is the proposal at its most confident. If a reader stopped here, it would be easy to conclude that Young believes in mystical oneness, and many readers have concluded exactly that.

The Accumulation

But the passage does not stop. The very next sentence reads: “I had walked alone, searching, seeing only, though I sought for an ultimate harmony, the fleeting image, the disrelation, the chaos begetting the chaos, the truth as but another illusion, that which must perish, the rose which must fade, the heart which must stop.”

Experience has entered, and it enters as a list. Each item in the list is something Vera actually found while looking for the unity she had just proclaimed. She sought harmony and found “the fleeting image.” She sought relation and found “the disrelation.” She sought truth and found “the truth as but another illusion.” Then come the mortal facts: the rose that fades, the heart that stops. The grammar matters. The monist vision was framed as a hope (“would there not be”). The accumulation is framed as memory (“I had walked”). Hope is set against what actually happened, and what actually happened keeps growing.

The next sentence adds more. “Nothing I had touched but that it had faded like a dream, there being no dream that would not fail, no life which would not cease, no soul which answered mine like deep calling to deep.” The phrase “deep calling to deep,” which a moment ago described the secret bond among all souls, now returns in the negative. No soul answered hers that way. Young has taken the proposal’s own language and turned it against the proposal.

The Collapse

The collapse follows in a single sentence. “That background of illusion from which I always fled like a drowning man who clutches at a straw, it was always that background of illusion confronting me again, even as the foreground, and there seemed no truth but what the erroneous mind provided, another dream which had nor purpose nor bearing.”

The monist doctrine said that the individual was the one illusion. The collapse says that illusion is everywhere, in the background and the foreground alike. The unity has not been disproved by a counterargument. It has been drowned. And near the end of the passage comes the conditional that seals it: “could I have believed in that ultimate harmony, I could have been among them, but there had been only, in my narrow experience, the dream of chaos repeating chaos.” The phrase “chaos repeating chaos” even anticipates the novel’s later treatment of eternal recurrence. What returns eternally, in Vera’s experience, is not the beloved moment but disorder itself.

What Remains

Something does survive the collapse, and it is important for everything that follows. The passage does not end in nihilism. It ends in searching. Vera asks, “Where should I go? Where should I turn?” and the bus keeps moving. The four hammer-blow sentences about oneness are not erased. They remain on the page, true as a longing, false as a doctrine. Young lets the reader hold both. The unity of all hearts is something Vera wants and cannot confirm, and the wanting itself becomes part of what she carries forward.

A Method for Reading

This single passage teaches a method that works throughout the novel. When a sentence begins to reach toward a universal claim, a reader can watch for three things.

First, the frame of the proposal. Young usually marks a large idea as a question, a hope, a conditional, or a thought belonging to a particular character. She rarely asserts a doctrine in her own narrative voice without such a frame.

Second, the point where lived detail begins to pile up. Lists are the signal. When Young starts a list, experience is entering the argument, and the list will usually keep going past the point of comfort.

Third, the turn, often marked by the return of the proposal’s own words in a changed or negated form. “Deep calling to deep” becomes “no soul which answered mine like deep calling to deep.” The eternal law of recurrence becomes billiard balls that “would not roll twice the same.”

With this method in hand, the famous difficulty of the prose begins to look different. The length of Young’s sentences is not self-indulgence. It is the room an idea needs in order to be tested. A short sentence can state a doctrine. Only a long one can show the doctrine failing.
