Chapter Nine: The Geometry of the Cross

← Part Three: The AND · Radical Immanence Collection · Chapter Ten: The Mandelbrot AND →

Before writing, before organized religion, before the wheel and the plow and the organized political state, human beings were making a specific mark on the surfaces available to them. It appears in the cave paintings of the Upper Paleolithic. It appears in the earliest known writing systems. It appears in the Neolithic petroglyphs of every inhabited continent. It appears in the cosmological systems of cultures that had no contact with each other and no shared tradition of any kind.

It is the simplest possible mark that contains two dimensions. It is two lines crossing.

The persistence of the cross across human cultures and human time is not accidental and not merely symbolic. It is the record of a recognition: the most economical possible representation of the structure of human existence, the two axes and their intersection, the horizontal world of current relation and the vertical time of transmission and receipt, the self at the crossing.

Western Christianity made the cross into a specific theological symbol, and that specificity has made it difficult for secular philosophy to think clearly about what the cross was before it was a theological symbol, and what it continues to be underneath the theology. But the cross is older than Christianity. It is older than any of the religious traditions that have claimed it. It is the mark that human beings make when they are trying to represent the structure they are inside: two axes, their intersection, the crossing point.

The horizontal arm of the cross is the arm of relation: the lateral extension of the self into its social world, the spread of the plural self across the full width of its accumulation, the wide atmospheric reach of grief and illusion and the witness in its lateral dimension. This is the arm that the ten pillars map. This is the arm that Marguerite Young built 1,198 pages to hold.

The vertical arm of the cross is the arm of transmission: the line running from the ancestors in the sediment to the not-yet-born in the not-yet-formed ground, the line that passes through the crossing point and continues in both directions further than any present moment can see. This is the arm that the eleventh pillar names. This is the arm that Boethius felt in the cell and the medieval illuminators encoded in the Wheel of Fortune and the figure at the bottom of the wheel is oriented along.

The crossing point is the center: the place where the two arms meet, the place where the horizontal accumulation is intersected by the vertical transmission, the place where the self is most fully itself because it is holding both axes at once.

The cross is not a symbol of death, or it is not only that. It is a symbol of the crossing point: the structure of existence that becomes visible when you stop moving along one axis long enough to feel the other. Every religious tradition that has made the cross central has been responding to this recognition, though most have translated it into the language of transcendence: the vertical arm points upward to God, to heaven, to the beyond. The eleventh pillar reads the vertical arm differently. It points downward as much as upward. It points into the sediment as much as into the sky. It is the transmission line, not the transcendence line, and the distinction is the whole of the philosophical difference.

The cross marks the crossing point. The crossing point is where you are. Here is the geometry of where you are.
