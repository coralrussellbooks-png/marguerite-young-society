Chapter 56

← Chapter 55 · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 57 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: Not One Self · Reality Is Constructed

The Questions

Mr. Spitzer dresses his dead brother “like himself,” in his own kind of suit. What is he making of the corpse?

He leaves the brother “nothing of his own but only the red rose by which he should be recognized.” If the two are dressed alike, what tells them apart?

Into the grave go poker chips, dice, a playing card. Why furnish a death with the props of the gambler’s life?

“Death was a formal occasion.” What kind of construction is a funeral?

The chapter shows Mr. Spitzer making his dead brother into a copy of himself. He dresses the corpse not in the “loud-checked tweeds” of the racetrack but in “swallow-tails… of the same cut as his own old black suit,” the sober elegance he himself wears, so that the gambler is buried looking like the composer. This is Not One Self performed on a body. The doubling that ran through the earlier chapters as confusion and incorporation here becomes a deliberate act: Mr. Spitzer clothes the dead man as his own double, erasing the difference between them at the graveside, making the two brothers one figure in two states.

And he leaves exactly one mark of difference. He gives the corpse “nothing of his own but only the red rose by which he should be recognized when he came to the portals of the great city.” A single rose is all that distinguishes the buried brother from the living one, one small token against total identity. The chapter presses the plural self to its edge: if the two are dressed alike, cut alike, faced alike, then the difference between them hangs on a flower, and the flower is not a fact of the self but a sign pinned on, a construction laid over an identity that has otherwise collapsed into sameness. Recognition itself has become a thing Mr. Spitzer has to supply, by hand, with a rose.

The grave is furnished as the gambler’s life was, and the furnishing marks a construction. Into the coffin go “two poker chips, a red and a black, two dice… a playing card,” the props of a life the composer did not share, arranged as carefully as the suit. Death, the chapter says, “was a formal occasion, one for which a man should always be properly” dressed and equipped. This is Reality Is Constructed at the threshold of the grave: even death is staged, costumed, propped, a formal construction assembled by the living, and Mr. Spitzer assembles it, dressing the dead and packing the coffin as one sets a scene.

So the funeral is the plural self and the built world in one act. Mr. Spitzer makes his brother his double by dressing him as himself, keeps them apart by a single constructed sign, and stages the death as a formal occasion he furnishes by hand. The difference between the brothers, the difference between the living and the dead, the very recognizability of a person, are shown here to be things made, laid on, arranged, a rose pinned and a scene set, rather than facts the world hands up. Even at the grave, especially at the grave, the self is a construction, and Mr. Spitzer is its careful, grieving builder.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Mr. Spitzer’s dressing of the corpse for morbid eccentricity, and the reading that treats the single red rose as a small poignant detail rather than the hinge of the chapter. Neither the eccentricity nor the mere poignancy is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that Mr. Spitzer makes his dead brother his double by dressing him as himself, so the difference between the two collapses into sameness and hangs entirely on one constructed sign, a rose pinned on by hand; and that the funeral is a formal occasion staged and furnished by the living, death itself a construction, the self’s very recognizability a thing made rather than given. Identity is laid on, not found, even at the grave. Mr. Spitzer dresses the dead, packs the coffin, pins the rose, and the chapter shows the builder of a self at work where a self is supposed to have ended.

For Reflection

If two people were made identical in every mark, what small constructed sign would carry the whole of the difference?

Is a person’s recognizability a fact about them, or something others supply and pin on?

What does it mean that even death has to be staged, costumed, and furnished by the living?
