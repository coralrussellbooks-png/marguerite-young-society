Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Love

Part of Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Life · Life, Love, and Truth · Truth →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Homer Capehorn · Joachim Spitzer · Madge Capehorn · Vera Cartwheel

Pillars: Grief Doesn't End · Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The word “love” appears 1,546 times, nearly as often as “life,” and the two are braided throughout. Young’s account of love is answered by two more terms of her system: Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act and Grief Doesn't End.

Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act is the stance of staying in the room with another person, painful and complicated as they are, without rushing to fix them or wrap them up. This is love as Young most trusts it, and the novel keeps setting it against a counterfeit. The counterfeit is love that projects an ideal onto another and then holds the ideal instead of the person. Vera, for much of the book, loves the image of love more than any love in front of her, and the search that drives her can look like devotion while it is really a fixation on a lost ideal. When she finally names the truest love she has known, it is not grand. The truest person she ever saw was “a poor old nursemaid walking along the seashore,” and the truest love is the love of that plain, simple, actual figure. Witnessing love does not idealize. It attends.

The failures of love in the novel are failures of witness. James, the chauffeur, wants not connection but revenge on human society. Catherine offers Vera a “poisonous compromise,” the same drugged oblivion she has taken herself, and calls it freedom, which is not witnessing another person’s life but overwriting it. The loneliness that runs through the book, the sense that the characters have “known no one” and are “always alone,” without a “prince charming” ever arriving, is the loneliness of people who cannot or will not witness one another. Mr. Spitzer’s heart, that “dry, imploring emptiness,” is the ache of a witness whose attention has nowhere it is received.

Grief Doesn't End is the second term, and it names the weather love lives in. Grief in this novel is not a stage to pass through on the way to recovery. It is permanent, and love is braided into it so tightly that many characters find love only in the memory of someone gone or in the nearness of death itself. Catherine’s deepest love is a dead love, kept alive in dreams. This looks like sickness if one expects grief to close and healing to arrive. Read through Grief Doesn't End, it is instead a refusal to perform a tidy recovery, a way of staying truthful about what was lost. The book will not let its mourners finish mourning, because to finish would be to lie about the size of the loss.

This is what saves the account from mere despair. The pairing of Madge and Homer as “a pair of lovers,” the wreckage of Catherine’s marriage, the emptiness in Mr. Spitzer, are not evidence that love is worthless. They are evidence that love, honestly held, carries its griefs without discharging them. The love that does not fail is not the perfect union finally attained. It is the witnessing that stays in the room, and the grief that stays honest about everyone the room has lost.
