Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 76

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Esther Longtree

Pillars: Unfinished Is Honest

The Questions

Esther comes to think “successful conceptions had been her worst mistakes.” Why would the completed births be the failures?

She calls them “her unfulfilled abortions, her many births.” What is a birth that would have been truer left unfinished?

She wonders “why she wanted to give birth, to bring life into this sad” world. Is the wish to complete a thing always a good?

If the stillbirths are right and the live births are the grievous errors, what has happened to the meaning of success?

The chapter performs the book’s most startling inversion: the completed births are the mistakes. Brooding in the empty hours, Esther comes to think “that her past life had been different from what she had thought—that there had been mistakes of another kind, no miscarriages, successful conceptions, that these successful conceptions had been her worst mistakes, her most grievous.” The finished child, the delivered whole, the successful conception, is recast as the error, the grievous mistake, while the stillbirths, the unfinished, are quietly exonerated. This turns the ordinary reckoning on its head. What the world would call success, a live birth, a completed thing, Esther calls her worst mistake, and the book lets the inversion stand.

She names the completed births “her unfulfilled abortions, her many births,” and the paradox is exact. The birth that succeeded, that fulfilled itself in a living child, is the “unfulfilled” one, the mistake, while the abortion, the unfinished, the never-delivered, is by implication the fulfilled thing. This is Unfinished Is Honest pushed to its most radical claim: not merely that the unfinished is honest and the finished is dead, but that to complete, to deliver, to bring the whole thing into the world, can be the grievous error, and to leave it unfinished can be the truer, fuller act. Esther’s grief has arrived at a place where finishing is the sin.

This puts the very wish to complete under question. Esther wonders “why she wanted to give birth, to bring life into this sad” world at all, and the wondering is not idle. The desire to finish a thing, to deliver it, to bring the incomplete to completion, which every ordinary instinct treats as good, is here held up and doubted. Is it right to want to complete? Is the drive toward delivery, toward the finished whole, always to be trusted? The chapter does not answer with a flat no, but it opens the question the book has been circling from Esther’s side: the wish to seal a thing into completion may not be the innocent good it appears, and the impulse to finish may itself be the mistake.

So the meaning of success comes apart. If the stillbirths are exonerated and the live births are the worst mistakes, then success, in the ordinary sense of a thing completed and delivered, is no longer the good it was assumed to be. The book has praised the unfinished and dreaded the sealed all along; here, through Esther’s grief-sharpened brooding, it reverses the words themselves, so that the successful is the grievous and the unfulfilled is the fulfilled. This is not despair but the furthest reach of the open construction’s logic: a world in which completing a thing is the error and holding it open is the truth, where Esther’s everlasting, undelivered pregnancy is not her tragedy but her rightness, the one conception she has not spoiled by finishing it.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Esther’s guilt over her live children for simple maternal pathology, and the reading that keeps the ordinary scale intact, in which a completed birth is a success and a stillbirth a loss. Neither the pathology nor the ordinary scale survives the chapter’s inversion.

The footing is that the completed births are recast as the grievous mistakes and the stillbirths quietly exonerated, so that the successful conception is the error and the unfinished is the truer thing; that to complete, deliver, and finish can itself be the sin, Unfinished Is Honest pushed to its most radical claim; that the very wish to finish a thing is put under question rather than trusted as an innocent good; and that the meaning of success comes apart, the finished no longer the good it was assumed to be. Finishing can be the mistake, and holding open can be the rightness. Esther’s undelivered pregnancy is the one conception she has not spoiled by completing, and the chapter lets that stand as truth rather than affliction.

For Reflection

Is there something one completed, delivered, finished, that might have been truer left open?

Is the wish to bring a thing to completion always a good, or can the impulse to finish itself be the error?

What happens to the meaning of success once the finished thing is no longer assumed to be the achievement?
