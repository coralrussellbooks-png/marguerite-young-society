Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 37

← Chapter 36 · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 38 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel

Pillars: Not One Self · Reality Is Constructed · Unfinished Is Honest

The Questions

Catherine says “order was a mad man’s dream, the maddest dream of all.” Is the sealed dreamer right about order, even from inside her own seal?

She has “so many selves that she could never know them all.” Is a self one can never fully know a failure, or the truth of what a self is?

“But who was the dreamer?” If the dream dreamed the dreamer, who is building whom?

If a world “without mistakes” would be “impoverished,” what is the error good for?

Catherine, sealed inside her dream, delivers one of the book’s clearest statements of the open. “Order,” she says, “was a mad man’s dream, the maddest dream of all,” and she prefers “confusion to order, these interesting, illusive ambiguities.” She is right, and the rightness is the chapter’s strange gift. Rigid order, the finished blueprint, the single clean account, is itself a kind of madness, a sealing that mistakes its own neatness for the world. That this truth comes from Catherine, whose dream is its own seal, is the book’s point about the planks: they are not owned by the open characters and denied to the sealed ones. They surface everywhere, and a sealed woman can see clearly the exact seal she does not happen to be caught in.

Her sense of the self reaches the book’s furthest edge. She has “so many selves that she could never know them all”; people wear one another’s heads, turn into their opposites, catch themselves in the mirror as a stag. Not One Self here is not just many but unknowable in its multiplicity, a crowd too large for its own member to count. This is not presented as her breakdown but as a truth her unsealed perception gives her access to: the self genuinely is a multitude beyond full self-knowledge, and the demand that it resolve into one knowable identity is the same mad man’s dream of order she has just refused.

And the builder is built by the building. “But who was the dreamer?” the chapter asks; there is “the dream which had dreamed the dreamer.” This turns Reality Is Constructed inside out. The one who builds the world is also produced by it; Catherine dreams her dream and the dream dreams Catherine, with no builder standing safely outside the construction directing it. This is the deepest version of the plank: not a self who builds an invention, but a building and a builder each making the other, no bedrock self prior to the construction it lives in. The dreamer is inside the dream she is dreaming.

The chapter makes error essential rather than regrettable. A world “without mistakes, errors of consciousness, or an erroneous creation,” Catherine sees, would be “impoverished.” The flaw is not the failure of the creation but its richness; the erroneous, the incomplete, the mistaken, is where the life is. This is Unfinished Is Honest stated from inside the dream: the perfect, finished, error-free world is the poor one, and the botched, unfinished, erroneous one is the rich one. Catherine, who built a dream to be spared reality’s errors, here praises the error as the thing worth having, which is one more way the book refuses to let her be only her seal.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that dismisses Catherine’s praise of confusion as mere symptom of her disorder, and the reading that takes her embrace of chaos as its philosophy, ambiguity for its own sake. Neither the symptom nor the pure chaos is the point.

The footing is that Catherine, sealed in her dream, sees clearly that rigid order is “a mad man’s dream,” the seal she is not caught in, which is how the book shows the planks belong to no one figure. The self is a multitude too large to fully know, and that unknowability is its truth, not its failure. The builder is built by the building, the dreamer dreamed by the dream, with no bedrock self outside the construction. And error is essential, the flawed and unfinished world the rich one. Order is its own seal, the self exceeds self-knowledge, and the error is where the life is. Catherine remains both the sealed dreamer and the clear-eyed critic of sealing, and the chapter holds the two without choosing.

For Reflection

Can one see a seal clearly from inside a different one?

Is a self one can never fully know a problem to solve, or the truth to accept?

What in one’s life is richer for being erroneous, unfinished, or mistaken?
