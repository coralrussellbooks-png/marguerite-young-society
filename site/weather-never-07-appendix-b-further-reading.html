Appendix B: Further Reading

← Appendix A: The Five Frameworks at a Glance · The Weather You Were Never Allowed to Have

The Chorus Model

William James, The Principles of Psychology (1890). The foundational argument that the self is relational and multiple rather than fixed and singular. James's concept of the social self, that a person has as many social selves as there are individuals who recognize them, anticipates by a century the clinical and neuroscientific research that has been confirming it since. The argument that the self is shaped by context and audience rather than being a stable core that moves through the world unchanged is the philosophical ground this book builds from.

Richard Schwartz, No Bad Parts (2021). The most accessible entry point into Internal Family Systems for general readers. The core argument: psychological health is not the achievement of unity but the development of a relationship among the parts. The concept of the capital-S Self that can hear all the parts without being hijacked by any of them is the clinical grounding for the Chorus Model's conductor metaphor.

Richard Schwartz, Internal Family Systems Therapy (1995). The original clinical text for readers who want the full framework and its therapeutic applications. More technical in register than No Bad Parts but essential for understanding how the parts framework was developed and what it found in clinical practice across multiple populations, including the men who came in telling Schwartz there was not much to find.

Niobe Way, Deep Secrets (2011). The developmental research on male friendship and the specific way boys lose the capacity for intimate friendship as they enter adolescence and the framework's enforcement intensifies. Way's research demonstrates that the emotional unavailability of adult men is not natural but learned, and learned at a specific and documentable moment in development. This is the most important single text in the further reading for understanding the Chorus Model's specific application to men. Way's finding that boys at ten and eleven have intimate friendships that most have lost by sixteen and seventeen, not through natural development but through sustained enforcement, is the empirical foundation for the book's central claim that what was learned can be unlearned.

Dan McAdams, The Stories We Live By (1993). The narrative psychology argument that the self is not an object to be discovered but a story being constructed in real time. McAdams's research on the life narrative as the primary vehicle of identity provides the psychological grounding for understanding the plural self as a storytelling system rather than a fixed entity, and connects the Chorus Model directly to the Working Story framework.

Bessel van der Kolk, The Body Keeps the Score (2014). While primarily a trauma text, van der Kolk's work on how the body holds the history of the self provides essential context for understanding why the plural self is not a pathology but the ordinary condition that trauma and suppression make more visible. Particularly useful for understanding why the management runs in the background of the body rather than only in the conscious mind, and why the work of unmanagement is also physical work.

The Working Story

Shelley Taylor, Positive Illusions (1989). The research foundation for the Working Story framework. Taylor's decades of work on the slightly inflated self-assessments that mentally healthy people carry almost universally, and the measurable benefit those assessments produce in terms of resilience, persistence, and willingness to act toward uncertain futures, remains the most rigorous scientific argument for the adaptive function of the productive illusion. Her finding that the accurate self-assessment produces worse outcomes than the slightly inflated one is counterintuitive, well-documented, and directly relevant to men who have been taught by the framework that the accurate external assessment is the strong man's assessment. The original research appeared across multiple journal articles from the 1980s; the most cited is Taylor and Brown, Illusion and Well-Being: A Social Psychological Perspective on Mental Health, Psychological Bulletin 103(2), 1988.

Michael White and David Epston, Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends (1990). The foundational text of narrative therapy. White and Epston's argument that the self is a story being written in real time, shaped by dominant cultural narratives, and that the work is to help the person become the author of their own narrative rather than a character in someone else's, is the clinical grounding for the Working Story audit. The concept of unique outcomes, the moments when the person acts outside the dominant narrative, is particularly important for understanding how the working story changes: not through a single decision but through the accumulation of moments when the story briefly failed to determine behavior.

Michael White, Maps of Narrative Practice (2007). A more developed account of the narrative therapy framework, particularly useful for understanding the externalizing of the problem and the re-authoring conversation. The argument that the problem is not inside the person but in the story the person has been living inside, and that separating the person from the story is the precondition for changing it, is especially important for men whose framework has done an unusually thorough job of fusing the man with the story of the man.

Jeff Greenberg, Shelley Solomon, and Tom Pyszczynski, The Worm at the Core (2015). The accessible account of terror management theory for general readers. The argument that human culture is largely organized around the management of mortality anxiety, and that the stories we live by are doing existential as well as psychological work, provides the deepest context for understanding why the identity-from-work story is experienced as catastrophic rather than merely inconvenient when it stops working. The masculine identity story is not only a social construction: it is an existential buffer, and when it fails the mortality anxiety it was managing has no container. The foundational research appeared in Greenberg, Pyszczynski, and Solomon, The Causes and Consequences of a Need for Self-Esteem: A Terror Management Theory, in Baumeister, ed., Public Self and Private Self (1986).

The Climate of Loss

Elisabeth Kübler-Ross, On Death and Dying (1969). Read as the origin of the stage model and the source of its misapplication rather than as a prescription. Kübler-Ross observed rather than prescribed: understanding what she actually argued is the first step toward understanding how the culture misused it, and how two systems, the stage model and the masculine framework, converged on the same incorrect destination.

Dennis Klass, Phyllis Silverman, and Steven Nickman, eds., Continuing Bonds: New Understandings of Grief (1996). The foundational text of continuing bonds theory. The research collected here directly challenges the detachment model and establishes the ongoing relationship with what is lost as the normal and healthy form of grief rather than the pathological one. For men specifically, the finding that the men who successfully performed the detachment and returned to function on the expected timeline were frequently the men carrying the grief underground, unnamed and unacknowledged, is the most important single finding in this volume.

Pauline Boss, Ambiguous Loss (1999). The essential text for understanding the grief that has no clean object or endpoint. Boss's framework is the most useful clinical tool for the specific grief of the relationship that is still continuing, the self that was lost gradually, the loss that has no funeral. For men doing this work, the most relevant form of ambiguous loss is the self the framework required: the man who did not need anything, who had it figured out, who did not have weather. That man was always a performance, and the leaving of the performance involves a grief that has no clean object because the thing being left was never a discrete thing.

Pauline Boss, The Myth of Closure (2021). Boss's more recent and more accessible work on why the cultural demand for closure is itself a form of harm. Useful for readers who have been told they have not grieved correctly, and for understanding why the masculine framework's demand for the handled grief, the grief that resolves on a timeline, is working against the process rather than supporting it.

Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut, The Dual Process Model of Coping with Bereavement (1999). The oscillation model: the research that establishes healthy grief as movement between loss-orientation and restoration-orientation rather than linear progression toward a fixed endpoint. Available as a journal article rather than a book; worth finding for readers who want the full research argument. The finding that the masculine framework rewards only the restoration-orientation while providing almost no tolerance for the loss-orientation means that men following the framework's instructions have been doing half of what grief requires and suppressing the other half.

Terry Real, I Don't Want to Talk About It (1997). The foundational clinical text on male depression and the specific way the dominant masculinity framework routes depression into anger, withdrawal, and the performance of invulnerability. Real's concept of covert depression, the depression that does not present as depression because the framework has given it no language and no permission, is the most useful single clinical framework for understanding what the foreclosure of the plural self costs men specifically in the domain of grief. Real's clinical finding that the treatment for covert depression is not the management of the symptoms but the naming of what is actually there connects directly to the Climate of Loss chapter's central argument: the naming is the beginning of the literacy. See also under On Masculinity and Not One Self.

The Standing Still

Daniel Siegel, The Developing Mind (1999). The foundational neuroscience text for the window of tolerance concept. Siegel's interpersonal neurobiology provides the scientific grounding for understanding why the witness position is not a spiritual achievement but a neurological capacity that can be developed and maintained. The finding that the regulated nervous system of one person in a relationship can widen the window of tolerance of the other is the neurological foundation for the book's argument that the interior work is not only personal: the man who widens his own window widens the windows of the rooms he inhabits.

Daniel Siegel, Mindsight (2010). The more accessible account of Siegel's framework for general readers. The clearest explanation of how the observer self functions neurologically and why the development of self as context rather than self as content is the foundation of psychological flexibility. More useful than the clinical text for readers who want the framework without the therapeutic apparatus.

Steven Hayes, A Liberated Mind (2019). The accessible account of Acceptance and Commitment Therapy for general readers. More useful than the clinical text for the purposes of this book. Hayes's distinction between self as content and self as context, and the defusion practices that create distance between the self and the thought, provide the most practically useful single tool for the man who has been trained by the framework to be his positions, his reactions, his performance. The defusion practice, the simple act of noticing I am having the thought that rather than simply being inside the thought, is the reclaiming of the space between the experience and the response that the feed and the framework together work to eliminate.

Steven Hayes, Kirk Strosahl, and Kelly Wilson, Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (1999). The clinical foundation of ACT for readers who want the full framework and its research base.

Jonathan Shay, Achilles in Vietnam (1994). The foundational text on moral injury. Shay's argument that the specific psychological damage of betrayed moral expectation is distinct from trauma and requires distinct responses is essential for understanding the standing still under sustained pressure. The extension of moral injury to the interior scale, the moral injury of the man who has spent decades knowing that the singular self the framework required was not an honest account of what he was and who was required to perform it anyway, is not Shay's application but follows directly from his framework.

Jonathan Shay, Odysseus in America (2002). Shay's extension of the moral injury framework to the reintegration of veterans and, by extension, to anyone navigating the return from sustained moral exposure to a world that has not shared the experience. Useful for understanding the specific isolation of the man who has done the interior work inside a context that has no framework for it.

Peter Fonagy, Gyorgy Gergely, Elliot Jurist, and Mary Target, Affect Regulation, Mentalization, and the Development of the Self (2002). The technical foundation of mentalization theory and affect regulation research. The distinction between emotional suppression, emotional flooding, and emotional integration, the below-the-window state, the above-the-window state, and the window itself, is the scientific grounding for the Standing Still chapter's central argument: the goal is not smaller feelings but a wider window. Integration is not achieved by managing the feelings into an acceptable size. It is achieved by widening the window until the full size of the feeling can be held inside it without the system going above or below. Technical in register; essential for readers who want the full scientific argument for the difference between the framework's preferred state and the witness position.

The Reef Method

Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun, Trauma and Transformation (1995). The foundational research on post-traumatic growth. Tedeschi and Calhoun's careful documentation of the measurable development that occurs through sustained engagement with significant loss and rupture provides the scientific grounding for the reef's core claim: that the wound is the material, not only the damage. The research is careful about the limits: the cost was real and the growth was also real and neither cancels the other.

Richard Tedeschi, Jane Shakespeare-Finch, Kanako Taku, and Lawrence Calhoun, Posttraumatic Growth: Theory, Research, and Applications (2018). The most comprehensive account of the post-traumatic growth research for readers who want the full scientific argument. The finding specific to men, that men who engage with the grief and the wound and the return show higher rates of measurable post-traumatic growth than men who follow the framework's instructions of detachment and return to function, is the single most important research finding for this book's argument about the reef. The framework's prescription is not only costly. It is actively working against the development it claims to support.

Clare Graves, synthesized in Don Beck and Christopher Cowan, Spiral Dynamics (1996). The developmental psychology argument that growth moves in spirals rather than lines, revisiting earlier territory from new vantage points. The most accessible account of Graves's research and its implications for understanding the nonlinear shape of a human life. The finding that the return to familiar territory is the development rather than the regression, that the territory encountered from a higher level of complexity is experienced differently than the same territory encountered from an earlier level, is the developmental science foundation for the reef's argument about the return as layer rather than regression.

Esther Thelen and Linda Smith, A Dynamic Systems Approach to the Development of Cognition and Action (1994). The complexity theory foundation for understanding human development as iterative rather than programmatic. The argument that self-organizing systems find the form that the specific conditions of the specific life require, rather than the form the template predicts, is the scientific grounding for reading the career that moved sideways and the relational pattern that appeared across multiple relationships not as failures of the template but as a self-organizing system doing its actual work. Technical in register but the central finding is accessible: the template was always wrong about the territory.

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, Creativity: Flow and the Psychology of Discovery and Invention (1996). The most relevant synthesis of creativity research for understanding the relationship between sustained constraint and creative breakthrough. The chapters on the incubation phase and the conditions for creative breakthrough document what the reef's third element, constraint as pressure, is building toward: the creative system under sustained pressure accumulates potential energy, and the longer and more sustained the constraint, the more total the breakthrough when the constraint releases. The man who has been performing the singular self for decades is not failing to break through. He is accumulating the structure the breakthrough needs in order to be total rather than partial.

On Masculinity and Not One Self

Terry Real, I Don't Want to Talk About It (1997). See above under Climate of Loss for the covert depression material. Also essential here for the broader account of how the masculine framework specifically forecloses the plural self: the routing of interior states into the indirect channels the framework permits, the specific way the management accumulates cost over decades, and the clinical finding that the treatment begins with the naming. Real's work is the most clinically grounded single account of what this book describes in philosophical and psychological terms.

Terry Real, Us (2022). Real's more recent work on relational health and the specific relational costs of the masculine framework. Useful for Chapter Eight and for understanding what the differentiated presence requires of the partnership or marriage that was built around the man's emotional unavailability.

bell hooks, The Will to Change: Men, Masculinity, and Love (2004). The most precise account of what the dominant masculinity framework costs men from the outside. hooks argues that patriarchy damages men as surely as it damages women, differently and less visibly, and that the liberation of men from the framework is not separate from but continuous with the liberation of everyone else from it. The argument that men are taught to be emotionally dishonest and that this emotional dishonesty is the foundation of the damage they do to themselves and others is the political and ethical grounding for the book's central claim.

Michael Kimmel, Guyland (2008). The sociological account of how the dominant masculinity framework is transmitted and enforced among young men, with particular attention to the peer enforcement mechanisms that do the most damage. Useful context for understanding the specific developmental window Niobe Way describes, and for understanding the specific precarity that makes false explanations so effective with young men who have experienced the gap between what the framework promised and what they actually found.

Niobe Way, Deep Secrets (2011). See above under Chorus Model. Listed again here because it is the most important single text for this book's specific argument: the emotional unavailability of adult men is not natural, it was learned, it was learned at a specific and documentable moment in development, and it can be unlearned.

Relational Foundations

John Bowlby, A Secure Base (1988). The most accessible single volume of Bowlby's attachment theory for general readers. The foundational argument that the self develops in relationship from the beginning, that the relational field is the medium in which the self develops rather than the context the developed self enters, provides the relational grounding for understanding why the interior work of the five frameworks is also always relational work. The chorus was shaped in relationship. The unmanagement happens in relationship. The witness makes the honesty possible in relationship.

Murray Bowen, Family Therapy in Clinical Practice (1978). The foundational text of Bowen family systems theory. Bowen's concept of differentiation, the capacity to maintain your own functioning and values while remaining in emotional contact with people who are important to you, is the most useful single clinical concept for understanding what the plural self requires of its relationships. The distinction between fusion, cutoff, and the differentiated position, the third option that is harder than either of the first two and more consistent with the plural self framework, is the theoretical foundation for Chapter Eight's account of the specific relational work the interior work requires.
