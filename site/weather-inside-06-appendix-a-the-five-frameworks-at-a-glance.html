Appendix A: The Five Frameworks at a Glance

Part of The Weather Inside, on the psychology in the work of Marguerite Young (1908 to 1995).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Author's Note · The Weather Inside · Appendix B: Further Reading →

Framework One: The Chorus Model

The core reframe: You are not one person. You are a chorus. The goal is not to find your true self but to become a better host to all the selves you carry.

The lie it replaces: There is a unified, authentic you buried beneath the confusion. The work is to excavate it.

The Chorus Model: You are multiple voices, different registers, different histories, all of them yours, none of them the whole of you. The question is not which one is real. They are all real. The question is who is conducting.

The three practices: Name three selves you have inhabited in the last year. Give each one a name that is not yours. Write down what each one wanted without editing or justifying. Which part of you is most in charge right now? How long has it been running things? What is it protecting you from? One sentence for each answer. Write a letter from one of your older selves to the one you are now. Not the self you hope to become. A self you have already been. Read it once. Do not revise it.

Framework Two: The Working Story

The core reframe: The stories you tell to survive are not delusions. They are tools. The question is not whether you live by illusion but whether your illusions are working for you.

The lie it replaces: Mental health means seeing things as they truly are. Hope, fantasy, and protective story-making are symptoms to be rooted out.

The Working Story: Not is this story true but is this story working. A Working Story is one that expands your capacity to act, love, and endure. A story that has stopped working is one that contracts you. The practice is learning to audit your stories rather than accept or reject them wholesale.

The three practices: Write down a story you are currently living by in its raw form. Ask: is this story expanding me or contracting me? What does the body say before the mind answers? Name a story you inherited. Where did it come from? What did the person who handed it to you need you to believe? Write one honest sentence about what it would cost to put it down and one honest sentence about what it is already costing you to keep carrying it. Identify a story that stopped working. Ask: who was this story working for? Then write: I carried this story as long as it was useful. It is not useful anymore. I am putting it down.

Framework Three: The Climate of Loss

The core reframe: Grief is not a stage you pass through on the way to resolution. It is the weather of a life fully lived. You do not heal from it. You learn to dress for it.

The lie it replaces: Grief moves in a linear sequence toward acceptance as endpoint. If it keeps returning, you are doing it wrong.

The Climate of Loss: Grief as permanent climate does not mean permanent devastation. It means grief changes form, intensifies seasonally, recedes without warning, and returns. You are not failing when it returns. You are experiencing weather. The practice is building grief literacy: learning to read your own patterns.

The three practices: Name a loss in your life that has no clean endpoint. Write it down without trying to resolve it in the naming. Sit with what you have written for one full minute before doing anything else with it. Map one season of your grief climate. What are the early signals? What makes it worse? What makes it survivable? You are building the map. The map does not stop the weather. It tells you where you are when the weather arrives. Identify who or what you are still in relationship with even though the form of that relationship has changed entirely. Write one paragraph about the current form of the relationship. Call it what it is: the continuing bond. The relationship persisting in the form available. This is not failure. This is grief doing what grief actually does.

Framework Four: The Standing Still

The core reframe: Seeing clearly is not the same as detachment. The witness is not the person who doesn't feel things. The witness is the part of you that can feel everything and still not be destroyed by it.

The lie it replaces: The goal of mindfulness is to feel neutral, to observe without being affected, to achieve equanimity as a permanent state.

The Standing Still: The witness is not someone who stands outside experience. The witness is someone who can stand still inside it. The Standing Still is a practice and a capacity: the ability to be fully present with what is happening without requiring it to be different before you can engage with it.

The three practices: Think of the last time you lost the witness position completely. What were the early signals you missed? Write them down. You are not reconstructing this to blame yourself. You are building the map of your own window. Practice the return right now. Find something you have been carrying that has narrowed your window. Let yourself feel the full weight of it for thirty seconds. Find the breath. Notice: I am feeling the weight of this. I am not only this weight. Write one sentence about what the return feels like in the body. Identify one place in your current life where you are requiring the experience to be different before you can be fully present to it. Write: I am here. Not when it is better. Now. In this. With what is actually here. Notice what shifts in the body when you say it without the condition attached.

Framework Five: The Reef Method

The core reframe: You do not build your life in a straight line. You build it the way a coral reef builds: layer by layer, returning to the same places, adding to what was already there. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is finished.

The lie it replaces: A good life moves forward without regression. Growth is directional. Returning to old territory means you have failed.

The Reef Method: Every return to old territory is a new layer on the structure you are building. The Reef Method replaces the metaphor of the journey, which implies a destination, with the metaphor of the reef, which implies accumulation, depth, and the necessity of return. You are not going somewhere. You are becoming more of what you already are.

The three practices: Do the backward read. Find five moments you have previously understood as setbacks or failures. For each one write one sentence that begins: this added to the structure by. What do you see when you read them as layers rather than failures? Identify the return you are currently in. Write down: what am I bringing to this return that I did not bring before? Write one paragraph about the difference between who you were in the last visit to this territory and who you are in this one. That difference is the layer. Identify the witness in your life. The person or community that has demonstrated it can hold what comes after: this hurts me to say because I don't even know if it's valid but it's how I feel. If you have that person, name one thing you have not yet said in their presence that the reef needs you to say. If you do not yet have that person, name one specific place you could look.
