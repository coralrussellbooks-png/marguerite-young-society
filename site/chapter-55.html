Summary

The twins had opposite mottos: Peron's "carpe diem," seize the day, and Joachim's "hoc opus, hic labor est," this is the work, this the labor. Joachim had planned a quiet life of music and stamp collecting in their twin house of mirrored rooms. Peron borrowed, forged his brother's checks, and left debts. When Peron died, Joachim paid for the funeral and the debts, went bankrupt, and lost the house. He now lives in a beachcomber's shack, still wondering whose funeral it was.

Key passages

From the research

  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates how he might have lived had his brother died earlier. It describes his orderly, carefully planned life dedicated to music, philately, and collecting rare artifacts. It shows his desire for certainty, order, and a calm pursuit of immortality through art, contrasting with the chaos brought by his brother. This sets up the fundamental tension between the brothers’ lives and personalities.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s conservative financial choices are described in detail. This symbolizes his attempt to secure an immutable future for his music and life, despite the apparent futility, reflecting his cautious and meticulous nature opposed to his brother’s recklessness.
  • The contrast between Joachim (Mr. Spitzer) and Peron are deeply explored as to their opposing characters. Joachim is altruistic, restrained, careful, and Peron was egotistical, reckless, and dismissive of Joachim’s order and sacrifices. It highlights Peron’s irresponsibility, including borrowing and forging checks in Joachim’s name, which caused Joachim’s financial ruin. The passage reveals Joachim’s feelings of invisibility, neglect, and resentment toward Peron, who never acknowledged or appreciated him.
  • The detailed description of the house, its symmetrical twin halves, and the doubled portraits and artifacts symbolize the intertwined yet opposed lives of the brothers. The house, with its precise architectural layout and mirrored rooms, reflects Joachim’s desire for balance and control, while Peron’s disregard for these arrangements symbolizes chaos and disruption. This metaphor extends to their personalities and fates.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s impaired perception and mental state is described as well as his physical and mental fragility. His difficulty distinguishing reality from hallucination or dream highlights his profound grief and alienation. His inability to fully “awaken” or “sleep” reflects his liminal, haunted existence after his brother’s death.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s wandering through memories and dreams, confusion over past and present, and his search for meaning in his fragmented life are poignantly rendered as an existential reflection on time, memory, and identity. He struggles with questions of identity, the passage of time as a fluid concept, and the absence of harmony connecting his life’s beginning and end. His experience of living “only at the end of time” and being lost between reality and illusion underscores the theme of existential disorientation.
  • The image of Mr. Spitzer unknowingly traveling in the wrong carriage, as the wrong passenger, driven by a skeletal coachman through unfamiliar landscapes, symbolizes his loss of control and direction in life. This haunting metaphor emphasizes his disconnection from reality and his brother’s lingering presence or shadow in his life.
  • The chapter contrasts Peron’s carefree motto “carpe diem” (seize the day) with Joachim’s solemn motto “hoc opus, hic labor est” (this is the work, this is the labor). This highlights their fundamental differences between Peron’s reckless enjoyment of the moment versus Joachim’s dedication to a difficult, serious artistic mission. It underscores the tragedy of their opposing paths and the sacrifices Joachim made for a legacy unappreciated by Peron.
  • The closing passage reflects Mr. Spitzer’s sense of isolation, the fading of the twin house and the life he once had, his confusion about his own identity, and his unresolved mourning for Peron who never mourned him. It is a reflection on loss, survival, and unresolved grief. It also evokes the futility and purposelessness he feels, despite his efforts to maintain order and shelter from life’s storms. The passage is a powerful summation of the chapter’s themes of loss, memory, and the fragile persistence of life

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer (Joachim)

  • A man of order, methodical, cautious, and devoted to a grand musical project he calls his “opus” or “dirge.”
  • Represents stability, intellectual rigor, and a desire for certainty in an uncertain world.
  • Lives a life of sacrifice, burdened by his brother’s recklessness and debts.
  • Suffers from physical and mental impairments, including distorted vision and a fragile body, symbolic of his fragile mental state.
  • Haunted by his brother’s absence and the loss of a shared past, struggling with identity and reality.
  • Symbolizes the rational, structured side of human nature, trying to impose order on chaos.

Peron (Mr. Spitzer’s Brother)

  • A gambler, extrovert, and embodiment of chaos, risk, and unpredictability.
  • Lives a life of abandon, irresponsibility, and indifference to consequence.
  • His death leaves Joachim to bear the burdens and debts he left behind.
  • Represents the impulsive, irrational, and destructive forces in life, contrasting sharply with Joachim’s order.
  • His laughter and ghostly presence continue to haunt Joachim.

  • Wealth, like many other things in the story, is portrayed as a form of illusion or "counterfeit," reflecting the transient and unstable aspects of life and human experience that the characters grapple with.The history of money is a journey from direct barter to symbolic representations of value, including commodity items, metal coins, paper notes, and modern digital currencies. This evolution was driven by the need for a more efficient and reliable medium of exchange as societies grew more complex. For most of early human history, transactions were based on barter or the direct exchange of goods and services. Early societies began to use easily tradable items with inherent value, known as commodity money. Around 9000– 6000 B.C., cattle, sheep, and camels were among the first forms of money. They were both a medium of exchange and a symbol of wealth. From around 1200 B.C., cowrie shells became the most widely and longest-used currency in history, particularly in Asia and Africa. Their durability, size, and scarcity made them a popular choice. Salt, grain, and even whale teeth were used as currency in various cultures. The use of precious metals marked a significant step in money's history, offering a more durable and standardized medium of exchange. As early as the 3rd millennium B.C. in Egypt and Mesopotamia, standardized weights of gold, silver, and other metals were used for trade. The Lydian kingdom (in modern-day Turkey) is widely credited with issuing the first standardized and officially stamped coins around 650–600 B.C.. Made from electrum (a gold and silver alloy), these coins featured a royal symbol that guaranteed their weight and value. Around the same time, Chinese mints produced metal coins in the shape of spades and knives. The invention of paper money was a solution to the inconvenience of carrying heavy metal coins for large transactions. Chinese merchants would deposit their metal coins with goldsmiths and receive a paper receipt, which they then used for purchases. This early system was known as "flying money". During the Song Dynasty (1020s), the Chinese government took control of the system, issuing the first official paper currency. Inspired by Marco Polo's accounts of Chinese paper money, Europeans began adopting it in the 17th century. Sweden was the first to introduce European-style banknotes in 1661. The formal backing of currency evolved from commodities to government decree. To control inflation and ensure stability, governments in the 19th and early 20th centuries adopted the gold standard, where they issued paper money redeemable for a fixed amount of gold. After World War II, many countries pegged their currencies to the US dollar, which was, in turn, fixed to gold. In the 1970s, this system was abandoned, and money became a fiat currency. Its value is not derived from a physical commodity but from a government decree and public trust in the issuing authority. The rapid evolution of technology has pushed money into the digital realm, from traditional systems to decentralized networks. Starting in the 1950s, modern credit cards allowed for cashless purchases, with the first successful general-purpose card being the Diners Club Card in 1950.
  • The first minting facility, or mint, was likely established by the Lydians in the 7th century BC for coining precious metals, a practice that spread to Greece, Rome, and beyond, with modern countries often operating one national mint. Coinage spread from Lydia to the Aegean Islands, Italy, Persia, and India, with Romans developing modern minting techniques by the 4th century BC. The first national mint in the United States was established by the Coinage Act of 1792 in Philadelphia.
  • Counterfeit money is currency produced outside of the legal sanction of a state or government, usually in a deliberate attempt to imitate that currency and so as to deceive its recipient. Producing or using counterfeit money is a form of fraud or forgery, and is illegal in all jurisdictions of the world. The business of counterfeiting money is nearly as old as money itself: plated copies (known as Fourrées) have been found of Lydian coins, which are thought to be among the first Western coins. Before the introduction of paper money, the most prevalent method of counterfeiting involved mixing base metals with pure gold or silver. During World War II, the Nazis forged British pounds and American dollars. Today, some of the finest counterfeit banknotes are called Superdollars because of their high quality and imitation of the real US dollar. There has been significant counterfeiting of Euro banknotes and coins since the launch of the currency in 2002, but considerably less than that of the US dollar. Some of the ill-effects that counterfeit money has on society include a reduction in the value of real money; an increase in prices (inflation) as a result of an increase in money being circulated in the economy; a decrease in the acceptability of paper money; and losses, when traders are not reimbursed for counterfeit money detected by banks, even if it is confiscated. Traditionally, anti-counterfeiting measures involved including fine detail with raised intaglio printing on bills which allows non-experts to easily spot forgeries. On coins, milled or reeded (marked with parallel grooves) edges are used to show that none of the valuable metal has been scraped off.

  • “Ideas on Taxation” There are many ideas and proposals for reforming the US tax system, ranging from modifications to the current income tax to radical changes like implementing consumption or wealth taxes. The ideas generally center on different goals, such as simplifying the tax code, improving economic growth, or addressing wealth inequality. Income tax reform includes many proposals that modify the current system of progressive income taxation. Some proposals suggest lowering tax rates across the board while simultaneously eliminating various tax credits, deductions, and exemptions. This would broaden the portion of income that is taxable. Other plans suggest making the tax system more progressive. Ideas include raising marginal rates for high-income earners, increasing the tax on capital gains and dividends to match ordinary income rates, and eliminating the cap on the amount of income subject to Social Security payroll taxes. Some proposals suggest simplifying or eliminating the AMT (Alternative Minimum Tax), which was originally created to prevent high-income individuals from avoiding income tax entirely but has become increasingly complex. A flat tax applies a single tax rate to all income levels. Consumption taxes are levied on spending rather than earning. This would tax all retail sales and could replace the federal income tax. Some proposals include a rebate for low-income households to offset the regressive nature of a sales tax. Value-added tax (VAT) is a tax on a product's value at each stage of production. The US is the only developed country without a national VAT, which tends to have lower administrative and compliance costs than income taxes. Consumed income tax would tax consumption but exempt savings and investments until they are spent. A wealth tax imposes an annual levy on the net worth of the wealthiest households. A carbon tax places a fee on carbon emissions, typically on fossil fuels at the point of extraction. The goal is to internalize the cost of pollution and disincentivize carbon-intensive activities, thereby reducing greenhouse gas emissions. Revenues could be used for various purposes, including being returned to citizens as rebates, funding green infrastructure, or lowering other taxes. A "carbon dividend" or rebate can be used to offset the higher costs for lower-income households. Universal Basic Income (UBI) and tax reform would provide a regular, unconditional income to all adults. To pay for it, it is often paired with a simpler, broader tax system.
  • “Gilts and estates” are distinct concepts from the United Kingdom and the United States, respectively, that intersect in the context of inheritance and investment. In the UK, a "gilt" is a government-issued bond that can be an asset within a person's "estate" (all of their property and debts) upon their death. A gilt is a high-security bond issued by the UK government, representing a loan from an investor to the government. The term "gilt-edged" reflects the perception of safety, as the UK government has never failed to make interest or principal payments on time. Gilts can be conventional, paying a fixed interest rate, or index-linked, where payments are adjusted for inflation. Gilts are treated like other investments and are included in a deceased person's estate, valued at their market price at the time of death, for inheritance tax purposes. An estate refers to all of an individual's assets and liabilities at the time of their death, including real estate, bank accounts, and investments. The estate's administration, including the distribution of assets, is governed by estate law. Individuals use wills and trusts to outline how their estate should be managed and distributed. This is the legal process of proving a will's validity and administering the estate under court supervision. Estate law is a complex field that varies by state. If someone dies without a will (intestate), state laws determine how their assets are distributed.

  • “New England Mortality Rates” Historically, New England mortality rates were characterized by significant changes over time, from periods of exceptionally high colonial-era life expectancy to high urban death rates in the 19th century, before undergoing a sharp decline in the early 20th century. Contrary to popular belief, 17th-century New England colonists enjoyed a long life expectancy, averaging 65 to 70 years. This was 15 to 20 years longer than in England at the time and was attributed to the healthy climate and spaced-out communities. Studies of early New England towns show surprisingly high child survival rates. For example, in Andover, Massachusetts, between 1640 and 1669, 87% to 91% of children survived to age 20. These long lifespans were frequently interrupted by devastating epidemics, such as outbreaks of smallpox, measles, and influenza. Because of low population density and less frequent exposure, community immunity would wane, leading to cyclic, highly destructive epidemics when a new disease was introduced. Europeans brought diseases like smallpox, measles, and cholera, to which Native Americans had no immunity. These outbreaks killed up to 90% of the Indigenous population in parts of New England. While overall U.S. mortality was low compared to Western Europe, urban areas saw a significant "mortality penalty". By the early 1900s, the life expectancy for white people in New England's urban areas was 46 years, compared to 54.7 years in rural areas. As towns grew, infectious diseases spread rapidly due to poor sanitation and crowded living conditions. As the leading cause of death in the 19th century, tuberculosis was a scourge in New England, affecting multiple members and generations of families. Diphtheria, scarlet fever, and whooping cough were major threats to children, contributing to high infant and child mortality rates. Cholera and typhoid fever also emerged as epidemic threats, particularly impacting port cities like Boston. The early 20th century saw a dramatic decline in mortality, largely due to public health measures and improved living standards, rather than new medical interventions.

The strings can be made of different materials (or thicknesses) and all be tuned to the same pitch, or identical strings can be tuned to different pitches. Besides being the only string instrument played solely by the wind, the Aeolian harp is also the only string instrument that plays solely harmonic frequencies. They are recognizable by the sound which is a result of this property, which has been described as eerie and ethereal. The Aeolian harp, already known in the ancient world, was first described by Athanasius Kircher (1602– 1680) in his books. It became popular as a household instrument during the Romantic era, and Aeolian harps are still hand-crafted today. Some are now made in the form of monumental metal sound sculptures located on the roofs of buildings and windy hilltops.
  • “sub specie aeternitatis, under the aspect of eternity, in its essential or universal form” This reflects a philosophical viewpoint regarding the nature of existence and identity beyond the limitations of time and individuality. This Latin phrase, traditionally used by philosophers like Spinoza, means to consider something "under the aspect of eternity," i.e., from a timeless, eternal perspective rather than a temporal or limited one. In the context of the chapter, it suggests looking at the self or existence not just as a particular individual bound by time, but as part of a universal, timeless whole. Mr. Spitzer is reflecting on his own identity and existence, recognizing that he is more than the sum of his temporal experiences. He contemplates that he may have been "retailored" or transformed in ways that transcend ordinary perception, and that his "essential or universal form" is something beyond his individual, physical self. The passage conveys his awareness of the fluidity and multiplicity of identity and existence, moving beyond the confines of linear time and fixed boundaries. It means considering oneself or one’s being from the perspective of eternity, seeing beyond the limited, changing self to a more fundamental, universal essence that is not confined by time or space.
  • It is an honorific expression denoting what is considered to be universally and eternally true, without any reference to or dependence upon temporal facets of reality. The Latin phrase can be rendered in English as "from the perspective of the eternal". More loosely, it is commonly used to refer to an objective (or theoretically possible alternative) point of view. Spinoza's "eternal" perspective is reflected in his Ethics (Part V, Prop. XXIII, Scholium), where he applies Euclid's method (with the use of geometry) to philosophical inquiry, starting with God and nature, before moving to human emotions and the human intellect to reach an understanding of moral philosophy. By proceeding sub specie aeternitatis, Spinoza sought to arrive at an ethical theory that is as precise as Euclid's Elements. In the history of philosophy, this way of proceeding may be contrasted with Aristotle's. Aristotle's methodological differentiations in his "philosophy of human affairs" and his natural philosophy are grounded in the distinction between what is "better known to us" and things "better known in themselves", or what is "first for us" and what is "first by nature", a distinction that is deliberately discarded by Spinoza, and also by other modern philosophers.
  • “Newport polka” refers to several 19th-century musical compositions, notably Carl Bergmann's "Midday Polka" and "Fillmore House Polka," which celebrated Newport, Rhode Island's social scene. These are notated instrumental pieces, with the "Midday Polka" from 1854 and the "Fillmore House Polka" from 1856 dedicated to the grand hotels of the city. The term can also refer to notated music sheets or other polka compositions from or related to Newport.
  • “schottische” is a 19th-century German partnered country dance, similar to a slower polka, that involves sidesteps and a slow turn, or sometimes a hop. Also known as the "Scottish dance," it originated in Bohemia and became a popular European and American folk dance, influencing various regional styles and music like Argentina's chotis and Finland's jenkka. It is typically performed to music in 4/4 time and is still a part of folk dance traditions worldwide.
  • “polka” is a lively, 19th-century Bohemian (Czech) dance and dance music genre characterized by couples dancing to music in 2/4 time with a basic pattern of quick steps and a hop. Popularized across Europe and the Americas, the name likely derives from the Czech word "půlka," meaning "half-step," though it has also been mistakenly linked to Polish women due to the similar-sounding Czech word.
  • “minuet” is a social dance of French origin for two people, usually written in 3/4 time. The English word was adapted from the Italian minuetto and the French minuet. The term also describes the musical form that accompanies the dance, which subsequently developed more fully, often with a longer musical form called the minuet and trio, and was much used as a movement in the early classical symphony. While often stylized in instrumental forms, composers of the period would have been familiar with the popular dance.
  • “mother-of-pearl Venus comb” is a species of large predatory sea snail, a marine gastropod mollusk in the family Muricidae, the rock snails or murex snails. The shell of this Indo-Pacific species has a very long siphonal canal and numerous spines. Perfect specimens of the shell are not easily found because of the fragility of the numerous long spines.
  • “Mozart” Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart was a prolific and influential composer of the Classical period. Despite his short life, his rapid pace of composition and proficiency from an early age resulted in more than 800 works representing virtually every Western classical genre of his time. Many of these compositions are acknowledged as pinnacles of the symphonic, concertante, chamber, operatic, and choral repertoires. Mozart is widely regarded as one of the greatest composers in the history of Western music, with his music admired for its "melodic beauty, its formal elegance and its richness of harmony and texture". Born in Salzburg, Mozart showed prodigious ability from his earliest childhood. At age five, he was already competent on keyboard and violin, had begun to compose, and performed before European royalty. His father, Leopold Mozart, took him on a grand tour of Europe and then three trips to Italy. At 17, he was a musician at the Salzburg court but grew restless and travelled in search of a better position. Mozart's search for employment led to positions in Paris, Mannheim, Munich, and again in Salzburg, during which he wrote his five violin concertos, Sinfonia Concertante, and Concerto for Flute and Harp, as well as sacred pieces and masses, the motet Exsultate Jubilate, and the opera Idomeneo, among other works. While visiting Vienna in 1781, Mozart was dismissed from his Salzburg position. He stayed in Vienna, where he achieved fame but little financial security. During Mozart's early years in Vienna, he produced several notable works, such as the opera Die Entführung aus dem Serail, the Great Mass in C minor, the "Haydn" Quartets and a number of symphonies. Throughout his Vienna years, Mozart composed over a dozen piano concertos, many considered some of his greatest achievements. In the final years of his life, Mozart wrote many of his best-known works, including his last three symphonies, culminating in the Jupiter Symphony, the serenade Eine kleine Nachtmusik, his Clarinet Concerto, the operas The Marriage of Figaro, Don Giovanni, Così fan tutte and The Magic Flute and his Requiem. The Requiem was largely unfinished at the time of his death at age 35, the circumstances of which are uncertain and much mythologised. His elder sister, Maria Anna Walburga Ignatia "Marianne" Mozart, nicknamed Nannerl, was a highly regarded musician from Salzburg, Austria. In her childhood, she developed into an outstanding keyboard player under the tutelage of her father Leopold. She became a celebrated child prodigy and went on concert tours through much of Europe with her parents and her younger brother Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart. At age 17, her career as a touring musician came to an end, though she continued to work at home teaching piano and performing on occasion. At age 33 she married, moved to a village six hours by carriage from Salzburg, and there raised her own and her husband's children. On her widowhood in 1801, she returned to Salzburg and resumed teaching and performance. She is known to have composed works of music, though no manuscripts survive. In her later years she contributed to the biographical study of her late brother.
  • “hieroglyph” a stylized picture of an object representing a word, syllable, or sound, as found in ancient Egyptian and other writing systems.
  • “siste viator” is a Latin phrase meaning "Stop, traveler," commonly used as an inscription on Roman roadside tombs. The phrase itself is an epitaph, a warning to passersby, encouraging them to pause and consider mortality and their own inevitable end. It invokes themes of mortality, transience, and the universality of death, a common motif in Roman funerary practices.
  • “morceau” is a short literary or musical composition.
  • “alack and a day” is an archaic interjection used to express sorrow, regret, grief, or a sense of misfortune, essentially meaning "woe to the day!" or "shame to the day!" the event occurred. It's a longer form of the word "alack," which itself functions similarly to "alas".
  • “playing the bones” can have several meanings, depending on the context. It could refer to using a musical instrument called the "bones", or it could be a slang term related to gambling, mob culture, or even sexual activity.
  • “parimutual” is a betting system in which all bets of a particular type are placed together in a pool; taxes and the house-take, or vigorish, are deducted, and payoff odds are calculated by sharing the pool among all winning bets. In some countries it is known as the tote after the totalisator, which calculates and displays bets already made. The parimutuel system is used in gambling on horse racing, greyhound racing, jai alai, and other sporting events of relatively short duration in which participants finish in a ranked order. A modified parimutuel system is also used in some lottery games. Parimutuel betting differs from fixed-odds betting in that the final payout is not determined until the pool is closed, in fixed-odds betting, the payout is agreed at the time the bet is made. Parimutuel gambling is frequently state-regulated, and it is offered in many places where gambling is otherwise illegal. Parimutuel gambling is often also offered at off-track facilities, where players may bet on the events without actually being present to observe them in person.
