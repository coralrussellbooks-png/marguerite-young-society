To All My Darlings

Chapter 55

On Chapter 55 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 12, 2026

← Chapter 54 · Home · Chapter 56 →

Content note: This chapter mentions the suicide of Mr. Spitzer’s twin brother, Peron.

Summary

Mr. Spitzer imagines the life he would have led if his brother had died first: his theoretical music, his butterflies and postage stamps, safe investments in sunken ships and dead Russian bonds, and a tenth symphony that would begin where other composers had ended their lives. He remembers the twin house he designed so that each brother had his half, every portrait, garden, and mirror repeated, and how Peron mocked it, moved the furniture, forged Joachim’s checks, and never did him a single favor. Since Peron’s death his eyes see double, the small things large and the great things small; he sleeps with his eyes open and wakes in the wrong carriage, the wrong passenger behind a skeleton coachman. Peron lived by carpe diem, Joachim by hoc opus, hic labor est. The walls of the twin house have been knocked down, and Mr. Spitzer, broken by the funeral and its debts, lives in an old beach-comber’s house, “still wondering whose funeral it was.”

Key passages

From the research

  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates how he might have lived had his brother died earlier. It describes his orderly, carefully planned life dedicated to music, philately, and collecting rare artifacts. It shows his desire for certainty, order, and a calm pursuit of immortality through art, contrasting with the chaos brought by his brother. This sets up the fundamental tension between the brothers’ lives and personalities.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s conservative financial choices are described in detail. This symbolizes his attempt to secure an immutable future for his music and life, despite the apparent futility, reflecting his cautious and meticulous nature opposed to his brother’s recklessness.
  • The contrast between Joachim (Mr. Spitzer) and Peron are deeply explored as to their opposing characters. Joachim is altruistic, restrained, careful, and Peron was egotistical, reckless, and dismissive of Joachim’s order and sacrifices. It highlights Peron’s irresponsibility, including borrowing and forging checks in Joachim’s name, which caused Joachim’s financial ruin. The passage reveals Joachim’s feelings of invisibility, neglect, and resentment toward Peron, who never acknowledged or appreciated him.
  • The detailed description of the house, its symmetrical twin halves, and the doubled portraits and artifacts symbolize the intertwined yet opposed lives of the brothers. The house, with its precise architectural layout and mirrored rooms, reflects Joachim’s desire for balance and control, while Peron’s disregard for these arrangements symbolizes chaos and disruption. This metaphor extends to their personalities and fates.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s impaired perception and mental state is described as well as his physical and mental fragility. His difficulty distinguishing reality from hallucination or dream highlights his profound grief and alienation. His inability to fully “awaken” or “sleep” reflects his liminal, haunted existence after his brother’s death.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s wandering through memories and dreams, confusion over past and present, and his search for meaning in his fragmented life are poignantly rendered as an existential reflection on time, memory, and identity. He struggles with questions of identity, the passage of time as a fluid concept, and the absence of harmony connecting his life’s beginning and end. His experience of living “only at the end of time” and being lost between reality and illusion underscores the theme of existential disorientation.
  • The image of Mr. Spitzer unknowingly traveling in the wrong carriage, as the wrong passenger, driven by a skeletal coachman through unfamiliar landscapes, symbolizes his loss of control and direction in life. This haunting metaphor emphasizes his disconnection from reality and his brother’s lingering presence or shadow in his life.
  • The chapter contrasts Peron’s carefree motto “carpe diem” (seize the day) with Joachim’s solemn motto “hoc opus, hic labor est” (this is the work, this is the labor). This highlights their fundamental differences between Peron’s reckless enjoyment of the moment versus Joachim’s dedication to a difficult, serious artistic mission. It underscores the tragedy of their opposing paths and the sacrifices Joachim made for a legacy unappreciated by Peron.
  • The closing passage reflects Mr. Spitzer’s sense of isolation, the fading of the twin house and the life he once had, his confusion about his own identity, and his unresolved mourning for Peron who never mourned him. It is a reflection on loss, survival, and unresolved grief. It also evokes the futility and purposelessness he feels, despite his efforts to maintain order and shelter from life’s storms. The passage is a powerful summation of the chapter’s themes of loss, memory, and the fragile persistence of life.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “If his brother had died, Mr. Spitzer would have lived.” What life does he imagine, and why does Young begin with a life that never happened?
  • “Silence was more beautiful than music, for the echo was more beautiful than the sound.” Why would a composer prefer silence?
  • “The morning had been the dream, and the night had been real.” What did the long winter night do to Mr. Spitzer?

Characters

  • Peron mocked “the mad man’s dream of absolute equality.” Why does the twin house offend him?
  • The bank teller “insisted that there was only one brother.” Was the teller wrong?
  • Peron lived by carpe diem and Joachim by hoc opus, hic labor est. Which motto does Mr. Spitzer live by now?

Connections

  • Mr. Spitzer wakes “in the wrong carriage” as “the wrong passenger.” How does his journey echo Vera’s bus ride?
  • “Could not the recording angel have made, also, a grand mistake?” Where else in the novel do ledgers, wills, and debts try to measure a life?
  • “After Peron’s death, he had dreamed he was two brothers, and one was dead.” Where else does one character carry another inside?

Reflection

  • “He had gone on when he could not go on.” What keeps a person going?
  • “If his goal had been realized, even for a moment, it would have ceased to be the object of his quest.” Is a goal worth more unreached?
  • Without Peron’s chaos “there would have been such perfect justice that there would have been a frozen world and nothing moving.” Does life need chance?

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer (Joachim)

  • A man of order, methodical, cautious, and devoted to a grand musical project he calls his “opus” or “dirge.”
  • Represents stability, intellectual rigor, and a desire for certainty in an uncertain world.
  • Lives a life of sacrifice, burdened by his brother’s recklessness and debts.
  • Suffers from physical and mental impairments, including distorted vision and a fragile body, symbolic of his fragile mental state.
  • Haunted by his brother’s absence and the loss of a shared past, struggling with identity and reality.
  • Symbolizes the rational, structured side of human nature, trying to impose order on chaos.

Peron (Mr. Spitzer’s Brother)

  • A gambler, extrovert, and embodiment of chaos, risk, and unpredictability.
  • Lives a life of abandon, irresponsibility, and indifference to consequence.
  • His death leaves Joachim to bear the burdens and debts he left behind.
  • Represents the impulsive, irrational, and destructive forces in life, contrasting sharply with Joachim’s order.
  • His laughter and ghostly presence continue to haunt Joachim.

Research: Themes

Duality and Mirror Imagery

See: Duality and Doubles

  • The chapter repeatedly emphasizes twins, mirrors, and reflections: twin portraits, twin gardens, twin portraits of historical figures, and the divided house.
  • Symbolizes the split between order (Joachim) and chaos (Peron), reason and passion, life and death.
  • The mirroring extends to the psychological level where the brothers are halves of one whole, each incomplete without the other.

Memory and Identity

See: Memory and the Past · Identity and the Self

  • Joachim’s identity is fragmented; his memory is unreliable, altered by grief and loss.
  • He questions whether he is himself or his brother, and whether he is awake or dreaming.
  • His life is a continuous search for meaning in the past and the present, often blurred and confused.

Order versus Chaos

See: Order, Chaos, and Discipline

  • Joachim’s life is governed by plans, maps, and precise arrangements designed to exclude chance and disorder.
  • Peron’s life is the antithesis: reckless, chaotic, and unpredictable.
  • The tension between these forces creates conflict and tragedy, illustrating the human struggle to balance these aspects.

Mortality and Immortality

See: Death and Mortality

  • Joachim views his music as a tomb, an immortal legacy that will outlive his physical existence.
  • Peron’s death and presumed freedom contrast with Joachim’s struggle to survive and find meaning.
  • The chapter meditates on death, mourning, and the desire for lasting significance.

Alienation and Isolation

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Joachim feels alienated from himself, others, and reality.
  • His impaired vision and mental confusion symbolize his estrangement from the world.
  • He lives in a liminal state, neither fully awake nor fully asleep, caught between life and death.

Research: Resources

  • “such a thing as a non-aural music”: Pythagoras taught that the planets make a music by their motion that human ears cannot hear. Boethius divided music into three kinds, the music of the world, the music of the human being, and the music of instruments, and only the last can be heard (Boethius, De institutione musica 1.2, about 510). See Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres.
  • “his great emperors and queens, his pearl-bordered moths”: Young’s collection is made of real butterflies and moths: the purple emperor, the queen, the pearl-bordered fritillary, the pebble prominent, the marbled white, and the mourning cloak, which the English call the Camberwell beauty. See The mourning cloak butterfly hibernating and Butterflies and Moths.
  • “his china shepherdesses for his twin marble chimney pieces”: may refer to the China shepherdesses that were popular for the wealthy in the nineteenth century and were often found on fictional mantels and compared in looks to the beauty of a female character.
    • Porcelain shepherdesses were made in pairs to stand at the two ends of a mantelpiece, by Meissen from the 1740s and by Chelsea, Derby, and Staffordshire potters into the nineteenth century.
  • “for he had been a great philatelist”: A philatelist is a person who collects and studies postage stamps, a practice also known as philately. The term combines the Greek roots “phil-“ (loving) and “ateleia” (tax-exemption), referring to the freedom from charges granted by a stamp. A philatelist may focus on the historical or artistic value of stamps, organizing their collections thematically or by country. The French collector Georges Herpin coined the word in 1864.
  • “a remarkable collection of triangles, monuments, commemoratives”: Collectors sort stamps by subject. The Cape of Good Hope triangulars of 1853 were the first triangular stamps, and the first United States commemoratives were the Columbian issue of 1893, marking four hundred years since Columbus’s first voyage.
  • “that the oldest coachman will stop and take on another passenger”: “Because I could not stop for Death – / He kindly stopped for me – / The Carriage held but just Ourselves – / And Immortality” (Emily Dickinson, “Because I could not stop for Death,” about 1862, published 1890). See The Black Coachman and American Writers.
  • “never have consulted Blackstone”: Sir William Blackstone’s Commentaries on the Laws of England (four volumes, 1765-1769) was the standard guide to the common law in England and America; Abraham Lincoln taught himself law from it.
  • “The other great composers had written only their ninth symphonies”: Curse of the ninth is a superstition connected with classical music. It is the belief that the ninth symphony is destined to be the composer’s last. After finishing it the composer is fated to die before finishing a tenth. Beethoven, Dvořák, and Bruckner each died with nine numbered symphonies, and Gustav Mahler, who feared the curse, left his Tenth unfinished at his death in 1911. See On the curse of the ninth and Music and Composers.
  • “as dead as Grand Russian Imperials today”: Imperial Russian government bonds, sold widely in France and abroad before 1917, became worthless when the Soviet government repudiated the tsarist debts in 1918. See the Grand Russian Imperial stocks and bonds in the Chapter 54 resources.
  • “the iron bells of old Czar Kolokol”: The Tsar Bell, also known as the Tsarsky Kolokol, Tsar Kolokol III, or Royal Bell, is a bell on display on the grounds of the Moscow Kremlin. The bell was commissioned by Empress Anna Ivanovna, niece of Peter the Great. It has never been in working order, suspended, nor rung. It was cast in bronze in 1733-1735 and cracked in the Kremlin fire of 1737, when an eleven-ton piece broke off. (Wikipedia)
  • “some old watered bond issues of the Lost Atlantis”: “Lost Atlantis” is a mythical island civilization described by Plato in his dialogues as a powerful, technologically advanced society that was destroyed by earthquakes and sank beneath the sea. While the story is a well-known myth and has inspired theories about real locations and even modern technological advancements, most historians and scientists believe Atlantis was a philosophical allegory created by Plato to serve as a cautionary tale against hubris. See Plato and Platonism. Watered stock is stock issued for far more than the company’s assets are worth, after the drovers said to have fed their cattle salt and let them drink before they were weighed for sale.
  • “sunken Roman galleys, Phoenician traders, Etruscan skills, Etruscan boatmen”: “Roman galleys” A galley is a type of ship optimised for propulsion by oars. Galleys were historically used for warfare, trade, and piracy mostly in the seas surrounding Europe. It developed in the Mediterranean world during antiquity and continued to exist in various forms until the early 19th century. Galleys were the primary warships used by the ancient Mediterranean naval powers, including the Phoenicians, Greeks and Romans. (Wikipedia)
    • “Phoenician traders” were ancient Mediterranean seafarers who established an extensive trade network from about 1550 to 300 B.C.E., exchanging goods like Tyrian purple dye, textiles, metalwork, and glass for raw materials such as copper, tin, ivory, and silver. They were skilled in navigation, shipbuilding, and craft production, establishing colonies and trading posts across the Mediterranean, from the Levant to Iberia and North Africa. Their innovations included the development of the alphabet and early forms of complex trading mechanisms like written contracts, weights, and even coins.
    • “Etruscan skiffs” is a general term used for the smaller, likely less complex boats associated with the Etruscan civilization, contrasted with their larger warships and merchant vessels. Evidence for these types of smaller boats comes from tomb paintings, like those in the Tomb of the Ship in Tarquinia, and clay models, though direct archaeological finds of Etruscan-specific small craft are scarce. These smaller vessels likely served for activities such as fishing, local transport, and possibly supporting larger ships.
    • “Etruscan boatman,” known as Charun or Charu, was a demon of the dead who ferried souls across a boundary to the afterlife, similar to the Greek Charon. Etruscan Charun was distinctively depicted with a hooked nose, hammer, and sometimes animal features like wings, and his presence on tombstones and funerary urns indicated the soul’s journey to the underworld ruled by Aita.
  • “the muted murmur of the paper nautilus”: The paper nautilus, or argonaut, is an octopus whose female makes a thin, paper-white shell to carry her eggs; Aristotle and Pliny believed she raised two arms as sails and sailed the sea in it. See Aristotle.
  • “The golden fleece would have turned to a grey beard”: Jason and the Argonauts sailed to Colchis for the Golden Fleece, the fleece of the winged ram, guarded by a dragon. See Greek Mythology.
  • “Should the alteration recognize that it was altered?”: Young turns Shakespeare’s “Love is not love / Which alters when it alteration finds” (William Shakespeare, Sonnet 116, 1609). See Shakespeare.
  • “leaving Joachim to face the music, pay the piper”: To pay the piper is to bear the cost, as in the proverb “he who pays the piper calls the tune.” The Pied Piper of Hamelin, cheated of his fee for ridding the town of rats, piped its children away.
  • “poor as a Skid Row bum”: See Skid Row in the Chapter 52 resources.
  • “a dead cue-ball artist”: See the billiards legend of Louis Fox in the Chapter 53 resources. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “twirled the bare-ribbed bird-cages”: In chuck-a-luck, also called birdcage, three dice tumble in an hourglass-shaped wire cage, and players bet on the numbers that come up. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “as if it were a sea of glass which had no end”: “And before the throne there was a sea of glass like unto crystal” (Revelation 4:6, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “all money was counterfeit even like the sun, moon, stars turned out at the great Mint”: Money, though officially minted, is worth what people agree it is worth, and Young sends the sun, moon, and stars to the same great Mint.
    • Before coins came barter and commodity money: cattle from about 9000 BC, and from about 1200 BC cowrie shells, the most widely and longest used currency in history, especially in Asia and Africa. Standard weights of gold and silver were used in Egypt and Mesopotamia by the third millennium BC. Lydia struck the first stamped coins of electrum about 650-600 BC, while Chinese mints cast coins shaped like spades and knives. Chinese merchants used paper receipts called “flying money,” and the Song government issued the first official paper currency in the 1020s; Sweden issued Europe’s first banknotes in 1661. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries governments tied paper money to gold. (Wikipedia)
    • The first minting facility, or mint, was likely established by the Lydians in the 7th century BC for coining precious metals, a practice that spread to Greece, Rome, and beyond, with modern countries often operating one national mint. Coinage spread from Lydia to the Aegean Islands, Italy, Persia, and India, with Romans developing modern minting techniques by the 4th century BC. The first national mint in the United States was established by the Coinage Act of 1792 in Philadelphia.
    • Counterfeit money is currency produced outside of the legal sanction of a state or government, usually in a deliberate attempt to imitate that currency and so as to deceive its recipient. Producing or using counterfeit money is a form of fraud or forgery, and is illegal in all jurisdictions of the world. The business of counterfeiting money is nearly as old as money itself: plated copies (known as Fourrées) have been found of Lydian coins, which are thought to be among the first Western coins. Before the introduction of paper money, the most prevalent method of counterfeiting involved mixing base metals with pure gold or silver. During World War II, the Nazis forged British pounds and American dollars. Traditionally, anti-counterfeiting measures involved including fine detail with raised intaglio printing on bills which allows non-experts to easily spot forgeries. On coins, milled or reeded (marked with parallel grooves) edges are used to show that none of the valuable metal has been scraped off. (Wikipedia)
  • “could certainly never have employed a Seeing Eye bookkeeper”: The Seeing Eye, the first guide dog school in the United States, was founded in 1929 by Dorothy Harrison Eustis, who had trained German shepherds for the Swiss army and police; it moved to Morristown, New Jersey, in 1931. See Eyes and Blindness.
  • “could not the recording angel have made, also, a grand mistake”: In Laurence Sterne’s Tristram Shandy, a book Young named among her influences, Uncle Toby swears an oath out of pity for the dying Le Fever, and “the ACCUSING SPIRIT, which flew up to heaven’s chancery with the oath, blush’d as he gave it in;—and the RECORDING ANGEL, as he wrote it down, dropp’d a tear upon the word, and blotted it out for ever” (Laurence Sterne, Tristram Shandy, vol. 6, ch. 8, 1762).
  • “from vastly towering cumulo-nimbus to darkening alto-stratus”: Cumulonimbus are the towering thunderheads; altostratus is the grey sheet of mid-level cloud that comes before steady rain or snow. The Latin cloud names go back to Luke Howard’s Essay on the Modifications of Clouds (1803). See Clouds and Storms.
  • “the long streamers of Aurora Borealis”: See the northern and southern lights in the Chapter 54 resources. See Astronomy and Exploration and the Poles.
  • “an old croupier, perhaps at the wrong wheel”: A croupier is the person in charge of a gaming table, gathering in and paying out money or tokens. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “Gilts and Estates”: A gilt is a British government bond, called “gilt-edged” for its safety, since the government has never missed a payment. An estate is everything a person owns and owes at death, distributed by will or, without one, by law.
  • “Last Bequests”: a bequest is a gift of property, money, or other assets made from an estate to a specific individual, organization, or trust through a will or other estate planning document. It is essentially a legacy designated to be distributed after the estate owner’s death. Bequests are a flexible way to support loved ones and charitable causes, with different types including specific gifts of particular items, general cash amounts, or a percentage of the remaining estate.
  • “Progress of Probate”: involves filing the will and petition with the court, identifying and appraising assets, notifying interested parties, paying debts and taxes, and finally distributing the remaining property to beneficiaries. The timeline for this process varies by state and the complexity of the estate, typically ranging from several months to over a year, with complications like will contests or estate taxes extending the duration.
  • “Authenticity of Will”: A will’s authenticity is established by confirming the testator’s intent and capacity at the time of creation, and by verifying the proper execution under state law, which generally requires a written document signed by the testator and two witnesses (unless it’s a handwritten “holographic will,” which is valid in many states without witnesses). A self-proving affidavit, signed by the testator, witnesses, and a notary, simplifies probate by attaching the will to the affidavit, making the process easier and saving time and money.
  • “Will of the Dead”: may refer to a legal will and testament, a legally binding document that specifies how a person’s property should be distributed after their death. In a spiritual or metaphorical context, it could refer to the concept of the deceased having continued influence or wishes, such as in Jesus’s “let the dead bury their dead” (Matthew 8:22), which turns attention to the living. In the New Testament the covenant is a will that takes effect at a death: “For where a testament is, there must also of necessity be the death of the testator” (Hebrews 9:16).
  • “Cases in Chancery”: are legal matters handled by a type of court, originally established in England, that focuses on fairness and equity rather than strictly applying common law rules. These courts typically address issues like trusts and estates, corporate and partnership disputes, real estate and mortgage foreclosures, adoptions and guardianships, and requests for equitable remedies such as injunctions and specific performance of contracts. Unlike criminal cases, chancery courts resolve civil disputes, and they often involve complex personal, financial, and familial relationships.
  • “Laws Governing New England Fishing”: the regulation of New England fishing was a fragmented mix of international treaties, state-level rules, and early federal actions that lacked comprehensive conservation measures. The period was defined by escalating conflicts, increasing fishing efficiency, and a growing understanding of resource depletion, all of which would eventually necessitate stronger federal oversight in the 1970s.
  • “Laws Governing Whaling”: Whaling was a largely unregulated industry until the start of loose agreements in the 1930s and culminating in the formation of the International Whaling Commission (IWC) in 1946. These laws attempted to manage whale stocks but struggled with enforcement and compliance.
  • “that the man who found the whale was its owner”: Young reverses the whalemen’s own law. In Moby-Dick, “A Fast-Fish belongs to the party fast to it,” and “A Loose-Fish is fair game for anybody who can soonest catch it” (Herman Melville, Moby-Dick, ch. 89, “Fast-Fish and Loose-Fish,” 1851). In Swift v. Gifford (1872), a Massachusetts federal court held that a whale belonged to the ship whose harpoon first struck it, not to the ship whose crew later killed and took it. See American Writers.
  • “The Musical Sensibilities of the King Walrus”: may refer to Lewis Carroll’s poem “The Walrus and the Carpenter” which first appeared in 1871. Its rhythmic narrative has been set to music multiple times over the decades, including the 1951 Walt Disney film Alice in Wonderland. There it is sung by J. Pat O’Malley in a vaudeville style, and the charming Walrus eats the oysters.
  • “Burke’s Peerage in the Penguin World”: may refer to Burke’s Peerage Limited, a British genealogical publisher, considered an authority on the order of precedence of noble families and information on the lesser nobility of the United Kingdom. It was founded in 1826, when the Anglo-Irish genealogist John Burke began releasing books devoted to the ancestry and heraldry of the peerage, baronetage, knightage and landed gentry of Great Britain and Ireland. His first publication, a Genealogical and Heraldic Dictionary of the Peerage and Baronetage of the United Kingdom, was updated sporadically until 1847, when the company began publishing new editions every year as Burke’s Peerage, Baronetage and Knightage (often shortened and known as Burke’s Peerage). Other books followed, including Burke’s Landed Gentry, Burke’s Colonial Gentry, and Burke’s General Armory. In addition to its peerage publications, the Burke’s publishing company produced books on Royal families of Europe and Latin America, ruling families of Africa and the Middle East, distinguished families of the United States and historical families of Ireland. (Wikipedia)
  • “New England Mortality Rates”: Historically, New England mortality rates were characterized by significant changes over time, from periods of exceptionally high colonial-era life expectancy to high urban death rates in the 19th century, before undergoing a sharp decline in the early 20th century. Contrary to popular belief, 17th-century New England colonists enjoyed a long life expectancy, averaging 65 to 70 years. This was 15 to 20 years longer than in England at the time and was attributed to the healthy climate and spaced-out communities. Studies of early New England towns show surprisingly high child survival rates. For example, in Andover, Massachusetts, between 1640 and 1669, 87% to 91% of children survived to age 20 (Philip J. Greven Jr., Four Generations: Population, Land, and Family in Colonial Andover, Massachusetts, 1970). These long lifespans were frequently interrupted by devastating epidemics, such as outbreaks of smallpox, measles, and influenza. Because of low population density and less frequent exposure, community immunity would wane, leading to cyclic, highly destructive epidemics when a new disease was introduced. Europeans brought diseases like smallpox, measles, and cholera, to which Native Americans had no immunity. These outbreaks killed up to 90% of the Indigenous population in parts of New England. While overall U.S. mortality was low compared to Western Europe, urban areas saw a significant “mortality penalty.” By the early 1900s, the life expectancy for white people in New England’s urban areas was 46 years, compared to 54.7 years in rural areas. As towns grew, infectious diseases spread rapidly due to poor sanitation and crowded living conditions. As the leading cause of death in the 19th century, tuberculosis was a scourge in New England, affecting multiple members and generations of families. Diphtheria, scarlet fever, and whooping cough were major threats to children, contributing to high infant and child mortality rates. Cholera and typhoid fever also emerged as epidemic threats, particularly impacting port cities like Boston. The early 20th century saw a dramatic decline in mortality, largely due to public health measures and improved living standards, rather than new medical interventions.
  • “Actuarial Statistics”: is the application of statistical theory and probability analysis to evaluate and manage financial and other risks for organizations, particularly in the insurance, finance, and healthcare industries. Actuaries use statistical models and mathematical tools to quantify the likelihood and financial impact of uncertain events, enabling businesses to forecast outcomes, price products, manage capital, and allocate funds to cover potential losses.
  • “The Habits of the Night-Crowned Kingfisher”: “The Habits of the Night-Crowned Kingfisher” is an invented title. It may blend the kingfisher with the black-crowned night heron, a nocturnal fishing bird whose Latin name, Nycticorax, means night raven, for its harsh, crow-like call.
  • “The Life of the Sphinx Moth”: The life of a sphinx moth involves four stages: egg, larva (caterpillar), pupa, and adult. After mating, the adult female lays eggs on a host plant, which hatch into ravenous caterpillars that feed and grow, molting multiple times. Once fully grown, the caterpillar burrows into the soil or leaf litter to form a pupa, overwintering in this stage. The following spring, or later if delayed, the pupa transforms into an adult moth, which emerges, feeds on nectar using its proboscis, mates, and then lays eggs to begin the cycle anew.
  • “the seraph with his wings folded across his eyes”: “Above it stood the seraphims: each one had six wings; with twain he covered his face, and with twain he covered his feet, and with twain he did fly” (Isaiah 6:2, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “his tortoise harp with the gut string stretched inside its empty shell”: The baby Hermes made the first lyre from a tortoise shell strung with gut, then gave it to Apollo (Homeric Hymn to Hermes). See Greek Mythology.
  • “each with its blind boy cupid”: See blind Cupid in the Chapter 52 resources. See Greek Mythology.

Careful to do what is right; thorough.
Coxcomb
The red crest of a rooster, or the jester’s cap shaped like one; also a vain, foolish man.
Croupier
The attendant who runs a gambling table.
Deluge
A great flood or downpour. The Deluge is Noah’s flood.
Demented
Out of one’s mind.
Demoniac
Wild and frenzied, as if possessed by a demon.
Dirge
A slow song of mourning.
Discursive
Rambling from one subject to another.
Distraught
Deeply upset.
Domino
A loose hooded cloak worn with a half mask at a masquerade.
Dominoes
A game played with small oblong tiles marked with spots.
Dovecote
A house for doves or pigeons.
Egotist
One who thinks mainly of oneself.
Elegiac
Mournful, like an elegy, a poem of lament for the dead.
Equable
Even-tempered; not easily disturbed.
Errant
Wandering; straying from the proper course.
Escarpment
A long, steep slope or cliff.
Ethereal
Light, airy, and delicate, as if not of this world; heavenly.
Extrovert
An outgoing person who draws energy from others.
Figurehead
A carved figure, often a woman, mounted on the bow of a sailing ship. See On the figurehead and intuition (Ch 40).
Flagstone
A flat paving stone.
Fritillary
A butterfly with orange wings checked with black, often silver-spotted beneath.
Glacial
Icy; like a glacier.
Hallucination
Something seen or heard that is not there.
Hieroglyph
A picture-sign of a writing system, as in ancient Egypt.
Imperceptibly
So slowly or slightly as to go unnoticed.
Impoverished
Made poor.
Incalculable
Too great to be counted.
Inclemency
Harshness, especially of weather.
Inscrutable
Impossible to understand or interpret.
Inscrutably
In a way that cannot be understood.
Interlocutor
A person who takes part in a conversation.
Irrevocably
In a way that cannot be undone.
Isocheim
A line on a map joining places with the same average winter temperature.
Jersey
A soft knitted cloth.
Lethargic
Sluggish and drowsy.
Liqueur
A strong, sweet, flavored spirit taken after dinner.
Luminous
Giving off light.
Lute
A plucked string instrument with a rounded back shaped like half a pear, popular in Europe from the Middle Ages through the 1700s.
Mandarin
A high-ranking official of imperial China.
Mauve
A pale purple.
Minaret
A tall, slender tower of a mosque, from which the call to prayer is given.
Minuet
A slow, stately dance in triple time for couples, and the music written for it.
Minuscular
Tiny. Young’s form of minuscule.
Mirage
An optical illusion, common in deserts, in which hot air bends light so that distant water or cities seem to appear.
Mollusk
A soft-bodied creature, such as a snail or clam, usually with a shell.
Morceau
A short piece of music or writing (French, a morsel).
Mortality rate
The number of deaths in a population over a given period.
Mosaic
A picture or pattern made by setting small pieces of colored stone, glass, or tile together.
Mother-of-pearl
The shining, rainbow-colored lining of some shells, used for inlay.
Mourning cloak
A dark butterfly with pale-edged wings that survives the winter as an adult; also a black cloak worn in mourning.
Nautilus
A sea creature with a spiral shell. The paper nautilus is a small octopus whose female makes a thin, papery shell.
Nether
Lower; under.
Nimbus
A halo or glow of light.
Oblivion
The state of being forgotten, or of being unaware of everything.
Omniscient
All-knowing.
Pagoda
An Asian tiered tower with multiple eaves, usually a Buddhist temple or shrine.
Parallelogram
A four-sided figure whose opposite sides are parallel.
Parimutuel
A form of betting in which the winners share the total amount bet.
Parsimonious
Very unwilling to spend money; stingy.
Parterre
An ornamental garden of flower beds laid out in a pattern. A parterred floor is inlaid in patterned sections.
Patrimonial
Inherited from one’s father.
Patrimony
Property inherited from one’s father or ancestors.
Pauperdom
The state of being a pauper; poverty.
Philatelist
A stamp collector.
Plinth
The square block at the base of a column, pedestal, or statue.
Polka
A lively couple dance from Bohemia.
Porte-cochere
A covered entrance where a carriage stops to let passengers out.
Precarious
Unstable and likely to fall or fail.
Presentiment
An intuitive feeling that something is about to happen.
Problematical
Doubtful; hard to settle.
Proffered
Held out; offered.
Proximity
Nearness.
Psyche
A tall mirror that swings in a frame; also the soul.
Pulverized
Ground to powder.
Quadrant
An instrument for measuring the height of stars, used in navigation and surveying.
Quavering
Trembling, as a voice.
Resonant
Echoing; deep and full in sound.
Sand dollar
A flat, round sea urchin whose white skeleton is found on beaches.
Sarcophagus
A stone coffin, often carved, placed above ground. The plural is sarcophagi.
Satire
The use of humor, irony, exaggeration, or ridicule to expose and criticize folly or vice.
Schottische
A round dance like a slow polka, popular in the nineteenth century.
Sequestration
Seclusion; a setting apart.
Seraphim
The highest order of angels, each with six wings. The singular is seraph.
Snuffers
A scissor-like tool for trimming or putting out a candle.
Speculative
Thoughtful and wondering; based on guesswork.
Steeplechase
A horse race over fences, ditches, and water jumps, first run cross-country from one church steeple to another.
Sulky
A light two-wheeled cart for one driver, used in harness racing.
Surrey
A light four-wheeled horse-drawn carriage with two seats, often with a fringed canopy on top.
Tapestry
A heavy cloth woven with pictures, hung on walls.
Tendon
A tough cord that joins muscle to bone.
Transpired
Happened; took place.
Tremulous
Trembling; quivering.
Tumult
A loud, confused uproar.
Turret
A small tower on a building or castle.
Tympanum
The eardrum; also the space within an arch above a door.
Vaporous
Full of mist or vapor.
Viol
A bowed stringed instrument of the Renaissance and Baroque, usually with six strings and frets, held upright.
Virginal
A small, legless harpsichord set on a table, popular in sixteenth- and seventeenth-century England.
Weathervane
A turning device on a roof that shows the direction of the wind.
Zither
A stringed instrument with a flat soundboard, played lying flat.
