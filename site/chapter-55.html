On Chapter 55 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.

Summary

Mr. Spitzer imagines the life he would have led if his brother had died first: his theoretical music, his butterflies and postage stamps, safe investments in sunken ships and dead Russian bonds, and a tenth symphony that would begin where other composers had ended their lives. He remembers the twin house he designed so that each brother had his half, every portrait, garden, and mirror repeated, and how Peron mocked it, moved the furniture, forged Joachim’s checks, and never did him a single favor. Since Peron’s death his eyes see double, the near things small and the far things large; he sleeps with his eyes open and wakes in the wrong carriage, the wrong passenger behind a skeleton coachman. Peron lived by carpe diem, Joachim by hoc opus, hic labor est. The walls of the twin house have been knocked down, and Mr. Spitzer, broken by the funeral and its debts, lives in an old beach-comber’s house, “still wondering whose funeral it was.”

Key passages

From the research

  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates how he might have lived had his brother died earlier. It describes his orderly, carefully planned life dedicated to music, philately, and collecting rare artifacts. It shows his desire for certainty, order, and a calm pursuit of immortality through art, contrasting with the chaos brought by his brother. This sets up the fundamental tension between the brothers’ lives and personalities.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s conservative financial choices are described in detail. This symbolizes his attempt to secure an immutable future for his music and life, despite the apparent futility, reflecting his cautious and meticulous nature opposed to his brother’s recklessness.
  • The contrast between Joachim (Mr. Spitzer) and Peron are deeply explored as to their opposing characters. Joachim is altruistic, restrained, careful, and Peron was egotistical, reckless, and dismissive of Joachim’s order and sacrifices. It highlights Peron’s irresponsibility, including borrowing and forging checks in Joachim’s name, which caused Joachim’s financial ruin. The passage reveals Joachim’s feelings of invisibility, neglect, and resentment toward Peron, who never acknowledged or appreciated him.
  • The detailed description of the house, its symmetrical twin halves, and the doubled portraits and artifacts symbolize the intertwined yet opposed lives of the brothers. The house, with its precise architectural layout and mirrored rooms, reflects Joachim’s desire for balance and control, while Peron’s disregard for these arrangements symbolizes chaos and disruption. This metaphor extends to their personalities and fates.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s impaired perception and mental state is described as well as his physical and mental fragility. His difficulty distinguishing reality from hallucination or dream highlights his profound grief and alienation. His inability to fully “awaken” or “sleep” reflects his liminal, haunted existence after his brother’s death.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s wandering through memories and dreams, confusion over past and present, and his search for meaning in his fragmented life are poignantly rendered as an existential reflection on time, memory, and identity. He struggles with questions of identity, the passage of time as a fluid concept, and the absence of harmony connecting his life’s beginning and end. His experience of living “only at the end of time” and being lost between reality and illusion underscores the theme of existential disorientation.
  • The image of Mr. Spitzer unknowingly traveling in the wrong carriage, as the wrong passenger, driven by a skeletal coachman through unfamiliar landscapes, symbolizes his loss of control and direction in life. This haunting metaphor emphasizes his disconnection from reality and his brother’s lingering presence or shadow in his life.
  • The chapter contrasts Peron’s carefree motto “carpe diem” (seize the day) with Joachim’s solemn motto “hoc opus, hic labor est” (this is the work, this is the labor). This highlights their fundamental differences between Peron’s reckless enjoyment of the moment versus Joachim’s dedication to a difficult, serious artistic mission. It underscores the tragedy of their opposing paths and the sacrifices Joachim made for a legacy unappreciated by Peron.
  • The closing passage reflects Mr. Spitzer’s sense of isolation, the fading of the twin house and the life he once had, his confusion about his own identity, and his unresolved mourning for Peron who never mourned him. It is a reflection on loss, survival, and unresolved grief. It also evokes the futility and purposelessness he feels, despite his efforts to maintain order and shelter from life’s storms. The passage is a powerful summation of the chapter’s themes of loss, memory, and the fragile persistence of life.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “If his brother had died, Mr. Spitzer would have lived.” What life does he imagine, and why does Young begin with a life that never happened?
  • “Silence was more beautiful than music, for the echo was more beautiful than the sound.” Why would a composer prefer silence?
  • “The morning had been the dream, and the night had been real.” What did the long winter night do to Mr. Spitzer?

Characters

  • Peron mocked “the mad man’s dream of absolute equality.” Why does the twin house offend him?
  • The bank teller “insisted that there was only one brother.” Was the teller wrong?
  • Peron lived by carpe diem and Joachim by hoc opus, hic labor est. Which motto does Mr. Spitzer live by now?

Connections

  • Mr. Spitzer wakes “in the wrong carriage” as “the wrong passenger.” How does his journey echo Vera’s bus ride?
  • “Could not the recording angel have made, also, a grand mistake?” Where else in the novel do ledgers, wills, and debts try to measure a life?
  • “After Peron’s death, he had dreamed he was two brothers, and one was dead.” Where else does one character carry another inside?

Reflection

  • “He had gone on when he could not go on.” What keeps a person going?
  • “If his goal had been realized, even for a moment, it would have ceased to be the object of his quest.” Is a goal worth more unreached?
  • Without Peron’s chaos “there would have been such perfect justice that there would have been a frozen world and nothing moving.” Does life need chance?

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer (Joachim)

  • A man of order, methodical, cautious, and devoted to a grand musical project he calls his “opus” or “dirge.”
  • Represents stability, intellectual rigor, and a desire for certainty in an uncertain world.
  • Lives a life of sacrifice, burdened by his brother’s recklessness and debts.
  • Suffers from physical and mental impairments, including distorted vision and a fragile body, symbolic of his fragile mental state.
  • Haunted by his brother’s absence and the loss of a shared past, struggling with identity and reality.
  • Symbolizes the rational, structured side of human nature, trying to impose order on chaos.

Peron (Mr. Spitzer’s Brother)

  • A gambler, extrovert, and embodiment of chaos, risk, and unpredictability.
  • Lives a life of abandon, irresponsibility, and indifference to consequence.
  • His death leaves Joachim to bear the burdens and debts he left behind.
  • Represents the impulsive, irrational, and destructive forces in life, contrasting sharply with Joachim’s order.
  • His laughter and ghostly presence continue to haunt Joachim.

  • “all money was counterfeit even like the sun, moon, stars turned out at the great Mint”: It suggests a profound skepticism or critique about the nature of material wealth and value.     • Wealth, like many other things in the story, is portrayed as a form of illusion or “counterfeit,” reflecting the transient and unstable aspects of life and human experience that the characters grapple with.The history of money is a journey from direct barter to symbolic representations of value, including commodity items, metal coins, paper notes, and modern digital currencies. This evolution was driven by the need for a more efficient and reliable medium of exchange as societies grew more complex. For most of early human history, transactions were based on barter or the direct exchange of goods and services. Early societies began to use easily tradable items with inherent value, known as commodity money. Around 9000-6000 B.C., cattle, sheep, and camels were among the first forms of money. They were both a medium of exchange and a symbol of wealth. From around 1200 B.C., cowrie shells became the most widely and longest-used currency in history, particularly in Asia and Africa. Their durability, size, and scarcity made them a popular choice. Salt, grain, and even whale teeth were used as currency in various cultures. The use of precious metals marked a significant step in money’s history, offering a more durable and standardized medium of exchange. As early as the 3rd millennium B.C. in Egypt and Mesopotamia, standardized weights of gold, silver, and other metals were used for trade. The Lydian kingdom (in modern-day Turkey) is widely credited with issuing the first standardized and officially stamped coins around 650-600 B.C.. Made from electrum (a gold and silver alloy), these coins featured a royal symbol that guaranteed their weight and value. Around the same time, Chinese mints produced metal coins in the shape of spades and knives. The invention of paper money was a solution to the inconvenience of carrying heavy metal coins for large transactions. Chinese merchants would deposit their metal coins with goldsmiths and receive a paper receipt, which they then used for purchases. This early system was known as “flying money.” During the Song Dynasty (1020s), the Chinese government took control of the system, issuing the first official paper currency. Inspired by Marco Polo’s accounts of Chinese paper money, Europeans began adopting it in the 17th century. Sweden was the first to introduce European-style banknotes in 1661. The formal backing of currency evolved from commodities to government decree. To control inflation and ensure stability, governments in the 19th and early 20th centuries adopted the gold standard, where they issued paper money redeemable for a fixed amount of gold. After World War II, many countries pegged their currencies to the US dollar, which was, in turn, fixed to gold. In the 1970s, this system was abandoned, and money became a fiat currency. Its value is not derived from a physical commodity but from a government decree and public trust in the issuing authority. The rapid evolution of technology has pushed money into the digital realm, from traditional systems to decentralized networks. Starting in the 1950s, modern credit cards allowed for cashless purchases, with the first successful general-purpose card being the Diners Club Card in 1950.
    • The first minting facility, or mint, was likely established by the Lydians in the 7th century BC for coining precious metals, a practice that spread to Greece, Rome, and beyond, with modern countries often operating one national mint. Coinage spread from Lydia to the Aegean Islands, Italy, Persia, and India, with Romans developing modern minting techniques by the 4th century BC. The first national mint in the United States was established by the Coinage Act of 1792 in Philadelphia.
    • Counterfeit money is currency produced outside of the legal sanction of a state or government, usually in a deliberate attempt to imitate that currency and so as to deceive its recipient. Producing or using counterfeit money is a form of fraud or forgery, and is illegal in all jurisdictions of the world. The business of counterfeiting money is nearly as old as money itself: plated copies (known as Fourrées) have been found of Lydian coins, which are thought to be among the first Western coins. Before the introduction of paper money, the most prevalent method of counterfeiting involved mixing base metals with pure gold or silver. During World War II, the Nazis forged British pounds and American dollars. Today, some of the finest counterfeit banknotes are called Superdollars because of their high quality and imitation of the real US dollar. There has been significant counterfeiting of Euro banknotes and coins since the launch of the currency in 2002, but considerably less than that of the US dollar. Some of the ill-effects that counterfeit money has on society include a reduction in the value of real money; an increase in prices (inflation) as a result of an increase in money being circulated in the economy; a decrease in the acceptability of paper money; and losses, when traders are not reimbursed for counterfeit money detected by banks, even if it is confiscated. Traditionally, anti-counterfeiting measures involved including fine detail with raised intaglio printing on bills which allows non-experts to easily spot forgeries. On coins, milled or reeded (marked with parallel grooves) edges are used to show that none of the valuable metal has been scraped off. (Wikipedia)

  • “Gilts and Estates”: are distinct concepts that intersect in the context of inheritance and investment. In the UK, a “gilt” is a government-issued bond that can be an asset within a person’s “estate” (all of their property and debts) upon their death. A gilt is a high-security bond issued by the UK government, representing a loan from an investor to the government. The term “gilt-edged” reflects the perception of safety, as the UK government has never failed to make interest or principal payments on time. Gilts can be conventional, paying a fixed interest rate, or index-linked, where payments are adjusted for inflation. Gilts are treated like other investments and are included in a deceased person’s estate, valued at their market price at the time of death, for inheritance tax purposes. An estate refers to all of an individual’s assets and liabilities at the time of their death, including real estate, bank accounts, and investments. The estate’s administration, including the distribution of assets, is governed by estate law. Individuals use wills and trusts to outline how their estate should be managed and distributed. This is the legal process of proving a will’s validity and administering the estate under court supervision. Estate law is a complex field that varies by state. If someone dies without a will (intestate), state laws determine how their assets are distributed.
  • “Last Bequests”: a bequest is a gift of property, money, or other assets made from an estate to a specific individual, organization, or trust through a will or other estate planning document. It is essentially a legacy designated to be distributed after the estate owner’s death. Bequests are a flexible way to support loved ones and charitable causes, with different types including specific gifts of particular items, general cash amounts, or a percentage of the remaining estate.
  • “Progress of Probate”: involves filing the will and petition with the court, identifying and appraising assets, notifying interested parties, paying debts and taxes, and finally distributing the remaining property to beneficiaries. The timeline for this process varies by state and the complexity of the estate, typically ranging from several months to over a year, with complications like will contests or estate taxes extending the duration.
  • “Authenticity of Will”: A will’s authenticity is established by confirming the testator’s intent and capacity at the time of creation, and by verifying the proper execution under state law, which generally requires a written document signed by the testator and two witnesses (unless it’s a handwritten “holographic will,” which is valid in many states without witnesses). A self-proving affidavit, signed by the testator, witnesses, and a notary, simplifies probate by attaching the will to the affidavit, making the process easier and saving time and money.
  • “Will of the Dead”: may refer to a legal will and testament, a legally binding document that specifies how a person’s property should be distributed after their death. In a spiritual or metaphorical context, it could refer to the concept of the deceased having continued influence or wishes, such as in the phrase “Let the dead bury the dead,” which encourages focusing on the living rather than the past or those who are spiritually dead. The phrase can also refer to the theological concept of the New Covenant in the Bible, where Jesus’s death is required for the will to take effect.
  • “Cases in Chancery”: are legal matters handled by a type of court, originally established in England, that focuses on fairness and equity rather than strictly applying common law rules. These courts typically address issues like trusts and estates, corporate and partnership disputes, real estate and mortgage foreclosures, adoptions and guardianships, and requests for equitable remedies such as injunctions and specific performance of contracts. Unlike criminal cases, chancery courts resolve civil disputes, and they often involve complex personal, financial, and familial relationships.

The period was defined by escalating conflicts, increasing fishing efficiency, and a growing understanding of resource depletion, all of which would eventually necessitate stronger federal oversight in the 1970s.
  • “Laws Governing Whaling”: Whaling was a largely unregulated industry until the start of loose agreements in the 1930s and culminating in the formation of the International Whaling Commission (IWC) in 1946. These laws attempted to manage whale stocks but struggled with enforcement and compliance.

  • “Burke’s Peerage in the Penguin World”: may refer to Burke’s Peerage Limited, a British genealogical publisher, considered an authority on the order of precedence of noble families and information on the lesser nobility of the United Kingdom. It was founded in 1826, when the Anglo-Irish genealogist John Burke began releasing books devoted to the ancestry and heraldry of the peerage, baronetage, knightage and landed gentry of Great Britain and Ireland.     • “sub specie aeternitatis, under the aspect of eternity, in its essential or universal form” This reflects a philosophical viewpoint regarding the nature of existence and identity beyond the limitations of time and individuality. This Latin phrase, traditionally used by philosophers like Spinoza, means to consider something “under the aspect of eternity,” i.e., from a timeless, eternal perspective rather than a temporal or limited one. In the context of the chapter, it suggests looking at the self or existence not just as a particular individual bound by time, but as part of a universal, timeless whole. Mr. Spitzer is reflecting on his own identity and existence, recognizing that he is more than the sum of his temporal experiences. He contemplates that he may have been “retailored” or transformed in ways that transcend ordinary perception, and that his “essential or universal form” is something beyond his individual, physical self. The passage conveys his awareness of the fluidity and multiplicity of identity and existence, moving beyond the confines of linear time and fixed boundaries. It means considering oneself or one’s being from the perspective of eternity, seeing beyond the limited, changing self to a more fundamental, universal essence that is not confined by time or space.
    • It is an honorific expression denoting what is considered to be universally and eternally true, without any reference to or dependence upon temporal facets of reality. The Latin phrase can be rendered in English as “from the perspective of the eternal.” More loosely, it is commonly used to refer to an objective (or theoretically possible alternative) point of view. Spinoza’s “eternal” perspective is reflected in his Ethics (Part V, Prop. XXIII, Scholium), where he applies Euclid’s method (with the use of geometry) to philosophical inquiry, starting with God and nature, before moving to human emotions and the human intellect to reach an understanding of moral philosophy. By proceeding sub specie aeternitatis, Spinoza sought to arrive at an ethical theory that is as precise as Euclid’s Elements. In the history of philosophy, this way of proceeding may be contrasted with Aristotle’s. Aristotle’s methodological differentiations in his “philosophy of human affairs” and his natural philosophy are grounded in the distinction between what is “better known to us” and things “better known in themselves,” or what is “first for us” and what is “first by nature,” a distinction that is deliberately discarded by Spinoza, and also by other modern philosophers. It is typically performed to music in 4/4 time and is still a part of folk dance traditions worldwide.
    • “polka” is a lively, 19th-century Bohemian (Czech) dance and dance music genre characterized by couples dancing to music in 2/4 time with a basic pattern of quick steps and a hop. Popularized across Europe and the Americas, the name likely derives from the Czech word “půlka,” meaning “half-step,” though it has also been mistakenly linked to Polish women due to the similar-sounding Czech word.
    • “minuet” is a social dance of French origin for two people, usually written in 3/4 time. The English word was adapted from the Italian minuetto and the French minuet. The term also describes the musical form that accompanies the dance, which subsequently developed more fully, often with a longer musical form called the minuet and trio, and was much used as a movement in the early classical symphony. While often stylized in instrumental forms, composers of the period would have been familiar with the popular dance. (Wikipedia)

  • “when he had found himself talking to Mozart”: “Mozart” Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart was a prolific and influential composer of the Classical period. Despite his short life, his rapid pace of composition and proficiency from an early age resulted in more than 800 works representing virtually every Western classical genre of his time. Many of these compositions are acknowledged as pinnacles of the symphonic, concertante, chamber, operatic, and choral repertoires. Mozart is widely regarded as one of the greatest composers in the history of Western music, with his music admired for its “melodic beauty, its formal elegance and its richness of harmony and texture.” Born in Salzburg, Mozart showed prodigious ability from his earliest childhood. At age five, he was already competent on keyboard and violin, had begun to compose, and performed before European royalty. His father, Leopold Mozart, took him on a grand tour of Europe and then three trips to Italy. At 17, he was a musician at the Salzburg court but grew restless and travelled in search of a better position. Mozart’s search for employment led to positions in Paris, Mannheim, Munich, and again in Salzburg, during which he wrote his five violin concertos, Sinfonia Concertante, and Concerto for Flute and Harp, as well as sacred pieces and masses, the motet Exsultate Jubilate, and the opera Idomeneo, among other works. While visiting Vienna in 1781, Mozart was dismissed from his Salzburg position. He stayed in Vienna, where he achieved fame but little financial security. During Mozart’s early years in Vienna, he produced several notable works, such as the opera Die Entführung aus dem Serail, the Great Mass in C minor, the “Haydn” Quartets and a number of symphonies. Throughout his Vienna years, Mozart composed over a dozen piano concertos, many considered some of his greatest achievements. In the final years of his life, Mozart wrote many of his best-known works, including his last three symphonies, culminating in the Jupiter Symphony, the serenade Eine kleine Nachtmusik, his Clarinet Concerto, the operas The Marriage of Figaro, Don Giovanni, Così fan tutte and The Magic Flute and his Requiem. The Requiem was largely unfinished at the time of his death at age 35, the circumstances of which are uncertain and much mythologised. His elder sister, Maria Anna Walburga Ignatia “Marianne” Mozart, nicknamed Nannerl, was a highly regarded musician from Salzburg, Austria. In her childhood, she developed into an outstanding keyboard player under the tutelage of her father Leopold. She became a celebrated child prodigy and went on concert tours through much of Europe with her parents and her younger brother Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart. At age 17, her career as a touring musician came to an end, though she continued to work at home teaching piano and performing on occasion. At age 33 she married, moved to a village six hours by carriage from Salzburg, and there raised her own and her husband’s children. On her widowhood in 1801, she returned to Salzburg and resumed teaching and performance. She is known to have composed works of music, though no manuscripts survive. In her later years she contributed to the biographical study of her late brother. (Wikipedia) See Music and Composers.

  • “In every gambler’s make-up”: Gambling or betting or gaming is the wagering of something of value on an event with an uncertain outcome with the intent of winning something else of value. Gambling dates back to the Paleolithic period, before written history. Financial Investments are generally not considered gambling when they meet certain criteria. Studies show that though many people participate in gambling as a form of recreation or to earn an income, gambling, like any behavior involving variation in brain chemistry, can become a behavioral addiction. Behavioral addiction can occur with all the negative consequences in a person’s life minus the physical issues faced by people who compulsively engage in drug and alcohol abuse. Problem gambling has multiple symptoms. Gamblers often gamble to try to win back money they have lost, and some gamble to relieve feelings of helplessness and anxiety. Gamblers may exhibit a number of cognitive and motivational biases that distort the perceived odds of events and that influence their preferences for gambles. Addiction by Design by Natasha Dow Schull shows how the manipulation of gambling pulls players into a trancelike state in which daily worries, social demands, and even bodily awareness fade away. Once in the zone, gambling addicts play not to win but simply to keep playing, for as long as possible, even at the cost of physical and economic exhaustion. Gamblers seek to lose themselves while the gambling industry seeks profit. Schull describes how the industry designs systems to profit from the maximum amount from gamblers and manipulates human psychology. Flag: after 1964, Natasha Dow Schüll’s Addiction by Design (2012). In some countries it is known as the tote after the totalisator, which calculates and displays bets already made. The parimutuel system is used in gambling on horse racing, greyhound racing, jai alai, and other sporting events of relatively short duration in which participants finish in a ranked order. A modified parimutuel system is also used in some lottery games. Parimutuel betting differs from fixed-odds betting in that the final payout is not determined until the pool is closed, in fixed-odds betting, the payout is agreed at the time the bet is made. Parimutuel gambling is frequently state-regulated, and it is offered in many places where gambling is otherwise illegal. Parimutuel gambling is often also offered at off-track facilities, where players may bet on the events without actually being present to observe them in person. (Wikipedia) The Paris entrepreneur Joseph Oller invented it in 1867. See Gambling and Playing Cards.

The chapter emphasizes Mr. Spitzer’s personal struggle with identity and memory, intertwined with these legal matters. The references to the legal world underscore the inadequacy of human systems (law, finance, inheritance) to fully account for the complexities of life, death, and memory. The mentions of finances and laws in this chapter symbolize the tension between human attempts at stability and the underlying flux and uncertainty of existence.   • The extensive list of royalty, animals, insects, famous buildings, and notable historical figures serves multiple symbolic and thematic purposes. The variety of figures represents the vast, interconnected, and layered nature of existence. It suggests that human life, history, and nature are woven together in a complex tapestry that transcends time and space. The mention of these figures alongside celestial and natural imagery emphasizes the novel’s theme of blurred boundaries between life and death, reality and dream. The dead are portrayed as continuing presences, their memories, and legacies lingering in the world and in the consciousness of the living, especially in the mind of Mr. Spitzer, the family lawyer who acts as a keeper of memory and history. The passage references metamorphosis explicitly and the transformation of earthly figures into ethereal or symbolic forms. This imagery aligns with the novel’s preoccupation with change, impermanence, and the fluidity of identity. The characters and elements listed symbolize various stages, states, and aspects of being, emphasizing that nothing remains fixed, and all life is subject to transformation. By mixing images of grandeur with decay, the list underscores the inevitability of death and the fading of all earthly glory. It stresses that all humans, regardless of status, are subject to mortality, a recurring theme in the novel. The cataloging of historical and mythical figures alongside ephemeral things like butterflies and moths, and symbolic references points to the fragility of memory and the losses that accumulate over time. Mr. Spitzer’s role is to navigate this complex legacy, but even he is often overwhelmed by the incompleteness and elusiveness of memory. The list contributes to the novel’s dreamlike and mythic quality. It mirrors the fragmented, layered storytelling style, where history, myth, and personal memory intertwine. This technique immerses the reader in the same blurred reality experienced by the characters, where time is non-linear and identities are fluid. The list is not meant to be read as a mere inventory but as a symbolic meditation on the themes of the novel: the passage of time, mortality, transformation, memory, and the intertwined nature of life and death. It reflects the complexity of existence that Mr. Spitzer faces as he attempts to make sense of the lives and legacies of those around him.
