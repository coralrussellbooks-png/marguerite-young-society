I. The Philosophy: Your Words Have a History and It Did Not Go Away

It is comforting to treat a word as a neutral tool. You reach for it, you use it, you move on. Whatever history is inside it feels like someone else's story, finished and behind us. Radical Immanence says time does not work that way. The past does not recede behind you as you write. It accumulates, layer by layer, pressing up into the present. Historical injustice is not a past event. It is a fact built into the words in your mouth right now. You cannot write honestly with a vocabulary you are pretending is neutral.

The word in your sentence is not a blank tool. It is the top layer of a deep stack, and writers handle the stack more directly than anyone, because your whole medium is inherited language. Every word you use was made by people who are dead. The grammar you obey was standardized by institutions that decided whose speech was correct and whose was error, whose tongue was a language and whose was only a dialect. Words carry the empires that spread them, the conquests that killed off other languages, the trades and the violences that are buried in their roots. The very idea of one proper English, or one proper anything, was enforced by schools and states and markets. None of this is a footnote to your vocabulary. It is your vocabulary's actual composition, as real as its meaning.

This is not an argument for guilt or for refusing to write. It is an argument for honesty. When you act as if your words came from nowhere, you write on ground you have agreed to pretend is neutral, and that pretense is exactly how the old harm stays buried and keeps working. To excavate the substrate is to ask where this word came from and let the answer change how you use it. The honest maker does not get to start clean. The honest maker starts by knowing what is underfoot, and what is inside the word.

II. Deep Journal Prompts

The Origin You Don't Ask About. Pick a word you reach for often without thinking. Trace it back as far as you can, its root, the language it came from, how it arrived in your mouth, what it once meant and who it was used against. Then write down how much of that is a real answer and how much you never knew. Sit with the gap. What does it cost the writing to not know?

The Speech You Were Taught to Fix. Write about the way you or your family actually talk, the words, the grammar, the rhythm that a teacher or an editor told you to correct. Trace who decided that was wrong, and who benefits from the one approved way. Then decide, plainly, what you want to do with that knowledge on the page.

III. Studio Exercises

The Linguistic Core Sample

Choose one word at the center of a piece you are writing and trace it back as far as you genuinely can before you build the sentence around it. Find its root, its history, the mouths it passed through, the power that standardized it. Where the trail goes cold, mark exactly where your knowledge ends, because that gap is part of the truth too. Write a short piece that moves through the layers, the oldest origin first, your own use of it last, with a line held for everything you could not find out. The piece is a core sample of the word's history, including the parts that were hidden from you.

The Honestly Sourced Piece

Write one piece using only language whose history you can actually feel and name: the plain words of your own childhood, the grammar you were told to fix, the speech of the people who raised you. The constraint will be hard and will make the piece smaller and plainer than you wanted. That smallness is the lesson. Notice how much of your usual writing depends on borrowing a voice you cannot source, and let this one honest piece show you what writing on known ground feels like.

IV. Cross-Medium Interference

The Layered Note

Write the history of one finished piece as a timeline that reads from the deep past up to your hand, every source of its language you know and every one you do not. Print it as a note and attach it to the piece so it travels with the work. Most author notes tell a clean origin story meant to sell. This one tells the geology. When someone reads it, they meet your writing standing on its actual ground, not the ground the market wanted them to imagine.
