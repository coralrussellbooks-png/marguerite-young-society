The Commons of Expression and the Right to Know

A Companion Document to the Constitution

The Philosophical Foundation

The media system is the primary mechanism by which shared public reality is produced and maintained. Every day, the decisions made by editors, producers, algorithms, and owners about what is covered and what is not, what is foregrounded and what is buried, whose voice is amplified and whose is filtered out, produce the version of reality that most people receive as simply the news. That production is not neutral. It encodes the interests, the blind spots, and the assumptions of the people making the decisions.

The framework this constitution is built on treats consensus reality as always a political choice. The dominant version of what is happening and why always reflects who is doing the constructing. The question of who controls that construction is the central political question of the information age.

The First Amendment as currently interpreted protects the right to speak. It does not protect the right to be heard, the right to accurate information, or the right to a media ecosystem that represents the full range of human experience rather than the range that is most profitable to represent. The protection of speech without the obligation of infrastructure produces a system in which the speech of people with the most resources drowns out the speech of everyone else, in which platforms optimize for engagement rather than truth, and in which the business model of journalism is the sale of attention to advertisers rather than the provision of accurate information to the public. The result is the media environment we currently inhabit: technically free, substantively captured.

The standard against which the media system must be measured is honest witnessing. A media system that is witnessing honestly does not decide in advance what is worth seeing. It brings the full weight of attention to what is actually there, including what is uncomfortable, including what challenges the dominant version of events, including the interior lives of people whose interior lives the dominant version has decided do not count as newsworthy. The journalism that covers the opioid epidemic as a public health crisis rather than as a criminal justice story is practicing this kind of witnessing. The journalism that covers climate change as an ongoing catastrophe rather than as a dispute between scientists and fossil fuel industry spokespersons is practicing it. Most American journalism is not. It is managing information in ways consistent with the financial interests of its owners.

The Problem of Ownership

The American media system is concentrated in the hands of a small number of corporations whose primary obligation is to their shareholders rather than to the public they ostensibly serve. Six corporations control the majority of American media: the television networks, the film studios, the publishing houses, the news outlets, the streaming platforms. The decisions those corporations make about what to cover, how to cover it, and which voices to amplify are made in the context of the financial interests of the corporation and its advertisers. Coverage that would threaten those interests is systematically underrepresented. Coverage that serves those interests is systematically overrepresented.

The local news crisis is the most acute expression of this concentration. The hedge funds and private equity firms that have purchased local newspapers across the country have applied the same financial logic they apply to any other acquisition: extract the maximum value in the minimum time, cut costs to the bone, and exit before the degradation of the product becomes a financial problem. The result is that thousands of communities have lost the journalism that covered their city council, their school board, their local courts, their local businesses. Communities that have lost local news show measurable declines in civic participation, increases in municipal borrowing costs, and reductions in electoral competition. Local journalism is not a luxury. It is the infrastructure of local democracy.

The consolidation of social media into a small number of companies whose algorithms determine what billions of people see is the newest and most powerful expression of the same problem. The algorithm is not neutral. It is designed to maximize engagement, and engagement is maximized by content that produces strong emotional responses, particularly outrage and fear. The content that produces outrage and fear is often false or misleading. A platform that profits from the spread of false and misleading content has a financial interest in not addressing it. Presenting the algorithm as a neutral technical system rather than a value-laden political choice is exactly the move this constitution's framework is designed to name: a dominant construction presenting itself as natural order.

Independent Public Media

The Constitutional Obligation

This constitution establishes independent public media as a constitutional obligation. Independent means editorially independent from the government that funds it: the model of the BBC, the CBC, and public broadcasters in the Nordic countries, where the funding mechanism insulates editorial decisions from political interference. The government funds the institution; the institution makes its own editorial decisions; neither the government nor any other funder has the power to determine what is covered or how.

Public means serving the full public, not the median consumer, not the demographic most attractive to advertisers, not the audience that already agrees with the editorial perspective. The public broadcaster has an obligation to represent the full range of human experience in this country: the communities that commercial media has decided are not profitable to cover, the stories that challenge the dominant version of events, the voices that are systematically excluded from the commercial media ecosystem. This is an editorial obligation, not a courtesy: the refusal to reproduce the hierarchy that decides which people's lives and experiences count as worth covering.

The funding mechanism must insulate the institution from political pressure. The current public broadcasting system in the United States, PBS and NPR, is funded through a combination of federal appropriations, state appropriations, and private donations, all of which are subject to political pressure. The threat to cut federal funding has been used repeatedly to influence editorial decisions. The alternative is a dedicated funding mechanism, such as a tax on spectrum use by commercial broadcasters or a small fee on digital advertising, that provides stable funding without annual Congressional appropriation and therefore without the leverage that annual appropriation provides.

Plurality as Obligation

The public media system is obligated to represent the full plurality of human voices and experiences, not to reproduce the consensus version of public reality. This means active investment in journalism from and about communities that commercial media systematically undercoverts: rural communities, immigrant communities, indigenous communities, communities of color, disabled communities, communities outside the major metropolitan areas that currently dominate media production. The production of public media from within these communities, not about them from outside, is a practice of honest witnessing: the refusal to treat people who are not the default subject of journalism as background material for someone else's story.

Language access is part of plurality. A country with the linguistic diversity of the United States requires a public media system with substantial production in languages other than English, not as a concession to non-English speakers but as the honest acknowledgment that the full range of public life in this country is not conducted in English. A public media system that only covers the English-speaking public is not covering the full public.

Disinformation and the Limits of Free Speech

This constitution names coordinated disinformation campaigns in referendum contests as election fraud. This document extends that principle and defines its limits. Free speech is the right of every person to express their views without government suppression. It is not the right of any person or institution to deliberately spread false information for the purpose of manipulating democratic processes, suppressing the votes of specific populations, or producing public confusion about matters of documented fact in ways that serve specific financial or political interests.

The distinction between protected speech and election fraud is not a distinction between true and false speech generally. Falsehood is protected speech in most contexts. The distinction is between the organic expression of views, however mistaken, and the coordinated industrial production and distribution of false information for the purpose of corrupting democratic processes. The tobacco industry's decades-long campaign to manufacture doubt about the link between smoking and cancer was not a good-faith expression of scientific disagreement. It was a deliberate fraud. The fossil fuel industry's campaign to manufacture doubt about climate change is the same fraud at larger scale. The political operatives who produce and distribute false information about voting procedures, polling locations, and electoral outcomes are not exercising free speech. They are committing election fraud.

Enforcing this distinction requires an independent regulatory body with genuine authority, adequate resources, and structural independence from the political interests that benefit from the fraud it is charged with preventing. The Federal Election Commission as currently constituted is designed to deadlock: it has six members, three from each party, and cannot act without bipartisan agreement, which is rarely achievable when one party benefits from the disinformation the commission is supposed to prevent. The rebuilt commission has a structure that produces genuine independence from partisan interest, clear authority, and real enforcement capacity.

The Right to Know

Democracy requires an informed public. An informed public requires access to accurate information about what the government is doing and why. The Freedom of Information Act provides some of this access, subject to extensive exemptions and a process slow enough to be functionally inaccessible for most citizens and most journalists working on deadline. The classification system, which removes information from public access on grounds of national security, has been used extensively to conceal information whose primary value to the government is embarrassment prevention rather than genuine security protection.

This constitution's transparency provisions, the real-time disclosure of campaign contributions, the forty-eight-hour disclosure of lobbying contacts, the public availability of officials' financial records, are the demand that power be seen. The right to know is the affirmative corollary of those provisions: not only must the government disclose what the constitution requires, but citizens have a right of access to government information that is enforceable in court, with the burden of proof on the government to demonstrate why disclosure should be denied rather than on the citizen to demonstrate why it should be provided.

The classification system is subject to independent judicial review. The national security claim is not self-executing: the government must demonstrate to an independent court that specific information poses a specific genuine security risk if disclosed. The blanket classification of entire categories of information, the reclassification of information already in the public record, and the classification of information about government programs that are themselves constitutionally questionable are prohibited. The government that classifies information to avoid accountability for its own conduct is using secrecy as a tool of self-protection rather than national protection.

Digital Rights and Platform Accountability

The major digital platforms, the companies that control the infrastructure through which most public communication now occurs, occupy a position with no precedent in the history of communication. They are private companies with no First Amendment obligations. They are also the effective public square: the place where political discourse, civic organization, community formation, and the distribution of news and information primarily happen. The legal framework that treats them as neutral conduits, immune from liability for the content they distribute, was designed for a much earlier version of the internet and does not account for the active role platform algorithms play in determining what content is amplified and what is suppressed.

The privacy provision of this constitution, the requirement that no government or corporation collect personal data without informed revocable consent, is the starting point. The surveillance capitalism model, in which the product being sold is the user's attention and the raw material is the user's personal data, is a constitutional violation under that provision. The person whose browsing history, location data, social connections, purchasing patterns, and political views are collected, aggregated, and sold to advertisers has not genuinely consented simply because they clicked through a terms of service agreement. Long-form terms of service that no one reads and that cannot be declined without losing access to the service are not consent. They are the managed appearance of consent in a system the user has no meaningful power to decline.

Platform accountability for algorithmic amplification of harmful content requires a regulatory framework that can keep pace with technological change: not specific rules about specific content, which are always behind the current form of the harm, but principles and obligations that apply regardless of the specific technology. The platform that profits from the amplification of content that suppresses votes, produces political violence, or spreads demonstrably false information about public health has a legal obligation to the public that its private legal status does not extinguish. The framework for enforcing that obligation is developed through the democratic process, subject to the First Amendment protections that apply to genuine speech, and administered by an independent regulatory body with the technical expertise to understand what it is regulating.

The Transition

The media transition is already happening, badly: the collapse of commercial journalism, the rise of platform-mediated information distribution, the fragmentation of the shared information environment. What is needed is not the preservation of the current system but the deliberate construction of its replacement, built on the principles this document establishes rather than on the financial logic that produced the current crisis.

The investment in public media, in local journalism infrastructure, in digital platforms that serve the public interest rather than the advertising market, in the media literacy education that gives people the tools to navigate the information environment they actually inhabit: these are not narrow media policy questions. They are the prerequisites for the democracy this constitution requires. The people cannot be the government if they cannot know what the government is doing. The people cannot make informed decisions about the laws that govern them if the information environment has been optimized for engagement rather than truth. The floor of the information commons is the floor of democracy itself.
