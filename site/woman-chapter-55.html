Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 55

← Chapter 54 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 56 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

The ordered life is this chapter's subject, and its collapse is the pillar. Mr. Spitzer maps his existence out in composition and collection, holds himself apart from chance, refuses the practical engagements of law and gambling, and the control is the masculine domain of order and legacy and the mask the book lays over dissolution, an order kept scrupulously above a self coming apart beneath it. Peron's death sets fraternal rivalry and fragmented identity going, masculine selves built through opposition and competition and inheritance, and the sidelining of women from that whole economy of heirs and legacies is the erasure the book keeps noting even as it follows the men.

His "conservative frozen investments" in "sunken ships" and "forgotten empires" are the submerged histories of women, valued as relics and never as living participants, and the financial disempowerment his brother's gambling brings on shows the precarity even of male authority under a system where chance unseats inheritance. His impaired vision, his inability to "see the music," his alienation from his own body, are the fractured self and the body as the place the self comes apart, a man who cannot reconcile the pieces of himself. The house split into mirrored halves, the strict boundary between the two brothers, is the binary the book keeps dismantling, and Peron's laughter at the "systematized life" is the disruption that exposes how unstable the sorting always was.

Broken bones and a "paper-thin skull" and "shattered glass" carry the vulnerability under the masculine ideal, and Mr. Spitzer drifting through places and times, occupying his brother's place without knowing it, is the fluid, unstable self the book prefers to any fixed one. His sense of impending disappearance, the "lack of mourning" that will meet it, is invisibility and erasure worked on the witness, and the "carpe diem" of Peron against the "hoc opus, hic labor est" of Joachim sets two masculine archetypes the book will not let stand as whole men, the impulsive and the disciplined both partial. The exploitation runs through the kinship, Joachim's sacrifices and losses for Peron the unseen labor the book keeps finding, and his "retinal rivalry" and "doubled vision" are dissociation and multiplicity carried in the eye itself.

The chapter ends on the loss of the twin house. Its mirrored halves collapse, and with them the rigid division of space that held the two brothers apart, and the collapse is the fall of a patriarchal order built on binaries and the book's whole motion toward fluidity and multiplicity completed in a building. The strict exactitude of the architecture, the desire for balance and control, gives way, and what stands after is not order restored but the categories gone, self and brother and space no longer sortable, the twin house down and the single self it was built to house dissolved with it.
