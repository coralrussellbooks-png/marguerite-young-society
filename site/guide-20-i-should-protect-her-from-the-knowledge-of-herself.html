Chapter 20: I Should Protect Her from the Knowledge of Herself

← Chapter 19 · Guide Home · Chapter 21 →

Research notes: Chapter 20

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Faith, God, and the Supernatural

Symbols: Hair, Wig, and Baldness · The House · Weddings and Funerals

Philosophy: Augustine and Christian Theology · Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Ancient Egypt · The Bible · The History of Opium

Chapter 20 is the last chapter of the Miss MacIntosh sequence: the four chapters that delivered the revelation and its aftermath across two nights and a dawn. After this, the novel will move on. Other stories, other characters, other rooms.

But before it moves on, Young gives us one more morning with Vera lying in the bed, eyes open now, watching Miss MacIntosh sleep. And she gives us the decision Vera makes.

It is the most important decision in the novel.

What actually happens in Chapter 20

It is full dawn. Vera lies beside the sleeping Miss MacIntosh and looks at her in the light. She sees, for the first time in daylight and without the excuse of shock or darkness, everything: the bald head, the missing breast, the deprivations that the night had revealed and that the morning confirms. She thinks about what she has seen. She thinks about God. She thinks about hair.

And then she makes her resolution: she will go on as if she never saw. She will pretend. She will iron Miss MacIntosh’s shirts with new eagerness, recite the genealogies, learn the ancient weights and measures, keep the secret locked in her own capacious heart. She will protect Miss MacIntosh from the knowledge of herself.

The chapter ends with Vera making the explicit promise that closes the sequence: “I should hold to every moment as if I lived with a dying man, and I should protect her from the knowledge of herself.”

The decision

Vera’s resolution to pretend is not cowardice. Young is careful about this. Vera is fourteen and has just had the floor taken out from under her for the second time in her life: the first being whatever happened to her father, about whom we learn only that he fell “while walking a frozen cable stretched between two snow-capped mountain peaks.” She knows what she is doing and why.

She is choosing to protect. To hold the secret in her own capacious heart and not let it surface through a flicker of an eyelid or a wandering gesture. She is choosing to give Miss MacIntosh the dignity of her own concealment. And she is choosing, at fourteen, to live with a dying person and know it and say nothing.

This is an enormous thing to choose. Young renders it plainly, without sentimentality, as the kind of moral decision that only becomes visible in retrospect as what it was: a child deciding, quietly and without fanfare, to be charitable. Which is what Miss MacIntosh asked of her, in the dark, pressing her claw-shaped hand to Vera’s forehead. There, that will teach you charity.

The teaching worked. The lesson cost everything.

Seeing her plain

Chapter 20 contains some of the most sustained and beautiful writing in the novel about what it actually means to see another person.

Young gives us Vera watching Miss MacIntosh in sleep with a clarity she has never had before: her face “sublimely peaceful even in its broken, anguished beauties such as I had hardly noticed or appreciated before.” The face that was always present, the face Vera saw every day for years, is only now being seen. The bald head, the “thin-lidded eyes closed in thin sleep,” the narrow and intolerable vacancy where the breast is missing, and in the middle of it, something that Young names as “affirmation, strong and positive, that which God Himself should tremble to see.”

Young refuses to resolve Miss MacIntosh into tragedy. She was stripped bare. She was also, in her stripping bare, something Young calls a sad masterpiece. Not a victim of her circumstances but a person who made herself, under impossible conditions, into the one authority Vera had truly known. That is the affirmation the chapter insists on finding in the wreckage.

The question Vera asks (“Whose work, I vaguely wondered, was this? Whose accomplishment, this sad masterpiece?”) does not receive an answer. It was Miss MacIntosh’s own work, built out of the materials available: proverbs, common sense, a wig, a salmon-colored corset, the courage to keep winding the alarm clock every day.

Was she deserted at the altar by her bridegroom or her bride?

Young gives Vera a list of questions about the hair: was it hair by hair “through many hesitant years, or all at one blow”? Was it her broken heart? “Was she in mourning for someone dead”? “Had some old crow plucked each hair away?” And then: “was she deserted at the altar by her bridegroom or her bride? Was she her only lover?”

Young does not answer any of these questions. She lists them as possibilities, in Vera’s wondering voice, and moves on. But the two final questions carry the weight of the sequence: the bridegroom or the bride. Both options held open simultaneously. And the last one, the strangest: was she her only lover?

A person whose inner life had no acceptable outward form, who could not find or keep a love that matched what she was, who had, perhaps, turned the capacity inward. Was she her only lover? Young asks it and does not answer it, which is the most respectful thing she could do.

Was God bald too

Near the chapter’s end, Vera’s mind drifts into a long meditation on God. Was God bald, “or bearded so that birds might hide” behind His dreaming hair? “What was God, defined in so many terms”: a fuller’s soap, a refining fire, “the light of the morning when the sun arises,” a stone of grace, “a friend that sticketh closer than a brother,” “the shadow of a great rock in a weary land?”

This passage is Young at her most formally ambitious and also her most playful, piling Biblical epithets for God one on top of another until they accumulate into something that feels less like theology and more like Vera’s mind refusing to land anywhere, floating through the possibilities the way Miss MacIntosh’s opium guests float through the alphabet.

But underneath the floating there is a serious question: if Miss MacIntosh’s baldness is a kind of nakedness before God, what does that say about God? If she is the sad masterpiece, is God the artist who made her so? Is God also somehow stripped, also somehow exposed, also bald in the sense of having nothing to hide behind? Young leaves this open. She almost always leaves this open. But she asks it, here, at dawn, over the sleeping head of the most practical woman in the novel.

What to watch for as you keep reading

Vera’s promise to protect Miss MacIntosh from the knowledge of herself is the emotional foundation of everything that follows in the novel. Every time Vera thinks about Miss MacIntosh (in memory, in the long search, in the chapters still to come) she is keeping that promise, or failing to keep it, or renegotiating what keeping it means. The novel is now about to expand enormously. Other characters, other histories, other geographies. You will sometimes go many chapters without returning to Vera directly. But you are now carrying what Vera carries: the knowledge of the bald head on the striped pillow, and the decision made at fourteen to go on as if she never saw.

The title of the novel appears in Chapter 20 for the first time in its full form, embedded in the prose: my plain darling. My darling. Not Miss MacIntosh, that plain nursemaid. My darling. The relationship has been named, at last, for what it always was.
