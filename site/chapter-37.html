Summary

Vera describes the world as Catherine experiences it: drifting mirrors, ceilings that become floors, clocks striking impossible hours. She asks who the dreamer is. Catherine declares that order is a madman's dream. She still schemes to bring Hannah back, but Hannah will not return. Death has throttled her, and silence follows.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a surreal vision of a world where mirrors drift, the ceiling becomes a fluid floor, clocks strike inept hours, and dolphins bark unusually. This sets up the motif of a "mistaken dream" with no clear boundaries between heaven and hell, life and death. The question "But who was the dreamer?" introduces the existential uncertainty that pervades the chapter.
  • Vera recounts a discussion with her mother, Catherine, and Mr. Spitzer about the nature of order and chaos. Her mother insists that "Order...was a mad man’s dream," a "suicidal plan omitting the irrational elements," emphasizing the complexity and irrationality of life and the universe. This passage challenges the human desire for simple, orderly explanations.
  • Catherine is portrayed as someone living under the "pervasive influence of opium," caught in a half-conscious dream state where time is an illusion. Death is described not as an end but as an "immeasurable extension" of this dream, blending life and death indistinguishably. Catherine’s inability or refusal to awaken to this knowledge symbolizes human denial of mortality and oblivion.
  • The chapter describes numerous surreal reversals highlighting the instability of identity and perception. This underscores the theme that nothing is fixed or certain, not even one’s own self or reality.
  • Cousin Hannah, a strong suffrage figure, is presented as having passed beyond death in a way Catherine cannot accept. Her adventures contrast with Catherine’s passive existence. Hannah’s death is final and unescapable, symbolizing the inevitability of mortality despite human pride and strength.
  • Catherine fears complete extinction that "land of infinite greyness" without memory, ego, or voice. This fear keeps her "horizontal," an invalid clinging to life’s illusions rather than facing the void. This passage poignantly captures human dread of nothingness and the unknown beyond death.
  • The chapter closes with the recognition that no matter Catherine’s plotting or longing, Cousin Hannah will not return. Death has "throttled her," and the echoes of life and struggle fade into silence. Mr. Spitzer’s metaphor of hearing silence after the last requiem symbolizes acceptance of death’s ultimate finality.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Catherine Cartwheel

  • Central figure whose fragmented consciousness and philosophical musings drive much of the chapter.
  • She is an invalid, sedated with opium, caught between life and death, dreaming yet partially aware.
  • She embodies the existential struggle with meaning, identity, and the fear of oblivion.

Mr. Spitzer

  • A morose figure who provides a skeptical or pessimistic counterpoint.
  • He lies and deceives but also embodies an anguished search for truth.
  • His reflections on death and memory contribute to the chapter’s philosophical depth.

Cousin Hannah

  • A symbol of strength and defiance in the face of mortality.
  • Her story is a narrative of resistance, a woman who cannot be possessed or ruled.
  • Her death and burial represent the finality Catherine tries to avoid accepting.

Research: Themes

Reality vs. Dream / Illusion vs. Truth

See: Illusion and Reality · Dreams and Imagination

  • The chapter questions what is real and what is dream, emphasizing the fluidity and uncertainty between the two.
  • Catherine and others seem trapped in an ongoing dream where time and space lose meaning.
  • The idea that life itself may be a mistake or dream without end is prominent, and death is seen as an immeasurable extension of this dream.

Death and Life: Blurred Boundaries

See: Death and Mortality

  • The chapter repeatedly blurs the line between living and dead; the living are those who have already died, and the dead are still living in some form.
  • Death is not a clear-cut event but an ongoing state or transformation, sometimes portrayed as madness or a mountebank (a deceiver).
  • Catherine fears oblivion, the total extinction of self, and clings to life despite its illusions.

Identity and Transformation

See: Identity and the Self

  • Identity is unstable and mutable, with images of people changing genders, faces, or even species.
  • This theme questions the essence of self, suggesting that we might be composites of many selves or none at all.
  • The recurring motif of masks, doubles, and shifting forms highlights the fracturing or multiplicity of identity.

Memory and Oblivion

See: Memory and the Past

  • Catherine fears the loss of memory and self-awareness.
  • There is a contemplation of a “land of infinite greyness” where nothing remains: no memory, image, or ego.
  • This fear underlies her refusal to fully surrender to death or oblivion.

Feminine Power and Resistance

See: Women, Gender, and Marriage · Fear and Courage

  • Cousin Hannah, a suffrage captain, symbolizes strength, resistance, and an indomitable spirit.
  • Her death and burial contrast with her bold life, and her story reflects the struggle for identity and agency beyond traditional roles.
  • Cousin Hannah’s adventures in exotic lands and her defiance of powerful men underscore themes of female empowerment and autonomy.

Research: Resources

  • The philosophy of reversals consists of various philosophical ideas and concepts related to the notion of something being turned upside down or reversed. It explores the nature of reversals in different domains: time, causality, values, societal structures. It examines how these reversals impact our understanding of reality, ethics, and meaning. Reversals in literature and art are used to create suspense, irony, and thematic complexity. It can be used to challenge expectations, expose hidden truths, and engage audiences on a deeper level. Aristotle’s reversal and recognition, Baudrillard’s Principle of Reversal examines how oppositions and differences can be overturned, leading to new forms of understanding and meaning. The philosophy of reversals is a prominent theme that permeates the narrative, revealing a worldview where binaries dissolve and opposites interpenetrate, creating a complex and unstable reality. This chapter explores the idea that opposites, such as night and day, sun and moon, beginning and end are not separate entities but are deeply intertwined and often indistinguishable. The narrator, Vera, reflects on how "his night was his day, and his day was his night," and "his dawn was his twilight, and his moon was his sun, and his sun was his moon, and his beginning was his end, and his end was his beginning". These statements encapsulate the core of the reversal philosophy: the collapsing of distinctions that we usually take for granted. The narrative also conveys a sense that reality itself is fluid and mutable, with physical and metaphysical elements constantly changing places or merging. For example, "there should be these somersaultings as when the ceiling was the mirroring floor, trees of enormous crystal growing with their roots in the clouded sky, their branches under the earth". This imagery suggests a world where spatial and conceptual norms are inverted, challenging linear and stable perceptions of existence. The chapter touches on the paradoxical nature of life and death, love and its absence. Vera notes that "Love might wear the face of death, the grinning skull, as death might wear the face of love", emphasizing that what seem like opposites are reflections or transformations of each other. This complicates the understanding of identity and existence, as individuals may be "other than we seemed," with two forms "blended into one which was amorphous, being neither". The philosophy of reversals extends to the cosmic scale, where even celestial bodies and natural laws are subject to change and ambiguity. The narrative questions the fixity of time and space, suggesting that "there should be no harmony, no silent music like the music of the spheres" in the world beyond, because "nothing should be simple there because nothing was simple here". This reflects a skepticism of absolute order or finality in the universe. It presents a vision of a world and perhaps a universe where dualities are illusory, and existence is marked by continuous transformation and contradiction. This philosophy challenges conventional binaries and invites readers to consider a reality that is simultaneously unified and divided, stable and unstable, known and unknowable. This philosophy is central to the novel's exploration of existence, identity, and perception, illustrating a world where certainty dissolves and the dreamlike nature of reality is revealed.
  • Time, perception vs reality, distorted time perception, and time as a mental construct is described as an emergent property in physics. Einstein stated that the distinction between past, present, and future is only a “Stubbornly persistent illusion”. The implications of the illusion of time for humans is it would redefine reality, raise profound philosophical questions (about the nature of existence, consciousness, and the meaning of time), and lead to a shift in perspective encouraging people to focus on the present moment and appreciate the flow of experience. Carlo Rovelli proposes that time is not a fundamental aspect of reality, but rather an emergent phenomenon. He suggests that time may be a complex network of events, and our perception of its flow is a projection onto this network. The chapter presents time as a profound mystery deeply connected to themes of illusion, memory, mortality, and the blending of opposites. Time is not a fixed, measurable entity but a fluid and enigmatic force that shapes and is shaped by human perception, dreams, and the cosmos.
  • Life as a state of experience and potential - Socrates thought death might be a dreamless sleep or passage to another realm where one can converse with the dead. Existentialism believes death is an inevitable aspect of human existence and that it can lead to a heightened sense of self-awareness and responsibility. Epicureanism argues death cannot harm a person, as it’s either not yet experienced (while alive) or not experienced at all (once dead). Death is defined as the loss of the capacity to engage in vital activities and the cessation of consciousness. Wittgenstein said that death is not an experience in life. Schopenhauer believed that understanding death as the ultimate end of life can provide a perspective on the meaning of life itself. Freud thought the life instinct (Eros) and the death instinct (Thanatos) are fundamental forces in human behavior. The chapter depicts life as a state of ongoing experience and potential characterized by uncertainty, illusion, and transformation. It is not a fixed reality but a mutable and often paradoxical condition where life and death, truth and illusion, identity and multiplicity coexist and where human understanding is limited and provisional.
  • The meaning of life in relation to death - Stoicism emphasized that facing death can lead to a deeper understanding of life’s meaning and the ephemeral nature of earthly possessions. Nietzsche used the thought experiment of eternal return (recurrence) to encourage individuals to examine the meaning and value of their lives, given the possibility of living them again and again. The chapter conveys that the meaning of life is deeply connected to death, and that understanding or embracing this connection is part of the human condition. It suggests that love, life, death, and identity are bound in an intricate, often paradoxical relationship that transcends simple definitions, and that this profound ambiguity is central to our experience of existence.
  • The paradox of life and death - some philosophers see life and death as a continuous process, with life as a moment of lucidity within the vastness of existence. Others view death as a finality, a cessation of consciousness and experience. The chapter conveys that life and death are two sides of the same coin, eternally connected, and that understanding this paradox is beyond simple human comprehension, being filled with mystery, ambiguity, and endless transformation.
  • Descartes’ dream argument - He used the experience of dreaming to question the certainty of our perceptions and beliefs. He argued that if we cannot definitively distinguish between waking and dreaming, then our sensory experiences, which are the foundation of our knowledge, might be illusions. There is philosophical skepticism because the possibility of dreams may be genuine or a fabrication. It leads one to question the validity of knowledge of the external world. Freud believed dreams are a symbolic representation of unconscious thoughts and desires. Jung expanded on Freud’s ideas that dreams can also reveal universal, archetypal patterns in the human psyche. Some theories suggest dreams are a natural byproduct of the brain’s activity during sleep, possibly involving memory consolidation, emotional processing, or the clearing out of mental clutter. Neuroscience has provided insights into the physiological processes which in turn influences philosophical discussion about the nature and functions of dreams. Some philosophers think dreams are a way of accessing another realm of existence or experiencing different perspectives on reality. The chapter concludes that dreams are not simply illusions but fundamental to existence and consciousness, blurring distinctions between reality and unreality, life and death, self and other, and that this ambiguity is a central and inescapable part of human experience.
  • Gender as a social construct, refers to the socially created roles, behaviors, expressions, and identities associated with being a woman, man, boy, or girl. It’s not inherently determined by biology, but rather shaped by cultural norms, societal expectations, and historical contexts. This means gender is not fixed and can vary across cultures and time. Not a biological reality, socially created, varies across cultures, dynamic and fluid, individual identity, distinct from biological sex attributes. The earliest recorded philosophical thought on gender emerged in ancient Greece with Plato and Aristotle, also Mary Wollstonecraft, Judith Sargent Murray, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, John Locke, Pythagoras. The chapter and novel presents gender as a fluid and changeable construct rather than a rigid, innate fact, using metaphorical and dreamlike language to question fixed dichotomies between male and female, self and other, life and death.

  • Atlantis can be a metaphor for something that is no longer attainable. It is a fictional island used as an allegory for the hubris of nations in works by Plato. It has had a large influence on literature, arts, and culture. Atlantis in this chapter serves as a metaphor for the lost or hidden realms beneath the surface of consciousness and history: a place where memories, dreams, and vanished realities reside. It underscores the novel's meditation on transience, the passing of time, and the elusive quest for meaning in a shifting and often inscrutable world.
