To All My Darlings

Chapter 37

On Chapter 37 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 10, 2026

← Chapter 36 · Home · Chapter 38 →

Content note: Vera’s mother, Catherine Cartwheel, is addicted to opium.

Summary

Vera describes the heaven Catherine expects, as disordered as this world: drifting mirrors, ceilings that become floors, clocks striking inept hours. Catherine has always said that order is “a mad man’s dream,” and that Mr. Spitzer, “wishing to be agreeable, had made a false report as to what might be upon the other side.” Drugged and nearly inert, she cannot tell life from death, and asks who the dreamer is. She sees the reversals of dream: man becoming woman, God become dog, trees with their roots in the sky. Mr. Spitzer insists that Hannah is dead and buried, gone where there is no consciousness and no memory, but Catherine cannot admit her extinction. What she fears is a grey land where none makes reply, and she stays horizontal on her mother-of-pearl boat bed so as not to take that step. She recalls Hannah’s adventures in the harems, unveiling her bearded face to a sheik who went mad, and bowing to a sheik who proved to be a beautiful woman. The riderless horse has already come, and Hannah will not return.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a surreal vision of a world where mirrors drift, the ceiling becomes a fluid floor, clocks strike inept hours, and dolphins bark unusually. This sets up the motif of a “mistaken dream” with no clear boundaries between heaven and hell, life and death. The question “But who was the dreamer?” introduces the existential uncertainty that pervades the chapter.
  • Vera recounts a discussion with her mother, Catherine, and Mr. Spitzer about the nature of order and chaos. Her mother insists that “Order… was a mad man’s dream,” a “suicidal plan omitting the irrational elements,” emphasizing the complexity and irrationality of life and the universe. This passage challenges the human desire for simple, orderly explanations.
  • Catherine is portrayed as someone living under the “pervasive influence of opium,” caught in a half-conscious dream state where time is an illusion. For Catherine, death is not an end but an “immeasurable extension” of this dream, blending life and death indistinguishably. Catherine’s inability or refusal to awaken to this knowledge symbolizes human denial of mortality and oblivion.
  • The chapter describes numerous surreal reversals highlighting the instability of identity and perception. This underscores the theme that nothing is fixed or certain, not even one’s own self or reality.
  • Cousin Hannah, a strong suffrage figure, is presented as having passed beyond death in a way Catherine cannot accept. Her adventures contrast with Catherine’s passive existence. Hannah’s death is final and unescapable, symbolizing the inevitability of mortality despite human pride and strength.
  • Catherine fears complete extinction, that “land of infinite greyness,” without memory, ego, or voice. This fear keeps her “horizontal,” an invalid clinging to life’s illusions rather than facing the void. This passage poignantly captures human dread of nothingness and the unknown beyond death.
  • The chapter closes with the recognition that no matter Catherine’s plotting or longing, Cousin Hannah will not return. Death has “throttled her,” and the echoes of life and struggle fade into silence. Mr. Spitzer’s metaphor of hearing silence after the last requiem symbolizes acceptance of death’s ultimate finality.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • Catherine asks who the dreamer is, “astonished that she was not the dreamer.” Who dreams her?
  • “In every lie was a grain of horrible truth—and in every truth was a beautiful lie.” Which kind does Mr. Spitzer tell?
  • Catherine stays “horizontal” to avoid one step. What step is it?

Characters

  • Mr. Spitzer was “that mourner who had felt no grief, no grief but his lack of grief.” Is that a kind of grief?
  • Why does Catherine plan “a future card game” with the dead?
  • One sheik went mad at Hannah’s bearded face, and another proved to be “a beautiful lady.” What do the two stories say about disguise?

Connections

  • In Chapter 34 Catherine was married by a St. Bernard who spoke church Latin. Here “God was dog,” and birds speak “ecclesiastical Latin.” What links them?
  • The riderless horse first came through the window on the day Hannah died, in Chapter 32. Why does it return here?
  • In Chapter 35 Mr. Spitzer called Hannah a card cheat. Here she is “one whose last card long ago was played.” Who won the game?

Reflection

  • Is order “a mad man’s dream”?
  • If life was always a dream, “how should death be different from life”?
  • Can a person be “her own dark lover”?

Research: Characters

Catherine Cartwheel

  • Central figure whose fragmented consciousness and philosophical musings drive much of the chapter.
  • She is an invalid, sedated with opium, caught between life and death, dreaming yet partially aware.
  • She embodies the existential struggle with meaning, identity, and the fear of oblivion.
  • She is “this horizontal invalid upon her mother-of-pearl boat bed.”
  • Her opinions, “even as to the simplest matters such as whether it was night or day,” “were always divided.”
  • “How many years since she had seen the sea which she could have seen at any time if she had ever allowed the shutters to be opened?”
  • “She would be crazed by dreams she had not dreamed.”

Mr. Spitzer

  • A morose figure who provides a skeptical or pessimistic counterpoint.
  • He lies and deceives but also embodies an anguished search for truth.
  • His reflections on death and memory contribute to the chapter’s philosophical depth.
  • “Mr. Spitzer, wishing to be agreeable, had made a false report as to what might be upon the other side.”
  • He longs for “some single point of view which would be at all times constant and unchanging.”
  • At Hannah’s burial he was “that mourner who had felt no grief, no grief but his lack of grief.”
  • He plucks “at his dead ear,” and “heard the silence.”

Cousin Hannah

  • A symbol of strength and defiance in the face of mortality.
  • Her story is a narrative of resistance, a woman who cannot be possessed or ruled.
  • Her death and burial represent the finality Catherine tries to avoid accepting.
  • She is “this daughter of the codfish aristocracy, her silver helmet gleaming, her scimitar uplifted like a new moon.”
  • “Death had been, at last, her bridegroom.”
  • Mr. Spitzer thought “she had been her own dark lover.”
  • She unveiled her “great, gaunt, bearded face” to a sheik who “had gone stark, staring mad,” and bowed to another sheik who was “a beautiful lady with large liquid eyes.”

Peron Spitzer

  • “Peron had been right—and Joachim had been wrong.”
  • Hannah is “gone where poker-faced Peron was.”

The Black Coachman

  • Catherine sees “perhaps the black coachman, the diffused radiance of the carriage lamps in the fog.”
  • No one should see Hannah again, “not even when the black coachman came, and he was dead.”

Research: Themes

Reality versus Dream, Illusion versus Truth

See: Illusion and Reality · Dreams and Imagination

  • The chapter questions what is real and what is dream, emphasizing the fluidity and uncertainty between the two.
  • Catherine and others seem trapped in an ongoing dream where time and space lose meaning.
  • The idea that life itself may be a mistake or dream without end is prominent, and Catherine sees death as an immeasurable extension of this dream.

Death and Life: Blurred Boundaries

See: Death and Mortality

  • The chapter repeatedly blurs the line between living and dead; the living are those who have already died, and the dead are still living in some form.
  • For Catherine, death is not a clear-cut event but an ongoing state or transformation, sometimes portrayed as madness or a mountebank (a deceiver). “Death was madness. Madness ruled supreme.”
  • Catherine fears oblivion, the total extinction of self, and clings to life despite its illusions.

Identity and Transformation

See: Identity and the Self

  • Identity is unstable and mutable, with images of people changing genders, faces, or even species. “One saw that God was dog.”
  • This theme questions the essence of self, suggesting that we might be composites of many selves or none at all.
  • The recurring motif of masks, doubles, and shifting forms highlights the fracturing or multiplicity of identity.

Memory and Oblivion

See: Memory and the Past

  • Catherine fears the loss of memory and self-awareness.
  • There is a contemplation of a “land of infinite greyness” where nothing remains: no memory, image, or ego.
  • This fear underlies her refusal to fully surrender to death or oblivion.

Order and Chance

See: Order, Chaos, and Discipline · Fate and Chance

  • “Order, had she not always said, was a mad man’s dream, the maddest dream of all.”
  • She asks “how should we live except by accident, for were we not already dead.”
  • The music shakes “the enamelled heavens and the earth like a dice box,” and she sees “stars marked like dice.”

Feminine Power and Resistance

See: Women, Gender, and Marriage · Fear and Courage

  • Cousin Hannah, a suffrage captain, symbolizes strength, resistance, and an indomitable spirit.
  • Her death and burial contrast with her bold life, and her story reflects the struggle for identity and agency beyond traditional roles.
  • Cousin Hannah’s adventures in exotic lands and her defiance of powerful men underscore themes of female empowerment and autonomy.

Research: Resources

  • “or there should be these reversals”: The philosophy of reversals consists of various philosophical ideas and concepts related to the notion of something being turned upside down or reversed. It explores the nature of reversals in different domains: time, causality, values, societal structures. It examines how these reversals impact our understanding of reality, ethics, and meaning. Reversals in literature and art are used to create suspense, irony, and thematic complexity. It can be used to challenge expectations, expose hidden truths, and engage audiences on a deeper level. Aristotle’s reversal and recognition. Baudrillard’s Principle of Reversal examines how oppositions and differences can be overturned, leading to new forms of understanding and meaning (Jean Baudrillard, Symbolic Exchange and Death, 1976). The philosophy of reversals is a prominent theme that permeates the narrative, revealing a worldview where binaries dissolve and opposites interpenetrate, creating a complex and unstable reality. This chapter explores the idea that opposites, such as night and day, sun and moon, beginning and end are not separate entities but are deeply intertwined and often indistinguishable. Vera’s “his night was his day” passage is in Chapter 61. See the Chapter 61 resources. The narrative also conveys a sense that reality itself is fluid and mutable, with physical and metaphysical elements constantly changing places or merging. For example, “there should be these somersaultings as when the ceiling was the mirroring floor, trees of enormous crystal growing with their roots in the clouded sky, their branches under the earth.” This imagery suggests a world where spatial and conceptual norms are inverted, challenging linear and stable perceptions of existence. The chapter touches on the paradoxical nature of life and death, love and its absence. Vera notes that “Love might wear the face of death, the grinning skull, as death might wear the face of love,” emphasizing that what seem like opposites are reflections or transformations of each other. This complicates the understanding of identity and existence, as individuals may be “other than we seemed,” with two forms “blended into one which was amorphous, being neither.” The philosophy of reversals extends to the cosmic scale, where even celestial bodies and natural laws are subject to change and ambiguity. The narrative questions the fixity of time and space, suggesting that “there should be no harmony, no silent music like the music of the spheres” in the world beyond, because “nothing should be simple there because nothing was simple here.” This reflects a skepticism of absolute order or finality in the universe. It presents a vision of a world and perhaps a universe where dualities are illusory, and existence is marked by continuous transformation and contradiction. This philosophy challenges conventional binaries and invites readers to consider a reality that is simultaneously unified and divided, stable and unstable, known and unknowable. This philosophy is central to the novel’s exploration of existence, identity, and perception, illustrating a world where certainty dissolves and the dreamlike nature of reality is revealed. See Aristotle.
    • Aristotle calls the reversal peripeteia and the recognition anagnorisis, and finds them together in Oedipus (Poetics 11).
  • “this mistaken earth which was perhaps its counter-earth”: The Pythagorean Philolaus set a counter-earth, the Antichthon, beyond the Earth, always hidden from it, to bring the heavenly bodies to ten (Aristotle, On the Heavens 2.13). See Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres and Aristotle.
  • “But who was the dreamer”: “You’re only a sort of thing in his dream!” Tweedledee tells Alice of the sleeping Red King (Lewis Carroll, Through the Looking-Glass, 1871). See Alice in Wonderland.
    • Zhuangzi dreamed he was a butterfly and woke not knowing “whether I am now a butterfly, dreaming I am a man” (Chuang Tzŭ, trans. Herbert A. Giles, 1889). See Taoism and Zhuangzi.
  • “Was he a domino or a man”: A domino is a masquerade cloak worn with a half mask.
  • “no silent music like the music of the spheres”: See the Chapter 36 resources and Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres.
  • “For each face hid the grinning skull”: In Hans Holbein’s The Ambassadors (1533) a stretched skull lies across the floor, seen whole only from the side, a memento mori hidden in the picture. See Death and Mortality.
  • “How should she know when she had crossed the invisible line of demarcation”: Descartes’ dream argument: He used the experience of dreaming to question the certainty of our perceptions and beliefs. He argued that if we cannot definitively distinguish between waking and dreaming, then our sensory experiences, which are the foundation of our knowledge, might be illusions. There is philosophical skepticism because the possibility of dreams may be genuine or a fabrication. It leads one to question the validity of knowledge of the external world. Freud believed dreams are a symbolic representation of unconscious thoughts and desires. Jung expanded on Freud’s ideas that dreams can also reveal universal, archetypal patterns in the human psyche. Some theories suggest dreams are a natural byproduct of the brain’s activity during sleep, possibly involving memory consolidation, emotional processing, or the clearing out of mental clutter. Neuroscience has provided insights into the physiological processes which in turn influences philosophical discussion about the nature and functions of dreams. Some philosophers think dreams are a way of accessing another realm of existence or experiencing different perspectives on reality. The chapter concludes that dreams are not simply illusions but fundamental to existence and consciousness, blurring distinctions between reality and unreality, life and death, self and other, and that this ambiguity is a central and inescapable part of human experience. See Descartes and Dualism and Freud and Jung.
    • “There are no certain indications by which we may clearly distinguish wakefulness from sleep” (René Descartes, Meditations on First Philosophy 1, 1641, trans. Elizabeth S. Haldane, 1911).
  • “for there was no time. There was the illusion of time”: Time, perception versus reality, distorted time perception, and time as a mental construct is described as an emergent property in physics. Einstein wrote that “the distinction between past, present and future is only a stubbornly persistent illusion” (Albert Einstein, letter to the family of Michele Besso, March 21, 1955). The implications of the illusion of time for humans is it would redefine reality, raise profound philosophical questions (about the nature of existence, consciousness, and the meaning of time), and lead to a shift in perspective encouraging people to focus on the present moment and appreciate the flow of experience. Carlo Rovelli proposes that time is not a fundamental aspect of reality, but rather an emergent phenomenon (Carlo Rovelli, The Order of Time, Riverhead Books, 2018). He suggests that time may be a complex network of events, and our perception of its flow is a projection onto this network. The chapter presents time as a profound mystery deeply connected to themes of illusion, memory, mortality, and the blending of opposites. Time is not a fixed, measurable entity but a fluid and enigmatic force that shapes and is shaped by human perception, dreams, and the cosmos. See Time, Physics, and Chaos Theory.
  • “There would be suddenly a whirling windmill”: The whirling windmill may recall the windmills that Don Quixote takes for giants (Cervantes, Don Quixote 1.8, 1605). See Don Quixote.
  • “How should death be different from life, my mother asked”: Life as a state of experience and potential: Socrates thought death might be a dreamless sleep or passage to another realm where one can converse with the dead. Existentialism believes death is an inevitable aspect of human existence and that it can lead to a heightened sense of self-awareness and responsibility. Epicureanism argues death cannot harm a person, as it’s either not yet experienced (while alive) or not experienced at all (once dead). Death is defined as the loss of the capacity to engage in vital activities and the cessation of consciousness. Wittgenstein said that death is not an experience in life. Schopenhauer believed that understanding death as the ultimate end of life can provide a perspective on the meaning of life itself. Freud thought the life instinct (Eros) and the death instinct (Thanatos) are fundamental forces in human behavior. The chapter depicts life as a state of ongoing experience and potential characterized by uncertainty, illusion, and transformation. It is not a fixed reality but a mutable and often paradoxical condition where life and death, truth and illusion, identity and multiplicity coexist and where human understanding is limited and provisional. See Plato and Platonism, Existentialism, Epicureanism, Wittgenstein, Schopenhauer, and Freud and Jung.
    • “Death, therefore, the most awful of evils, is nothing to us, seeing that, when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not” (Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus, trans. Robert Drew Hicks, 1925).
    • “Death is not an event of life. Death is not lived through” (Ludwig Wittgenstein, Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus 6.4311, trans. C. K. Ogden, 1922).
    • Freud wrote of the death drive in Beyond the Pleasure Principle (1920). The name Thanatos came from his followers.
  • “Death was this dream’s immeasurable extension”: The meaning of life in relation to death: Stoicism emphasized that facing death can lead to a deeper understanding of life’s meaning and the ephemeral nature of earthly possessions. Nietzsche used the thought experiment of eternal return (recurrence) to encourage individuals to examine the meaning and value of their lives, given the possibility of living them again and again. The chapter conveys that the meaning of life is deeply connected to death, and that understanding or embracing this connection is part of the human condition. It suggests that love, life, death, and identity are bound in an intricate, often paradoxical relationship that transcends simple definitions, and that this profound ambiguity is central to our experience of existence. See Stoicism and Nietzsche.
    • Nietzsche’s demon asks, “This life as thou livest it at present, and hast lived it, thou must live it once more, and also innumerable times” (Friedrich Nietzsche, The Joyful Wisdom 341, 1882, trans. Thomas Common, 1910).
  • “One had laughed one’s self to death”: The Stoic Chrysippus is said to have died laughing at a donkey eating his figs (Diogenes Laertius, Lives of the Eminent Philosophers 7.185). See Stoicism.
  • “Death was a mountebank”: Mountebank is a person who deceives others, especially in order to trick them out of their money.
    • The word comes from the Italian montimbanco, one who mounts a bench to sell quack remedies.
  • “her face darkening and contorted like the face of a drowning woman”: Opium slows breathing. Signs of an overdose include slow or stopped breathing, blue or purple lips and face, and pinpoint pupils. See The History of Opium.
  • “aware of the earth which, viewed from another celestial body, would seem like a dead moon”: Seen from the Moon, the Earth goes through phases, full when the Moon is new. See Astronomy.
  • “to death’s-head moths”: The death’s-head hawkmoth, Acherontia atropos, bears a skull on its thorax and is named for the Fate who cuts the thread of life. See Butterflies and Moths.
  • “which shook the enamelled heavens and the earth like a dice box”: Einstein wrote to Max Born on December 4, 1926, that he was convinced God does not throw dice. See Time, Physics, and Chaos Theory.
  • “the dream which had dreamed the dreamer”: “With relief, with humiliation, with terror, he understood that he too was a mere appearance, dreamt by another” (Jorge Luis Borges, “The Circular Ruins,” 1940, trans. James E. Irby, in Labyrinths, 1962).
  • “as man became woman with her heaving breasts”: Gender as a social construct refers to the socially created roles, behaviors, expressions, and identities associated with being a woman, man, boy, or girl. It’s not inherently determined by biology, but rather shaped by cultural norms, societal expectations, and historical contexts. This means gender is not fixed and can vary across cultures and time. In short, it is not a biological reality: it is socially created, varies across cultures, is dynamic and fluid, and is distinct from biological sex. Philosophical thought on gender goes back to ancient Greece, where Pythagoras’s table of opposites set male against female (Aristotle, Metaphysics 1.5) and Plato argued that women could be guardians (Republic 5). Later came John Locke, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Judith Sargent Murray (“On the Equality of the Sexes,” 1790), and Mary Wollstonecraft (A Vindication of the Rights of Woman, 1792). The chapter and novel present gender as a fluid and changeable construct rather than a rigid, innate fact, using metaphorical and dreamlike language to question fixed dichotomies between male and female, self and other, life and death. See Women, Gender, and Marriage, Plato and Platonism, Aristotle, and Empiricism (Locke, Berkeley, Hume).
    • “One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman” (Simone de Beauvoir, The Second Sex, 1949, trans. H. M. Parshley, 1953).
  • “one’s father who gave birth to the dead child”: Zeus bore Athena from his head and Dionysus from his thigh. See Greek Mythology.
  • “seeing that he was a stag”: Actaeon, who saw Diana bathing, was turned into a stag and torn apart by his own hounds (Ovid, Metamorphoses 3). See Greek Mythology.
  • “trees of enormous crystal growing with their roots in the clouded sky”: The Bhagavad Gita (15.1) speaks of an eternal fig tree, the ashvattha, with its roots above and its branches below. See Hinduism.
  • “For the living were those who had already died, and the dead were the living”: The paradox of life and death: some philosophers see life and death as a continuous process, with life as a moment of lucidity within the vastness of existence. Others view death as a finality, a cessation of consciousness and experience. The chapter conveys that life and death are two sides of the same coin, eternally connected, and that understanding this paradox is beyond simple human comprehension, being filled with mystery, ambiguity, and endless transformation.
  • “this daughter of the codfish aristocracy”: The codfish aristocracy were New England families grown rich on the cod fishery. The Sacred Cod, a carved wooden fish, has hung in the Massachusetts State House since the eighteenth century.
  • “the footless messengers”: Birds of paradise were believed to have no feet and never to land, because their skins reached Europe with the feet cut off. Linnaeus named the greater bird of paradise Paradisaea apoda, “footless,” in 1758. See Birds.
  • “birds who could speak ecclesiastical Latin”: Ecclesiastical Latin was developed to discuss Christian thought.
    • In Chapter 34 Catherine was married by a St. Bernard who spoke church Latin. See the Chapter 34 resources.
  • “the floor of the Lost Atlantis”: Atlantis can be a metaphor for something that is no longer attainable. It is a fictional island used as an allegory for the hubris of nations in works by Plato. It has had a large influence on literature, arts, and culture. Atlantis in this chapter serves as a metaphor for the lost or hidden realms beneath the surface of consciousness and history: a place where memories, dreams, and vanished realities reside. It underscores the novel’s meditation on transience, the passing of time, and the elusive quest for meaning in a shifting and often inscrutable world. See Plato and Platonism.
    • Plato tells the story in the Timaeus and the Critias.
  • “The cross had turned into the immortal rose”: The rose upon the cross is the emblem of the Rosicrucians, announced in the Fama Fraternitatis (1614). Yeats wrote “To the Rose upon the Rood of Time” (1892). See Astrology, Alchemy, and Esoterica.
  • “a cross upon a crossroad”: In England suicides were buried at crossroads, sometimes with a stake through the body, until the Burial of Suicide Act of 1823 ended the practice. See Suicide in History and Literature.
  • “Her head was eaten by great birds”: “The eye that mocketh at his father, and despiseth to obey his mother, the ravens of the valley shall pick it out, and the young eagles shall eat it” (Proverbs 30:17). See The Bible.
  • “that which was void and without form”: “And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep” (Genesis 1:2). See Genesis.
  • “that eternal point where all lines converge”: Nicholas of Cusa taught that in the infinite all opposites coincide, so that an infinite circle would be a straight line (De docta ignorantia 1.13, 1440). See the Chapter 36 resources.
  • “land of infinite greyness”: “A land of darkness, as darkness itself; and of the shadow of death, without any order, and where the light is as darkness” (Job 10:22). See The Book of Job.
  • “Death had been, at last, her bridegroom”: “Because I could not stop for Death – / He kindly stopped for me –” (Emily Dickinson, 1890). Death comes as a suitor in his carriage. See American Writers.
  • “Had she not gone to the court of the emperor worm”: Poe’s “The Conqueror Worm” (1843) ends, “And its hero, the Conqueror Worm.” Wallace Stevens wrote, “The only emperor is the emperor of ice-cream” (1922). See Edgar Allan Poe and American Writers.
  • “the tormented whirlwinds turning upon spindles”: “The infernal hurricane that never rests / Hurtles the spirits onward in its rapine” (Dante, Inferno 5, trans. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, 1867), the circle of the lovers. See Dante.
  • “evolving from pearls in the womb of time”: “There are many events in the womb of time which will be delivered” (Shakespeare, Othello 1.3). See Shakespeare.
  • “She had crept into a seraglio among a great shiek’s wives”: Seraglios: See the Chapter 36 resources.
  • “she had lifted her veil”: In Thomas Moore’s “The Veiled Prophet of Khorassan,” from Lalla Rookh (1817), the prophet Mokanna hides his face behind a silver veil, and when he lifts it, it is hideous. See Romantic Poets.
  • “not even, as Mr. Spitzer sometimes remarked, a Chinaman’s chance”: A “Chinaman’s chance,” no chance at all, is a racist American phrase of the late nineteenth century. California’s Supreme Court had ruled in People v. Hall (1854) that Chinese people could not testify against whites, and the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 barred them from the country. See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “Had not the riderless horse already come”: A riderless horse with boots reversed in the stirrups follows the coffin in a military funeral, as Black Jack did at John F. Kennedy’s funeral in November 1963. In Chapter 32 a riderless horse “white as snow came through the window” the day Hannah died. See Horses and Carriages.
  • “The last conch trumpet had already blown”: Triton blew his conch to call back the waters of the flood (Ovid, Metamorphoses 1). “In a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trump: for the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised incorruptible” (1 Corinthians 15:52). See Greek Mythology and The Bible.

A game played with small oblong tiles marked with spots.
Enigma
A mystery or puzzle; a person or thing that is hard to understand.
Exhume
To dig up from the grave.
Fetters
Chains for the feet.
Gargoyle
A carved grotesque figure on a building, often serving as a waterspout.
Gibbet
A gallows on which a body was hung in chains.
Hallucination
Something seen or heard that is not there.

Ice floe
A sheet of floating ice.

Too great to be counted.
Incipient
Just beginning.
Intricacy

Lodestar

Luminous

Mother-of-pearl
