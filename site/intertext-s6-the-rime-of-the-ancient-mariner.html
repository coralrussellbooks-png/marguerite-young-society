Definition from research

Category: optional. Coleridge is never named, but his best-known lines turn up as common sayings in three chapters, and once the whole story comes with them.

Samuel Taylor Coleridge's "The Rime of the Ancient Mariner" (1798) tells of a sailor who shoots an albatross. His ship lies becalmed, "As idle as a painted ship / Upon a painted ocean," with "Water, water, every where, / Nor any drop to drink." His shipmates hang the dead bird around his neck, and for the rest of his life he must wander and tell his tale.

In chapter 68 the hotel landlord remembers piloting a yacht around the world "for a mad rich man who had killed his sweetheart." The jury excused him, "but his conscience had not excused him." "He travelled around the world, but he never saw it." He "was always feeding crumbs to invisible birds," and he would say, "Water, water everywhere, and not a drop to drink." That is the line as people usually remember it, not as Coleridge wrote it, and it comes with the Mariner's whole story: a killing, an endless voyage, and the birds.

The becalmed ship lies in Mr. Spitzer's grief for Peron, in "the days of endless calm when not one wind would blow through the sails of a ship stuck at the far horizon, when not one cloud would move" (chapter 27). The albatross returns in the last chapter as a saying. Talking in his sleep, the stone deaf man tells a girl named Lucie Bell, "You'll hang around his neck like an albatross when you'll be gone" (chapter 82).
