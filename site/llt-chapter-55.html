Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 55

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: Not One Self

The Questions

“If his brother had died, Mr. Spitzer would have lived.” Why can he not be sure which of them is the one who died?

He had “mapped out his entire life” and would not have “deviated by one inch from his course.” Is the planned life he lost something to mourn, or something he was spared?

He wonders whether there is “a non-aural music, a non-visual language, a reality not dependent upon the ear, the eye.” What reality would that be?

He collects stamps from “kingdoms this world had never seen, republics never marked upon any map.” What is he cataloguing?

The chapter opens on the undecidable death. “If his brother had died, Mr. Spitzer would have lived,” and the conditional is the trouble: he cannot be sure the condition holds, cannot be sure it was his brother and not himself who died on that great fall. Not One Self reaches the point where a man cannot say which of his two selves is the corpse. This is not confusion to be cleared up but the deepest form of the doubling the book has tracked in him: the border between the living and the dead brother runs through Mr. Spitzer so completely that he must reason about his own survival as a conditional, uncertain which side of it he is on.

Against the life he has, the chapter sets the life he planned and lost. He had “mapped out his entire life” and “would not have deviated by one inch from his course unless it had been planned,” a future of quiet musical study and undisturbed collecting, fame waiting patiently at his door. It reads at first as a loss, the orderly existence his brother’s death tore up. But the map is a finished blueprint, a life sealed in advance, deviating by not one inch, and by the book’s own terms it is the sealed thing. His brother’s death did not only rob him of the planned life. It broke the plan open, threw him off the mapped course into the unmapped, unsealed existence he actually leads. What he lost was a seal.

And the chapter reaches, in passing, toward the book’s next move. Mr. Spitzer wonders “if there was such a thing as a non-aural music, a non-visual language, a reality not dependent upon the ear, the eye.” He is feeling for a meaning that does not need its physical medium, a music without sound, a language without sight, and the wondering plants what the silent music will soon make real. It is the question of whether the sacred, the meaningful, the communicated, lives in the medium or beneath it, and Mr. Spitzer, losing the reliable evidence of his senses, is beginning to suspect it lives beneath.

His collections carry the same reach past the mapped and the given. He gathers “stamps from kingdoms this world had never seen, republics never marked upon any map,” cataloguing places that do not exist on any chart, the unmapped answering the unmapped life. The man whose planned map was broken now collects the mapless, the postage of nowhere, as though the loss of his sealed course had turned him toward everything that no course could contain. The unfinished life and the unmapped kingdoms and the medium-less music are one reaching: toward a reality not fixed by plan, sense, or chart, held open past every seal.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Mr. Spitzer’s lost planned life for a pure bereavement, the orderly existence he should have had, and the reading that takes his uncertainty about which brother died for a mere muddle. Neither the stolen order nor the muddle is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that the border between the living and the dead brother runs through him so completely that his own survival is a conditional he cannot settle; that the life he mapped and lost was a finished blueprint, sealed in advance, so his brother’s death broke a seal rather than only stealing an order; and that his wondering after a non-aural music, a reality beneath the ear and eye, reaches toward the meaning that lives under its medium, which the silent music will make real. The lost plan was a seal, and the unmapped life is the open one. Mr. Spitzer collects the postage of kingdoms no map holds, and the chapter leaves him reaching past every chart.

For Reflection

Is the carefully planned life one has lost always a bereavement, or sometimes a seal one was spared?

Is there a meaning that lives beneath its medium, a music without sound, a language without sight?

What draws a person, after the loss of a fixed plan, toward everything no plan could contain?
