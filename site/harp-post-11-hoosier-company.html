Riley, Nye, the Tall Sycamore, and the politics of an Indiana town

The chapters from about page 353 to 400 are the warmest in the book. They return to James Whitcomb Riley, whose death opened the first chapter, and they show Debs among the friends and rivals of his Indiana years: the humorist Bill Nye, Senator Daniel Voorhees, and the Republican general who would become president, Benjamin Harrison.

The poet in the pub

Young sets one scene in an Irish pub in Terre Haute's railroad district. When Debs and Riley walked in together, there was "always something of an uproar of joy as suddenly the workmen coming in for drinks realized that the great poet was among them, whereas the presence of Debs was taken for granted" (379). Debs was one of them. Riley was a celebrity.

Young is candid about Riley's drinking. His touring partnership with Nye broke down amid newspaper jokes like "Riley Leaves Nye for Rye" (380). Kate Debs, she notes, worried about the gossip when the two men were seen together. Riley, for his part, wrote to Nye after one visit: "Have latterly returned from a brief visit to Debs, at Terre Haute" (381), and passed along Debs's greetings.

In 1886 Riley published "Regardin' Terry Hut," a comic tribute to the town, and in it he wrote the lines about his friend that would follow Debs for the rest of his life. Debs was "a man 'at stands / And jes' holds out in his two hands / As warm a heart as ever beat" (385).

Three sycamores

Young traces a local title through three men. The "Tall Sycamore of the Wabash" was first William Henry Harrison, the Indian fighter, then Senator Voorhees, and "the last Tall Sycamore would be Debs" (385). It is a small joke with a real point. The same Indiana soil produced the general who drove out the Native nations, the Democratic senator who made his career in Washington, and the labor leader who would run for president from a prison cell.

Debs campaigned for Voorhees and for the Democrats in these years. At a rally in 1888 he spoke against the Republican candidate, Benjamin Harrison. Young reports the charges that had followed Harrison since the railroad strike of 1877: that he had said a dollar a day was enough for any workingman (390), and that he had boasted he would wade through the blood of strikers (391). She presents them carefully as accusations "continually resurrected" against him. Later, Debs would conclude that "Harrison had been Carnegie's man, root and branch." Then he added a question that shows where his thinking was headed: "But whose man was Cleveland?" (396).

That question marks a turning point. Debs was beginning to suspect that both parties answered to the same owners.

The silence of 1886

Young does not hide a moment when Debs fell short. In 1886, when the Haymarket anarchists were arrested in Chicago after a bomb killed several policemen, Debs's voice was "stifled by various fears for his Firemen's Brotherhood" (353). He did not defend them. The union was fragile, public hysteria was enormous, and he stayed quiet.

Young records this without excuse and without condemnation. It is Grief Doesn't End in a different form, a refusal to tidy up the hero's record. The Debs who would later defend every persecuted radical in America was, at thirty, still weighing the safety of his own organization.

Toward federation

By the late 1880s, the separate craft brotherhoods were failing in strikes, often because one union's men would run the trains when another's walked out. Debs began to dream of joining them all. Young says he was traveling toward "what he would call the Ultima Thule of labor federation" (374).

The phrase echoes the first sentence of the book, where Debs "took the long leap to the Ultima Thule of prison." Ultima Thule means the farthest edge of the known world. Young uses it for both the prison cell and the united union. That repetition is The Past Doesn't Go Away as structure. The road to one farthest edge leads to the other.

What the lens shows

These chapters are about friendship across disagreement. Riley never shared Debs's politics. Voorhees was a party man. Nye was, in Young's phrase earlier, of the right wing. Debs loved them anyway. The Sacred Is Here lives here in the pub, the rally, and the bread-and-butter poem, in a politics that grows out of local affection rather than theory.

Checking the record

  • "Regardin' Terry Hut." Accurate. Riley's poem appeared in the Indianapolis Journal in 1886 and in his 1887 collection Afterwhiles.

  • Harrison's alleged remarks. The "dollar a day" charge dogged Harrison in several campaigns. He denied it, and it was never proved. Young is right to present it as an accusation.

  • Debs and Haymarket. Young's account of his silence in 1886 matches the historical record. His public defense of the Haymarket men came much later.
