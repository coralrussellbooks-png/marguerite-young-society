Pillar: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act · Across every book

Characters: Esther Longtree · Vera Cartwheel · Walter

The fourth pillar is the one that does the ethical work of the system.

The first three pillars describe conditions: the self is plural, reality is constructed, grief is permanent. They tell you what the world is. The fourth pillar tells you what to do about it, which is not what you might expect from a philosophical system that has spent three chapters dismantling the foundations of certainty.

What you do is witness.

Not act, not fix, not resolve, not transcend. Witness. Stay in the room with what is actually there. See it without flinching and without concluding. Maintain the full attention that the thing in front of you deserves, the attention that does not look away when the looking becomes painful and does not rush toward resolution when the contradiction becomes uncomfortable. The witness is the one who can hold the multiple things at the same time, without requiring that one of them be more real than the other, without needing the scene to resolve into something more manageable than it actually is.

This sounds passive. It is not. It is the most demanding ethical stance Young's system makes available, and it is demanding because it refuses the satisfactions that make ethical action feel complete. The hero acts and the problem is solved and the story ends. The witness stays and the problem remains and the story keeps going and the witness keeps staying. There is no arrival. There is only the quality of attention brought to the continuing.

Young's novel is itself an act of witnessing. Her characters are not judged, diagnosed, concluded and the interior world is not positioned as inferior to the world outside it. It does not offer the reader the comfort of a clinical or moral frame that would explain who is right, who is wrong.

The length of "Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" is not excess. It is the time required to witness properly. You cannot witness in summary. You cannot witness at a distance. Witnessing requires duration and proximity and the willingness to keep looking after the looking has become difficult, which it becomes almost immediately in a novel this committed to not looking away.

The practice has a source in Young’s life. The nightly reading to Minna Weissenbach, described in the previous chapter, was a witness of exactly this kind: full attention given to a world she did not share, kept up for about two years, without correction. Young’s creed, taken from Browning, was that “the poet is the great reporter.”

Young’s characters can also be imperfect witnesses. The characters who fail in Young's novel are not the ones who suffer. Suffering is not failure in Young. The characters who fail are the ones who stop witnessing, who replace genuine attention with a frame that makes the thing in front of them manageable at the cost of its actual complexity.

Young's fourth pillar is in deep, unacknowledged conversation with Simone Weil's ethics of attention. Weil argued that the foundation of ethics is not duty or utility or virtue but attention: the capacity to truly see the person in front of you, to give them the full weight of your regard without the distortions of projection or self-interest or the desire to make them into something more manageable than they actually are. For Weil, attention is an act of love, and love is not a feeling but a practice, the practice of keeping your eyes open when everything in you wants to look away or simplify or conclude.

Young arrives at the same place through fiction rather than philosophy, which means she arrives there with different tools and different evidence but the same essential claim.

The difference between them is instructive. Weil's ethics of attention operate between persons: you attend to the other, the one in front of you, the one whose suffering makes a claim on your regard. Young's witnessing operates at a different scale. It is not only interpersonal but intrapersonal: you witness yourself, your own plurality, your own grief, your own constructed reality, with the same attention you owe the other. Young's witnessing is Weil's attention made systemic. It is attention not as an individual ethical act but as a philosophical stance, a way of being in the world that shapes everything the self does and makes and builds.

A political culture organized around the witness would look radically different from what we have. What we have is organized around action: the decisive leader, the transformative policy, the revolution that changes everything, the election that finally gets it right. The political imagination is saturated with the fantasy of arrival. Young's system says: the story does not end. The problem is not the kind of thing that gets solved. The question is the quality of attention brought to the continuing. This does not mean political action is pointless. It means political action that is not grounded in genuine witnessing is dangerous. Every authoritarian movement in history has depended on the refusal of witnessing: the reduction of the complex plural self to a simple sortable category, the replacement of genuine attention with ideology, the decision that what cannot be processed by the frame does not need to be seen.

That is the fourth pillar. Stay in the room. Keep looking. Do not conclude.

The novel shows the witness at its fullest. In the early poems a hunter comes home with nothing because “he has stood all day and watched / The bright, unfrightened birds.” In “The Clinic” (1941), fifty faces watch a state execution through glass, and watching without stopping becomes complicity. In "Angel in the Forest", witnessing becomes a tool of power, and the missing witness becomes the way people vanish from history. In the novel itself a white detective will not look at the deaths of Black children. In "Harp Song for a Radical" the pillar splits between Pinkerton’s “The Eye That Never Sleeps” and Debs as spokesman for those “who had no words.” Across her work the witness grows more institutional and more political.

Phase One, Childhood and the Early Poems (1908 to 1934)

Pillar 4: Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The clearest case closes the phase. In "Woodlands Far Removed" (1934) the hunter goes out to kill, the neighbors mock him, and he comes home with nothing because "he has stood all day and watched / The bright, unfrightened birds." Attention replaces harm. "Proud Lady" is vain not of rings but "Of orioles / And sky and cloud," a pride in what she notices. The farmer of "The Seeker" spends "hours and hours peering through a glass" at his postcards, the witness as dreamer.

Complication: the witnessing here is private and gentle. It has no institutions and no politics yet.

Phase Two, The Chicago Years, Prismatic Ground, and the Graduate Essays (1935 to 1937)

Pillar 4: Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

This is the phase of the founding witness. For about two years, night after night, Young read aloud to Minna Weissenbach as the woman passed into opium dreams, refusing the drug each night, and stayed through balloon fish, golden cockerels, and near-comas. Dowling says she realized she was providing "the verbal passport" to another world. Her creed, taken from Browning, was that "the poet is the great reporter."

The poems complicate witnessing at once. In "Wild Deer" (1936) a woman means to burn a fire to frighten off the deer, and instead "She listened for their coming." In "Death in Lilliput" (1937) the giant stumbles into a tiny world, the lady dies, and only then does he see "The chrysalis... spotted wings": "Her eyes were shut / Where his were wide." He sees too late. In "The Dark Wood," being watched is the terror: "They all watched her." The "Two Angels," asked to testify about a dead girl, admit they "Cannot collect the melody / We have heard of but have not heard." "Daniel" (1937) gives his account "As a matter of record" and doubts his own faith.

Complication: the witness can arrive after the harm, the watcher can be the threat, and testimony can fail.

Phase Three, The Philosophical Poems (1938 to 1945)

Pillar 4: Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

"The Clinic" (1941) is the decisive poem. "Fifty faces peering against glassed screens" watch an execution in "the room the state has walled / Transparent to its glassy eyes." The warden weeps and writes "The man you kill tonight is six years old," and the poem asks, "Who will ever shut a final door / And watch the fume upon a face?" Witnessing without stopping is complicity. "The White Rat" (1943) makes the scientific gaze a cruelty: the rat runs "Caged in a theatre of emptiness," and the poem asks, "Does God with diamond eyes look down on these / For the purposes of what intrinsic studies?" In "The Parachutist" (1944), "So multiple is every witnessed lady / Who dissolves like vapor on a glass," and "Seeing, is it believing."

Attention as love appears too. "Flea Circus on Times Square" describes a showman who "would never mistake one flea for another," because to themselves "each is very different from the other." In "Night Watches" (1942), "we are the jewel-eyed watchers in space."

Complication: this phase turns witnessing toward institutions: the state execution chamber, the laboratory, a God who studies rats.

Phase Four, "Angel in the Forest" and “Complex Life and Complex Letters” (1945 to 1946)

Pillar 4: Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Witnessing becomes a tool of power. Rapp is "the sole eye witness to the authentic Posey County angel." Elders at funerals have "no official function but to be eye witness." Of one killing: "the only eye witnesses to this murder were the murderers." Frederick Rapp listens to the Count "with the utmost intensity" and "came almost to fraternize with that very Devil." The record leaves people out: much is said of Owen's materialism "but not a word as to the lady and her spoons."

Complication: this is the phase's main turn. "The Clinic" showed useless witnesses. "Angel in the Forest" shows witnessing as authority, and the missing witness as the way people vanish from history.

Phase Five, "Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" (written from the mid 1940s, published 1965)

Pillar 4: Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The form is witness: monologue after monologue, each figure given full attention without being answered. Vera's role is listening: "So I listened to Esther Longtree's tale of woe, listened all morning." Near the end she holds back her own grief, listening "as one might listen for one lonely heartbeat in the midst of the great silence."

The sharpest material is the white detective in Esther's mind, who solves white children's deaths while "the deaths of the little black children were not his business, there being so many and so unimportant." Esther's dead child Walter is "my black mystery whom the white detective will not find." Witnessing is refused along a color line, and the refusal is the injustice.

Complication: the witness keeps silent at a cost, and Vera wonders what might have happened had she spoken.

“The Clinic” showed witnesses who were complicit, and "Angel in the Forest" showed witnessing as a tool of power. The novel shows who goes unwitnessed, and names that refusal as the injustice.

Phase Six, The Occasional Prose, Interviews, and Self-Interpretation (1940s to 1995)

Pillar 4: Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

"The poet is the great reporter." "I'm a mirror." The essays prize witnesses: Miss Greenwood, who notices what Old James cannot see; Dorothy Caruso, who "takes down in shorthand the speech of her husband, living or dead"; the nurse Mrs. Houghton at Poe's cottage. "The 'type' is always a laziness of vision." She faulted Doris Lessing's Martha for an idealism that "begets violence," and Henry Miller for a "romantic individualism, which also blows trumpets and waves flags."

Complication: "I'm never much interested in individuals, even my closest friends." In "Old James," Miss Greenwood ends as "herself a collaborator." Young took sides: she wrote to Kennedy about atomic tests, recorded "Poets for Peace," and championed Debs. Moore's account of her treating an intermediary "like a lackey" shows that her attention was uneven.

Phase Seven, "Harp Song for a Radical" (written in her last decades, published 1999)

Pillar 4: Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The book gives the pillar's fullest politics. On one side is Pinkerton's "The Eye That Never Sleeps," the spy McParlan among the Molly Maguires, Booth watching John Brown hang, and Lincoln's son removing "flaws of fact" from a manuscript. On the other is Debs, "the most radiant spokesman for the dead souls of America who could not speak and who had no words," and Thoreau's railroad sleepers: "Each one is a man." Family Bibles show records "with statistics altered or wholly missing," so that a person "might have been blotted out completely."
