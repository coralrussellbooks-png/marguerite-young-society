Chapter 53

On Chapter 53 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Summary

No instrument is wide enough for Mr. Spitzer's feelings. He contrasts Peron, a chameleon of many colors, with himself, a timid chess player who never made the final move. Since the death he has been mistaken for his brother and has come to know things only Peron knew: boxing records, gambling, speakeasies. He calls himself a pale replica, divided between himself and his twin.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, Mr. Spitzer is described as a "lost musician with his unfinished song," struggling to find a way to express the vast range of his feelings and experiences, which surpass any available instrument or keyboard. His music is marked by many segnos and signs, suggesting incomplete or concealed meanings. This metaphor extends to his life itself, indicating a sense of incompletion and fragmentation, a yearning for unity and integration that remains unfulfilled.
  • A central motif is the blurring of identities between Mr. Spitzer (Joachim) and his brother Peron. Since Peron’s apparent suicide, Mr. Spitzer has been mistaken for Peron and struggles with the paradox of being a "pale replica," a mask or dream rather than flesh and blood. The reversal of roles with Joachim seeming to be Peron, exposes the fragile boundary between life and death, self and other, reality and illusion.
  • Time is portrayed as fluid and illusory. Mr. Spitzer contemplates the collapse of centuries into moments and the eternal cry of a lost child wandering in darkness. He is haunted by the feeling that he has "missed his brother by only a minute," symbolizing missed opportunities and the relentless passage of time. The imagery of trains passing by, always missing the right connection, reinforces this sense of temporal dislocation and existential solitude.
  • Peron’s life is described as volatile, colorful, and many-sided, like a chameleon changing colors, while Joachim’s is opaque, timorous, and marked by a search for equilibrium. Peron's life is a "crazy patchwork" of many identities and experiences, whereas Joachim is the "heart of darkness," withdrawn and selfless. This contrast underscores themes of individuality, fate, and the search for meaning in a fractured existence.
  • Death is depicted as the "heart of life," and yet life itself may be the "heart of death." Mr. Spitzer reflects on the inevitability of change, the impossibility of stasis, and the paradox of "changelessness" as a form of change. Love, represented by the blind god Cupid, is indifferent to distinctions between the living and the dead, blurring boundaries and questioning the significance of identity itself.
  • Mr. Spitzer is a solitary mourner, wandering through empty landscapes seeking his dead brother and a place where they might be reunited. His grief is diffused and pervasive, spreading like an ink blot over his unwritten music and his existence. The imagery of the black cloak, the ivory-headed cane, and the shadowed self accentuate the theme of mourning and existential alienation.
  • Through his late-blooming sympathies with his brother, who was involved in shady and illegal activities, Mr. Spitzer acquired unexpected knowledge of the underworld of gambling, speakeasies, boxing, billiards, and other sports and games. This knowledge, alien to his former life as a musician and lawyer, symbolizes the crossing of boundaries between worlds, the mingling of high and low, life and death, order and chaos.
  • Recurring images of chess, billiards, and card games illustrate life's strategic, repetitive, and sometimes futile nature. Mr. Spitzer sees himself as a timid chess player afraid to make a move that would mean his final defeat, highlighting the paralyzing fear of change and the interplay of fate and free will. The idea that he might have "missed his cue" in life or become a "dead man walking" underscores the theme of existential uncertainty and the search for selfhood.
  • The chapter closes with the idea that identity is fluid and that men are like chameleons or accidental conformations, changing with circumstances and perceptions. Joachim’s fixed, black-velvet pall identity contrasts with Peron’s many-colored, shifting persona. This evokes the essential ambivalence and mystery of human existence and the elusive nature of the self.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer (Joachim)

  • A composer and musician, burdened by an unfinished “song,” representing his incomplete life and unresolved feelings.
  • He is introspective, sensitive, and often alienated from the world. His reflections reveal a man caught between worlds like life and death, self and other, reality and illusion.
  • He wrestles with the sense of being overshadowed by his brother’s legacy and struggles with his own identity and place in existence.

Peron

  • Mr. Spitzer’s brother, a gambler and more flamboyant, volatile personality.
  • Peron’s life is described as colorful, many-sided, and seemingly successful, but also transient and elusive.
  • His death creates a “rift” in Spitzer’s life and intensifies Spitzer’s struggle with his own existence and identity.

Research: Themes

  • Identity and Duality The chapter explores the paradox of the two brothers’ overlapping identities. Joachim as the “pale replica” of Peron, and Peron as an elusive, many-personality figure. The idea that Joachim might be Peron and vice versa underlines the instability of self and questions the fixity of identity.
  • Life, Death, and Memory Mr. Spitzer contemplates death not as an end but a transformation and continuation beyond physical existence. The boundaries between life and death blur, as love and memory maintain connections beyond mortality. He mourns his brother, yet also mourns himself, reflecting on the theme of grief diffused over time.
  • Time and Eternity Time is depicted as fluid, collapsing moments, and shifting perspectives. Mr. Spitzer experiences a disorienting sense of timelessness, where centuries might seem like minutes, and events overlap or miss connections like trains passing at the wrong station.
  • Failure and Unfulfilled Potential Spitzer’s sense of failure pervades the chapter. His unfinished compositions, his timid nature, and his unrealized potential as a musician contrast with his brother’s more vibrant, reckless, life.
  • The Illusory Nature of Reality The narrative suggests that much of human experience is illusion or performance, with shifting roles, identities, and perceptions. Peron’s chameleon-like existence contrasts with Joachim’s desire for stability and order.
  • The Intersection of the Mundane and the Metaphysical Spitzer’s knowledge expands beyond his musical sphere to encompass gamblers, fighters, sportsmen, and other facets of earthly life, blending the terrestrial and the spiritual, the everyday and the eternal.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

  • Music and the Unfinished Composition Symbolizes Spitzer’s incomplete life and emotional fragmentation. Music also represents the ineffable, the transcendent, and the ungraspable aspects of existence.
  • The Train and Missed Connections Represents lost opportunities, failed encounters, and the passage of time. The recurring image of trains passing the wrong station illustrates disjunctions in Spitzer’s life and identity.
  • Chess and Cards Symbolizes strategic life moves, fate, and chance. Spitzer’s timidness as a chess player echoes his fear of making irreversible decisions.
  • The Black Cloak and Ivory Cane Emblems of mourning, identity, and secondary intelligence. The cane serves as an extension of Spitzer’s self and his cautious, measured approach to life.
  • Sports and Fighters Represent struggle, endurance, defeat, and human frailty. Spitzer’s extensive knowledge of fighters and sportsmen symbolizes life’s battles and the inevitability of loss.
  • The Weather and Nature (Fog, Snow, Wind) Reflect Spitzer’s moods and the shifting, uncertain nature of reality. The snow’s color shifts (black, white) and the fog symbolize ambiguity and blurred perception.

These suits predate the more internationally recognized French suits (hearts, diamonds, clubs, and spades). German-suited cards typically feature 32 or 36 cards with these four suits. The acorns suit is one of the oldest. Leaves is another of the original suits. Hearts are the same in both the German and French systems. Bells are a distinctive German suit. German suits are believed to have influenced the development of the French suits. For example, the French "clubs" are thought to be derived from the German "acorns". German-suited cards are still used in many parts of Central Europe.
  • “medieval ladies snow balling” there are historical depictions of medieval ladies participating in snowball fights. A 15th-century fresco from Trento, Italy, portrays individuals engaged in a snowball fight, showcasing the postures and expressions of players. Snowball fights are also referenced in medieval chronicles.
  • In Greek mythology, the Argonauts were a group of heroes who, led by Jason, sailed on the ship Argo to retrieve the Golden Fleece from Colchis. The myth of the Argonauts is one of the most famous tales from Greek mythology.
  • Chinese kite fighting, also known as "fighter kite" flying, is a competitive sport where participants attempt to cut the strings of their opponents' kites using specially designed kites and string. The string is often coated with abrasive materials like glass or other substances to enhance its cutting ability. The last kite remaining in the air wins the contest. Kites date back to over two thousand years in China.
  • “turned to cross North Square” North Square is in Boston’s North End, where the Paul Revere House still stands. Built around 1680, it is the oldest house remaining in downtown Boston, and Revere lived there from 1770 to 1800.
