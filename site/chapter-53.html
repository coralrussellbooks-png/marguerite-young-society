To All My Darlings

Chapter 53

On Chapter 53 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 6, 2026

← Chapter 52 · Home · Chapter 54 →

Summary

Mr. Spitzer has found no instrument wide enough for his feelings and no unity for his life, only an unfinished song marked by many segnos. Since Peron’s apparent suicide he is mistaken everywhere for his brother: strangers stop him on Boston Common and in Scollay Square to ask who won on the dogs and the horses, and to his chagrined surprise he knows. He knows the speakeasies and billiard halls, who wore the featherweight crown longest, the fighter who believed he battled his father’s ghost, the fistmele of archery and the suits of an ancient card game, though he has never seen a fight. He walks Boston in his black cloak, past the Cockerel Church, the State House, and the Beacon Hill house with its twin parlors, and carries a coin for Charon that the old ferryman will not take. He recalls a ward-heeler found at death to have been a woman and concludes that “this life is the chrysalis, and no one knows whether he will be the butterfly or the moth.” Peron was a chameleon crawling across a cloth of many colors; Joachim crawls forever upon a black velvet pall.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, Mr. Spitzer is described as a “lost musician with his unfinished song,” struggling to find a way to express the vast range of his feelings and experiences, which surpass any available instrument or keyboard. His music is marked by many segnos and signs, suggesting incomplete or concealed meanings. This metaphor extends to his life itself, indicating a sense of incompletion and fragmentation, a yearning for unity and integration that remains unfulfilled.
  • A central motif is the blurring of identities between Mr. Spitzer (Joachim) and his brother Peron. Since Peron’s apparent suicide, Mr. Spitzer has been mistaken for Peron and struggles with the paradox of being a “pale replica,” a mask or dream rather than flesh and blood. The reversal of roles with Joachim seeming to be Peron, exposes the fragile boundary between life and death, self and other, reality and illusion.
  • Time is portrayed as fluid and illusory. Mr. Spitzer contemplates the collapse of centuries into moments and the eternal cry of a lost child wandering in darkness. He is haunted by the feeling that he has “missed his brother by only a minute,” symbolizing missed opportunities and the relentless passage of time. The imagery of trains passing by, always missing the right connection, reinforces this sense of temporal dislocation and existential solitude.
  • Peron’s life is described as volatile, colorful, and many-sided, like a chameleon changing colors, while Joachim’s is opaque, timorous, and marked by a search for equilibrium. Peron’s life is a “crazy patchwork” of many identities and experiences, whereas Joachim is the “heart of darkness,” withdrawn and selfless. This contrast underscores themes of individuality, fate, and the search for meaning in a fractured existence.
  • Death is depicted as the “heart of life,” and yet life itself may be the “heart of death.” Mr. Spitzer reflects on the inevitability of change, the impossibility of stasis, and the paradox of “changelessness” as a form of change. Love, represented by the blind god Cupid, is indifferent to distinctions between the living and the dead, blurring boundaries and questioning the significance of identity itself.
  • Mr. Spitzer is a solitary mourner, wandering through empty landscapes seeking his dead brother and a place where they might be reunited. His grief is diffused and pervasive, spreading like an ink blot over his unwritten music and his existence. The imagery of the black cloak, the ivory-headed cane, and the shadowed self accentuate the theme of mourning and existential alienation.
  • Through his late-blooming sympathies with his brother, who was involved in shady and illegal activities, Mr. Spitzer acquired unexpected knowledge of the underworld of gambling, speakeasies, boxing, billiards, and other sports and games. This knowledge, alien to his former life as a musician and lawyer, symbolizes the crossing of boundaries between worlds, the mingling of high and low, life and death, order and chaos.
  • Recurring images of chess, billiards, and card games illustrate life’s strategic, repetitive, and sometimes futile nature. Mr. Spitzer sees himself as a timid chess player afraid to make a move that would mean his final defeat, highlighting the paralyzing fear of change and the interplay of fate and free will. The idea that he might have “missed his cue” in life or become a “dead man walking” underscores the theme of existential uncertainty and the search for selfhood.
  • The chapter closes with the idea that identity is fluid and that men are like chameleons or accidental conformations, changing with circumstances and perceptions. Joachim’s fixed, black-velvet pall identity contrasts with Peron’s many-colored, shifting persona. This evokes the essential ambivalence and mystery of human existence and the elusive nature of the self.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “His character did not correspond to his experience.” What does Mr. Spitzer mean?
  • “Changelessness, too, was a kind of change.” How can a man who never moves keep changing?
  • “The wound was the creative thing.” What wound, and what does it create?

Characters

  • Strangers ask him “Who won on the dogs? Who won on the horses?” Why does being taken for Peron both disconcert and “oddly” flatter him?
  • Peron was “like a chameleon … crawling forever across a many-colored cloth,” Joachim “like a chameleon crawling forever upon a black velvet pall.” What does each brother reflect?
  • After the ward-heeler’s secret is found, Mr. Spitzer decides “that many men must live to be the widow’s of themselves.” What does he mean, and does it describe him?

Connections

  • Mr. Spitzer carries a coin “for old Charon,” and the ferryman will not take it. Where have Charon and his fare appeared before?
  • The ward-heeler’s secret is found only at the post-mortem, as Cousin Hannah’s wedding gowns were found only after her death. Why does the novel keep its deepest secrets for the dead?
  • “Death danced with the lady.” How does this echo Hannah’s “mad dance, dance of death” in Chapter 43?

Reflection

  • “Love knew no time.” Can love outlast the line between the living and the dead?
  • “No man lived to die his own death—but in this life, died many times.” Is that true?
  • Mr. Spitzer would pity anyone who “had not lived also through a mistaken dream.” Is a mistaken dream part of a life?

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer (Joachim)

  • A composer and musician, burdened by an unfinished “song,” representing his incomplete life and unresolved feelings.
  • He is introspective, sensitive, and often alienated from the world. His reflections reveal a man caught between worlds like life and death, self and other, reality and illusion.
  • He wrestles with the sense of being overshadowed by his brother’s legacy and struggles with his own identity and place in existence.
  • A bankrupt attorney closing last estates, mistaken everywhere for his gambling twin, he finds he knows Peron’s world of speakeasies, billiard halls, and boxing rings “much to his chagrined surprise.”

Peron Spitzer

  • Mr. Spitzer’s brother, a gambler and more flamboyant, volatile personality.
  • Peron’s life is described as colorful, many-sided, and seemingly successful, but also transient and elusive.
  • His death creates a “rift” in Spitzer’s life and intensifies Spitzer’s struggle with his own existence and identity.

Catherine Cartwheel

  • Peron was blind to “the opium lady’s beautiful and enigmatic face which Joachim had seen in every cloud conformation or rippling shadow.” Joachim once escorted “my drugged, dazzled mother” to archery meets on a seaside golf course, and Vera’s voice slips into his story.

Research: Themes

Identity and Duality

See: Twins and Doubles · Identity and the Self

  • The chapter explores the paradox of the two brothers’ overlapping identities. Joachim as the “pale replica” of Peron, and Peron as an elusive, many-personality figure. The idea that Joachim might be Peron and vice versa underlines the instability of self and questions the fixity of identity.

Life, Death, and Memory

See: Death and Mortality · Memory and the Past

  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates death not as an end but a transformation and continuation beyond physical existence. The boundaries between life and death blur, as love and memory maintain connections beyond mortality. He mourns his brother, yet also mourns himself, reflecting on the theme of grief diffused over time.

Time and Eternity

See: Time

  • Time is depicted as fluid, collapsing moments, and shifting perspectives. Mr. Spitzer experiences a disorienting sense of timelessness, where centuries might seem like minutes, and events overlap or miss connections like trains passing at the wrong station.

Failure and Unfulfilled Potential

See: The Search for Meaning

  • Spitzer’s sense of failure pervades the chapter. His unfinished compositions, his timid nature, and his unrealized potential as a musician contrast with his brother’s more vibrant, reckless, life.

The Illusory Nature of Reality

See: Illusion and Reality

  • The narrative suggests that much of human experience is illusion or performance, with shifting roles, identities, and perceptions. Peron’s chameleon-like existence contrasts with Joachim’s desire for stability and order.

The Intersection of the Mundane and the Metaphysical

See: Cards, Games, and Chance

  • Spitzer’s knowledge expands beyond his musical sphere to encompass gamblers, fighters, sportsmen, and other facets of earthly life, blending the terrestrial and the spiritual, the everyday and the eternal.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Music and the Unfinished Composition

See: Music and Instruments

  • Symbolizes Spitzer’s incomplete life and emotional fragmentation. Music also represents the ineffable, the transcendent, and the ungraspable aspects of existence.

The Train and Missed Connections

  • Represents lost opportunities, failed encounters, and the passage of time. The recurring image of trains passing the wrong station illustrates disjunctions in Spitzer’s life and identity.

Chess and Cards

See: Cards, Games, and Chance

  • Symbolizes strategic life moves, fate, and chance. Spitzer’s timidness as a chess player echoes his fear of making irreversible decisions.

The Black Cloak and Ivory Cane

See: Clothing, Hats, and Skirts · Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • Emblems of mourning, identity, and secondary intelligence. The cane serves as an extension of Spitzer’s self and his cautious, measured approach to life.

Sports and Fighters

See: Fear and Courage

  • Represent struggle, endurance, defeat, and human frailty. Spitzer’s extensive knowledge of fighters and sportsmen symbolizes life’s battles and the inevitability of loss.

The Weather and Nature (Fog, Snow, Wind)

See: Fog and Mist · Snow, Ice, and Winter

  • Reflect Spitzer’s moods and the shifting, uncertain nature of reality. The snow’s color shifts (black, white) and the fog symbolize ambiguity and blurred perception.

Research: Resources

  • “People were indifferent to those things which interested Mr. Spitzer”: Young drew Peron’s world from her own reading: “I’ve read a great many books on gambling, and I’ve been interested in it since early childhood, because I knew gamblers in early childhood.” Of her research she said, “Baseball, gambling, vaudeville, burlesque jokes, all this became part, as well as Moby Dick, the Bible” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See Marguerite Young on Her Craft and Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “the one chance in a million”: Young told Studs Terkel, “Mr. Spitzer is really two people. There are two Mr. Spitzers. One is a musical composer who has lived in memory of his gambling brother, the suicide, and who since his brother’s death has never composed a music which can be heard.” Peron “is a gay gambler, an impresario in the world of games and sports,” who describes himself “as the gambler who took one chance in a million, committed suicide, and lived.” In the third draft she realized “that it was not just a case of twins, but the two parts of the same man… a beautiful case of duality, which might be in all of us, like left and right, shadow and sunlight” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See Marguerite Young on Her Craft and Twins and Doubles.
  • “as if suddenly an angel had passed”: When a conversation falls suddenly silent, the French say “un ange passe,” an angel is passing.
  • “They probably would not have understood Mr. Spitzer’s swan song”: The legend that the swan sings most beautifully just before it dies is old; in Plato’s Phaedo (84e-85b) Socrates says swans sing at their death out of joy that they are going to the god they serve. See Plato and Platonism.
  • “the old shell games in which the victim had no chance”: In the shell game, or thimblerig, a pea is shuffled under three shells or thimbles and the operator palms it, so the mark can never win. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “A child was always wandering lost, crying in the night somewhere”: “But what am I? / An infant crying in the night: / An infant crying for the light: / And with no language but a cry” (Alfred Tennyson, In Memoriam A. H. H., LIV, 1850). See Romantic Poets.
  • “For who should catch a moment on the wing”: “He who binds to himself a joy / Does the winged life destroy; / But he who kisses the joy as it flies / Lives in eternity’s sun rise” (William Blake, “Eternity”). See Romantic Poets.
  • “Madness had also its reasons”: Young turns Pascal’s “Le cœur a ses raisons que la raison ne connaît point,” “The heart has its reasons which reason knows nothing of” (Blaise Pascal, Pensées, 1670).
  • “the heart of darkness, as Mr. Spitzer sighingly remarked”: Joseph Conrad’s Heart of Darkness (1899) follows Marlow up the Congo to the ivory trader Kurtz, through the horrors of Belgian King Leopold’s rule, in which millions of Congolese died.
  • “he should knock upon all doors, all ivory doors”: Through the gate of ivory, Homer and Virgil say, come false dreams, and through the gate of horn the true. See the gates of horn and ivory in the Chapter 42 resources. See Greek Mythology.
  • “whose faces were parallelograms”: Young sets the textbook definition beside the glass prism that breaks light into colors: in geometry a prism is a solid whose two ends are equal, similar, and parallel polygons and whose sides are parallelograms.
  • “as if she were Venus rising from the sea”: See Botticelli’s The Birth of Venus in the Chapter 52 resources. See Greek Mythology.
  • “that which he carried now in his purse for old Charon”: Charon, the ferryman of the dead in Greek myth, took a coin for the crossing of the Styx. See the coins on Cousin Hannah’s eyes in the Chapter 52 resources. See Greek Mythology.
  • “old Dan Cupid, the wizened cherub, the blind god of love”: Dan was an old title of respect, from the Latin dominus; Berowne calls him “Dan Cupid” in Shakespeare’s Love’s Labour’s Lost (3.1). See blind Cupid in the Chapter 52 resources. See Shakespeare.
  • “each snowflake different from every other”: Wilson A. Bentley of Jericho, Vermont, photographed more than five thousand snowflakes and found no two alike (W. A. Bentley and W. J. Humphreys, Snow Crystals, 1931). Each forms around a speck of dust, as Young’s “grain of wandering stardust.”
  • “turned to cross North Square”: North Square is in Boston’s North End, where the Paul Revere House still stands. Built around 1680, it is the oldest house remaining in downtown Boston, and Revere lived there from 1770 to 1800.
  • “the golden Capitol dome”: The State House or the Massachusetts State House is the state capitol and seat of the government. Charles Bulfinch’s State House on Beacon Hill was completed in 1798; its dome, sheathed in copper by Paul Revere’s company in 1802, was gilded in 1874.
  • “why were there no cypress trees in New England?”: The cypress has been the tree of mourning since antiquity. Ovid tells of the boy Cyparissus, who killed his beloved stag by accident and was changed into a cypress so that he might grieve forever (Metamorphoses 10). See Greek Mythology.
  • “Cockerel Church, the golden cock upon the weathervane”: The Cockerel Church was the New Brick Church on Hanover Street in Boston’s North End, later the Second Church, nicknamed for the gilded rooster weathervane Shem Drowne made for it in 1721; the rooster now turns above the First Church in Cambridge. The cock became Peter’s emblem because he denied Jesus three times before the cock crew (Matthew 26:74-75), and Pope Gregory I is said to have deemed it a suitable Christian symbol. Pope Nicholas I decreed that roosters could be used on weathervanes. Protestant churches used this symbol on church steeples to distinguish them from Catholic churches.
  • “Parker House which, he was pleased to see, was still doing an excellent trade”: Parker House may refer to the Omni Parker House. A historic hotel in Boston, Massachusetts. It opened in 1855 and gave its name to the Parker House roll; Boston cream pie is also said to have been created there.
  • “an old Beacon Hill house where he once had lived”: Beacon Hill is an historic neighborhood in Boston with Victorian style brick houses lit by antique lanterns.
  • “In Scollay Square, they thought he was a lawyer”: Scollay Square was a vibrant city square in downtown Boston, Massachusetts. A district of theaters, burlesque houses, and sailors’ bars, it was razed in 1962 to make way for Government Center.
  • “In the region of Boston Common”: Boston Common is a central public park in downtown Boston, Massachusetts. Set aside in 1634, it is the oldest public park in the United States.
  • “when he crossed an iron bridge”: There are several historic iron bridges in Massachusetts.
  • “some painted with enormous human eyes”: Eyes painted on a ship’s bow, the oculus, go back to ancient Egypt and Greece and survive on Chinese junks and Maltese fishing boats, so that the boat can see its way.
  • “he crossed the Public Gardens”: The Boston Public Garden was the first public botanical garden in America. It was decorative and flowery from its inception, featuring meandering pathways for strolling. Its swan boats, designed by Robert Paget, have carried passengers around the lagoon since 1877.
  • “a black coachman sleeping in the coachman’s box”: See The Black Coachman.
  • “shadowed Brattle Street”: Brattle Street is the most famous residential street in Cambridge, Massachusetts. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow lived at 105 Brattle Street.
  • “the roof of the Sistine Chapel”: The Sistine Chapel is a chapel in the Apostolic Palace in Vatican City. Michelangelo painted its ceiling between 1508 and 1512.
  • “the tombs of the Medicis”: The tomb of the Medicis is the Medici Chapel at the Basilica of San Lorenzo, Florence, Italy. The pawnbroker’s sign of three golden balls is often traced to the balls, or palle, on the Medici arms.
  • “Mt. Ararat, where Noah’s Ark was supposed to have rested”: Mount Ararat is a snow-capped dormant volcano in Turkey. “And the ark rested in the seventh month, on the seventeenth day of the month, upon the mountains of Ararat” (Genesis 8:4, KJV). See Genesis.
  • “hanging gardens in Babylon”: The Hanging Gardens of Babylon: see Chapter 36, Resources, for the full entry.
  • “the hoof of Pegasus striking water in an arid place”: The hoof of Pegasus is a myth about the winged, divine horse in Greek mythology. Wherever it stepped a water spring would burst forth. The spring he struck on Mount Helicon was Hippocrene, the horse’s spring, sacred to the Muses. See Greek Mythology.
  • “the shrouded Bedouins”: The Bedouins are a nomadic Arab tribe.
  • “old Brookline or old Newburyport or old Newport”: Brookline is a suburb in Boston, Massachusetts. Newburyport is a coastal city in Massachusetts. Newport is a city in Rhode Island.
  • “the stone men upholding the roofs of the houses of the dead”: Columns carved as men holding up a roof are called atlantes, after Atlas, who bears the sky; carved women are caryatids. See Greek Mythology.
  • “the music of the spheres”: Pythagoras held that the planets make a music by their motion, too constant for human ears to notice; in the Myth of Er at the end of Plato’s Republic a siren on each sphere sings one note, and together they make one harmony. See Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres.
  • “an old billiard-hall king who had once miscued”: Young retells the legend of Louis Fox, who lost the American billiards championship to John Deery at Rochester, New York, on 7 September 1865. In the story a fly landed on the cue ball as Fox neared victory; trying to shoo it, he touched the ball, miscued, lost, and drowned himself in the Genesee River. Only the match, his disappearance in December 1866, and the finding of his body in May 1867 are on record; the fly and the suicide are legend.
  • “the only man who could beat Louis XIV at billiards”: Louis XIV is said to have taken up billiards on his doctors’ advice, and Antoine Trouvain engraved him at the table in 1694. Michel Chamillart “had originally won the king’s favour through his honesty and his skill at billiards” (Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Michel Chamillart”); he became Controller-General of Finances in 1699 and Secretary of State for War in 1701. Louis XIV encouraged gambling at Versailles to entertain the nobles and keep them close, though gambling was banned for the general populace.
  • “the cue-stick was sometimes called a baton”: The cue grew out of the mace and is similar in shape to a cane. There is no reference for a cue-stick being called a baton. Cue-sticks could be very elaborate with jewels, inlays, and works of art. A baton was used in the military as a weapon and in heraldics. Billiards was first played with the mace, a stick with a broad head; players began to turn it around and strike with the narrow tail, the queue, which gave the cue its name. In English heraldry the baton sinister, a shortened diagonal band, marked royal bastards.
  • “He knew who was behind the 8-ball”: Being behind the eight ball refers to being in a difficult or disadvantageous position, often one from which it is hard to recover. The term originates from the game of pool, where being “behind the eight ball” means your cue ball is blocked by the eight ball, making it difficult to make a shot.
    • Pool is a series of cue sports played on a billiard table. The table has six pockets along the rails, into which balls are shot. Of the many different pool games, the most popular include: eight-ball, blackball, nine-ball, ten-ball, seven-ball, straight pool, one-pocket, and bank pool. Eight-ball is the most frequently played discipline of pool, and it is often thought of as synonymous with “pool.” The generic term pocket billiards is sometimes also used, and favored by some pool-industry bodies, but is technically a broader classification, including games such as snooker, Russian pyramid, and kaisa, which are not referred to as pool games. There are also hybrid games combining aspects of both pool and carom billiards, such as American four-ball billiards, bottle pool, cowboy pool, and English billiards. (Wikipedia)
  • “Death rode a stumbling nag”: In Revelation, “I looked, and behold a pale horse: and his name that sat on him was Death, and Hell followed with him” (Revelation 6:8, KJV), and the swift horse of the archer is the white horse of Revelation 6:2: “he that sat on him had a bow” (KJV). The stumbling nag keeps the pale horse’s meaning, signifying the arrival of death and its accompanying consequences, but with an added emphasis on a slower, less forceful, or more uncertain approach. This imagery emphasizes a sense of the fragility or the unpredictable nature of death. It is a vivid metaphor conveying the imperfect, faltering, and perhaps unpredictable nature of death. The image of death riding a “stumbling nag” (a nag being an old or worn-out horse) suggests that death is not a graceful, certain, or dignified force but one that is awkward, uneven, and uncertain in its approach or effect. In the context of the chapter, which is deeply concerned with mysteries, dreams, identities, and the blurred boundaries between life and death, this metaphor emphasizes the theme that death is not a neat or final event but an ambiguous, elusive presence that stumbles through the lives of the characters. It aligns with the overall tone of the narrative that questions reality, identity, and the nature of existence itself. This metaphor also complements the broader imagery in the chapter of confusion, fading identities, and the overlapping of shadows and realities. Death, like the other great uncertainties and mysteries is portrayed as something that is at once inevitable and yet strangely unstable, not easily grasped or understood. It is part of the blurred, dreamlike world where distinctions between life and death, reality and dream, are not clear-cut See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “Martin Luther was a great believer in bowling”: Martin Luther, the Protestant Reformation founder, had a bowling lane built next to his house. Mr. Spitzer thought Martin Luther recommended bowling as an outlet for “...human aggression and the thirst for conquest…” Organized sports has been compared to simulated war and as a diversion for the populace.
  • “a queen of the desert sands had played billiards”: The “queen of the desert” who played pool may refer to Cleopatra in Shakespeare’s play Antony and Cleopatra where she is bored and asks Charmian to play billiards. “Let it alone; let’s to billiards: come, Charmian” (2.5), an anachronism, since billiards is a much later game. See Shakespeare.
  • “Sir Walter Raleigh”: Sir Walter Raleigh was beheaded but there is no mention of him playing cards with his executioners. He is said to have felt the edge of the axe and called it a sharp medicine that would cure all diseases. See Sir Walter Raleigh.
  • “what was a playing card with eight pips”: A playing card with eight pips is any of the four eights in a standard deck. Each suit (hearts, diamonds, clubs, and spades) contains an 8 card with eight corresponding suit symbols (pips) on the face. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “what was an act of trumping”: Trump comes from triumph, the name of an old card game, which is why the musician first thinks of an angel blowing a trumpet. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “who was the jack of discs, who was the jack of cups”: The Latin suits of Italian and Spanish cards and of the tarot are cups, coins, swords, and batons; in tarot the coins are often drawn as discs or pentacles. A jack of cups is the knave of that suit. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “knew that in skijoring”: Skijoring is a winter sport in which a person on skis is pulled by a horse, dogs or a motor vehicle. Although skijoring is said to have originated as a mode of winter travel, it is currently primarily a competitive sport.
  • “what was the difference between a heavyweight and a middleweight”: Boxing in Young’s day had eight traditional weight divisions: flyweight, bantamweight, featherweight, lightweight, welterweight, middleweight, light heavyweight, and heavyweight, grouping fighters of similar size. Flag: after 1964 more divisions were added, and today there are 17 for men and 17 for women, from strawweight to heavyweight.
  • “who wore the featherweight crown for the longest time in history”: John Patrick Kilbane was an American featherweight boxer in the early part of the 20th century. He held the World Featherweight title from 1912 to 1923, the longest period in the division’s history, having defended the title against four contenders during the reign. He was inducted into the Ring Magazine Hall of Fame in 1960 and the International Boxing Hall of Fame in 1995.
    • Tony Canzoneri held the world light-welterweight title for only 33 days in 1933.
  • “who lost his leathers”: In boxing, “leathers” is slang for boxing gloves, specifically those made of leather. It refers to the traditional material used for boxing gloves, which offers superior protection, comfort, and durability compared to synthetic alternatives.
  • “He knew who changed his weight from fight to fight”: Henry Jackson Jr. was an American professional boxer and a world boxing champion who fought under the name Henry Armstrong. He is the only fighter to ever hold world championships in three divisions (featherweight, lightweight and welterweight) simultaneously. Armstrong was one of the few fighters to win titles in three or more different divisions: featherweight, lightweight, and welterweight. He defended his welterweight title a total of 19 times.
  • “a whole Decameron of sports”: Decameron is a collection of novellas by Giovanni Boccaccio. Written about 1353, it gathers a hundred tales told over ten days by ten young people who have fled the plague in Florence.
  • “who fought a spurred angel until the angel lost its feathers”: In Genesis 32:22-32 Jacob wrestles with an angel. See Genesis.
  • “his black etude for the black keyboard only”: Black keyboards where the sharps are white were found in harpsichords and fortepianos. The white keys with black sharps became the standard because it was easier to see the keys.
  • “what great contention was fought for more than an hour”: Young’s bout without a blow is not on record, but the longest fight on record was far from bloodless. On April 6, 1893, Andy Bowen and Jack Burke fought the longest fight in history. Bowen had originally scheduled the fight with another opponent, however after dropping out of the fight, Jack Burke, who was the latter’s trainer, fought the bout instead. The fight took place at the same Olympics Club Bowen had defended his title from Carroll. The fight lasted 110 rounds over seven hours and 19 minutes (each round lasting three minutes). It was reported that the fight went on for so long that the spectators who stayed to watch the fight had fallen asleep in their seats. It was also recorded that at round 108, with no clear end in sight, referee John Duffy made the decision that if no winner had emerged in the next 2 rounds, the bout would be ruled a “no contest.” With both men having become too dazed and tired to come out of their corners, Duffy declared the match a no contest. Burke broke all the bones in both of his hands and was bedridden for six weeks. He had considered retiring after the fight but chose to continue competing. Bowen fought four more times after the fight. The club where the fight took place would burn to the ground four years later.
    • Andy Bowen died at 27 years old after his 1894 fight with Kid Lavigne.
  • “who hit the canvas eighty-seven times and rose again”: The highest number of knockdowns recorded in a single professional fight involved Christy Williams, who was knocked down 42 times by Battling Nelson in a 1902 bout. Nelson himself was knocked down 7 times in that same fight, which he ultimately won by knockout in the 17th round. Another fight between Joe Jeanette and Sam McVey lasted for three and a half hours for forty-nine rounds in 1909 where Jeanette was knocked down twenty-seven times and McVey nineteen times. Jeanette won the fight. Jeanette was never given a shot at the world heavyweight title because he was African-American. At that time the rules did not state that when a fighter is knocked down the other fighter must go to a neutral corner.
  • “the umpire counts to twelve”: Referees have a ten count rule where the boxer must get to his feet, unaided, by that time. The count does not refer to the number of seconds since there have been times when a long count has resulted in a boxer having more than ten seconds. Modern boxing rules have a standing eight count.
  • “a former featherweight who had thought, every time he entered the ring, he was battling his father’s ghost”: The fighter retired from the ring and lived in isolation believing he had killed his father’s ghost when he killed that opponent. This is similar to the plot line in the Shakespeare play Hamlet where the ghost is Hamlet’s dead father. See Shakespeare.
  • “He knew a man who had fought on when he had broken his hand”: It is part of a richly symbolic and dreamlike meditation on perseverance, struggle, and endurance despite injury or limitation. Though the passage is metaphorical and layered with ambiguity, this particular phrase likely illustrates the idea of continuing to strive or fight even when physically or emotionally impaired, suggesting resilience in the face of hardship. Within the broader context of the chapter, Mr. Spitzer reflects on various images of endurance, loss, and identity, often blending reality and dream. The mention of a man who fights on despite a broken hand can be read as symbolizing the human capacity to endure suffering and continue struggling even when “broken” or diminished. It resonates with the novel’s themes of fragmented identities, the persistence of hope or effort despite injury or loss, and the ambiguous boundary between life and death, reality and dream. The phrase embodies the motif of persistence in adversity, highlighting that even with an injury that should impair one’s ability, the will to continue battling remains. It serves as a metaphor for the characters’ emotional or spiritual struggles, such as Mr. Spitzer’s own wrestling with loss and identity, or the suffragette’s relentless, though ultimately tragic, quest for love and meaning in a shifting, uncertain world. The phrase underscores resilience, the refusal to give up despite damage or limitation as a theme central to the novel’s meditation on human endurance amid mystery and loss.
    • Burke is said to have broken all the bones in both his hands during the fight with Bowen.
  • “He knew a champion steeple-sitter”: The champion pole sitter was Alvin “Shipwreck” Kelly, who started the flagpole-sitting fad in 1924 and in 1930 sat 49 days and one hour atop a pole on the Steel Pier at Atlantic City. The first pole sitters were the stylites, Christian hermits like Simeon Stylites, who lived for decades on a pillar in Syria in the fifth century.
  • “a man who was a Bridge of Sighs, a Bridge of Lies, a lake of fire”: Bridge of Sighs is a bridge in Venice, Italy. Built about 1600 over the Rio di Palazzo, it led prisoners from the Doge’s Palace to the prisons, and their sighs at a last view of Venice gave it its name. Bridge of Lies is a legendary pedestrian bridge in Sibiu, Transylvania, Romania. Lake of fire is a place of after-death punishment in Egyptian and Christian religions. “And death and hell were cast into the lake of fire. This is the second death” (Revelation 20:14, KJV). See Revelation and the Apocalypse and Ancient Egypt.
  • “many had wound up their days on Skid Row”: See Skid Row in the Chapter 52 resources.
  • “No man lived to die his own death”: Rainer Maria Rilke prayed, “O Herr, gib jedem seinen eignen Tod,” “O Lord, give each his own death” (The Book of Hours, The Book of Poverty and Death, 1905).
  • “Death danced with the lady”: This phrase metaphorically captures the intimate, complex, and ambiguous relationship between the lady (the old suffragette) and death. The lady is portrayed as someone who has lived a life filled with paradoxes, mystery, and transformative journeys. She is described as both active and passive, as a traveler between realms of life and death, reality and dream. The imagery of dancing with death suggests a close, almost companionable engagement with mortality, as if death is not merely an end but a partner in the ongoing movement of existence and identity. The “dance” implies a dynamic, fluid interaction rather than a static or fearful state. The lady, having abandoned conventional roles (such as bride or wife), and having lived a life of wandering, disguise, and defiance (including climbing mountains, flying, and escaping societal norms), engages with death not as a final cessation but as an essential part of her being and journey. It reflects her liminal status: between life and death, reality and dream, presence and absence. This metaphor also aligns with the novel’s broader themes about the blurred boundaries between states of being, the elusive nature of identity, and the intertwining of love, loss, and mortality. The lady’s dance with death is a poetic way to express that she has been inextricably linked to death, not just physically in dying but symbolically in how she lived, loved, and searched for meaning amid illusions and truths. It signifies the profound and enigmatic connection between the suffragette and death, highlighting the novel’s exploration of mortality as a dance, a continual movement where endings and beginnings coexist, and where death is not a distant stranger but a close and intimate partner.
    • It may refer to the artistic and literary theme of the Danse Macabre, also known as the Dance of Death. This theme, popular in the late medieval period, depicts death, often personified by a skeleton, leading people of all social classes in a dance to their graves. It serves as a reminder of the inevitability of death for everyone. This artistic and literary motif emerged in the late medieval period, particularly following the Black Death, to remind people of their mortality. Death is often portrayed as a skeleton or a figure of decay, leading people from all walks of life in a procession to the grave. The Danse Macabre highlights the idea that death is a great equalizer, affecting everyone regardless of social status or wealth. The theme is expressed in various forms of art, including paintings, woodcuts, and literature. Camille Saint-Saëns wrote the musical composition, “Danse Macabre,” which is a symphonic poem inspired by the theme. See Music and Composers.
    • See Cousin Hannah’s “mad dance, dance of death” on a mountain top in the Chapter 43 resources.
  • “perhaps for angels in all his angles”: Angels in all his angles may refer to a Latin pun attributed to Pope Gregory I when he saw pale skinned boys at the slave market. Bede tells the story (Ecclesiastical History of the English People 2.1): seeing fair-skinned boys for sale in the Roman marketplace and told they were Angles, Gregory said they had the faces of angels, a pun remembered as “Non Angli, sed angeli,” “Not Angles but angels.”
  • “when the parties changed”: The two political parties in the United States switched roles in the nineteenth century.
  • “this traditional Republican”: A traditional Republican may refer to the original Republican Party that emerged in the 1850s, founded by anti-slavery activists who opposed the expansion of slavery into US territories. During the 19th century, the party was strongly associated with the abolition of slavery and the preservation of the Union. See The Civil War.
  • “admirer of Garrison”: Garrison may refer to William Lloyd Garrison who published a newspaper in Boston against slavery and was for women’s rights. His paper was The Liberator (1831-1865).
    • Included is Althea Garrison because she was the first transgender person to be elected to a state legislature although she was not active in politics at the time Young wrote this novel.
  • “he had been a woman masquerading as a man”: Young’s ward-heeler has a real counterpart. Murray Hall, a Tammany Hall politician in New York who worked the 13th Senatorial District for some twenty-five years, lived as a man for about forty years, married twice, and voted at a time when no woman in New York could. When Hall died of breast cancer in January 1901, the discovery that Hall had been born female made headlines across the country, and Hall was buried in women’s clothing with none of the political colleagues at the funeral (Smithsonian Magazine, “The Mystery of Murray Hall”). See Women’s Suffrage.
  • “the rules introduced by the Marquis of Queensberry”: The Marquis of Queensberry, John Douglas (9th Marquess of Queensberry), did establish the Queensberry rules that are the basis for rules in modern boxing. The rules were written by John Graham Chambers and published under the Marquess’s sponsorship in 1867.
  • “John L. Sullivan”: John L. Sullivan was the last bare-knuckle heavyweight champion, defending his title against Jake Kilrain over 75 rounds in 1889, and he lost it to James J. Corbett in 1892 in the first heavyweight championship fought with gloves under the Queensberry rules.
  • “Gentleman Jim with his diamond-studded teeth”: Gentleman Jim may refer to James J Corbett who revolutionized boxing and was the only man to beat John L. Sullivan. There is no reference to Corbett having diamond-studded teeth but after his boxing career he did star in low budget films. He was considered the first sports sex symbol of the era.
  • “his old Prince Albert suit”: The Prince Albert suit is a frock coat with double-breasted, peaked lapels.
  • “leather punching bags, stuffed with sand or flour”: It serves as a metaphorical reflection on ancient human practices and the continuity of certain tools or symbols throughout history. This statement highlights the deep historical roots of the sandbag dummy (an object used for training, practice, or perhaps symbolic combat) and suggests it has been part of human culture for as long as human history itself. By referencing leather punching bags stuffed with sand or flour used long before the discovery of the Americas, the text emphasizes the long-standing human engagement with forms of struggle, preparation, or perhaps confrontation, whether physical or symbolic. Within the broader context of the novel, which often blends reality, memory, dream, and myth, this line likely underscores themes of timelessness, endurance, and the persistence of human rituals or symbols across ages. It may also reflect on the idea that certain human experiences (like conflict, self-discipline, or preparation) are universal and ancient, transcending specific historical moments. This line connects the concrete image of the sandbag dummy to a vast temporal scale, reinforcing the novel’s motifs of history, memory, and the layering of time.
    • Evidence suggests that ancient Egyptians, Greeks, and Romans utilized various forms of punching bags for combat training. These bags were often crafted from materials like leather and filled with sand or other readily available materials. Ancient Chinese and Japanese civilizations also used animal skin punching bags filled with sand or grain for martial arts training. Indian wrestling traditions also employed practice equipment called “mukha dhanda,” which were essentially hanging leather pouches filled with sand, pebbles, or grain. See Ancient Egypt.
  • “he knew the Jew who was knocked out by an angel”: It is an allusion rich with symbolic and possibly biblical meaning. Although the text does not provide explicit context for this phrase, it likely references the biblical story of Jacob wrestling with an angel, a well-known episode from the Book of Genesis 32. In that story, Jacob wrestles with a mysterious divine being (often interpreted as an angel or God himself) throughout the night and is physically and spiritually transformed by the encounter. The phrase “knocked out by an angel” suggests a powerful, overwhelming, possibly humbling or transformative experience. Within the context of chapter and the broader themes of the novel, the reference may symbolize a profound confrontation with truth, fate, or spiritual challenge. It could reflect a moment where a character is metaphorically ‘knocked out’ or overwhelmed by a higher power, a revelation, or an internal struggle. Given the novel’s dense and poetic style, filled with dreamlike, mythic, and spiritual imagery, this phrase serves as a metaphor for a transformative or decisive encounter that shakes the foundations of identity or belief.
    • This may refer to Genesis 32 that describes Jacob wrestling with a “man” throughout the night. In the Book of Hosea, this figure is explicitly called an “angel.” During the struggle, Jacob is injured, specifically having the socket of his hip put out of joint. This injury left him with a limp. Jacob is considered a foundational figure in Judaism and the patriarch from whom the nation of Israel descends. “Yea, he had power over the angel, and prevailed: he wept, and made supplication unto him” (Hosea 12:4, KJV). See Genesis and Judaism.
  • “the Greek fighters had used the cestus”: In ancient Greece and Rome, the cestus (also spelled caestus) was a type of combat glove, similar to modern-day brass knuckles, used in boxing and gladiatorial combat. Early Greek cesti were primarily leather straps or thongs used to protect the hands during boxing matches. As they evolved, particularly in Roman gladiatorial contexts, they became more elaborate, incorporating metal studs, spikes, and even lead or iron to inflict greater damage.
  • “who beat Mary, Queen of Scots, at golf”: Mary, Queen of Scots, is often called one of the first women golfers. The Book of Articles (1568), drawn up by her enemies, charged that after the murder of her husband Lord Darnley in February 1567 she played golf and pall-mall at Seton with the Earl of Bothwell, whom she married that May.
  • “what famous baseball player never made a come-back”: symbolizes a theme of loss, failure to return, or the impossibility of reclaiming a former glory or state. It metaphorically reflects characters or situations in the novel where someone or something once prominent or vital is unable to regain their previous status or vitality, much like a celebrated athlete who fails to return successfully after an absence or decline.
    • It may refer to Lou Gehrig. He was forced to retire from baseball in 1939 at the age of 36 due to amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), which became known as “Lou Gehrig’s disease.” He played his last game on April 30, 1939 and never played another major league game after taking himself out of the lineup on May 2, 1939, ending his consecutive game streak at 2,130. He died two years later in 1941.
  • “old Egyptian wrestling holds”: Ancient Egyptian wrestling, depicted in tomb paintings and sculptures, showcased a variety of holds and techniques, many of which are still recognizable in modern wrestling styles. These depictions, particularly those at Beni Hasan, reveal over 4000-year-old techniques, including throws, takedowns, and ground grappling, emphasizing both athletic prowess and strategic combat training. See Ancient Egypt.
  • “who drove a team of hairless racing dogs across the Arctic zone”: This can be interpreted as emblematic of vulnerability and exposed speed or urgency. The “hairless” aspect suggests a stripping away of protection or covering, implying nakedness, bareness, or exposure. The “racing dogs” evoke speed, pursuit, and perhaps desperation or a frantic chase. Together, this image conjures beings that are both vulnerable and driven forward rapidly, possibly symbolizing characters or forces in the narrative caught in a relentless, exposed pursuit of something: be it fate, love, or identity.
    • It may refer to Joseph-Armand Bombardier’s Tracked Vehicles, a precursor to the snowmobile.
    • In the winter of 1925, relays of dog teams carried diphtheria antitoxin 674 miles across Alaska to Nome in five and a half days, and the lead dogs Togo and Balto became famous.
  • “who vaulted the farthest distance”: The phrase belongs to Mr. Spitzer’s list of sporting records, and it suggests someone who has made the greatest leap, physically, emotionally, or spiritually, surpassing all others in attempting the impossible.
    • It may refer to Jesse Owens who held the men’s long jump world record for twenty-five years. He achieved his record-breaking leap of 8.13 meters (26 ft 8 in) on May 25, 1935. This record stood for an impressive 25 years until it was broken by fellow American Ralph Boston in 1960.
    • If the vault is the pole vault, Cornelius Warmerdam, vaulting on a bamboo pole, was the first to clear fifteen feet (1940), and his best, 4.77 meters (15 feet 7¾ inches) in 1942, stood as the world record until 1957.
  • “that early tennis balls were black”: Historically, balls were either black or white, depending on the background color of the court.
  • “the pebble embedded in Goliath’s forehead”: “And David put his hand in his bag, and took thence a stone, and slang it, and smote the Philistine in his forehead, that the stone sunk into his forehead; and he fell upon his face to the earth” (1 Samuel 17:49, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “tallyho was a term in hunting”: “Tallyho” is a cry traditionally used in fox hunting to signal the sighting of the quarry (the fox). It’s also used as an exclamation of excitement or triumph, particularly when hunting.
  • “archery was employed originally only in hunting”: Archery has been a crucial hunting method for millennia, with evidence of its use dating back to prehistoric times. Bows and arrows were also essential for warfare in many cultures throughout history. The widespread adoption of firearms, particularly flintlock weapons, by the 1800s led to a decline in the use of archery for hunting, especially in regions where firearms were readily available. However, archery did not disappear entirely. In fact, the 19th century saw a revival of archery as a sport and recreational activity, with the establishment of archery clubs and competitions in Europe and elsewhere. A notable example is the Toxophilite Society in London, founded in 1781. While firearms became more prevalent, archery continued to be used for hunting in some areas, particularly where firearms were not as accessible or by those who preferred the traditional methods. The “primitive” regions of the world continued using bows and arrows in warfare. Archery’s cultural significance extended beyond its practical uses. It was often associated with spirituality, skill, and social status in various societies. The book “The Witchery of Archery” by the Thompson brothers, who took up archery after the American Civil War, is credited with inspiring a revival of the sport in the United States.
  • “that fistmele, which he might at one time have supposed was a term in boxing”: Fistmele refers to the brace height, which is the distance between the bowstring and the deepest part of the bow’s grip, traditionally measured using a fist with the thumb extended. It’s a critical measurement for traditional bows, impacting performance and noise levels.
  • “are swimming strokes”: The butterfly stroke grew out of the breaststroke in the 1930s and was first swum as a separate Olympic event in 1956; the crawl is the freestyle stroke.
  • “the bird cage, of course, has something to do with gambling”: “birdcage” typically refers to Chuck-a-Luck, a dice game played with a distinctive hourglass-shaped cage containing three dice. The game is also known as “birdcage” or “sweat rag.” Players bet on the outcome of the dice roll after the dealer flips the cage and the dice come to rest. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “the flying horse is a hold in wrestling”: “Flying Mare” or “Flying Horse” (also known as Kasek Neyja or Mare) is a throw where a wrestler rotates their opponent over their back using a rope grip, similar to a shoulder throw but with a focus on rotational momentum and a bending forward motion. This technique is used in various wrestling styles, including Cornish wrestling and Collar and Elbow wrestling. In professional wrestling, “flying” moves refer to aerial maneuvers like dives and leaps onto opponents.
  • “acorns, hearts, bells, and leaves”: are associated with German-suited playing cards, a traditional card system used in Central Europe. These suits predate the more internationally recognized French suits (hearts, diamonds, clubs, and spades). German-suited cards typically feature 32 or 36 cards with these four suits. The acorns suit is one of the oldest. Leaves is another of the original suits. Hearts are the same in both the German and French systems. Bells are a distinctive German suit. German suits are believed to have influenced the development of the French suits. For example, the French “clubs” are thought to be derived from the German “acorns.” German-suited cards are still used in many parts of Central Europe. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “medieval ladies snow-balling”: There are historical depictions of medieval ladies participating in snowball fights. The January fresco of the months in the Torre Aquila of the Castello del Buonconsiglio at Trento, Italy (about 1400), portrays individuals engaged in a snowball fight, showcasing the postures and expressions of players. Snowball fights are also referenced in medieval chronicles.
  • “the clouded Argonauts”: In Greek mythology, the Argonauts were a group of heroes who, led by Jason, sailed on the ship Argo to retrieve the Golden Fleece from Colchis. The myth of the Argonauts is one of the most famous tales from Greek mythology. See Greek Mythology.
  • “Chinese kite fighting”: Chinese kite fighting, also known as “fighter kite” flying, is a competitive sport where participants attempt to cut the strings of their opponents’ kites using specially designed kites and string. The string is often coated with abrasive materials like glass or other substances to enhance its cutting ability. The last kite remaining in the air wins the contest. Kites date back to over two thousand years in China.
  • “Peron had been like a chameleon”: Chameleons are popularly believed to take on the colors of whatever they stand on, which is why an old joke asks what happens when a chameleon crawls onto plaid. In fact they change color mostly to signal and to control their temperature, by tuning tiny crystals in their skin (Jérémie Teyssier and others, Nature Communications, 2015). Flag: after 1964.
