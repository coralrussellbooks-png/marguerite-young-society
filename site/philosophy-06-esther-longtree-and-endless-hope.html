Chapter 6. Esther Longtree and Endless Hope

Part of The Philosophy in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Previous · Philosophy of MMMD Home · Next →

See also: Esther Longtree

Characters: Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Allusions: Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest

Chapters discussed: 72, 82

Definition from research

The Promise

Late in the novel, in the chapters set in the American interior, Vera meets Esther Longtree, a waitress in a small-town restaurant. Esther is the most alive person in the book. The narrator asks, “who could have been more robustly alive than she” (ch. 72). And she is “known for her everlasting pregnancy and her loving heart which knew no lover and her everlasting loneliness” (ch. 72).

Esther’s system is hope. She lives entirely toward the future, toward what will be born. She tells Vera she is “heading toward the coasts of birth where the old ghost babies stand,” while Vera, in her view, is headed “toward the coasts of death” (ch. 72). She promises, “Something will be born. Wait and see.” Her pregnancy is the body of hope itself: a life organized around a birth that is always about to happen.

There is an irony in where Vera finds her. In the first chapter, Miss MacIntosh urged Vera to leave the house of dreams and “see America first, the broad interior, the spacious Middle West,” where she would find “the clear reality of simple things.” Vera follows the advice. And in the broad interior she finds a woman pregnant with nothing that will ever be born.

The Loading

Young loads Esther’s hope with its own history. Esther has had many pregnancies, and all have ended in stillbirth. The salesmen passing through town are “the fathers of her stillborn whom she had hidden everywhere under the leaves and under the buds and under the snow and in the rivers and in the streams and in the still places and in the night” (ch. 72). The list runs on, as Young’s lists always do when experience is entering the argument. “There were as many stillborn as the autumn leaves, the snowflakes, and I could not count them,” Esther says. And she knows what her condition means: “I have been pregnant for an undue long time.”

Hope, lived this way, becomes recurrence. If she laughs too hard, she might lose this pregnancy too, “and then would have to begin it all over again, the great enterprise of her great loneliness” (ch. 72). What Mr. Spitzer proposed as a philosophical doctrine, Esther lives in her body. The eternal return is not the return of a beloved moment. It is the return of the same hope, and the same loss, forever.

The Break

The break comes in Esther’s own voice, and it is a break in the most basic of categories. “It is not true that people are either a success or a failure in life. They could be both and me the failure like the stillborn” (ch. 72). The narrator notes that “This thought had obviously astonished her.” Esther has discovered, in the middle of a conversation about coffee, that the categories by which lives are judged do not divide cleanly. Her hope is both her success and her failure. She is both mother and not a mother. She even says she has the “feeling of not being born myself yet.”

This is the logic of the whole novel stated by its least educated character. The categories of success and failure, born and unborn, living and dead, fail not because a philosopher dismantled them but because a waitress with swollen ankles lived long enough inside them to see them give way.

What Is Left

What is left is Esther’s love, and it proves stronger than her hope. When she first meets Vera, she crushes her hand and introduces herself: “I am me, Esther Longtree, the passive policeman’s only daughter, and you’re you,” and so, she says, they have “our common humanity” in common (ch. 72). It is a plain and almost comic version of the monist vision from the first chapter, stripped of all mystical language. Every heart is not the other heart. But Esther is Esther and Vera is Vera, and that shared separateness is itself a bond.

At the novel’s end, Esther has become the figure through whom Vera sees everything. “She was the mother of the stillborn clapping their hands in clouds, and she loved us all and all things hurt or silent or broken or dead or living” (ch. 82). Hope failed. Love remained, and it extended to everything hope had lost. The final sentence about Esther is a single line: “Esther Longtree would be me.” The woman of endless hope becomes the future of the narrator herself.
