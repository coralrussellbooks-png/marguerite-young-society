Jacqueline / Jackie White: The Life Made From a Name

← Homer Capehorn · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · The Tavern Landlord →

Characters: Homer Capehorn · Jacqueline White · Madge Capehorn · Vera Cartwheel

Madge is built from a face. Jacqueline is built from less than that. She is the dead girl inside the invented marriage, the rival Homer loved and never won, and she is never once on the bus. Vera invents Madge out of a sleeping stranger, and then, inside that invention, Madge invents Jacqueline out of a name Homer cries in his sleep. She is a ghost at two removes, a made-up woman's made-up rival, and the novel gives her more ache and more shine than either of the sleepers who dreamed her.

On the surface she is the county's golden girl. She could “skate like a comet,” and she was “not afraid to skate where the ice was thinnest,” which is also the shape of her dying. She was the cheerleader, the best dancer, first in every sport and every prize, the one carried on shoulders while the boys who loved her fought in the grass and never won her. Now, in the cold dead spring of the story, she is going to her grave of “galloping consumption,” coughing blood, spreading it through the town like a rumor, while her father turns her plate face-down at the family table among the plates of the other dead. Every fact about her is a fact someone else supplies. She never speaks. She never appears. She is entirely reported, and most of the reporting is invented on the spot.

This is where Jacqueline pushes the book's deepest claim past where Madge left it. The Madge piece said that to see a stranger is already to make her up. Jacqueline is the case with nothing to see. Homer swears he had not thought of her in years. It is Madge who says the name first, Madge who “resurrected Jacqueline,” Madge who keeps “blowing the dead sparks to life and making the dead ashes glow,” and only then does Homer begin to remember. The haunting is manufactured. Homer even states the law out loud, that “women dreamed too much, imagining things which were not there, and sometimes they brought them to life,” which is Radical Immanence spoken by a man who is himself being imagined as he speaks it. And Madge, the guilty one, admits her own method without flinching: “Things that people tell me, and the rest I can imagine.” A stranger on a bus invents a husband, the husband's invented wife invents his dead sweetheart, and the dead sweetheart aches. Reality is made rather than found, and here it is made out of jealousy and a cried name.

Because Jacqueline has no inside, she becomes a mirror, and the mirror shows Madge. “No one ever read Jacqueline's mind or knew what she thought,” and “she had never spoken, and so they had all loved her.” She is a blank the whole county writes itself onto, which is exactly why Madge cannot keep herself separate from her. Jacqueline is light, fast, unpregnant, unmarried, adored, everything Madge was before the child and the ruin, so the rival is really the lost self, the girl Madge should have been, “so light, so light on her feet.” Homer, half asleep, tries to hold them apart with a broken geometry lesson, insisting “You can't be she, Madge. She can't be you,” that two bodies cannot occupy the same place in space, but the passage keeps melting them together anyway, and he finally lands the truth by accident: “It's you who think too much of her, too much of Jacqueline.” The jealous woman and the girl she envies are one heart split in two, which is the oldest law of this novel, that every heart is the other heart.

Her death is not private either. When Jacqueline coughs, the whole town coughs, the preacher in the pulpit and the horses on the road and the fiddler in the saloon, a chorus of contagion, as if one girl's dying could be shared out among everyone who ever loved her. The men line up to drink from her glass to prove they are good sports. The sister who kissed her on the lips caught it and followed her down. Grief in this book does not stay where it starts, and it does not resolve. It spreads, it accumulates, and it takes the living along with the dead.

This is the strange dignity the novel hands its most invented character. Homer gives it the plainest words anyone gives anything: “She was all of us, and she was none of us. We elected her to be immortal.” That is consensus reality set down in a single sentence. Jacqueline is real the way the shared world is real, not because anyone found her but because enough people agreed to. She even chose the class motto, “To thine own self be true,” which is the book's quiet joke, since she is the one figure with no self to be true to, a girl who is all of us and none of us being handed the oldest line about the solid inner core this whole novel denies anyone has.

The lesson for a writer sits one layer deeper than it did with Madge. A careful writer keeps invention tethered to something seen. Young cuts the tether entirely. Jacqueline is invented out of a name, by a character who is herself invented out of a face, and she is still the most vivid person in the chapter: aching, skating, dying, adored. The novel is showing that a made thing does not need a real thing under it to be real. The girl assembled from a cried name and another woman's jealousy carries as much weight as anyone who was actually there, because in this book being made up by someone who loves you, or envies you, is not the opposite of existing. It is how existing works.
