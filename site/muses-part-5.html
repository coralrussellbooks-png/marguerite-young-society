Inviting the Muses, Part 5

← Part 4 · Inviting the Muses: A Reading, Part by Part · Part 6 →

Part 5 finishes "The Old Sweetheart," covers "The World of Silence" and the title essay "Inviting the Muses" in full, and ends on the first page of "The Artist as Wanderer."

"The Old Sweetheart," conclusion (Mademoiselle, 1951)

The woman in the forest turns out to be the emotional center of the search. Unaware that McClanahan had already given Riley her tintype, she sends him one herself, "the shadow of her hand," wrapped with a ribbon and a lock of her hair. Young lays out the triangle with real delicacy: she loves Riley next to McClanahan, McClanahan loves Riley next to her, and Riley loves her after McClanahan, "all three separated by space." When they finally meet there is no disappointment, but she is dying, and she belongs to the wanderer. McClanahan comes back, they marry, Riley stands as best man, and she lives two years before becoming "their sacred memory."

Then Young lets comedy back in. Ella Wheeler Wilcox, another correspondent, meets Riley at last and he scolds her fashionable gown and her bang: would Mrs. Browning have dressed that way? Wilcox snaps back that Mrs. Browning "would have looked better with a bang than with those dreadful corkscrew curls in that old tintype." The poet is still searching for the girl in the poem even after he has met the real woman. He greets another literary editor with "Where are your corkscrew curls?"

The ending is Young's answer to her own question. Asked by a reporter on the telephone where his wife is, Riley says she might be in heaven, might be anywhere, that he might never have seen her, that "he might be talking to her even then, that she might be at the other end of the wire." The old sweetheart was never one woman. She was the search, the Muse, the ideal that no living person could be. Young handles this with more sympathy than she gave Old James, but the pattern is the same: a man who loves a figure in his head more than anyone who can walk into the room.

"The World of Silence" (Flair Annual, 1952)

This is the most ambitious essay so far, part reporting and part meditation on what it means to perceive. Young opens with Condillac's statue, a marble figure given the senses one at a time until it wakes into consciousness, and concludes that the world is built out of "our dreaming senses." The deaf, in her image, are statues complete except for one sense, missing "the footsteps in the ruined garden."

Robert Owen appears early, deaf in old age and holding long debates on socialism with spirits whose voices he "distinctly heard," publishing everything they said. For the author of Angel in the Forest, which dealt with Owen's New Harmony, it is a telling choice: the utopian who can hear only the voices he imagines.

Her best insight comes in one line: "The tragedy of those born deaf is not that they do not hear but that others hear." The problem, in other words, is social exclusion. From there the essay traces a history of cruelty, with deaf children thrown into pits in Sparta, into the Tiber, into holes in the ice, confined in imperial Japan, and "extinguished" in Nazi Germany. She does not let America off either. The first deaf education in the colonies, in 1776, went to the aristocratic Bolling family, with the teacher bound for five thousand dollars not to reveal his method, "the spirit was exclusive," while the poor deaf went unnoticed. In modern New York, she notes, some deaf people still live without any language at all.

The middle of the essay favors lip-reading and oral schooling, which was the dominant view of her time and is now strongly contested by Deaf communities who see sign as a full language. Yet Young's heart is plainly with the signs. She calls their rhythms "the counterpart of music," says they exist "all in the present tense," and suspects that for the deaf "time may be an illusion, a human construct." She describes Plains sign language and a Greenwich Village coffee shop where an Indian and a deaf painter discuss "Kantian metaphysics, baroque architecture, Virginia Woolf, Stalin's Russia" entirely by hand. Some of her claims show their age, such as her assertion that Chinese writing rests on pictures and that Semitic traders brought Egypt "the sense of time," and a passing generalization about Jewish deaf and criminality.

The essay's recurring word is amputation. The deaf man's life is "amputated from hearing," his isolation "a real amputation." After the grandmother's foot and the tintype of the hand, the collection's body-part motif now carries real weight: a missing sense treated as a missing member that society either restores or buries.

Its most moving pages are religious. A lay rabbi signs the stories of Noah's ark and Jacob's ladder, and Young raises a hard psychiatric question: how does a man with no language describe "his guilt complex if he has not even one small word?" At St. Ann's, meeting at St. Mark's in the Bowery, the service is signed, and "the hearer feels, in this church, his difference from others." Then comes a deaf Hamlet, "to be or not to be, which is the problem of the silence." The essay ends on the signs for mother, ocean, and burial: "the hands lowered slowly to the ground, the earth which is the mother."

"Inviting the Muses" (Mademoiselle, 1965)

The title essay is a look back at Young's teaching, first at Iowa and then at the New School, Columbia, Fairleigh Dickinson, and Fordham. It opens with one enormous sentence that runs from a Quonset hut in the snow through seals, penguins, frozen butterflies, mailboxes lost in drifts, and tin roofs drumming in the rain. That sentence is not self-indulgence. It is a demonstration of what she teaches, which she names at the end as "the dragnet sentence," where students drag the waters of memory and association and bring in whatever haul there is.

Her creed is compact. Talent is "almost a commonplace," like dreaming, but most people are stopped by self-criticism before anything reaches the page. The great writer "has no tollkeeper at the gate" and lets even the ridiculous through, trusting that the final form will make it cohere. She throws out the "beginning and middle and end" formula and asks instead for "memory and observation, dream and reality, conflict and character." Two definitions carry over directly from "Complex Life and Complex Letters": "Form is that which keeps the reader reading. What is style? Style is thinking." Twenty years after her fight with the commonsense critics, her position has not moved an inch.

There is humor in her picture of the poets next door, arguing all night over a raindrop and suspicious of prose, and real generosity toward students who later told her the long sentence was the first time they felt "the wonder of being a writer." The essay ends: "For literature is that which is memorable."

"The Artist as Wanderer" (opening only)

The first page sets up the next essay's terms: the artist as "a dedicated dreamer exploring the dream," asking whether the dream is real or reality the dream. She works through Pasternak's poems "The Waves" and "Finale" and a derivation of Mary from the word for both sea and horse.

Across part 5

The three complete pieces all turn on the same question from different angles: how a person reaches what they cannot directly perceive. Riley reaches toward a sweetheart who exists only in words, the deaf reach a world through the hand and eye, and the writing student reaches the unconscious through the long sentence. In each case Young favors the path through imagination and the body over the plain, sensible route, and the hand keeps reappearing as the instrument, whether it holds a Chinese fan in a tintype, signs the Book of Genesis, or casts the dragnet.
