Research notes: Chapter 63 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 63

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

Miss MacIntosh enters this chapter as a unified, stable, almost aggressively singular identity. She is the nursemaid. The pragmatist. The woman with the umbrella and the silver-dollar watch. But as the scene progresses, her self fractures visibly and irreversibly.

She speaks in her brother Richard's voice, in the voice of a would-be husband, in the voice of a man who might have lifted his hat at horse races:

"My elder brothers called me George. I had no younger brothers. My sisters had long ropes of hair."

She imagines herself as male, as a Quaker reformer, as a drowned sea captain, as Emperor Constantine, as Canute. She says:

"Perhaps it would have been easier, my dear, to have been a man than a woman, and I have always said so, easier to have played a man's role in this man's world."

The wig itself is the most concentrated symbol of plural selfhood. It has existed in multiple colors: red (her brother Richard's color), black (in memory of another who disappeared), and a grey she was considering:

"There have been several such, in fact, and of various shades, and I was thinking of the grey."

Each wig is a self. Each color is a different grief, a different allegiance, a different attempt to inhabit a coherent identity. The bald head beneath is not the "true self" revealed; it is the absence of any fixed self, the ivory dome that belongs to everyone and no one:

"Here's the livid skull that might be any fool's or king's."

Not One Self pillar holds that the self is multiple, simultaneous, and irresolvable, not a problem to fix. Miss MacIntosh has spent her entire life treating her multiplicity as a problem to fix, covering it with wigs, with common sense, with a black umbrella. The chapter is the moment she stops fixing it.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Miss MacIntosh is, at the outset, the novel's designated enemy of illusion. She is the person who would beat back "tides of imagination which break upon no shore", who dismisses simulacra and graven images of truth. She is the anti-illusionist.

And yet: she is entirely constructed.

Her hair is a wig. Her eyebrows are painted on. Her breast is a pad over an absence. Her common sense is a performance so total it has become indistinguishable from a delusion. The narrator perceives this with dawning horror:

"That is not your hair you are wearing. That is undoubtedly something which came from an ordinary wig-maker's. Perhaps every hair came from a different head."

The philosophical irony is devastating: the person most committed to unmasking illusion is the most thoroughly masked. But the Radical Immanence framework does not treat this as hypocrisy. It treats it as evidence. The line between illusion and reality is itself a construction. Miss MacIntosh did not lie about reality; she made a reality, as all selves do, and she made it with particular care because she understood, at some deep level, how necessary the making is. Her confession confirms this:

"Perhaps I have seemed deceiving, but you should have recognized me long ago, just by all that I was not and could not be, just by what was missing from these features!"

What was missing is the truth. The absence (of eyebrows, of eyelashes, of a breast, of hair) is the real. But the real is only legible through the construction that surrounded it. There is no sunlit world outside the cave. There is only a larger cave, more carefully lit. Miss MacIntosh's common sense was the lighting.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

This chapter is saturated with grief that has no origin point and no resolution. Miss MacIntosh's losses are enumerated but never explained: the broken nose, the missing breast, the absent brothers (Richard, the unnamed one who disappeared), the heart that was broken by someone unnamed, the marriage that never happened, the honest man she never found.

She does not grieve dramatically. She grieves continuously, in the mode of weather:

"For myself, I do not mourn. I mourn for others without end."

This is the grammar of accumulated grief. It does not belong to a single event. It is the climate of her entire existence. The losses have sediment: the red wig for Richard, the black wig for another, the grey wig she was considering for some future loss or perhaps for the self she has not yet mourned.

Her statement about the obituary columns is particularly revealing:

"Here's a man who has died far from home. Here's one who has died intestate. Here's one who had no name or face. Here's a child. Here's one who was never born and yet has died."

She reads the obituaries not as news but as taxonomy of her own losses. The one who had no name or face. The one who was never born. These are not strangers. They are the accumulated weather of her inner life, catalogued in newsprint because there is nowhere else to put them.

The honest life, under Radical Immanence, stops pretending grief resolves. Miss MacIntosh has always pretended otherwise: has insisted on common sense, on getting on with the day's business, on sweeping the waves back. This chapter is the moment she stops pretending, and the grief does not arrive as a wave. It arrives as a climate change. The temperature simply drops, and stays dropped.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator, Vera, is the primary witness of this chapter, and her witnessing is tested severely. She has provoked this crisis (has named the wig, has demanded honesty) and now must remain in the room with what her demand has produced.

Her response when the wig comes off is notable for what it refuses to do. It refuses pity. It refuses horror. It refuses the comfortable escape of resolution:

"Oh, no, Miss MacIntosh... Indeed, I do not mind. Indeed, I do not, Miss MacIntosh,’ I begged her, ‘and I will always love you, and I will never part from you."

This is the ethical stance of the Witness. Not to fix. Not to conclude. Not to rush toward the comfort of a resolved narrative. To stay. The narrator does not say "I understand now" or "all is forgiven" or "you should have told me." She says: I will not leave.

But the chapter also tests whether the narrator can witness without simplifying. Her speech after the wig comes off risks exactly this:

"You are the same person you always were... The outer man is nothing... Perhaps that's the only reality there really is."

This is almost a failure of witnessing: a rush toward philosophical consolation that would close the wound rather than stay with it. The narrator is tempted to make meaning too quickly, to convert the revelation into a lesson. Miss MacIntosh gently refuses this:

"A tree without flower or fruit and a stone without grass and a sea without water."

She will not be consoled into a tidy philosophical statement. She insists on the intractability of what has been revealed. The witness must hold this intractability without resolving it.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Miss MacIntosh's entire life has been a recursive construction. She builds a self (the practical nursemaid, the enemy of illusion) knowing at some level that the building is provisional, that the wig will eventually come off, that the breast pad will eventually be named. And then she rebuilds:

"Shall we not rather go back to our normal ways, Vera, and forget this incident, try to mend holes which grow as we mend them?"

This is not denial. This is the recursive constructor's knowledge: the building is never finished. The mending creates new holes. You return to the beginning with what you learned. You build again.

Her speech about the umbrella (Jonas Hanway, the Orient, the hackney drivers) is itself a recursive construction. She builds an elaborate philosophical and historical digression in the middle of her own unmasking. She is constructing while being deconstructed. The umbrella story is not a distraction. It is the demonstration: even in the moment of radical exposure, the self keeps building its shelters, keeps finding new pagodas to carry over its bald head.

The umbrella is the perfect symbol for Pillar 5. It is provisional: cloth over ribs of steel, easily blown inside out by oceanic storms. It does not stop the rain. It creates a temporary private space within the rain. You build it anyway. You carry it anyway. The rain continues.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Miss MacIntosh's confession is structurally incomplete. She never fully explains her baldness. She never names the man who broke her heart. She never identifies the brothers clearly. She never resolves the question of whether she is George or Miss MacIntosh, man or woman, nursemaid or something else entirely.

"There is much to do this morning, all my clothes closets to clean out, and we will not discuss the subject further."

This is not evasion. This is Unfinished Is Honest enacted. The demand for full explanation, for the complete narrative of who she is and how she came to be bald and why she has no breast, would be a violence against the experience. The ongoing sentence is her integrity. The concluded one would be a lie.

The narrator protests: "But Miss MacIntosh...", and is hushed. The hush is ethical. It says: the incompletion is the truth. To complete it would be to falsify it.

Her final statement in the chapter is the most compressed expression of this pillar:

"I suffer especially when there are rainbows."

This sentence is radically incomplete. It offers no explanation, no context, no resolution. It is the ongoing sentence. And it is more honest, more devastating, more real than any complete explanation could be.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Miss MacIntosh is, in the most literal sense, a figure of the marginal. She is bald in a world where women's hair is their "glory". She has one breast. She may be, in some sense, a man named George. She is a nursemaid (a servant, economically marginal) who has been sleeping in doorways. She follows the Stock Exchange with imaginary investments. She is, as she says, "God's poorest and lowest".

But the chapter reveals that her marginality is not incidental to her role in the novel. It is structural. She has been positioned as the voice of common sense, of reality, of the undeceived world: precisely because she is the most marginal figure in the house. Her marginality was mistaken for clarity.

The Margins Reveal the Truth holds that the edges of categories are where the constructed nature of reality becomes most visible. Miss MacIntosh is at the edge of every category: gender, class, embodiment, sanity, life and death. And at every edge, the construction shows:

"Heaven preserve you from a beardless man. Heaven preserve you from a hairy woman."

She is the hairy woman who has no hair. The embodied woman who has no breast. The practical realist who has been performing reality. She is not the exception. She is the evidence that all the categories (man/woman, real/illusory, sane/mad) are constructions maintained by the effort of covering.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The temporal structure of this chapter is entirely sedimentary. Miss MacIntosh's losses do not exist in a linear past; they press up through the present moment of the breakfast table, through the newspaper, through the wig.

The red wig carries her brother Richard. The black wig carries another unnamed loss. The broken nose carries a history never explained. The missing breast carries a surgery or a violence from "a long time ago". The Emperor Constantine, who changed the calendar, is a figure she encounters in the present tense of her daily constitutional, not as a historical figure but as someone she might still give a piece of her mind to.

This is sedimentary time. Constantine is not behind her. He is beneath her, still pressing up. Benjamin Franklin is not history. He is the reason she cannot sleep. The old lamp-lighters are not gone. They are the absence that shapes the present:

"No old lamp-lighters wanted,’ she sighingly remarked. ‘None coming with their ladders now."

The want ads she reads aloud are a geological record of accumulated loss: the ships' chandlers starving, the umbrella repair men who cannot afford their shrouds, the rag-pickers naked. These are not economic statistics. They are the sediment of a world that was and still presses up through the world that is.

Her most explicit statement of sedimentary time is about the calendar:

"The time was the time, and it was too late for his regret, too late for him to mend his manners, too late for her to mend hers."

Too late, but not gone. The wrong day of the week persists. The changed calendar still structures every day she lives. The past is not behind her. It is the ground she walks on.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The language of this chapter is in constant self-examination. Miss MacIntosh's speech is characterized by a particular rhetorical mode: the self-interrupting declaration. She states a position, then immediately undermines it, then rebuilds it in a different form:

"The Bible exaggerates. You cannot break that which was always broken."

"God Himself was ignorant."

"Who holds the cards?"

These are not the statements of someone who believes language can transparently convey truth. They are the statements of someone who knows that every sentence is a construction, that every declaration carries its location: its speaker's wound, its speaker's wig, its speaker's missing breast.

Her most honest statement about language comes near the end:

"Words are as nothing, and I have always said so, that we speak when we live not — and when we live, we are silent. Love has shut many a woman's mouth."

This is language naming its own limitation. Language is what happens in the absence of living. The wig coming off is not a verbal act; it is the moment language fails and something else takes over. The honest language of the chapter is not Miss MacIntosh's speeches. It is the gesture: she lifted off her wig, much as if this were a very ordinary thing to do.

The narrator's language also names its own location. She speaks "with another's reproachful voice"; she is aware that she is a ventriloquist's dummy, that her demand for honesty is not purely her own but has been shaped by all the voices that have accumulated in her. Her language is not transparent. It is located in a history of being spoken through.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter does not arrive from outside. It arrives through the ordinary objects on the breakfast table, through the newspaper, through the wig on the chair back.

When the wig comes off, the scene becomes briefly transcendent, but the transcendence is entirely immanent. It is located in the bald head reflecting light like a mirror, in the crystal chandelier casting prisms:

"She arose and walked about, clapping her hands, dancing, almost dancing, spinning under the crystal chandelier which cast the shadows of its prisms upon the air in sudden flushings of light, prisms upon her face, her head as the wind blew, prisms of rose and blue and green and gold with tinklings of sound like the sound of the distant sea."

This is the sacred. Not God arriving from outside. Not a vision or a revelation in the conventional sense. A bald woman dancing under a chandelier, her head refracting light into colors. The transcendence is entirely produced by the immanent, by glass and light and a head that has no hair to absorb the colors.

Miss MacIntosh's final gesture toward transcendence is also entirely immanent:

"I suffer especially when there are rainbows."

The rainbow is not a divine sign here. It is an ordinary meteorological event that intersects with a particular wound. The sacred is the intersection: the place where the ordinary world touches the accumulated grief and produces an unbearable beauty. This is The Sacred Is Here: the sacred pointing deeper into the ordinary, not outward to a beyond.

Her earlier statement about electric lights is also, unexpectedly, a statement about immanent transcendence:

"No one had ever seen the true face of death or love."

She does not want electric lights because they prevent the kind of seeing that requires darkness: the seeing that happens in the body's own luminescence, in the phosphorescent brain, in two people lying down in the dark not knowing quite what they have married. The sacred, for Miss MacIntosh, lives in the unlit corner, in the street without lights, in the private dream in which a poor man may die.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The final and most structurally important pillar. Miss MacIntosh is not a self moving through time. She is where time crosses itself.

She is constituted by her brothers (Richard's red hair on her head, the unnamed brother's black) by the Emperor Constantine who changed the calendar she still lives by, by Benjamin Franklin whose lightning she still resents, by Jonas Hanway whose umbrella she still carries, by the honest man she never married, by the child she never had, by the woman she nursed who is now the narrator.

And she is constituting the ground the not-yet-born will stand on. Her final speeches to Vera are explicitly about this transmission:

"Oh, Vera, take heed of my warning. Be not afraid of life."

"Take this from one who has no time to dream in, whose life is very short."

She is the Crossing Point. The ancestors are in her sediment: the brothers, the lost loves, the old Chicago, the broken nose. And she is depositing herself into Vera's sediment. The carrying continues.

Her statement about the two brothers Spitzer is the most explicit articulation of this:

"What choice had the two brothers Spitzer if ever there were, as I have heard, two brothers, one who said no when he meant yes, one who said yes when he meant no? We are not all like your mother, confounded among the beautiful and enigmatical choices, unable to move or teach the pawn to move. Time moves us when we move not. There is no time."

Time moves us when we move not. This is the Crossing Point in its most compressed form. The self does not move through time. Time moves through the self. You are where the old choices and the not-yet-made choices intersect. The obligation is not chosen. It is structural.

And her final departure, which she announces but does not yet enact, is framed as a continuation of this structural carrying:

"I will step out of my shoes. I will step out of my life, dear, and you will never find me."

She will step out. But she will not disappear. She will become sediment. She will become the ground Vera stands on. The carrying continues.
