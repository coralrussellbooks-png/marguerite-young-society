Boethius, De Musica, and the Architectural Structure of Miss MacIntosh, My Darling

I. The Word Count That Matters

The word music, including musician and musical, appears over 1,100 times in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling. The phrase music of the spheres or its equivalent, the silent or unheard music that Mr. Spitzer composes, appears nine times. Dead appears over 1,600 times. Love appears thousands of times across the novel's second half. These are not incidental repetitions. In a novel written over eighteen years by a scholar who controlled every sentence, a word that appears 1,100 times is a structural element.

The philosophical framework that makes this repetition architecturally coherent is Boethius's De Institutione Musica, his sixth-century treatise on the nature of music. Boethius divided music into three categories: musica mundana, the music of the spheres, the mathematical harmony of the cosmos that is inaudible to human ears; musica humana, the internal music of the human body, the harmony between body and soul; and musica instrumentalis, the audible music produced by singers and instruments. He said all reality is musical. His drawings in De Musica appear striking and may be an inspiration for the organization of Young's novel.

That may be one of the most significant architectural observations in the entire project. Young may have taken visual inspiration from Boethius's diagrams. The three-part structure of musica mundana, musica humana, and musica instrumentalis maps onto the three registers in which music operates throughout Miss MacIntosh: the cosmic, the interior, and the absent.

II. Musica Mundana: The Music No One Hears

Mr. Spitzer is the novel's primary musical consciousness. He is described as a composer of silent music, music he has written in scraps and fragments all his life, music that no one has ever played. His compositions are intended for an audience of the dead. He wonders whether there is such a thing as a non-aural music, a non-visual language, a reality not dependent upon the ear or the eye.

This is musica mundana translated into the novel's register. Boethius's music of the spheres is mathematically real but inaudible to human ears. Mr. Spitzer's music is compositionally real but unperformed, unheard, written for instruments no merely mortal musician could play. The parallel is exact. Mr. Spitzer inhabits the same structural position in the novel that musica mundana holds in Boethius's system: the highest category, the one that orders everything else, the one that exists most fully precisely because it cannot be directly heard.

He had added to his last will and musical testament, that his musical rags had not covered him, for there was no great tailor, no tailor but that mad tailor stitching in a dream... if he had known what he was doing in the first place... perhaps he never would have done so, but would have allowed his music to write him.

The music writes the composer. The cosmos composes itself. Musica mundana is not created by anyone; it exists in the mathematical relationships of the celestial bodies. Mr. Spitzer's silent music exists in the same way: not as a score that could be performed but as the structure of his consciousness, the harmony that organizes his grief and his love and his endless meditation on the dead. He is not the composer. He is the conduit.

III. Musica Humana: The Body and Soul in Argument

Boethius's musica humana is the harmony, or disharmony, between body and soul. It is the internal music of the human being, the tuning or mistuning of the self's parts. In Miss MacIntosh, the relationship between body and soul is one of the most persistently examined questions in the novel. The word soul appears over 250 times. Body appears throughout in relation to soul, most often in states of division, conflict, or uncertain reunion.

“Two tenants wrangled in an empty house, that which was his body and his soul.” Mr. Spitzer's formulation is pure musica humana: the internal music of a self whose parts are not in harmony, whose body and soul are tenants in the same house arguing over possession. Every character in the novel has a version of this dissonance. Catherine's body lies horizontal in bed while her consciousness travels through opium dreams that are more real to her than the body's location. Miss MacIntosh's body is ordinary and practical and fully present; her secret, her baldness, her history, is a soul that does not match the body's performance of normalcy. Vera's body is on a bus traveling through Indiana; her consciousness is everywhere and nowhere simultaneously.

Musica humana as a structural principle explains why the novel can move so fluidly between exterior event and interior consciousness without those movements feeling like violations of realism. The novel is not a realistic novel that has been interrupted by a stream of consciousness. It is a novel organized around the idea that the interior music of each character is as real and as architecturally significant as anything they do or say. The body and the soul are both instruments in the same composition. The composition requires both.

IV. Musica Instrumentalis: The Sounds That Are and Are Not Heard

Musica instrumentalis is Boethius's category for audible music, the music produced by singers and instruments. In Miss MacIntosh this category is populated with instruments that appear throughout: flutes, pianos, banjo, clarinet, violin, trumpet, the alpenhorn that Cousin Hannah owns and that Mr. Spitzer steals after her death, the seven-stringed harp whose strings bear the music of soul and spirit and astral body.

But musica instrumentalis in the novel is almost always either silent or heard by the wrong ears. The stone deaf man at the end of the novel, whom Vera falls in love with, has never heard a traffic horn in his travels across America. His musical voice rolls through silence. He hears not with ears but with eyes and fingers. The last reference to music in the novel is Vera's statement about loving this stone deaf man: his musical voice rolling through silence as I heard him who could not hear me. Musica instrumentalis, the category of audible music, ends in silence. The sound reaches only the one who cannot hear it.

This is Boethius inverted, and the inversion is the novel's most serious argument about music. Boethius's three categories are arranged hierarchically: musica mundana is highest, musica instrumentalis is lowest. Young takes the lowest category and makes it the novel's final image: the musical voice that cannot be heard, the love that communicates across the impossibility of shared sound. Musica instrumentalis does not disappear into the higher categories. It persists, imperfect, audible to those who have no ears for it, real precisely because it cannot be contained by the hierarchy that was supposed to organize it.

V. The Diagrams and the Novel's Visual Architecture

The idea that Boethius's drawings in De Musica appear striking and may be an inspiration for the organization of Young's novel points toward a dimension of the architectural argument that cannot be fully developed without the diagrams themselves. Boethius's visual representations of musical proportion, of the mathematical relationships between the three categories of music, of the hierarchy from the cosmic to the human to the instrumental, are geometric as well as conceptual. They organize space as well as sound.

If Young took visual as well as conceptual inspiration from these diagrams, then the organization of the novel's chapters may follow a spatial as well as a temporal logic. The chapters are not arranged as episodes in a sequence; they are arranged as positions in a structure. Catherine's opium chapters, Mr. Spitzer's legal and musical meditations, Vera's bus journey and her memories, Cousin Hannah's suffrage and her secret love, Esther Longtree's perpetual pregnancy: these may be positions in a Boethian diagram, each occupying a specific place in the hierarchy of musica mundana, musica humana, and musica instrumentalis, each demonstrating a different relationship between the cosmic order and the individual consciousness that cannot quite hear it. This is the architectural argument that most requires return to the novel itself. The diagrams in De Musica need to be laid beside the chapter structure of Miss MacIntosh and the correspondence traced. That work is the next pass. What this chapter establishes is the framework: Boethius said all reality is musical, and Young built a novel that proves it, at 1,198 pages, by demonstrating all three categories of music in every register of the novel from the cosmic to the corporeal to the finally, beautifully, inaudible.
