Radical Immanence Across Marguerite Young's Writing Life

Chapter Three: Grief Doesn’t End

← Chapter Two: Reality Is Constructed · Radical Immanence Across Marguerite Young's Writing Life · Chapter Four: Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act →

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Esther Longtree · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

There is a particular cruelty in the way we narrate grief. We tell it as a journey with stages. You move through denial and anger and bargaining and depression and you arrive at acceptance, which is the destination, which is the place where grief becomes manageable, integrated, resolved into the texture of a life that has resumed its forward motion. The story of grief is the story of getting over it, or if not over it then through it, which amounts to the same thing: grief as temporary weather, difficult while it lasts, but bounded, finite, something that eventually passes.

Young does not believe this. More precisely: Young does not believe it because it is not true, and she is too honest to reproduce a comfort that falsifies the actual experience it claims to describe. In Young's system, grief is not temporary weather. It is the climate. It is the permanent atmospheric condition of a world in which everything you love is always already changing into something you didn't expect, in which the people you need most are dying or absent or constructed differently than you understood, in which the self that does the loving is itself plural and unstable and not fully available to itself.

Grief in Young is not the response to loss. It is the structure of attachment in a world where nothing holds still.

And it cannot stop. That is the crucial thing. The grief does not process. It does not move through stages toward resolution. It accumulates. Each new understanding of what has been lost adds to rather than replaces the grief that preceded it, so that everything grieved, is layered and present and unresolved.

This is not presented as pathology. Young is presenting grief as accurate. As the appropriate response to actual loss in an actual world where loss is permanent and cumulative and does not resolve. The alternative, the grief that resolves, requires at some level deciding that the lost thing was not so irreplaceable after all, that life without it is livable, that the self that loved it has found a way forward. Young finds something dishonest in that decision. The lost thing was irreplaceable. Life without it is not the same life.

Grief is the longest form of a pillar that runs through everything Young wrote. She said her first poem, written at five, was about loss. “Recurring” (1930) speaks to a grief that stays: “You have not forgotten, / You will not forget.” “Proem to Reality” (1945) gives the plainest statement in her work: “the wound of absence is forever ours.” Late in life she named her single subject as “paradise lost, always the lost cause, the lost leader, the lost utopia.”

The third pillar connects to the first in a specific way. If the self is plural, then grief is not a single experience moving through stages. It is multiple experiences happening simultaneously in different registers of the self, some of which may be moving toward something like acceptance while others are still in the rawness of fresh loss, and all of which are present at the same time. Young's permanent weather is more accurate to the phenomenology.

Grief as permanent weather has political implications that Young's work makes available without explicitly stating. A society that cannot grieve honestly cannot reckon honestly. The losses that have been built into the foundation of a country, the theft of indigenous land, the centuries of enslaved labor, the systematic exclusion of women and queer people and immigrants from the promises the country made about itself, these are not losses that have been processed. They have been managed, minimized, periodically acknowledged and then returned to the category of the past. Young's philosophy says: the grief does not resolve. A politics built on Young's third pillar would not ask for closure. It would build structures for honest ongoing acknowledgment, for the permanent carrying of what has been lost, not as punishment or paralysis but as the condition of collective integrity.

What grief gives, in Young's system, is depth. The self that has not grieved is thinner than the self that has, not morally thinner but experientially thinner, less capacious, less able to hold the full weight of what existence actually contains. Grief is the education that cannot be acquired any other way. The self that has learned to live in the permanent weather of grief is not a diminished self. It is a witnessed self, one that has stayed in the room with its own loss and not looked away and not concluded prematurely and not replaced the grief with a performance of recovery.

That is the third pillar. Grief is the climate. The honest life is the one that stops pretending it isn't.

Of all the pillars, this is the most constant, from the earliest poems, widened in the war years to vanishing species, given a whole town of mourners in "Angel in the Forest", and carried to the last words of "Harp Song for a Radical", where Debs’s elegiac sense continues “perhaps long afterward.” It is also something her critics have noticed: from the start, Young’s grief produces beauty and comedy as well as loss. She called the novel “an American existentialism” carried with “a certain exuberance,” and called herself “a comic writer.”

Phase One, Childhood and the Early Poems (1908 to 1934)

Pillar 3: Grief Doesn’t End

This is the strongest pillar of the phase. "Recurring" (1930) is grief that stays: "You have not forgotten, / You will not forget... They were never purchased, / They cannot be sold." In "Jonathan's House" (1929), the dead man "would never seem / Like one who died. He was more like the rain, / Recurring." "Mothers" tells a grown child that the mother "was younger than you are / When she died." "Door-Way" (1929) places death in the neighborhood, "like an old door-way / Half hidden on a little street of elms... Not far from where the rosy children play." "Little Fawn" is grief as the first lesson of life.

Complication: grief can also be held. The "Ballad-Loving Woman" (1930) "kept her heart together" with old songs, and "Her sorry tears were dried." The refrain contains the sorrow without ending it.

Phase Two, The Chicago Years, Prismatic Ground, and the Graduate Essays (1935 to 1937)

Pillar 3: Grief Doesn’t End

"Departing Song" ends on "whatever must go / And is not gone." In "Tree of Blood" the birds leaving the dying body are "Homeless as hail" and "will find no other / Branch flowering with lost azure." "Milton's Wife" (1937) shows a woman left in "a hollow house / Where no footfalls disturbed the dust, / A ghost suspicious of a moth." "Elegy for the Body" ends: "But she, the girl, / Does not emerge."

Complication: this phase also begins Young's turn of grief into gift. "Song's Preface" (1935): "lean into the lightning shaft of pain / By whose amazing will I do uplift / Such plumes, such flowers; I bear a heavy gift." In "The Visit" the speaker returns "From lost encounters... whole." "Lenten Feast" chooses to feast on "pigeon shadows" rather than fast.

Phase Three, The Philosophical Poems (1938 to 1945)

Pillar 3: Grief Doesn’t End

"Proem to Reality" gives the plainest statement in all of Young's work: "the wound of absence is forever ours." "The Dead Moon" has "moonlight [that] does not cease" and nights that are "a discrepancy / Between the event and its knowledge in me." The widowed raven of "The Raven" (1943) becomes "dour mourner of all moons deceased." "Leaves, Leaves" (1942): "And O, my darling, the spiritual leaves must fall." In "The Process," even if time ran backward, "Yet would we persist in elegy."

The war years widen grief to extinction. "Death by Rarity" (1940) fears for "all marvelous / Names of birds whose names are poems." "The Long War" (1940) lists the scarlet ibis and the whooping crane as "casualties which must be listed." "The Angels" (1942) seeks the angels' winter grounds where "The beautiful birds are not destroyed yet," and ends: "death is the flight of angel and golden plover."

Complication: "The Home-Coming" (1942) arrives whole: "We have come home and brought us here entirely."

Phase Four, "Angel in the Forest" and “Complex Life and Complex Letters” (1945 to 1946)

Pillar 3: Grief Doesn’t End

The book's central chapter is "Paradise Was Lost." The Rappites are "suicidal as lemmings." Robert Dale Owen finds that "The dead, however, were insistent," "impossible to reason into nothingness." A widower in 1940 says of his wife, "She is not anywhere."

Complication: the book ends on a joke. The doctor is out shooting birds when the baby comes, and the undertaker is "out bringing lambs into the world" when someone dies. Grief persists, and so does comedy.

Phase Five, "Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" (written from the mid 1940s, published 1965)

Pillar 3: Grief Doesn’t End

The novel is Duncan's "epic elegy for all creation." Strehle shows Miss MacIntosh teaching Vera that death is not a culmination but "an ongoing, echoing loss." Mr. Spitzer has written no audible music since his twin's suicide. Esther's murdered child returns at eight asking, "mother, why did you kill me." Cousin Hannah hides forty unworn trousseaus. Miss MacIntosh loses "her hair, her love, her brother, her identity." Shaviro: "the chord that would complete our harmonies is always already lost," with "no final cadence."

Complication: Friederich and Shaviro find loss to be the source of the book's splendor, "the necessary condition of beauty and exuberance." Young called the book "an American existentialism" carried with "a certain exuberance," and called herself "a comic writer."

What the novel adds is a whole population of mourners. In the poems grief belonged to a speaker or a single figure, and in "Angel in the Forest" to a town. Here nearly every figure carries a loss that does not end, and the critics who find loss to be the source of the book’s splendor are describing something new in Young’s work: grief and exuberance in the same sentences.

Phase Six, The Occasional Prose, Interviews, and Self-Interpretation (1940s to 1995)

Pillar 3: Grief Doesn’t End

She named her single subject: "paradise lost, always the lost cause, the lost leader, the lost utopia." "The Artist as Wanderer" names a century of "so many people displaced, so many flights, exoduses, barriers, walls, concentration camps." "The World of Silence" traces the deaf thrown into pits in Sparta, into the Tiber, and "extinguished" in Nazi Germany. In her writing on James Whitcomb Riley, his old sweetheart turns out to be an endless search rather than a woman.

Complication: "out of the flaw may come the universe."

Phase Seven, "Harp Song for a Radical" (written in her last decades, published 1999)

Pillar 3: Grief Doesn’t End

Debs visits "the graves of dead children and poets and socialists, he being that kind of permanently elegiac fellow." He is the "elegiast" of the railroad dead. His life "would be spent in memory of early and late martyrs." The main text ends: "It would continue for as long as he lived and perhaps long afterward. As will be seen."

Complication: the magazine refuses "universal elegy": "Our memories are not catacombs." Grief must not stop the work.
