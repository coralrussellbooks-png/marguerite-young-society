Lesson 3: Grief Doesn't End

By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Pillar: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End · Across every book

Act I: The Fractured Interior

Grief is not an obstacle course with a finish line. It is a permanent shift in atmospheric pressure.

Somewhere along the way, grief got a schedule. Five stages, a year of firsts, and then acceptance, the tidy exit sign above the door. People still quietly count the months for each other. When the sorrow outlasts the calendar, the grieving person starts to wonder what is wrong with them. This lesson is for everyone who has wondered that.

The Framework

The five stages came from Elisabeth Kübler-Ross's On Death and Dying (1969), and they described dying patients, not the bereaved. Kübler-Ross later said plainly that the stages were never meant as a straight line. The culture kept the ladder anyway, because ladders promise an end.

Marguerite Young offers no ladder. Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is built around a disappearance. Vera Cartwheel loses the nursemaid who raised her, a woman who walked into the sea, and spends the entire enormous novel traveling toward that absence. The loss is not solved by the journey. It becomes the journey. It shapes every bus ride, every stranger, and every sentence.

C.S. Lewis kept a notebook after his wife Joy died, published as A Grief Observed (1961). He wrote that "Grief is like a long valley, a winding valley where any bend may reveal a totally new landscape." A valley is not a hallway. There is no far door. There is only terrain, and the terrain keeps changing.

Radical Immanence pushes the image one step further. Loss is geological. Each grief settles as a layer, and the layers stack. A new death disturbs the old ones. A smell in a hardware store can crack a stratum laid down thirty years ago. Grief does not leave. It becomes part of the ground and part of the air.

The counselor Lois Tonkin described a model that many grieving people find kinder than the stages. Grief does not shrink over time, she suggested. Life slowly grows larger around it. Researchers on continuing bonds have made a related case: healthy mourners often keep a living relationship with the dead rather than letting go. Neither idea asks anyone to get over anything. Both describe learning to live in a changed climate.

This matters because the pressure to finish grieving is its own wound. Toxic positivity tells a person that their ongoing sorrow is a failure of effort. Permanent weather tells the truth: some skies stay grey, and a person can still learn to walk, work, and love under them.

The Art Companion

Käthe Kollwitz lost her son Peter in the first weeks of the First World War. She spent nearly two decades making The Grieving Parents, two kneeling granite figures now placed at the Vladslo German war cemetery in Belgium. The father holds himself tight. The mother bends as if under weight. Neither will ever stand up. Kollwitz did not sculpt recovery. She sculpted endurance.

For music, listen to Henryk Górecki's Symphony No. 3, the Symphony of Sorrowful Songs (1976). It moves slowly, repeats itself, and refuses to hurry toward resolution. It sounds the way long grief feels from the inside.

Reflection Questions

  • Which loss still changes the air pressure in certain rooms, seasons, or songs?

  • Who told me, in words or in silence, that I should be over it by now?

  • What does my grief know that the rest of me has not yet learned?

  • In what ways has my life grown around the loss rather than past it?

The Exercise: A Grief Weather Log

  1. For fourteen days, write one line each evening describing the day's grief in weather terms: fog, drizzle, clear with a cold front, heavy pressure, sudden storm.

  2. Beside each line, note anything that shifted the weather, such as a date, a place, a smell, or a conversation.

  3. On the fifteenth day, draw a simple cross-section of the ground, like a geology diagram. Label each layer with a loss, oldest at the bottom. Mark which layers pressed upward during the two weeks.

  4. Write a short forecast. It should not promise clear skies. It should name what to carry on heavy days: a person to call, a walk, a meal, a piece of music.
