Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 7

← Chapter 6 · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 8 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Homer Capehorn · Madge Capehorn · Moses Hunnecker · Musidora Tidings

Pillars: Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The Questions

Homer and Madge tell Josh’s death two different ways. Is one of them lying, or are both building?

If “transition seemed the only permanence,” what does that do to any fixed account of what happened?

Madge says some things are known “better through never having been there.” What kind of truth is that?

What do the poor, the mad, and the exploited on this road see about the built world that the comfortable do not?

The chapter opens on a line that is nearly the system in miniature: “Transition seemed the only permanence.” Nothing here is final; the one thing that lasts is that nothing lasts. This is Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary stated as a condition of the world rather than a counsel. If change is the only permanence, then every fixed account, every sealed version of what is real, is already false to the flux it freezes.

That is why Homer and Madge can tell Josh’s death two entirely different ways and neither is simply lying. Madge builds a story of murder and conspiracy; Homer builds one of accident and no blame. These are not a true report and a false one but two constructions, each held in place by the teller’s need. Madge even names the method: there are things “you know better through never having been there.” Her truth is built from the inside, out of conviction, not dug up as a fact. The chapter does not arbitrate, because arbitrating would seal one version and call it the earth.

Language does the building. The conflicting accounts, the rumors, the stories the road tells about its dead, are worlds spoken into place, and Moses doubting whether he sees with his eyes or hears with his ears is the same recognition from the other side: the reported world is made, and the making is unstable.

And the chapter sees all this from the edge. Its truth-making happens among the poor, the mad, the violent, and the exploited, Musidora’s family, the relief worker, the people nobody grieves. The cruelty here is structural, built into who is fed and who is abandoned, and it shows most clearly to those it grinds. The book keeps handing its sharpest sight to the people the settled center has written off, because the edge is where the construction of the ordinary world stops being invisible.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that wants it to settle what really happened to Josh, to pick the true account and discard the false. To do that is to seal one construction and call it fact, which the chapter’s own governing line forbids. It refuses, too, the comfort of treating the road’s cruelty as bad luck rather than structure.

The footing is that transition is the only permanence, so the honest stance toward any account is to hold it open, knowing it is built and revisable. Truth here is perspectival and constructed, which does not make it worthless but makes it unfinishable, and the clearest view of the built world comes from its margins. No single sealed version is the earth, and the cruelty is structural. The death is never resolved, and the chapter leaves it, rightly, unresolved.

For Reflection

When two people tell the same event incompatibly, what is gained by refusing to pick a winner?

If transition is the only permanence, what fixed account of one’s own past is quietly failing the flux it froze?

Whose sight has one discounted because they stand at the edge?
