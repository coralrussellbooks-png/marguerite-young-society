Guilt, Innocence, and Justice

Part of To All My Darlings, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).

Definition from research

Chapter 12 · Freedom, Innocence, and Joy

  • There is a moment of profound joy and freedom as Vera walks out into the night, celebrating the beauty and unity of nature.
  • This freedom is described as innocence and clarity of vision, untainted by past sorrow or fear.
  • Vera feels connected to the natural world, sensing stars, flowers, and animals as real and meaningful.

Chapter 14 · Law and Order versus Chaos

  • Mr. Spitzer embodies legal order but is portrayed as overwhelmed by the disorder of life and his own ghosts.
  • Catherine’s world is chaotic, filled with shifting images, ghosts, and memories that resist neat categorization.

Chapter 26 · The Law and Its Limits

  • The law is portrayed as an imperfect, sometimes arbitrary system that tries to impose order on the chaos of life and death. Mr. Spitzer hopes for a future beyond law, where harmony replaces discord.

Chapter 48

  • “he was not the practitioner of Blackstone”: A practitioner of Blackstone is a lawyer. Sir William Blackstone was an English jurist, judge and Tory politician of the eighteenth century, most noted for his Commentaries on the Laws of England (1765-1769), an influential treatise on the common law in four volumes covering the rights of persons, the rights of things, private wrongs and public wrongs. The Commentaries were the leading work on English law and shaped the American legal system. Blackstone’s ratio is his statement that “it is better that ten guilty persons escape, than that one innocent suffer” (Commentaries, Book 4, chapter 27). Benjamin Franklin raised it to “a hundred guilty Persons” (letter to Benjamin Vaughan, March 14, 1785), and John Adams, who had studied Blackstone, argued that “it’s of more importance to community, that innocence should be protected, than it is, that guilt should be punished; for guilt and crimes are so frequent in the world, that all of them cannot be punished,” and that if innocence itself is condemned, “virtue itself, is no security” (closing argument, Boston Massacre trials, December 1770). Blackstone appears again in Chapter 55, where Mr. Spitzer might “never have practiced law, never have consulted Blackstone.”

Chapter 49

  • “a minority representing a majority, a hidden majority like those sleeping in the churchyard”: Mr. Spitzer is the self-appointed advocate of unpopular and overlooked causes. He speaks for those society does not openly support or acknowledge, who may be a great silent majority, the marginalized, ignored, and forgotten, like the dead in the churchyard who lie without public recognition. No one chose or asked him; he took the role from conviction. Later in the chapter he would present his indictment to the Senate for the passenger pigeons and the Indians.
  • “How precious then was life”: Life is a brief, precious moment, and much that sustains it, the dreamer and the dream, may be “illegal” in the grand scheme of things. Political authority, legal systems, and the great powers oppress, limit, and destroy individual lives, as they destroyed the pigeons. The passage holds both the preciousness of life in its fragility and the dreamlike quality of existence itself.
  • “would have been threatened with the Federal pen”: The Federal pen is the federal penitentiary. For “giving a voice to all those beauties which never should have been expressed,” the old pigeon lobbyist would be accused of anarchy and sedition. In 2013 the programmer and activist Aaron Swartz, facing federal charges that carried up to thirty-five years in prison for downloading academic articles, died by suicide. The “beauties” are the elusive, perhaps forbidden, aspects of life and art, hidden truths and deep emotions beyond ordinary perception or social acceptance; to expose them would bring punishment from legal or governmental authority, the power of law enforcement or censorship to suppress such revelations.

Chapter 51

  • “Was life a legal fiction”: A legal fiction is something the law assumes to be true for its purposes though it may not be literally true. Mr. Spitzer asks whether life itself is such a fiction, taken on faith but unprovable either way. The imaginary house with its ivory porches no waves should ever reach is a fragile, beautiful, perhaps illusory construct, complete-seeming but isolated and fading, “its lights already lost.” What seems real and substantial may be a delicate illusion that cannot withstand change and mortality.
  • “trying to practice law where there was no law and no lawyer”: The rule of law holds that everyone, the government included, is accountable to laws that are publicly known, equally enforced, and independently judged: “no one is above the law.” Mr. Spitzer practices where there is no law at all.

Chapter 68

  • “if there was no corpus delicti”: Corpus delicti, “the body of the crime,” means the proof that a crime was committed, not the corpse itself, though without a body courts were long unwilling to find a murder.

Chapter 68 · Human Imperfection and Guilt

  • Characters are deeply flawed and haunted by guilt, failures, and secrets (e.g., Mr. Weed’s reflections on the imperfect hanging, criminals who act unknowingly, and the failure to prevent tragedies).
  • Mr. Weed discusses the subtlety of crime and how even well-planned acts can betray the perpetrator by small, unnoticed details.
  • The narrative suggests a tension between the desire for justice and the complexity of human nature.

Chapter 74

  • “as if she had resisted”: She was “sleeping in the grass” and “unresponsive except to memory”; consent cannot be given asleep or unconscious.

Chapter 74 · Societal Judgment and Isolation

  • Esther fears the court trial, anticipating gossip, blame, and misunderstanding from the community.
  • She is alienated and misunderstood, with the jury and townspeople serving as a metaphor for harsh societal judgment.

Chapter 75

  • “had not borne witness against herself”: Esther had resisted and not “...borne witness against herself, for it would have been illegal to do so, and her own father was the policeman.” This may refer to the Fifth Amendment in the United States where, Pleading the Fifth, is often used to invoke the self-incrimination clause when witnesses decline to answer questions where the answers might incriminate them. The amendment (1791) says no one “shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself.”
  • “even if there were no corpses derelicti”: Esther’s “corpses derelicti” mangles corpus delicti, “the body of the crime,” the legal rule that a crime must be shown to have happened before anyone can be convicted of it; she mixes in corpses and derelicts. The phrase is often misread to mean that there can be no murder conviction without a body.

Chapter 75 · Justice and Injustice

  • Esther faces suspicion and possible accusation of murder regarding her children.
  • The detective who raped her threatens her, accusing her of killing his children, adding a layer of societal judgment and legal peril.
  • Her father being a policeman adds complexity to her relationship with authority and justice.

Chapter 76

  • “the hangman’s noose or the electric chair”: The electric chair was first used at Auburn Prison, New York, on William Kemmler in 1890; the execution took two jolts and eight minutes.

Chapter 76 · Memory and Guilt

  • Esther is haunted by memories of past events, including her twin’s death in utero, her own repeated losses, and her interactions with men.
  • These memories induce a sense of guilt, self-accusation, and uncertainty about truth and justice.

Chapter 76 · Justice and Investigation

  • The detective’s role symbolizes the pursuit of truth and justice, but also the intrusion and exposure of private pain.
  • His tireless search uncovers “quiet murders” that society ignores, reflecting on the nature of crime and detection beyond the obvious.

Chapter 77

  • “If the jurymen came to her bedroom”: Young reminds us that the jury will consist of men. Mississippi, in 1968, was the last state to let women serve on juries, and in Taylor v. Louisiana (1975) the Supreme Court ruled that jury pools excluding women violated the Sixth Amendment.

Chapter 77 · Guilt and Innocence

  • Esther feels guilt for the “murder” of her children, though this guilt is ambiguous: is she guilty or a victim of fate or society?
  • The detective’s pursuit symbolizes societal judgment and the invasive quest for truth. Esther fears exposure but also wishes for some form of public trial to assert control over her narrative.

Chapter 77 · Racism and Social Injustice

  • The white Chicago detective is interested only in the deaths of white children, ignoring black children’s deaths, revealing racial prejudice and societal neglect.

Chapter 78

  • “her having killed somebody without shedding of blood”: “And almost all things are by the law purged with blood; and without shedding of blood is no remission” (Hebrews 9:22, KJV).

Chapter 78 · Guilt and Innocence

  • Esther is burdened with a guilt she cannot fully understand or escape: accused of infanticide before she was even born. This theme explores the complexity of innocence and culpability.

Chapter 79

  • “The bleeding hand of a dead child, lifting, might testify against her”: By the old ordeal of the bier, a murdered body was believed to bleed afresh when its murderer came near. In Richard III, Henry VI’s corpse bleeds before Gloucester: “Dead Henry’s wounds / Open their congeal’d mouths and bleed afresh!” (1.2).

Chapter 80 · Guilt and Redemption

  • Esther harbors deep guilt over her first-born son, Walter, whom she believes she "killed" by abandoning him at birth.
  • Despite her guilt, Walter is alive in her memories and visions, accusing her of her sins and questioning her about his father.
  • Her attempts to redeem herself manifest in her ongoing pregnancies and her hope that the new child will compensate for the past losses.

Chapter 81 · Innocence and Experience

  • The toy salesman’s childlike innocence contrasts with the harsh realities he has survived.
  • His survival against all odds introduces questions about fate, guilt, and innocence lost.

Chapter 81 · Survival and Guilt

  • The salesman is tormented by surviving a disaster where others perished.
  • Esther calls him a "murderer" for not drowning with the others, highlighting survivor's guilt and moral ambiguity.
