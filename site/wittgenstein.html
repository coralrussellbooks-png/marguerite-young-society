Definition from research

Chapter 6

  • In metaphysics the philosophy of identity is the relation each thing bears only to itself. The notion of identity gives rise to many philosophical problems, including the identity of indiscernibles (if x and y share all their properties, are they one and the same thing?), and questions about change and personal identity over time (what has to be the case for a person x at one time and a person y at a later time to be one and the same person?). The philosophical concept concerns a relation, specifically, a relation that x and y stand in if, and only if they are one and the same thing, or identical to each other (i.e. if, and only if x = y).The law of identity originates from classical antiquity. The modern formulation of identity is that of Gottfried Leibniz, who held that x is the same as y if and only if every predicate true of x is true of y as well.Ludwig Wittgenstein wrote (Tractatus 5.5301): "That identity is not a relation between objects is obvious." At 5.5303 he elaborates: "Roughly speaking: to say of two things that they are identical is nonsense, and to say of one thing that it is identical with itself is to say nothing."

Chapter 37

  • Life as a state of experience and potential - Socrates thought death might be a dreamless sleep or passage to another realm where one can converse with the dead. Existentialism believes death is an inevitable aspect of human existence and that it can lead to a heightened sense of self-awareness and responsibility. Epicureanism argues death cannot harm a person, as it’s either not yet experienced (while alive) or not experienced at all (once dead). Death is defined as the loss of the capacity to engage in vital activities and the cessation of consciousness. Wittgenstein said that death is not an experience in life. Schopenhauer believed that understanding death as the ultimate end of life can provide a perspective on the meaning of life itself. Freud thought the life instinct (Eros) and the death instinct (Thanatos) are fundamental forces in human behavior. The chapter depicts life as a state of ongoing experience and potential characterized by uncertainty, illusion, and transformation. It is not a fixed reality but a mutable and often paradoxical condition where life and death, truth and illusion, identity and multiplicity coexist and where human understanding is limited and provisional.

Chapter 39

  • an old "idiot who had become a great philosopher, a philosopher who had become a great idiot," - Wittgenstein, despite his genius, was known for his unconventional behavior and challenging personality. He even questioned his own sanity, asking Bertrand Russell if he was an idiot. Russell asked for proof and after reading his philosophical piece declared he was not an idiot. His profound insights into philosophy, language, and logic led to his recognition as a major philosopher. The idea of a philosopher becoming an “idiot” is a common trope often referencing Diogenes of Sinope. Arthur Schopenhauer was also considered eccentric.

Chapter 47

  • Another of philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein contributions was silence. “Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent.” Language has logical limits, and when we try to use it to express things that are beyond these limits (like metaphysical or ethical truths), we should be silent. Silence for him was a space for potential meaning and understanding, a recognition of the limits of language, and a way of acknowledging what lies beyond the grasp of words. Wittgenstein's statement in this chapter is a thematic anchor underscoring the novel's exploration of the ineffable aspects of human existence. It implies a humility before the mysteries that lie beyond speech and reason, advocating silence as a form of respect or acknowledgment when words fail. This emphasis on silence and the limits of language permeates the novel’s meditations on death, reality, and the spiritual unknown.
