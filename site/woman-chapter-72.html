Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 72

← Chapter 71 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 73 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Esther Longtree · Miss MacIntosh

Esther Longtree arrives in this chapter, and the interrupted birth she carries is the book's oldest shape given at last its fullest body. She is "everlastingly pregnant," swelling toward deliveries that end in stillbirth and miscarriage and, at the extreme, "the birth of nothing," and the pregnancy that will not complete, which the old doctor and the wild mares and the empty country all carried before her, is here made flesh in a waitress at the "Greasy Spoon." The hearse and the old grey mule halted outside, the mural of birds with human faces, set the boundary between life and death open before she speaks, a diner where the funeral procession stops and the dead and the living take their coffee together.

Her body is the book's plainest catalog of coming-apart, the swelling that never resolves, the bald spot spreading on her head, the loose hair, the heart "leaking like the sky," and Esther's flesh rejects the ideal the way Miss MacIntosh's bald head rejects it, the body as the place the self comes apart worn openly and even with humor. She is lonely, without romantic love, the sterile "fathers" absent or indifferent, and she knits garments "without needles or thread" for children who will not arrive, the maternal creativity and the maternal loss carried in the same empty hands.

Her memory reaches back to wild mares "pregnant without males," bearing "waters and winds," and the mares are the interrupted birth widened into a female fecundity that owes nothing to men, generation independent of the fathers who fail her, a mystical creation set against the sterile salesmen and the disbelieving town. Esther's sustained pregnancy despite every disbelief is the book insisting on a woman's experience the world refuses to see, invisibility and erasure met by a body that will not stop being pregnant no matter who denies it.

At the chapter's end Esther nurses her own clenched fist beside the lost children and asks "Who killed cock robin?", answering that "all people killed him," and the answer is the diffusion of agency the book stated at the start, a death with no single author, the same logic that would not say who killed Miss MacIntosh now spread across every loss Esther bears. Her "Never, never" is her refusal of the guilt the world would pin on her alone, a woman declining to be the single hand behind a violence the whole world commits, and the chapter leaves her pregnant and luminous and unaccounted for, the fullest figure the book has yet made of the birth that will not come.
