Movement IV. Grief That Stays: The Weather That Does Not Lift

← Movement III. The Ones Who Do Not Fit: The Margins Reveal th · Exploring Young's Poetry · Movement V. The Many Selves: The Self That Will Not Stay One →

Characters: Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer

Grief in these poems is not an illness to recover from. It is a climate the living go on living inside, and it was there from the beginning. Two of the earliest poems are already pure elegy: "Little Fawn," told that its mother's "tracks are lost in the snow," and "Mothers," which forbids a child the comfort of recovering a parent dead too young. The grief never lets up and never tidies. "The Visit" states the whole weather in a breath, "Here grief is delicate and light, / Falling like leaves on sightless sight," while "Elegy for the Body" and "The Funeral" stay in the room with a death and refuse to hurry it toward a lesson. The Middle Years add the ashen "Phoenix," a resurrection bird that will not rise. A second group treats every parting as a voyage out with no promised arrival, from the early "Door-Way," where death is an ordinary threshold near where children play, to "The Departure" and "Farewell at the Station." None of these poems performs the tidy healing that grief is usually asked to perform. Their refusal is the point. To keep the loss unresolved, to let it fall and go on falling, is the truthful thing, and truth is worth more here than comfort.

"Little Fawn"

"Can't you stay by yourself, little fawn? ... You must not always be looking for / Your mother's tracks in the snow," and then the hard line, "Her tracks are lost in the snow." The mother is gone and will not be found, and the poem offers the fawn no comfort, only the fact. Grief Doesn't End is already here in its plainest form, a loss the poem refuses to soften.

"Mothers"

"Child with the withered mouth ... Dream not that now / By fierce remembrance you should recover / The light sweet frame of her ... She was younger than you are / When she died." A child cannot recover a mother lost too young, and the poem forbids the consolation of memory doing that work. It is the same refusal "Two Angels" makes years later, made early and made bare.

"Jonathan's House"

A couple planned a dream house with a hidden escape panel; Jonathan died; she married Thomas, who built a stone version, but "No panel gave. The wide, unyielding stone was there." Grief is the discovery that the lost thing cannot be rebuilt, that the second house is a lifeless replica of the first. The dead dream will not be reoccupied, and the poem sits with that stone fact, the failed replica edging it toward the constructed unreal of Movement II.

"Song of Moving Away"

Moving house, the speaker takes "These old, old days and my old friends / And all that I cannot forsake," and "Old sunlight and old rain." The past is not left at the old address; it is carried, unforsaken, into the new street. The grief of leaving is answered by refusing to leave the past behind, which is also The Past Doesn't Go Away in its gentlest, earliest form.

"Door-Way"

"I think that Death is like an old door-way / Half hidden on a little street of elms ... Not far from where the rosy children play, / Yet quiet with a peace that overwhelms." Death is domesticated here, an ordinary threshold in a familiar neighborhood rather than a cosmic gate. The passage out is set down among the elms and the playing children, grief made neighborly.

"Lover."

The poem carries "the burdens of existence" and the pull between spirit and flesh toward an enduring essence beyond physical decay. Its grief does not resolve into consolation; it holds the weight and the decay together. Grief Doesn't End means refusing to trade the body's loss for a tidy promise about the soul.

"Elegy for the Body."

It asks what "bird or flower" could revive a heart that has stopped, and lets the question stand unanswered. The poem wants resurrection and knows better than to grant itself one. That withheld comfort is the honesty the movement is built on.

"The Tree."

The dead go on shading and accompanying the living, their thoughts persisting like branches over the path. Grief here is not recovery but ongoing company, the lost kept present rather than released. The weather does not lift; it keeps the shape of who is gone.

"Death in Lilliput."

A fragile, dreamlike world of fireflies and "under-meadows" shrinks death to a small, flickering scale. The poem grieves at miniature size, which ties it to the diminished heaven of Movement I even as it stays elegy. Loss is no less total for being tiny.

"Heart Who Stays."

The "dark Omnivorous and eyeless Head" swallows everything and sees nothing, death as a blind appetite the heart must go on facing. The poem stares at the thing that takes the dead and refuses to look away. To stay with that Head is Grief Doesn't End at its hardest.

"The Visit."

"Here grief is delicate and light, / Falling like leaves on sightless sight," sorrow so pervasive it becomes the air. The poem states the movement's whole climate in two lines. It does not push toward healing; it lets the leaves keep falling.

"Two Angels."

The poem admits it "cannot collect the melody" of the "rose-soft lady," that memory fails to hold the one it loved. Grief here is the failure of recall itself, the beloved slipping past every attempt to keep her. What cannot be reassembled is mourned as it dissolves.

"Recurring."

Sorrows are folded "in a pretty box" and locked under "two quaint locks," grief made private and guarded from "rats" and "moths." The poem shows mourning as something kept rather than cured. Grief Doesn't End includes the instinct to protect the wound from tidy exposure.

"Tree of Blood."

The body is a tree losing its vitality, a "light bloom" flaring under "a shrunken sun." The poem watches life ebb without softening the decline. Its grief is for the ordinary fact of a body giving out.

"Death by Rarity."

The nighthawk, the roseate spoonbill, the trumpeter swan: rarity itself becomes the omen of extinction, beauty vanishing because there is too little of it. The poem grieves the disappearing at the level of a species. In the novel Mr. Spitzer's mourning for Martha, the last passenger pigeon, is the same elegy for the last of a kind.

"White Lethe."

A robin redbreast dies "with no savior," dissolving like stars in rain, abandonment without rescue. Lethe, the river of forgetting, names a grief that ends in erasure rather than peace. The poem refuses even the comfort of being remembered.

"Moderate Fable."

The poem opens in uncertainty and disbelief in love, a spiritual desolation with no resolution offered. Its grief is quiet and total, the flat weather after feeling fails. The title, a modest and unfinished story, also belongs to the open construction of Movement II, and that doubleness is worth keeping in view rather than settling.

"The Funeral."

A coming "cold rain" and whitening willow leaves mark an approaching end, the natural world dressing for mourning. The poem stays with the ritual and the decay without hurrying past them. Grief is given its full slow weather.

"The Pattern."

A "still-born heir" and generations growing "colder than its star" make loss into an inherited condition, mortality handed down. The poem grieves and also looks toward the Crossing Point, the cold passed from one generation to the next. In the novel this is Esther Longtree's repeated stillbirths, the mental sorrow of all ending made flesh.

"The Whales."

A southern whale turns toward home because "a memory maketh the whale to turn," the vast creature carrying grief and longing across the sea. The poem holds loss and the pull of homecoming together, and it also touches the Crossing Point, since a hunted species is what a people spends. Its mourning reaches past the single death toward the vanishing kind.

"Leaves, Leaves."

The speaker stands "in the long avenue of the arid vistas," a barren place "without clock," time and guidance both stripped away. Grief here is desolation, an emptiness beyond even the measures that usually hold a life. The falling leaves of the title are the only motion left.

"The Raven."

The raven takes "a single wife for life," an "imperishable" bond marked all the while by time's passage. The poem holds enduring love and inevitable loss in one image, devotion that outlasts and still grieves. What is faithful is also what will be bereaved.

"Drowning Song."

The drowning is a surrender to a vast, enveloping water as familiar as "father's clover meadows," death met as inheritance rather than terror. The poem illustrates going under a return to lineage and origin. Its grief is calm, which is its own kind of honesty.

"The Departure."

"Glow worms" and "seraphic darkness" light the border between life and death, a leaving that is luminous and dark at once. The poem treats departure as passage, neither denied nor consoled. It stands at the threshold and names it.

"Departing Song."

A love "faint," "tremulous," "softly blown to the ground" is also the "brightest" and "saddest" the speaker holds. The poem lets tenderness and loss occupy the same breath. Grief Doesn't End keeps the brightness inside the sorrow instead of choosing between them.

"The Journey."

A "glass-walled chamber wheeling / under the low sky's snow ceiling" carries the traveler through a cold, enclosed Christmas Eve. The passage is fragile and solemn, life moving toward an unnamed end. The poem grieves the journey even as it takes it.

"Farewell at the Station."

"Every farewell" is a departure of a voyager, the Pullman "death and voyage simultaneously." The poem makes each ordinary parting rehearse the final one. To say goodbye at all is to practice the kind of leaving that has no return ticket.

"Phoenix"

The bird of resurrection is shown "ashen," "crying like the lost sheep's crying," its "wings folded in monstrous quiet," a phoenix that does not rise. The poem takes the one myth that guarantees return and refuses it, leaving the bird dead. Grief Doesn't End works by declining exactly the consolation the phoenix is supposed to provide.

"Love Mourned"

A fractured elegy for love: "Tower of mourning, my lost bloom," lovers who "kiss souls" and "breathe in other ways, / together," a mourning that dissolves the line between the two even as it grieves. Grief Doesn't End here is inseparable from the merging of selves, the loss and the union spoken in one broken voice, which is why it also touches Not One Self.

"Death Bed of Beauty"

"The early death of Beauty," whose lover then "has taken her sister to sleep," so that "The children of that old sister are Beauty's, her children, / Her unburied eyes." Beauty dies and continues sidelong, through the substitute and her children, grief and persistence folded together. The dead keep a foothold in the living, as they do in "The Tree."

"The Dead Moon"

"The dead moon whose moonlight does not cease," the dead persisting as light, and at the end "all my nights were a discrepancy / Between the event and its knowledge in me." The lost go on shining like a moon already dead, present and gone at once. Grief here is the gap between what happened and what the mind can hold of it, mourning that never closes.
