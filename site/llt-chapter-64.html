Chapter 64

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Pillars: Grief Doesn't End · The Sacred Is Here

The Questions

Solomon the tortoise is put outdoors and Vera is told “you will never miss him.” What does the small removal teach her that she is told she will not feel?

His absence is “the absence of something which, though very precious, I had never appreciated before.” Why does the ordinary creature become precious only in going?

Miss MacIntosh arranges the drawer with “undeviating exactitude.” What is the exactitude holding at bay?

Is a fifty-year-old tortoise under the bed too small a thing to grieve?

The chapter turns on a small removal and the grief it uncovers. Solomon, the ancient tortoise who has “lived under my bed,” is put out of doors, and Miss MacIntosh tells Vera “you will never miss him.” But Vera does, at once. His absence is “the absence of something which, though very precious, I had never appreciated before,” and the bedroom fills “with emptiness” because of it. This is Grief Doesn't End arriving through the smallest possible door, a lethargic tortoise who “never did very much,” and it arrives exactly against the instruction not to feel it. The chapter sets a flat denial of loss, you will never miss him, against the immediate fact of missing, and lets the fact win.

The tortoise is The Sacred Is Here in its humblest form, and the chapter’s point is that its value shows only in its going. Solomon is nothing grand, an ordinary creature who “push his head in and out of a hard shell,” underfoot and unremarked, and Vera never appreciated him until the space he filled was empty. This is how The Sacred Is Here usually works and usually fails: the meaning is genuinely there, underfoot, in the creature and the day, and it goes unnoticed precisely because it is ordinary, becoming visible only when removed. The grief teaches the value the presence never managed to.

Miss MacIntosh’s exactitude stands over the scene as the blueprint’s defense. She keeps the dresser drawer in “undeviating exactitude,” she disposes of the tortoise with brisk certainty, she settles the small loss with a flat prediction that it will not be felt. The exactitude is what the sealed self does with loss: manage it, tidy it, predict it away, keep the drawer perfect so the emptiness has nowhere to show. Against this Vera’s unmanaged missing of Solomon is the small crack the blueprint cannot close, the ordinary grief that will not obey the instruction to feel nothing.

And the chapter insists the small thing is not too small. A fifty-year-old tortoise under a bed is precisely the scale at which The Sacred Is Here lives, and precisely the scale the sealed, exact, managing mind dismisses. The book has set its largest griefs, for the dead brother, for the lost love in the snow, at the center of whole chapters; here it sets a tortoise, and treats Vera’s missing of him as the same truth in miniature. Loss is loss at every scale, the ordinary is sacred at every scale, and the instruction that one will never miss the small precious thing is exactly the seal the book distrusts, refused here by a child who misses her tortoise.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Solomon’s removal for too trivial a thing to carry weight, and the reading that takes Miss MacIntosh’s brisk exactitude for sensible management rather than a sealing against loss. Neither the triviality nor the good management is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that grief arrives through the smallest door, an ordinary tortoise, exactly against the instruction not to feel it, and the fact of missing wins over the denial; that The Sacred Is Here shows its value only in going, the humble creature precious once removed, the meaning that presence never announced; and that Miss MacIntosh’s undeviating exactitude is the sealed self managing loss into tidiness, the crack Vera’s unmanaged grief opens anyway. Loss is loss at every scale, and the ordinary is sacred at every scale. Vera misses her tortoise against the instruction, and the chapter lets the small grief stand as the large one’s equal.

For Reflection

What ordinary, underfoot thing has shown its value only once it was gone?

Is there a small loss one was told, or told oneself, one would never feel, and felt anyway?

What does an insistence on exactitude and tidiness keep from showing?
