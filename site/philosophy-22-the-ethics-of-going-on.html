Chapter 22. The Ethics of Going On

← Previous · Philosophy of MMMD Home · Next →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · The Stone Deaf Man · Vera Cartwheel

Symbols: Twins and Doubles

Philosophy: Nietzsche

Allusions: Deafness and Sign Language

Chapters discussed: 1, 32, 54, 59, 63, 72

Definition from research

The sentence of Young’s that this book has returned to most often is an ethical one. “When you have examined all the illusions of life and know that there isn’t any reality, but you nevertheless go on, then you are a mature human being.” The important word is “nevertheless.” Young’s system does not end in despair or in a triumphant affirmation. It ends in continuation.

Against Two Easy Endings

There are two easy ways out of the collapse of every system, and the novel refuses both.

The first is despair. If nothing is certain, nothing matters. Mr. Spitzer comes close to this. He feels at times that “there was no reality, and there was nothingness” (ch. 54). But he does not stop. He keeps walking the city, keeps searching trunks, keeps writing music no one will hear. The nothingness does not become a reason to stop.

The second easy ending is heroic affirmation, and it belongs to Nietzsche. Nietzsche’s answer to the collapse of all values was the thought of eternal recurrence: the demand that a person live so as to will every moment to return forever. The novel takes this thought seriously. Mr. Spitzer proposes it in the forty-second chapter. But the novel’s experience undoes it. The billiard balls “would not roll twice the same” (ch. 59). Esther lives recurrence as endless loss, beginning “it all over again, the great enterprise of her great loneliness” (ch. 72). Vera sees in her own life only “the dream of chaos repeating chaos” (ch. 1). The eternal return, lived, is not glorious. It is either impossible or unbearable.

What Young offers instead is quieter. If no moment can ever exactly repeat, then each moment is unrepeatable. Going on does not require willing the return of everything. It requires only willing the next step, over the abyss that has always been the floor.

The Virtues That Survive

The trials of Part Two each left something behind when their systems broke, and these remnants form an ethics.

From Catherine comes the knowledge that the world is invented, and the warning that invention cannot shield a person from sorrow. From Mr. Spitzer comes respect for other people’s mirages, and the patience to leave locked trunks unbroken. From Esther comes love that outlives hope. From Cousin Hannah comes the discovery that action and meditation are not opposites, and that the greatest battles may be “fought in the domestic wars by those who had never left home” (ch. 32). From Miss MacIntosh comes courage: “Be not afraid of life” (ch. 63).

None of these is a rule. Each is a disposition, a way of standing in the world once the world has stopped offering solid ground. Together they describe a person who knows she lives in illusion and treats others with the tenderness that knowledge makes possible.

Going On as Search

Vera’s whole story is the ethic of going on made visible. The novel opens with her riding a bus through fog, searching for a woman she knows is probably dead. She asks, “Where should I go? Where should I turn?” (ch. 1). She has no system to answer. She goes anyway.

The search does not find its object. Vera never finds Miss MacIntosh. What she finds instead are people she was not looking for: Esther, the stone deaf man, a life. This is the shape of going on in Young’s philosophy. The search is not justified by its success. It is justified because it keeps a person moving among other people, where love can happen.
