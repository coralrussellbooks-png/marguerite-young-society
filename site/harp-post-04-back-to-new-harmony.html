Harp Song for a Radical

Chapter 4. Back to New Harmony

← Chapter 3 · The Unfinished Harp: Reading Harp Song for a Radical · Chapter 5 →

Weitling's tour of America's broken utopias, and Young's return to her own ground

In 1945, Marguerite Young published Angel in the Forest: A Fairy Tale of Two Utopias, her history of the two communities that took turns on the same stretch of Indiana riverbank. The first was Father George Rapp's celibate Harmony Society. The second was Robert Owen's rational New Harmony, built in the houses the Rappites left behind. That book made Young's name as a prose writer.

Half a century later, in her last book, she comes back to this ground. She does not come back head on. She arrives sideways, riding along with Wilhelm Weitling as he tours the American interior looking for a place to build the workers' paradise. Rapp and Owen, who once filled a whole book, appear here as ruins, rumors, and bad advice. That change in scale is the most interesting thing about these chapters (roughly pages 72 to 115). Young is treating her own earlier subject as one layer among many.

The peddler of paradise

Weitling reached New York late in 1846 expecting to edit a German workers' paper. It had already gone bankrupt. So he set out west with a bag of pamphlets, by train to Pittsburgh, by boat down the Ohio and the Mississippi to New Orleans, and even into Texas, founding workers' clubs as he went.

Young's account of this journey is comic and very little of it concerns grand theory. The Philadelphia workers' association he helped found collected dues of "three cents a week" (76). The money paid for doctors and medicine, for coffins and gravediggers, for a building and loan fund, and for a singing society. Utopia, on the ground, looked a great deal like burial insurance and a choir.

The recruiting went badly. In Cleveland, many of the German workers he addressed drifted off to "a picnic with harps and flutes and songs" in the woods six miles away, and he signed up only fourteen members (82). In Chicago, "Brother Weitling was able to bring into his harvest only one member, and that was a brewer" (84). In Wisconsin, he learned that many farmers saw hard currency once a year, a single silver dollar at harvest, and lost it at once to debts at the country store (84–85).

Young is laughing at Weitling here, but she is not sneering. The harps win against the pamphlets, and it is hard to blame the picnickers. This is The Sacred Is Here taking its revenge on the grand plan. People who had worked all week wanted music and grass more than a lecture about the future.

Communia

Weitling finally found his promised land in Clayton County, Iowa, at a struggling colony called Communia, where the Turkey and Volga rivers meet. He arrived after a stagecoach ride through mud up to the horses' heads, and he announced, "For the first time in my life I stand on the sacred soil of a communal brotherhood. This land, as far as the eye can see, belongs to us" (85).

The land did belong to them, and most of it went unplowed. Young catalogs the collapse with a farmer's eye. Many acres lay uncultivated. The hay sat out so long that neighbors' cattle ate half of it. The sawmill was a terrible investment. The iron castings for the flour mill rusted, and no one would haul the millstones up from the river. A promising gleam in the rocks turned out to be lead rather than silver, producing, as Young puts it, "a tree bearing the leaden apples of discord" (90). Members complained "that there were no feather beds for communitarians to sleep on" (87). The one fiddler knew a single tune and broke it in the middle, playing an instrument Young calls "the coffin of music" (89).

Then comes a passage that stands out from everything around it. In the middle of the chaos, Weitling went out to help finish the colony's dam, digging mud and standing in freezing water, because the workers would not. Young writes that there he "had found perhaps his only happiness during his sojourn there" (90).

Young follows this with a long meditation on beavers. John Jacob Astor, the German immigrant who became America's richest man, had built his fortune on beaver pelts. Young describes the beavers as cooperative brotherhoods of builders, destroyed along with the Native peoples who had hunted them with care and apology. She pictures Weitling at his dam, in his red beaver hat, as an "old beaver king" come back to life (91–92).

The image carries the lens this study uses. The prophet who wanted to remake the world found joy only in plain work with his hands, in cold water, beside other people. It lasted a short time. After the banquet celebrating the dam, "The harmony in which Brother Weitling had left his people had soon broken again into chaos" (93).

Young still does not let the colony end as a joke. She mentions a Polish barber in an eastern city who kept paying his dues for years, planning to retire to Communia and become its fiddler (105). The Margins Reveal the Truth is sometimes this small. One barber kept a failed dream alive with his monthly payments, and Young makes room for him.

Ruins on top of ruins

From Iowa, Weitling crossed to Nauvoo, Illinois, and Young slows down, because Nauvoo is a stack of layers.

It began as a Native village, which a white captain acquired for two hundred sacks of corn. It was called Venus, then Commerce, then Nauvoo, when Joseph Smith bought it for his Mormon Zion (95). After Smith's murder in 1844, the Mormons were driven out, and their great temple was burned. Leaving, Brigham Young told a follower who mourned the unfinished temple, "But we have the satisfaction of taking the substance with us, leaving behind us only the shadow" (95). Then, in 1849, the French Icarians moved into the ruins.

This is The Past Doesn't Go Away in its most literal form. Nothing in Nauvoo was built on empty ground. Young's sentence about the Icarians could serve as a motto for every utopia in the book. Their community began in a place "which in a psychological as well as a physical sense was never the pure beginning" (99).

Icaria on paper and in the mud

The Icarians followed Étienne Cabet, a French lawyer whose novel Voyage to Icaria described a perfect communist society. Young takes real pleasure in the book's blueprint. In Cabet's Icaria, everything is public and discussed by all, and every fact is backed by "statistics, statistics, statistics distributed free" (101). Children learn a simplified language in which "Words in the imaginary Icaria were spelled as they sounded and thus contained no useless letters" (101–102). The children are "the most perfect in the world" (101).

Young sets that frozen, finished plan against the real Icarians. On Owen's advice, Cabet had bought land in Texas without seeing it. The land turned out to be laid out like a checkerboard, communal squares alternating with privately owned ones, so that "it was impossible for an Icarian to drive the cattle home to their shelters at night without stepping from communism into capitalism" (97). Fever killed many. Half the survivors went home to France and sued Cabet for fraud. The rest ended up in Nauvoo, where the children were taken from their parents at two and "were never very happy in their isolated state except for the Sunday afternoons when they visited their parents" (103).

The Texas checkerboard is the principle of Reality Is Constructed turned into a map. A property line is only a drawn thing, but a cow crossing it moves between two economic systems. The children's Sunday visits are the principle of Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary, told as grief. A plan that is perfect on paper made small children lonely six days a week.

The prosperous saints

Near Pittsburgh, Weitling visited Economy, the third and last home of Father Rapp's Harmony Society. This is the community at the heart of Angel in the Forest, and Young's portrait here is sharper than affection.

The Rappites believed they embodied the Sun Woman of the Book of Revelation. They lived as married celibates, waiting for the world to end. Meanwhile they grew rich. Young lists their holdings: "coal mines, sawmills, oil wells" (111), factories, even an oil pipeline. She also notes that Rapp collected six percent interest on a mortgage "upon farmland upriver on the bluffs overlooking the Wabash at Terre Haute" (114). It is the first time the town where Debs will be born appears in these pages, and it appears in a lender's ledger.

Young gives the hardest judgment to Rapp's own adopted son. Frederick Rapp told the reformer Frances Wright that the community amounted to "a wageless white slavery" (112), and that he would leave if he could not change it. The members' reward was deferred. As Young puts it, "The Rappites, because of the sacrifices they had made on earth, would be the first to be transported to heaven" (114).

This is the sharpest test in Part One of the principle this study calls The Sacred Is Here. A promised heaven is the oldest way to justify unpaid labor now. The Rappites worked for nothing in a present that earned others real money, on the strength of a future that never came. Weitling's critics in Cincinnati made the same charge against him, that he promised workers "an earthly paradise which they would not live to see or would see only when they were dead" (108). Young records both charges and endorses neither side completely. She lets the pattern show.

She also keeps the dark rumors. Frederick Rapp died alone on a forest road, and talk of murder spread. Rapp was whispered to have castrated his own son. Young reports both as tavern tales, and then adds a remarkable sentence: "Perhaps there could be few or no utopias which could be utopias unless they contained within them strange murders whether real or imaginary" (113). A perfect society, she suggests, grows its own shadow stories, and the stories are part of what it is.

The palimpsest

The best image in these chapters comes near the end. When Rapp's followers left their first Harmony in Pennsylvania, the buyer, a man named Ziegler, took over their houses. Young writes that "a new utopia had been laid over the first like a palimpsest through which one saw the gleamings of an older writing" (114). A palimpsest is a manuscript scraped clean and written over, with the old text still faintly visible underneath.

Then comes the punch line. Ziegler put up "For Rent" signs on the Rappite houses and barns and orchards, leaving alone only the graves, and "had opened a show of wildly dancing puppets" (114–115).

Every utopia in these pages is written over another. Owen's New Harmony was written over Rapp's second Harmony. The Icarians wrote over the Mormons, who had written over a Native village. Even Oneida, the perfectionist commune Weitling admired in New York, paid its way with a steel-trap business, "a nakedly capitalistic venture of their own and without shame" (81). And the final layer is the landlord and the puppet show.

Owen at a distance

Robert Owen, the hero or antihero of Angel in the Forest, hardly appears here in person. He turns up in Paris during the 1848 revolution, papering the walls with appeals for universal happiness while archbishops and babies die in the streets. Young calls him a man "who had never admitted the defeat of his utopia on the banks of the Wabash" (77). Later he is simply "Robert Owen of the busted New Harmony" (100), the adviser whose recommendation sent Cabet's followers into Texas.

It is tempting to read this demotion as a judgment, and it may partly be one. It is also the method of Harp Song applied to Young's own career. In 1945 New Harmony was the center of her story. In the Debs book, it is one layer in a thick stack, visible under the newer writing, part of the ground a boy from Terre Haute will stand on. Young practiced The Past Doesn't Go Away on her own earlier work.

What passes on

In the middle of Weitling's failures, Young sets down two small and unexpected sentences about the future.

The first comes in Ohio, where she stops to describe "a little dwarf child worker with a harelip" (83), barefoot and bleeding as he helps his father sow seed. The boy is John Peter Altgeld. He will become governor of Illinois and will pardon the surviving Haymarket anarchists at the cost of his career. The second comes in the beaver passage. Young writes that the slaughter of the beavers, the buffalo, and the Native peoples "would bring the awakening of the social consciousness of Eugene Victor Debs, if there was such a thing as his awakening, if it had not been his from the day when he was born" (80).

This is the Crossing Point. Weitling's utopias fail one after another, but they fail in a country that is raising the people who will carry the fight forward. Young does not claim a straight line between them. She puts them in the same pages and lets the reader draw the connection.

Checking the record

Much of Young's detail holds up. Communia was a real German colony in Clayton County, Iowa, and Weitling did pour his workers' league's money into it. Zoar, the German separatist community in Ohio, was founded in 1817 under Joseph Bimeler, practiced celibacy for its first years, and helped dig the Ohio and Erie Canal. Its garden was laid out after the New Jerusalem of Revelation, as Young says. The Icarians did settle in Nauvoo in 1849, the year after the Mormon temple burned. Rapp's first Harmony in Pennsylvania was in fact sold to a buyer named Ziegler.

Several points need correcting or flagging:

  • The wrong revolution. Young has Weitling reaching Paris "two days before the end of the July revolution of 1830" (76). Everything she then describes belongs to 1848: Louis Philippe fleeing to England as "William Smith," Owen's appeals on the walls, the archbishop of Paris shot at the barricades. The date should be 1848.

  • "Sight unseen." Young says Owen bought Rapp's Indiana Harmony sight unseen (96–97). Owen visited Harmony in December 1824 and completed the purchase in January 1825.

  • Cabet and the "carabinieri." Young says Cabet became "a leader in the carabinieri" (99). The carabinieri are the Italian military police. Cabet belonged to the French Charbonnerie, the secret society modeled on the Italian Carbonari. The slip is small but telling, since only one word is wrong.

  • Nashoba. Frances Wright founded Nashoba in 1825, not 1827, and it stood on the Wolf River in Tennessee, not the Fox River (112).

  • The death of Frederick Rapp. Frederick Rapp died in 1834. The falling oak and the murder rumor are best treated as the tavern legend Young says they became.
