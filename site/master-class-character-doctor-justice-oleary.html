Doctor Justice O'Leary: The Witness Who Stopped Seeing

← Mr. Weed · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · James the Chauffeur →

Spoiler note: This portrait is written for readers who have finished the novel. It discusses the character’s whole story, including how it ends.

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Justice O’Leary · Miss MacIntosh · Moses Hunnecker · Vera Cartwheel

The reader meets him first as a reflection, a dead man in a bus driver's rear-view mirror, and only later, through the thin wall of an Indiana inn, learns his full story. Both meetings are the right because being a reflection, a haunt, a voice raving through a wall, a man half in the grave and still practicing medicine, is the whole of what he is.

Dr. Justice O'Leary, so he calls himself, announcing his name and his greatness to anyone within earshot, the best medical practitioner the country ever saw, left to grow old among ungrateful clients. He is the baby doctor of a poor country town, the one who brings the babies in. He has blown his breath into the mouths of dead men whose mouths stank. He is drunk, reeling, borrowing a nickel for the next drink, paid for his deliveries in turnips and rotten walnuts with the worms still crawling in them, a cancer eating into his own face. He was killed earlier that same evening in an illusory traffic accident, and by rights should be pinned under a steering wheel in a flooded meadow, and he is up all night anyway, practicing. And he keeps a dead woman in his room. Her name is Sarah, his sister, who died at the age of one and lies under a stone in the vegetable garden, and who has come back, silent, perhaps imaginary, to disgrace him, and whom he cannot make leave.

Radical Immanence makes witnessing the central act, staying present with what one cannot fix or resolve, and it gives that act its clearest keeper in Vera. O'Leary is the same act turned inside out. He is the professional witness of the threshold, the man stationed at both doors of the world, the one who ushers the living in and watches the dying go out. The question the book asks through him is not why he is so ruined, but what becomes of a witness who has seen too much. The answer is that he goes blind to the very thing he witnesses.

The first time O'Leary watched a man die, he saw something steal out of the room, a mist, a vapor, the soul going out by the door. He has watched and listened ever since, at deathbed after deathbed, and never once seen or heard anything leave again. Death, he says, has ceased to be a drama. That is the exact reverse of Vera, who comes close to each person and refuses to conclude, who keeps every mystery open by never solving it. O'Leary has stood nearer than anyone to the central mystery of the whole book, the passage between the living and the dead, and standing there for a lifetime has worn it smooth. He is the witness who witnessed until there was nothing left to see. Where Vera's long looking keeps people alive and unfinished, O'Leary's long looking has closed the door on wonder itself.

The numbness has a body. He cannot feel the pulse of another, the book says, because his own pulse has failed and his own artery has hardened. A doctor is a person whose whole art is to lay a finger on another life and feel it beating, and this one cannot, because the beating has stopped in him first. It is the plainest image in the novel for what a witness becomes when the witnessing empties out. He is present at every threshold and can feel none of them. He has so much faith in the past, the book says, that he has none in the future. A man that full of the dead has no room left in him for the living to register.

Sarah is the proof. The numbed witness of a thousand deaths cannot make his own single dead sister go. He knows she is dead, says so, insists he has put her out of his memory and devoted his life to hard work, and still she is there every night, silent, on the floor, in the bed, her feet cold as ice, and he argues with her until the walls shake and the slop jar dances and he never wins, because there is no winning against a grief that will not end. He calls her an impostor, the wrong woman, a senile baby dead at the age of one, and none of it makes her leave. The man who lost the power to see the soul steal out of the room is haunted by the one soul that will not. His deadness to death and his helplessness before this single death are the same condition. He went numb, it seems, precisely because he could not bear the door Sarah keeps propping open.

His name is the book's flat joke and its deepest one. Justice O'Leary presides over a world with no justice in it, women brought to child-bed against their will, help asked for and never given, a good doctor paid in worm-eaten walnuts, a cancer eating the face of the man who might have been a Mayo brother curing cancer, a God who has deserted him. He is named for the thing his world most lacks and he can least supply. The name is not a label the reader is meant to trust. It is a claim the book raises in order to bring it down, the way it raises Miss MacIntosh's sensible reality and Catherine's madness and then quietly swaps their places. A man called Justice who cannot feel another's pulse is the novel saying, in a single name, that the grand words people give themselves are the last thing anyone should believe about them.

He belongs, in the end, to the book's company of the not-quite-living, alongside the clam-digger who buries himself in the sand and Moses Hunnecker who drives too long after he is dead. O'Leary was killed in the accident and is up all night practicing; he is the doctor of the threshold who lives on the threshold. It is fitting that the one physician stationed between birth and death cannot say which side of his own doorway he is on. He keeps the crossing not as its guide but as its longest resident.

The lesson for a writer is in how to build the dark twin of a virtue. Vera's witnessing is the book's ethical center, and a lesser novel would have left it there, admired and unopposed. Young sets against it a man who does the same thing for a living and has been ruined by it, so that the reader sees at once what witnessing is worth and what it looks like when it fails. O'Leary is not the opposite of Vera the way a villain is the opposite of a hero. He is what her gift becomes when it is spent, when the looking has gone on so long that nothing looks back. To place that figure a few rooms down from the one who witnesses well is to show, without a word of argument, both the worth of the thing and its cost. A writer who wants a virtue to carry weight can build, somewhere in the same house, the person it destroyed.
