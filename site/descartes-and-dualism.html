Chapter 6

  • “She dreamed that she was not herself”: Philosophy of self examines the idea of the self at a conceptual level. Most philosophical definitions of self (per Descartes, Locke, Hume, and William James) are expressed in the first person. The particular characteristics of the self determine its identity.
  • “One must be a spirit not anywhere in space”: Philosophy of mind is a branch of philosophy that deals with the nature of the mind and its relation to the body and the external world. Dualism and monism are the two central schools of thought on the mind-body problem. Descartes held that the mind takes up no space, and Homer guesses that a spirit must be “outside of space.”

Chapter 8

  • “that dream within the dream”: This may refer to Edgar Allan Poe’s poem “A Dream within a Dream” (1849), which says, “All that we see or seem / Is but a dream within a dream.” This dream within a dream is a recurring theme throughout the book. The idea that life is a dream goes back to Plato, Aristotle, and Descartes. The first stanza ends with that statement, and the last stanza turns it into a question: “Is all that we see or seem / But a dream within a dream?” This symbolizes the recursive, elusive quality of existence in the novel: a reality constantly refracted through multiple dreamlike states that challenge the notion of a fixed, singular truth.

Chapter 14

  • “she had a third eye in her forehead”: The third eye is a mystical or esoteric concept which provides perception beyond normal sight. The third eye symbolizes a mystical insight and a deeper understanding of the unseen worlds as an insight that both enlightens and alienates Catherine from ordinary human experience. Descartes placed the soul’s seat in the pineal gland, which some have called the third eye.

Chapter 23

  • “What was so rare a thing as common sense”: Thomas Paine’s polemical pamphlet Common Sense (1776) has been described as the most influential political pamphlet of the 18th century, affecting both the American and French revolutions. Today, the concept of common sense, and how it should best be used, remains linked to many of the most perennial topics in epistemology and ethics, with special focus often directed at the philosophy of the modern social sciences.

Chapter 29

  • “Were these two souls which moved through him”: The soul is the purported immaterial aspect or essence of a living being. It is typically believed to be immortal and to exist apart from the material world. The three main theories that describe the relationship between the soul and the body are interactionism, parallelism, and epiphenomenalism. Anthropologists and psychologists have found that most humans are naturally inclined to believe in the existence of the soul and that they have interculturally distinguished between souls and bodies. In philosophy, the soul has been the central area of interest since ancient times. Socrates envisioned the soul to possess a rational faculty, its practice being man’s most godlike activity. Plato believed the soul to be the person’s real self, an immaterial and immortal dweller of our lives that continues and thinks even after death. Aristotle sketched out the soul as the “first actuality” of a naturally organized body: form and matter arrangement allowing natural beings to aspire to full actualization. The soul holds profound significance as a complex and elusive entity intertwined with themes of identity, mortality, and the metaphysical boundaries between life and death. Vera, through Mr. Spitzer’s reflections, presents the soul not as a singular, fixed entity but as something multifaceted and possibly plural. The soul is described as comprising body, soul, and spirit, each with distinct but interconnected roles, and these components may exist simultaneously or sequentially, blurring the lines between life and death, self and other. This ambiguity reflects the novel’s broader theme of fragmented identity and the elusive nature of selfhood. The soul is portrayed as engaging in a continuous interplay with life and death. Mr. Spitzer contemplates how the soul might live beyond death, or how life and death might be inseparable, with moments of existence overlapping in ways that defy linear time. The soul’s journey is likened to traversing between worlds, sometimes indistinguishable from shadows, clouds, or reflections, emphasizing the thin veil between existence and non-existence. The soul is connected to natural and cosmic imagery (clouds, stars, moths, butterflies, and sea creatures) suggesting that it is part of a larger, universal process of transformation and transmutation. This imagery conveys the soul’s ephemeral and yet eternal qualities, linking human experience to the rhythms of nature and the cosmos. Mr. Spitzer’s meditations reveal the soul as a source of both wonder and confusion. He acknowledges his limited understanding of the soul, its origins, and its destiny. The soul’s elusive nature challenges certainty and invites contemplation about the limits of human knowledge, the nature of existence, and the possibility of immortality or reincarnation. The chapter discusses the relationship between soul and memory, suggesting that memory might be a manifestation or extension of the soul. Memory, like the soul, is imperfect and fragmentary, and the dead may live on through memories or echoes rather than physical presence. This concept supports the novel’s recurring theme of the persistence of identity beyond physical life. The soul is significant as a symbol and concept embodying the novel’s exploration of identity, mortality, and the metaphysical uncertainties of human existence. It is neither wholly present nor absent but exists in a liminal space between being and non-being, connected intimately to the natural and cosmic order, and enveloped in mystery and ambiguity. (Wikipedia) The chapter’s “two souls” and “three souls or the amalgamations of many souls” reflect the fragmented and overlapping nature of consciousness and existence.

Chapter 37

  • Descartes’ dream argument - He used the experience of dreaming to question the certainty of our perceptions and beliefs. He argued that if we cannot definitively distinguish between waking and dreaming, then our sensory experiences, which are the foundation of our knowledge, might be illusions. There is philosophical skepticism because the possibility of dreams may be genuine or a fabrication. It leads one to question the validity of knowledge of the external world. Freud believed dreams are a symbolic representation of unconscious thoughts and desires. Jung expanded on Freud’s ideas that dreams can also reveal universal, archetypal patterns in the human psyche. Some theories suggest dreams are a natural byproduct of the brain’s activity during sleep, possibly involving memory consolidation, emotional processing, or the clearing out of mental clutter. Neuroscience has provided insights into the physiological processes which in turn influences philosophical discussion about the nature and functions of dreams. Some philosophers think dreams are a way of accessing another realm of existence or experiencing different perspectives on reality. The chapter concludes that dreams are not simply illusions but fundamental to existence and consciousness, blurring distinctions between reality and unreality, life and death, self and other, and that this ambiguity is a central and inescapable part of human experience.

Catherine takes this one step further at the end of the last chapter and asks, “What if God is dreaming?” See the Philosophy of Dreaming. In a sense people do live in a dream world that is created by the mind. The chapter weaves together the philosophical inquiries of Zhuangzi, Plato, Aristotle, and Descartes on dreams to underscore the complexity and mystery of human consciousness and the elusive quest for truth amid illusions.

Chapter 49

  • Peter Abelard was a medieval French scholastic philosopher, leading logician, theologian, teacher, musician, composer, and poet. In philosophy he is celebrated for his proposed logical solution to the problem of universals via nominalism and conceptualism and his pioneering of intent in ethics. Often referred to as the "Descartes of the twelfth century", he is considered a forerunner of Rousseau, Kant, and Spinoza. He is sometimes credited as a chief forerunner of modern empiricism. In Catholic theology, he is best known for his development of the concept of limbo, and his introduction of the moral influence theory of atonement. He is considered (alongside Augustine) to be the most significant forerunner of the modern self-reflective autobiographer. He paved the way and set the tone for later epistolary novels and celebrity tell-alls with his publicly distributed letter, The History of My Calamities and public correspondence. In history and popular culture he is best known for his passionate, tragic love affair and intense philosophical exchange with his brilliant student and eventual wife, Héloïse d'Argenteuil. After they secretly married and went to different convents Heloise’s guardian had Abelard ambushed and castrated. Roscellinus and others made fun of him. He did father a son before that event.

Chapter 50

  • Dualism was defended by Descartes who believed that the mind and body were separate and only humans had a mind. Modern philosophers maintain that the mind is not separate from the body.

Chapter 51

The Wisdom Paradox is the more a person learns the more he is exposed to what he does not know. The variety of ways to see the world is infinite. In the philosophy of consciousness there are generally two camps: dualism (the conscious mind or conscious mental state is non-physical in some sense) and materialism (the mind is the brain and is caused by neural activity).
