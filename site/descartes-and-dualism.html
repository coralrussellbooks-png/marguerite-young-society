Chapter 23

  • Thomas Reid wrote common sense works as such: If all men observe an item and believe the same qualities about that item, then the knowledge of that item is universally true. It is common knowledge, which without explanation is held true by other people; so, what is universally seen is universally believed. "The real, then, is that which, sooner or later, information and reasoning would finally result in, and which is therefore independent of the vagaries of me and you. Thus, the very origin of the conception of reality shows that this conception essentially involves the notion of a community, without definite limits, and capable of a definite increase of knowledge". The combination of the same ideas, of a thing, by multiple people, is what confirms the reality of an object. Reid also believes that the philosophers of his time exaggerated what is truly real. Where most philosophers believe that what we see is not fully what that thing is, for example, Descartes, Reid counters this argument simply by stating that "such a hypothesis is no more likely to be true than the common-sensical belief that the world is much the way we perceive it to be". Reality is what we make it out to be, nothing more.

Chapter 29

  • The philosophy of conversion explores the concept of a significant and transformative change, often in belief, behavior, or identity. It can be viewed within religious, philosophical, and psychological contexts, examining the nature of this change, its motivations, and its implications for individuals and societies. Religious Conversion Christian conversion, Jewish conversion, Islamic conversion, Hinduism doesn't have a formal conversion process. In Plato's philosophy, the concept of conversion is represented in the allegory of the cave, where a prisoner's awakening involves turning away from shadows and towards the light of truth. Philosophical conversion can also refer to an existential change in the individual, adopting new moral standards or making a fundamental shift in their understanding of reality. Moral conversion involves a change in an individual's moral beliefs, values, and actions, often driven by a critical self-reflection and a new understanding of moral principles. Philosophers like Descartes and Husserl are considered to have undergone a form of philosophical conversion in their pursuit of universal science and phenomenology, respectively. The philosophy of conversion is significant as it encapsulates the novel’s central concerns with change, the instability of identity, and the interplay of life and death. Conversion is not merely a religious or moral act but a profound existential process that embraces ambiguity, transformation, and the continual search for meaning amid uncertainty and flux. This philosophy enriches the novel’s exploration of human consciousness and the elusive nature of reality.

Chapter 37

  • Descartes’ dream argument - He used the experience of dreaming to question the certainty of our perceptions and beliefs. He argued that if we cannot definitively distinguish between waking and dreaming, then our sensory experiences, which are the foundation of our knowledge, might be illusions. There is philosophical skepticism because the possibility of dreams may be genuine or a fabrication. It leads one to question the validity of knowledge of the external world. Freud believed dreams are a symbolic representation of unconscious thoughts and desires. Jung expanded on Freud’s ideas that dreams can also reveal universal, archetypal patterns in the human psyche. Some theories suggest dreams are a natural byproduct of the brain’s activity during sleep, possibly involving memory consolidation, emotional processing, or the clearing out of mental clutter. Neuroscience has provided insights into the physiological processes which in turn influences philosophical discussion about the nature and functions of dreams. Some philosophers think dreams are a way of accessing another realm of existence or experiencing different perspectives on reality. The chapter concludes that dreams are not simply illusions but fundamental to existence and consciousness, blurring distinctions between reality and unreality, life and death, self and other, and that this ambiguity is a central and inescapable part of human experience.

Chapter 49

  • Peter Abelard was a medieval French scholastic philosopher, leading logician, theologian, teacher, musician, composer, and poet. In philosophy he is celebrated for his proposed logical solution to the problem of universals via nominalism and conceptualism and his pioneering of intent in ethics. Often referred to as the "Descartes of the twelfth century", he is considered a forerunner of Rousseau, Kant, and Spinoza. He is sometimes credited as a chief forerunner of modern empiricism. In Catholic theology, he is best known for his development of the concept of limbo, and his introduction of the moral influence theory of atonement. He is considered (alongside Augustine) to be the most significant forerunner of the modern self-reflective autobiographer. He paved the way and set the tone for later epistolary novels and celebrity tell-alls with his publicly distributed letter, The History of My Calamities and public correspondence. In history and popular culture he is best known for his passionate, tragic love affair and intense philosophical exchange with his brilliant student and eventual wife, Héloïse d'Argenteuil. After they secretly married and went to different convents Heloise’s guardian had Abelard ambushed and castrated. Roscellinus and others made fun of him. He did father a son before that event.
