Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is a book almost no one has finished. Marguerite Young worked on it for the better part of two decades, and when it appeared in 1965 it ran to some 1,198 pages, a single unbroken current of prose with almost no plot to hold on to. Its admirers, Anaïs Nin among the most devoted, called it a masterpiece. Most readers called it an ordeal. In the years since it has become a famous kind of absence, a novel more often invoked than opened, honored for an ambition few have the stamina to test.

What it is, underneath the reputation, is simpler than its length suggests. A young woman named Vera Cartwheel rides a decrepit bus across a fog-drowned Midwest, searching for the nursemaid who raised her and then vanished. Behind that search stands a house given over to opium, dream, and the dead: a mother in a lifelong coma, a lawyer who cannot tell himself from his dead twin, a canary that keeps dying and being quietly replaced. On nearly every page the book asks one question. What is real, what is illusion, and whether the line between them can be drawn at all.

This book reads the novel through a framework it calls Radical Immanence, a set of claims about where meaning lives and how a world gets made. The pillar that governs Young’s handling of reality and illusion is the second, and it can be put plainly: illusion is primary. Most of what a person takes for solid ground is built rather than found. Money, borders, law, rank, even the steady sense of being one continuous self, are agreements sustained by repetition rather than truths uncovered in the ground. They feel like bedrock because everyone keeps acting as though they are. Illusion in this sense is not the opposite of reality and not a mistake to be corrected. It is the stuff reality is made of. To notice the construction, to catch the agreement in the act of being kept up, is the first loosening of its hold.

No novel takes that claim more seriously. Young does not treat illusion as an error her characters need to escape. She treats it as the primary condition of being alive, and she builds her whole world to prove it. The easy way to read the book is to sort its two great women into opposites. Catherine, the mother, drowned in opium dreams, stands for illusion. Miss MacIntosh, the nursemaid, plain and practical and armed with her black umbrella, stands for reality. The novel offers this reading openly, and then takes it apart.

Catherine’s dream world is never presented as a simple failure of perception. She reverses the terms herself. In Vera’s words, she “pretended that the real was the dream, that the dream was the real.” Her coma is a chosen sovereignty rather than a defeat, a world she governs because the waking one failed her. Vera, raised at the edge of that sovereignty, later arrives at the line the whole book turns on: “The individual is the one illusion.” If the separate, bounded self is itself something built and agreed upon, then illusion is not a room a few damaged people live in. It is the house everyone lives in.

Miss MacIntosh is the proof. The character who stands for reality is herself a construction. The red hair that fixes her in Vera’s mind is a wig. Beneath it she is bald, without brow or lashes, her solidity maintained and performed and held up by daily repetition, exactly as the pillar describes. When Vera’s hand finds the bare skull in the dark, the book’s central opposition falls apart. There was never a clean floor of fact under the dreaming. There was construction all the way down, some of it honest about itself, some of it not. Catherine hides inside her illusions and Miss MacIntosh hides behind hers, and the difference between them turns out to be a matter of candor, not of contact with some bedrock real.

This is what it means to say illusion is primary. Not that nothing is real, but that the real and the made cannot be pried apart, and that anyone who goes looking for solid ground beneath the illusions comes back, as Vera does, holding another illusion. The novel names the reward of that search without flinching: “the illusion were the only reality, and our self-deception was the only rose.” Truth, when Vera reaches for it, turns out to be “but another illusion.” The search is not thereby pointless. It is simply honest about what it will and will not find.

Young did not invent this vision on her own. She writes inside an old lineage: Calderón’s play Life Is a Dream, Simone Weil’s veil of illusion laid over the world, and the long argument in Western thought, taken up in later chapters through Merleau-Ponty, Bergson, Sartre, and others, about how much of what we see we have quietly put there ourselves. What is her own is the patience of the demonstration. Where a philosopher states the claim, Young lives inside it for well over a thousand pages, until the reader, too, loses the ability to say for certain where the dream ends and the waking begins.

The chapters that follow take up the novel’s world through this pillar, organized by theme rather than by reading schedule. Perception, identity, mortality, ethics, and narrative and time are each examined in turn, and each is read as a further case of the same primary fact, that the world Young’s characters move through is made and not given, and that seeing it as made is the beginning of whatever freedom is available to them. The last chapter asks what reality and illusion finally mean in Young. The aim throughout is to let a reader who has not finished the novel, or not yet begun it, follow the argument, and to show why a book so hard to read repays the reading.

Reflection Questions

  1. Money, borders, rank, and the sense of being one continuous self are called agreements kept up by repetition. Which agreements in daily life feel most like bedrock?

  2. What changes when the character who stands for reality turns out to be constructed too?

  3. If Catherine hides inside her illusions and Miss MacIntosh hides behind hers, is candor a real difference or only a matter of degree?

  4. Can a search be worth making if it admits in advance that it will find only another illusion?

  5. Why might a novel show that illusion is primary more convincingly than a work of philosophy can?
