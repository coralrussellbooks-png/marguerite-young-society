The Setting

← Characters as Representations · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Regions of the United States →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

The settings in this novel are usually read as mirrors. A decaying house for a decaying mind, a frozen waste for a frozen heart, a fog-bound coast for a soul that cannot see its way. That reading is available and it undersells what Young is doing. Setting here is the second pillar of Radical Immanence built out of brick and weather. Reality is constructed rather than found, and the places in this book are the plainest proof of it. They are made by the people who need them, they will not hold still, and in the end they are swept away. Setting is not a backdrop that reflects the self. It is the self's construction, and it fails the way every construction in the novel fails.

The sea-blackened house on the New England coast is the clearest case. It is vast, dark, and almost empty, roofed in "many-colored glass," ringed by roaring surf, and it does not stay where it was put. It "sometimes seems to move in the night from place to place," set down in strange dead cities, its furniture changing, its mirrors "hanging lopsided," its windows "looking out upon a different sea or a different street." A house that relocates while its occupants sleep is not a symbol of instability. It is instability, rendered in the floor plan. The reader is not being told that Catherine's mind is unmoored. The reader is watching the ground itself refuse to be ground, which is the thing the novel keeps insisting: that the solid world is a thing someone built, and a thing that was built can shift, or move in the night, or come down.

Catherine has populated the house the way she populates everything, by making the company she needs. The rooms hold "enchanted clocks stopped at different hours," imaginary servants, guests out of the silent films, "royal sleighs with rusted bells," "giant crabs like monks," "old pontiffs walking in the mist." The servants take these callers for real, and the taking is the point. A dream world is not a lapse here. It is a place, as fully inhabited as any address in the book, and the atmosphere of "snow crystals, halos, mock suns and moons" is not the failure of a real setting but the success of a made one. The house is at once protective and imprisoning, a reality she constructed and cannot now leave.

Whatever moves in the house moves in the cities too. Boston's South Station is "crowded with submarine musicians" and watery apparitions. The Brooklyn Bridge appears as "a purple lace glove reaching through the darkness." Times Square burns with a thousand lights, and Roman fountains splash in courtyards where they do not belong. These are real cities that have stopped behaving like real cities, their streets moving, their buildings changing shape, their landmarks turning up where no map would place them. The polar extremes do the same work by other means, "glaciers like mirrors moving through darkness," the aurora borealis and australis lighting the sky so that the two poles cannot be told apart. So does the Indiana village at night, its "upright houses like coffins," its "pale, dented moon like a skull," a "conspiracy of illusion" filled with amorphous shapes and dead souls. City, ice, and village are not three settings with three meanings. They are one refusal, made three times: the refusal of a place to stay fixed long enough to be trusted.

Against all of this stands Miss MacIntosh, and her world is built to be the exception. Where the house shifts and the cities dissolve, her environment is plain, old-fashioned, and stripped of illusion. She prefers the wind and the hail and the snowstorm to the enclosed, hallucinatory rooms, and her presence is meant to ground the narrative in the solid and the given. It is worth reading her setting as carefully as the book reads her, though, because her plain outdoor world makes the same claim she makes and gets it wrong the same way. She insists that reality is single, found, and reported, that a place is simply there. The novel spends its length demonstrating that even her steadiness is a construction, a self assembled out of a wig and a corset and a rule that there is a place for everything. Her austere world is not the one true setting among the false ones. It is one more built place, kept plain on purpose, by a woman who could not admit that plainness was a choice. The counterpoint holds, but it is not the counterpoint of the real against the unreal. It is one construction that knows itself set beside several that do.

Then the houses come down, and the novel makes its argument in rubble.

Catherine's mansion is a "citadel of dreams and visions and imaginary pretenders to vanished thrones," a "house of shades and monsters," even a "private mad house," the place where her "dreams begot the only realities." After she dies, the sea she dreamed beside all her life reaches up and takes it. The house is "swept over" and "carried away," and the water that filled her visions reclaims the room those visions were kept in. This is not punishment and it is not tidy symbolism. It is the plainest statement of the pillar of reality is constructed. She built a place to survive a life she could not otherwise survive, and the place was temporary, as every built place is, and the tide that was always at the window finally came in.

Mr. Spitzer's houses tell the same story in a lower key. He lives at first in a "grey, impoverished house" of "general confusion" and disrepair, and the novel remembers an earlier house of exact "twin arrangements," the mirrored order that his brother's death shattered. At the end he is in a "beach-comber's shack" with the old clam-digger, and the same storm that takes Catherine's mansion takes the shack. The man who could never say whether he was himself or his brother loses even the walls around him to the one weather. His anchor was always provisional, and the sea does not distinguish between the citadel and the shack.

Cousin Hannah's house is destroyed by a different force, and the difference matters. Her Boston mansion "between two water towers" was "torn down long ago to make space for an asphalt highway," and Mr. Spitzer keeps visiting the empty lot, noting that the place "had disappeared as if by magic." No storm, no sea, only the highway, the ordinary machinery of progress paving over where her life once stood. It fits her, the woman of tent cities and deserts and mountains, whose home was never a structure but the going itself. Her house did not need the ocean to prove it impermanent. The city did it, on schedule, for the sake of a road.

Three houses, three ways of coming down, storm and storm and demolition, and the same lesson under each. The houses in this novel are where memory and identity and the dream are kept, and they are all, in the end, unkept. What survives is not the building. It is the going-on after the building is gone, Spitzer walking the emptied lot, Vera carrying the drowned house forward in the only place it can still stand, which is her telling of it.

The turn for a writer is the one the book keeps making in a new key. A setting does not have to reflect a character to do its work, and it does more when it stops reflecting and starts behaving. Young does not describe a shifting house to signal a shifting mind. She lets the house shift, and the reader feels the ground go before any character explains it. She does not call the mansion fragile. She floods it. The strongest setting is not a mirror held up beside the person. It is a thing the person made, standing on its own until the weather comes, and a writer who learns to build a place that can move in the night, and then be swept away, has learned to make the world of a book carry the argument that the sentences would otherwise have to carry alone.
