Martha J. Sattler's Chronology (Marguerite Young, Our Darling)

A reading for the Miss MacIntosh project.

The source at a glance

This is the "Chronology" compiled by Martha J. Sattler for the festschrift "Marguerite Young, Our Darling," edited by Miriam Fuchs (Dalkey Archive Press, 1994). Sattler was the Young bibliographer whose separate bibliography Dalkey commissioned and then canceled, as Steven Moore recounts, so this timeline is the nearest thing the project has to an authoritative factual backbone. Unlike the interviews, it is dated, checked, and dry, and its chief value is that it converts most of the date drifts this set has tracked into settled facts, while leaving a few genuine open questions. It should be treated as the spine against which the looser memories of the interviews are measured.

The dates it settles

The chronology resolves nearly every contested point that has surfaced across the interviews:

  • Her parents separated in 1911, when she was three, and she and her sister went to live with the grandmother, whom Sattler names as Marguerite Herron Knight. Young was named for her.

  • "Prismatic Ground" was published in 1937, which settles the 1937-versus-1939 drift in favor of 1937.

  • She was at the University of Chicago from 1933, took the MA in 1936, and studied with Robert Morss Lovett, Ronald S. Crane, and T. V. Smith, the teachers she names in the Friedman and Fuchs interview. The opium lady is given as Minna Weissenbach, confirming "Minna" over the "Mette" of the 1983 recording.

  • "Angel in the Forest" began in 1940 as sixty ballads, then blank verse, and was turned into prose in 1942, exactly as she describes it.

  • The novel's working title was "The Worm in the Wheat," and Maxwell Perkins accepted forty pages of it in 1945, which fixes the commission at 1945 rather than the 1944 of the Paris Review, and confirms the working title Moore recalled.

  • Her Scribner's editors ran from Perkins, who died in 1947, to John Hall Wheelock, to Burroughs Mitchell.

  • She was on the Iowa faculty from 1955 to 1957 and taught John Gardner there, confirming the blood-contract account.

  • "Angel in the Forest" was reissued by Scribner's in 1966 with Van Doren's introduction, which dates the reissue talk to about 1966, and she began the Riley biography that year under the title "The Muse and the Little China Dog," setting it aside for Debs in 1967.

  • The WBAI program of dramatized readings was broadcast in 1974, which places the Ruas recordings in that period, and the American Professors of Creative Writing honored her in 1983, the year of the AAPL interview.

  • The first Debs volume, "Prelude in the Golden Key," was completed in 1991 and accepted by Knopf in 1992 for projected 1995 publication; Dalkey brought out the two-volume Miss MacIntosh in January 1993 and reprinted "Angel" with "Inviting the Muses" in 1994.

New facts it adds

Beyond settling old questions, the chronology supplies details no interview gives. Her first published poem was "Worship" in Indiana University's Crimson Quill in 1927, followed by "Jonathan's House" and a Butler literary prize; she took her BA from Butler in 1930 and first appeared in Poetry that year. In 1940 she was at Bread Loaf as assistant to Louis Untermeyer. Her first published fiction was "The Dead Woman" in American Prefaces in 1943, and her last lyric poem, "Proem to Reality," appeared in Partisan Review in 1945, the year she also took the National Institute of Arts and Letters Award. Albert Erskine, not only Van Doren, is named as her editor for the two 1945 books at Reynal and Hitchcock. She won the Guggenheim in 1948 and that year joined twenty-seven writers, led by Sinclair Lewis, backing Truman for president; a Newberry Library fellowship followed in 1951. In 1952 she sailed to Rome with the Stephans on the same ship as the McCullerses, stayed at the Hotel Inghilterra, and met Princess Caetani, who published "The Opium Lady" in Botteghe Oscure number ten. The teaching record is now complete: New School and Columbia from 1958, Fairleigh Dickinson from 1960 to 1962, Fordham from 1966 to 1977, Seton Hall from 1971 to 1977, and the New School again from 1977 to 1992. She and Nin met at Lawrence Maxwell's bookshop around 1959 and 1960, their close friendship dating from December 1960, and she recorded a "Poets for Peace" album against the Vietnam war in 1967.

Cautions

A few discrepancies remain rather than dissolve. The chronology itself lists Young as meeting Nin in 1943 and again at Maxwell's bookshop around 1959 and 1960, so the safest reading is an early acquaintance and a later close friendship. The number of pages Perkins accepted in 1945 is given here as forty, against the fifteen Young cites in the Ruas and Paris Review interviews. And the commission year, 1945 here, still sits against the 1944 of the Paris Review, though the weight of evidence, this chronology plus the complete Ruas interview, favors 1945. These are the points to flag in the compilation as settled where possible and openly noted where not.

How it anchors the compilation

This document is the timeline the rest of the archive can hang on. Where an interview offers a remembered date, the chronology usually supplies the checked one, and where they disagree, it marks the disagreement clearly rather than papering over it. For the compilation it does two jobs at once: it retires most of the "drifts" this project has been tracking, turning them into facts, and it preserves the handful of real uncertainties as uncertainties. Read beside the interviews, it also quietly confirms how consistent Young's memory actually was on the large shape of her life, even as she varied the small details and the origin stories from telling to telling.
