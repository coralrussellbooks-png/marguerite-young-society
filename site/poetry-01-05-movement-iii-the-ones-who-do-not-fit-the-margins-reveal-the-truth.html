Movement III. The Ones Who Do Not Fit: The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth · Across every book

Characters: Madge Capehorn · Miss MacIntosh

The people who cannot be filed are the ones who see the filing system, and Young returns to that figure from her earliest work to her last. In "The Seeker" a farmer dreams his whole life over postcards of places he will never reach, and in "Flame" a landlocked woman sails a peddler's wagon "through waves of wheat" and takes it out one last time as she is dying, both of them shaped to lives that had no room for them. The Prismatic Ground poems sharpen the figure into the outright victim: "Silken Hair," destroyed for nothing but "the fact of ambiguous hair," and "The Return," where the land itself rises "like whips" against the stranger. "Lot's Wife," "Plea for Sodom," and "Milton's Wife" speak for the condemned and the unheard. And in the late "The Contravalid" the whole stance hardens into a philosophy, "the crippled dancer is the dance, at last, / The only truth." Seen from the center, these figures are failures or footnotes. Seen from where they stand, they are the only ones with a clear view, because the margin is the one place the seams of the arrangement actually show.

"The Seeker"

A farmer spends his life dreaming over "faded ancient picture postal cards" of places he saved to visit and never reached, sending his son to Paris in his place while he still plows "beneath a hot white sun." He is the dreamer stranded in a life with no room for him, kin to "Biography" and "Lesser Magellan." The Margins Reveal the Truth falls on the man whose longing points always at an elsewhere the plow will never let him reach.

"Flame"

A woman "reared inland" who longed for the sea gets "a wagon / To steer through waves of wheat," a restless peddler with "wide sailor's eyes" who on her last morning, dying, takes the wagon out anyway. She is the singular woman who makes her own voyage on land and will not be held, even by death. The Margins Reveal the Truth honors the one who never fit the settled life the village kept for her.

"The Return."

"Buffalo weed," "wild onion flower," and "blue crab grass" rise like whips to bar the one who tries to come back, the land itself refusing the stranger. The Margins Reveal the Truth is literal here: the returning outsider is the one who feels the country's hostility, because belonging was never extended. The margin is a place a person is put, not a place they choose.

"Lesser Magellan."

An "intrepid explorer" is lost "in the politest wilderness," his grand quest shrunk to a wandering with no discovery at its end. The poem watches the heroic frame fail the man inside it. Seen from the edge, the great voyage is just a person who does not fit the map he was given.

"Biography."

"Young Harry" plays chess with Lucifer and loses everything while barely present, a soul out of step with the game the world set for him. The Margins Reveal the Truth falls on the dreamer the system reads as a failure. His inability to play by the rules is also the clearest sight of what the rules cost.

"Woodlands Far Removed."

The hunter sets out gallantly each morning and returns each night defeated, "he whom nothing small eludes" and who still catches nothing. The poem sits with fruitless pursuit and the neighbors' quiet judgment of it. To keep going out and coming back empty is the outsider's whole biography.

"Gulliver."

Young's Gulliver is the giant among the small and the small among the giants, never the right size for the world he lands in. His displacement becomes a way of seeing what the natives cannot, the arbitrariness of their scale. The one who does not fit measures the room.

"Silken Hair."

A woman's entire "tragic fault" is "the fact of ambiguous hair," an ambiguity the town cannot place and therefore cannot forgive. The Margins Reveal the Truth is stated at its barest: she is destroyed by nothing she did, only by being unclassifiable. In the novel this is Miss MacIntosh's essence, the figure whose strangeness the world can neither hold nor pardon.

"The Dark Wood."

Ellen is trapped in a maze, "too far from the sun," watched by "armies of frogs" whose eyes stream like a miner's lamp. The poem is pure surveillance and entrapment, the outsider hemmed in and stared at. The edge here is a cell with a hundred watching eyes.

"Marriage in Heaven."

The "cozened spouse" was "taught no trade / But to grace the lorn beautiful," a woman's whole life narrowed to appearance and submission. The poem speaks from inside a constraint the social order calls normal, which is exactly where The Margins Reveal the Truth sees most, and its ironic title marks a heaven that is really a cage. In the novel the same walls close on Madge Capehorn, submissive to a "spectral kiss."

"Voyager Man."

The "voyager man" hangs "between the upper and the under air," a liminal figure belonging to neither element he crosses. Isolation is his medium; he is defined by the realms he falls between. The poem also leans toward the passages of Movement IV, since to voyage endlessly is a way of departing without arriving.

"The Home-Coming."

A gate and a pasture mark a threshold reached in "the mind's eye," a return that is spiritual before it is physical. The poem turns on belonging, the outsider's oldest ache, and on whether coming home is ever quite possible. It touches Movement IV as well, because a homecoming imagined this hard is close to a leave-taking.

"The Giraffe."

The creature is "born out of error or misalignment," a flawed thing that names human imperfection through its own odd shape. The Margins Reveal the Truth falls on the being that creation seems to have gotten wrong. Its wrongness is a mirror.

"The Parachutist."

The poem rejects "neutrality and public facades" for the uniqueness of one person's inner view, dropping in alone and apart. To refuse the shared surface is to stand at the edge on purpose. The individual perception it insists on is also a quiet version of Language Shapes What's Possible's warning against the voice from nowhere.

"Lot's Wife."

Young's treatment is tender, "Poor dear! Her fault," siding with the woman punished for the human need to look back. The poem takes the condemned figure's part against the verdict handed down on her. The Margins Reveal the Truth here is a defense attorney's.

"Plea for Sodom."

The poem argues for the small doomed city itself, refusing to accept its destruction as deserved. It speaks up for the place everyone else was content to see burn. To plead for Sodom is to insist the condemned had a life worth the name.

"Milton's Wife."

The poem gives voice to the woman written out of the great man's story, the wife whose account was never allowed to count. The Margins Reveal the Truth recovers the silenced witness at the margin of a famous life. Her side of it was there the whole time, unheard.

"The Contravalid"

"More beautiful ... is the contravalid, the mouse-sized insect ... the crippled dancer is the dance, at last, / The only truth, the double-headed dwarf of paradox." The poem raises the marginal and the deformed above the valid and the logically true, making the broken figure the bearer of the real. The Margins Reveal the Truth becomes an explicit metaphysic here: the seams show clearest at the edges, so the edges are where the truth is. It carries Unfinished Is Honest with it, since "the circumnavigators of being always err."
