Jock Cartwheel: The One Nobody Could Name

← Miss Georgia MacIntosh · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · The Old Clam Digger →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Jock Cartwheel · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

You do not meet him. That is the meeting. Jock Cartwheel is named once in the whole book, in a garbled newspaper notice, and otherwise he is a hole in the shape of a man because being barely there, being a name other people fill in however they need to, is the whole of what he is.

Here is nearly everything the book will tell you about him, and watch how little it holds still. A Boston paper, recalling a social season that never happened, calls Vera's mother the late Mrs. Catherine Cartwheel, widow of Jock, the fashionable dog fancier, the fabulous yachtsman, the great mountain-climber who had disappeared when the mountains moved like waves. Three men in one clause, and the clause is a mistake. The notice was jumbled by a printer's devil, and Mr. Spitzer, reading it, decided not to correct it, on the grounds that he could not say which of the world's errors were worth fixing and this one hardly seemed to be. Vera, his daughter, whispers to her imaginary horse the secret that she never knew her father. Catherine, his wife, cannot say who her husband was. She was married on a tennis court by passing a few balls, she was married on an archery range by shooting a golden arrow through a cloud, she was married to a sailboat that lifted its wings and sailed away, to a porpoise and a star, to an iceberg, to a church bell, to a phantom whose face she never saw, to all the fading images of this world. Every fact about Jock is somebody else's guess, and no two guesses agree.

Ask the wrong question and Jock is simply underwritten, a minor dead husband the book never got around to fleshing out. Ask the right one and he is the theme carried to its far edge. The question Radical Immanence puts to him is not why is he so thin, but what is a person who is never present, never single, never known, assembled entirely from the outside? Every figure in this novel is a self that will not stay one. Jock is that idea pushed until it vanishes: a self so plural, so absent, that it never gathers into a body at all.

He is the other end of the scale from Mr. Spitzer, and the two of them frame the book's whole picture of the divided self. Spitzer gets the longest section in the novel to hold his split identity, the man you can watch pace around Boston in a cape, two watches in his pockets, unable to say whether he is himself or his dead twin. Jock gets one jumbled sentence. They prove the same thing by opposite means. Spitzer proves that a person is not one by being given more room than anyone and still not cohering. Jock proves it by never showing up, so that the only way to see him at all is to look at the shape of the hole he left in the people who loved him. One is the plural self drawn from the inside, at enormous length. The other is the plural self seen only as an absence, in a single line.

It would be easy to call his thinness a flaw and move on. The opposite is true. A Jock who was fully drawn, handed a face and a settled history and one dependable identity, would quietly contradict everything the novel believes about people. Instead he is drawn the way the book thinks everyone actually is, with the usual illusion of solidity stripped off: a set of images held in other minds, different in each mind, never the same twice, and gone the instant you reach past them for the man himself. The great lover, the book says of Catherine's phantom bridegroom, is always the absent one, one whom no one has ever known or loved. Jock is that sentence turned into a character.

And he does not stay dead in the tidy way the dead are supposed to. Catherine mourns him alongside Peron and the others, and in her dreaming world the husband she could not name blurs into the lover she could not keep, until the two dead men become one changing beloved she grieves without being able to tell them apart. It is her picture of how love works, aimed at a composite of the lost that keeps swapping its mask. Jock's part in that composite is to be the one who was already absent before he died, the husband who was a phantom at the altar, so that losing him to death only finished a disappearance that had started on their wedding day. He was gone before he was gone. That is how a woman who can barely remember he existed can mourn him so completely.

For Vera the absence cuts deeper, because it sits at her own root. The father is the answer to the oldest question about who she is, and he is the one blank the whole long book cannot fill. The novel never lets him resolve into a single knowable man, never even settles which dead man stands at her origin, the phantom husband or the lover in the next room, so that her beginning refuses, like everything else here, to be single and given. She is the daughter of an uncertainty in parentage. Her search for the dead, which fills the entire novel, starts from a father who was never there to lose in the first place.

The lesson for a writer is that a character does not have to appear in order to be present. Jock never enters a room, never speaks, is dead before the first page and was barely alive to anyone before that, and he still pulls on the story, because presence on the page is not the same as time on the page. It is the size and shape of the hole a person leaves in other people. A writer can build a figure out of nothing but the contradictory things others say about him and the space he takes up in their grief, and if the contradictions are precise enough, the absence itself becomes a person. Jock is the proof that the book can make, out of almost nothing, exactly the character it intends: a man who was never one man, never fully there, and mourned all the harder for it.
