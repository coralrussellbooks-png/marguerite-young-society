To All My Darlings

Chapter 62

On Chapter 62 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 9, 2026

← Chapter 61 · Home · Chapter 63 →

Summary

Mr. Spitzer wonders whether he has ever truly slept. He dreams of rowing down a river that turns to fire and then to ice, followed by a great water orchestra of seagulls turned musicians. By lamplight he rewrites his will, leaving all his scraps of music to the world, even to deaf and dead ears, on condition that it be played only on water; he notes that the old pack rat who steals his papers always leaves something in return. No great music, he decides, is ever played as written. He remembers an old horn-blower dying of tuberculosis who cannot stop blowing his horn, and a clarinetist who jumped from a Gloucester roof leaving a note that he had gone to join a greater orchestra. He remembers, too, learning that the silent violinist of the Library steps had played for twenty years in the Philadelphia Symphony without once touching the strings and was dismissed the day he played a real note. Crossing Louisburg Square, he asks where he is and is startled to hear: Boston. He thought he was in Philadelphia.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, Mr. Spitzer dreams of rowing down a river that transforms from molten fire to frozen ice, surrounded by fantastical imagery: old walruses, seals with diamond necklaces, seagulls turning into musicians, and a great water orchestra. This symbolizes the fluid, transformative nature of reality, music, and life itself, flowing, burning, freezing, and shimmering between states, much like the elusive nature of perfect sleep and death which he never attains. This dreamscape is laden with surreal and symbolic images that represent the merging of reality and illusion, life and death, music and silence.
  • Mr. Spitzer rewrites his will meticulously, with instructions reflecting his eccentric personality and his deep connection to his music and to the world around him. The will includes bequests of his musical works to the world “even to indifferent ears and even to deaf ears and even to dead ears and to sea conches”; this shows his acceptance of obscurity and his desire for his music to endure beyond his earthly life. The will also contains poetic and mysterious instructions, such as leaving secretive eggs and milk, and canceling subscriptions, which reflect his detachment from worldly affairs but attachment to ritual and symbolism. The will is an act of final artistic and existential expression, blending legal formality with poetic imagination.
  • Mr. Spitzer reflects on how music, like life, is fleeting and imperfect, filled with unplayable notes, paradoxes, and silent chords. He contemplates how his compositions incorporate sounds of nature, cosmic events, and the elusive music of the dead or music that can never be fully realized or heard. This passage explores the metaphysical nature of music as both creation and loss, as something that connects the living and the dead, the real and the imagined. He acknowledges that no music is ever played exactly as composed, and that silence may be the truest form of music, an idea that profoundly illustrates the fragility and mystery of artistic expression.
  • The story of the old horn-blower who has tuberculosis caused by his relentless playing of his instrument is a poignant metaphor for the physical and spiritual toll of devotion to art. Despite his suffering, the horn-blower cannot stop because music is both his love and his torment. Mr. Spitzer’s sympathy and sorrow for the horn-blower highlight the paradox of artistic creation that it can be both life-giving and life-destroying. The horn-blower’s determination to “blow on” even unto death embodies the tragic dedication of the artist.
  • Mr. Spitzer recounts the tragic suicide of a clarinetist who felt “out of tune” despite reassurances of his perfection. This introduces the theme of alienation and despair among musicians who cannot reconcile their inner experiences with external reality. The clarinetist’s note about joining a “greater orchestra” evokes the hope or belief in a transcendent musical realm beyond this life. It also inspires Mr. Spitzer’s thoughts about an orchestra that includes all lost and unheard music, suggesting an eternal, cosmic harmony that surpasses earthly limitations.
  • This surreal image of an old violinist silently playing an invisible violin with imaginary bow gestures symbolizes pure artistic expression divorced from physical constraints. Mr. Spitzer recognizes this as perhaps the “best music,” the music of silence, the unplayed and unplayable music that expresses the ineffable. The violinist’s story of having been a second violinist who never actually touched his strings, and who was dismissed after accidentally producing a “false note,” highlights themes of illusion, invisibility, and injustice in the artistic world. It represents the paradox of the artist’s life: the tension between visibility and invisibility, reality and performance, acceptance and rejection.
  • Mr. Spitzer muses on the nature of memory and time, suggesting that events must happen twice to be real, that memory is inherently inaccurate, and that past and present coexist in complex ways. This philosophical reflection ties into the themes of music and life as cyclical, ephemeral, and intertwined with illusion and reality. The idea that memories may come from an “alien source” adds a mystical dimension to his understanding of self and art.
  • In the closing sections, Mr. Spitzer contemplates the ultimate silence that surrounds all music and life. He considers silence not as mere absence but as a profound presence, “more silent than the grave.” Near the end Mr. Spitzer remembers almost applauding the silent music of the old violinist and his recognition that music may never fully be heard or understood but still holds profound beauty and meaning. The passage also touches on his existential confusion and isolation, highlighted by the moment he forgets where he is, symbolizing disconnection from reality.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • Mr. Spitzer leaves his music “even to indifferent ears and even to deaf ears and even to dead ears and to sea conches.” For whom is he really writing?
  • The violinist was dismissed “because once, once only, he had come over into reality.” What is Young saying about performance and truth?
  • “Death did not balance with life.” How does the language of bookkeeping work in this chapter?

Characters

  • The clarinetist leaves a note: “I am out of tune. I have gone to join a greater orchestra.” Why does Mr. Spitzer take an idea from it?
  • Mr. Spitzer cries “Blow on, horn-blower, blow on!” while “recognizing that he was negating himself.” Why does he contradict his own advice?
  • Mr. Spitzer wants “to show perfect justice to all parties, both the abuser and the abused,” even the pack rat. What does the pack rat’s trade teach him?

Connections

  • The horn-blower “had been usher to a dying king and now was both the usher and the king.” How does he carry on the usher of Chapter 59?
  • Of the Library steps violinist, Young now writes: “The man had been a member of a great orchestra.” How does this change the silent music of Chapter 58?
  • The chapter ends with “My God! I thought I was in Philadelphia.” How does this answer Chapter 61’s naming of Boston?

Reflection

  • Mr. Spitzer believes that “anything which was worth happening at all was worth happening twice.” Is something real only when it is repeated?
  • “Memory provided in retrospect that which had never occurred.” When does memory invent?
  • “From all his musical rags, future musicians must stitch together a musical coat.” What do the living make from what the dead leave?

Research: Resources

  • “Sometimes Mr. Spitzer wondered if he had ever really slept”: This is a richly symbolic and dreamlike meditation on the nature of sleep, death, and reality as experienced by Mr. Spitzer. It expresses his profound uncertainty about whether he has ever truly experienced perfect sleep or death, suggesting that both are illusions that elude him personally. This sets a tone of existential questioning and disorientation that pervades much of the novel. Mr. Spitzer’s dreams are vivid and surreal, featuring intense transformations of natural elements. These images evoke a fluid boundary between opposites such as life and death, warmth and cold, light and darkness. The river of fire and river of ice symbolize continual change and the merging of contradictions, reflecting the novel’s themes of flux and ambiguity. The imagery creates a haunting, otherworldly scene. The juxtaposition of natural arctic animals with opulent, human settings blends the real with the surreal, the living with the symbolic, underscoring the theme of blurred boundaries between reality and illusion. The final reflection that all these rivers might be one great river of reality flowing and transforming encapsulates the novel’s exploration of existence as a continuous, mutable process rather than fixed states. The image of a “burning star in the frozen cloud” as another star above ice pinnacles suggests a cosmic dimension to this ongoing transformation, linking the personal experience of Mr. Spitzer to universal cycles of life, death, and rebirth. This paragraph encapsulates key motifs of the novel: the elusive nature of sleep and death, the interplay of opposites and transformation, and the blending of dream imagery with reality to explore profound philosophical questions about existence. It conveys Mr. Spitzer’s inner turmoil and his attempt to make sense of a world where boundaries are fluid and certainty is impossible.
  • “brilliant as five-pointed Arcturus”: Arcturus is the brightest star in the northern constellation of Bootes. “Canst thou guide Arcturus with his sons?” (Job 38:32, KJV). See Astronomy and The Bible.
  • “old walruses sitting upon gilded chairs of the fin de siecle period”: Fin de siècle, “end of the century,” names the 1890s, with their sense of an age ending in elegance and decay. Compare the Walrus of Lewis Carroll’s “The Walrus and the Carpenter” (Through the Looking-Glass, 1871).
  • “members of some great orchestra following with violins and lutes painted with human faces”: The instruments serve as a rich symbolic network that conveys the novel’s deep meditation on the nature of existence, the limits of human understanding, the transient beauty of life and art, and the enduring but often silent music of the soul. The musical imagery ties together themes of memory, loss, death, creativity, and the eternal quest for meaning amidst chaos and silence. See Music and Instruments.
  • “glass harmonicas”: Benjamin Franklin invented the glass armonica in 1761, a row of nested glass bowls turning on a spindle and played with wet fingers; Mozart wrote for it in 1791. See Music and Instruments.
  • “for this was his swan song, his silent song, the swan being mute”: The mute swan is not truly mute; it hisses, snorts, and grunts. See the swan song in the Chapter 61 resources. See Birds.
  • “there were great fiddler crabs following the boats”: The male fiddler crab has one enormous claw, which he waves like a fiddler’s bow to court females and warn off rivals. See The Sea and Water.
  • “there was far away a viola da gamba faintly singing, preceding the violoncello”: Viola da gamba is a bowed, fretted string instrument that was popular during the Renaissance and Baroque periods and is held between the legs. It belongs to the viol family, is distinct from the cello, and typically has six or seven strings tuned in fourths with a major third in the middle. Key features include frets on the fingerboard to aid intonation, a softer and brighter sound than a cello, and a flat back with sloping shoulders.
    • Violoncello (or cello) is a large, bowed string instrument in the violin family, played sitting down with the instrument held between the knees.
    • See the viola da gamba in the Chapter 60 resources.
  • “flying naiads”: Naiads: In Greek mythology, the naiads, sometimes also hydriads, are a type of female spirit, or nymph, presiding over fountains, wells, springs, streams, brooks and other bodies of fresh water. See Greek Mythology.
  • “piccolos and hautboys”: Piccolo is a type of small, shrill woodwind instrument. The instrument is a small version of a flute played an octave higher.
    • Hautboy is an oboe or similar treble double reed instrument.
  • “running coach dogs running silently”: The Dalmatian was the coach dog, bred to trot beside or beneath carriages and guard the horses; fire engines kept the custom. See Dogs and Horses and Carriages.
  • “Hyperborean bees”: Hyperborean means a member of a people in Greek mythology who lived in perpetual sunshine. The Hyperboreans lived “beyond the North Wind,” Boreas. See Greek Mythology.
  • “many golden cockerels flapping their wings and crowing on singing weathervanes”: Compare Rimsky-Korsakov’s opera The Golden Cockerel (first performed 1909), whose golden bird crows warnings from its perch. See Music and Composers and Birds.
  • “phaeton shells”: Phaeton: Phaëton was the son of the sun god Helios (or Phoebus) in Greek mythology. He asked to prove his parentage by driving his father’s sun chariot, but his lack of skill caused the horses to run wild, burning the earth and creating deserts. Zeus eventually struck him with a thunderbolt to stop him. Horse-drawn light, open, four-wheeled carriage popular in the 18th and 19th centuries. It was owner-driven (no outside driver’s seat) and often had a folding top. An open-style car without fixed weather protection, similar to its horse-drawn predecessor. See Greek Mythology.
  • “the faded music of the harpsichord”: Harpsichord is a keyboard instrument that produces sound by plucking strings with a plectrum when a key is pressed, unlike the piano which strikes strings with hammers. It was a dominant keyboard instrument during the Renaissance and Baroque eras and is known for its distinctive sound. See the harpsichord in the Chapter 60 resources.
  • “the large-eyed squids staring at the paraselene”: Paraselene or Moon dog or mock moon is an atmospheric optical phenomenon which consists of a bright spot on one or both sides of a full moon.
    • Giant and colossal squids have the largest eyes of any animal, as big as dinner plates. See The Sea and Water.
  • “he was the great conductor conducting the water music”: Handel’s Water Music was first played on a barge on the Thames in July 1717, for King George I, who had it repeated three times. See Music and Composers.
  • “like an osculating star he crossed the face of the moon”: When the moon passes in front of a star, astronomers call it an occultation; Young’s “osculating” means kissing. See Astronomy.
  • “his largo”: Largo is an Italian term that means “slow and broad” and indicates a very slow tempo.
  • “his barcarolle which was his elegy”: Barcarolle is a folk song of Venetian gondoliers, which is imitated in classical music to create a gently rocking rhythm, often in 6/8 or 12/8 time. The most famous examples are Jacques Offenbach’s “Barcarolle” from the opera The Tales of Hoffmann and Frédéric Chopin’s Barcarolle, Op. 60 for solo piano. See On the barcarolle. See Music and Composers.
  • “his body should be draped in the American flag though he had never fought in any war”: In the United States the custom of covering a soldier’s coffin with the flag spread during the Civil War; the government still gives a burial flag to veterans. See The Civil War.
  • “instructions not to deliver the Boston Transcript”: The Boston Evening Transcript was a daily afternoon newspaper in Boston, Massachusetts, published from 1830 to 1941. It is no longer in publication, with its final issue printed on April 30, 1941.
    • T. S. Eliot satirized its readers in “The Boston Evening Transcript” (1915): “The readers of the Boston Evening Transcript / Sway in the wind like a field of ripe corn.” See American Writers.
  • “The International Stamp Collector”: And “The Society of Interplanetary Butterfly Lovers” These appear to be Young’s inventions, gently comic versions of the hobby journals and collectors’ societies that flourished in the early twentieth century. Stamp collecting and butterfly collecting were both popular pastimes of the era, and both suit Mr. Spitzer’s habit of preserving fragile, fading things.
  • “the secretive egg of some great bird like the sea gannet”: Sea gannet is a large seabird, most commonly referring to the Northern Gannet, known for its spectacular plunge-diving to catch fish. These birds are white with black and gold markings and spend their lives at sea, only coming to land to nest. See Birds.
  • “two quarts of Pasteurized milk”: Pasteurized milk is milk that has been heated to kill harmful bacteria and other pathogens, making it safe to drink without risk of serious illness from harmful microbes. This process, named after Louis Pasteur, involves heating milk to a specific temperature for a set time, such as 145 degrees for 30 minutes or 161 degrees for 15 seconds, before cooling it down and packaging it. Pasteurization significantly extends shelf life and is the gold standard for milk safety, with most commercially available milk being pasteurized.
  • “the Pledge of Allegiance”: Pledge of Allegiance is a patriotic recited verse that promises allegiance to the flag of the United States and the republic of the United States. The first version was written in 1885 by Captain George Thatcher Balch, a Union army officer in the Civil War who later authored a book on how to teach patriotism to children in public schools. In 1892, Francis Bellamy revised Balch’s verse as part of a magazine promotion surrounding the World’s Columbian Exposition, which celebrated the 400th anniversary of Christopher Columbus’ arrival in the Americas. Bellamy, the circulation manager for The Youth’s Companion magazine, helped persuade then-president Benjamin Harrison to institute Columbus Day as a national holiday and lobbied Congress for a national school celebration of the day. The magazine sent leaflets containing part of Bellamy’s Pledge of Allegiance to schools across the country and on October 21, 1892, over 10,000 children recited the verse together. Bellamy’s version of the pledge is largely the same as the one formally adopted by Congress 50 years later, in 1942. The official name of The Pledge of Allegiance was adopted in 1945. The most recent alteration of its wording came on Flag Day (June 14) in 1954, when the words “under God” were added. However, Bellamy’s authorship has been contested, as evidence has come out contradicting his claim. Several countries have a form of national pledge or oath, though the practice of daily, mandatory recitation in schools (as commonly associated with the US) is unusual by international standards. In many nations, oaths are typically reserved for specific occasions like citizenship ceremonies or for public officials.
  • “author of the music of the spheres”: See Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres.
  • “dance of the golden cock-of-the-rock”: The cock-of-the-rock is a real bird of South American cloud forests; the brilliant orange males gather at leks, where they dance and display to win the females. See Birds.
    • It is a metaphor for a powerful, transformative force, both earthly and cosmic that embodies life’s vitality, the passage of time, and the spiritual or mystical dimensions underlying existence. This interpretation aligns with the novel’s broader themes, where imagery of birds, especially cocks and roosters, recur as symbols of time, change, and the boundary between life and death. The golden cock-of-the-rock’s dance may thus represent the dynamic, sometimes mysterious forces that govern the characters’ experiences and the novel’s dreamlike atmosphere. The phrase symbolizes a potent, golden, life-affirming yet elusive force expressed through dance, evoking awakening, cosmic rhythms, and transformation within the novel’s complex symbolic universe.
  • “graduate of the New England Conservatory of Music”: The New England Conservatory of Music (NEC) was founded in Boston, Massachusetts, in 1867 by Eben Tourjée, making it the oldest independent music school in the United States. From its beginnings in a few rented rooms above the Boston Music Hall, the conservatory grew into a major music institution with a celebrated venue, Jordan Hall, which was granted National Historic Landmark status jointly with the NEC in 1994. Over its history, NEC has become a global leader in music education, known for its innovative approach and for attracting influential musicians as faculty and students.
  • “North, Northwest of the Great Bear”: Northwest of Ursa Major, the Great Bear, is the constellation Camelopardalis. Camelopardalis is a large, faint constellation that borders the northwestern part of the Great Bear. See Astronomy.
  • “his brother’s milky blue stone ring and ticking jeweled wrist watch”: The right hand that signs the lawyer’s will wears Peron’s ring and Peron’s ticking watch, while the left hand signs as the musician; the twins divide Mr. Spitzer’s body between them. See Twins and Doubles.
  • “Harvard graduate magna cum laude”: Harvard University’s history began in 1636 when the Massachusetts Bay Colony founded it as New College to train clergy. It was renamed Harvard College in 1639 after its first major benefactor, clergyman John Harvard, and has since grown into the oldest institution of higher learning in the United States, renowned for its academic prestige, wealth, and influence.
    • At Harvard, graduating magna cum laude is a distinction awarded to students with “great distinction,” typically those in the top 15% of their class. It is a Latin honor determined by a combination of departmental recommendations for High or Highest Honors and college-wide GPA cutoffs, which are set annually.
  • “never seen at a Pop concert in the Great Shell”: Pop concert in the Great Shell may refer to Edward A. Hatch Memorial Shell, commonly referred to as the Hatch Shell, is an outdoor concert venue on the Charles River Esplanade in the Back Bay section of Boston, Massachusetts. Built in 1939-1940, it is one of the city’s prominent examples of Art Deco architecture. The Hatch Shell is best known for hosting the Boston Pops annually for the Boston Fourth of July celebration, but is also used for free concerts most weekends and many weeknights during the summer months. The grass pavilion in front of the stage has no permanent seating. There is a memorial nearby to Arthur Fiedler, first permanent conductor of the Pops.
    • Arthur Fiedler began the free Esplanade concerts in 1929 in an earlier wooden shell.
  • “the pack rat who lined his papery nest”: The pack rat, or wood rat, is also called the trade rat, because it often drops what it is carrying to pick up something shinier, seeming to leave a fair exchange. See The Pack Rat.
  • “greater than Haydn’s Creation”: Haydn’s Creation or The Creation is an oratorio written by Haydn and considered to be one of his masterpieces. It is a celebration of the creation of the world as in the Book of Genesis. See Genesis.
  • “Johann Strauss’s Boston Dreams”: Johann Strauss’s Boston Dreams may refer to 1872 when Strauss visited America and Boston built a stadium that sat 100,000 people.
    • The 1872 event was Patrick Gilmore’s World’s Peace Jubilee, whose Coliseum was said to hold 100,000; Strauss conducted “The Blue Danube” there with an orchestra of more than a thousand.
  • “Gustave Pick’s Fiacre Song”: Gustave Pick’s Fiacre Song may refer to Gustav Pick’s Fiakerlied which is a Viennese song. Pick wrote it in 1885, the song of a Viennese cab driver proud of his horses.
  • “old trot, trot sheet music”: The “trot, trot” is the cab horse’s gait in Pick’s Fiakerlied, the song of a Viennese fiacre driver. See Horses and Carriages.
    • “old trot, trot sheet music” may refer to Trot, an old-style Korean pop music genre that emerged in the early 1900s, characterized by a repetitive, simple beat and ornamented, emotive vocals. It was influenced by Western dance styles like the foxtrot, Japanese enka, and Korean folk melodies, blending them into a unique style that expresses emotions like sadness, endurance, and hope.
  • “songs for the gondolieri”: Gondolieri is the Italian word for the gondola boatmen of Venice.
    • Compare Gilbert and Sullivan’s The Gondoliers (1889).
  • “he was that poor skeleton sleeping in the oyster bed”: See the skeleton with a missing rib in the Chapter 59 resources.
  • “without expectations of emoluments”: Emoluments: a salary, fee, or profit from employment or office.
  • “given unto the generations”: The public domain consists of all the creative work to which no exclusive intellectual property rights apply. Those rights may have expired, been forfeited, expressly waived, or may be inapplicable. There has been recent criticism of the current copyright laws in the US in that they do not help the public as they were meant to do but stifle creativity and scientific progress. One of the most famous examples is the copyrighted song Happy Birthday to You which a US Judge ruled invalid in 2015 (after 1964).
  • “given by his mortmain, his dead hand”: Mortmain is a French term meaning “dead hand,” is a legal concept referring to the perpetual, inalienable ownership of land by a corporation, such as a church or charity. Historically, this practice was disfavored because it removed land from the feudal system and deprived lords of valuable feudal incidents. To curb this, Statutes of Mortmain were enacted to regulate or prohibit the transfer of land to corporations without a license. See On mortmain.
  • “the constellation Orion”: Orion: Orion is a constellation visible globally and features the famous Orion’s Belt. The Orion Nebula, a stellar nursery within it, is one of the brightest in the sky. In Greek myth, Orion was a giant and mighty hunter. His death story varies, but most legends involve his death by the bow of Artemis or a scorpion’s sting, after which he was placed in the heavens. See On Orion, Taurus, the Pleiades, the Hyades. See Astronomy and Greek Mythology.
    • Taurus: In astrology, Taurus (April 20-May 20) is the second sign of the zodiac, representing the bull and associated with being grounded, practical, and sensual. As a constellation, it’s one of the oldest and most recognizable in the night sky, visible in the Northern Hemisphere and home to the Pleiades and Hyades star clusters.
    • Pleiades: also known as the Seven Sisters, refers to both a prominent star cluster in the constellation Taurus and the seven daughters of Atlas and Pleione in Greek mythology. The star cluster is about 100 million years old and consists of thousands of stars, with six or seven visible to the naked eye. Its bright blue stars and visibility in the winter night sky have made it a significant feature in cultures around the world for centuries.
    • Hyades: the mythological sisters were associated with rain, and after their brother Hyas died, they were transformed into the star cluster, which also became known as “the Rainy Ones.” The star cluster is the closest one to Earth, appears as a V-shape that forms the face of Taurus, and includes the bright star Aldebaran (though Aldebaran is not a true member).
    • See the Pleiades in the Chapter 60 resources.
  • “east of almighty Taurus like a great wentletrap”: Wentletrap: Wentletraps are small, often white, very high-spired, predatory or ectoparasitic sea snails, marine gastropod mollusks in the family Epitoniidae. The word wentletrap originated in Dutch, and it means spiral staircase. These snails are sometimes also called “staircase shells,” and “ladder shells.” Precious wentletraps once sold for great sums, and Chinese counterfeits are said to have been molded from rice paste. See The Sea and Water.
  • “pearl-bordered fritillaries”: Fritillaries are a type of butterfly and a genus of flowering plants known for their checkered patterns. See Butterflies and Moths.
  • “his coronachs”: Coronachs are a song or hymn of mourning composed or performed as a memorial to a dead person.
    • “He is gone on the mountain, / He is lost to the forest” (Sir Walter Scott, “Coronach,” The Lady of the Lake, 1810). See Romantic Poets.

  • “old Siberian kings”: Old Siberian kings: There was no unified “Siberian monarchy” or single line of “Siberian kings” in the modern sense. Instead, Siberian rulers were organized into various khanates, with the most prominent being the Khanate of Sibir, ruled by competing dynasties like the Taibugids and Shaybanids. The most famous “Siberian king” in this context was Khan Kuchum, the last ruler of the Khanate of Sibir, who was defeated by the Cossack Yermak Timofeyevich, leading to the Russian conquest of the region. The House of Sibirsky was the foremost of many Genghisid families formerly living in Russia. It traced its descent from Kuchum, the last of the Siberian khans. Kuchum’s sons were captured by Yermak’s Cossacks and brought to Muscovy, where they were settled in Yaroslavl and other towns and were authorized to style themselves Tsarevichs of Siberia. Kuchum’s grandson Alp-Arslan was even installed as a puppet khan of Kasimov between 1614 and 1627. In 1591, the son of Kuchum, Abul Khayir was the first of his dynasty to convert to Christianity. His conversion was followed by the conversion of his entire family who eventually assimilated into the Russian nobility. In 1686, the tsar decreed that the dynasties of the king of Imeretia in the Caucasus along with the princes of Siberia and Kasimov were to be entered into the Genealogical Book of the Russian nobility. The tsarevichs married into the best Russian families. One of their princesses was the wife of Peter the Great’s maternal uncle, another married a son of the Georgian king.

Born in 1819, she lived at Kensington Palace, had few companions her own age, and was not allowed to be alone. Despite her controlled childhood, she pursued hobbies like painting, drawing, writing in her diary, and singing.

It began to lean by 1178 after the third floor was completed, due to a weak, shallow foundation on soft, sandy soil. Construction halted for nearly a century, a delay that allowed the soil to settle and prevented its early collapse. The tower was completed in 1372 after architects attempted to compensate for the lean by making the upper floors taller on one side. Modern efforts in the 1990s stabilized the tower and reduced its tilt to about 4 degrees, a lean that is now intentionally preserved.

The empire’s history is defined by significant expansion, internal reforms, and eventual collapse during the Russian Revolution of 1917, which was exacerbated by its participation in World War I.

The sky or the heavens, imagined as a solid dome overhead, as in Genesis 1.
Forfend
To ward off; to forbid.
Freighter
A ship that carries cargo.
Fritillary
A butterfly with orange wings checked with black, often silver-spotted beneath.
Fugacious
Fleeting; falling soon.
Fugue
A composition in which one theme is taken up in turn by several voices; also a flight from one’s own identity.
Gannet
A large white seabird that dives for fish.
Gargoyle
A carved grotesque figure on a building, often serving as a waterspout.
Glacial
Icy; like a glacier.
Griffin
A mythical beast with the head and wings of an eagle and the body of a lion.
Hallucinatory
Like a hallucination; seeming real but not there.
Harpsichord
A keyboard instrument, ancestor of the piano, whose strings are plucked rather than struck.
Hautboy
An old name for the oboe.
Helm
The wheel or tiller that steers a ship.
Ice floe
A sheet of floating ice.
Infinitesimal
Extremely small.
Inkhorn
A small portable ink pot, once made of horn.
Lagoon
A stretch of shallow water separated from the sea by a reef or sandbar.
Largo
In music, very slow and broad.
Lute
A plucked string instrument with a rounded back shaped like half a pear, popular in Europe from the Middle Ages through the 1700s.
Mica
A mineral that splits into thin, shiny, nearly transparent sheets and makes rock glitter.
Moonstone
A milky, pearly-blue gemstone.
Mortmain
“Dead hand”; ownership that can never be passed on.
Mother-of-pearl
The shining, rainbow-colored lining of some shells, used for inlay.
Mundane
Ordinary; of this world.
Naiad
A nymph of springs, streams, and fountains.
Nocturne
A dreamy piece of music suggesting the night.
Oilskin
Cloth made waterproof with oil; a raincoat of it.
Opalescence
A milky, shifting iridescence like that of an opal.
Opalescent
Shimmering with changing colors, like an opal.
Overture
An orchestral piece that opens an opera or other work.
Pagoda
An Asian tiered tower with multiple eaves, usually a Buddhist temple or shrine.
Pall
A cloth spread over a coffin; anything dark that covers.
Paradox
A statement or situation that seems to contradict itself and yet may be true.
Parallelogram
A four-sided figure whose opposite sides are parallel.
Paraselene
A mock moon; a bright spot beside the moon seen through thin cloud.
Pariah
An outcast.
Pavane
A slow, stately court dance.
Perigee
The point in an orbit nearest the earth.
Phaeton
A light, open, four-wheeled carriage; also a name for some seashells.
Pinnacle
The highest point; a tall, pointed mountain peak or the slender spire on a building.
Plaintive
Sad and mournful in sound.
Plumed
Decorated with feathers.
Precarious
Unstable and likely to fall or fail.
Purlieus
Outskirts; haunts.
Quinquevalent
Having five valences; Young’s music of five roads.
Quitclaim
A legal release of a claim or right.
Requiem
A Mass or song for the dead.
Retort
A glass vessel with a long bent neck, used by alchemists for distilling.
Retrospect
A looking back on the past.
Saffron
Orange-yellow, the color of the spice.
Sand dollar
A flat, round sea urchin whose white skeleton is found on beaches.
Scalloped
Edged with a series of curves like the rim of a scallop shell.
Segno
A sign in written music marking where a repeat begins or ends.
Sentinel
A guard or watchman.
Sequin
A small shining disk sewn on clothing; first the zecchino, a Venetian gold coin.
Sonorous
Deep and full in sound.
Spume
Foam or froth on the sea.
Steeplejack
One who climbs and repairs steeples and chimneys.
Surrey
A light four-wheeled horse-drawn carriage with two seats, often with a fringed canopy on top.
Tallow
Animal fat used for candles and soap. Tallow-colored is a dull, yellowish white.
Tenuto
A mark telling the player to hold a note its full value.
Thimble
A small cap worn on the finger to push the needle in sewing.
Tine
A prong or point, as of an antler or fork.
Translucence
The quality of letting light pass through without being clear.
Tumultuous
Loud, wild, and disorderly.
Turret
A small tower on a building or castle.
Vaporous
Full of mist or vapor.
Viola da gamba
The bass viol, an old fretted string instrument held between the knees.
Weathervane
A turning device on a roof that shows the direction of the wind.
Wentletrap
A sea snail whose shell winds like a spiral staircase.
Whaler
A whaling ship or a man who hunts whales.
Zenith
The highest point.
