Three Things Miss MacIntosh Carries

Some of the most important things in this novel are things you could hold in your hand. A red wig. A black umbrella. A looking glass. Each one belongs first to Miss MacIntosh, and each one passes, in a different form, to the other people in the book. Young built her nursemaid partly from life. She told an interviewer in 1983 that Miss MacIntosh was a composite of Indianapolis spinsters, one of whom wore a wig and kept a spare (American Academy of Poets interview), and Susan Neville's profile names her: a Miss Alexander, who wore a spare wig to keep her head warm (Neville profile).

Flipping the Cave follows the night Vera finds her bald, and Survival in the Fragile House follows her history of the umbrella. This essay looks at the three objects themselves: what each one shelters, and what each one costs.

The Wig as a Mourning Ring

Young called the wig the illusion itself, “the dream by which she lived” (interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT, 1965). In the WNYC interview she went further: Miss MacIntosh “is really a bald prince, disguised with a red wig and the woman's gear” (WNYC interview). And she told Duncan MacDonald that the missing hair was “the symbol of the dream and of the feminine in life” the nursemaid was never given (Observation Point, 1965).

But the novel adds something the interviews leave out. The wigs are also a way of remembering the dead. In Chapter 63, bald before Vera, Miss MacIntosh says of “this poor, burned wig which was purchased from an ordinary wig-maker’s”: “There have been several such, in fact, and of various shades, and I was thinking of the grey. Perhaps my vanity was always too great. The red was my poor brother Richard’s color. You may remember him. It is probable that he lost his head. There was also the black in memory of yet another one who disappeared.” In Chapter 65, as Vera tells it, the black was for Lorena: “Lorena’s hair, Miss MacIntosh said, was black—and Lorena had already left her.” And the red she wore “in memory of her brother Richard who had disappeared among the islands of Oceania.”

So the red hair that stood for plain good sense through the first half of the novel was a mourning color all along, worn for a lost brother, the way people once wore a lock of the dead in a ring. The woman who told Vera never to dream wore her grief on her head every day. The wig hides one truth and carries another.

And it could not be undone. In Chapter 64 she tells Vera, “I have no hair anywhere on my body. I am a hairless woman,” and “A barber in Chicago told me he could do nothing.” Young took the scene from life. She asked her own hairdresser whether a bald woman had ever come in, and got the answer she gave the barber in the book: “I couldn't grow one hair on your head for a million dollars, and if I could, I wouldn't need a million dollars” (Terkel interview). Miss MacIntosh's own verdict follows: “Bald I came into this world, and bald I will go out of it, little doubt. I will be lucky if I can take my umbrella or have even my shadow where I step.”

The Umbrella and the Shadow

That last line ties the wig to the umbrella. In Chapter 17, when Vera first sees her bald, Miss MacIntosh has “not one hair to cast its shadow.” In Chapter 64 she explains why she carries the umbrella: “So I chose the fog,” she said, “because the fog was like feathers, angels protecting this poor angel who had no feathers, and I chose my black umbrella to cast its shadow in my walk.” Then: “May I never lose my umbrella, for my umbrella is my friend,” she continued, “and what should I be if I should lose my shadow, if I should have no shadow here?”

The woman who casts no shadow of her own carries one with her. Young knew the practical side of this. Light burns a hairless woman's skin, she said, so Miss MacIntosh carries the umbrella even on sunny days, and the umbrella “casts a shadow around her and forbids intimacy” (WNYC interview). She carried it “indoors and out,” as a woman “avidly normal” (Observation Point, 1965). Young called it “a kind of canopy between her and reality” (Terkel, 1965). It is shelter, and it is distance, and it is the borrowed shadow that lets her pass for a woman like any other.

In Chapter 3 the sea gives it back: “her broken black umbrella beating like a bird’s wounded wing at the edge of the sea which had returned it.” Young said she wrote the sea-walk with Virginia Woolf's drowning in mind, and that an editor who had known Woolf told her Woolf “had left her walking stick at the tide's end” (Observation Point, 1965). It comes back once more in Chapter 68: “A torn black umbrella was blowing like a crow against the surf.”

Every Woman's Umbrella

The umbrella is not Miss MacIntosh's alone. Catherine has a white one. In Chapter 39, never leaving her room yet “always travelling,” she is “sometimes raising her white umbrella over her like a cloud.” In Chapter 41, among the things her visitors leave behind, “An emperor’s attendant left the emperor’s white umbrella, billowing like a white bird in the wind.” In the old courts of Siam and Burma, the white umbrella was the sign of the king. Miss MacIntosh's black umbrella keeps the world off. Catherine's white one crowns her in a world of her own making.

Cousin Hannah turns the parasol into a weapon. In Chapter 32 the suffrage captain, looking at the society ladies who used their frilled parasols for flirtation, “had urged woman to use her parasol as her sword or her shield, her greatest weapon of offense or defense,” and “to fly her skirts as her banners in her war against tyrannical man.” And in Chapter 63 Miss MacIntosh gives the umbrella to everyone, in her history of Jonas Hanway, the umbrella that once shaded only an emperor “might now be carried by each man over himself.” Three women, three umbrellas: one for keeping the world away, one for ruling a dream, one for fighting.

The Mirror That Was Not Looked Into

The third object is the one Miss MacIntosh avoids. In Chapter 17, Vera sees her bald head as “a great mirror where one should see one’s self if one looked closely and did not lose one’s mind.” In Chapter 18, Miss MacIntosh tells Vera that what she has seen “was nothing another should see. She had never seen it herself except in a mirror.” And in Chapter 22: “For many years now, she had never looked into a looking glass. She could not remember the last time she had seen herself.”

When the truth is out, in Chapter 63, her first cry is for the mirrors: “Mirrors, mirrors! Drape all the mirrors with black. Cover me over again.” Mirrors in a house of mourning were covered, by Jewish custom and in Victorian homes. Miss MacIntosh mourns herself.

Catherine lives the other way, among mirrors. In Chapter 39 her house “continually extended like the idea of infinity,” “and space was the illusion in a frameless mirror,” “every room being but the partial reflection of every other.” In Chapter 35, her selves are “these many, beautiful, waxen ladies parading in a room of mirrors and phantoms, each but the partial portrait of the other so that each was incomplete.” Cousin Hannah, by contrast, had “seemingly included no such multiple mirror images and imaginary loves.” One woman will not look. One lives inside an endless hall of reflections. One seems to have no reflections at all.

And the mirror can break a person in two. In Chapter 6, on the bus, Madge, pregnant and frightened: “She was breaking into two, herself and the other, this face unknown. She was this sad, unfamiliar reflection in her powdery looking glass, this other face which smiled.” Mr. Spitzer, whose twin is his living mirror, once walked straight through a pane of glass he never saw. In Chapter 27 he is proud “that he had walked through a wall of invisible glass, doing great harm, no doubt, to the wall but none, in the last analysis, to him.”

Young described her own method in the same terms. Late in life she told Susan Neville, “I'm a mirror” (Neville profile). The novel holds up a glass to each of its people, and none of them sees only one face.

The Radical Immanence Reading

In the language of Radical Immanence, the wig and the umbrella are Pillar 2 (Reality Is Constructed): the plainest woman in the book is built and kept up by things bought from an ordinary wig-maker and carried in the hand. The red wig worn for a lost brother and the black one for Lorena are Pillar 3 (Grief Doesn't End): she wears her dead on her head every day. And the mirrors, Catherine's rooms each “but the partial reflection of every other,” Madge “breaking into two,” are Pillar 1 (Not One Self).
