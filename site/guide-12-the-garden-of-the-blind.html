Chapter 12: The Garden of the Blind

Symbols: Clocks and Watches · Eyes and Blindness · Keys, Doors, and Locks

Chapter 12 is the longest and most important chapter we’ve had in a while, and it ends on a sentence that will change how you read everything that comes after it.

Stay with this one. It earns its length.

What actually happens in Chapter 12

It is past ten o’clock at night. Vera is in her bedroom, kept company by Miss MacIntosh’s alarm clock: the one reliable clock in a house where all the others have been stopped. Restless and lonely, she gets up, picks up Miss MacIntosh’s Coca-Cola fan, and eventually slips out of the house into the night.

She wanders into the garden and finds herself, without having planned it, in a very particular place: a garden her mother’s father planted long ago for his blind wife. A garden designed not to be seen but to be touched and smelled. Vera closes her eyes and moves through it with her fingers, feeling the petals and stamens and whorls of flowers she cannot name in the dark.

In this heightened, strange state (joyful, almost ecstatic, certain of the reality and beauty of the world) her mind opens into a long meditation on Miss MacIntosh. What Miss MacIntosh taught her. What she was like. What it meant to have her. And then, at the very end of the chapter, a sentence arrives that tells you something enormous is about to happen.

Young is preparing you. Chapter 13 will deliver what Chapter 12 has been building toward.

The clocks, and what they mean

Before Vera goes into the garden, Young gives us two kinds of clocks, and the contrast between them is one of the most precise things in the novel.

Miss MacIntosh’s clock is Old Reliable: plain, practical, no carved figures, no weeping ladies, no sporting cupids. It keeps accurate time. It does “not repeat the hours, a day being gone when a day was gone.” It was made for servants, Vera tells us, for people who must actually wake up and do things at a specific hour.

“All the other clocks in the house” have been stopped, each one frozen at a different hour from the long period when Catherine gradually retreated from the world. Sometimes, deep in the night, one of them shudders awake and strikes (three bells here, four bells there, from distant corners) but the darkness does not lift.

This is Young telling you, with great economy, exactly what the house is: a place where time has been refused. Where a woman decided, slowly and not without hesitation, to stop. Miss MacIntosh’s one wound clock is the only thing in the entire house insisting that time is real and moving and will require something of you in the morning.

The garden of the blind

The garden Vera wanders into is one of the most beautiful and strange settings in the novel. Catherine’s father built it for his wife, who was blind. Not a garden to look at, but a garden to move through by touch and smell: odorous blooms, textured petals, flowers chosen for their whorls and stamens and the way they move under a moving finger.

His wife, we are told, was the most immediate of persons. Practical. Unimaginative. She used to walk a circular path in the garden, and it was for her simply a household habit, nothing poetic about it. She argued with her coachman about whether it looked like rain. She chose clothes in muted, inconspicuous colors. She had no interest in art, until she was blind, and then she traced the stone faces in the garden with her fingers, and the lips and nostrils seemed to move.

Vera steps into this garden at the height of her happiness, certain that the world is real and beautiful and made for human senses to inhabit. She closes her own eyes and touches the flowers. And Young quietly notes: this “first vision of the wonders and glories of this planet” would also be the last.

Something is coming.

What Vera remembers about Miss MacIntosh

The center of the chapter is a long, spiraling memory of what Miss MacIntosh was and what she did for Vera.

At seven years old, Vera was, in Miss MacIntosh’s words, a nervous wreck. “She was baying at the moon.” Pretending to be a dog. Digging for bones in the garden. She believed she was the reincarnation of Chopin. She had a wireless attached to her head through which she heard the voices of the dead. She held conversations with a gentleman in a snuff-colored velvet coat who carried a music box full of buzzing bees.

Miss MacIntosh solved most of these problems by ignoring them. Where she could not ignore them, she applied firm, practical remedies: box a sandbag dummy, run a mile on the beach, restore your circulation and your waning common sense. She held Miss MacIntosh’s thimble over Vera’s forehead (the one engraved with the word Juliet, 1899, though who Juliet was or why 1899 we are never told) and tapped it authoritatively.

She taught Vera “to admit, quite simply, that nothing had happened, that not a leaf had fallen,” not a blow had struck, not a wave had broken. That “omniscience had not been given to us, fortunately,” because if we could see everything “we should all most certainly lose our minds.”

And it worked. Miss MacIntosh held Vera’s “attention by yelling louder than the dead.” That is the sentence Young gives us, and it is both funny and heartbreaking: the only thing that could compete with Vera’s hallucinations was a very loud, very matter-of-fact woman from What Cheer, Iowa.

Miss MacIntosh’s philosophy, in her own words

This chapter gives us the most extended portrait of Miss MacIntosh’s worldview we’ve had yet.

She believed that God, having created this world somewhat erroneously and filled with remorse ever since, does “not ask us to understand His traffic.” We should attend to our own knitting. We are plain. “We were intended for no high” office, “no imaginary diadem, no fine feathers.” The wisest man is a fool in God’s eyes. We are here to endure ourselves, not to test God.

On love, she can only guess. Love is “not an inquiry beyond what meets the natural eye.” It is greatest when it sees another’s face in daylight and hears another’s voice. She is, she admits without self-pity, “a plain, old-fashioned, unvarnished spinster, her” shirtwaist bristling with needles and pins “to forbid inquiry and to suggest that hers was not a good shoulder to weep on.”

And on the question of time and its passing: “she would not give a pin for” the suggestion that time passes through us rather than us through it. When something is gone, it is gone. She kept her feet planted on earth, which she acknowledged was not solid, and she let her head drift nowhere.

She had only one face. Not two: one for the world, one for heaven. Only one.

Hold that. It matters enormously in Chapter 13.

The sentence at the end

The chapter closes with Vera’s mind circling back to Mr. Spitzer, and her mother’s old suspicion that he sometimes imagines “himself to be his dead brother.” And then the chapter simply ends, mid-thought, carrying you directly into Chapter 13.

Chapter 13 begins: “Farewell to the last certitude, even that tender night of my fourteenth birthday when I trembled on the margin of sentient life—farewell to Miss MacIntosh.”

Something happened in that garden. Something Vera saw. And Young has spent all of Chapter 12 making sure you understand what Miss MacIntosh meant to Vera before she shows you what Vera found.

Don’t stop here. Chapter 13 is why this chapter exists.
