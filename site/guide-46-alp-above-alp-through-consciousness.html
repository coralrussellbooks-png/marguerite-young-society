Chapter 46: Alp Above Alp Through Consciousness

Research notes: Chapter 46

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Love · Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos

Symbols: Clothing, Hats, and Skirts · Fog and Mist · Moon and Stars · Mountains · Music and Instruments

Allusions: Romantic Poets · The History of Opium · Women’s Suffrage

There is an alpenhorn in Chapter 46. It is twice Cousin Hannah's height. Mr. Spitzer, as her executor, takes it, he collects musical instruments, and carries it away from her death chamber into a flat, fog-bound city where it cannot do what it was built to do. An alpenhorn requires mountains. It requires the specific acoustic landscape of the Alps, the way its call bounces off stone walls of ice and carries across valleys that have no equivalent anywhere else on earth. In a coastal lowland, blown by one old lawyer with a silk hat and a second-remove relationship to life, it is a beautiful and useless and entirely right thing.

Chapter 46 is about what we do with the instruments of dead people. Their tools, their methods, their causes, their love: how we carry these things into landscapes where they no longer fit, because we cannot put them down.

What actually happens in Chapter 46

Mr. Spitzer, now acting as Cousin Hannah's executor, sorts through the aftermath of her life with the thoroughness and the grief that the role requires. He takes the alpenhorn. He reflects on what she was: not only a suffrage captain, not only a political figure, but a climber of mountains in the most literal and most metaphorical sense, peaks greater than peaks, Alp above Alp through consciousness, the ascent continuing even after the body gave out.

He wanders afterward through a fog-bound city asking strangers if they have seen her. People take him for mad. He is, in the specific way the novel has always used madness, more precisely sane than the people who regard him with concern. He is doing what grief requires: looking for the person who is gone in every face that isn't hers, asking the question that has no answer because asking it is the only honest response to the loss.

A steeplejack answers him. This tall figure perched high on some ecclesiastical structure, comfortable at heights where most people cannot go, speaks to Mr. Spitzer in paradoxes: “I live because I am dead.” I love because I love not. “All who have loved have died” and drowned. Time is a cloud crossing a clock face that tells no time. The chapter closes with Cousin Hannah's final cry from the deathbed, resurfacing here as the chapter's last image: “Ah, bright star of my love, find your way to the lightless star. Find your way to one who has neither wings nor light. For I am dying now, and” my love goes down.

The alpenhorn in the lowlands

Young's choice of the alpenhorn as Cousin Hannah's instrument, the thing Mr. Spitzer inherits from her death chamber, is characteristically precise.

The alpenhorn is the instrument of distance and altitude. Historically it was used to communicate across Alpine valleys, to call cattle home from high pastures, to announce arrivals and departures across distances that voices could not reach. It is five to twelve feet long. It is made of wood. It produces a sound that is part call and part echo, built for an acoustic environment that most of the world does not have.

In a flat coastal landscape, held by an old lawyer who has never climbed a mountain, it can make a sound but not the sound it was built to make. And yet Mr. Spitzer takes it. He carries the impossible instrument of Cousin Hannah's inner life into a world that cannot receive it properly, because what else do you do? You cannot leave the alpenhorn behind. The alpenhorn is the cause, the love, the aspiration that was Cousin Hannah's and is now, by the accident of survival, his to carry.

This is Young's image for every inheritance of a great life: you receive the instrument. You carry it into your own landscape. You cannot produce the original sound. You try anyway.

Mountains of consciousness

The chapter's opening description of Cousin Hannah as a climber of mountains greater than mountains (peaks of consciousness, ascent continuing Alp above Alp even past death) is one of Young's most concentrated formulations of what the novel means by greatness.

Physical mountains are the obvious correlative: the Matterhorn, Mont Blanc, the Wetterhorn, the Monch, peaks Cousin Hannah is said to have climbed, each one a formidable achievement. But Young immediately escalates: the real mountains were interior. They were the mountains of consciousness: the problems of identity and freedom and love and the nature of reality that Cousin Hannah climbed all her life, without equipment, without a guide, in weather conditions that would have turned back anyone with better judgment.

Nan Shepherd's meditation on the living mountain, the insistence that to aim for the highest point is not the only way to climb, is the counterpoint here. Cousin Hannah aimed for the highest point. Every time. That was her gift and her wound. She could not imagine walking around. She could only imagine going up, and so she fell great distances, and so the distances she fell were proportional to the heights she reached, and so the achievement and the loss were the same scale.

The steeplejack's paradoxes

The steeplejack is one of Young's most quietly extraordinary figures: a person whose profession requires living at heights others cannot reach, maintaining the structures that point upward while everyone below walks on level ground. He appears at the moment when Mr. Spitzer is wandering in grief, asking strangers about the dead woman, and he answers in the only language that the novel has been building toward: paradox.

“I live because I am dead.” I love because I love not.

These are not riddles. They are exact descriptions of states the novel has been mapping since Chapter 1. To live because you are dead: Mr. Spitzer, who has been at a second remove from life since his twin died, who has been waiting to prove that he himself died long ago, who persists without quite living; he lives in this sense, animated by the negation at his center. “I love because I love not”: Cousin Hannah, whose love was defined by its suppression, whose devotion to the cause was the form her unexpressed love took. You love most fiercely what you cannot or will not claim. The refusal is itself a form of love.

Time is a cloud crossing a clock face that tells no time. Young has been building toward this formulation for forty-six chapters. Time in this novel is never the reliable sequential progression that clock-faces propose. It is weather passing over an instrument that was built to measure something it cannot actually contain. The cloud crosses. The clock registers nothing. This is the time Catherine lives in, and Mr. Spitzer, and anyone who has been genuinely inside grief or love or the kind of consciousness that does not move in lines.

The sterility that begets all beautiful things

Mr. Spitzer's meditation on the power of dead beauty, the sterility which begets all beautiful things, is one of the chapter's most philosophically concentrated passages.

The idea has a long lineage. Keats wrote about negative capability, the capacity to remain in uncertainty without irritably reaching after fact and reason, as the condition for great art. The Romantics understood that beauty is inseparable from mortality, that what makes a thing beautiful is in part its transience, its impossibility of being held. Young takes this further: the sterility that begets. Not despite the absence of fertility, but because of it. The dead love, the love that was never consummated, the cause that was never completed; these beget things precisely because they were not finished. Completion closes. Incompletion keeps the door open.

Cousin Hannah's love, killed by false modesty, is more alive in Mr. Spitzer's consciousness than any love that was lived out and resolved and put away. The dead beauty has power that the living beauty never had, because the living beauty becomes ordinary and the dead beauty remains absolute.

Ghosts always clothed

Young offers, in Chapter 46, a curious observation: ghosts in dreams never appear naked. They are always dressed, always clothed in some form, always wearing something. This applies to the living as much as the dead. Identity is always layered. There is no unmediated self available for inspection, no moment when the mask is fully removed and the truth of a person stands bare.

This is not pessimism. It is the novel's honest accounting of what persons are: not naked truths but layered presences, the accumulated costume of a life. Cousin Hannah's iron keys and whaler's hat and rubber coat. Catherine's opium dreams and satin pillows. Mr. Spitzer's silk hat. These are not disguises over a real self; they are as real as anything underneath them. What you put on is what you are, and also more than what you are, and also less.

The ghost appears in the dream wearing the clothes of their life. This is how we know them. This is how we recognize the dead when they come back: by what they were wearing.

Prufrock in the fog

To All My Darlings notes that Mr. Spitzer's existential struggle closely parallels Eliot's The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock: the paralysis, the duality, the sense of time slipping away, the inability to act on what is felt, the awareness of mortality that makes every gesture seem both too small and too large.

Prufrock walks through the fog-bound city asking: do I dare? And in the end, he does not dare. He hears the mermaids singing each to each and does not think they sing for him. He turns back from the overwhelming question. Mr. Spitzer wandering the fog-bound streets asking strangers if they have seen Cousin Hannah is Prufrock's walk rendered without irony, not the missed opportunity of a timid man but the genuine grief of a man who loved in the only way he could manage, which was obliquely, from a distance, and too late.

Young admired Eliot. She knew her Prufrock. The fog that was so precise in that poem, the yellow fog that rubs its back upon the window-panes, is the same fog that has been moving through this novel since the first chapter. It is the atmosphere of consciousness uncertain of itself, of love that knows it cannot be received, of the person who has measured out his life in coffee spoons and is now, in the shadow of a woman's death, finally measuring it against something that was genuinely large.

Ah, bright star of my love

The chapter closes with Cousin Hannah's cry from the deathbed, restored here in its fullness: “Ah, bright star of my love, find your way to the lightless star. Find your way to one who has neither wings nor light. For I am dying now, and” my love goes down.

The echo of Keats's Bright Star sonnet is deliberate. Keats wrote to the star out of his own dying: tuberculosis at twenty-five, the bright star as an emblem of constancy that he could not himself achieve, the tension between the desire for permanence and the knowledge of the body's limits. Cousin Hannah, dying in her white tent, calls to a bright star that is also a dead star: a love that was lightless, wingless, unreachable not because it was too far but because she never allowed herself to reach for it while it could have been reached.

My love goes down. Not upward, into the mountains she climbed all her life. Down. Into the ground, into the dark, into the sea that has been lapping at the edges of this novel since Vera got on the bus. The alpenhorn cannot call it back. The steeplejack knows it. Mr. Spitzer carries the instrument into the lowland fog and tries anyway.

What to hold onto

Chapter 46 is an elegy, not for Cousin Hannah only but for every love that was not said in time, every instrument carried into the wrong landscape, every star called bright that was already going out. Mr. Spitzer collecting the alpenhorn is the novel's emblem for what we do with the lives of the people we could not save and could not fully know: we take what we can carry, we move through the fog asking questions that have no answers, and we listen when the steeplejack speaks from the heights we cannot reach.

Hold onto the steeplejack's paradoxes. “I live because I am dead.” I love because I love not. These will keep mattering. The novel is built on them.
