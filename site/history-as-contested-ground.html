Movement Two: The Five Pillars in the Traditions

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Memory and the Past

Philosophy: William James and Pragmatism

Allusions: Angels and Archangels · Exploration and the Poles · The History of Opium · Women's Suffrage

Definition from research

Young does not use history the way most novelists use it: as backdrop, as period texture, as the reliable ground on which invented characters walk. In Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, history is itself a character, and like all the novel's characters it is unreliable, plural, contested, and saturated with grief. The historical record arrives in the novel already interrogated, already doubled, already running against itself, and this is not an accident or irony. It is architecture. Every pillar of Young's philosophy requires a particular relationship to history, and the novel builds that relationship into the tissue of every historical reference it makes.

The clearest statement of the novel's position on history is Miss MacIntosh's own: "History lies, and geography is uncertain, unstable as water or a sick lady's dreams, and we know not who or what we are in these modern times or which star we inhabit." She delivers this immediately after dismissing Julius Caesar: "Who knows that he crossed the Rubicon or that there was a Caesar? What man alive ever saw him?" This is the witness pillar performing its most provocative function. Miss MacIntosh is the novel's embodiment of practical common sense, the figure who insists on the verifiable and the actual, and here she is dismantling the verifiability of history itself. Not as a philosophical argument but as an impatient, drowsily nodding observation between questions about Kansas and the Bosphorus. The flat pragmatist arrives at the same epistemological place as the most radical skeptic: we were not there, we cannot know, history is a story told by those who needed it to go a certain way. That the novel's realist comes to this conclusion is not ironic. It is Young's deepest claim about what honest attention to the world actually produces.

Miss MacIntosh uses history as a pedagogical weapon. Her lessons to Vera arrive as cascading historical catastrophes: the great Chicago fire and how many were unburied, how many unidentified; the date of the sinking of the Titanic; the floods, the fires, the earthquakes, plague or famine or drought. These are not lessons in historical knowledge. They are lessons in historical scale, in the individual's smallness against accumulated catastrophe, in what it means to reckon honestly with how much suffering has already happened before the present moment began. This is grief as permanent weather operating through the historical record: history is not a sequence of events moving toward the present but an accumulated weight of irreversible loss, and every date Miss MacIntosh demands is a demand that Vera hold that weight consciously, that she not look away from the count of the unidentified dead.

The suffrage movement receives more sustained treatment than any other historical force in the novel, and the treatment is architecturally complex. Cousin Hannah, the great suffrage captain, is described as "cuirassed by suffrage," her shield "emblazoned with a cherub carrying a lightning bolt," a figure who had bearded human lions in their dens in Persia and startled great sheiks in their ivory beds and negotiated great torrents of ice and snow. The suffrage movement in Young's hands becomes simultaneously a genuine historical struggle for political equality and an image of the plural self operating at historical scale: the movement is simultaneously its captain and its cause, simultaneously personal love and collective crusade, simultaneously the battle raging "in heaven and on earth" and the iron girdle worn by a Crusader's wife waiting at home. The historical suffrage movement is real, Young insists: the meetings, the campaigns, the bodies in the field. And it is also something the novel can only approach through accumulated imagery of armor and crusade and celestial battle, because the inner life of anyone who gives everything to a cause is never adequately captured by the historical record, which records the outcomes but not the weather inside the person who made them.

The murdered archduke at Sarajevo appears in the context of the mother's precognitive knowledge: she had known of the murder "several hours before the news had reached the rest of the world," just as she had known of the murders of the Russian czar and royal family, and of the deaths of Saladin and Solymon the Magnificent and many Babylonian kings. Young collapses historical time here with total deliberateness: the assassination that triggered the First World War sits in the same sentence as Crusade-era sultans and ancient Assyrian kings. The mother's omniscience is not supernatural. It is the logic of illusion as primary applied to history: if all time is simultaneous in the perceiving mind, if the years collapse to a moment whenever she looks back, then the archduke at Sarajevo and Sardanapalus king of Assyria are equidistant, are equally present, are both available to her third eye. History under this pillar is not a linear sequence but a room all of whose inhabitants are present at once, which is exactly what the mother's opium house embodies.

The passenger pigeon Martha, who "expired September 14, at one o'clock in the afternoon just as the clock was tolling one, 1918, A.D., at the Cincinnati zoo," receives the most exacting historical precision in the entire novel: a specific date, a specific hour, a specific location. Young gives us the date of Martha's death with the careful documentation of a legal record while surrounding it with Mr. Spitzer's grief, his opalescent tears, his weeping walks under drizzling clouds. This precision is not sentiment. It is the recursive construction pillar making an argument about the relationship between the historical particular and the universal: she had died "as one passenger pigeon who was all the passenger pigeons who had ever been and much less and much more and would not be again." The death of one is the death of all. The historical record of Martha's death is unique in all of history: "perhaps only once, in fact," had anyone ever known the exact date of the death of an entire species. Young makes this unique historical precision the vehicle for the novel's deepest claim about extinction, grief, and the irreversibility of loss. What history can document exactly, when it finally achieves that exactness, is the moment a kind of being passes from the world forever.

The Marne and the Lusitania arrive tattooed on a man's body: "Remember the Marne," written under a battleship sailing through the hairs of his chest, a bullet still in his leg from the Last War still predicting rain. The official historical record lives, in this novel, in the flesh of those who survived it. The man is a walking archive, his body the document. This is the plural self carrying history inside it, the living person as contested ground where public event and private survival converge. He was on the Titanic and the Lusitania and the White Star Castle and the Prince Edward. All four went down. He kept surviving. History accumulates in him not as knowledge but as scar, as the wet twinge before a storm.

Miss MacIntosh demands that Vera know the date of the Titanic's sinking, the cause of the Chicago fire, the population of Kansas. Captain Cook goes traipsing off to the North Pole and is accused of "forgery, perjury, simulation, lying, counterfeiting discovery." The mother knows the archduke will be murdered before the newspapers report it. History in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is always three things at once: the official record, which lies; the body's memory, which survives; and the grief that outlasts both, which is the only part that cannot be disputed because it is the only part that was never a story to begin with.
