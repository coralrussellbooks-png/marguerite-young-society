Chapter Six: Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest · Across every book

Characters: Esther Longtree · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

The sixth pillar emerges from the first five as their ethical consequence.

Young's system does not only describe a world in which things do not resolve. It makes an ethical claim about incompletion: that the unfinished is not a failure but a form of honesty, and that the demand for completion is a form of violence against the actual texture of experience. This is not a limitation. It is a moral position.

The Western philosophical tradition has a profound discomfort with the unfinished. The claim must be statable, the argument must be followable, the conclusion must be reachable. The unfinished argument is not a philosophical contribution. It is a draft. This is the tradition's limitation, not Young's. The world does not resolve. The self does not arrive. The grief does not process. The construction of reality is never complete. A philosophy that requires completeness as the condition of validity is a philosophy that can only describe a world that does not exist.

Young's system accepts the unfinished as its natural state. Not because she could not finish it, but because finishing it would require lying about the world it describes. The sixth pillar names this acceptance as an ethical stance: the unfinished is not failure but integrity, the honest acknowledgment that the work is still ongoing because the world is still ongoing and the honest response to an ongoing world is an ongoing engagement.

Young had stated the principle in verse more than twenty years before the novel appeared. In “A Crystal Principle” (1942), the earth would be perfect crystal “Were it not for accident unknown / And least departure,” and the flaw is what makes it “valid in being.” Her later statement was shorter: “There must always be a flaw.” Of the novel she said it has no beginning, middle, or end “because life has no beginning, middle or end.”

Walter Benjamin's own work is famously unfinished. The Arcades Project, his massive study of nineteenth-century Paris as the origin point of modern consumer culture, was never completed. Benjamin died before he could assemble thousands of notes and fragments into the book he had planned. But the incompleteness of The Arcades Project is not incidental to its argument. The argument is about the accumulated fragments of the past, about the way that meaning emerges from the constellation of particulars rather than from the systematic whole. A completed, systematic Arcades Project would have betrayed its own argument by imposing the false unity of a finished system on material that resists systematic completion.

The unfinished is the honest form. Young understood this. Benjamin understood this. The sixth pillar names what they both knew: that the honest engagement with a world that does not resolve produces work that does not resolve, and that the work's incompleteness is not its failure but its most rigorous achievement.

The political consequences of the sixth pillar are the most challenging of all the pillars. Political life, as it is currently practiced, demands the finished product. The policy must be complete before it can be implemented. The platform must be coherent. The candidate must have a position, a clear and statable position, on every question. The unfinished is not a political option. It is a liability. Young's system says: this demand is the demand that produces the long con. The finished policy, the complete platform, the clear position: these are the products of a political culture that has decided that the appearance of resolution is more important than the honest acknowledgment of ongoing complexity. The policy that presents itself as finished is the policy that cannot be revised when it turns out to be wrong.

The Youngian political life builds the unfinished into its structure. It creates the mechanisms for ongoing revision, for the acknowledgment that the current arrangement is provisional, for the return to the beginning that the recursive construction requires. It treats the unfinished not as a liability but as the most honest form that political integrity can take.

The theory of the flaw takes shape in the poems of 1942, passes into the mouth of the undertaker in "Angel in the Forest", and becomes the open form of the novel. "Harp Song for a Radical", her last book, was left unfinished at her death, “a complete creation, without having been entirely finished,” in its editors’ words. That incompletion was not chosen. Young also wanted to decide or be in control, as when she tried to reclaim the Debs book uncut.

Phase One, Childhood and the Early Poems (1908 to 1934)

Pillar 6: Unfinished Is Honest

The pillar appears mostly in reverse, in the stone house of "Jonathan's House" and the scattering song of "Song's Dissemination." The theory of the flaw has not yet appeared.

Phase Two, The Chicago Years, Prismatic Ground, and the Graduate Essays (1935 to 1937)

Pillar 6: Unfinished Is Honest

"Experiment in Azure" holds the unhatched: "No one could tell what bird might be," and "the shell / Broken would be the broken sky." "Song's Preface" calls the song "fugitive," one that "comes no sooner than it goes again." The theory of the flaw is still a few years off.

Phase Three, The Philosophical Poems (1938 to 1945)

Pillar 6: Unfinished Is Honest

This is the phase in which Young formulated the theory of the flaw. "A Crystal Principle" (1942): earth would be perfect crystal "Were it not for accident unknown / And least departure: and valid in being therefore / And beautiful, the escape from each simple of total / Is the heartbeat thief, mistake, flaw." Its draft adds "there is a pattern in the anomaly." The giraffe (1942) is "a vain and vexed / But relevant mistake, and scheme of all our flaws." "The Pattern" (1941) names "these present flaws / Whereof the rain crow cries, rose petal falls." "Failing Perspective": "we shall never see the completed earth." "Of Deed, Love, Death": "our inclination is always the business unfinished." The five drafts of "The Submarine" show the method itself as a spiral that never closes.

Complication: the title poem "Moderate Fable" calls incompletion a curse. Seven bridegrooms come to the daughter of God and die before "the act of union entire," "cursed by severance."

Phase Four, "Angel in the Forest" and “Complex Life and Complex Letters” (1945 to 1946)

Pillar 6: Unfinished Is Honest

Perfection is death. Owen's heaven is "a Parmenidean quietude, all crystal, stainless, and perfect, without wind, without motion." A baby's head is "a huge mistake of nature, like perhaps every other head." The undertaker warns that no one can remove the flaws from the universe without destroying it, the theory of "A Crystal Principle" put into a character's mouth.

Complication: when the Rappites leave Economy, the barns are "half painted" and the fields "half plowed." The unfinished can also mean abandonment.

Phase Five, "Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" (written from the mid 1940s, published 1965)

Pillar 6: Unfinished Is Honest

Young said the novel has no beginning, middle, or end "because life has no beginning, middle or end." Fuchs quotes its broken visions as "more complete in their incompletion than if they had been whole," and shows that when Young uses plot she uses it "to negate its legitimacy." Hannah's forty trunks come with forty keys, always one too many or too few. The ending stays in the conditional: Esther "would hang" her sign.

Complication: Vera first meets the unfinished with dread, calling the bald Miss MacIntosh "this unfinished thing... which had no business in creation." Vera's mother, for whom "everything was complete, even the incompletion," shows completeness as denial. Young herself was "hysterically anxious to finish," and called the book "very controlled." The openness is designed.

Incompletion as her novel’s moral position, what is new is scale. The flaw of “A Crystal Principle” was a principle stated in a poem. Here it is the design of a 1,198 page book, and the narrator first meets it with dread before the book teaches her to live with it.

Phase Six, The Occasional Prose, Interviews, and Self-Interpretation (1940s to 1995)

Pillar 6: Unfinished Is Honest

"There must always be a flaw." Her teaching threw out "beginning and middle and end" for "memory and observation, dream and reality, conflict and character." Clark read her gift of stolen hotel keys as a lesson that there is no closure adequate to any ending.

Complication: when Knopf wanted to cut the Debs book, she tried to reclaim it uncut. She wanted to decide when something was done, that she had “tried her best”.

Phase Seven, "Harp Song for a Radical" (written in her last decades, published 1999)

Pillar 6: Unfinished Is Honest

The book is itself unfinished. It ends on a promise of the Pullman strike and the prison years that never arrive. The editors call it "a complete creation, without having been entirely finished." Weitling dies leaving "his unfinished treatise on moon and sun," the fruit "of fourteen years of mental toil." The Icarians' "statistics, statistics, statistics" and Weitling's "science without flaws" are the completeness the book distrusts.

Complication: this incompletion is accidental, the result of illness, mixed drafts, and death with "Harp Song for a Radical".
