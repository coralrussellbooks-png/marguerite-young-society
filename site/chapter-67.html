To All My Darlings

Chapter 67

On Chapter 67 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 10, 2026

← Chapter 66 · Home · Chapter 68 →

Summary

The bus reaches the Tavern at the end of its journey and cannot wedge into the space between a leaking milk truck and a hearse. Moses Hunnecker, drunk or so Vera thinks, curses old Doc, the mad Dr. Justice O’Leary, whose invisible ghost buggy, an old Ford full of the children Moses jeers at, he believes is taking the space. He sings scraps of old songs, boasts of the buzzards returning to Rush Creek, and threatens to scatter Doc all over the street. Vera, Madge, and Homer stay in their seats out of infinite weariness, for they have come home to no home. Madge laughs at a misery that is nothing new to her and likens them to people trapped in a crazy house of mirrors with no doors, and Homer wonders which door Jackie found.

Characters present

Moses Hunnecker · Justice O’Leary · Madge Capehorn · Homer Capehorn · Vera Cartwheel

Key passages

From the research

  • Moses curses and talks to old Doc, an unseen presence whom children shoot at with bean-shooters. This dialogue reveals a sense of loneliness and grandiosity, as Moses argues with someone who cannot respond. Moses feels that old Doc should move his “old ghost buggy” to let the bus park, suggesting a metaphorical traffic conflict between the living and the dead, or between progress and stasis. Moses’s speech about traffic rules (chalk lines, detour signs, slow turns) symbolizes the need for order in life and relationships, but also the impossibility of such order in this surreal setting.
  • Moses’s exhaustion is palpable as he drapes himself over the steering wheel, singing softly and lamenting his situation. Homer and Madge are caught in an “infinite weariness,” reluctant to leave the bus, suggesting a deeper existential fatigue or a reluctance to face reality. Moses’s talk of increasing the town’s population by three contrasts with old Doc’s apparent failure to contribute positively, adding layers of irony and social commentary.
  • Moses describes Old Doc’s “old ghost buggy” as an old Ford without essential parts, and accuses Doc of being dead “from the shoulders down.” This blurring of lines between life and death deepens the surreal atmosphere. Moses threatens to scatter “heathen Chinee” inside Doc’s buggy and warns of a “hot time in the old town tonight” with buzzards arriving, intensifying the ominous tone. This passage may symbolize the clash between vitality and decay, or between the living’s struggle to move forward and the dead’s hold on space and memory.
  • Vera briefly contemplates that old Doc might be the undertaker in the hearse, blending the roles of doctor and death. This ambiguity deepens the theme of mortality and the thin boundary between life and death. The undertaker as “the best doctor” implies a dark irony or that death is the ultimate end, beyond complaint or cure.
  • The passengers’ rocking back and forth, Madge’s high, thin laughter, and Homer’s absent-mindedness contribute to a dreamlike, nightmarish atmosphere. Madge describes their stalled situation as being trapped in a crazy house with mirrored walls and no doors, evoking feelings of entrapment and confusion. Homer’s mention of “Jackie” and tuberculosis introduces a note of illness and impending loss, heightening the emotional tension.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “Probably half the conversation in America occurs in this manner.” What kind of conversation does Vera mean?
  • “I’m increasing the population of this town by three, and that is more than you can ever bring.” Who are the three, and what is Moses boasting of?
  • “Perhaps the undertaker, cutting the jugular vein, was the best doctor.” What joke is Vera making?

Characters

  • Moses addresses “old Doc, a grizzled, unseen man.” Who is old Doc, and why can only Moses see him?
  • Madge laughs “at the bus-driver’s misery, Doc’s misery, which was nothing new to her.” Why is it nothing new to her?
  • “He was impatient as well as absent-minded, dreaming of a bride his wife might never know.” What does this tell us about Homer and Madge?

Connections

  • “After all, we had come home to no home.” How does this answer the journey that began in Chapter 1?
  • “The generations of buzzards do go on, honest to God.” Compare “One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh” (Ecclesiastes 1:4). What is Moses saying about old Doc?
  • “Jackie found the door,” Homer says. Which door does he mean?

Reflection

  • “There had to be some traffic rules.” Is Moses talking about traffic?
  • “The past was the past, though. Time waited for no man.” Does this chapter agree?
  • “You felt along the glass, but you did not know how to get out, which mirror was a door.” When has life felt like that?

Research: Characters

Moses Hunnecker, the bus-driver

  • A long-haired, possibly drunk or deeply weary man who curses and talks aloud to “old Doc,” an unseen figure. He embodies frustration, loneliness, and a struggle against an unyielding obstacle.

Old Doc, Dr. Justice O’Leary

  • Old Doc is Dr. Justice O’Leary, the mad old doctor whose imaginary patients are “those pregnant women who were dead and whom old Doc seemed to think were living again.” Only Moses sees him here.
  • Moses sees him in an old “ghost buggy” (car), who occupies a parking space and seems to symbolize obstruction, death, or the past. As a doctor he may represent death or stagnation.

Madge

  • Laughs breathlessly at Moses’s misery, indicating a detachment or acceptance of the surreal situation.

Homer

  • Impatient and absent-minded, dreaming of a bride he fears his wife might never know.

Vera

  • Observes and reflects on the scene, feeling tired and dreamlike.

Research: Themes

  • Loneliness and Communication with the Unseen: Moses talks to old Doc, who is unseen and possibly a ghost or symbolic figure. This “conversation” reflects the human need to vent frustration and to communicate even when no one is listening or present. It highlights isolation and the internal battles people face.
  • The Struggle Between Life and Death: Old Doc’s “ghost buggy” and the hearse symbolize death or the past, while the bus and its passengers represent life moving forward. Moses’s frustration in trying to park reflects the tension between these forces, with death obstructing the path of life.
  • Rules and Order vs Chaos: Moses imagines traffic rules (chalk lines, detours, traffic lights) as metaphors for social order and fairness. The chaotic reality, with obstacles like old Doc’s ghost buggy, represents life’s unpredictability and unfairness.
  • Existential Weariness and Dreamlike Reality: Vera and passengers feel an “infinite weariness,” and the situation is likened to being stuck on a ferris wheel or in a crazy house with mirrored walls and no exits. This surreal imagery evokes the feeling of being trapped in life’s endless struggles and the confusion about how to move forward.
  • The Passage of Time and Memory: Moses references the past (“the past was the past”) and the inevitability of time moving on. Old Doc’s presence and the image of buzzards returning evoke decay and the cyclical nature of life and death.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

  • Moses pleads repeatedly with Doc to move over, symbolizing the desire for obstacles (death, the past, inertia) to yield to life and progress.
  • “I can’t give you anything but love, baby,” sung by Moses, contrasts the harshness of his situation with a tender expression, perhaps hinting at the human capacity for care amid struggle.
  • Moses’s comment about the passengers in old Doc’s buggy suggests confusion or denial about the nature of life, lineage, or reality.
  • It’s “like being stalled on a ferris wheel at the top of the sky.” Madge’s description captures the surreal, trapped feeling of the moment.

Further notes from the text

  • The bus tries to wedge into the space between a milk truck leaking from its cans and a hearse, birth and death on either side of the passengers. See Comedy in Chapter 67.
  • Moses’s cursing of old Doc becomes a comic theory of marriage and traffic: perhaps there should be a chalk line down the middle of every bed, with signs for detours, narrow shoulders, and steep inclines, or a man might be ditched.
  • He boasts that he is bringing in real business, increasing the population of the town by three, more than Doc can say, and calls Doc’s car an old ghost buggy that could be parked on a postage stamp.
  • His report of the buzzards that have staged a return and brought their relatives, eighty or ninety seen over Rush Creek at sundown, ends with a cheerful jab: the generations of buzzards go on.
  • Vera says the passengers stay in their seats out of infinite weariness: “we had come home to no home,” and perhaps they wished to prolong the journey.
  • Madge’s laughter is high and thin, at the driver’s misery and Doc’s, while Homer dreams of a bride his wife might never know. Vera expects a bird to fly out of the bus-driver’s beard.

Research: Resources

  • “the pregnant girl with the bird upon her hat”: Hats trimmed with whole birds were the fashion around 1900. In 1896 two Boston women, Harriet Hemenway and Minna Hall, founded the Massachusetts Audubon Society to stop the slaughter of birds for the plumage trade, and the federal Lacey Act of 1900 and the Migratory Bird Treaty of 1918 followed. See Madge Capehorn. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “He addressed old Doc, a grizzled, unseen man”: Old Doc is Dr. Justice O’Leary. See Justice O’Leary.
  • “Roosevelt in the White House”: There were two Roosevelt presidents, Theodore and Franklin D. Since Miss MacIntosh talked about not seeing the Golden Gate Bridge which was built in the 1930s it is a good guess and since Moses hates Democrats, he is probably talking about Franklin D. Roosevelt. He was the President of the US from 1933 until his death in April 1945, the only president elected four times.
  • “Move over, Doc”: See Moses Hunnecker.
  • “cretin idiots”: “Cretin” first named people with congenital iodine deficiency, from an Alpine French word for “Christian,” that is, a fellow human being; “water on their brains” is hydrocephalus. Both became insults.
  • “I can’t give you anything but love, baby”: I Can’t Give You Anything But Love, Baby is an American song from 1928. The idea behind the song came during a stroll Fields and McHugh were taking one evening down Fifth Avenue. They saw a young couple window-shopping at Tiffany’s. McHugh and Fields understood that the couple could not afford to buy jewelry from Tiffany’s. They heard the man say, “Gee, honey, I’d like to get you a sparkler like that, but right now, I can’t give you nothin’ but love!” Within an hour McHugh and Fields came up with the song. The lyricist was Dorothy Fields, one of the first women to succeed as a Broadway and Hollywood songwriter, with Jimmy McHugh; the song was introduced in the all-Black revue Blackbirds of 1928. See Music and Composers and Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “How many rabbit tests old Doc had run”: In the Friedman test (1931), a woman’s urine was injected into a female rabbit, whose ovaries were examined a few days later for signs of pregnancy. The rabbit was killed either way, so “the rabbit died” was never the sign. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “Time waited for no man”: The proverb “Time and tide wait for no man” is centuries old in English.
  • “the one-hoss shay, she ain’t what she used to was”: Moses mixes Holmes’s “One-Hoss Shay” with the folk song “The Old Gray Mare,” who “ain’t what she used to be.”
    • The poem is Oliver Wendell Holmes’s “The Deacon’s Masterpiece; or, The Wonderful One-Hoss Shay” (1858). In the poem, a deacon laments the fact that carriages break down due to weakness at one place or another. The carriages were usually built locally by enterprising villagers. The deacon deduces that if he were to build a one hoss shay using the finest materials from top to bottom, it would have no weaknesses and would therefore endure forever. So the deacon decides to build the most reliable one hoss shay there ever could logically be, in accordance with the Puritan principles that critics of the era believed Oliver Wendell Holmes to be satirizing. Every part of it is equally as strong as every other part. This marvel of logical construction lasts and lasts through a parade of decades and deacons, providing perfect service all the while. But as Holmes says, logic is logic, and eventually it catches up to the shay. Exactly one hundred years to the day that the deacon finished it, the whole thing collapses in a heap of particulates, causing the current deacon embarrassment and a sore behind.
  • “Sweet Mary O’Grady”: Moses sings Sweet Mary O’Grady but the famous song is called Sweet Rosie O’Grady. “Sweet Rosie O’Grady” (1896) was written by Maude Nugent, a vaudeville singer, and “down by the old mill stream where I first met you” is from Tell Taylor’s “Down by the Old Mill Stream” (1910); Moses runs the two together. See Music and Composers and Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “Why did a doctor have to go on a doctor’s holiday?”: A pun from a busman: a busman’s holiday is a day off spent doing what one does at work.
  • “the junk that was going to Japan”: Through the 1930s the United States sold Japan great amounts of scrap iron and steel, which went into the war in China, until Roosevelt embargoed scrap exports in October 1940. Protesters warned that the junk would come back as bombs.
    • The U.S. sent significant goods and aid to Japan primarily during the post-WWII Occupation (1945-1952) through massive aid packages for reconstruction, and earlier, U.S. trade with Japan began after Commodore Matthew Perry forced open ports in 1853, leading to regular shipments of goods and commodities, with Japan importing more U.S. products than exporting to the U.S. until the mid-1960s.
  • “a case where an invulnerable force met with an invulnerable obstacle”: The old paradox asks what happens when an irresistible force meets an immovable object.
  • “These heathen Chinee”: “The Heathen Chinee” was the popular name of Bret Harte’s poem “Plain Language from Truthful James” (1870). Harte meant to mock the white cardsharps who cheat a Chinese player, but the phrase became an anti-Chinese slur, sung and shouted during the agitation that led to the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882. See Society, Class, and Race.
    • “A Chinaman’s chance” meant no chance at all. Chinese workers were given the most dangerous work on the railroads and in the mines, and in People v. Hall (1854) the California Supreme Court ruled that a Chinese witness could not testify against a white man. See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “There was going to be a hot time in the old town tonight”: “There’ll Be a Hot Time in the Old Town Tonight” (1896), by Theodore Metz and Joe Hayden, became the marching song of Theodore Roosevelt’s Rough Riders in 1898. See Music and Composers.
  • “They have staged a return and have brought their relatives with them”: Turkey vultures, called buzzards in America, leave the northern states for the winter and return each spring; Hinckley, Ohio, has celebrated their return every March since 1957.
  • “Be a good Boy Scout, Doc”: The Boy Scouts of America, founded in 1910, pledged a good turn daily.
  • “The generations of buzzards do go on”: “One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh: but the earth abideth for ever” (Ecclesiastes 1:4, KJV). See Ecclesiastes.
  • “cutting the jugular vein”: In arterial embalming, fluid is pumped in through an artery, usually the carotid, while the blood is drained out through the jugular vein. See Death and Mortality.
  • “an old Ford with black canvas curtains flapping”: The Ford Model T (1908-1927) was built by the millions; its open touring cars kept out the rain with side curtains of black canvas.
  • “It was like being stalled on a ferris wheel at the top of the sky”: George Ferris built the first Ferris wheel for the World’s Columbian Exposition in Chicago in 1893.
  • “Jackie found the door”: See Jacqueline White. Before streptomycin came into use after 1944, tuberculosis was the leading killer of young adults, and its patients were sent away to sanatoriums for months or years.

Vocabulary

Bean-shooter
A peashooter or slingshot.
Carapaced
Covered with a hard shell, like a beetle or a turtle.
Casket
A small, often ornate box for jewels or valuables. In American usage, also a coffin, so Young’s “casket containing no jewel” carries both meanings.
Corset
A close-fitting undergarment, stiffened with whalebone or steel and laced tight, worn to shape the waist.
Cretin
A person stunted in body and mind by a lack of iodine from birth; later an insult.
Grandiose
Grand in an exaggerated, self-important way.
Grizzled
Streaked with grey.
Harangue
To lecture loudly and at length.
Invulnerable
Unable to be wounded or harmed.
Jugular
The great vein of the neck.
Luna moth
A large pale green moth of North America with long tails on its hind wings.
Maudlin
Tearfully sentimental, as a drunk can be.
Melodiously
Tunefully.
Monopoly
Sole possession or control of something.
Seesaw
To move up and down or back and forth.
Shay
A light, one-horse carriage; a chaise.
Testimonial
A formal statement given as evidence or proof of something.
Thatched
Covered with straw or reeds, like a roof; of hair, thick and shaggy.
Transfusion
The transfer of blood into a person’s veins.
