Summary

The bus reaches an Indiana tavern and cannot park between a leaking milk truck and a hearse. Moses curses at old Doc, whom he believes is blocking the space with his ghost car. The passengers rock wearily in their seats. Madge laughs breathlessly, and Homer recalls a fun house of mirrors with no doors, wondering which door Jacqueline found.

Characters present

Moses Hunnecker · Justice O’Leary · Madge Capehorn · Homer Capehorn · Vera Cartwheel

Concepts present

Philosophy: Augustine and Christian Theology

Key passages

From the research

  • Moses curses and talks to old Doc, an unseen presence whom children shoot at with bean-shooters. This dialogue reveals a sense of loneliness and grandiosity, as Moses argues with someone who cannot respond. Moses feels that old Doc should move his "old ghost buggy" to let the bus park, suggesting a metaphorical traffic conflict between the living and the dead, or between progress and stasis. Moses’s speech about traffic rules (chalk lines, detour signs, slow turns) symbolizes the need for order in life and relationships, but also the impossibility of such order in this surreal setting.
  • Moses’s exhaustion is palpable as he drapes himself over the steering wheel, singing softly and lamenting his situation. Homer and Madge are caught in an "infinite weariness," reluctant to leave the bus, suggesting a deeper existential fatigue or a reluctance to face reality. Moses’s talk of increasing the town’s population by three contrasts with old Doc’s apparent failure to contribute positively, adding layers of irony and social commentary.
  • Moses describes Old Doc’s "old ghost buggy" as an old Ford without essential parts, and accuses Doc of being dead "from the shoulders down." This blurring of lines between life and death deepens the surreal atmosphere. Moses threatens to scatter "heathen Chinee" inside Doc’s buggy and warns of a "hot time in the old town tonight" with buzzards arriving, intensifying the ominous tone. This passage may symbolize the clash between vitality and decay, or between the living’s struggle to move forward and the dead’s hold on space and memory.
  • Vera briefly contemplates that old Doc might be the undertaker in the hearse, blending the roles of doctor and death. This ambiguity deepens the theme of mortality and the thin boundary between life and death. The undertaker as "the best doctor" implies a dark irony or that death is the ultimate end, beyond complaint or cure.
  • The passengers’ rocking back and forth, Madge’s high, thin laughter, and Homer’s absent-mindedness contribute to a dreamlike, nightmarish atmosphere. Madge describes their stalled situation as being trapped in a crazy house with mirrored walls and no doors, evoking feelings of entrapment and confusion. Homer’s mention of "Jackie" and tuberculosis introduces a note of illness and impending loss, heightening the emotional tension.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Bus-driver

  • A long-haired, possibly drunk or deeply weary man who curses and talks aloud to "old Doc," an unseen figure. He embodies frustration, loneliness, and a struggle against an unyielding obstacle.

Old Doc

  • Moses sees him in an old "ghost buggy" (car), who occupies a parking space and seems to symbolize obstruction, death, or the past. As a doctor he may represent death or stagnation.

Madge

  • Laughs breathlessly at Moses's misery, indicating a detachment or acceptance of the surreal situation.

Homer

  • Impatient and absent-minded, dreaming of a bride he fears his wife might never know.

Vera

  • Observes and reflects on the scene, feeling tired and dreamlike.

Research: Themes

  • Loneliness and Communication with the Unseen Moses talks to old Doc, who is unseen and possibly a ghost or symbolic figure. This "conversation" reflects the human need to vent frustration and to communicate even when no one is listening or present. It highlights isolation and the internal battles people face.
  • The Struggle Between Life and Death Old Doc’s "ghost buggy" and the hearse symbolize death or the past, while the bus and its passengers represent life moving forward. Moses’s frustration in trying to park reflects the tension between these forces, with death obstructing the path of life.
  • Rules and Order vs Chaos Moses imagines traffic rules (chalk lines, detours, traffic lights) as metaphors for social order and fairness. The chaotic reality, with obstacles like old Doc’s ghost buggy, represents life's unpredictability and unfairness.
  • Existential Weariness and Dreamlike Reality Vera and passengers feel an "infinite weariness," and the situation is likened to being stuck on a ferris wheel or in a crazy house with mirrored walls and no exits. This surreal imagery evokes the feeling of being trapped in life’s endless struggles and the confusion about how to move forward.
  • The Passage of Time and Memory Moses references the past ("the past was the past") and the inevitability of time moving on. Old Doc’s presence and the image of buzzards returning evoke decay and the cyclical nature of life and death.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

  • Moses pleads repeatedly with Doc to move over, symbolizing the desire for obstacles (death, the past, inertia) to yield to life and progress.
  • “I can’t give you anything but love, baby,” sung by Moses, contrasts the harshness of his situation with a tender expression, perhaps hinting at the human capacity for care amid struggle.
  • Moses’s comment about the passengers in old Doc’s buggy suggests confusion or denial about the nature of life, lineage, or reality.
  • It’s “like being stalled on a ferris wheel at the top of the sky.” Madge’s description captures the surreal, trapped feeling of the moment.

  • Moses recites a line from the poem The Wonderful One-Hoss-Shay by Oliver Wendell Holmes. In the poem, a deacon laments the fact that carriages break down due to weakness at one place or another. The carriages were usually built locally by enterprising villagers. The deacon deduces that if he were to build a one hoss shay using the finest materials from top to bottom, it would have no weaknesses and would therefore endure forever. So the deacon decides to build the most reliable one hoss shay there ever could logically be, in accordance with the Puritan principles that critics of the era believed Oliver Wendell Holmes to be satirizing. Every part of it is equally as strong as every other part. This marvel of logical construction lasts and lasts through a parade of decades and deacons, providing perfect service all the while. But as Holmes says, logic is logic, and eventually it catches up to the shay. Exactly one hundred years to the day that the deacon finished it, the whole thing collapses in a heap of particulates, causing the current deacon embarrassment and a sore behind.
