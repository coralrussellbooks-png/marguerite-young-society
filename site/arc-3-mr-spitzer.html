Arc 3: Mr. Spitzer (Chapters 25–29)

← The Birthday Night and Its Aftermath · Story Arcs Home · Cousin Hannah →

Overview

A portrait of Mr. Spitzer: Peron's fall from a window, the glass wall and the clerk who declared him dead, his law practice among the dead, the two watches, and his abandoned butterflies, which become a meditation on death, resurrection and the soul.

Central figures: Miss MacIntosh · Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah

Chapter by chapter

Chapter 25

Vera considers Mr. Spitzer, whom Catherine accuses of being dead, or of being his own dead brother. We learn how Peron died. He fell through a fourteenth-floor window, perhaps by suicide, perhaps mistaking the window for a door. Joachim himself once walked through a glass wall in another lawyer's office without noticing, and his valet later found glass behind his ears. Bills for the wall followed. When Joachim finally tried to collect damages for his discomfort, a clerk told him that Mr. Spitzer had died several years ago.

Chapter 26

Mr. Spitzer's field of law is the dead: wills, deeds, archives, and lost heirs. One case involves two Boston spinsters, each believing she is Cleopatra. One left her fortune to her Siamese cats under his supervision, and the cats seem likely to live forever. He asks what Peron would have done had their places been reversed. He concludes that Peron would never have been faithful to Catherine's bedside, but would have dragged her into the living sunlight even if it killed her. He reflects that to reach the upper world one must first travel through the lower.

Chapter 27

Vera grows exasperated with the adults who circle their problems endlessly. Mr. Spitzer carries calling cards and an old passport so that, if he is found dead, he will be identified as himself and not his brother. He wears Peron's accurate wristwatch, and has lately rediscovered his own identical watch, broken and stopped near the time of Peron's death, and wears it too. He is always late and always caught between streams of traffic. A stranger once asked him on a crowded street whether he wanted to die. He said no while nodding yes, and was led safely across.

Chapter 28

Mr. Spitzer admits that he may be only another of Catherine's hallucinations, and that he truly lives only during these evenings in her room. He recalls the butterfly collection he abandoned after Peron's funeral, his correspondence with fellow collectors, and the births and deaths of butterflies he watched. If one were called away at the crucial moment, he observes, one could never tell whether one had seen a beginning or an end.

Chapter 29

Mr. Spitzer speaks at length about butterflies. He compares the difficulty of telling butterfly from moth to telling brother from brother, and happiness from sorrow. He names species by the hundreds and dwells on the butterfly as psyche, the soul. He describes Peron's funeral, crowded with gamblers and merry mourners, and the one person who saw his grief, an old lamplighter weeping on a street corner. He wonders whether a sock darned again and again until no original thread remains is still the same sock. The chapter ends with him as a dark atheist who knows more of the life beyond the grave than of this one.
