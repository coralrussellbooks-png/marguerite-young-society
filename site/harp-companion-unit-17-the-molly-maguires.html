Unit 17, pp. 433-472: The Molly Maguires

← Unit 16 · Harp Song for a Radical: A History Companion · Unit 18 →

Background for this unit: The History in Harp Song for a Radical

This unit tells the story of the Irish miners of Pennsylvania's anthracite country, the railroad president who set out to destroy their union, and the Pinkerton spy who lived among them for two and a half years. Twenty men were hanged. Young's sympathy is with the condemned, and she frames the trials as a model for later frame-ups from Haymarket to Sacco and Vanzetti (p. 466).

Major entries

Franklin B. Gowen (pp. 437, 445-447, 456, 465-472)

  • Who he was: A Philadelphia lawyer (1836-1889) and former district attorney of Schuylkill County who became president of the Philadelphia and Reading Railroad in 1869. He bought huge tracts of coal land through the Philadelphia and Reading Coal and Iron Company, so that one corporation carried, mined, and sold the coal.

  • What he did: In October 1873 he hired Pinkerton, through the agency's superintendent George Bangs (p. 437), to infiltrate the Irish miners. He then served as a special prosecutor at the Molly Maguire trials, delivering the closing arguments himself.

  • His end: On December 13, 1889, he was found shot dead in Wormley's Hotel in Washington, where he had come to argue a rate case against Standard Oil before the Interstate Commerce Commission (p. 471). It was ruled a suicide, though rumors of a Molly revenge circulated.

  • Further reading: Kevin Kenny, Making Sense of the Molly Maguires (1998).

James McParlan (pp. 441, 450-464)

  • Who he was: An Irish-born Pinkerton detective (1844-1919) from County Armagh. From October 1873 he lived in the coal towns as "James McKenna," a drifter and heavy drinker, moving through Swatara, Tremont, Rausch Creek, Donaldson, and other towns (p. 450). He joined the Ancient Order of Hibernians and rose to secretary of the Shenandoah lodge.

  • The trials: From May 1876 he testified for days, naming dozens of men. Defense lawyers argued that he had known of killings in advance and failed to stop them, even encouraged them (p. 464).

  • Later career: As head of the Pinkertons' Denver office, he took the 1906 confession of Harry Orchard, who blamed the leaders of the Western Federation of Miners, Big Bill Haywood, Charles Moyer, and George Pettibone, for the bombing murder of former Idaho governor Frank Steunenberg (p. 441). Debs wrote his fierce article "Arouse, Ye Slaves!" in their defense. Clarence Darrow won Haywood's acquittal in 1907.

  • Further reading: J. Anthony Lukas, Big Trouble (1997), on the Idaho case.

The Molly Maguires and the Ancient Order of Hibernians (pp. 437-460)

  • Who they were: The name came from Irish rural secret societies such as the Ribbonmen (p. 457), which used threats and violence against landlords and their agents. In Pennsylvania it was applied to Irish miners, many from Donegal (p. 452), accused of killing mine bosses and officials from the 1860s through 1875. Their meetings were said to happen within lodges of the Ancient Order of Hibernians, a legal Irish Catholic fraternal society.

  • The debate: Historians agree some killings happened and that some AOH members were involved. They dispute whether an organized conspiracy existed or whether Gowen used a few crimes to discredit the whole Irish working class and its union.

  • The church: Catholic bishops condemned the Mollies. Young tells of a priest, Father Koch, barring the burial of a suspected member (pp. 458-459).

The Workingmen's Benevolent Association and the Long Strike (pp. 446-447, 455-456)

  • What it was: The anthracite miners' union, founded in 1868 under John Siney (p. 455). In 1869 it accepted a sliding scale, which Young calls the "Gowen Compromise," tying wages to the price of coal (pp. 446-447).

  • The Long Strike: From January to June 1875 the miners struck against wage cuts. Gowen and the other operators starved them out, and the union collapsed. The murder trials that followed completed the destruction of organized labor in the anthracite fields for a generation.

  • Further reading: Harold D. Aurand, From the Molly Maguires to the United Mine Workers (1971).

The trials and hangings (pp. 463-470)

  • What happened: Juries excluded Irish Catholics. Young lists the men hanged (p. 463). On June 21, 1877, remembered as Black Thursday or the Day of the Rope, six were hanged at Pottsville and four at Mauch Chunk. Among those hanged at Pottsville were Thomas Munley, convicted of killing the mine boss Thomas Sanger on testimony such as that of Robert Heaton (p. 465), and several men convicted of killing the Tamaqua policeman Benjamin Yost (p. 470). Ten more were hanged by 1879, twenty in all.

  • Jack Kehoe: The Girardville tavern keeper of the Hibernian House and county delegate of the AOH (p. 453). He was hanged in December 1878 for the 1862 killing of the mine foreman Frank Langdon (p. 467). In 1979 Governor Milton Shapp granted him a posthumous pardon.

  • Alexander Campbell: Hanged at Mauch Chunk in 1877. Legend says he pressed his hand on his cell wall to leave a print as a sign of his innocence, and that it cannot be removed.

  • Further reading: Kenny, Making Sense of the Molly Maguires (1998).

Shorter entries

  • Muff Lawler and Pat Dormer (pp. 452-454): Michael "Muff" Lawler was the Shenandoah bodymaster, or lodge head. Pat Dormer kept a tavern where McParlan made his first contacts.

  • The Chain Gang and the Sheet Iron Gang (p. 453): Local gang names that appear in McParlan's reports, which Young suggests he folded under the single label of the Mollies.

  • The Centennial Exposition (p. 472): The 1876 world's fair in Philadelphia, where the giant Corliss steam engine powered the Machinery Hall. William Dean Howells described it in the Atlantic Monthly.

  • Mark Twain's "Revised Catechism" (p. 461): A satire in the New York Tribune of September 27, 1871, which answers the question of who is God with "Money is God."

  • Henry Adams's Democracy (p. 467): An anonymous 1880 novel of Washington corruption, whose heroine is Madeleine Lee.

  • The 1876 election (p. 468): Samuel Tilden won the popular vote, but Rutherford Hayes took the presidency after a disputed count (Unit 19).

  • Jesse James's death (p. 471): Bob Ford shot him in his home at St. Joseph, Missouri, on April 3, 1882.

  • Sacco and Vanzetti (p. 466): Italian anarchists executed in Massachusetts in 1927 after a trial widely seen as unjust.

  • Leo Tolstoy (p. 466): The Russian novelist, whose later Christian pacifism and anarchism Debs admired.

Allusions

  • Shylock (p. 433): Shakespeare's moneylender, for the railroad men.

  • David and Goliath (p. 466): The weak against the strong.

  • Cromwell (p. 451): Oliver Cromwell's brutal conquest of Ireland in 1649-1650, remembered by every Irish miner.

  • Bunyan's Pilgrim (p. 451): From The Pilgrim's Progress.

  • "Westward the Star of Empire Takes Its Way" (p. 447): From Bishop Berkeley's 1726 poem, a slogan of expansion.

  • The Great Pyramid (p. 467): Of Giza, for monuments built by labor.
