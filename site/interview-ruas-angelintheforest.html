The Ruas and Schwartz Interview on "Angel in the Forest," WBAI (undated)

See also: Angel in the Forest: A Reading in Fourteen Chapters

A reading for the Miss MacIntosh project.

The interview at a glance

This is a WBAI conversation recorded in New York, conducted by Charles Ruas, who taught at Columbia, and Joan Schwartz, a video artist. Unlike the other interviews, it is almost entirely about "Angel in the Forest," Young's nonfiction book on the two utopian communities at New Harmony, Indiana, and Miss MacIntosh barely enters. The transcript gives the date only as October 25 with no year. Young's reference to the 1961 plane crash that killed the champion skater Laurence Owen sets a floor, and Ruas conducted his WBAI author interviews in the late 1970s and early 1980s, which is the likely window. The talk is a long piece of oral storytelling, and its value for the project is twofold: it is the fullest record of how Young worked when the material was real history rather than invented lives, and it shows utopia, her declared lifelong theme, at its source.

How the book was made: no research, only living

Young is emphatic that "Angel in the Forest" grew from experience, not study. "I never did any research whatever on New Harmony," she says, and "the research took three days," spent checking a few dates. Her mother and a theater-owning stepfather had moved there and summoned her with a promise: "You'll love this new town. It's the scene of two lost utopias." She arrived around 1940 and "lived there for seven years, in and out," gathering the book from the old-timers, among them the town undertaker, Perry Schultz, who held the property deeds running back to both founders. The form came from her poetry. She began with "a series of 60 ballads in a Robert Browning manner," out of a Depression-era wish to travel the Ozarks and the Blue Ridge collecting American versions of Scottish ballads, and calls herself a "graveyard poetess" of that period, with Gray's "Elegy" and Edgar Lee Masters' "Spoon River Anthology" behind her rather than "Winesburg, Ohio," which she had not yet read. What she found at New Harmony was "a living folklore that is really unique," the only place with two utopias at once, one of married celibates and one of free thinkers.

The approach by water and the symbolic frame

Young reads the place symbolically from the first page. New Harmony sits "toward the toe of the boot" of Indiana on the Wabash, and she entered by water, crossing on a ferry summoned by a great iron bell that had stood "at the river's edge for 50 years," under a WPA bridge so costly that "nobody could afford to go over" it, so that everyone "laughed at Roosevelt's folly." Her governing image is the sandpiper, whose nest-holes in the surf are "older than man." The birds "built no nests," while "the two utopian communities attempted to build nests," and against the ancient shore the utopias "seem transient." She frames the water approach as "the lost Atlantean feeling," the notion that when Atlantis sank "it heaved up America." The utopias themselves she sets as opposites: Rapp's community expecting the world's end by fire and giving up "conjugal bliss," and Owen's built "upon the idea of an eternal future and nothing supernatural," though Owen still said "happiness is the instinct of the universe," which Young notes is itself "a supernatural thought."

Rapp's utopia: celibacy, surveillance, and the end of time

George Rapp came from Wurttemberg, founded a first "Harmony" near Pittsburgh, sold it, and moved inward because, in one explanation Young offers, "he apparently had to have the threat of nature in order to keep his community solidified." The Harmonists were celibate millennialists who believed "the true man was like the true angel," hermaphroditic, beyond the division of the sexes. Young reports the dark rumor, published decades later, that Rapp's son John "died during an act of castration," and notes the adopted Frederick Rapp, a liberal who became one of Indiana's first senators. Rapp solidified the community by claiming a vision of Gabriel, whose stone footprints an English traveler, Schoolcraft, identified in 1832 as those of a prehistoric Indian runner. When Young arrived, a WPA worker was re-chiseling "the footprints of the angel" at fifty cents an hour, explaining that he had "to buy the baby's shoes." Rapp kept order through an appearance of omniscience, "as many eyes as the eyes in the feathers of a peacock's tail," aided by an underground passage with peepholes, so that the daily confessions stayed trivial: "I have torn my coat. I have lost a button." Even the doorways were built so that "your back door would never face another person's back door," to prevent "back door gossip." White flags marked the property's edge, past which the Rappites never went, and they never learned English. A town crier sang each night, "Another Day Passes One Step Nearer the End." Whiskey was "the greatest of all their products." At the center of the maze, a symbol "of birth," sat a little golden book, which Young notes "came before Joseph Smith's discovery of the golden book of the angel Moroni." Byron gave the community a footnote in "Don Juan," calling it "an economy" he would not join because it faced "extinction at the end of the line." The one thing Rapp could not communize was the chicken yards. The Harmonists "would give up sex" and "individuality" but "would not give up their chickens," and Young delights in "father Rapp and the chickens that he could not control."

Owen's utopia: reason haunted by the irrational

Robert Owen bought the ready-made town after reading an advertisement, "fell in love with the description of the hattery, the cocoonery, the orchards," and set on that "Eviless and Adamless Eden" a community of free thinkers with "freedom of woman." A reformed mill owner from New Lanark, "an enlightened capitalist," he taught three states of mankind: the "capitalistic state of the past"; the present, a "rudderless ship" on "an unknown sea"; and a future of "cooperative socialism." He replaced the commandments with "twelve laws" and, as a young man, had debated Coleridge in Manchester against the existence of spirits. Yet the irrational, Young says, "is what appealed to me." When his communes failed and he went deaf, Owen "began to hear voices in the deep," among them "the voice of Coleridge, the voice of Thomas Jefferson, the voice of the Duke of Kent." The colony itself came apart in ordinary human ways. The intellectuals arrived on a keelboat Young calls "the boatload of knowledge," which froze in the ice; then "the mechanics and the intellectuals were having battles," and "social snobbery" reappeared among "the classes they had escaped." Women resented becoming "the mother of every child in the community." A glassblower, Bakewell, wondered "whether human nature could be blown into new forms." A goat ate the community bookkeeper's ledgers, and he "was fired for negligence." An old man, Greenwood, walked in a thunderstorm holding an iron bar, "praying to God to strike me dead." One resident summed up the disillusion: "you can keep your Utopias, but I do want my own shirts." Owen would not admit failure, sold the ground to a man named Walker who stripped the town and opened a saloon, leaving "a community of happy drunks still talking about happiness," and carried his crusade back to the British labor movement. He died at Newtown in Wales, lying down in the room where he was born to say, "relief has come." Along the way Young sets his great comic scene at the Congress of Aix-la-Chapelle, where he tried to hand the Tsar of Russia a scroll of the laws of human nature and was told, "I have no pockets," and later that "the spirits are very active tonight."

What drew her: the irrational and the city nowhere

The interview makes plain what Young was after. It was not the blueprint of utopia but "the whole inquest into the irrational things about utopias," the negative alongside the positive, and the founders' own descent into the supernatural, Rapp's Gabriel and Owen's voices. Her conclusion is that "the dream of utopia was never realized in its completion," that it was "a city outside every latitude and every longitude," "nowhere," which she likens to "Dickens' Castle Nowhere." Yet she credits both communities with lasting gifts, "beautiful workmanship in the Rappite community" and, from the Owenites, "the education of women" and the idea of housing with greenery, holding that "the whole thing is still a possibility for the future." Her final division is a good summary of the book's balance: "what we have of Rapp is physical," his brick houses and maze, while "what we have of Owen is spiritual," a continuing influence on "American political life and social consciousness."

New Harmony as living folklore

The last stretch describes the town Young knew as a place where the two utopias still quarreled. Sympathizers of Rapp and of Owen divided "like between Republicans and Democrats," so that a drink or a kiss drew the warning "father Rapp will get you," and the split was written even in the trees, the Owenites' golden rain trees against the Rappites' black locusts, with rain-tree shoots for sale "6 for 25 cents." She catalogs the Rappite dolls "under a glass bell," the farm women who chewed cloth to test whether the colors would run, and a newspaper editor who invented gossip until believing him left one "in a state of minor hysteria." The Rappite graveyard, still Rappite property, "has no tombstones," because Rapp wanted no one troubled with "rolling them away when Gabriel blew his horn" and wanted no "individuality" carried into death; its open graves were "filled with flowers," and the quail rustle in the grass "like dead souls," over an older Indian graveyard the Rappites never knew was beneath them. Young names the keepers and heirs: Jane Owen, Robert Owen's great-granddaughter-in-law, restoring the town and housing visiting poets and philosophers; the novelist Grace Zaring Stone, who wrote as Ethel Vance and hoarded Owen documents in Connecticut in fear of fire; and Frances Wright, whose harpsichord survived. The macabre threads keep coming: the self-styled Count Leon who lured a third of the later community into "Epicurean sex"; Frederick Rapp killed by a falling oak, "the foxes had eaten away his limbs," amid a rumor the tree had been "partially sawn"; and one man who lived "under celibacy and free love and polygamy in a single lifetime," moving from Rapp to the free lovers to the Mormons. The land was finally sold to Bethlehem Steel, "a fabulous fate for the old dream," and Young ends on the champion Owen skaters killed in the 1961 crash, "their silver skates strewn over the landscape for miles."

Set against the other interviews

For the compilation, this interview grounds several patterns the others state in the abstract. It shows utopia as her real subject, the "communal utopia" she names in 1983 as the first of her three, and it lets the graveyard sensibility she jokes about elsewhere ("I love dead people") be seen at work in a book born of a "graveyard poetess" among actual graves. The irrational that draws her to the opium lady in the Miss MacIntosh interviews is the same pull she names here toward Rapp's and Owen's visions, and the Gogol she credits in 1983 surfaces in the quail moving "like dead souls." Browning returns as well, now as the model for the ballads that became the book. The most useful cross-link is geographic: in 1983 Young says Vera arrives, in Miss MacIntosh, at the same New Harmony village on the Wabash "with all utopian references removed." This interview is the key to that erasure, since it holds the utopian history she stripped out to leave only the town. What this interview does not do is dwell on the making of the novel, so its drifts are few; its job in the set is to supply the lived Indiana ground beneath the whole body of work.
