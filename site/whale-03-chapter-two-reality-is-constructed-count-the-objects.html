Chapter Two. Reality Is Constructed: Count the Objects

← Chapter One. Not One Self: I Will Kill You If You Tell · Be Bak in a Whale · Chapter Three. Grief Doesn't End: The Earth Took Them Comple →

Pillar: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed · Across every book

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

I loved horror as a kid. Not the idea of it. The actual thing. I had a closet full of it. While other girls read romance I read horror, and especially Stephen King, because King understood something about the world that the sanitized version of childhood was not allowed to say: that the thing in the dark is real, that the structure holding everything together is more fragile than the adults want you to know, that fear is a form of honesty.

I stopped at Gerald's Game.

The premise was a woman handcuffed to a bed with a man on top of her who has just died. I read the setup and thought, now that is horrifying. And then I thought, this is ugly and nasty and not what I want to read from a man's point of view. I didn't have the language yet for what I was feeling. I just felt the wrongness of it, the specific quality of wrongness that comes when you understand, below the level of articulation, that the woman in the story is not the subject. She is the situation. Her constraint is the spectacle. Her interior life, whatever it contains, is the setting for someone else's horror, framed for someone else's consumption.

I put the book down and started looking at the shelves.

Everything I had been consuming over decades (books, movies, television). The directors. The authors. The critics who told me what was worth reading and watching. All men. I had been surrounded by women in my actual life, women who were the texture and substance of my daily reality, and none of them were anywhere in my interior life. The interior life I had built, the one stocked from years of reading and watching and absorbing what I had been told was the best, had been curated entirely by men and I had received that curation in good faith because I had no framework yet for questioning it.

That had to stop.

The decentering took years and it came in layers. The shelves first, then the directors, then the deeper discovery, the one that cut the deepest was eighteen years of formal education, the best pedagogy money could buy, built almost entirely on studies of men and boys. The curriculum I had trusted as rigorous and complete had been constructed from half the species and handed to me as universal. I had received it in good faith. I had taught from it in good faith. And then the wig came off and I understood that the solid ground of my entire intellectual formation was a construction, a very specific curation of what counted as knowledge and what counted as the best, and the curation had never been neutral.

I recentered. I went looking for what the curation had left out.

Marguerite Young was what was waiting at the end of the search.

⁂

The second pillar of Young's philosophy is this: illusion is not the distortion of reality but its medium. Human beings do not live in the world and sometimes retreat into illusion. They live in illusion the way fish live in water, and what we call reality is one particular illusion that has achieved consensus.

This is more radical than it sounds and Young knows it. The Western tradition has organized itself around the real and the illusory as a foundational opposition since Plato's cave as the shadows on the wall are illusion, the sunlit world outside is reality, and the philosopher's task is to drag people toward the light. The entire project of epistemology, of figuring out how we know what we know, is built on the assumption that there is something to know and that knowing it means getting past the distortions that stand between the mind and the real.

Young answers this differently: the sunlit world outside the cave is not reality. It is a larger cave, more elaborately lit, whose inhabitants have agreed to call their shadows real. Consensus reality is not less constructed for being agreed upon. It is just more crowded.

The vehicle for this demonstration is Catherine Cartwheel.

⁂

Catherine is Vera's mother. She lives in an opium world in her mansion on the eastern seaboard while Vera is raised by Miss MacIntosh. The critical tradition has read her as the novel's negative pole: the deluded one, the absent mother, the cautionary example of what happens when you abandon consensus reality for interior fantasy. Young refuses that reading at every turn, and the way she refuses it is through the catalog.

Young does not sketch Catherine's world. She does not gesture at it or summarize it or give the impressionistic outline of a dream sequence. She catalogs it. Object by object, detail by detail, with the same obsessive specificity she brings to everything else in the novel. Catherine's interior world has geography, population, history, and internal logic. It has the weight of accumulated specific reality. Young gives it the same density as the Indiana plains, the same insistence on its own existence, the same refusal to be treated as secondary or derivative or less than.

I want to be honest about what I did with the catalog in the early readings. I thought that if I dig into it far enough I will find the answer. I followed it the way you follow a difficult argument, looking for the point where the meaning would crystallize, where Young would finally say what the catalog was for.

The answer is not at the end of the catalog. The answer is the catalog itself.

The accumulation is the argument. Young is not building toward a revelation about Catherine's world. She is demonstrating, through sheer density and specificity, that Catherine's world has as many things in it as the solid world, as much weight, as much insistence on its own reality. The catalog is Young saying: count the objects. Count the details. Count the specific gravity of what Catherine inhabits. And then tell me which world is real.

You cannot. That is the point. The catalog makes the question unanswerable not because it is evasive but because it is honest. The question of which world is real presupposes a framework for adjudicating between them, and that framework is what Young is dismantling. You cannot stand outside both worlds and judge them from neutral ground because there is no neutral ground. There is only the world you are living in, which is as constructed as Catherine's and as real as Catherine's, and the belief that yours is the standard against which hers should be measured is itself the illusion the novel is built to expose.

⁂

The war on drugs was a war on Catherine.

Not literally, not exclusively, but structurally. The criminalization of substances that alter consciousness is the political expression of the second pillar's refusal, of the insistence that consensus reality is the only legitimate reality, that the interior life built outside its boundaries is not a world but a pathology, and that the people who inhabit those interior worlds must be brought back into line or destroyed. The threat was made so high that one step out of place meant falling to the bottom, and in this country the bottom is a literal hell. Not a metaphor. Incarceration, poverty, death, the permanent mark of a record that follows you out of every door you try to open.

I didn't smoke marijuana until it was legal in my state. And my reaction when I finally did was wondering what exactly the fuss was about. And I knew immediately. The fuss was never about the substance. It was about the interior life the substance made possible. The relaxation that allowed the questioning. The relaxation that gave one the ability to step outside the productive arrangement. The self that refuses to be only legible and useful to the system that requires it to stay in place. Catherine's opium world is not the failure of a self that couldn't cope with reality. It is the interior life of a person for whom the exterior world as constructed offered insufficient reality, and the system that criminalized it was the same system that handcuffed the woman to the bed and called it horror for the reader's entertainment.

Young understood this in 1965. She built Catherine's world with the full weight of her attention because she understood that the interior life of a woman is not the setting for someone else's story. It is a world. It has a catalog. The catalog is the proof.

⁂

I was a daydreamer. I checked out by listening to music and reading continuously, and later by watching films, and I was conscious and careful about what I consumed. I looked for what was considered the best. I followed the lists and the critics and the syllabi and the canon because I trusted the curation. I believed the best was the best because the people telling me it was the best were the people the system had designated as the authorities on bestness.

What I was doing, though I didn't have the language for it yet, was exactly what Catherine does. Building an interior world from the materials available. Stocking it as carefully and specifically as Young stocks Catherine's opium world. The difference is that my materials had been pre-approved. My interior world was built from the canon and the canon had been built by men and called universal, and I had received it as universal because the construction of it as universal was so complete and so long-standing that it had achieved the status Young identifies as consensus reality: not less constructed for being agreed upon, just more crowded.

Gerald's Game was the moment the catalog failed. Not King specifically, not horror specifically, but the specific image of a woman whose interiority is the setting for a man's premise, framed for a male reader's consumption, handed to me as part of the best that the genre had to offer. And I felt it before I named it. This is not my world. This catalog does not contain me. The consensus reality of this interior life that I have been building from approved materials does not have room for what I actually am.

That feeling is what Young calls the recognition that there is no single reality.

Not the philosophical argument. The felt experience of the argument landing before the argument has been made. The moment you understand that the world you have been living in is a construction and you have been mistaking the construction for the world.

⁂

The political consequences of the second pillar are where Young's philosophy becomes most dangerous to the systems that depend on the naturalization of their own construction.

Every dominant system depends on appearing not to be a system. On appearing to be simply the way things are, the natural order, the obvious truth that doesn't require defense because it is just reality. The literary canon is the best literature because it is the best literature. The curriculum is rigorous because it is rigorous. The drugs are dangerous because they are dangerous. The woman handcuffed to the bed is horror because horror is horror. None of these require examination because they are not constructions. They are facts.

Young's second pillar removes the ground from under all of it.

If all reality is constructed, then the natural order is always already a political choice, and the political choice can be named and challenged and remade. The canon is not the best literature. It is the literature that the people with power to define the best decided what was the best, and that decision encoded their interests and their blind spots and their specific construction of what interiority looks like and whose interiority counts. The curriculum is not rigorous. It is the knowledge that the people who built the curriculum decided was worth knowing, and they built it from studies of men and boys and called it universal because calling it universal was more convenient than calling it partial.

This is why Young's insistence on Catherine's catalog is not just an aesthetic choice. It is a political act. Giving Catherine's world the same density as Miss MacIntosh's world, the same weight of accumulated specific reality, is Young refusing to participate in the hierarchy that decides which interiors count as real. It is Young saying: I will not write Catherine thin. I will not make her world decorative or derivative or secondary. I will give it the full weight of my attention and let the reader count the objects and decide for themselves which catalog is real.

That is the same act I performed when I stopped trusting the curation. When I looked at the shelves and the syllabi and the lists and understood that what had been handed to me as universal was particular, as neutral was interesting, as the best was a construction built by people who had decided in advance whose interior life was worth the catalog.

Men had their writing poured over for decades. Young had been filed under difficult women writers and left there. There was no reason I couldn't do what the tradition had done for the writers it valued. I would pour the attention over her work until it yielded what it actually contained rather than what the filing system had decided it contained.

That decision was itself the second pillar enacted as a scholarly practice. The realization that the constructed real of the literary canon was a political choice, not a natural order. The refusal to mistake the dominant construction for the only construction. The decision to build a different catalog.

⁂

Young's formal choices in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling are themselves an argument about illusion that goes deeper than content.

The novel does not distinguish between Catherine's interior world and the exterior world in its prose. The sentences that move through Catherine's opium visions have the same texture, the same density, the same commitment to specificity as the sentences that describe the Grey Goose bus or the Indiana landscape or Miss MacIntosh's belongings left on the beach. Young refuses the formal signals that literary fiction conventionally uses to mark the boundary between interior and exterior, between dream and waking, between illusion and reality.

This is not a stylistic choice. It is a philosophical position built into the craft. If the prose treated Catherine's world as thinner, as marked by the conventional signals of unreality, it would be reproducing the hierarchy it is dismantling. The form would be saying what the argument denies. Young is too rigorous for that inconsistency.

The result is the disorientation of the early chapters, which I experienced as drowning. I could not find the boundary between what was real and what was Catherine's opium world because Young had removed the boundary. Not hidden it. Removed it. Philosophically. Because the boundary was the construction the novel was built to expose.

The disorientation was not a failure of communication. It was the communication. Young was doing to the reader what the second pillar does to the concept of reality: making the boundary unstable, and then asking you to live in the instability with your eyes open.

I had spent years reading toward this without knowing it. Every daydream, every album listened to in the dark, every film chosen because someone had called it the best, every hour with my closet full of horror novels. I had been building an interior world from the materials available and I had been doing it because the exterior world as constructed was insufficient. Not wrong, not evil, not to be rejected entirely. Insufficient. It did not have room for the full complexity of what I was, and so I went somewhere else, and the somewhere else was as real as anything I was being told was real, and Young was the first writer I encountered who built a novel that said so in its bones.

⁂

There is something Young says in the Friedman and Fuchs interview that I return to every time I teach this pillar to myself:

When you have examined all the illusions of life, and know that there isn’t any reality, but you nevertheless go on, then you are a mature human being. You accept the idea that it is all mask and illusion and that people are in disguise. You see the crumbling of reality and you accept it.

Maturity, in Young's system, is not the achievement of certainty. It is the willingness to keep going after the certainty has crumbled. Not because a new certainty will replace it. Because going on is what an honest life requires when you have stopped pretending that the mask is the face.

The people who are most damaged by the refusal to examine illusion are the people whose lives have been most thoroughly organized by someone else's construction of reality. The woman in Gerald's Game. The people whose interior worlds the war on drugs criminalized. The students who are educated on a curriculum built from half the species and presented as universal. Young understood this. She saw that the demand to accept consensus reality as the only reality was not a philosophical position. It was a political mechanism for keeping the constructed order in place by making the construction invisible.

Catherine's catalog is the refusal of that mechanism. It is Young pouring the full weight of her attention onto an interior world that the mechanism had decided did not count, and insisting, object by object, detail by detail, that it counts.

The catalog is the argument. The accumulation is the proof. The density is the politics.

⁂

I said in the prologue that I never belonged anywhere until I read this book. The second pillar is part of the reason why.

The interior world I had built across decades of reading and listening and watching and dreaming had always felt, at some level, like a confession of inadequacy. Like evidence that the exterior world as given was sufficient for normal people and I was the anomaly who kept retreating from it into music and books and film and daydream. The culture that handed me Gerald's Game as horror also handed me the implicit message that the woman who needed a richer interior life than the approved catalog provided was the problem, not the catalog.

Young's second pillar says: the catalog was always the problem. The interior world you built was not a retreat from reality. It was a reality. It had objects in it. It had weight. It had the specific gravity of everything you brought your full attention to, and the full attention was itself the proof that the world was real.

Catherine is not the cautionary example. Catherine is the witness to what happens when the interior life is given room. When someone refuses to make it secondary or derivative or thin. When someone catalogs it with the same obsessive care that the tradition reserves for the exterior world it has decided is real.

Young catalogs Catherine. I catalog Young. The act is the same. The politics are the same. The refusal is the same.

There is no single reality.

The beauty of it is that you nevertheless go on.

Walking toward the catalog.

Counting the objects.

Finding that the world you were told was not real has more things in it than the world you were handed.
