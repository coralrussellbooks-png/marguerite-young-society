Unit 21, pp. 577-581: Afterword and Editorial Note

Part of a reading of Harp Song for a Radical (1999), Marguerite Young’s life of Eugene Victor Debs.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Unit 20 · Harp Song for a Radical: A History Companion

Background for this unit: The History in Harp Song for a Radical

The book stops short of the Pullman strike, the chapter Young was writing when she fell ill. The Afterword instead lets Debs speak for himself, through his essays remembering the nameless pioneers of labor, and closes with Victor Hugo and Norman Thomas. The Editorial Note sketches what the book leaves out: the strike of 1894 and Debs's first term in jail, where his socialism began.

Major entries

Debs's essays on the labor movement (pp. 577-579)

  • What they are: Young quotes three pieces. "Unionism and Socialism: A Plea for Both" (1904) remembers the "untitled agitators of the earlier day" who organized their trades and often "filled an unknown grave" (p. 577). "Socialism" contrasts idle capitalists "choosing judges, buying editors, hiring preachers" with workers who "sweat and groan and bleed and die" (pp. 578-579). "The American Movement" surveys the century and concludes that "the few have ruled, the many have served, the parasite has worn the purple of power" (p. 579).

  • Why it matters: These essays are Debs's own account of the history this book reconstructs. His claim that "No strike has ever been lost" (p. 578) expresses his belief that every defeat, from the anthracite fields to Pullman, built the movement.

  • Further reading: Tim Davenport and David Walters, eds., The Selected Works of Eugene V. Debs (2019 onward), which collects his writings by year.

Victor Hugo's words on socialism (pp. 579-580)

  • What it is: Debs drew strength from a passage Hugo wrote in exile in 1864, in the book William Shakespeare: "The transformation of the crowd into the people—profound task! It is to this labor that the men called Socialists have devoted themselves during the last forty years" (p. 580).

  • Why it matters: Hugo was exiled to the Channel Islands from 1851 to 1870 for opposing Napoleon III. His socialism was moral, humane, and national, the tradition Debs belonged to, closer to Weitling and Phillips than to Marx's economics.

The Pullman strike and boycott of 1894 (p. 581)

  • What happened: In the depression of 1893 George Pullman cut wages at his car works by about a quarter without lowering rents in his company town. His workers struck in May 1894. In June the American Railway Union, Debs's industrial union of all railroad workers, voted to stop handling Pullman cars. Traffic halted across much of the country west of Chicago.

  • The government's response: The railroads' General Managers' Association had the U.S. attorney general, Richard Olney, a former railroad lawyer, obtain a sweeping federal injunction forbidding the union's leaders to direct the strike. President Cleveland sent federal troops to Chicago over Governor Altgeld's protest. Some thirty people were killed. Debs was arrested for contempt of the injunction.

  • Where the record differs: The Editorial Note names the union the "American Railroad Workers Union" (p. 581). It was the American Railway Union.

  • Further reading: Richard Schneirov, Shelton Stromquist, and Nick Salvatore, eds., The Pullman Strike and the Crisis of the 1890s (1999).

Debs in Woodstock jail and *In re Debs (p. 581)

  • What happened: In December 1894 Judge Peter Grosscup's court sentenced Debs to six months for contempt, without a jury. In In re Debs (1895) the Supreme Court unanimously upheld the injunction as a use of federal power over interstate commerce and the mails. Debs served his term in the county jail at Woodstock, Illinois, in 1895. The American Railway Union never recovered.

  • Becoming a socialist: In his essay "How I Became a Socialist" (1902), Debs wrote that visitors, among them the Milwaukee socialist Victor Berger, brought him books in jail, including Marx's Capital. He came out saying the injunction had taught him what the government served. He backed the Populist William Jennings Bryan in 1896 and declared himself a socialist in 1897.

  • Thoreau: The Editorial Note compares Debs to Henry David Thoreau, jailed for a night in 1846 for refusing to pay a poll tax that supported the Mexican War and slavery. Thoreau's essay "Civil Disobedience" (1849) came from it.

  • Further reading: Salvatore, Eugene V. Debs (1982), chapters 5 and 6.

Shorter entries

  • The House of the Dead (pp. 577, 581): Dostoyevsky's prison memoir (Unit 20). The Editorial Note says Debs felt "a shock of recognition" reading it.

  • Plato and Sir Thomas More (p. 577): The Republic and Utopia (1516), the founding books of the utopian tradition that fills Part One.

  • Norman Thomas's portrait (p. 580): Young ends with Thomas's image of Debs walking the earth in his gray suit among the stars (Unit 1).

Allusions

  • "Scrip in his purse" (p. 577): From Christ's instructions to the apostles to travel without provisions, Luke 10.

  • "Screw courage to the sticking point" (p. 577): Lady Macbeth's words in Macbeth, Act 1.

  • "By its fruits we know the tree" (p. 579): Matthew 7 and 12.

  • Les Misérables (p. 579): Hugo's poor, whom Debs saw in every factory town.
