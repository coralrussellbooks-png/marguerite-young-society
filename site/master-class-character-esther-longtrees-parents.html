Esther Longtree's Parents: The Watchman and the Mourner

Characters: Esther Longtree · Rosemary

Esther Longtree was made by two failures of attention, and between them they explain her. Her father is "the passive policeman," "the night policeman who separated loving couples in the graveyard and always whistled to warn them when he came." He is the law that refuses to catch anyone. He "had even let the bank-robber slip through his fingers." He climbs the graveyard hill in the moonlight "swinging his stick against the tombs so he would catch no guilty one." He is big-boned and gentle, and his gentleness is a form of absence: even if he had known his daughter was endlessly, secretly pregnant, "he might have said nothing." He keeps his watch among the dead while the living crisis happens behind his back, and he hears, at the crucial hour, "only the tooting of the river boat."

Her mother is the opposite kind of failure. She sees too much of the wrong thing. She is frail, with "small, delicate veins, so frail and recessive a heart," who nearly died bearing Esther and never forgave her for it. And she is the keeper of Rosemary, the perfect stillborn twin, whom she mourns by turning the grief into a weapon. She "constantly reminded" Esther, "ever since she could remember, that one was missing, that she had killed it with her big feet threshing in the womb." On the shared birthday "her mother had baked two cakes or none." She loves the dead child she built out of words and cannot love the living one she got, and she tells her so, in a thousand small ways her gentle husband never notices.

So the daughter grows up unseen by one parent and misseen by the other. The father who will not look and the mother who looks only at the dead perfect version make, between them, a child who is invisible as herself and hyper-visible as a murderer. "Little did her parents ever realize what was ever unrealized." They register "her weight and her fluctuations and her voice," and they miss the whole truth of her. "They should have seen, if they had had eyes to see with." This is the book's steady charge against everyone who fails its one commandment, and here it falls on the two people who were supposed to be the first witnesses of a life and were the first to look away.

The horror underneath them is that they repeat, one generation up, the very pattern that will define Esther. The mother's own pregnancy was not from the passive policeman but at the old mule farm, from "the other woman" who "was, it seemed in that terrible minute, a man against whom her struggles were all useless," "cold as stone." A legal father who is not the real one, a conception with a stone stranger, a child who is a reproach and looks "like the old go-between." Esther will do it all again with the little toy salesman she chooses as "the legal father." The past is not behind her, it is beneath her, and she claims to have witnessed it, "present" at her own conception, having "seen the whole transaction with her little eyes before her eyes were blinded by the light." The thing that made her is buried under her and pressing up, and she was there for it.

The turn for a writer is the one that closes the whole set, because these two are the making of the maker. The father is the guardian who will not look, who whistles so he catches no one, who keeps his watch among the tombs while the real thing happens behind him, complicit by his gentleness. The mother is the one who loves the perfect dead version over the living flawed one, who builds her grief into an accusation and hands it to the child as an identity. Between the watchman who refuses to witness and the mourner who witnesses only the ideal, they make a daughter who spends her whole life trying to give birth to the child who would finally be seen and finally be loved. This is how makers get made, the book quietly says, out of the two ways of not being witnessed, and the maker's whole work afterward is the attempt to do for her creatures what was never done for her, to stay in the room, to look at the living one, to love the thing that actually came rather than the perfect thing that did not. Esther, endlessly pregnant behind her counter, is what the two failures of attention produce, and she is trying, delivery after delivery, to correct them.
