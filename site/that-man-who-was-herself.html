Definition from research

He had found an old woman who had been, in her youth, the greatest Lady Macbeth, though she had left the stage because of a nervous disorder, her conviction that she had killed Banquo, and for years had wandered from hobo camp to hobo camp, dressed as a man. Then one night, protecting her honor, defending herself against a man who had believed she was a man, she had killed him, shooting him with a rusted pistol. And she had drifted on, swearing like a drunken sailor, smoking a brown cheroot, perhaps

because of that one man she could not kill, that man who was herself.

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling

The Play and Its Contamination

In Shakespeare's Macbeth, Lady Macbeth is the engine of the murder. Her husband wavers. She calls on spirits to unsex her, to fill her with cruelty, to stop the passage to remorse. She organizes the killing of King Duncan. She comes apart afterward: the sleepwalking, the compulsive handwashing, the cry that all the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand. She kills herself offstage. The play does not show her death. She simply disappears from it while still alive, broken by the guilt she summoned the spirits to remove and could not finally remove.

Shakespeare's Lady Macbeth is entirely consumed by guilt for what she actually did. She organized a real murder. She cannot wash the blood off her real hands. Her madness is the return of the remorse she tried to block.

The novel's Lady Macbeth is consumed by guilt for what she only performed. She played the role. She was in her youth the greatest Lady Macbeth. And then the role became real to her: she left the stage because of a nervous disorder, her conviction that she had killed Banquo. Not that she had played the role of someone who killed Banquo. Her conviction that she had done it. The performance crossed into reality not through action but through belief. She became Lady Macbeth not by doing what the character does but by experiencing what the character experiences: the guilt that cannot be washed off, the conviction of murder, the nervous disorder that is the form remorse takes when it has no actual crime to attach to.

The Woman Who Became Her Role

She left the stage. This is the first crossing: from representation to belief. The stage is where you perform guilt for an audience and then go home. She could not go home. The guilt followed her out of the theater because it had become real to her, not as performance but as experience. The conviction that she had killed Banquo is not delusion in the clinical sense of having no connection to anything real. It is the actor's occupational hazard taken to its extreme: total identification with the character, the interior life of the role becoming indistinguishable from the actor's own interior life.

She wandered from hobo camp to hobo camp, dressed as a man. This is the second crossing: from guilt to disguise. Lady Macbeth called on spirits to unsex her: make thick my blood, stop up the access and passage to remorse, come to my woman's breasts and take my milk for gall. She asked to be made not-a-woman so that she could do what she needed to do without the passage to remorse that womanhood supposedly carried. The actress, living out the character's logic rather than the character's plot, enacts this unsexing literally: she dresses as a man. She passes out of the category of woman into the hobo camps, the rough male world, the cheroot-smoking drifting life. She has become Lady Macbeth's wish rather than Lady Macbeth's fate.

Then one night, protecting her honor, defending herself against a man who had believed she was a man, she had killed him, shooting him with a rusted pistol. This is the third crossing: from performance to actual act. She has now done what Lady Macbeth organized but did not personally execute. She has killed a man. The guilt that had been unearned guilt, guilt for a performance, guilt for Banquo who was never real, has now earned itself a real body. She has done, in her own person, in self-defense, the thing that destroyed Lady Macbeth from the inside.

That Man Who Was Herself

And she had drifted on, swearing like a drunken sailor, smoking a brown cheroot, perhaps because of that one man she could not kill, that man who was herself.

This sentence is the AND Philosophy's plural self at its most compressed and most violent. There is the man she could not kill: herself. The self she could not eliminate is gendered male in the syntax because she has been living as a man, has killed a man, has become through performance and disguise and actual murder something that the original female self could not have contained. She is a man who is also a woman who is also a character she played who is also a murderer who is also the guilty one who cannot wash her hands. She cannot kill the man who is herself because that man is also herself, and you cannot kill yourself without dying, and she has not died, she has drifted on.

Mr. Spitzer finds her in one of the hobo camps he visits in his wanderings among the derelicts. He catalogs her alongside a banker who did not know he was a banker, a lawyer who did not know he was a lawyer, a judge who did not know he was a judge, a cancer specialist who had not known he was a cancer specialist. All are people who have shed or lost or hidden the identity that the world assigned them. The Lady Macbeth actress is their logical extreme: not someone who has lost an identity but someone who has gained too many, who has crossed from one into another so many times that the original boundary is gone. She is not hiding. She is lost. She has too many selves stacked inside her: the woman, the actress, the character, the murderer, the man.

Shakespeare's Character Become Real

Lady Macbeth is a leading character in Shakespeare's play who goads her husband into regicide, becomes queen, is driven to madness by guilt, and commits suicide offstage. Young takes Shakespeare's character and allows her, through the medium of an actress, to complete the trajectory the play left incomplete.

Shakespeare's Lady Macbeth kills herself offstage and the play moves on to Macbeth's death at Macduff's hand. The guilt that broke her is not resolved, only ended. She exits the play through offstage death before the play reaches its conclusion. Young's actress-Lady Macbeth does not exit through suicide. She exits through transformation: she becomes a man, she kills a real man, she drifts on. She is still alive when Mr. Spitzer finds her. She has survived what Shakespeare's character could not survive, not because the guilt has been resolved but because she has accumulated so many selves that no single guilt can break the whole structure. She cannot wash the blood off Lady Macbeth's hands because she also has the hobo's hands and the man's hands and the killer's hands and the actress's hands and the woman's hands and none of them are the same hands.

The AND Philosophy Reading

The Lady Macbeth passage is the AND Philosophy's plural self demonstrated through the mechanism of theatrical possession. An actor does not become a character. An actor uses the character to discover what the character's experience would feel like from inside. The actress who was the greatest Lady Macbeth discovered, from inside, that the guilt felt real. This is not a failure of sanity. This is the actor's craft taken to its fullest and most dangerous extension: the complete inhabitation of an interior life that is not your own, which is also the AND Philosophy's definition of the witness. The witness inhabits what they are watching. The witness does not observe from outside. The witness feels from inside the thing they are witnessing. The actress who left the stage because she had killed Banquo was not deluded. She was witnessing from inside.

The grief as permanent weather pillar: she drifts from hobo camp to hobo camp because the guilt has no fixed location to attach to. The crime was performed. The conviction is real. The body count has now been made real in self-defense. But there is no house where she can go back to wash her hands. There is no offstage where she can exit. There is only the drifting, the cheroot, the swearing like a drunken sailor, the knowledge of that one man she could not kill.

That man who was herself is the novel's most concentrated statement of the self that grief and guilt and performance and disguise and actual action have produced: a self so plural, so accumulated, so built from crossings and survivals and transformations that it cannot be addressed as a single entity. She cannot kill the man who is herself. She cannot stop being the woman who is also that man. She drifts on. Mr. Spitzer finds her. He catalogs her. He moves on. She is still there, the greatest Lady Macbeth, in the hobo camp, in the cheroot smoke, in the space between the self she started as and the man she became and the self that survives them both.
