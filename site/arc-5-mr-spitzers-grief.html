Arc 5: Mr. Spitzer's Grief (Chapters 53–62)

← Cousin Hannah · Story Arcs Home · Miss MacIntosh's Last Month →

Spoiler note: This page covers Chapters 53 to 62. If you are reading along, read it after you finish Chapter 62.

Overview

Mr. Spitzer and his twin: their opposite natures, the mirrored house, the funeral and its debts, the tailor's bill that will not stop, and his wanderings among street musicians, a silent violinist, wild ducks and a mute who loses his frog, ending in a will that leaves his music to the world.

Central figures: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer · Cousin Hannah · Vera Cartwheel · Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh

Chapter by chapter

Chapter 53

No instrument is wide enough for Mr. Spitzer's feelings. He contrasts Peron, a chameleon of many colors, with himself, a timid chess player who never made the final move. Since the death he has been mistaken for his brother and has come to know things only Peron knew: boxing records, gambling, speakeasies. He calls himself a pale replica, divided between himself and his twin.

Chapter 54

From his years with Catherine, Mr. Spitzer knows the underworld of dreams. He wanders a Boston whose buildings keep rearranging themselves. He helps derelicts and lost heirs, and is troubled by an old accusation that he stole a rajah's jewel. He prefers glass jewels to real ones. The chapter ends with his elegy, written on scraps of paper, blowing away down a dark street while he stands weeping and stamping his cane.

Chapter 55

The twins had opposite mottos: Peron's "carpe diem," seize the day, and Joachim's "hoc opus, hic labor est," this is the work, this the labor. Joachim had planned a quiet life of music and stamp collecting in their twin house of mirrored rooms. Peron borrowed, forged his brother's checks, and left debts. When Peron died, Joachim paid for the funeral and the debts, went bankrupt, and lost the house. He now lives in a beachcomber's shack, still wondering whose funeral it was.

Chapter 56

Mr. Spitzer recalls dressing Peron's body in an elegant black suit cut like his own, and burying him with poker chips, dice, a playing card, and a red rose, while a one-armed violinist and a racetrack bugler played. Over the years he gave Peron's huge wardrobe away to the poor. He doubts reality, wonders who tailored his strange life, and carries a golden key to a door that no longer exists.

Chapter 57

A bill keeps arriving from the tailor who made Peron's funeral suit, though the tailor and his shop cannot be found. Mr. Spitzer follows directions from his dreams and searches city directories in the library. There he meets a Confederate soldier who believes the South won, and readers debating immortality. He imagines a rose named for both brothers, red and white on a single bush.

Chapter 58

On the library steps, Mr. Spitzer meets an old violinist with shaking fingers who plays an imaginary violin. Mr. Spitzer recognizes the silent piece at once, a forgotten minor composition, and hears what is not there. He admits he distinguishes less and less between reality and his dreams, and suspects this may be his only wisdom.

Chapter 59

Mr. Spitzer tosses coins to street musicians and beggars without knowing who is poor or rich, living or dead. His eyesight dims. He considers chance and order and tries to write an equation for the place between life and death. He suspects that the skeleton inside him is not his own, and concludes that everything would be explained if he admitted he was already dead.

Chapter 60

Mr. Spitzer wonders whether he is a musician at all or has really been a gambler, since he reads only the sporting pages. At dusk he practices moose calls in the city, and a moose seems to answer through the fog. Then wild ducks follow him for days, and he decides their cry can be his elegy.

Chapter 61

In a thieves' café, Mr. Spitzer befriends an old mute who has trained a frog in his mouth to speak for him, even to recite the Lord's Prayer. When the frog dies, the two men bury it in an upland meadow, and the walk back seems shorter than the walk there. Mr. Spitzer wonders whether the mute is his ideal audience. He thinks about silence, and about the music he has scattered on scraps across Boston and other cities.

Chapter 62

Mr. Spitzer dreams of rowing down a river that turns from fire to ice. He rewrites his will, leaving his music to the world. He learns the full story of the silent violinist from the library steps, and remembers a clarinetist who killed himself, leaving a note that he was going to join a greater orchestra. Crossing Louisburg Square, he is startled to learn he is in Boston and not Philadelphia.
