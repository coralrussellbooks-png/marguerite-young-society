Arc 5: Mr. Spitzer's Grief (Chapters 53–62)

Part of Story Arcs, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated November 10, 2026

← Cousin Hannah · Story Arcs Home · Miss MacIntosh's Last Month →

Spoiler note: This page covers Chapters 53 to 62. If you are reading along, read it after you finish Chapter 62.

Overview

Mr. Spitzer and his twin: their opposite natures, the mirrored house, the funeral and its debts, the tailor's bill that will not stop, and his wanderings among street musicians, a silent violinist, wild ducks and a mute who loses his frog, ending in a will that leaves his music to the world.

Central figures: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer · Cousin Hannah · Vera Cartwheel · Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh

Chapter by chapter

Chapter 53

Mr. Spitzer has found no instrument wide enough for his feelings and no unity for his life, only an unfinished song marked by many segnos. Since Peron’s apparent suicide he is mistaken everywhere for his brother: strangers stop him on Boston Common and in Scollay Square to ask who won on the dogs and the horses, and to his chagrined surprise he knows. He knows the speakeasies and billiard halls, who wore the featherweight crown longest, the fighter who believed he battled his father’s ghost, the fistmele of archery and the suits of an ancient card game, though he has never seen a fight. He walks Boston in his black cloak, past the Cockerel Church, the State House, and the Beacon Hill house with its twin parlors, and carries a coin for Charon that the old ferryman will not take. He recalls a ward-heeler found at death to have been a woman and concludes that “this life is the chrysalis, and no one knows whether he will be the butterfly or the moth.” Peron was a chameleon crawling across a cloth of many colors; Joachim crawls forever upon a black velvet pall.

Chapter 54

From his years with the opium lady, Mr. Spitzer knows the subterranean world of dreams, and since Peron’s death he hears all day the noises of Peron’s friend the sound effects man, who could make a city where no city was. He walks a Boston that will not hold still: the Flatiron and Fishback buildings loom in the fog, a news-hawk cries that President Wilson lies dead, and the man who carried the lantern for Paul Revere asks him for a tip on the horses. Mistaken for Peron or for a madman, he hands out cheroots to the dead, refuses the railroads, bridges, and lodestars that derelicts offer him, and searches for lost heirs, his greater work being “to reunite a man with himself.” He refuses a carbuncle from a maharajah’s crown, preferring glass to real jewels, then reads that a jewel is missing from a rajah’s crown and that the thief’s description is his own, until he finds the newspapers are ten years old and carry Peron’s obituary, which mourns a famous sportsman. At the end his elegy, written on scraps of paper kept in his opera hat, blows down a long, dark street while he stamps his cane and weeps.

Chapter 55

Mr. Spitzer imagines the life he would have led if his brother had died first: his theoretical music, his butterflies and postage stamps, safe investments in sunken ships and dead Russian bonds, and a tenth symphony that would begin where other composers had ended their lives. He remembers the twin house he designed so that each brother had his half, every portrait, garden, and mirror repeated, and how Peron mocked it, moved the furniture, forged Joachim’s checks, and never did him a single favor. Since Peron’s death his eyes see double, the near things small and the far things large; he sleeps with his eyes open and wakes in the wrong carriage, the wrong passenger behind a skeleton coachman. Peron lived by carpe diem, Joachim by hoc opus, hic labor est. The walls of the twin house have been knocked down, and Mr. Spitzer, broken by the funeral and its debts, lives in an old beach-comber’s house, “still wondering whose funeral it was.”

Chapter 56

Mr. Spitzer remembers dressing Peron for burial in a black swallow-tail suit cut like his own, with a red rose to be known by at the gates of the great city, two coins for the ferryman, poker chips, dice showing only sevens and elevens, a racing form, and the six of hearts. A one-armed violinist and the track bugler played at the grave. Over the years he gave Peron’s enormous wardrobe to the tramps of Boston, Savile Row suits, hats of every kind, and twelve jockeys’ silks, and found among them the tiny suit of a little man no one could explain. He mourns for everything that changes, from the dew on a flower to the Dog Star, and comes to believe that reality is only clothing on a dream. He is a tailor’s dummy, a puppet whose puppeteer is dead, the scraps and patches of a man who does not know whether he is living or dead. He still carries the golden key to a door that is no more, and the dead musician in him will not stop singing.

Chapter 57

A polite, threatening bill keeps arriving from Peron’s tailor for the black funeral suit Mr. Spitzer is sure he paid for in cash, without receipts. In a recurring dream he journeys to pay it twice and finds no tailor, only empty lofts, and wakes from seeing himself in Peron’s brown derby. Awake, he searches Boston for the tailor’s shop, turning left for right because dreams go by opposites. At the Public Library he combs city directories and old newspapers and finds Peron’s death notice and his own name in the society columns, but no tailor and no street. Beside him a man argues with a dead man about the soul, and a young man who cannot be older than twenty swears he took a bullet at Appomattox, where the South won. Outside, at twilight, Mr. Spitzer watches the clouds become a skull, a hand, a bird, and a rose, and wishes for a rose named for both brothers, red and white on a single bush.

Chapter 58

On the Library steps Mr. Spitzer meets an old violinist with a wounded chin and a wild white mane who plays an imaginary violin, and he recognizes the soundless tune at once: a forgotten waltz by a minor nineteenth-century composer, remembered from his childhood. Watching, he decides that the player might have been anyone with a different stick in his hand, batter, billiard player, shepherd, judge, fisherman, king, or conductor, for reality is made of just so many sticks. The music turns his thoughts to Peron, to the broken old sportsmen Peron befriended, and to Peron’s fatal leap. He fears the old man is having a fit or is already dead, but is afraid to applaud or offer money. He has come to distinguish less and less between reality and his dreams, and suspects that this may be his only wisdom.

Chapter 59

Mr. Spitzer wanders the foggy city tossing coins to street musicians, never sure who is poor or rich, living or dead. One old horn-blower he pitied for years turns out to play in a great symphony orchestra and to be richer than he is. Another, a pale cavalier in white silks outside the old Opera House, turns out to be an usher waiting for his cue; Mr. Spitzer’s gold coin lands in his horn, and soon the whole street is tossing coins. He hears bells, horns, and the cries of migratory birds, and writes his silent music on his shirt cuff where Peron once kept his horse scores. He wonders whether Peron’s suicide was a creative act, a reach for life beyond the grave. He grows convinced that the skeleton inside him is not his own, that body, soul, and spirit may have been exchanged, and that he might even be Peron. What he fears most is a third body that is neither brother. At last he suspects that one fact would explain everything: he is already dead.

Chapter 60

Mr. Spitzer wonders whether he is really a musician or only a gambler like Peron, who read nothing but the sporting page, never went to a concert, and could never have imagined silence. A gambler would know how to call moose, and Mr. Spitzer remembers practicing moose calls on an empty city street at dusk until a great purple moose answered him through the fog; ever since, he has only whispered. A gambler would know duck calls too, and once, absent-minded in a windy avenue, Mr. Spitzer must have made one, for a flight of wild ducks came down and rushed past him, or through him, while he stood still like a lighthouse. He decides they were mandarin ducks blown far off course, exotic pilgrims to this shore. Their honking follows him for days or years, and he concludes that he need not write his elegy after all: the wild duck’s horn is his elegy.

Chapter 61

In a thieves’ café, Mr. Spitzer befriends an old mute who has trained a frog to speak in his mouth. One rainy night the frog recites the Lord’s Prayer; later it asks for a light and a nickel for coffee and tells him to put his money on the Robins. When the frog dies on Maundy Thursday, Mr. Spitzer goes with the mute to bury it in a cigar box in a wild meadow far inland, addresses a funeral speech to the frog, and drops a red rose into the grave. On the walk home through the city at dawn he guides the grieving mute through the traffic, hears a bullfrog croak “Too deep, too deep,” and thinks about the deaf: what the mute has lost and what he has been spared, and whether a man who cannot hear is the ideal audience for Mr. Spitzer’s own unheard music, scattered on scraps all over Boston, Philadelphia, Trenton, Camden, and Asbury Park.

Chapter 62

Mr. Spitzer wonders whether he has ever truly slept. He dreams of rowing down a river that turns to fire and then to ice, followed by a great water orchestra of seagulls turned musicians. By lamplight he rewrites his will, leaving all his scraps of music to the world, even to deaf and dead ears, on condition that it be played only on water; he notes that the old pack rat who steals his papers always leaves something in return. No great music, he decides, is ever played as written. He remembers an old horn-blower dying of tuberculosis who cannot stop blowing his horn, and a clarinetist who jumped from a Gloucester roof leaving a note that he had gone to join a greater orchestra. He remembers, too, learning that the silent violinist of the Library steps had played for twenty years in the Philadelphia Symphony without once touching the strings and was dismissed the day he played a real note. Crossing Louisburg Square, he asks where he is and is startled to hear: Boston. He thought he was in Philadelphia.
