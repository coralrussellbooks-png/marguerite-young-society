Chapter Eleven: The Seven Generations as Philosophical Method

← Chapter Ten: The Mandelbrot AND · Radical Immanence Collection · Part Four: What This Requires →

The seven generations principle is usually presented as an ethical rule: make decisions with attention to their effects seven generations forward. This presentation, while not wrong, undersells the principle by treating it as a longer version of the same forward-looking calculation that Western ethics already performs. It is not a longer version of that calculation. It is a different kind of attention.

The difference is epistemological before it is ethical.

When you are reasoning from inside a present moment, the frame of your decision-making is bounded by the horizon of the immediately foreseeable. You can see the consequences that fall within that frame. You cannot see the consequences that fall outside it, not because you are not trying but because the frame itself is the limitation. The frame is the present moment, and the present moment is bounded by what is visible from the horizontal axis: the lateral spread of current relations and immediate consequences.

When you are reasoning from inside the crossing point, from the place where the horizontal axis meets the vertical line of transmission, the frame changes. You are no longer reasoning from inside a present moment. You are reasoning from inside a structure. The structure is the transmission: what was given, what is being carried, what will be deposited. The question is not what the consequences of this decision will be in the foreseeable future. The question is what kind of deposit this decision makes.

This is a different question. It requires different attention. And it makes visible things that the present-moment frame cannot see.

Consider the decision to extract and burn fossil fuels. From inside the present moment, this decision looks like an energy policy question: how do we balance current energy needs against future climate risk? The calculation is forward-looking but bounded by the foreseeable: the consequences within the next fifty or one hundred years are visible, the consequences beyond that horizon are speculative.

From inside the crossing point, this decision looks like a transmission question: what are we depositing in the sediment? The answer is: we are depositing carbon that took three hundred million years to sequester, releasing it in a geological instant, changing the atmospheric chemistry of the planet in ways that will persist for tens of thousands of years. We are not making an energy policy decision. We are making a geological deposit. We are adding a layer to the sediment that the not-yet-born will stand on, a layer that will press down on every layer above it for longer than any human civilization has yet existed.

The seven generations frame does not make this calculation longer. It makes it different. It shifts the question from what will happen to what are we depositing. It shifts the reasoning from consequentialist calculation to geological reading. It shifts the epistemological stance from the present moment to the crossing point.

This shift is not only available for large-scale political and environmental decisions. It is available for every decision that a crossing point makes, which is every decision. What am I depositing in the sediment of the people I am in relationship with? What layer am I adding to the ground that my children or students or colleagues will stand on? What am I passing forward, and in what condition?

These questions do not have clean answers. The seven generations principle is not a decision procedure. It is a method of attention: a way of orienting yourself at the crossing point so that the vertical axis becomes visible alongside the horizontal, so that the transmission line becomes palpable alongside the lateral spread of the present moment, so that you are making decisions from inside the full structure of where you are rather than from inside the partial structure of the present moment alone.

The method is not comfortable. It makes visible things that the present-moment frame keeps invisible. It makes the weight of what you are depositing palpable in a way that is harder to ignore than the abstract knowledge that future generations exist. But it is honest in the way that the ten pillars have been requiring honesty all along: not the comfortable honesty of acknowledging what you already knew, but the demanding honesty of attending to what you have been not-seeing.

The seven generations as philosophical method is the eleventh pillar's practical expression. It is how you live at the crossing point. It is how you make decisions from inside the vertical axis. It is how you carry what you carry with the attention that the carrying deserves.
