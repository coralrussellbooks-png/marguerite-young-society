Catherine Snowden Cartwheel: The Woman to Whom Everything Happens

Part of Teaching a Writing Masterclass, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Mr. Peron/Joachim Spitzer · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Vera Cartwheel →

Spoiler note: This portrait is written for readers who have finished the novel. It discusses the character’s whole story, including how it ends.

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Jock Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

She is in a bed, and she will be in that bed for almost the whole book. That is how you meet Catherine Cartwheel, and it is the right way to meet her, because stillness on the outside and a whole turning world on the inside is the entire fact of her.

Catherine Cartwheel, maiden name Snowden, is Vera's mother. She lives under opium, in what the novel itself calls an opium paradise, a world of dreams that run almost on schedule, tended by imaginary guests and the phantoms of the men she has lost. She rarely leaves the bed. She talks for hours to people who are not in the room and does not always notice the one person who is. She mourns a dead husband, Jock, a fashionable dog fancier, and a dead lover, Peron, and she waits on a surviving brother, Joachim, whom she believes attends her. When the ordinary daylight world presses on her, she answers it by becoming someone else: Catherine the Great, sleeping in a bed with pillars of twisted amethyst.

If Mr. Spitzer is the man to whom nothing happens, Catherine is the woman to whom everything happens, and everything happens on the inside. She is all interior. He is looked past; she does not look out at all. He cannot throw anything away; she cannot stop making things up. They are the two still figures the first half of the book rests on, and they are opposite in the way a photograph and its negative are opposite, which is to say they are the same image.

Catherine looks like the book's cautionary tale: the addict who chose the dream over her own child, who let a governess raise her daughter while she rose and plunged through the years like a rider who will not dismount. Miss MacIntosh, the sensible one, is hired precisely as her correction, the discipline set against the dream, the daylight sent into the opium house. Read this way, Catherine is the problem and Miss MacIntosh is the cure.

The disproportion that undoes that reading is quiet, the novel does not finally take Miss MacIntosh's side. It gives Catherine the richer, truer perceptions and lets the sensible cure turn out to be the deeper illusion. Catherine, dismissed as the one who lost hold of reality, is the one who sees. She is convinced, long before anyone else, that Spitzer is already dead, married to the worm or to his brother, a pale version of himself. Her opium sight looks straight through his living flesh to the dead man inside it. She is right. The confession at the end confirms what the addict perceived decades early and the rational people around her could not. So the novel performs Radical Immanence’s second pillar, that reality is constructed rather than found, from the direction no one expects: it is not the dreamer who is fooled and the sensible woman who keeps hold of the real. The dreamer is the accurate witness, and the sensible woman is wearing a wig.

The opium paradise is a world Catherine built and moved into for good, which makes her the clearest case in the book of a person who treats reality as something you construct and inhabit rather than something you submit to. The imaginary guests, the crowd of illustrious dead in her rooms, are not lapses; they are her holding the lost in the present, the same refusal to let the dead be gone that Spitzer performs with his cheap cheroots. The many dead lovers who blur into one are not confusion; they are her picture of how love actually works, aimed at a composite of the lost that keeps changing its mask. She is not one thing dreaming she is many. She is many, and the dreaming is what the many does.

The living man she cannot see is inside the very blur she mourns. The Peron she grieves and the Spitzer she ignores are one body. Her dream has the right man in it and cannot recognize him, because it holds him under a dead name. She dreams of her love while her love pours her tea. And at the end Spitzer carries her dying body into the garden by the sea and begins at last to tell her the truth, that he is Peron, that he loves her, that he stayed. She dies an hour before, at the start of his whispers. The one recognition the whole counterpart was built toward does not happen. The dreamer who saw everything true does not get to hear the one true thing said aloud.

What stays standing, once the dream and the dreamer come apart, is that Young will not let the collapse become a verdict. Catherine is not simply the addict who wasted a life, any more than the confession simply solves Spitzer. The book treats her constructed world with something closer to respect than to pity, because her way of bearing the unbearable, dreaming the dead into a single deathless beloved, is set exactly level with Spitzer's way, keeping the archive and paying the debts. Two techniques for one task, which is bearing what keeps leaving. Neither works. Both are love. The reality she built was false and it was also the only place she could survive her losses, and the difficulty, which is the whole of Young's honesty, is that both are true at once.

Catherine is the hardest kind of character to write without condescending to her, because everything about her invites the easy verdict: the drug, the neglected child, the retreat from the real. Young's solution is structural. She does not defend Catherine in argument. She simply arranges the book so that the dreamer's perceptions come true and the sensible woman's solidity comes off with her wig, and lets the reader discover, too late to feel superior, that the person they were prepared to pity was the person seeing clearly the whole time. The lesson for a writer is that you can overturn a reader's judgment of a character without ever arguing against it, if you are patient enough to let the plot do the correcting. You withhold the defense and spring it as recognition, which is the same move the novel makes on Catherine herself, and just as merciless about its timing.
