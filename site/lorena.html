In the research: Chapter 64, Chapter 66

Definition from research

Chapter 64

  • Lorena is depicted as a beautiful dancing girl, dark and fat, with long hair described like a "midnight curtain concealing the star until the star was revealed." She first encountered Miss MacIntosh when the latter was working in an orphanage.
  • Lorena laughed at Miss MacIntosh and then ran away, eventually becoming a strip-tease dancer. Years later, Lorena was paroled to Miss MacIntosh’s care, but she ran away again, and Miss MacIntosh could not pursue her due to her role as parole officer.
  • Lorena symbolizes a more flamboyant, elusive, and perhaps tragic aspect of femininity and life experience, contrasting sharply with the sober, practical, and grounded Miss MacIntosh. While Miss MacIntosh is associated with order, responsibility, and a no-nonsense approach to life, Lorena embodies escape, performance, and the allure of the spotlight.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s relationship with Lorena is tinged with affection and regret with they were “very dear friends” for a time, but Lorena’s life took a path of repeated flight and apparent self-destruction, as she “slept with many men” and aged into baldness, a fate that mirrors Miss MacIntosh’s own physical barrenness and loneliness.
  • Miss MacIntosh reflects on Lorena’s invitation to join her in the strip-tease, suggesting Lorena believed Miss MacIntosh would have made a sensational artist in that world. This highlights the tension between Lorena’s world of public display and Miss MacIntosh’s private, concealed existence (especially with her baldness and wig).
  • Lorena’s life is marked by a search for light and visibility, “danc[ing] in the spotlight,” while Miss MacIntosh sought the shadow, the hidden, and the protective fog.
  • Lorena enriches the narrative by providing a foil to Miss MacIntosh’s character by showing a different trajectory of womanhood, freedom, and loss. Lorena’s story also deepens the themes of love, disappointment, and identity, as Miss MacIntosh mourns not only her own solitude but also the loss of a connection that might have been different if circumstances had allowed.
  • The mention of Lorena evokes a poignant sense of fragmented relationships and missed possibilities in the world the novel inhabits.

Chapter 66

  • Lorena appears only briefly but her presence is significant in relation to Miss MacIntosh and the broader themes of the novel.
  • Lorena is mentioned as a beautiful dancing girl with dark hair, described as fat, who ran away and became a strip-tease dancer. This characterization contrasts sharply with Miss MacIntosh’s practical, no-nonsense, and grounded nature. Lorena represents a more sensual, flamboyant, and perhaps socially transgressive aspect of femininity, set against Miss MacIntosh’s sobriety, austerity, and emotional reserve.
  • Lorena’s knowledge of Miss MacIntosh’s baldness before Vera discovers it suggests an intimacy or at least a closer social awareness of Miss MacIntosh’s vulnerability and true self.
  • Lorena’s laughter behind her fan and her awareness of this physical fact highlight the difference in how women in the novel perceive and interact with each other, often marked by a mixture of judgment, awareness, and perhaps envy or pity.
  • Lorena helps underscore Miss MacIntosh’s loneliness and isolation. While Lorena embodies escape, sensuality, and perhaps a more conventional form of social engagement (albeit in a marginal way), Miss MacIntosh remains solitary, bald, and burdened by her practical concerns and emotional detachment. Lorena’s figure acts as a foil to Miss MacIntosh, illuminating the nursemaid’s deeper struggles with identity, acceptance, and the harsh realities she faces.
