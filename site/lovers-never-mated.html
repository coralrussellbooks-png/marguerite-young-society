Miss MacIntosh, My Darling

The Four Couples on the Wall

Miss MacIntosh arrives alone at a honeymooners' hotel. She registers as a single person in a free country. The hotel clerk stares. She has a right. She does not remove her wig. The room has mirrors on the ceiling and mirrors on the walls. The moonlight falls around her bald head in all the mirrors. On the hotel walls, among the sporting blind cupids with golden arrows and the signs about credit not being allowed, hang portraits of doomed lovers: couples who had been separated from each other in one way or another way, who had never tangled or who had missed their loves or were jerked away from their loves by a cruel fate.

Four couples. Romeo and Juliet: separated by family war, united only in death, the love that cannot exist in the world as the world is currently organized. Paul and Virginia: eighteenth-century French colonial lovers separated by class and convention; Virginia drowns in a shipwreck because she refuses to remove her clothing before the sailors who might save her, choosing death over the exposure of her body. Eloise and Abelard: the brilliant student and the great philosopher-theologian, secretly married, then separated by castration and convent walls, their love conducted thereafter entirely in letters, in words, in the written correspondence that became the foundation of European epistolary literature. Tristram and the white Yseult: the knight and the Irish princess who drink a love potion meant for another and are condemned to a love they cannot choose, cannot refuse, and cannot consummate without betraying the king.

Miss MacIntosh looks at these portraits and finds them a dark note where all should have been optimism. Divorce lawyers are working the hotel, handing out cards. The honeymooners are already planning their escapes. The portraits of doomed lovers on the walls of a honeymooners' hotel are not ironic decorations. They are the hotel's honest statement about what love is: not the beginning of happy domesticity but the inauguration of a particular kind of suffering, the suffering of people jerked away from what they most want, who never get it except in the moment of losing it.

Miss MacIntosh has never married. She has no bridegroom. She lies in the honeymoon room with her wig off, bald in all the mirrors, and she is not wrong to think these portraits are a dark note. They are a precise account of what she has avoided and what she has therefore also missed. The plain, practical woman from What Cheer, Iowa is surrounded by the whole tradition of Western doomed love, alone in the room with no one to lose.

What the Couples Have in Common

Each of the four couples is separated by an external force rather than by failure of love. Romeo and Juliet love without reservation; their families kill them. Paul and Virginia love without reservation; the social order and a shipwreck kill them. Abelard and Heloise love without reservation; the guardians' revenge mutilates one and the Church walls in both. Tristram and Yseult love without reservation or even without choice: the potion decides. In every case the love is total and the world is the obstacle. The love does not diminish. The world does not relent. The couples are destroyed by the gap between the completeness of their love and the impossibility of their situation.

This is the AND Philosophy's grief as permanent weather taken back to its origins in the Western tradition of love as fundamentally incompatible with the world. Courtly love, from which both the Tristan legend and the Abelard correspondence emerged, was structurally organized around impossibility. The troubadour loved the unattainable lady. The knight loved the queen who belonged to the king. The philosopher loved the student he should not have touched. The love was most itself when it could not be consummated without catastrophe. Heloise understood this more clearly than Abelard: she argued against their marriage because marriage would diminish what they had. She preferred to be his mistress, his concubine, his anything but wife, because the formal bond would make ordinary what was extraordinary. The world destroyed their love anyway; but Heloise was right that the love was most perfectly itself in its impossible form.

The Book Opened at Page Seven

The opium lady dies reading a little book opened at page seven. Mr. Spitzer notes this with his lawyer's trained attention to unimportant details that might be important in some other case. He does not read the book. He does not know its title with certainty. He has the impression that it was a romance of lovers never mated, Eloise et Abelard.

Matthew Arnold's poem Tristram and Iseult has seven sections, and that Cousin Hannah's copy of the book was open to section seven. The opium lady dies at page seven. Miss MacIntosh carries two pennies for the closing of her own eyes, and they are found in her purse on the beach after she drowns: her own eyes were not closed with pennies because there was no one on the beach when she walked in. The opium lady's eyes are closed with pennies by Mr. Spitzer: one in memory of the sun, one in memory of the moon.

The two pennies are the Charon tradition: coins on the eyes of the dead for the ferryman's payment. But Mr. Spitzer's pennies are differentiated: one for the sun, one for the moon. This is not Charon's undifferentiated toll. This is a cosmological pairing: the opium lady carries the sun and the moon into death on her eyelids, the two governing lights, the day and the night, each represented by a single penny weighted on the eye that will no longer see. One for her absent husband who was her sun. One for herself, who was always and only the moon: reflective, governing by borrowed light, pulling the tides without having any life of her own.

She died at page seven. Matthew Arnold's seventh section of Tristram and Iseult is the conclusion: after Tristram's death and Yseult's death, their bodies lie side by side, and the poem considers what they were, what their love was, whether it mattered. The seventh section is the aftermath. The opium lady dies in the aftermath. She has been in the aftermath of her husband's death for the entire novel. She has been living at page seven, in the section after the main action, in the elegiac meditation on what the love was and whether it was worth it, without ever having been in sections one through six, without ever having drunk the potion or ridden to Ireland or fought the king. She has been in the aftermath of something she never experienced directly, loving a man whose death she did not witness, whose life she barely inhabited, whose ghost has organized everything.

Which Was She?

Mr. Spitzer's question at Cousin Hannah’s deathbed is the chapter's center: which was she? Was she Tristram, or was she the white Yseult? He asks but dares not look, for he was no physician even to a centaur and never would he awaken the dead only that they might die again.

The question is not about identification with a character in a story. It is about the structure of her love. Was she Tristram: the active one, the knight, the one who crossed the sea, who fought for the beloved, who was the engine of the tragedy? Or was she the white Yseult: the one sought, the one loved, the one whose fate was determined by others' actions, who was carried to Cornwall to marry a king she did not choose, who loved Tristram without choosing to love him because the potion chose for her?

The white Yseult is distinguished in the legend from the other Yseult, Yseult of the White Hands, whom Tristram marries in his exile but cannot love. The white Yseult is the one who is loved absolutely. The white Yseult of the White Hands is the one who is married uselessly. The opium lady, who loved her dead husband absolutely and could not be substituted for by any other relationship, including whatever Cousin Hannah offered her, is clearly the white Yseult: the one who is loved and loves back, whose love is organized around an original bond that no substitute can replace. But she might also be Tristram: the one consumed by the potion, the one who cannot help loving, who would cross any sea for the beloved, who dies calling for the ship with the white sail that means the beloved is coming.

Mr. Spitzer does not answer his own question. He closes her eyes. He places the pennies. He does not look at the book again to determine its title. The title fades in his unretentive mind. No language contained truth. The truth was by its very nature the inexpressible. The word never written. The name never told. The door never knocked upon. He has weighed down her eyelids with the sun and the moon and left the question open at page seven.

The Book That Turned a Page After She Died

One more detail from Cousin Hannah’s deathbed scene, the detail that comes before the Abelard question: the opium lady had turned a page of the little book she had been reading when she died, had turned a page after she had died. The book moved. The pages continued to turn after she was gone.

This is not supernatural. It is formal: the story keeps going even when the reader stops. The love story does not end with the death of the person reading it. The doomed-lovers tradition is precisely characterized by this quality: it outlasts every reader, every telling, every death. Abelard and Heloise have been dead since the twelfth century. Tristan and Iseult are fictional and were always fictional. Romeo and Juliet are a play performed endlessly before new audiences who have never seen it before. Virginia drowned in 1788 and is still drowning. The page turns after the reader dies. The love story does not require its reader to continue.

The opium lady reading Eloise et Abelard, or perhaps Tristram and Yseult, at the moment of her death is in correct company. She has spent her life in the doomed-lovers tradition: loving completely, being separated by external force, conducting the remainder of her love in the language of letters and dreams and opium-bedroom visitors because the actual beloved cannot come. Her love does not require her husband to continue. It does not require her to continue. The page turns after she dies. The rose left by the imaginary visitor withers in the storm. The waters grow to still pools forever extending. Mr. Spitzer walks on and on, coming to no end, as the book lies open at page seven with the pennies on her eyes and the sun and the moon weighted shut.

The AND Philosophy Reading

The doomed-lovers tradition is the AND Philosophy's grief as permanent weather made literary: the tradition that has named and formalized the human experience of loving what the world will not allow you to keep. Every pair of portraits on the honeymooners' hotel wall is a previous naming of what the opium lady is living. She is not unique in her grief. She is in the longest and most populated tradition in Western literature. Heloise understood this better than almost anyone: love is most perfectly itself when it cannot be consummated, when the world's interference has reduced it to letters, to words, to the inexpressible things that no language contains, the word never written, the name never told.

The witness pillar: Mr. Spitzer at the deathbed is the witness to a life organized around the doomed-lovers structure. He cannot answer his own question about which role she occupied. He cannot determine the book's title. His mind is like a sieve. He places the pennies and walks on and on. The witness in the doomed-lovers tradition is always the one who survives the love story and carries the question forward: which was she? Which were any of them? The question does not resolve. The page turns. The book stays open at seven. The hotel portraits go on hanging. The divorce lawyers keep working the lobby. The honeymooners keep checking in and planning their exits. Miss MacIntosh lies alone in the room with her wig off, bald in all the mirrors, having carried her two pennies all these years for the closing of her own eyes, which were never closed.
