Unit 19, pp. 513-544: Indiana in 1876 and 1877

Part of a reading of Harp Song for a Radical (1999), Marguerite Young’s life of Eugene Victor Debs.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Unit 18 · Harp Song for a Radical: A History Companion · Unit 20 →

Background for this unit: The History in Harp Song for a Radical

Young follows the Great Strike into Indiana, after a comic detour through the 1876 governor's race in which a farmer in blue jeans beat Benjamin Harrison. When the railroad men of Indianapolis struck in July 1877, Harrison helped organize armed citizens, and a federal judge jailed strikers for contempt. In Terre Haute, Debs's own lodge stayed on the job.

Major entries

The 1876 Indiana governor's race (pp. 517-528)

  • What happened: The Republican nominee, Godlove Orth, was tainted by scandal over claims he had pressed for Venezuelan bond interests (p. 517), and Judge Walter Q. Gresham said openly, "I don't believe that Orth is an honest man." Orth withdrew in August 1876. Benjamin Harrison, who had not sought the job, was drafted to replace him (p. 518).

  • "Blue Jeans" Williams: The Democrat, James D. Williams, was a six-foot-four farmer from Wheatland (p. 517) who campaigned in homespun blue jeans against "Kid Gloves" Harrison, mocked for his aristocratic manner. Williams won narrowly and served from 1877 until his death in 1880.

  • The Greenbackers: The Independent Greenback candidate, Anson Wolcott, withdrew at the last moment (p. 522). The Greenback movement wanted paper money to relieve debtors and farmers in the depression.

  • Further reading: Emma Lou Thornbrough, Indiana in the Civil War Era, 1850-1880 (1965).

The Hayes-Tilden election and the Compromise of 1877 (pp. 513, 525-526, 539)

  • What happened: In November 1876 Democrat Samuel Tilden won the popular vote, but returns from Florida, Louisiana, and South Carolina were disputed. A special Electoral Commission gave every disputed vote to Republican Rutherford B. Hayes, who took office in March 1877. In the settlement, Hayes withdrew the last federal troops from the South, ending Reconstruction.

  • The tax charge: Young notes the Republican charge that Tilden, a railroad lawyer, had underreported a ten-thousand-dollar fee from the Terre Haute, Alton and St. Louis Railroad on his 1862 income tax (p. 513).

  • Why it matters for labor: Within months, the troops pulled from the South were sent against striking railroad workers in the North.

  • Further reading: C. Vann Woodward, Reunion and Reaction (1951).

The 1877 strike in Indianapolis (pp. 523-538)

  • What happened: On July 23, 1877, railroad men in Indianapolis stopped freight trains, led by the fireman Warren Sayre (p. 530). Governor Williams and Mayor John Caven refused to use force (p. 526). Businessmen formed a Committee of Public Safety to arm citizens and a Committee of Mediation to negotiate (pp. 530-532). Harrison served on both and commanded a militia company.

  • Judge Gresham: Several railroads were in bankruptcy under federal receivers, so Judge Walter Q. Gresham treated interference with their trains as contempt of his court. Federal marshals and troops arrested Sayre and others, who were tried by the judge without a jury.

  • Why it matters: This use of court power over bankrupt railroads was a forerunner of the sweeping injunction used against Debs in 1894, when he was jailed for contempt without a jury trial. Harrison's role haunted him with labor voters for the rest of his career.

  • Further reading: Bruce, 1877: Year of Violence (1959).

The Vandalia strike in Terre Haute (pp. 539-543)

  • What happened: The Vandalia's president, Riley McKeen, was a local man with a reputation for treating employees decently (p. 539). When the strike reached Terre Haute, it was brief and peaceful. Debs's Vigo Lodge of the Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen did not support it.

  • Richard W. Thompson: A Terre Haute Republican and Hayes's secretary of the navy (1877-1880), who urged a hard line (p. 541).

  • Why it matters: Debs at twenty-one believed labor and capital could be partners. Watching Gresham's court, the troops, and the blacklists that followed, he began the long change of mind that ended in socialism.

Denis Kearney and the San Francisco riots (p. 514)

  • What happened: On July 23, 1877, a San Francisco meeting called in sympathy with the eastern strikers turned into three days of attacks on Chinese workers and businesses. Denis Kearney, an Irish-born drayman, then built the Workingmen's Party of California with speeches on the city's sandlots under the slogan "The Chinese must go!"

  • Why it matters: The anti-Chinese movement drove the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882. Young notes the bitter irony that Irish and Chinese workers alike had died building the railroads.

  • Further reading: Beth Lew-Williams, The Chinese Must Go (2018).

Shorter entries

  • William H. Vanderbilt and the Albany strike (pp. 515-516): Vanderbilt's New York Central faced strikers at West Albany in 1877. The brakeman John Van Hoesen was arrested for saying workers would answer a diet of bullets with bullets.

  • Schuyler Colfax (p. 519): Indiana's Republican leader and Grant's first vice president, ruined by the Crédit Mobilier scandal.

  • Lew Wallace and Ingersoll (p. 529): Wallace said an 1876 train conversation with Ingersoll started him on the novel Ben-Hur (1880).

  • Petroleum V. Nasby (p. 529): The comic Copperhead preacher created by the Ohio journalist David Ross Locke, a favorite of Lincoln.

  • Albert G. Porter (pp. 529, 532): An Indianapolis lawyer who helped Harrison counsel the strikers and was later governor (1881-1885). Young calls him a general.

  • Coeur d'Alene and Homestead (pp. 536-537): Both struck in 1892, during Harrison's presidency. Federal troops occupied the Idaho mining district, and Pinkerton guards fought the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers at Carnegie's Homestead works.

  • Krupp (p. 544): The German arms maker. Debs's magazine reprinted an 1889 article on him.

  • "The only good Indian" (p. 544): A saying attributed to General Sheridan, which Young finds echoed in the press of the day.

Allusions

  • Regulus (p. 517): The Roman general who returned to torture in Carthage rather than break his word.

  • Vox populi, vox dei (p. 519): "The voice of the people is the voice of God."

  • Hamiltonian and Jeffersonian horses (p. 531): Rule by the propertied few against rule by the many.

  • "A Mighty Fortress Is Our God" (p. 513): Luther's hymn.

  • Bret Harte's "heathen Chinee" (p. 514): From his 1870 poem "Plain Language from Truthful James."

  • Byron's Childe Harold (p. 544): The wandering romantic hero.
