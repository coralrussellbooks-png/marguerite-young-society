Chapter 51

On Chapter 51 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of The Wheel and the Spokes.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 50 · Wheel and Spokes Home · Chapter 52 →

Research notes: Chapter 51 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 51

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

"He did not know whether he was the trumpeter or the trumpeter's cry, the cry or the echo. He did not know whether he was the door or the door opening. He did not know whether he was the key or the key turning in the lock."

Mr. Spitzer is explicitly, relentlessly multiple. He is simultaneously conductor, lamp-lighter, oarsman, lawyer, doctor, postman, lighthouse keeper, musician, gambler, beach-comber. These are not metaphors for a single underlying self; they are competing simultaneous ontological claims, none of which resolves into the others. The text refuses to select one.

The question "whether he was Joachim or Peron" is not a riddle with an answer. It is the condition itself. His identity is not hidden beneath the multiplicity; the multiplicity is the identity. The self here is, as Pillar 1 states, irresolvable, and the chapter enacts this by never settling the question, not even at the end.

Most strikingly, Mr. Spitzer may be his own dead twin:

"Had he given birth to his dead twin, becoming death in life, or had his dead twin given birth to him, becoming life in death, or were they ever one and yet not understood"

This is not psychological ambiguity. It is ontological plurality as the ground condition of selfhood. He does not have an identity crisis. He is the crisis, permanently, structurally.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

"And if all were illusion, then all might be true as often he had remarked. All might be true if all were false."

The chapter does not merely suggest that Mr. Spitzer is confused about reality. It systematically dismantles the distinction between real and unreal as a meaningful opposition. The dream is not secondary to the waking world; it is the only world available:

"it might be that his dream was real, that the dream was the only reality there was"

The philosophy of Pillar 2, "the line between illusion and reality is itself a construction", is enacted through the chapter's prose style itself. The syntax never lands. Clauses accumulate without resolution. The reader cannot find stable ground because the text refuses to provide any, mirroring Mr. Spitzer's condition. There is no outside perspective from which to judge what is real. The larger cave is simply more carefully lit by fireflies.

The image of the squid's ink is particularly precise:

"scattering clouds of ink like the ink of the squid who deceived his enemies into thinking the squid was there when there was only the ink darkening the water"

This is not merely a description of Mr. Spitzer's legal practice. It is a theory of representation: the sign obscures rather than reveals, and what we take for presence is the residue of an escape.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

"Never could this love be born. Never could be found that which had been lost, especially if it had lived only in a dream beyond verification."

Grief in this chapter is not an event. It is the climate. Mr. Spitzer does not grieve and recover. He exists inside grief as inside an atmosphere. The dead love, the dead brother, the suffragette he may never have known; these are not losses he processes. They are the conditions of his perception. He sees everything through them.

"He lived by mourning the death of the deathless one."

This is Pillar 3 stated with almost epigrammatic precision. The grief is permanent precisely because its object, the deathless one, cannot be finally lost. There is no stage of acceptance available because the loss is never complete. The dead brother may be alive. The love may never have existed. The grief has no terminus because its object has no stable ontological status.

The chapter even theorizes why resolution is impossible:

"the greatest happiness might come through the sorrow of waiting, of postponement, for one might know that when happiness came, life melted like the snowflake in the summer's cloud, and thus one's happiness might be one's death, even the death of one's sorrow, that sorrow which had been one's happiness."

Grief and happiness are not opposites here. They are the same weather system, cycling. The honest life, Mr. Spitzer's life, stops pretending otherwise by simply continuing to walk, to reel, to weave.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

"He questioned not the other man's illusions and asked not whether a woman's love was the living or the dead... He asked not a woman's age."

Mr. Spitzer's ethical stance is precisely that of the Witness. He does not fix, conclude, diagnose, or simplify. He stays in the room. He believes the tramp who claims to be a great shah. He believes the beggar who claims to be a financier. This is not naivety. It is a principled refusal to override another's self-construction.

His legal practice enacts this:

"He had learned to accept a man at his own estimate, no matter how wrong — for well Mr. Spitzer knew that every other man contained these abysses, sudden and unexpected waterfalls, evasions, clouds, mists, fogs."

The Witness does not simplify. Mr. Spitzer's inability to simplify (his myopia, his absent-mindedness, his stamping of wrong seals) is reframed here not as incompetence but as a kind of ethical fidelity to complexity. He cannot reduce people to cases because he knows that the reduction would be a violence.

The chapter makes this explicit in the image of the piano-tuner:

"an old piano-tuner tapping every forehead with his tuning fork, tapping Mr. Spitzer's forehead and declaring hollow, hollow"

The piano-tuner (who does conclude, who does diagnose) finds only hollowness. The Witness who refuses to conclude finds everything.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

"he would go back to place the stone where it had been for fear that by changing one stone, he might change rivers in their course."

Mr. Spitzer builds, dismantles, and rebuilds continuously. He signs documents, then questions them. He places stones, removes them, replaces them. He writes wills, adds blots, reconsiders. This is not indecision. It is recursive construction: returning to the beginning with what was learned, knowing the building is never finished.

The legal documents he produces are themselves recursive structures:

"waiting for the ink to dry as he decided whether to change his mind or his will or whether his mind or his will might not change him"

The builder and the building are mutually constituting. His will shapes the document; the document shapes his will. There is no original intention prior to the process of inscription. This is Pillar 5 as epistemological condition: you cannot step outside the construction to evaluate it from neutral ground. You build from inside the building.

His shipbuilding imagery makes this explicit:

"this shipbuilder whose ships were ever on the tide, builder of great honeycombs, builder of islands floating away from continents, builder of continents which were already submerged"

The building is always already dissolving. That is not failure. That is the only mode of construction available in a world of tides.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

"some problems unresolved, some differentials not accounted for, some vistas unexplored as unacknowledged love, some non-negotiable remainder"

The chapter is itself an enactment of incompletion. Its sentences do not end so much as accumulate and dissolve. Its paragraphs do not conclude; they submerge. Mr. Spitzer's legal career is defined by incompletion: cases never brought, documents never properly sealed, wills stamped with the wrong seal.

But the chapter insists this incompletion is not failure:

"Perhaps, of course, he was guilty of more mistakes than accuracies... but what was accuracy in Mr. Spitzer's realm, that of all things which had already departed?"

Accuracy (completion, resolution, the concluded sentence) is revealed as an inappropriate standard for the domain Mr. Spitzer inhabits. His realm is the realm of the departed, the marginal, the already-gone. In that realm, the demand for completion is, as Pillar 6 states, its own violence against experience.

The uncompleted elegy is named directly:

"Perhaps his was that uncompleted elegy which must complete itself."

The elegy that completes itself is no longer an elegy. The incompletion is the form. Integrity looks like the ongoing sentence.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

"Perhaps, of course, he was guilty of more mistakes than accuracies... by a gambler's error he lived, practiced that which some believed was law, pursued lawlessness"

Mr. Spitzer is the marginal figure as philosophical evidence. He is the lawyer who does not practice in courts. He is the musician who cannot hear his own music. He is the doctor who has lost his patient. He inhabits every professional category from its edge, and from that position he reveals what the center conceals.

His clients are equally marginal:

"lawyer for a dolphin and a rose, no doubt, lawyer for a starfish, lawyer for a snow-flake"

"perhaps he represented a dead moth with its wings trembling... Perhaps he represented a mouse in a paper nest"

These are not absurdist jokes. They are ontological claims: the marginal entity (the moth, the mouse, the starfish) has standing. It has a case. The legal system that excludes it is revealed as a construction, not a natural order. Mr. Spitzer, by representing the unrepresentable, makes visible the constructed exclusions of the categories he nominally inhabits.

The old woman who believed she was a reindeer the woman who married the Emperor of China on her deathbed the old woman who thought she was a bird cage; these are not case studies in pathology. They are, in the framework of Pillar 7, the evidence. Their marginality from normative selfhood reveals the normative self as equally constructed, merely less visible in its construction.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

"The past increased as the future diminished to one sigh or cry or spot of light, one ripple in the dying water, and the past was by comparison secure, safe, garnered like the lily lights of the imagination streaking the clouds, preserved, kept by him surely somewhere when all else failed."

This is Pillar 8 stated with extraordinary precision. The past does not recede. It accumulates. Mr. Spitzer's present is constituted by sedimentary layers of loss, document, memory, and inheritance: his brother's butterfly correspondence the cancelled postage stamps the church steeple brought in by a tide, the old racing forms gnawed by the mouse.

The sky itself is described as sedimentary:

"The sky was streaked with lines like the sea shell and like the tree bole as if there had been development."

The lines in the shell, the rings in the tree; these are the visible record of accumulated time pressing upward. What was lost is not behind; it is beneath, still pressing through.

His legal practice is fundamentally an archaeology of this sedimentation (wills, testaments, mortgages, old leases) documents that are themselves the sedimentary record of lives:

"wills and testaments accompanying him like a consort as he wove from shore to shore, his papers whispering"

The papers whisper because they are alive with deposited time. They are not records of the past. They are the past, still pressing upward through the present.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

"there were more hieroglyphs to the alphabet of reality than had ever appeared in reality... more words than had ever been spoken or uttered or written or heard, arcana beyond translation, amphoras of secret sound"

The chapter is acutely aware of language's constructive and limiting function. Language does not describe reality; it carves a portion of reality into visibility while leaving the rest in darkness. Mr. Spitzer's long-looping S signature, the mark of his legal authority, is revealed as arbitrary, as contingent as a meteor's tail.

The chapter performs this through its own prose. The syntax is deliberately excessive, recursive, self-interrupting. It enacts the inadequacy of language to contain what it is attempting to say, while simultaneously demonstrating that this inadequacy is not a failure of the writer but a structural condition of language itself.

The mouse who gnaws the documents is a figure for language's self-destruction:

"a mouse who had gnawed old leases, mortgages, testimonials, wills until they were pulverized or seemed like flakes of snow... a mouse who had gnawed his musical manuscripts, jumbling symphonies"

The archive, language's attempt to fix and preserve, is always already being consumed from within. The gnawing is not an accident. It is the condition of all inscription: language shapes what can be seen, and what it cannot contain, it destroys.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

"The star burned like an ember at the edge of the surf. Man might be the finest musician, but when laid away and not played by anyone, he was a poor instrument, indeed, an empty wooden box like the coffin of music strung with a few cat-gut strings."

The sacred in this chapter is never elsewhere. It is in the firefly on the sleeve the ember at the surf's edge the live coal burning on Mr. Spitzer's lips the light burning in a skull. These are not symbols pointing toward transcendence. They are the transcendence itself, located entirely within the immanent world.

The chapter explicitly refuses the elsewhere:

"perhaps there was no change, no transition as one jumped from world to world, for one jumped always into this world, no matter where one jumped."

This is Pillar 10 as ontological argument. There is no beyond. The jump from world to world lands in this world. The sacred does not reside in another dimension; it resides in the grey particularity of Mr. Spitzer's mantle, his reeling walk, his dead jeweled hand.

The most beautiful statement of The Sacred Is Here in the chapter:

"If all the stars were lighted at once, there would still be darkness like the coal packs in the Milky Way... Life was but a small circle of light, an incandescence different from any star, for its light did not mate with the light of this world."

The sacred light is not the stars. It is the small circle. The incandescence that does not mate with the world's light. The ordinary made luminous precisely by its failure to transcend, by its insistence on remaining here, burning at the edge of the surf.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar 11

"perhaps there was no change, no transition as one jumped from world to world, for one jumped always into this world, no matter where one jumped. And some might take the short jump, and some might take the long jump. It seemed that this world jumped with him, and all were hedge-hoppers, and even the stars were jumping."

Mr. Spitzer is explicitly the Crossing Point. He is constituted by the dead (his brother, the suffragette, the dead love) and he constitutes the ground for others. His legal practice is the structural carrying of obligation: he witnesses wills, assigns properties, bequeaths to "the known or unknown heirs of". He is the mechanism by which the sediment of the dead is transmitted to the not-yet-born.

The chapter makes the structural, not chosen, nature of this obligation explicit:

"remember the child never conceived and never born and give to him a kingdom and a crown"

The obligation extends even to those who do not exist. This is not sentiment. It is the structural logic of the Crossing Point: you are constituted by what was deposited before you arrived, and you are constituting the ground the not-yet-born will stand on. The child never conceived has a claim on Mr. Spitzer's legal attention because the Crossing Point is not a personal relationship. It is a structural position.

His cape, his hat, his walking stick; these are not costume. They are the material form of the crossing: the dead brother's mantle, literally, winding around him like a shell like a cocoon unravelling. He carries the dead forward. The carrying continues.
