From the guide

On loving by indirection.

The phrase has an Emersonian ring: Emerson wrote of the way great truths arrive slant, the way the soul is addressed indirectly. Emily Dickinson famously said tell all the truth but tell it slant. Young's Mr. Spitzer is working in this tradition: a person who cannot say the thing directly but circles it, approaches it from the side, expresses it through the objects and causes and extinct species he tends. The indirect lover is not a lesser lover. The indirection may be the only form the love can take given the particular shape of the self that holds it. Young does not apologize for Mr. Spitzer. She gives him his full scale, his full sadness, and the dignity of a love that expresses itself in the only way it can.

On '“every Martha must have her Mary.”' Young's reference to Mary and Martha from the Gospel of Luke sets up the chapter's governing contrast: Martha is the practical one, the active servant, the one burdened with the work of the world. Mary sits at Jesus's feet and listens, and when Martha complains that she does all the work while her sister sits idle, Jesus says Mary has chosen the better part. The passenger pigeon Martha maps onto biblical Martha: the one who carried the burden, who worked without recognition, who was not chosen. Who died. The Mary who should have accompanied her is the part that listens, that attends, that does not forget. Mr. Spitzer is trying to be that Mary. He attends. He does not forget. He weeps.
