Movement Two: The Five Pillars in the Traditions

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Homer Capehorn · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft · Plato and Platonism · Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres

Allusions: Angels and Archangels · Astronomy · Homer's Epics · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · The History of Opium

Definition from research

The copyright page of Miss MacIntosh, My Darling lists Young's previous publications before the novel begins. Two are poetry collections: Prismatic Ground (1937) and Moderate Fable (1944). The novel was begun in 1947 and not finished until 1963. This means Young was building the novel's philosophical architecture for sixteen years while her two collections of poetry sat on the copyright page as its declared ancestors. Young herself said that all her writing reflected common themes, that she had been thinking and writing about these things since she was nine years old. The poetry is not a precursor to the novel. It is the same project in a compressed form, the five pillars in their most concentrated state, before the accumulation of 1,198 pages expanded them to their full structural weight. For a reader who cannot yet commit to the novel, the poems are the door. For a reader who has already entered the novel, the poems are the architectural drawings.

The plural self pillar is visible in the poems decades before Mr. Spitzer arrives. In "Elegy for the Body," Young writes: "Oh, she was plural; She multiplied; All sparrows fell / If once she died." The movement here is identical to the novel's "Every heart is the other heart. Every soul is the other soul. Every face is the other face. The individual is the one illusion." Both make the same claim: the individual is not singular, and the death or disappearance of one particular person carries within it the death of something universal. The difference is scale. The poem compresses the claim into eight syllables. The novel gives it Miss MacIntosh: "hers had been the face of every face, the heart of every heart," and then spends the entire remaining narrative in the grief produced by that fact. The poem contains the philosophical position. The novel inhabits it.

The grief as permanent weather pillar appears in its purest form in "Strange Prophecy": "Only by bees will be visited / The lonely portals of the dead. / They will come intent on trade / Of light within that house of shade... The skull's enigma, what it was, / Become a hive for honey bees." Young takes the image of the skull, the most conventional emblem of mortality, and fills it with bees, with the productive hum of living things conducting their ordinary labor inside the space that death has emptied. This is grief as permanent weather made architecturally visible: the skull does not stop. The bees move through it. Life conducts its business inside the house of the dead. The novel's equivalent is the honey being made in Miss MacIntosh's skull after her death: "They will make their honey in her skull. She's sleeping in the grass, and you can hear the dead birds singing." Young wrote the poem in 1937. She embedded the image in the novel twenty-six years later in exactly the same form, because the philosophical claim had not changed. Grief does not empty the house. It fills it with a different kind of life.

The witness pillar runs through "Dubious Ground," one of the most formally precise poems in Prismatic Ground: a sleepwalker who is "always aware" of his surroundings, moving "lightly, with the small sound / Of dew stopped in the air," perceived from outside as part of the environment rather than separate from it. The sleepwalker does not interpret what he witnesses. He moves through it with a heightened, intuitive attention that operates below the threshold of analytical consciousness. This is exactly the quality Young gives to Vera as narrator: a consciousness that attends to everything, judges nothing, and allows the full weight of what it perceives to accumulate without premature resolution. The poem establishes the perceptual method. The novel applies it across 1,198 pages of accumulated observation.

The illusion as primary pillar appears in "That Apple Was Mental," which Young includes in Moderate Fable: "that apple was mental, a constellation of ideas." The poem asks whether something real can be invisible, whether sound can be inaudible yet exist as harmonious music, whether the mental and the physical are as distinct as we pretend. The poem's answer, worked out in Young's characteristic associative accumulation, is that they are not: "we eat and drink ideas, we are clothed with them." The novel's mother Catherine is the full-scale embodiment of this claim. Her opium house is full of Platonists and cherubim and Pythagoras shedding golden feathers because the mind's constructions are as real as any physical fact, possibly more so, because they are more densely inhabited. The poem states the epistemological position in fourteen lines. The novel gives it a bedroom and forty years of accumulated visitors.

Recursive construction is the pillar most visible in the formal relationship between the poems and the novel itself. "A Crystal Principle," from Moderate Fable, describes a world where even "accident unknown / And least departure" from crystalline order generates meaning, where "pattern in anomalies" persists, where the "miscreant heart" and the "outlaw rose" are as essential to the design as the crystal's perfect planes. Young is describing her own compositional method: a structure that builds by accumulation, that incorporates its own deviations, that generates patterns from the interplay of order and departure. The novel builds by exactly this method. Each chapter's deviation from what came before is part of the structure. Mr. Spitzer's socks, darned and redarned until they are not the same socks, are a crystal principle: identity persisting through replacement, structure maintained through accumulated change.

The poems also offer the most compressed versions of the novel's major characters. "Lot's Wife" is Miss MacIntosh: the woman who insists on not looking back, for whom the backward glance is the fatal error, whose entire pedagogical project with Vera is to prevent exactly the turning that Lot's wife performed and was punished for. "He who lives in what has flown is less than salt, is less than stone," the poem concludes, and this is Miss MacIntosh's argument in miniature, the argument she makes every time she demands that Vera reckon with current facts rather than inherited dreams. "Biography," the chess game with Lucifer in which the player loses not because he is wicked but because he is "distracted by the poetic," is Mr. Spitzer: "an old ballade, a witch's name, / A wraith of smoke...but not the game." The poems are character studies written before the characters were named.

"The Departure" is Esther Longtree: "the lady thineed / Autumnally walks... The snail meanwhile / Drawn cold as she, / Traces her still / Wan Odyssey / Across the purple / Leaves in flood, / Beautiful / Of her heart's blood." The slow walk, the deformed movement, the beauty inseparable from blood and suffering, the odyssey that is not heroic but simply continuous. Young wrote this poem a decade before Esther Longtree existed as a character. The image arrived first. The character grew to fill it.

"Ballad-Loving Woman," one of the most formally direct poems in the collections, is the novel's description of its own method: "With singing words from long ago / She knew that life will stay." The novel is built from accumulated singing words from long ago, from Boethius and Bunyan and Homer and the Old Woman in the Shoe and the passenger pigeon Martha, all the inherited forms of human knowledge and grief and comedy that Young assembled into a single structure that is also a voice, also a woman, also a long journey by bus through a Middle West that is also everywhere. "Ballad-loving woman / Who lived until she died." That is what the novel is. That is what her poetry always was.

For a reader who begins with the poems, particularly the shorter, more formally contained poems of Prismatic Ground, each one is a door into the novel's architecture. "Sky of Grasses" gives you grief as permanent weather in twelve lines. "Strange Prophecy" gives you the witness pillar in eight. "Lot's Wife" gives you Miss MacIntosh's entire philosophical position in a single image. "Elegy for the Body" gives you the plural self. "A Crystal Principle" gives you recursive construction. Enter any of these doors and you are already inside the novel's five pillars. The novel is what happens when you refuse to leave.
