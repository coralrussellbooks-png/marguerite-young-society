Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 81

Characters: Esther Longtree

Esther chooses a "legal father" for her child in this chapter, and the crossing point runs through the choosing. The "little toy salesman" she selects is small and childlike, "drinking milk from a baby bottle," unable to "tie his own shoe-laces," and the man infantilized against Esther's swelling dominance is the crossing point in the roles themselves, the woman grown large and strong where the man is reduced to an infant, the seam the book writes from opened in a marriage where the wife protects and teaches the husband. She is the "swollen bride of a little man," and the inversion turns the whole patriarchal arrangement over, female power set against a masculinity shrunk to dependence.

His body is its own coming-apart, a "large head," a "puffy body," a "waddling gait" set against a "powerful" and "pugnacious" upper half, an incomplete manhood the book keeps deflating. His survival of a shipwreck in which all the others drowned, "the women, the children, the moon, the sun, the stars," is male entitlement to survival named without mercy, a man who lived at the expense of everyone the sea took, and his "murderer" status is the guilt of that uneven survival, the burden the book keeps placing on those who outlast the drowned.

His psychological and physical deterioration, his "black mourning band" and "dead rosebud," his haunted memory of the drowned, are the trauma the book grants even its diminished men, and the "dead child's hand" clinging to him is the same spectral weight Esther carries, the lost child present at the threshold across two bodies. His stunted development set against Esther's "everlasting pregnancy" is the interrupted birth doubled, a man who never grew and a woman who never delivers, two arrested lives bound in a marriage neither the world nor the book will let complete.

Esther plans to "teach him" proper behavior, to make of this infant husband a father, and her hope for marriage and legitimacy is a woman reaching for the sanction the world withholds even as she inverts its terms. The looming war, the destruction and loneliness ahead, and her thought that "maybe the dead loved the dead" fold the boundary between life and death into her future the way it has folded into her past, and the chapter closes on Esther as a "pregnant old mule," the burden and the strength of a female endurance that carries dreams and lost children through a hostile world, still creating, still unfinished, still at the threshold the book keeps for its own.
