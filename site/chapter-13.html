Chapter 13

Summary

On the night of her fourteenth birthday, Vera says farewell in advance to her last certainty. After this night Miss MacIntosh, the one person who never seemed to change, will be a stranger Vera has lived with for seven years. Vera compares herself to the rider who crossed frozen Lake Constance in the dark and dropped dead when he learned where he had been. She bids farewell to the old moralities, the rote questions and answers, and What Cheer, Iowa, all of which now seem a lie. She wanders through her mother’s imagined gardens, where Galen, Harvey, Goldsmith, and Ben Jonson stroll at a perpetual garden party and life-sized chessmen move on a board of light and fog, until she reaches the garden of the deaf. There, as Catherine would tell Mr. Spitzer, the ear is dead. Catherine insists the dead ear is his, though he hears a roaring in his right ear that does not come from this world, and that this is why his music is silence.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a poignant farewell to Miss MacIntosh, a figure who had seemed ordinary and unchanging but is now revealed to be mysterious and complex. Vera reflects on having taken her for granted, washing and ironing her clothes without understanding who she truly was. This passage symbolizes the moment of awakening to the deeper, often hidden realities beneath familiar aspects. The metaphor of the weary traveler crossing the frozen but never fully frozen Lake Constance powerfully conveys the shock of realizing that what seemed solid and known is actually fragile and deceptive. The traveler’s death upon comprehending the impossible crossing parallels Vera’s own transformation in perception. This passage marks the loss of innocence and the beginning of a more complex understanding of life and people.
  • Following the initial farewell to Miss MacIntosh, Vera bids farewell to the stability of past certainties: the “old moralities,” “proverbial wisdom,” and the “fabric of self-deception” that both she and Miss MacIntosh had lived by. This passage underscores a profound existential rupture where the past, reason, purpose, and future become unreliable, even illusory. The imagery of cherry blossoms, peach orchards, and dusty lanes as if they were lies evokes a sense of disillusionment with the world as it was once understood. This farewell is not just to a person but to an entire worldview, highlighting the theme of awakening to a more ambiguous and unsettling reality.
  • Vera wanders through a surreal, neglected garden that is described as “the garden of the deaf,” a place where “the ear is dead.” On the way she passes through Catherine’s “immortal gardens,” where Dr. Galen and Dr. Harvey stroll among the guests. The garden of the deaf is filled with vivid, ghostly images: statues, flowers that fly away like moths, and chessmen moving invisibly. The garden symbolizes a realm where sound and communication are absent, reflecting a profound isolation. The metaphor of the “deaf ear” extends into the relationship between Vera’s mother and Mr. Spitzer, emphasizing themes of silence, unhearing, and the inability to connect or be heard. Catherine’s insistence that the ear is dead and the world is soundless captures the tragic condition of isolation and the fading of meaningful communication.
  • The exchanges between Vera’s mother and Mr. Spitzer are significant for illustrating the tension between reality and perception. Mr. Spitzer represents a figure trying to engage with the world, arguing the possibility of sound and communication despite Catherine’s firm belief in their absence, even as he hears a roaring in his right ear “which did not come from this world.” Their dialogue about whose ear is dead (literal or metaphorical) introduces the theme of death not only of the physical ear but also of the heart and spirit. This passage deepens the motif of silence and the loss of voice, highlighting human suffering and the struggle to maintain connection amid internal and external voids.
  • Throughout the chapter, there is a recurring motif of things appearing familiar but becoming “increasingly unfamiliar,” such as the Venus statue “bearded by patriarchal fog,” flowers that fly away when touched, and shadows cast by barnacle-formed cherubim faces. These images evoke the fragility of memory and the instability of reality as perceived by Vera, underscoring how the past and present intertwine in confusing ways. The luminous yet ghostly descriptions of the garden and the figures within suggest a world where the boundaries between life and death, reality and dream, presence and absence blur.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • The rider “dropped dead with the shock of the after-knowledge.” Why is knowing worse than the danger itself?
  • Vera says her “ignorance had been perhaps my salvation.” Is it?
  • “One should see the bird and the shadow of the bird, but one should not hear the song.” What does Catherine lose by choosing silence?

Characters

  • Miss MacIntosh walked through cloudbursts with “her lips blue,” her face “twisted with unuttered pain.” What was Vera not seeing?
  • Catherine “feigned death because it was an enchantment greater than fleeting existence.” What does she gain by it?
  • Mr. Spitzer is “trying to learn not to turn his head every time he heard a traffic horn.” What is he trying not to hear?

Connections

  • Chapter 12 ended in the garden of the blind, and this chapter ends in the garden of the deaf. What does each garden take away?
  • The life-sized chessmen stood in the garden in Chapters 1, 2, and 3. Who are the invisible players?
  • Mr. Spitzer’s “music was the silence.” How does that answer Chapter 2’s “silent music”?

Reflection

  • Is it better not to know where one has been?
  • Can a person live with someone for seven years and not know her?
  • Which would be harder to lose, sight or sound?

Research: Characters

Vera Cartwheel

  • She begins with a poignant farewell to the “last certitude,” including the tender moment of her fourteenth birthday and the figure of Miss MacIntosh, who had been a constant presence for seven years.
  • This farewell symbolizes a significant psychological and emotional shift: a departure from innocence and an acknowledgement that the familiar is no longer certain or reliable.
  • Vera admits to having taken Miss MacIntosh for granted, thinking her the most ordinary person, only to realize she was mysterious and unknowable. This signals an awakening to the complexity and ambiguity of human beings and relationships.
  • She compares her previous ignorance to a weary traveler crossing a frozen lake under unbearable conditions, unaware that the ice beneath is treacherous and that survival was against all odds.
  • This metaphor vividly captures the precariousness of Vera’s former worldview: one that was sheltered and unknowing, yet preserved her from the shock of truth.
  • The crossing into awareness is described as a moment of death and rebirth, forever altering perception: “Everything... would be different... the simplest gesture having a different meaning”.
  • Following this awakening, Vera experiences a profound fragmentation of reality, where the familiar world loses its coherence and reliability.
  • The surrounding environment, including natural elements like moons, stars, and waters, fails to provide reassurance.
  • Vera explicitly bids farewell to “old moralities,” “proverbial wisdom,” and “the past and its illusion,” highlighting a rejection of previously accepted truths and a plunge into existential uncertainty.
  • Her farewell to the past is not simple nostalgia but a confrontation with the past as a “dream” that must be “laid bare like a corpse.”
  • This suggests a critical and almost forensic examination of memory, recognizing that the past is layered with deception as well as unexpected truths. Vera anticipates surprise and shock in discovering the absence of qualities once assumed and the presence of others previously unrecognized.
  • Vera’s reflections are imbued with elaborate, poetic imagery. These images evoke a dreamlike, liminal space between reality and illusion, life and death, sound and silence.
  • The “garden of the deaf,” where “the ear is dead,” becomes a powerful symbol of isolation, silence, and possibly the failure or refusal of communication.
  • Her environment is suffused with paradox and ambiguity, reflecting Vera’s inner turmoil and the collapse of conventional understanding.
  • Vera wrestles with themes of presence and absence, sound and silence, life and death.
  • Throughout the chapter, Vera’s tone is melancholic, marked by a sense of loss, estrangement, and an almost mournful awareness of change.
  • The farewell to Miss MacIntosh and the past, the dissection of memory, and the surreal descriptions of the garden convey a profound alienation from the world once known, both external and internal.

Miss MacIntosh

  • Miss MacIntosh, once a fixed star in her life, becomes like a “perfect stranger.”
  • Lips blue and face “pale and twisted with unuttered pain,” she walked through cloudbursts on her constitutional.
  • Her rote questions: “Where is North Dakota, and what is a monsoon, and what is a mirage, Vera Cartwheel?”

Catherine Cartwheel

  • The recurring motif of the “dead ear,” especially in relation to Mr. Spitzer and Catherine, underscores a world where communication fails and the essence of existence is isolated from meaning.
  • Catherine’s insistence that “the ear was dead” and that “there was not a sound to be heard” suggests a bleak vision of human experience as disembodied from relational and sensory engagement.
  • Catherine is depicted as a woman caught between life and a kind of suspended existence.
  • Catherine is enveloped in a self-imposed or circumstantial state of near-death or withdrawal from reality, an enchantment that protects her but also alienates her from the world around her.
  • The “garden of the deaf” is a powerful metaphor for Catherine’s mental and emotional state.
  • This deafness is not just literal but symbolic of a disconnection from the external world and from meaningful human interaction.
  • In this garden, “one should see the bird and the shadow of the bird, but one should not hear the song,” encapsulating Catherine’s experience of perceiving the world without fully engaging with it or being able to communicate effectively.
  • Her interactions with Mr. Spitzer, an old friend and legal professional, further reveals her state of mind.
  • Despite his efforts to speak and argue, Catherine insists that the “ear was dead,” and that no sound truly reaches them. This exchange highlights her detachment and the barrier she has erected between herself and others, emphasizing her internal silence and the futility of communication.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s “subdued roaring” in his ear, a noise which “did not come from this world,” parallels Catherine’s own internal condition, suggesting shared but differently manifested forms of alienation.
  • Catherine’s fear of the silence “unbroken by the sound” and her particular dread “when a frog screamed” indicate deep psychological distress. The silence is oppressive, a void that she cannot endure, and the rare disruptive sounds become sources of terror rather than comfort. This paradox of fearing both silence and sudden noise points to a troubled mind caught between extremes, fragile and haunted.
  • Throughout the chapter, images of decaying flowers, fading colors, and ghostly sounds surround Catherine, reinforcing the themes of impermanence and dissolution.
  • She is part of a world slipping away from life into something spectral or unreal. This imagery parallels her mental state which is caught in a liminal space between reality and illusion, life and death.
  • Despite her distance, Catherine remains a focal figure in Vera’s consciousness. Her presence is felt strongly even as she withdraws, indicating Catherine’s paradoxical role as both a source of life and a symbol of loss and mystery.

Mr. Spitzer

  • Mr. Spitzer is described as “pompous” and “beclouded,” suggesting a man who carries a certain self-importance or authority, yet his thoughts and arguments are somewhat confused or unclear. He is “many-valved,” implying layers or complexities in his personality or reasoning, which are not easily penetrable.
  • He is a lawyer who argues cases and makes decisions, which indicates a rational, structured mind accustomed to logic and human argument. Yet this external rationality contrasts sharply with the surreal, silent garden environment where “the ear is dead”.
  • The repeated motif of deafness is central to his character. While Mr. Spitzer physically experiences a kind of internal “roaring” in his right ear, a noise which “did not come from this world,” he is metaphorically deaf to true communication or sound in the broader existential sense described by Catherine.
  • The conversations with Catherine underline Mr. Spitzer’s role as a symbol of human limitation and isolation. Despite his attempts to speak and reason, he is trapped in a world where “there was no sound” and “he could not hear another’s voice, not even his own voice speaking”. This frames him as a figure caught between the rational world of law and argument and a deeper, more profound silence that challenges or negates those faculties.
  • Their interaction reveals a tension between denial and acceptance of this silence and deafness. Mr. Spitzer tries to argue against the idea that his ear is dead, pointing out that his dead brother’s ear was not buried on the estate, yet Catherine remains insistent that “the ear was dead,” emphasizing a metaphysical or symbolic deafness beyond just physical hearing.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s character embodies the tragic human predicament of isolation and the failure of communication. The novel suggests that even in his legal profession, where voice and argument are paramount, his voice is unheard in the cosmic or existential sense. This heightens the pathos of his character as someone who functions in society but is fundamentally cut off from true connection or understanding.

Research: Themes

Transformation and Loss of Certitude

See: Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • The chapter begins with a farewell to the last certainty Vera held, symbolized by the tender memory of her fourteenth birthday and the figure of Miss MacIntosh.
  • Vera realizes that Miss MacIntosh, once considered an ordinary and stable presence, is in fact mysterious and unknowable. This realization marks a profound change in her perception of reality.
  • The metaphor of crossing a frozen Lake Constance, an impossible feat, symbolizes the transition from ignorance to knowledge and the shock of awakening to a new reality that alters all previous understandings.

Illusion versus Reality

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Vera describes the world and past experiences as fabrications or illusions, comparing the past to a corpse laid bare to reveal its true nature, which often contradicts prior assumptions.
  • This theme is emphasized through the farewell to familiar places, routines, and moralities that now seem deceptive or false.

Memory and Change

See: Memory and the Past

  • Miss MacIntosh, once a familiar figure, will henceforth be seen as almost a stranger due to Vera’s new awareness.
  • Vera anticipates that all memories and familiar objects will be differently understood, weighted with new meanings.
  • This reflects the theme that memory is mutable and that new knowledge reshapes how the past, and people once thought known, are perceived.

The Garden of the Deaf

See: Music, Sound, and Silence

  • Vera imagines wandering a mystical, non-symmetrical garden filled with historical and literary figures, a symbol of the mind’s landscape where past and present, life and death intermingle.
  • The garden is where the “ear is dead,” meaning silence reigns and sound is absent, which symbolizes isolation, the limits of communication, and the finality of death.
  • The dialogue between Catherine, who insists on the silence, and Mr. Spitzer, who argues against it yet cannot escape it, dramatizes the struggle to find meaning or connection in a silent, soundless world.

Life, Death, and Silence

See: Death and Mortality · Music, Sound, and Silence

  • The chapter frequently reflects on mortality and the disappearance of sound, which metaphorically represents the death of communication and presence.
  • The silence is described as pervasive and absolute, underscoring the theme of existential isolation and the ineffability of certain experiences.

Research: Resources

  • “my fourteenth birthday”: The birthday party that never happened, in Chapters 10 and 12. See the Chapter 10 resources and the Chapter 12 resources.
  • “a perfect stranger I had lived with for seven years”: “From my seventh to my fourteenth birthday” (Chapter 9). See Miss MacIntosh and the Chapter 9 resources.
  • “the weary traveller who rode over Lake Constance”: The legend is Gustav Schwab’s ballad “Der Reiter und der Bodensee” (The Rider and Lake Constance, 1826), later set to music by Robert Schumann. A horseman crosses the frozen lake in a snowstorm without knowing it and, told on the far shore where he has been, drops dead of fright. See Snow, Ice, and Winter.
    • Lake Constance is made up of three bodies of water on the Rhine at the foot of the Alps.
    • The lake seldom freezes over. It froze in 1830, 1880, and the winter of 1962-63.
  • “terra firma”: Latin for “solid ground.”
  • “a trembling sea of glass”: “And before the throne there was a sea of glass like unto crystal” (Revelation 4:6). See Revelation and the Apocalypse and the Chapter 10 resources.
  • “Where is North Dakota, and what is a monsoon, and what is a mirage”: Schoolbooks of the time taught by rote question and answer, as the catechism did.
  • “fixed like the North star”: “But I am constant as the northern star, / Of whose true-fix’d and resting quality / There is no fellow in the firmament” (Julius Caesar 3.1). See Shakespeare and Moon and Stars.
  • “my mother feigned death”: Juliet drinks the Friar’s potion to lie as if dead (Romeo and Juliet 4.1). See Shakespeare and the Chapter 12 resources.
  • “farewell to What Cheer”: What Cheer, Iowa, Miss MacIntosh’s hometown. See Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest and the Chapter 9 resources.
  • “immortal gardens”: In Plato’s Apology, Socrates looks forward to death as a chance to converse with Orpheus, Homer, and Hesiod. See Plato and Platonism.
  • “Dr. Galen”: Galen (129-c. 216), born Aelius Galenus, was a Greek physician, surgeon, and philosopher. He was one of antiquity’s most influential medical researchers, making significant contributions to anatomy, physiology, and pharmacology, among other fields. His work, based on animal dissections and detailed observations, dominated Western medicine for over 1,400 years until it was challenged during the Renaissance. He believed that medicine and philosophy were intertwined and wrote extensively on logic, ethics, and the nature of the soul. He built his theory of temperament on the four humors. He taught that the liver makes blood and the body uses it up, which Harvey overturned.
  • “Dr. Harvey”: William Harvey was the first known physician to describe in detail the human circulatory system, in De Motu Cordis (1628).
    • “sublunar mysteries”: Aristotle divided the cosmos into the changing, mortal world below the moon and the unchanging heavens above it.
  • “Oliver Goldsmith”: Oliver Goldsmith was an Irish writer, poet, playwright, and novelist known for works like the novel The Vicar of Wakefield and the play She Stoops to Conquer. He was a versatile author who also achieved success as a poet. The Deserted Village is a poem by Goldsmith published in 1770, a lament for a lost country village, as Vera bids farewell to What Cheer. It is a work of social commentary, and condemns rural depopulation and the pursuit of excessive wealth.
  • “Ben Jonson”: Ben Jonson was a significant early modern English poet and playwright, known for his satirical comedies, masques, and poetry. He was a contemporary of Shakespeare, was crowned England’s first (unofficial) Poet Laureate in 1616, and is celebrated for plays like Volpone (a dark comedy about greed and corruption) and The Alchemist (a satire about fraud and human folly). “To Celia,” also known as “Drink to me only with thine eyes,” is a famous love song by Ben Jonson, first published in his 1616 collection The Forest. See Shakespeare.
  • “a perpetual garden party omitting the tragic spirit”: The Epicureans met in the Garden, the school Epicurus founded in Athens. See Epicureanism and Marguerite Young on Her Craft.
    • Marguerite Young was a reader of Anatole France’s work. Anatole France was a French author, poet, and critic who won the Nobel Prize for Literature 1921. His short story “Marguerite” first appeared in book form in Paris in 1920, with woodcuts by Siméon, and in J. Lewis May’s English translation in 1921. France was also known for his socialist sympathies and support of the Russian Revolution in 1917.
    • The Garden of Epicurus by Anatole France is a book of short essays and reflections (1894) that explores the nature of happiness, the pursuit of knowledge, and the struggle for personal freedom. France suggests that to disclose oneself, a person must first know something about themselves. He also suggests that in reminiscence, loss of detail can yield a gain in beauty. One of his favorite periods was the transition from paganism to Christianity.
    • The Epicurean “fourfold remedy,” handed down by Philodemus: Don’t fear god. Don’t worry about death. What is good is easy to get. What is terrible is easy to endure.
  • “anti-masks and masks”: The antimasque, a grotesque comic dance before the stately masque, was Ben Jonson’s invention in The Masque of Queens (1609).
  • “the life-sized chessmen”: In Lewis Carroll’s Through the Looking-Glass (1871), Alice crosses a countryside laid out as a chessboard with living pieces. The chessmen have stood in this garden since Chapter 1. See Alice in Wonderland, Cards, Games, and Chance, and the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “as if played by invisible players who could perceive an order in multiple confusions of movements and sounds, a secret plan”: “’Tis all a Chequer-board of Nights and Days / Where Destiny with Men for Pieces plays” (Edward FitzGerald’s Rubáiyát of Omar Khayyám, 1859). Heraclitus wrote, “Time is a child playing draughts,” and “The hidden harmony is better than the obvious.” See Fate and Chance and Heraclitus and Flux.
    • Heraclitus (Greek philosopher) main lessons: “The straight and the crooked path of the fuller’s comb is one and the same”; “The way up is the way down”; “Beginning and end, on a circle’s circumference, are common”; and “Thou shouldst unite things whole and things not whole, that which tends to unite and that which tends to separate, the harmonious and the discordant; from all things arises the one, and from the one all things.” “Mortals are immortals and immortals are mortals, the one living the others’ death and dying the others’ life”; “As the same thing in us is living and dead, waking and sleeping, young and old. For these things having changed around are those, and those in turn having changed around are these”; and “Cold things warm up, the hot cools off, wet becomes dry, dry becomes wet.” Diogenes Laërtius summarizes Heraclitus’s philosophy as follows: “All things come into being by conflict of opposites, and the sum of things (‘the whole’) flows like a stream.” This fragment from Heraclitus’s writings has survived in three different forms:
    • “On those who step into the same rivers, different and different waters flow” - Arius Didymus, quoted in Stobaeus
    • “We both step and do not step into the same river, we both are and are not” - Heraclitus Homericus, Homeric Allegories
    • “It is not possible to step into the same river twice” - Plutarch, On the E at Delphi
    • Heraclitus said “strife is justice” and “all things take place by strife”. He called the opposites in conflict, “strife”, and theorized that the apparently unitary state, “justice”, results in “the most beautiful harmony”
  • “the Venus was bearded by patriarchal fog”: Velázquez’s Venus at Her Mirror (1647-51), the Rokeby Venus, hangs in the National Gallery, London. The Rokeby Venus was vandalized by a British suffragette in 1914. “I have tried to destroy the picture of the most beautiful woman in mythological history as a protest against the Government for destroying Mrs Pankhurst, who is the most beautiful character in modern history. Justice is an element of beauty as much as colour and outline on canvas. Mrs Pankhurst seeks to procure justice for womanhood, and for this she is being slowly murdered by a Government of Iscariot politicians. If there is an outcry against my deed, let every one remember that such an outcry is an hypocrisy so long as they allow the destruction of Mrs Pankhurst and other beautiful living women, and that until the public cease to countenance human destruction the stones cast against me for the destruction of this picture are each an evidence against them of artistic as well as moral and political humbug and hypocrisy.” - Mary Richardson’s statement in The Times. London. 11 March 1914. See Women’s Suffrage.
    • The bearded Venus of Cyprus, a double-sexed Aphrodite, is described by Macrobius (Saturnalia 3.8).
  • “The mist flowers drooped their purple, langorous heads”: Mistflower, a real wildflower of the American South, blooms in soft purple heads in late summer. See Roses and Flowers.
  • “flew away like luna moths”: The pale green luna moth lives about a week as an adult and has no mouth to eat with. See Butterflies and Moths and the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “I had come to the garden of the deaf”: The companion to the garden of the blind in Chapter 12. See Silence and Deafness and the Chapter 12 resources.
  • “A frog screamed”: Frogs scream when seized by a predator. Bashō’s haiku (1686): “An old pond / a frog jumps in / the sound of water.”
  • “not even the creaking of starlight”: Aristotle reports the Pythagorean answer: the spheres do make music, and people do not hear it because they have heard it since birth (On the Heavens 2.9). Lorenzo tells Jessica, “Such harmony is in immortal souls; / But whilst this muddy vesture of decay / Doth grossly close it in, we cannot hear it” (The Merchant of Venice 5.1). See Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres, Aristotle, and Shakespeare.
  • “a subdued roaring, a noise which came from the head only”: Tinnitus, a ringing or roaring in the ears with no outside source. Beethoven described it in the Heiligenstadt Testament (1802). See Joachim Spitzer, Deafness and Sign Language, and the Chapter 4 resources.
  • “his music was the silence”: John Cage’s 4′33″ (1952) is a piece of silence, in which the audience hears only the sounds of the room. Mr. Spitzer has written “only silent music” (Chapter 2). See Joachim Spitzer, Music, Sound, and Silence, and the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “One should see the bird and the shadow of the bird, but one should not hear the song”: Wallace Stevens, “Thirteen Ways of Looking at a Blackbird” (1917): “The blackbird whistling / Or just after.” See Birds.
