Research notes: Chapter 72 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 72

Characters: Esther Longtree

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

"I am me, Esther Longtree, the passive policeman's only daughter, and you're you,"

Esther announces herself as if identity were stable, but the announcement immediately dissolves. She is simultaneously:

  • The waitress behind the counter
  • The pregnant woman carrying the living child
  • The mourner of the stillborn
  • The child crying for water inside her own voice
  • The mother nursing her own fist in the dark
  • The mule at the dead-end of creation
  • The white piano with all its strings intact

She does not move between these selves sequentially. They are simultaneous and irresolvable. Her speech enacts this plurality: mid-sentence she shifts from addressing the customer to addressing a child to addressing herself to addressing the void. The grammar of her monologue is the plural self: no single subject holds.

Most strikingly, she speaks in voices that interrupt each other:

"I heard, in the midst of her blurred speech, another voice, a child’s voice calling for water, mama, water, water. It was her own voice interrupting her voice."

This is not psychological disorder in the clinical sense. This is the Radical Immanence diagnosis of selfhood: you are not one person. Esther does not need to be fixed. She is what a self actually is, stripped of the social performance of unity.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

"This was not the restaurant. This was the womb. This was the grave."

The restaurant is simultaneously all three. The walls are painted with hollyhocks that "merely dripped from the brush," a waterfall, daft ducks with human faces, a palm tree on a scene of everlasting ice. The décor does not represent reality; it constructs a layered unreality that is more honest than a plainly decorated room would be, because it admits its own constructedness.

Esther's pregnancy is the central instance of this pillar. The novel never definitively tells us whether she is pregnant in any biological sense. The old Doctor cannot confirm it. Her parents do not see it. The salesmen write years later asking if the child was ever born. And yet:

"She was certain of her pregnancy, the vast, the moving silence."

The pregnancy is real as experience: the morning sickness, the evening sickness, the shadow of a little foot, the droplets of milk, the ticking heart. The line between illusion and reality is not the question the chapter asks. The chapter asks: what kind of reality is constructed by longing, by the body's insistence, by grief's accumulation? There is no sunlit world outside the cave of Esther's womb. There is only a larger cave, more carefully lit by fireflies and marsh butterflies.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

It is only "because of the long pregnancy, the many nights, the days, the fireflies, the bull frogs, the memories, the way I go on here against all odds."

Esther does not grieve and recover. She accumulates grief. Each stillborn is added to the count (as many as the autumn leaves, as the snowflakes) and none is forgotten. They whistle in her ear. They flock to the restaurant in the cold morning. They cry for water in the night.

The chapter's structure is the structure of accumulated grief. There is no narrative arc, no movement toward resolution. Esther circles the same losses repeatedly, approaching them from different angles, never arriving at acceptance or closure:

"The things past, the things to be, the things to never be."

Past, future, and the permanently foreclosed exist simultaneously in her climate. The salesmen's packages arrive year after year (a white shoe size one, size two, size three) measuring the age of a child who was never born, tracking the growth of an absence. This is grief as permanent weather: it does not pass through stages. It deposits.

Most devastatingly:

There was "the memory of a dead child staring into her looking glass each night, dark-haired, pale-eyed, growing, someone who had three teeth or was eight years old or was older and would not eat a crumb upon the window sill."

The dead child grows. Grief does not freeze the lost at the moment of loss. It carries them forward, aging them in the imagination, constructing the life they did not live. This is the honest life refusing to pretend otherwise.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator's role in this chapter is almost entirely that of the Witness. They sit hunched, cold, hands folded, ordering coffee seven times, and they stay. They do not correct Esther, do not diagnose her, do not rush her toward a point. They receive her monologue without flinching:

"I sat still, expressionless, waiting as in a windy railroad station for a train that would never come."

The train that would never come is resolution. The narrator refuses to seek it. This is the central ethical stance of the chapter: attention without conclusion.

Esther herself performs a kind of witnessing toward her own experience. She sees the bald spot growing, she sees the droplets of milk, she sees the shadow of the little foot. She does not look away from her own body's testimony, even when that testimony is contradicted by everyone around her. She is her own most faithful witness, which is why she survives. The chapter's refusal to resolve whether Esther is "really" pregnant, "really" sane, "really" a mother; this refusal is itself an act of witnessing. Refusal to simplify is the act.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

"Sometimes she would unravel the stitches in order to be able to knit them again, but lately, with her fingers busy and her mind clear, she was knitting without needles or thread and never did unravel any more."

This is the pillar made literal. Esther knits and unravels and reknits: the provisional building, the return to the beginning with what was learned. The little black suit for the first born who would live is never finished, because finishing would be a kind of death of hope. The ongoing knitting is the life.

The shift to "knitting without needles or thread" is crucial. She has internalized the recursive construction so completely that the physical materials are no longer necessary. The building continues in pure intention, pure practice, beyond the reach of anyone who might unravel it. The building is never finished. That is not failure. That is how it works.

The pregnancy itself is recursive: she loses it and gains it again, like her weight. Each conception returns her to the beginning. Each stillbirth is the unraveling. Each new pregnancy is the reknitting. She does not experience this as tragedy but as structure.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

"There is something about all of us, he said, that is unrealized."

Old Doctor O'Leary's diagnosis is the chapter's philosophical thesis. The unrealized is not failure. It is the honest condition of all beings. Esther's stillborn are the most visible form of what is true of everyone: we are all, in some essential way, unfinished.

The chapter itself formally enacts this ethic. It has no plot. It does not conclude. Esther's monologue does not arrive anywhere. The coffee is heated and cooled and heated again and never satisfactorily drunk. The narrator ordered seven cups and we never see them finished. The chapter is the ongoing sentence.

Esther's failures are catalogued with particular care (she failed at the normal college, the beauty college, the island school, was rejected by the undertaker) but each failure is presented not as diminishment but as evidence of ongoing life. The demand for completion, the demand that she produce a living child, that she succeed professionally, that she be marriageable, is the violence. Her integrity is exactly her incompletion.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

"Ugly outside I may be as an old mule the color of dung or a mud fence, but beautiful am I inside, great waters and unexplored."

Esther Longtree is the marginal figure made central. She is:

  • Unmarried and therefore marginal to the category of "mother"
  • Pregnant and therefore marginal to the category of "old maid"
  • Employed and therefore marginal to the category of "dependent woman"
  • Possibly delusional and therefore marginal to the category of "reliable witness"
  • Possibly visionary and therefore marginal to the category of "ordinary person"

The chapter places her at every edge simultaneously. And at every edge, the constructed nature of the categories becomes visible. What is a mother? What is a child? What is birth? What is a body? Esther's existence makes these questions impossible to answer with confidence.

The mule appears at the beginning of the chapter (patient at the dead-end of creation, wearing a lavender crocheted hood, with dark, lustrous, intelligent eyes) and recurs throughout as Esther's shadow self. The mule is the marginal animal: neither horse nor donkey, neither wild nor fully domesticated, sterile by nature. Esther's parents call her a mule. She calls herself one. But the mule at the dead-end of creation has intelligent eyes from which the narrator might have taken warning. The marginal is not the exception. It is the evidence.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

"The past will be the future."

Old Doctor says this, and it is the chapter's temporal logic. The stillborn do not recede. They accumulate beneath Esther, pressing up through everything she does:

She "had buried layettes laid out in long white boxes, digging at night, had buried them under the roots and vines and under the moon-globed persimmon."

The landscape is literally sedimentary with her losses. Under the turnip fields, under the gooseberry bush, under the snow, in the hollow tree: the stillborn are deposited in layers throughout the earth. When Esther walks through the fields, she walks over her own grief. It is beneath her, still pressing up.

The salesmen's packages (the white shoe in size one, size two, size three) are sedimentary time made postal: the past accumulating in the present, measuring the growth of what was never born. The past does not recede. It deposits shoes at the post office.

Even Esther's body is sedimentary:

"Her spinal column curved by the weight, the heavy weight of memory and snow."

Memory and snow are equivalent weights. Both accumulate. Both curve the spine. The body is the archive.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter's prose style is itself an argument about language. The sentences are long, recursive, self-interrupting, refusing to conclude. They circle their subjects rather than naming them directly. The language does not pretend to speak from nowhere:

"She was plum-colored but square, and her eyes were lustrous, dull but shining."

Lustrous and dull. These are contradictions that ordinary descriptive language would resolve by choosing one. The prose refuses to choose. It names its own location: the location of a narrator who is witnessing rather than judging, who refuses the violence of the concluded description.

Esther's own language is explicitly self-aware as construction:

"I am a white piano," she insists, "and my keys not dusty, and my petals good. My

pedals good. My echoes."

The slip from "petals" to "pedals" is not error; it is language discovering its own constructedness in real time. She is flower and instrument simultaneously, and the language marks the crossing point between the two. She then adds "My echoes", which belongs to neither piano nor flower but to both, and to the acoustic space between them. Language here is not a vehicle for a pre-existing truth. It is the act of making the truth possible.

The old Doctor's language is similarly self-aware:

"The ebb of reason, he said, is accompanied by the tide of imagination, bringing all old wreckage into shore."

He knows he is speaking metaphorically. He knows the metaphor shapes what can be seen. He is a doctor who has retired from instruments and now makes his search without them: a figure for language that has given up the pretense of neutral description.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

"It is because I have an enlarged heart with ample leakage and great sympathy like the sky which goes everywhere and has no ending."

The sacred in this chapter is entirely immanent. It is located in:

  • The enlarged, leaking heart
  • The bald spot growing like the bald spot of the earth
  • The droplets of milk on the breasts like dew on flowers
  • The shadow of a little foot playing against the side
  • The robin's egg crushed by the deformed foot

There is no transcendence that points outward. The wild white mares on the desert island are pregnant by the wind: fecundated by pure immanence, by the world's own breath, with no male anywhere. The old Doctor's explanation:

"If the world could go on in no other way, it would go on in imagination and the wild white mothers be fecundated by the wind."

Imagination is the immanent sacred. It is not a lesser substitute for the real. It is the real, the only fecundating force in a world where the fathers are sterile and the children are stillborn. The sacred is here, in the grey restaurant, in the cold coffee, in the leaking heart of the pregnant waitress. This is where the sacred lives.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

"Working here to pass the time away between two shores am I, heading toward the coasts of birth where the old ghost babies stand, waving their handkerchiefs, but you are headed in a gale toward the coasts of death."

Esther identifies herself explicitly as the crossing point. She is between the coasts of birth and death simultaneously. She is not moving through time toward a destination. She is where time crosses itself.

The stillborn she has deposited throughout the landscape are the ancestors in the sediment, not behind her, but beneath her, constituting the ground she stands on. The child she carries now is the not-yet-born, and she is constituting the ground it will stand on. This is not chosen. It is structural:

"Never was I so far advanced as now," she sighed, "so heavy, staggering, and

hearing all the voices of all the stillborn everywhere, a whistling to remind me."

She carries the past (the whistling stillborn) and the future (the shadow boxer under her heart) simultaneously. She is not a self moving through time. She is where time crosses itself, constituted by what was deposited before her, constituting the ground for what has not yet arrived.

The chapter's final lines crystallize this:

"Who killed cock robin? Nobody, she said. All people killed him... He was killed by the death that was in him from the time of his birth. He killed himself... A little child killed him. Ah, I knew, she saw, who killed him! Never she, she said, biting her lips, for she was always pregnant. Never, never."

She is always pregnant. This is not a biological claim. It is an ontological one. The carrying continues. The obligation is structural. The ancestors are in the sediment. The not-yet-born are under her heart. She is the crossing point, and she will not stop carrying, because to stop carrying would be to stop being the place where time crosses itself.
