Inviting the Muses, Part 3

Part of a reading of Inviting the Muses (1994), Marguerite Young’s stories, essays and reviews.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Part 2 · Inviting the Muses: A Reading, Part by Part · Part 4 →

Part 3 covers four essays: "Complex Life and Complex Letters," "An Afternoon with Marianne Moore," "Mari Sandoz," and "Horse Utopia." Together they shift the collection from fiction into Young's public voice as a critic and portraitist in the late 1940s.

"Complex Life and Complex Letters" (Vogue, 1946)

This is a manifesto, and it reads like a defense of the novel Young was then deep inside writing. It opens: "Literature is our night life, our dream life, and the sun may not rise tomorrow." She pictures God as "the greatest fiction writer of them all," whose sentences are "replete with the most baroque imagery, and quite circumlocutious, and even a little long-drawn." The joke has a point. If creation itself is baroque, a plain style is not more truthful, only more limited.

Her target is the "commonsense critic," with J. Donald Adams named as the most eminent. These critics want literature "simple, plain, sensible, healthy, unassuming, underwritten, comfortable," and they believe it is enough to say "The cat was on the mat." Young answers that such a literature has never existed. Even Pilgrim's Progress has "its wild Gothic elements," nursery rhymes carry buried violence, and "an honest literature, from Job to Richard Wright, has even been a little morbid in spots." She lines up the writers she considers honest: Faulkner, Welty, Stead, McCullers, Robert Penn Warren, Stein, Joyce, Broch, Mann's The Transposed Heads. Against them she sets the mass-produced "average" book turned out "with the efficiency of Willow Run," and she singles out William Maxwell's The Folded Leaf as having "one neat insight per page, timed like a metronome."

Two moments carry real weight. When American reviewers attacked Grimm's fairy tales as a "German orgy of brutality," Young notes that "Goering and Goebbels were sensible," which turns the charge around: the atrocity came from the orderly, rational side, not from the disorderly dream world. And she points to sheep and goats being placed on ships for the atomic tests in the Pacific, asking "How can literature be of the modern world and not a crazy thing?" The essay closes with a parody of critics scolding Hawthorne, Melville, and Kafka, ending flatly: "The cat is on the mat, and that's the end of that."

There is a thread here back to "Old James," where the blank spaces were where the truth hid. The commonsense critics, she says, would prefer "even the blank page."

"An Afternoon with Marianne Moore" (Vogue, 1946)

This is the piece the Publisher's Note mentioned, where a paragraph cut by Vogue was restored. It is almost certainly the parenthetical beginning "Actually, although cut out by editorial scissors," which explains that Moore had borrowed from Yeats and let the line be quoted as hers. Young attaches this to "real toads in imaginary gardens," but in Moore's own notes to "Poetry" the Yeats debt is for "literalists of the imagination," while the toads are Moore's. Young seems to have blurred the two, which may be one reason an editor cut it.

The essay starts on the subway to Coney Island in winter, with Brooklyn's "spires like huge, inverted ice cream cones" and a boardwalk "Niagara Falls of pasteboard," then settles Moore into that landscape as the poet who "gives voice to marvels." The portrait is precise and affectionate: the narrow head and pale blue eyes, the apartment full of ivory mice and a baby ebony sea horse, the mixed furniture ("We mix our furniture systematically because we cannot help it"), and Moore's mother, whose remark "Let us have done with the umbrella of our contagion" shows the household talking the way the poems sound. A misshapen strawberry becomes "a strawberry that's had quite a struggle" and then a poem.

Young reads Moore through her own convictions. Moore's speech seems to reveal "the secret heart of things," but then "there is no secret heart, no simple solution but another problem." Everything in the poems "zigzags." The book reprints "The Mind Is an Enchanting Thing" in full because Young takes its phrase "conscientious inconsistency" as the key to Moore. She repeats the argument from the previous essay almost word for word: the dull is falsely called "good, old American," when America "has been, if anything, the land of crazy unreason." Moore becomes the proof, "a true pioneer in a wilderness of thought."

The ending lingers on Moore as collector: notebooks "as small as prayer books," envelopes labeled "lizards, Goths, elephants, cosmetics," a box of feathers, and a final gift offer: "She offered me a bluebird's foot." After "My Grandmother's Foot," it is hard not to notice.

"Mari Sandoz" (Flair, 1950)

A short profile built on the idea that Sandoz carries "the look of the land as it was." Young opens by making her "sand-colored like the Nebraska hills," like a bird camouflaged for protection, and sees her as "a passionate archaeologist of the Old West," interested in the psychology of the frontier as much as its events. She reads the gunfighters and outlaws as "the paranoids of the past," and Old Jules as a parable for "the frontiers of all the world," with readers writing to Sandoz from the Arctic, China, and "the inner isolations of Brooklyn."

The best detail is the map on her wall, pasted on cheesecloth so it won't tear in the wind, showing the old names and dried streams as they ran before irrigation, twenty-one years in the making. It is Old James's lost Indianapolis made rigorous and useful. The piece ends on a list of Sandoz's frontier skills (delivering babies before ten, sewing up her own wounds, molding bullets) and the note that she "has never, she says, learned to balance a teacup."

"Horse Utopia" (Flair, 1950)

This is the strangest piece so far. It describes the Lazy VV Ranch in Colorado, run by bean magnate Lynn W. Van Vleet as a preserve for Arabian horses and a summer gathering of guests who may not mention their professions, religions, or politics. The horse material is sincere: the breed's hardiness in blizzards, cowboys riding purebreds on mountain trails, the Mitanni clay tablets on horse training from about 1350 B.C.

The human side is harder to read. Van Vleet insists on the title "Boss," which Young calls "delusively simple." He is "the Prospero of this scene," and even the Continental Divide "seems only a cardboard backdrop of illusion." His utopia restores men's "ancient dignities" away from "valleys of petticoat domination": women wear Levis, may not wear lipstick, must wait on the men and catch their fish, and those who rebel face "a New England ducking stool." Young reports all of this without open comment, and the essay ends right on the ducking stool.

For a writer whose nonfiction book, Angel in the Forest, was about the utopian communities at New Harmony, the piece reads as another study in a man's dream of order imposed on others. Van Vleet belongs with Old James: a rich man with a private vision who assumes everyone else will fill in around it. Whether the magazine format kept her from saying so directly, the flat final image lets the reader judge.

Across part 3

The essays make explicit what the stories implied. Young's central belief is that reality is stranger and more complex than common sense allows, and that American life in particular is a land of marvel rather than plainness. Moore and Sandoz are her models, women who collect, map, and preserve the particular against the general. Van Vleet is the counterexample, the benevolent despot of a tidy utopia. Running under all of it is the war and the bomb, which, in her view, made the case against the "sensible" once and for all.
