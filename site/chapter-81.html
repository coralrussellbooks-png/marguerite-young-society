Content note: The Esther Longtree chapters (72 to 81) deal with stillbirth and pregnancy loss, rape, infanticide, racist violence including lynching, and cruelty to children.

Summary

On a rainy day, Esther hopes to marry at last. She remembers the little toy salesman: childlike, nearly toothless, drinking milk from a baby bottle, the son of a famous spy mother and a window-peeping father. He was the sole survivor of a shipwreck, drifting in a rubber lifeboat past the drowned while a shark circled. He told her the bad men would blow up the world one calm day, just for fun. She considers marrying him so the little shadow boxer will have a legal father, though she can no longer say who fathered him.

Key passages

From the research

  • Esther is described as “always pregnant,” swelling like a dark tide, carrying a “little shadow boxer” under her heart. This pregnancy is not just physical but symbolic of her hope, expectancy, and also her loneliness. Despite her past of “stillborn” children and losses, she remains hopeful that something real and good will come in the future. She imagines marriage and a legal father for her unborn child, the little toy salesman, who is peculiar yet innocent in his own way.
  • The little toy salesman is portrayed as a fragile, almost childlike man with physical oddities: “only three teeth,” “pale blue eyes,” “bandy-legged,” and “tearless.” He drinks milk from a baby bottle and seems innocent to a fault. His past is mysterious and tragic: his mother was a world-famous beauty and spy, his father a windowpeeper, and he himself was a “bottle baby,” never knowing maternal love. He flirts with Esther, seeks comfort, and longs for a home and normal childhood, which he never had. Esther feels a mix of pity, nervousness, and a maternal instinct to “teach him” how to behave and live a normal life.
  • A pivotal passage reveals that the little toy salesman survived a catastrophic sea storm that drowned all others: captain, crew, women, children, even “the moon, the sun, the stars, the kittens.” He was the “lone survivor,” saved by accident in a rubber life boat. This survival haunts both him and Esther: he is a “murderer” in her eyes for not having drowned with the others, for having lived when so many died. His survival is marked by loss; he has lost memory, taste buds, tear ducts, and reason. This episode is described with apocalyptic and mythic imagery, emphasizing the loneliness, guilt, and alienation of the survivor.
  • Throughout his ordeal, the little toy salesman is haunted by a dead child’s ivory-colored hand, “always uplifted from the slow, swelling waters,” wearing a gold ring like his own. This hand tugs at him, clings to him, symbolizing guilt, loss, and an inability to escape the past or the dead. It represents the children Esther has lost and hidden, as well as the burden carried by the survivor. The hand is described as luminous and moth-like, a ghostly presence that symbolizes the thin line between life and death, presence and absence.
  • After the storm, he is pursued by a great white shark, which he initially mistakes for a woman but recognizes as a predator. This episode symbolizes the ongoing threat to survival and the thinness of safety. Despite delirium and starvation, he clings to the memory of the dead child’s hand and the symbolic tokens he carries: the baby bottle, the withered branch, the rosebud, the mourning band. He returns to America a stranger, physically and mentally altered, bearing the weight of trauma and alienation.
  • The chapter closes with Esther’s reflections on the terrible future forewarned by the little toy salesman: a great war, the world blown up, the death of all living things, the end of music, stars, fireflies, and life itself. Yet, in this bleak vision, Esther remains forever “pregnant with her dreams,” nurturing the little shadow boxer, the symbol of hope and continuity. The chapter ends on a note of ambiguous hope and mystery, with Esther wondering who fathered the child and suggesting that “the dead loved the dead” and that this might be how the world goes on.

  • Esther Longtree is portrayed as a deeply complex and tragic figure whose identity is heavily shaped by themes of perpetual pregnancy, loss, and loneliness. The narrative presents her as a woman "always pregnant" with a "little shadow boxer" inside her, symbolizing both hope and despair. This endless state of pregnancy is marked by a haunting series of stillbirths and hidden children buried in various secret places, which underscores a profound maternal suffering and grief. Her body is described vividly as swollen and luminous, with breasts marked by small teeth, signifying the physical and emotional scars of her repeated losses. Esther harbors a desire for a legitimate father for her unborn child, choosing the little toy salesman as the "legal father" not because of love but for the sake of appearance and legality. This man is himself a peculiar and fragile character, described as childish, innocent, and physically weak is an echo of Esther’s own vulnerability. His presence exacerbates her anxiety and sense of fragility, as he is unable to see her obvious pregnancy and the pain it entails. Despite his flaws and oddities, Esther feels maternal towards him and plans to teach him basic emotional responses and social manners, further illustrating her longing for care and order amidst chaos. The little toy salesman is also a symbol of survival and guilt; he is the lone survivor of a great storm at sea in which all others, including children, drowned. Esther’s complex feelings toward him, both pity and accusation, reflect her broader struggle with abandonment, survival, and loss. She calls him a "murderer," not because of direct harm, but because he survived when others, including many children, did not. His survival represents a painful exception and a haunting reminder of the deaths she has endured. His repeated visits and the spectral presence of a dead child’s hand clinging to him evoke a liminal state between life and death, memory and oblivion. Esther’s inner world is one of continuous tension between hope and despair, reality and dream, maternal love and fatal loss. She imagines a future where she might be married and her child might live, but she remains haunted by the many children who never were. Her relationship with the little toy salesman encapsulates this tension: he is both a source of comfort and a symbol of her grief and unfulfilled desires. The chapter reveals her as a figure caught in an endless cycle of pregnancy and mourning, embodying themes of fertility, death, survival, and the elusive nature of identity and belonging. Esther Longtree is a tragic, almost mythic figure embodying the paradoxes of motherhood: creation shadowed by destruction, hope intertwined with loss within the dreamlike and melancholic atmosphere of the novel.
  • Esther Longtree symbolizes a complex and tragic history of maternal suffering, loss, and the burden of unrealized potential in the United States. Her character embodies themes of repeated loss, loneliness, and the haunting presence of stillborn or hidden children, which can be read as a metaphor for the nation's troubled past, especially concerning the cycles of hope, despair, and unfulfilled promise. Her "everlasting pregnancy" and the many stillborn children she has buried symbolize the countless hopes and dreams that have failed to come to fruition in American history. The repeated motif of her "children" hidden in various natural places (tree stumps, streams, roots of flowers) suggests a hidden, suppressed history of loss and forgotten lives. This can be interpreted as reflecting the historical erasures and silencing of certain groups and tragedies within the nation. The interaction with the little toy salesman who survived a great storm at sea that drowned all others and who appears as a fragile, infantile man with the burden of survivor's guilt adds a layer of historical allegory. The storm and the drowning of the many except the lone survivor who "should have drowned" but did not, can be seen as symbolizing catastrophic events in American history (such as mass tragedies, wars, or social upheavals) where many perish or are lost, but some survive carrying the weight of memory and guilt. Esther's sorrow over the little toy salesman's survival, her bitter recognition that he is a "murderer" simply because he lived while others died, reflects a historical ambivalence about survival and progress in the US: the idea that some have survived or prospered amid the suffering or destruction of others. Her physical description (large, swollen, always pregnant but marked by small teeth bites on her breasts) further evokes the land or the country itself as fertile yet scarred, nurturing yet wounded, carrying the weight of generations of loss and unrealized futures. Esther Longtree symbolizes the historical experience of America as a land marked by repeated cycles of birth and death, hope and loss, survival and guilt. She embodies the maternal figure of a nation perpetually pregnant with unrealized futures, haunted by its stillborn children, and struggling with the legacy of survival amid widespread tragedy.

  • The concept of a "Jewish conspiracy" is an antisemitic myth with roots in medieval Europe that became the foundation for modern political antisemitism. These conspiracy theories falsely allege that Jewish people secretly manipulate institutions, banks, the media, and governments for malevolent purposes, such as achieving world domination. It is a dangerous and debunked fiction used throughout history to justify discrimination, persecution, and violence against Jews. In the Middle Ages, hostility toward Jews was often rooted in religious prejudice and false accusations, which served as a basis for later conspiracy myths. The antisemitic myth of blood libel emerged in the 12th century with the story of William of Norwich. It falsely claimed that Jews ritually murdered Christian children to use their blood for religious rituals, particularly for Passover matzah. This accusation frequently led to violent massacres and expulsions of Jewish communities. During the Black Death in the mid-14th century, Jews were used as scapegoats and falsely accused of poisoning wells to cause the plague. This led to the destruction of hundreds of Jewish communities across Europe. Due to restrictions that prevented Jews from owning land or joining certain trades, many worked as moneylenders. This created resentment and gave rise to stereotypes of Jews as greedy and obsessed with money, which later fueled secular conspiracy theories about Jewish wealth and control. In the 19th century, antisemitic ideas evolved from religious prejudice into modern political and racial theories of antisemitism, giving rise to more elaborate conspiracy narratives. German novelist Hermann Goedsche published Biarritz, which featured a fictional scene of a secret rabbinical cabal meeting in a Jewish cemetery to discuss a plot for world domination. This work was a commercial success and was later plagiarized to create The Protocols of the Elders of Zion (circa 1903), this fabricated document is the most infamous antisemitic text. It was a forgery created by the Russian secret police to stir up antisemitism and deflect from public discontent with the Tsarist regime. The Protocols claim to be the minutes of secret meetings held by Jewish leaders outlining a plan to take over the world. Though repeatedly exposed as a hoax, it continues to circulate and influence conspiracy theories to this day. Antisemitic conspiracy myths played a central role in the rise of Nazism and the Holocaust. Adolf Hitler and the Nazi Party adopted the Protocols as proof of a "Jewish world conspiracy". They used it to justify their persecution and extermination of six million Jews. They falsely claimed that Jews were behind communism, capitalism, and Germany's loss in World War I. The American industrialist Henry Ford distributed massive quantities of antisemitic material, including articles based on the Protocols, through his newspaper, The Dearborn Independent. He later retracted and apologized for his publications following a lawsuit, but the damage was already done. Following the Russian Revolution, an antisemitic conspiracy theory known as "Jewish Bolshevism" or Żydokomuna claimed that Jews controlled the Soviet Union and international communist movements. This false notion was promoted by anti-communist factions and heavily used by Nazi propaganda. In the modern era, antisemitic conspiracy theories have adapted to contemporary fears and platforms, continuing to spread hatred. "ZOG" theory (Zionist Occupation Government) theory, promoted by some white nationalists, claims that Jews secretly control Western governments. "Great Replacement" theory is a white nationalist conspiracy theory, which has influenced mass shootings, and claims that Jews are orchestrating a demographic "replacement" of white people through immigration. At the 2017 Charlottesville rally, participants chanted "Jews will not replace us". The internet and social media have facilitated the widespread dissemination of antisemitic conspiracy theories, with groups like QAnon promoting versions of the myth. These online forums and platforms amplify old lies and connect them to new global events, continuing the cycle of prejudice and misinformation.

  • The shipwreck described in this chapter is a powerful and symbolic event that carries deep thematic weight. The shipwreck is recounted primarily through the perspective of the little toy salesman, who is portrayed as the lone survivor of a catastrophic maritime disaster during a fierce storm at sea.
  • The storm and subsequent sinking of the ship represent a cosmic upheaval, described as "a wild, raging storm between the cracking Poles," with the sea rising "in billows to the dark heavens hammering," and the moon and stars falling like sparks and rain of blood. This imagery evokes an apocalyptic vision of the world’s end, suggesting the collapse of order and the overwhelming power of nature and fate.
  • The little toy salesman survives the wreck, sitting alone in a rubber life boat while all others perish, including the captain, crew, women, children, and even celestial bodies like the moon and stars. His survival is depicted ambiguously; he is both pitied and condemned as a "murderer" because he did not drown with the others and thereby "killed" them by surviving. His survival seems almost accidental or unwilling; he "had wanted to do so," but was upheld by "swollen bladders" and "heaving breasts".
  • After the storm, the salesman experiences a prolonged "calm" that is described as worse than the storm itself, filled with loneliness, disorientation, and existential dread. He is surrounded by floating debris and bloated bodies, with a haunting "dead child's hand" that clings to him, symbolizing his inescapable connection to death and loss. This hand, which wears a gold ring like his own, is a spectral reminder of the dead children and the burden of survival.
  • The little toy salesman loses his memory, taste buds, tear ducts, and reason during the storm and the aftermath, which symbolizes a loss of self and disconnection from reality. His mind "cracking" and being "lost between the Poles like a leaf falling nowhere" reflects the disintegration of identity and the fragility of human existence in the face of overwhelming catastrophe.
  • The narrative critiques human selfishness and cruelty during the disaster: the old German sea captain orders men to jump into the sea while women and children take the lifeboats, reflecting societal and gendered survival biases. The passengers are described as conceited and snobbish, oblivious to their impending doom. The little toy salesman's survival contrasts with the collective fate, emphasizing themes of exclusion and difference.
  • The shipwreck is accompanied by powerful, almost liturgical imagery: "leaden church bells," "mourning bands," "a golden flood of music more beautiful than Bach, Beethoven, or Brahms," and the captain conducting as he goes down with the ship. This suggests the shipwreck as a kind of requiem or cosmic ritual. The black mourning band in the sky spanning horizon to horizon intensifies the sense of universal mourning.
  • The little toy salesman’s story parallels Esther Longtree’s ongoing "everlasting pregnancy" with the "little shadow boxer," symbolizing themes of birth, death, and hope amidst despair. The shipwreck and the salesman’s survival underscore Esther’s loneliness and the burden of nurturing life in a world shadowed by loss and uncertainty.

  • The "English-only" movement in the US was characterized by waves of nativism and forced Americanization, peaking around World War I to target immigrants and minority languages. Most landmasses have their opposite point in the ocean, as 70% of Earth is water. The US mainland aligns with the Indian Ocean, Australia with the North Atlantic, and New Zealand with Spain/Portugal.
  • Nimbus primarily refers to a dark gray rain-bearing cloud (often associated with nimbostratus) or, traditionally, a luminous cloud/halo surrounding a deity or saint. Derived from Latin for "cloud," it represents an aura, atmosphere, or a specialized cloud type that produces rain.
  • Aureole is a circle of light or brightness surrounding something, especially as depicted in art around the head or body of a person represented as holy.
  • “the little rubber salesman, a victim of the Jews” may refer to Arthur Miller's play Death of a Salesman. The play features a character, Willy Loman, who is a failing salesman. While Arthur Miller was Jewish, the play itself is often considered a universal American tragedy about the failure of the American Dream, and Willy's Jewish identity is a long-debated topic among literary critics. Some productions and analyses have framed Willy as a Jewish character whose assimilation makes him vulnerable to societal pressures. The rubber pipe that Willy uses to commit suicide is a key symbol in the play. This is also a representation of the character's antisemitism, prejudiced and paranoid worldview within the fictional narrative.
  • Mongolians are an East Asian ethnic group native to Mongolia, China (primarily Inner Mongolia), and parts of Russia, numbering around 10 million in total. Known for a historical, enduring, and nomadic pastoralist tradition, they are closely linked to the Mongol Empire founded by Genghis Khan. They primarily speak Mongolian languages and practice Tibetan Buddhism and shamanism.
  • “The burning, sunlight was blackening his skin” may refer to the damage done by a nuclear bomb.
  • When the stranger, identified as the little toy salesman, recovers consciousness on the shore, the items he sees symbolize America as a landscape of accumulated detritus, lost potential, and the complex interplay of natural decay and human impact.
  • “purple oyster sponges, crab shells, jellyfish like glass, and bones of dead fish”: These natural elements represent the raw, indifferent cycles of nature, where life and death are constantly intertwined. The "glassy" jellyfish can also hint at the blurred lines between reality and illusion that permeate. This aspect of America is ancient, untamed, and a vast receptacle for the remnants of countless lives, both human and non-human.
  • “little carcasses of other little children”: This deeply tragic image symbolizes the profound loss of innocence and unfulfilled potential within the American experience. It resonates with Esther Longtree's story of stillbirth fantasies and lost children, suggesting a hidden sorrow or the casualties of a harsh reality that often goes unacknowledged.
  • “playing cards”: Playing cards in the novel are often associated with chance, fate, and the illusions of control, as seen in scenes with gamblers. Their presence on the shore suggests America as a place where fortunes are gambled, where outcomes are subject to unpredictable forces, and where the remnants of failed risks are left behind.
  • “old junk”: This broad category signifies the accumulation of discarded material, reflecting a society of consumption and the inevitable detritus of progress. It speaks to America as a land that produces and discards, where forgotten objects bear witness to past lives and aspirations.
  • “rubber tires”: As specific man-made discards, rubber tires represent the waste product of modern industry and transportation. They highlight the impact of human activity on the natural landscape, suggesting a departure from a pristine wilderness towards an environment marked by the remnants of ceaseless movement and consumption. This also connects to the little toy salesman's later identity as a "rubber salesman".
  • “birth control devices”: The inclusion of these items is particularly striking, pointing to themes of sexuality, control over reproduction, and perhaps the anxieties or deliberate choices related to creating or preventing life. In a novel deeply concerned with motherhood, loss, and the nature of existence, these devices symbolize attempts to manage or deny natural processes, or the consequences of such interventions in the American context.
  • Collectively, these items portray America not as a land of simple, idealized dreams, but as a complex, often melancholy landscape where the natural world is inextricably mixed with human-made refuse and the poignant echoes of lives lived and lost. It is a place of stark reality, littered with the tangible evidence of decay, chance, and the profound, sometimes tragic, consequences of human existence, leaving the returning "stranger" with a sense of alienation and a confrontation with a less glamorous truth.
  • “there would be a great war and that the bad men would blow up the world some day of calm just for fun” may refer to the Cold War. The Cold War was a period of international geopolitical rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union and their respective allies, the capitalist Western Bloc and communist Eastern Bloc, which began in the aftermath of the Second World War and ended with the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991.
  • "red haw" most commonly refers to the Red Hawthorn, a small, thorny deciduous tree or shrub native to North America known for its white spring flowers and bright red fruits. It is also referred to as Downy Hawthorn or Scarlet Hawthorn.
