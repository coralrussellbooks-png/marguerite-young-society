Chapter 30

  • The white women’s suffrage movement, primarily organized and led by middle-class educated white women, focused on gaining voting rights for white women, culminating in the passage of the 19th amendment in 1920. The movement came out of the abolitionist movement with leaders being: Elizabeth Cady Stanton, Susan B. Anthony, Alice Paul, CArrie Chapman Catt, and Sojourner Truth (who played a significant role in the movement’s background). Suffragists employed various tactics, including lobbying, organizing marches and parades, and civil disobedience. After decades of activism, the 19th Amendment to the US Constitution was ratified in 1920 granting some women the right to vote. The 19th Amendment did not include women of color. It wasn’t until the Voting Rights Act of 1965 until all women’s voting rights were protected.

Chapter 30

  • Young's inclusion of women engaged in various sports and non-traditional roles for their time carries significant thematic weight. This imagery reflects the novel’s broader exploration of gender roles, identity, and societal expectations, particularly highlighting the contrast between conventional femininity and more liberated or unconventional expressions of womanhood. The detailed references to women participating in sports such as tennis, skiing, horseback riding, and even archery, as well as wearing attire adorned with sports motifs, underscore a break from the traditional, passive roles often assigned to women in earlier eras. These women vividly illustrate an active, robust female presence engaged in physical and public life, which was unconventional at the time. This challenges the era’s typical expectations of women’s domestic confinement and passive social roles. Miss MacIntosh’s advocacy for physical exercise such as bowling, horseshoes, and skipping ropes, and her disdain for luxury and affectation, emphasize a robust, self-reliant female identity that contrasts with the fragile, dreamlike femininity embodied by Vera’s mother, Catherine. The active, sporty women serve as a symbol of independence and agency, affirming a woman's right to self-determination and physical presence in the world. The references to sporty and athletic women suggest an ideal of physical health, vitality, and engagement with life, counterbalancing the decay, passivity, and dream states that afflict other characters. By incorporating women in roles and activities traditionally reserved for men, Young subtly reflects the social transformations occurring in the early 20th century, including the women’s suffrage movement and the gradual expansion of women’s rights and public roles. This is echoed in the figure of Cousin Hannah, a suffragette and adventurer who defies conventional female roles through her militant activism and boundary-crossing journeys, contrasting with the passive, isolated femininity of other characters. It also emphasizes the complexity of female identity. These external symbols of activity and engagement contrast with internal struggles over self-perception, societal acceptance, and the tension between appearance and reality, which are central themes in the novel. Young’s references to women in various athletic and unconventional roles serve to critique restrictive gender norms, celebrate female strength and autonomy, and reflect the complex, often contradictory realities of womanhood. They help to illuminate the novel’s central concerns with reality versus illusion, freedom versus confinement, and the evolving social landscape for women during the period.

The novel portrays complex gender dynamics, especially through Vera’s mother Catherine and Miss MacIntosh. Catherine inhabits a world of illusion and decay, surrounded by hallucinations and dominated by male absences and betrayals. Miss MacIntosh, by contrast, embodies a practical, no-nonsense counterpoint to Catherine’s dreamlike passivity, and she strongly critiques illusions and instability. This tension reflects the gendered power struggles Millett describes, though in a symbolic and psychological mode. Cousin Hannah, the suffragette figure in the novel, represents a militant, radical feminist presence, dedicated to overthrowing patriarchal domination, which aligns with Millett’s critique of male control over women’s bodies and lives. Cousin Hannah’s battles for women’s freedom and her rejection of traditional feminine roles resonate with Millett’s political feminism. The novel’s exploration of pregnancies, stillbirths, and female suffering (notably through Esther Longtree’s story) touches on the control of women’s sexuality and reproduction, key concerns in Sexual Politics. The novel’s treatment of female bodies as sites of pain, mystery, and societal expectation parallels Millett’s analysis of the political control exerted through sexuality.

Chapter 35

  • A eunuch is a man who has been castrated. Throughout history, castration often served a specific social function. The earliest records for intentional castration to produce eunuchs are from the Sumerian city of Lagash in the 2nd millennium BCE. Over the millennia since, they have performed a wide variety of functions in many different cultures: courtiers or equivalent domestics, for espionage or clandestine operations, castrato singers, concubines, or sexual partners, religious specialists, soldiers, royal guards, government officials, and guardians of women or harem servants. Eunuchs would usually be servants or slaves who had been castrated to make them less threatening servants of a royal court where physical access to the ruler could wield great influence. Eunuchs were seen as more trustworthy and less interested in establishing their own dynasty. In cultures that had both harems and eunuchs, eunuchs were sometimes used as harem servants. In the Ottoman Empire, eunuchs were typically slaves imported from outside their domains. A fair proportion of male slaves were imported as eunuchs. The Ottoman court harem in Istanbul was under the administration of the eunuchs. Black eunuchs were African slaves who served the concubines and officials in the Harem together with chamber maidens of low rank. An important figure in the Ottoman court was the Chief Black Eunuch. In control of both the Harem and a net of spies among the black eunuchs, the Chief Eunuch was involved in almost every palace intrigue and thereby could gain power over either the sultan or one of his viziers, ministers, or other court officials. One of the most powerful Chief Eunuchs was Beshir Agha in the 1730s, who played a crucial role in establishing the Ottoman version of Hanafi Islam throughout the Empire by founding libraries and schools. The eunuch was used by a great sheik to guard a "black veiled love," supposedly in perfect safety, yet he was ultimately strangled and left to die in a desert courtyard. This story conveys the vulnerability and tragic fate even of those placed in seemingly powerful or protected positions. It may also evoke the theme of betrayal and the harsh realities beneath appearances. The eunuch, as a figure who was mutilated and powerless, yet tasked with guarding love, can be interpreted as a symbol of suffering, control, and the complex dynamics of power and helplessness, themes that resonate with Cousin Hannah's battle against patriarchal domination and social constraints. The anecdote contrasts with Cousin Hannah's hardy temper and unyielding spirit. While the eunuch was pitiful and ultimately victimized, Cousin Hannah was described as courageous, unyielding, and victorious in her suffrage fight.

Chapter 44

  • Lucy Stone and other suffragists adopted the Bloomer costume, which consisted of a knee-length skirt worn over trousers or pantaloons, in the early 1850s. Stone loved the comfort and practicality of the outfit and wore it both at home and in public for about three years. However, the Bloomer costume attracted significant public scrutiny and ridicule, and critics of the women's rights movement often focused on the reformers' attire rather than their arguments for suffrage and other reforms. By late 1853, many prominent suffragists, including Elizabeth Cady Stanton, abandoned the Bloomer costume due to the negative attention it generated. Stanton persuaded Stone to follow suit. Stone resumed wearing long skirts, although she never again wore the restrictive corsets and voluminous dresses of the fashionable style. Other notable suffragists like Susan B. Anthony also stopped wearing Bloomers around the same time. Amelia Bloomer, the editor of The Lily newspaper and namesake of the costume, continued to wear it until 1858. Only Elizabeth Smith Miller, who had helped popularize the style, continued to wear bloomers longer, until 1861.

Chapter 50

  • The history of hyphenating a woman's name is tied to shifts in social customs, legal frameworks, and evolving gender roles. Initially, hyphenated surnames were primarily associated with the British aristocracy and were a way to preserve family names and inheritances, especially when there were no male heirs. Over time, the practice spread to other social classes and eventually to the United States, where it became a symbol of gender equality and a way for women to maintain their professional identity while acknowledging their marital status. In Britain as part of the aristocratic tradition, hyphenated double surnames were historically used by the aristocracy to combine family fortunes and affiliations through marriage. This practice was particularly relevant when a family had no male heirs to carry on the family name, allowing the name to be passed down through a daughter's marriage. Double-barreled surnames became associated with social status and prestige, reflecting the wealth and connections of the families involved. During the Victorian Era hyphenation gained popularity among the British upper middle class during the Victorian era, moving beyond the aristocracy. In the United States, the tradition of women taking their husband's surname was firmly established, with hyphenation being less common. The American suffragist Lucy Stone, who famously retained her birth name after marriage in 1855, became a symbol of women's rights and the movement to retain maiden names. Formed in the early 20th century, the Lucy Stone League advocated for women to keep their surnames upon marriage. The second-wave feminist movement in the 1960s and 70s challenged traditional gender roles and societal expectations, leading to greater acceptance and adoption of hyphenated surnames. Several states in the US began to make it easier for women to retain their surnames legally, removing barriers like restrictions on passports, bank accounts, and other legal documents. Today, hyphenating a woman's name is often a personal choice, driven by a desire to maintain professional identity, honor family heritage, or express a commitment to gender equality.

Chapter 52

  • Mr. Spitzer wonders if doubt should move mountains “which faith had not moved." This is a direct inversion of the common saying that faith can move mountains. This highlights a core philosophical tension between believing in something despite a lack of evidence (faith) and questioning everything (doubt). - Matthew 17:20. The image of the suffragette searching for a lost skirt, leading to the death of her lover, suggests the absurdity of human pursuits. The pursuit of something seemingly trivial (the skirt) leads to tragic consequences, mirroring the existentialist idea that life is inherently meaningless, and humans create their own meaning through their actions. The description of the skirt as "white lace gleaming through snow and its little flowers which were like the memories of little loves" blurs the lines between reality and memory. It suggests that our perceptions of reality are shaped by our past experiences and emotions, leading to a subjective and potentially distorted understanding of the world. The mention of the suffragette crying "Long live woman! Vive la femme! Viva la donna!" in many languages, "perhaps even in Sanskrit which was the language written upon a silent snowflake," touches on the philosophy of language. It suggests that language can be a powerful tool for expressing universal concepts (like the importance of women) but that there are also limits to what language can convey. There are "secret languages never to be translated into words," implying that some truths are beyond the reach of verbal expression. The reference to, "the suffragette who had lost her apostle" could also be referencing the loss of an ideal form. Platonism is the belief that everything we see is a shadow or "lesser" version of a perfect form. The loss of her apostle, who could be a student or follower, is also the loss of what the suffragette sees as a perfect version of herself. The next paragraph emphasizes the individual's choice and action in the face of an indifferent or absurd universe. The suffragette's decision to dress as a man can be seen as an assertion of her freedom and rejection of societal norms. The fantastical images of footmen and golden stairways highlight the human tendency to create meaning and beauty in a world that may ultimately be meaningless. The contrast between Cousin Hannah's practical clothing and the elaborate attire of other climbers could be interpreted as a reflection of the philosophical debate between idealism and materialism. The suffragette's focus on functionality suggests a more materialistic worldview, while the other climbers' emphasis on appearance could be seen as representing idealism. The repeated blurring of the lines between mountains and clouds, and the shifting perceptions of the moon, raise questions about the nature of reality and the limitations of human perception. The statement, "the moon had floated most amazingly distending worlds," suggests that reality is not fixed but is shaped by our individual perspectives. The image of the footman cutting a golden stairway from a frozen lake to the mountains of the moon could be an allusion to Plato's Allegory of the Cave. The stairway could represent the philosopher's ascent from the darkness of the cave (the material world) to the light of the sun (the realm of Forms). The mountains of the moon could symbolize a higher realm of knowledge and understanding. The paragraph's emphasis on nature, individual experience, and the search for spiritual meaning aligns with the themes of Transcendentalism. The suffragette's mountain climbing could be seen as a metaphor for the individual's journey to transcend the limitations of the material world and connect with a higher power or truth. The mention of "horizons unknown as the stars floated" could also be interpreted in a nihilistic way, suggesting that there are no ultimate goals or destinations and that all human endeavors are ultimately meaningless. At first it's a whimsical and imaginative description of mountain climbing, then, beneath the surface, it explores fundamental philosophical questions about freedom, reality, perception, and the meaning of life which invites the reader to think about their own philosophical reflections.

Chapter 52

  • Coverture was a legal doctrine in common law systems where a married woman's legal existence was subsumed into that of her husband's, essentially making her a legal non-entity. Under coverture, a married woman (feme covert) had no independent legal rights, meaning she couldn't own property, enter into contracts, sue or be sued, or control her own earnings. This concept, rooted in the idea that husband and wife were one legal entity, with the husband as that entity, has significantly shaped legal and social landscapes, particularly for women. This concept formed the core of coverture, asserting that husband and wife were a single legal entity, with the husband as the representative. Coverture has roots in feudal Norman customs and English common law. It was a fundamental aspect of the legal system in early America, shaping social and legal norms. Coverture influenced not only legal practices but also societal expectations and gender roles. As the women's rights movement gained momentum, coverture was increasingly criticized as oppressive, leading to reforms. These acts, passed in the late 19th century, began to modify or dismantle coverture by granting women greater control over their property. Coverture was gradually weakened and eventually eliminated through legislation and legal reforms in the United States. While coverture is no longer in effect, its legacy continues to shape legal and social discussions, particularly concerning gender equality. Some legal scholars argue that vestiges of coverture persist in contemporary legal frameworks, particularly in areas like real estate and tax law. The opening lines directly address the historical struggle for women's suffrage.
