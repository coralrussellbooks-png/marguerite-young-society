Chapter 2

  • New England spinsters like Susan B. Anthony and Louisa May Alcott, were famous. Spinsters in general were celebrated for their “uncompromising morality”. Spinster was an honorary term for a woman who earned money spinning wool. They were regarded as “highly moral and fully womanly creatures'' and many documented in their journals that they “remained unmarried not because of individual shortcomings but because they didn’t find the one ‘who could be all things to the heart”.

Chapter 13

  • “the Venus was bearded by patriarchal fog”: Velázquez’s Venus at Her Mirror (1647-51), the Rokeby Venus, hangs in the National Gallery, London. On March 10, 1914, the suffragette Mary Richardson slashed it with a meat cleaver. “I have tried to destroy the picture of the most beautiful woman in mythological history as a protest against the Government for destroying Mrs Pankhurst, who is the most beautiful character in modern history. Justice is an element of beauty as much as colour and outline on canvas. Mrs Pankhurst seeks to procure justice for womanhood, and for this she is being slowly murdered by a Government of Iscariot politicians. If there is an outcry against my deed, let every one remember that such an outcry is an hypocrisy so long as they allow the destruction of Mrs Pankhurst and other beautiful living women, and that until the public cease to countenance human destruction the stones cast against me for the destruction of this picture are each an evidence against them of artistic as well as moral and political humbug and hypocrisy.” (Mary Richardson’s statement, The Times, London, March 11, 1914).

Chapter 30

  • “Because of the loss of a skirt”: Young told Studs Terkel that this came from an Alpine story in which two bloomer girls shed their skirts at a summit, and the younger, going back for hers, was lost: “the lady lost her life for a skirt” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See the Chapter 31 resources.
  • “so many foreign and domestic wars for the emancipation of woman”: The white women’s suffrage movement, primarily organized and led by middle-class educated white women, focused on gaining voting rights for white women, culminating in the passage of the 19th Amendment in 1920. The movement came out of the abolitionist movement with leaders being: Elizabeth Cady Stanton, Susan B. Anthony, Alice Paul, Carrie Chapman Catt, and Sojourner Truth (who played a significant role in the movement’s background). Suffragists employed various tactics, including lobbying, organizing marches and parades, and civil disobedience. After decades of activism, the 19th Amendment to the US Constitution was ratified in 1920 granting some women the right to vote. On paper the 19th Amendment covered all women. In practice it did not secure the vote for women of color. Black women in the South were kept from the polls by poll taxes, literacy tests, and terror. Native American women were not citizens until 1924. Asian immigrant women could not become citizens until 1952. It wasn’t until the Voting Rights Act of 1965 until all women’s voting rights were protected.

Chapter 31

  • “girding her loins with suffrage like that iron girdle worn by a great Crusader’s wife”: “Gird up now thy loins like a man” (Job 38:3). The chastity belt of Crusaders’ wives is largely a legend of later centuries.
  • “Here lies one who searched all her life for her dead love, a body covered by the snow”: Young told Studs Terkel that Cousin Hannah came partly from an Alpine story in which two bloomer girls shed their skirts at a summit, and the younger, going back for hers, was lost: “the lady lost her life for a skirt.” She also drew on Margaret Fuller, who drowned off Fire Island in 1850 within reach of a sandbar (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See the Chapter 30 resources.

Chapter 32

  • “to fly her skirts on tallest mountain peaks”: Young’s Alpine story of the two bloomer girls who shed their skirts on a summit, one lost going back for hers.
  • “picketing in a cold, sleet rain in front of a skirt factory”: The Silent Sentinels of the National Woman’s Party picketed the White House in all weathers from January 1917, and many were jailed. In the 1912 Lawrence, Massachusetts, “Bread and Roses” strike, women mill workers marched through the New England winter. Charles Studin’s suffragist, Young’s source for Cousin Hannah, “chained herself to a lamppost in front of the White House” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).
  • “challenging the conductor to a duel”: Studin’s suffragist “challenged a British prime minister to a duel and the prime minister refused to bear arms against the lady” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).

Chapter 32 · Cousin Hannah as a Warrior and Adventurer

  • Cousin Hannah is depicted as a forceful, uncompromising woman, a “great captain” who went “brandishing her sword like a cane even when there was no one” and preferred “the strident clash of arms, the pitched battle against man.”
  • Her sword symbolizes her militant feminism and unyielding fight against patriarchal oppression. The sword, usually a weapon of war and honor, here is also described metaphorically as a cane, suggesting both her authority and perhaps a certain defiance despite solitude. The image of her as a “veteran of so many winter campaigns, desert marches through burning sands” evokes the harshness of her battles, both literal and metaphorical.

Chapter 34

  • “this cynical suffrage captain”: Through Cousin Hannah, Marguerite Young is not speaking about the futility of suffrage even though it was a long fought battle that has not been fully documented. In The Dialectic of Sex, Firestone quotes Carrie Chapman Catt: “To get the word ‘male’ in effect out of the Constitution cost the women of the country fifty-two years of pauseless campaign… During that time they were forced to conduct 56 campaigns of referenda to male voters, 480 campaigns to get legislatures to submit suffrage amendments to voters, 47 campaigns to get state constitutional conventions to write woman suffrage into state constitutions, 277 campaigns to get state party conventions to include woman suffrage planks, 30 campaigns to get presidential party conventions to adopt woman suffrage planks in party platforms and 19 successive campaigns with 19 successive Congresses” (Carrie Chapman Catt and Nettie Rogers Shuler, Woman Suffrage and Politics, 1923, as quoted in Shulamith Firestone, The Dialectic of Sex, 1970). Firestone adds, “Thus defeat was so frequent, and victory so rare—and then achieved by such bare margins—that even to read about the struggle for suffrage is exhausting, let alone to have lived through it and fought for it.” In light of this Cousin Hannah’s relentless energy is seen as not disproportionate to the women’s suffrage movement. Cousin Hannah’s attitude to marriage and men (at least the outward appearance) would reflect the fundamental struggle. Again from The Dialectic of Sex, “To make both women and children totally independent would be to eliminate not just the patriarchal nuclear family, but the biological family itself” (Shulamith Firestone, The Dialectic of Sex, 1970).

Chapter 35

  • “even a British prime minister to quail”: Young’s source for Cousin Hannah, a suffragist “battle-axe” described by Charles Studin, a founder of the NAACP, “challenged a British prime minister to a duel and the prime minister refused to bear arms against the lady” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).
  • “She had wished to overthrow man, both as to the marital state”: The suffrage movement did not oppose marriage. The anti-suffrage movement opposed suffrage because it feared that women would be “masculinized” and that this in turn would “feminize” men. Under coverture, after marriage the man and woman became one legal unit that the man controlled. Women in many cases were not allowed to own property, sign contracts, earn a salary, or enter education without their husbands’ permission.
  • Gertrude Bell crossed these deserts and helped draw the borders of Iraq in 1921, yet by 1909 she was honorary secretary of the Women’s National Anti-Suffrage League.
  • “She had wished to rescue the slave, not only the economic slave but also the slave of love”: Cousin Hannah’s struggle is presented in terms of gender emancipation and suffrage activism. The economic slavery she aims to end is linked to the patriarchal and marital structures. Her battle is against male dominance and the social and domestic oppression of women.

Chapter 35 · Freedom and Emancipation

  • Cousin Hannah embodies the fight for women’s rights and freedom from traditional roles.
  • Her life is a metaphor for breaking boundaries: social, geographic, and existential.
  • The suffrage movement is portrayed as her husband, her master, and her true love.

Chapter 36

  • “the queen of the strong-minded”: “Strong-minded women” was a common nineteenth-century name, often a sneer, for advocates of women’s rights.

Chapter 38

  • “The lonely cause of suffrage had seemed to my mother to be quite unworthy”: Catherine does not believe in suffrage, and that was common among rich, white women from elite social and political backgrounds at the time. They argued that it would disrupt gender roles and the family structure, doubted women’s political capacity, and opposed broader progressive reforms such as prohibition. Racism drove them too, for they feared the vote would spread to women of color. They argued that it would simply double the size of the electorate without adding any new value or perspective. Opposition also came from business and industry since it may lead to increased regulation and reforms that would hurt their profits.
  • On May 21, 1914, Emmeline Pankhurst was arrested at the palace gates as suffragettes tried to bring a petition to King George V.

Chapter 38 · Sacrifice and Activism

  • Cousin Hannah embodies sacrifice for a cause, dedicating her life to suffrage and battling societal “ogres.”
  • Her life is one of relentless struggle and fortitude, marked by physical and emotional trials.
  • Catherine contrasts this with her own withdrawal and guilt for not engaging in such struggles.

Chapter 39

  • “the rational suffrage movement”: Cousin Hannah is described as being part of the “avant-garde” women’s suffrage movement and that is a real thing with Inez Milholland Boissevain as a prominent figure who led the 1913 suffrage parade in Washington, D.C., and was known for her radical activism. She was part of a Greenwich Village group of progressives and socialists who were involved in creating The Masses, a progressive magazine that combined art, satire, and political commentary. She was dressed in a flowing white cloak and riding a white horse. She was a lawyer, a peace activist, and part of a group that embraced radical art and political commentary, making her a figure who pushed the boundaries of activism. Her beauty and the symbolism of her attire during the parade helped to capture public attention and advance the cause of suffrage. She was part of a broader movement that included other notable figures like Dorothy Day, Crystal Eastman, and Louise Bryant, all involved in radical and progressive movements. While other leaders like Susan B. Anthony, Elizabeth Cady Stanton, and Alice Paul were also key figures in the suffrage movement, Milholland’s involvement in a group that pushed the boundaries of art and activism, along with her leading role in the parade, made her a prominent figure in the avant-garde of the movement.

Chapter 41

  • “riding her white horse through a cloud”: Inez Milholland rode a white horse at the head of the 1913 suffrage parade in Washington.
  • “trooping her suffrage colors of rose on white”: The real suffrage colors were purple, white, and gold for the National Woman’s Party, yellow for the National American Woman Suffrage Association, and purple, white, and green for the British suffragettes. In Tennessee in August 1920, when the last state needed ratified the Nineteenth Amendment, “Supporters of suffrage wore yellow roses in public; the anti-suffragists wore red roses,” in “what became known as the War of the Roses” (National Constitution Center, “The man and his mom who gave women the vote”). Hannah’s “red rose of her blood” was, in Tennessee, the flower of her enemies.
  • “patriarchal suffrage captain”: Cousin Hannah is given this oxymoron title “patriarchal suffrage captain.” Patriarchy is a system of social organization where men hold the primary power and authority versus suffrage which is the fundamental right of all to vote which would lead to all citizens having the power to influence political decisions and hold leaders accountable. The paradox is suggesting that someone who holds patriarchal power but is also advocating for or leading a movement that challenges this very power structure.

Chapter 42

  • “But she had outlived her fame”: Cousin Hannah dying in obscurity is reminiscent of Shulamith Firestone’s funeral where mourners grappled with the idea that a whole generation of feminists had been unable to thrive in the world they had done so much to create (Susan Faludi, “Death of a Revolutionary,” The New Yorker, April 15, 2013).
  • The British burned Boer farms and held Boer women and children and Black Africans in concentration camps, where some 26,000 Boers, most of them children, and at least 14,000 Black Africans died. Emily Hobhouse exposed the camps, and the suffrage leader Millicent Fawcett headed the women’s commission sent to inspect them in 1901 (Thomas Pakenham, The Boer War, 1979).
  • Sixteen-year-old Sybil Ludington is said to have ridden some forty miles through what is now Putnam County, New York, on the night of April 26, 1777, to rouse her father’s militia after the British burned Danbury. The story first appeared in print in Martha J. Lamb’s History of the City of New York (1880), and Paula D. Hunt found no reliable evidence of the ride from the time (“Sybil Ludington, the Female Paul Revere: The Making of a Revolutionary War Heroine,” New England Quarterly 88, no. 2, 2015).
  • “Cousin Hannah as the great bloomer girl”: Amelia Bloomer’s The Lily promoted the loose trousers worn under a short skirt in 1851, and the costume took her name. See Young’s account to Studs Terkel of the bloomer girls who shed their skirts, in the Chapter 38 resources.
  • “leading the needle workers to strike albeit she was the owner of a mill”: In the Uprising of the 20,000, the New York shirtwaist strike of 1909, wealthy women such as Alva Belmont and Anne Morgan, the “mink brigade,” paid bail and fees for the strikers. Earlier, the Lowell mill girls of Massachusetts turned out in 1834 and 1836. Harriet Hanson Robinson, who led her room out at eleven, became a suffragist (Harriet H. Robinson, Loom and Spindle, 1898).
  • “the narrow channels of a lost cause”: Suffrage for women took a hundred years and many rights for women while Young was alive were not in place. Women and children were still property. Seeing again the erosion of these rights and the incompleteness of them worldwide (2025 saw Bangladesh Islamists protesting the expansion of human rights to women and a general attempt at rolling back gender rights for most parts of the world) one can see where there has to be a complete love (obsession) for something that might never be achieved in one lifetime.
  • “organized the pickets around an old town clock”: See the Silent Sentinels of the National Woman’s Party, who picketed the White House in 1917, in the Chapter 32 resources. Arrested pickets were beaten and choked at the Occoquan Workhouse on the “Night of Terror,” November 14-15, 1917, and Alice Paul was force-fed.
  • “No slave might wait on the great Abolitionist”: The suffrage movement grew out of abolition. Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott met at the World Anti-Slavery Convention in London in 1840, where the women delegates were refused seats, and they called the Seneca Falls convention in 1848.

Chapter 43

  • “the dead cast no vote”: Padding the rolls with the names of the dead, the “graveyard vote,” was an old charge against city machines such as Tammany Hall. Mr. Spitzer’s brother believed otherwise, and Mr. Spitzer, who has not “been inside a ballot box for years,” sits at the deathbed of a suffrage captain.
  • “In heaven the black rooster lays the egg, and the white hen crows”: An old proverb says “A whistling woman and a crowing hen are neither fit for God nor men.” It was used against women who spoke out of their expected place, which makes a crowing hen a fitting image for a suffragette. A rooster laying an egg was considered unnatural and dangerous. Medieval legend held that a rooster’s egg hatched the basilisk, and in Basel in 1474 a rooster accused of laying an egg was tried and burned (E. P. Evans, The Criminal Prosecution and Capital Punishment of Animals, 1906). Hannah’s heaven reverses the order of the barnyard. The hen crows, “and none shall stop her.”
  • Bloomers are divided women’s garments for the lower body. They were developed in the 19th century as a healthful and comfortable alternative to the heavy, constricting dresses worn by American women (Wikipedia). They take their name from their best-known advocate, the women’s rights activist Amelia Bloomer. It became a symbol of women’s rights in the early 1850s. See Amelia Bloomer in the Chapter 42 resources.

Chapter 44

  • “that great bloomer girl”: Lucy Stone and other suffragists adopted the Bloomer costume, which consisted of a knee-length skirt worn over trousers or pantaloons, in the early 1850s. Stone loved the comfort and practicality of the outfit and wore it both at home and in public for about three years. However, the Bloomer costume attracted significant public scrutiny and ridicule, and critics of the women’s rights movement often focused on the reformers’ attire rather than their arguments for suffrage and other reforms. By late 1853, many prominent suffragists, including Elizabeth Cady Stanton, abandoned the Bloomer costume due to the negative attention it generated. Stanton persuaded Stone to follow suit. Stone resumed wearing long skirts, although she never again wore the restrictive corsets and voluminous dresses of the fashionable style. Other notable suffragists like Susan B. Anthony also stopped wearing Bloomers around the same time. Amelia Bloomer, the editor of The Lily newspaper and namesake of the costume, continued to wear it until 1858. Only Elizabeth Smith Miller, who had helped popularize the style, continued to wear bloomers longer, until 1861 (Wikipedia). See Amelia Bloomer in the Chapter 42 resources.
  • “the white feather of cowardice”: In August 1914 Admiral Charles Penrose-Fitzgerald founded the Order of the White Feather, whose women handed white feathers to men out of uniform. Emmeline and Christabel Pankhurst turned the WSPU from the vote to recruiting, and Sylvia Pankhurst wrote that their followers handed out feathers to young men in civilian dress (E. Sylvia Pankhurst, The Home Front, 1932).
  • “this old maid”: In Henry James’s The Bostonians (1886), the Boston suffragist Olive Chancellor loves the young speaker Verena Tarrant. Two women who made a home together came to be called a Boston marriage.

Chapter 45

  • “this great father of suffrage”: Young named her own sources for Cousin Hannah (see the Chapter 30 resources and the Chapter 31 resources), and Susan B. Anthony and her sister, Hannah, offer a historical comparison. Mr. Spitzer described Cousin Hannah as “this great father of suffrage.” Susan B. Anthony’s father was the first one in the family to become interested in the women’s suffrage movement. She was interested in social reform. She was a teacher and was paid less than her male counterparts (then as now). Anthony said, “I wasn’t ready to vote, didn’t want to vote, but I did want equal pay for equal work.” The family started out as Quakers and Anthony claimed to be a Quaker but the father moved away from the order to join the Unitarian Church. They were abolitionists and temperance advocates and had the likes of Frederick Douglass speak at their house. She formed a partnership with Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucy Stone. Stanton kept a room for her at her house where she also spent most of her life working with her. Anthony and Stanton went on to write the History of Woman Suffrage. Anthony said this kind of work, “makes me feel growly all the time... No warhorse ever panted for the rush of battle more than I for outside work. I love to make history but hate to write it.” Anthony did not believe in her writing skills which mostly went to Stanton. Most of her quotes come from speeches and lectures. Anthony did not live to see the ratification of the 19th Amendment or equal rights. “There have been others also just as true and devoted to the cause—I wish I could name every one—but with such women consecrating their lives, failure is impossible!” “Now, after 40 years of agitation, the idea is beginning to prevail that women were created for themselves, for their own happiness, and for the welfare of the world.” Back to her religion. It is a mixed bag in that they moved to being Unitarian although proud of her Quaker roots. She was involved with the Friends of Human Progress that included “Christians, Jews, Mahammedans, and Pagans.” She tried to start her own Free church where all were welcome. She later, according to Stanton, became agnostic. “Work and worship are one with me. I can not imagine a God of the universe made happy by my getting down on my knees and calling him ‘great.’” When Anthony’s sister Hannah was on her deathbed, she asked Susan to talk about the great beyond, but Anthony later wrote, “I could not dash her faith with my doubts, nor could I pretend a faith I had not; so I was silent in the dread presence of death.” Hannah was known for her domestic nature, but also for her full sympathy with the reforms championed by her sister. As a teen, Anthony went to parties, and she had offers of marriage when she was older, but there is no record of her ever having a serious romance. Anthony loved children, however, and helped raise the children in the Stanton household. As a young worker in the women’s rights movement, Anthony expressed frustration when some of her co-workers began to marry and have children, sharply curtailing their ability to work for the understaffed movement (Wikipedia). When Lucy Stone abandoned her pledge to stay single, Anthony’s scolding remarks caused a temporary rupture in their friendship. It was a bit more than a temporary rupture. They both formed separate suffrage movements. Anthony wanted a federal constitutional amendment. Stone wanted a more gradual state-by-state approach. Anthony opposed the 15th Amendment because it did not grant everyone the right to vote. Stone supported it as a stepping stone. It was 20 years before their two organizations worked together again. Stone worked out with her husband a separate but equal marriage where Stone retained rights to her property and finances and her personal liberty. Lucy Stone wore bloomers. She also kept her name. They publicly declared their commitment to equality and rejected the idea of a wife’s legal subordination. Journalists repeatedly asked Anthony to explain why she never married. She answered one by saying, “It always happened that the men I wanted were those I could not get, and those who wanted me I wouldn’t have.” To another, she answered, “I never found the man who was necessary to my happiness. I was very well as I was.” To a third she said, “I never felt I could give up my life of freedom to become a man’s housekeeper. When I was young, if a girl married poor, she became a housekeeper and a drudge. If she married wealth she became a pet and a doll. Just think, had I married at twenty, I would have been a drudge or a doll for fifty-nine years. Think of it!” In a speech in 1877, Anthony predicted “an epoch of single women. If women will not accept marriage with subjugation, nor men proffer it without, there is, there can be, no alternative. The woman who will not be ruled must live without marriage.”
  • “the innumerable coal mine strikes she had helped to start”: Mary Harris “Mother” Jones organized coal miners’ strikes from the 1890s into the 1920s. She put her class before suffrage and is reported to have said, “you don’t need the vote to raise hell!” (Russell E. Smith, “March of the Mill Children,” Social Service Review 41, no. 3, 1967, 298). To suffragists who called her anti, she answered, “I’m not an anti to anything which brings freedom to my class” (The Autobiography of Mother Jones, 1925, 203).

Chapter 46

  • “surely the greatest ever known among ladies”: Real suffragists climbed too. Annie Smith Peck made the first ascent of Huascarán’s north peak in 1908 and planted a “VOTES FOR WOMEN” flag on Coropuna in Peru in 1911. Fanny Bullock Workman of Massachusetts was photographed in the Karakoram about 1912 holding a newspaper headed “Votes for Women.”

Chapter 50

  • Hyphenated double surnames began among the British aristocracy as a way to keep a family name and inheritance alive when there was no male heir, and spread to the Victorian upper middle class. In the United States wives took their husbands’ names; the suffragist Lucy Stone kept her own at her marriage in 1855, and the Lucy Stone League, founded by Ruth Hale in 1921, urged women to do the same. Second-wave feminists of the 1960s and 1970s made the hyphenated name a common choice, and states removed barriers to keeping a birth name on passports, bank accounts, and other documents. For the old suffragette captain “the hyphen was all.” (Wikipedia)

Chapter 52

  • “She had chained herself to a lamp-post”: Chaining was a signature suffrage protest. In January 1908 Edith New and Olivia Smith chained themselves to the railings of 10 Downing Street and shouted “Votes for Women!” so that Flora Drummond and Mary Macarthur could slip inside. On 28 October 1908 Muriel Matters and Helen Fox of the Women’s Freedom League chained themselves to the grille of the Ladies’ Gallery in the House of Commons, and the section of grille had to be removed with them still attached. Chaining herself to a lamp post, she makes herself a public fixture, visible and accountable, a woman who takes control of her fate even while bound by it.
  • “this old bloomer girl”: Elizabeth Smith Miller wore loose trousers under a short skirt in Seneca Falls in 1851, and Amelia Bloomer championed the costume in The Lily, her temperance and women’s rights paper, so that it took her name. Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Susan B. Anthony wore it for a time before ridicule drove most women back into long skirts.

Chapter 52 · Women, Gender, and Marriage

  • Cousin Hannah fought “for the overthrow of man” and urged all ladies to “divest themselves of excess luggage,” yet her forty trunks hold only wedding gowns. Mr. Spitzer cannot tell whether she dreamed of being the bridegroom or the bride, and he decides the world need not know.

Chapter 53

  • “He knew a man who had fought on when he had broken his hand” - It is part of a richly symbolic and dreamlike meditation on perseverance, struggle, and endurance despite injury or limitation. Though the passage is metaphorical and layered with ambiguity, this particular phrase likely illustrates the idea of continuing to strive or fight even when physically or emotionally impaired, suggesting resilience in the face of hardship. Within the broader context of the chapter, Mr. Spitzer reflects on various images of endurance, loss, and identity, often blending reality and dream. The mention of a man who fights on despite a broken hand can be read as symbolizing the human capacity to endure suffering and continue struggling even when "broken" or diminished. It resonates with the novel’s themes of fragmented identities, the persistence of hope or effort despite injury or loss, and the ambiguous boundary between life and death, reality and dream. The phrase embodies the motif of persistence in adversity, highlighting that even with an injury that should impair one's ability, the will to continue battling remains. It serves as a metaphor for the characters’ emotional or spiritual struggles, such as Mr. Spitzer’s own wrestling with loss and identity, or the suffragette’s relentless, though ultimately tragic, quest for love and meaning in a shifting, uncertain world. The phrase underscores resilience, the refusal to give up despite damage or limitation as a theme central to the novel’s meditation on human endurance amid mystery and loss.

Chapter 53

  • “Death danced with the lady” This phrase metaphorically captures the intimate, complex, and ambiguous relationship between the lady (the old suffragette) and death. The lady is portrayed as someone who has lived a life filled with paradoxes, mystery, and transformative journeys. She is described as both active and passive, as a traveler between realms of life and death, reality and dream. The imagery of dancing with death suggests a close, almost companionable engagement with mortality, as if death is not merely an end but a partner in the ongoing movement of existence and identity. The "dance" implies a dynamic, fluid interaction rather than a static or fearful state. The lady, having abandoned conventional roles (such as bride or wife), and having lived a life of wandering, disguise, and defiance (including climbing mountains, flying, and escaping societal norms), engages with death not as a final cessation but as an essential part of her being and journey. It reflects her liminal status: between life and death, reality and dream, presence and absence. This metaphor also aligns with the novel’s broader themes about the blurred boundaries between states of being, the elusive nature of identity, and the intertwining of love, loss, and mortality. The lady's dance with death is a poetic way to express that she has been inextricably linked to death, not just physically in dying but symbolically in how she lived, loved, and searched for meaning amid illusions and truths. It signifies the profound and enigmatic connection between the suffragette and death, highlighting the novel’s exploration of mortality as a dance, a continual movement where endings and beginnings coexist, and where death is not a distant stranger but a close and intimate partner.

Chapter 53

  • “who vaulted the farthest distance” The phrase likely alludes to someone who has made the greatest leap (physically, emotionally, or spiritually) beyond ordinary limits or boundaries. In the context of the chapter's exploration of the suffragette's far-ranging adventures, both literal (mountain climbing, flying, traveling) and symbolic (crossing boundaries of gender, identity, time, and reality), “vaulting the farthest distance” can be understood as transcending conventional human experience or limitations. It suggests surpassing all others in attempting or achieving the impossible, going beyond the known world, or bridging the gap between life and death, reality and dream. This fits with the novel's recurring motifs of journeys through unknown realms, the blurring of reality and illusion, and the search for lost love or identity.

Chapter 54

  • The children kicking the skull on a playing field evokes a sense of innocence or youth interacting carelessly with death or mortality. It symbolizes how life and death coexist, often with the young being unaware or indifferent to the gravity of death. The skull, a universal emblem of death, is treated like a mere toy, indicating a cycle where death is a part of life’s play or perhaps ignored or trivialized by the living. This image resonates with the novel's broader themes of transience, the elusive nature of identity and love, and the blurred boundaries between life, death, reality, and dreams. Mr. Spitzer’s reflections on death, lost loves, and the mysterious suffragette’s life suggest that death is not an end but part of an ongoing, ambiguous process where the living and the dead intermingle in memory and imagination. The children’s act of kicking the skull could be seen as a metaphor for this ambiguous relationship, where death is present but not fully understood or feared; it’s a part of the world that continues in cycles. In context, it may also suggest the loss of reverence or the innocence of youth confronted with mortality, and how the past (symbolized by the skull) is treated by the succeeding generations: sometimes with disregard or playfulness, but always marking a connection between life and death.

Chapter 54

  • Bye, bye, blackbird - It is a poetic and philosophical meditation on the mysteries of a singular life that defies easy understanding, embodying themes of elusive identity, the interplay of dream and reality, the nature of love and loss, and the human desire to find meaning in the face of unknowability. Mr. Spitzer’s exploration of the suffragette’s estate and legacy becomes a metaphor for the quest to understand the self and others, a quest that is ultimately inconclusive but deeply meaningful.

Chapter 54

  • “mockingbird psychology” It is a metaphorical expression rather than a straightforward psychological term. Throughout the novel, there is a deep exploration of identity, illusion, reflection, and the blurred boundaries between reality and dreams. The "mockingbird" is a bird known for mimicking the songs of other birds, often symbolizing imitation, lack of originality, or an identity that is constructed from external voices rather than authentic selfhood. In this context, "mockingbird psychology" likely refers to a psychological state or pattern wherein a person’s identity, thoughts, or emotions are shaped by imitation or mimicry of others rather than originating from an authentic center. It suggests a kind of fragmented or dependent consciousness that reflects and echoes external stimuli or influences without a stable, original self. This fits with the novel’s frequent motifs of doubles, shadows, reflections, and spectral presences, as with Mr. Spitzer’s reflections on his own duality and the suffragette’s elusive identity. Characters often seem caught in a liminal state between self and other, reality and dream, original and copy. It can be understood as the psychological condition of being a reflective echo or mimic, lacking a fixed, authentic self, resonating with the novel’s larger meditation on identity, memory, and the elusive nature of reality.

Chapter 64

  • Red bloomers, named after reformer Amelia Bloomer, emerged in the 1850s as a radical symbol of women's dress reform for health and freedom, featuring loose trousers under a short skirt for mobility, but faced public scorn as "unwomanly" and threatening gender norms, leading many suffragists to adopt less controversial symbols like a red shawl, though bloomers persisted as gym/cycling wear.
