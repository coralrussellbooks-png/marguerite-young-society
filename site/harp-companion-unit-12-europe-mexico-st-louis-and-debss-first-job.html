Unit 12, pp. 257-272: Europe, Mexico, St. Louis, and Debs's first job

Part of a reading of Harp Song for a Radical (1999), Marguerite Young’s life of Eugene Victor Debs.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Unit 11 · Harp Song for a Radical: A History Companion · Unit 13 →

Background for this unit: The History in Harp Song for a Radical

Young closes Mary Lincoln's story with her return from France and death in Springfield in 1882, then turns to the fourteen-year-old Debs going to work in the Vandalia railroad shops. The unit follows him onto the footboard as a locomotive fireman and to St. Louis during the depression of 1873, where he met Gilded Age poverty up close.

Major entries

Debs goes to work on the railroad (pp. 261-268)

  • What happened: In 1870, at fourteen, Debs left school for the paint shop of the Terre Haute and Indianapolis Railroad, the line later called the Vandalia, scraping grease and paint from locomotives for fifty cents a day. Young credits the introduction to a family friend, the French car painter Pierre Solomon, and names the shop boss "Old Man Pebbles" (p. 264), details that are unverified.

  • The fireman: By sixteen he was a locomotive fireman, shoveling coal into the firebox. Young describes the winter runs, snow driving into his eyes so that he seemed "to see two moons at once" (p. 265). Firemen died often: Young notes a young man named Stewart killed near Terre Haute, whose death Debs kept from his mother (p. 268).

  • Why it matters: Railroading was among the deadliest work in America. In the 1880s thousands of railroad workers were killed each year, and brakemen and firemen could not buy insurance. That danger is why the railroad brotherhoods began as insurance societies (Unit 13).

  • Further reading: Walter Licht, Working for the Railroad (1983).

St. Louis and the depression of 1873 (pp. 267-272)

  • What happened: When the Panic of 1873 struck, the Vandalia laid Debs off. He went to St. Louis looking for railroad work and wrote home about bedbugs and the misery around him (p. 268). He returned to Terre Haute in 1874 to clerk for the Hulman grocery firm.

  • Why it matters: The depression that began in 1873 lasted until 1879, the longest in American history until the 1930s. Unemployment, wage cuts, and tramping men on the roads set the stage for Tompkins Square (Unit 18) and the Great Strike of 1877.

  • The city: St. Louis was a railroad and river hub and home to many German Forty-Eighters. Its smoke and fogs over the "River Queen" (p. 270) come from the coal economy Debs fed as a fireman.

  • Further reading: Scott Reynolds Nelson, A Nation of Deadbeats: An Uncommon History of America's Financial Disasters (2012).

Joseph Pulitzer in St. Louis (pp. 270-271)

  • Who he was: Born in Hungary in 1847, he came to America in 1864 as a recruit for the Union cavalry. In St. Louis he became a reporter for the Westliche Post, the German paper co-owned by Carl Schurz, and a Missouri legislator.

  • Why it matters: He campaigned with Schurz's Liberal Republicans against the corruption of the Grant administration (p. 270). In 1878 he founded the St. Louis Post-Dispatch, and in 1883 he bought the New York World, pioneering the crusading popular press. His prizes for journalism began in 1917.

  • Further reading: James McGrath Morris, Pulitzer: A Life in Politics, Print, and Power (2010).

Herbert Spencer and Social Darwinism (p. 266)

  • Who he was: English philosopher (1820-1903) who coined the phrase "survival of the fittest" in 1864. He applied evolution to society and argued that the state should not interfere with competition.

  • Why it matters: Spencer was hugely popular with American industrialists. Andrew Carnegie called himself a disciple, and Spencer was feted on his 1882 American tour. His The Man Versus the State (1884) attacked socialism and reform legislation as doomed to fail, the view Young sets against Debs's faith in human perfectibility (p. 266).

  • Further reading: Richard Hofstadter, Social Darwinism in American Thought (1944).

Mary Lincoln's last years (pp. 259-261)

  • What happened: After her release she lived in exile at Pau, in southern France, from 1876 to 1880. Sailing home on the Amérique, she nearly fell down a stairway and was caught by Sarah Bernhardt, who told the story in her memoirs (p. 259). She died on July 16, 1882, in the Springfield house of her sister Elizabeth and brother-in-law Ninian Edwards, the house where she had married Lincoln. She was buried beside him at Oak Ridge Cemetery.

  • How Young uses it: Young ties the year of her death to Twain's The Prince and the Pauper, a tale of rags and riches. That book appeared in 1881, the year before she died.

Shorter entries

  • Manifest Destiny (p. 257): The belief, named in 1845, that the United States was destined to spread across the continent. It justified the Mexican War.

  • Seward and Mexico (p. 257): Secretary of State William Seward pressed Napoleon III to withdraw French troops from Mexico after the Civil War, which doomed Maximilian.

  • The Reverend James A. Reed (p. 260): The Springfield Presbyterian minister who preached Mary Lincoln's funeral sermon.

  • Daniel Debs, "Dandy Lion" (pp. 262-268): Young's nickname for Debs's father, who read French and German classics aloud to his children. Hugo's Les Misérables and its hero Jean Valjean shaped the boy (pp. 266-267).

  • The heroes of liberty (p. 262): Young lists the figures the young Debs admired: the abolitionists William Lloyd Garrison, Wendell Phillips, and William Wilberforce; the Hungarian patriot Louis Kossuth, who toured America in 1851-1852; and the Italian republican Giuseppe Mazzini.

  • The Southern Illinois railroad and Belleville (p. 267): Lines Debs knew around St. Louis. Belleville was a center of German radicalism in Illinois.

Allusions

  • Quetzalcoatl and Montezuma (p. 257): The Aztec feathered-serpent god and the emperor defeated by Cortés.

  • The True Cross (p. 257): The relic of Christ's cross, here a symbol of imperial piety.

  • Charles Martel (p. 262): The Frankish leader who stopped the Muslim advance at Tours in 732.

  • John Chrysostom (p. 262): The fourth-century bishop called "golden-mouthed" for his preaching.

  • Plato's Republic (p. 262): The first great utopia.

  • Lazarus (p. 263): The beggar at the rich man's gate in Luke 16.

  • Nimrod (p. 266): The mighty hunter of Genesis, for Daniel Debs.

  • Gog and Magog (p. 269): The hostile powers of biblical prophecy.
