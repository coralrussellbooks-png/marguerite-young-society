A House Built Out of Nouns

The best way into Young's thinking is through one of her sentences. Here is the Cartwheel house as it first appears, in Chapter 1:

The great, sea-blackened house with golden spires and cornices and towers peeled by the salt air, dark allées, hidden interiors, the empty drawing rooms where the hostess had not set foot for many years, as many drawing rooms as tideless years, the rooms too many for mortal use, chambers within chambers, the gilded, mirroring ballrooms where no one danced, the hangings of scaly gold and rain-stained velvet, the heathen monsters everywhere, the painted, clouded ceilings illuminated by partial apparitions of the gods, the silken, padded walls, the ropes of rusted bells, the angels and the cherubim and the immortal rose, the dream of heaven, the lily-breasted virgins sporting in fields of asphodel, the water-gurgling gargoyles or those coated by dust, the interior and exterior fountains, the broken marble statues in ruined gardens sloping towards the sea, the disc throwers, the fat cupids, the thin psyches with flowing curls, the mute Apollo Belvedere, the king’s horsemen, the life-sized chessmen seeming to move against the moving clouds that moved above the moving waters, the sea light lighting their wooden eyes, the seagulls perched like drifts of snow upon their heads.

Look at what this sentence does not do. It never gets to its verb. The house never does anything. Thing is laid on thing: the towers, the empty drawing rooms, the ballrooms “where no one danced,” the angels and the gargoyles, the broken statues, the gulls. Nothing moves the sentence forward, because nothing in the house moves forward. The hostess “had not set foot” in those rooms “for many years,” and there are “as many drawing rooms as tideless years.” Time in this house does not pass. It piles up, the way the sentence piles up.

And nothing in the pile outranks anything else. The “dream of heaven” sits beside the dusty gargoyles. The “immortal rose” sits beside the rain-stained velvet. The great statue of Apollo stands in the same line as the seagulls on the chessmen's heads. Every thing gets in.

Miss MacIntosh's Straight Line

The very next sentence turns to Miss MacIntosh: “What could Miss MacIntosh, a simple woman with a broken nose, find to admire in any broken marble statue, that which had been sculptured by man dreaming that he was other than he was or that he was man?” She lives by a different grammar. In Chapter 3 Vera remembers that “She always proceeded directly, straight to the point, never dilly-dallying, not shifting her course with every new blow of the wind.” “Common sense is the finest sense, she had always said” (Chapter 1), and for her “the way was very plain.”

Her sentences have somewhere to go. They have verbs, and the verbs give orders. Even when she admits how much there is in the world, she turns it into a reason to move on: “We must hasten, and we must not loiter nor imagine we could gather up all the broken seashells on this old beach, even though we should gather seashells until the end of time, for some would always be missing.” The broken shells are real to her. She just will not stop for them.

When she walks into the sea, the novel loses its one voice that always knew where it was going. What is left is the other grammar, the one that keeps adding.

“And,” Not “But”

There are two small words that sort the world in two different ways. “But” divides. Say the fog was thick, but the coast was real, and the sentence has made a ruling: the coast counts, and the fog is only something in the way. “And” adds. Say the fog was thick and the coast was there, and both stay in the world together.

Young writes with “and.” Look at how Vera first describes the woman she has been searching for: “Long years, drifting without other purpose, I had searched for that hale companion of my lost childhood, no one but a fusty, busty old nursemaid, very simple-minded, very simple, the salt of common sense, her red hair gleaming to show that quick temper she always had, that impatience with which she would dismiss all shades and phantoms, even herself should she become one, for self-pity was not her meat, not her drink.” Vera does not say Miss MacIntosh was simple-minded but honest, ranking one thing under another. Simple-minded, the salt of common sense, the red hair, the temper, the scorn for phantoms: each is set down beside the others, and each one stays.

This is more than a habit of style. It is how the novel treats its people. Miss MacIntosh's plain facts and Catherine's opium visions get the same weight in the sentence. Neither is marked as the real one. The question marks, nearly 4,000 of them, do the same work from another side, holding each claim open so that none of them hardens into the one fact everyone else must obey (see Nearly Four Thousand Questions).

Where Young Learned It

Young had studied how sentences are built. As the Preface tells, she read with Ronald S. Crane at the University of Chicago, and her 1936 master's thesis there was on the birds and beasts of John Lyly's Euphues. Lyly's prose moves by pairs and parallels, one comparison from nature set beside another. Lyly's comparisons close on a moral. Young kept the habit of setting things side by side and let go of the closing moral. Her “and” does not lead to a lesson. It leads to one more thing.

Everything Flowing

A sentence made of “and” lets things touch, and once they touch, they can change into each other. In Chapter 47, Mr. Spitzer's memories are “subterranean memories seldom coming to the surface of life, all images being blurred and all forms flowing into each other like fishes turning into flowers, flowers turning into keyholes, flowers turning into birds, all walls being permeable as sands through which the waters flowed.” The same sentence that does not rank also does not wall things off. Fish, flower, keyhole, bird: each turns into the next, and the walls between them are as thin as sand with the water running through.

That is why so much of the novel is water and fog. The sea, the mist, and the long sentence work the same way. Each one lets the edges go soft. Each one carries everything along together, and none of them will hold still long enough to be sorted.

Esther in the Same Sentence as the Sea

The clearest case is Esther Longtree. A doctor or a social worker of the 1940s might have had a file ready for her: the waitress who is always pregnant, whose children are always stillborn, who tells her story to strangers. Young will not let the sentence turn her into a case. In Chapter 72, the men who fathered her children “came as mourners when they came to her. They came to be forgiven by the milky breakers, the generous luminosity, the unforgiven mother, Esther Longtree, great heart beating like a thousand stars.”

Esther is set in the same line as the sea, the light, and the stars. She is not a departure from nature. She is part of it. A file needs a single label, and this sentence will not give one. The grammar has made her too large to be reduced.

What the Sentence Protects

That is the quiet politics of the “and.” Anyone who wants to rule over people needs them sorted: this one belongs and that one does not, this one is real and that one is dreaming, this one is well and that one needs correcting. Miss MacIntosh, for all her love, did a small version of this to Vera, treating the child's dreams as a fault to be fixed. A person who knows she is many things at once, grief and dream and daily work and family history, cannot be fully held by any one description of her. She is always more than the file. That extra is her protection.

The novel turns the same patience on its own past. In Chapter 18, remembering the night she saw Miss MacIntosh bald, Vera says: “Perhaps, in retrospection, every lost event must be changed by the illumination larger than that of any instant, larger than life.” No memory stays fixed. Everything that came after keeps adding to it. And in Chapter 4: “Yet years after she was gone, I had found her ivory knitting needles in the sand.” Not the woman. Not an answer. Only the needles, one more thing the sea gave back, set down beside everything else.

The Radical Immanence Reading

In the language of Radical Immanence, this essay is Pillar 9 (Language Shapes What's Possible): what a sentence lets in is what a mind can hold, and Young's “and” lets everything in. It is also Pillar 1 (Not One Self): a list without ranking is the shape of a self made of many parts, none of them the boss of the others. And it is Pillar 7 (The Margins Reveal the Truth): the waitress at the lunch counter shares her sentence with the breakers and the stars.
