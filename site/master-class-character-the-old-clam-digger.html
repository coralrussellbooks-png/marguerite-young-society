The Old Clam Digger: The Witness the Gulls Mistook for a Rock

← Jock Cartwheel · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Moses Hunnecker →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

The reader meets him the way the seagulls do, by mistaking him for part of the beach. He is Mr. Spitzer's servant, a grey, close-mouthed man out on the salt flats before dawn with his clam buckets and a torn tennis net he drags through the tide pools as if fishing for lost balls, and you would take him for a rock or a heap of eel grass, which is exactly what the birds do because being taken for scenery, being beneath all notice, is the position from which he sees everything.

He is Mr. Spitzer's valet and houseman, a former bookie trained in silence upon the racetrack, a man who knows his master's habits as well as he knows the clam's. He is a hopeless keeper of a house and a faultless keeper of clams, whose economy, the book says, is highly organized, and he goes out at dawn and by starlight to dig them up and to listen to their dying voices. He plucks the broken glass of a fallen chandelier from behind Spitzer's ears without a word. And he digs holes. He digs holes up and down the beach and climbs down into them, buries himself in the sand with only a small opening left for his mouth and nose, sleeps in a hollowed half-shell that rocks like a cradle and would do just as well for a coffin, so that Spitzer, walking at night, is afraid of stepping on him, and the gannets and the herring gulls come down and land on him, taking him for moss or a moving rock. He seemed more native to that shore, the book says, than Mr. Spitzer with his cane and his cape.

Ask the wrong question and he is there for atmosphere, a bit of gothic set-dressing behind the elegant lawyer. Ask the right one and he is the book's plainest bet and its riskiest. Radical Immanence holds that the truth is not at the shining center but at the margin, in the people nobody watches, and the clam-digger is that claim carried to its limit. He is the lowest figure in the whole novel, a mute servant the birds mistake for the ground. The question the book asks through him is whether the truth really does live down there, in the person beneath notice, or whether that is only a consoling idea. His answer is yes, and it is total.

Two things at the end turn the set-dressing into the point. The first is that he knew. On the day of Catherine's funeral, in heavy rain, driving the rickety Packard, he hears Mr. Spitzer confess at last that he is Peron and not Joachim, the largest secret in the book, the thing the whole confession is built toward, and he shows no surprise. He had known all along, or would not have been surprised either way, and he had said nothing for all those years. The secret had been riding in the front seat the entire time, kept by the one man no one thought to look at. The second is that when the great storm comes and only Spitzer survives it, the clam-digger drowned, Spitzer's grief for him runs greater than his grief for Catherine, greater than for anyone, a grief that rises into rage as he stamps his cane on the Boston sidewalks and the traffic lights seem to lower. The book, which uses its length to tell a reader where to look, spends its tenderest late pages not on the famous beauty or the dead lovers but on a drowned servant who dug clams and must, Spitzer says, be counted among his grievous losses. That is the argument, made in the placement itself. The truth was at the margin, exactly where the book kept promising it would be.

And he holds it in the one way the center never can, in silence. He is clam-mouthed, trained to say nothing, and his silence is not emptiness but the shape his knowing takes. Spitzer's whole tragedy is that he cannot stop talking, cannot take the short line to anything, circles and defers and confesses too late for it to land. The clam-digger is his opposite and his completion, the man who carries the same secret and simply keeps it closed, like the clam whose dying voices he rows out to hear. Where language fails Spitzer, holding him forever short of himself, silence serves the clam-digger perfectly. He never has to say that he knows. He knows the way the shore knows the tide.

His burying is the other half of it. A man who spends his nights climbing into graves he dug for himself and sleeping in them, breathing through a hole in the sand, is rehearsing death every night and refusing it every night, and neither the book nor Spitzer can quite say whether the old fellow is fully alive at all. Spitzer half believes he pulled him out of a doorway that was really a tomb and kept his body going by some unusual bargain while the spirit slipped away, so that the clam-digger became a preserved shell with the man gone out of it, a living corpse combing the beach. He held up a dark mirror, the book says, to Spitzer's own illness. He is the crossing point between the living and the dead worn by a servant instead of a gentleman. The two of them are one condition at two heights, the man in the cape who cannot say whether he is himself or his brother, and the man in the sand who cannot be called clearly alive or clearly dead. The margin does not merely hold the truth about the center. It holds the same truth, stripped of the cape.

The lesson for a writer is about where to hide what matters. Young could have handed the book's deepest secret to a major figure and let it go off at the center, in full light. Instead she gave it to the one person the reader was trained to skip, the servant taken for a rock, and let him keep it in silence until the last pages, so that learning he knew, a reader also learned how much is missed by looking where the book told her to look. To place your truth at the margin is to make the reader complicit in overlooking it, and then to turn the overlooking into the lesson. The clam-digger is beneath notice on purpose. He is proof that the person never watched was holding everything, and that when the grief finally comes, it comes hardest of all for him.
