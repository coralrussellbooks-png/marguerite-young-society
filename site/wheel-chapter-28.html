Research notes: Chapter 28 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 28

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

Mr. Spitzer is perhaps the most literal embodiment of Not One Self possible in fiction; he is a twin whose identity has become permanently entangled with his dead brother Peron. The text makes this explicit when it describes how his brother's death "had divided and sub-divided his own rational soul almost to the point of lunacy". He cannot answer the most basic ontological question: "How should he know the actual man he was?". He is not one person. He is the living remainder of a divided unit, haunting himself. His selfhood is structurally incomplete because it was always constituted by its double. Not One Self here is not metaphor; it is the literal architecture of the character.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter exists inside a layered hall of mirrors. Mr. Spitzer visits a woman, the narrator's mother, who lives entirely inside narcotic hallucinations: "a bearded Russian bishop or an iridescent lighthouse keeper, perhaps the Antarctic Queen Maud Mountains with her snow-capped mountain peaks and white umbrellas". What is remarkable is that the text does not position her visions as inferior to Mr. Spitzer's perception. He himself wonders whether he might be "among my mother's irresponsible hallucinations, only another of the luminous and metamorphic dead she entertained". The text refuses to establish a stable ground of the real. The mother sees things invisible made visible. Mr. Spitzer sees butterflies that are "almost incorporeal". Neither perception is privileged. There is no sunlit world outside this drugged room, only a larger, more elaborately furnished cave.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

This is perhaps the chapter's most sustained philosophical commitment. Grief here is not an event but a climate. Mr. Spitzer stopped collecting butterflies "on the day of Peron's devastating funeral", and that cessation was total: "many other things had come to an end on the day of Peron's devastating funeral, including, of course, his own capacious heart, the living heart, his way of life or most of it, the important part". Years have passed. Nothing has resolved. He still comes to this room. He still remembers the butterflies "though for years quiescent in his mind". The grief does not move through stages. It accumulates beneath everything he does. His evenings here are not healing; they are the ongoing weather of loss, the only climate he now inhabits.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Mr. Spitzer's entire existence in this chapter is an act of witnessing. He comes each evening "as to a brilliant star" to sit by the bedside of a woman lost in hallucination, a woman who denies and turns him away, and yet he returns. He does not fix her. He does not conclude anything about her. He simply stays in the room. The text describes his renewal coming "perhaps by my mother's obtuse faith that he would always return": his faithfulness is not productive, not therapeutic, not goal-directed. It is pure attendance. Attention as practice. The refusal to simplify is enacted structurally: the prose itself refuses to resolve, piling image upon image, clause upon clause, never arriving at a summary judgment about what any of this means.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Mr. Spitzer returns every evening to the same room where "no chair had changed its place for years". This is recursive construction made spatial. He rebuilds the same evening, provisionally, knowing it will not hold, knowing he will be "denied and turned away", and yet returning to begin again. His butterfly memories follow the same pattern: "quiescent in his mind" for years, then suddenly returning, rebuilt from the same materials. He does not add to the collection. He simply remembers it. The building is never finished. The return is the practice.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The prose style itself enacts this pillar. These are among the longest, most syntactically unresolved sentences in literary fiction: subordinate clauses breeding subordinate clauses, lists that do not conclude, images that transform into other images mid-sentence. The chapter never arrives. It is the ongoing sentence, not the concluded one. And thematically, Mr. Spitzer's life is explicitly described as "problematical": his living, his dying, his identity, all of it unresolved: "it was also problematical, that spindle of creative flame which is said to sleep in the eternal darkness, weaving our bodies, weaving our separate souls". The demand for completion is refused at every level: stylistic, thematic, ontological.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Every figure in this chapter occupies the margins of legible categories. Mr. Spitzer is a twin without a twin: a category that has lost its defining term. His mother exists at the border of consciousness and unconsciousness, reality and hallucination. The dead brother Peron haunts the living without being present. The butterflies Mr. Spitzer collected exist at the border of flower and insect, life and death: "the butterfly perching upon the flower had seemed as identical to the flower as twin to twin, and the flower had seemed to fly away, and the winged petals had remained". These are not exceptions to the order of things. They are the evidence that the order of things is constructed. The marginal is the site of revelation.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This pillar is perhaps most explicitly theorized in the chapter's own language. The past does not recede for Mr. Spitzer; it accumulates. His dead brother is remembered "with an increasing vivacity when he had forgotten himself": the brother grows more present as the self grows dimmer. The room itself is sedimentary: unchanged chairs, old mirrors, candle flames, bellropes, all of it "ethereal gleamings of gold and silver and watered silk" preserved from another era. The mother's hallucinations are themselves sedimentary: old waltzes, old bishops, old landscapes pressing up through her drugged consciousness. The past is not behind Mr. Spitzer. It is beneath him, constituting the ground he stands on, pressing upward through every evening he spends in this room.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The language of this chapter is conspicuously, almost aggressively, constructed. It does not pretend to transparency. The lists of the mother's hallucinations ("the headless horseman fording a brook, the flowers turning into serpents", "the pagodas moving on the waves, the swan weathercocks lifting their wings") are not descriptions of a reality but constructions of an interior. The prose announces its own artifice. The butterfly descriptions, "brimstone-colored butterflies burning like torpid flames in the wind, bejeweled butterflies as precious to him as the most precious jewels", are language making reality, not reporting it. The chapter's style is itself an argument: that what we call experience is always already shaped by the language we use to hold it.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

There is no beyond in this chapter. No afterlife is affirmed. Mr. Spitzer knows "the dead did not return except in our thoughts". His brother "would not be born again". And yet the sacred is absolutely present, in the candle flames reflected in dark Venetian mirrors, in the gossamer wings of butterflies reflecting prismatic lights, in the booming bells like the loud sea bittern, in the moment of the butterfly's birth and death which are indistinguishable. The transcendence is entirely immanent; it lives inside the drugged room, inside the ephemeral wings, inside the ambiguous moment when you cannot tell if what you are witnessing is beginning or end. "The manifestations of birth and death were the same in this realm". The sacred does not point outward. It points deeper in.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Mr. Spitzer is, structurally, a crossing point. He is where his dead brother's time and his own living time intersect. He carries Peron forward, not by choice but by constitution. "His faithfulness drew him each evening here", not will, not decision, but structural obligation. He is made of what was deposited before him: his twin's life, his mother's faith, the unchanged room, the accumulated butterfly memories. And he is, without knowing it, constituting the ground the narrator stands on; this chapter is being narrated by the child of the woman he visits, meaning his faithful presence has shaped the consciousness that is now telling his story. The ancestors are in the sediment. He is both the ancestor and the sediment. The carrying continues, whether or not he understands himself to be carrying anything.
