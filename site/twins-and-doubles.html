Twins and Doubles

Definition from research

Chapter 1 · The Double-Headed Man

  • A mysterious figure by the roadside symbolizing fragmented identity or duality.

Chapter 2 · Two Sister Ravens

  • The ravens appear symbolically and thematically as mysterious, almost otherworldly figures that contribute to the surreal, dreamlike atmosphere surrounding Catherine and her suspended existence between life and death.
  • The ravens are described as "two sister ravens who had created the universe" This description situates the ravens not merely as birds but as powerful, almost divine entities involved in cosmic creation and the shaping of reality itself.
  • Their role emphasizes Catherine’s immersion in a realm where boundaries between reality and illusion, life and death, are blurred.
  • The ravens, linked with creation, suggest a world that is constantly in flux, shaped by mysterious forces beyond ordinary human perception.
  • The ravens contribute to Catherine’s perception of her own life as “more monstrous than hers and terribly extra-human,” reflecting her alienation from the normal flow of time and human relations.
  • Their presence as mythic creators contrasts with Catherine’s physical stasis and withdrawal, underlining the tension between dynamic cosmic forces and her horizontal, dream-bound existence.
  • Ravens traditionally symbolize mystery, death, and prophecy in literature and myth, fitting well with Catherine’s liminal state caught between life and death, and her visions of phantoms, spirits, and the dead.
  • Their “sister” relationship may also suggest duality and companionship in a lonely, spectral world where Catherine resides, further personifying the enigmatic and haunting nature of her dreams.

Chapter 3 · Two Pennies

  • Found in her purse, symbolizes her humility and the little she possessed.
  • Also a poignant reminder of her mortality and disappearance.

Chapter 10 · Twin Iron Bedsteads

  • Represent memory, duality, and the coexistence of past and present selves (Mr. Spitzer and his deceased brother).

Chapter 12

  • “perhaps with two Mr. Spitzers, one who had died, one who had lived”: Joachim and Peron Spitzer, the twins.
  • “She had not two faces”: Janus, the Roman god of doorways and beginnings, looks two ways at once.

Chapter 13

  • “his brother had not been buried anywhere on this vast, amorphous estate”: Peron Spitzer, Joachim’s twin, lies elsewhere and is not among Catherine’s dead guests.

Chapter 14

  • “their cosmic union in the womb of time”: “There are many events in the womb of time which will be delivered” (Othello 1.3).
  • “the case of Spitzer versus Spitzer”: A case in which he “must appear as both the defender and the accuser.”

Chapter 25

  • “In the still chambers which had never bisected life from death, its twin”: In Hesiod’s Theogony, Sleep and Death are twin brothers, the sons of Night.
  • “he being the ambiguous twin”: Joachim and Peron (Jock Cartwheel) Spitzer may refer to Castor and Pollux, also known as the Dioscuri, twin brothers in Greek and Roman mythology. They are depicted as demigods, associated with horses, sports, and considered patrons of travelers, especially sailors. The constellation Gemini, the twins, in the sky is named after them. When mortal Castor died, Pollux shared his immortality with him, and the brothers took turns between Hades and Olympus, one dead while the other lived.

Chapter 25 · Life and Death, Liminality

  • Mr. Spitzer exists in a state between life and death, embodying the ambiguity of human existence.
  • The chapter explores death as both a physical end and a metaphysical continuum.
  • The interplay between living and dead, memory and oblivion, is central.

Chapter 25 · Identity and Duality

  • The twin brothers symbolize duality: life/death, faith/faithlessness, decisiveness/hesitation.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s identity is fractured, as he carries both his own life and that of his dead brother.
  • The confusion over who he is and who he might become reflects existential uncertainty.

Chapter 25 · The Twin Iron Beds and Golden Cradles

  • Symbolize the intertwined destinies of Mr. Spitzer and his brother.
  • Represent infancy, death, and the passage of time.

Chapter 26 · Duality of Human Nature

  • Through the contrast between Joachim and his twin brother Peron, the duality of restraint versus impulsiveness, order versus chaos, and life versus death is examined.

Chapter 26 · The Dead Brother

  • A symbol of lost potential, memory, and the haunting presence of the past in the present.

Chapter 27

  • “they had always suffered, except for this final accident, the same accidents, one not falling unless the other fell”: The twins, like Castor and Pollux in Chapter 25.

Chapter 28

  • “the butterfly perching upon the flower had seemed as identical to the flower as twin to twin”: Zhuangzi (c. 300 BC) dreamed he was a butterfly, and woke to write: “Now I do not know whether I was then a man dreaming I was a butterfly, or whether I am now a butterfly, dreaming I am a man” (trans. Herbert Giles, 1889).

Chapter 28 · Contrast Between Mr. Spitzer and His Brother Peron

  • Peron is portrayed as a man focused on the immediate, material world, indifferent to spiritual or afterlife concepts, and unconcerned with the delicate beauty Mr. Spitzer values.
  • Peron’s death, “by accident or suicide,” marks a turning point for Mr. Spitzer, leading to the end of many of his pursuits and a deepening of his introspection.

Chapter 29

  • “the similitude of death, its twin brother”: Sleep and Death are twins in Hesiod (Chapter 25), and sleep is “death’s counterfeit” in Macbeth 2.3.
  • “Io on one wing and Io on the other wing as if they knew they had two souls”: Io was one of the mortal lovers of Zeus in Greek mythology.

Chapter 29 · Multiplicity of the Soul

  • The idea of multiple souls or a divided self, reflecting Mr. Spitzer’s sense of identity and the presence of his brother’s spirit within him.

Chapter 33

  • “even her funeral, as he remembered his”: Mr. Spitzer remembers his own funeral because he buried his twin. Young said, “Grief was so great that he thought the wrong brother died” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).

Chapter 36

  • “if there had been no dead brother with his identical face”: The chapter portrays a triangular relationship between Peron, Joachim, and Catherine, marked by unfulfilled love, contrasting lives, and the tragic consequences of loss. Catherine’s preference for Peron and her rejection of Joachim create a dynamic of longing and sorrow that defines the emotional core of the narrative. Joachim embodies steadfastness and grief, while Peron represents passion and escape, and Catherine is caught between these polarities, her own existence fragile and dreamlike.

Chapter 36 · Rivalry and Envy

  • “His envy had never died, having outlived its subject or its object.”
  • Without a dead brother “with his identical face,” he “would have been a rival to himself and still unsatisfied.”
  • “He was this obstacle, this darkness between my mother and her love, her dead love, it little mattering who that dead love might be.”

Chapter 48

  • “the portmanteau personality carrying another soul within his soul”: Portmanteau is a large trunk or suitcase opening into two halves, or a word blending the sounds and combining the meanings of two others, a sense Lewis Carroll gave it: “You see it’s like a portmanteau—there are two meanings packed up into one word” (Through the Looking-Glass, 1871, chapter 6).

Chapter 50

  • “There was no twin star to this”: The twin star is the lost brother; see Peron, who “would not live” beyond Mr. Spitzer.

Chapter 51

  • “Had he given birth to his dead twin”: See Peron Spitzer.

Chapter 53

  • “the one chance in a million”: Young told Studs Terkel, “Mr. Spitzer is really two people. There are two Mr. Spitzers. One is a musical composer who has lived in memory of his gambling brother, the suicide, and who since his brother’s death has never composed a music which can be heard.” Peron “is a gay gambler, an impresario in the world of games and sports,” who describes himself “as the gambler who took one chance in a million, committed suicide, and lived.” In the third draft she realized “that it was not just a case of twins, but the two parts of the same man… a beautiful case of duality, which might be in all of us, like left and right, shadow and sunlight” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).

Chapter 53 · Identity and Duality

  • The chapter explores the paradox of the two brothers’ overlapping identities. Joachim as the “pale replica” of Peron, and Peron as an elusive, many-personality figure. The idea that Joachim might be Peron and vice versa underlines the instability of self and questions the fixity of identity.

Chapter 55 · The Twin House and Mirroring Rooms

  • Symbolize the divided self and fractured identity; also the attempt to impose order on life’s chaos.

Chapter 57 · Identity and the Double

  • Mr. Spitzer’s identity is fluid and fragmented. He sometimes confuses himself with his brother, dreams of being the tailor, or living a double existence. His struggle to reconcile these selves reflects the complexity of human identity, especially after trauma or death.

Chapter 57 · Timepieces (Two Wrist Watches)

  • Represent duality and the conflicting measures of time, cosmic and earthly, highlighting Mr. Spitzer’s fractured perception of time.

Chapter 59 · Skeleton and Bones

  • The alien skeleton inhabiting Mr. Spitzer’s body symbolizes the uncertainty of identity and the fracturing of the self.
  • His bones creaking and speaking imply the body’s betrayal or the intrusion of something foreign within him.
  • This is also foreshadowing of his true identity.

Chapter 62

  • “his brother’s milky blue stone ring and ticking jeweled wrist watch”: The right hand that signs the lawyer’s will wears Peron’s ring and Peron’s ticking watch, while the left hand signs as the musician; the twins divide Mr. Spitzer’s body between them.
