Arc 4: Cousin Hannah (Chapters 30–52)

← Mr. Spitzer · Story Arcs Home · Mr. Spitzer's Grief →

Overview

Catherine's memories summon Cousin Hannah, the suffrage captain, and with her Catherine's own spectral marriage and the architecture of her opium mind. The arc then moves to Hannah's deathbed and its long aftermath, as Mr. Spitzer, her executor, pieces together a hidden life, finds the trunks of wedding gowns, and keeps her secret. Volume One ends at Chapter 50.

Central figures: Joachim Spitzer · Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Vera Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer

Chapter by chapter

Chapter 30

Catherine and Mr. Spitzer discuss unreal persons as if they were real, especially Eustace, an invisible visitor who takes many shapes. Then Cousin Hannah Freemount-Snowden enters through memory. She was a Boston suffragette, archer, duelist, climber, and traveler who crossed deserts disguised as an Arab king, and she seemed to foresee assassinations. She is now dead and buried. She never cared for the Spitzer twins. Catherine longs for her company and imagines sending her to search the Alps for Jock, Catherine's husband, who vanished while mountain climbing.

Chapter 31

Catherine and Mr. Spitzer admire Cousin Hannah, whose great deeds made their own lives seem unfulfilled. Her secret grief is circled but never named: the mirrors of her house draped in black, her failed battle to rescue Catherine from opium, her indifference to both Spitzer brothers. Mr. Spitzer remembers his long faithfulness to her, knocking at a door that never opened. He struggles to write her epitaph, drafting and discarding version after version.

Chapter 32

Catherine remembers Hannah as a storm of a woman with a saber scar and burning eyes, who tried to break up Catherine's wedding by beating the bridegroom with her white umbrella and carrying the bride away. Catherine's marriage itself is spectral: a faceless bridegroom, a wedding to a sailboat, a porpoise, a star, a church bell. Hannah died old and alone and never married, though Catherine believes she still visits. Catherine always wanted to die while traveling, believing that death comes from partial knowledge.

Chapter 33

A Boston newspaper once referred to Catherine as "the late Mrs. Catherine Cartwheel," and Mr. Spitzer never told her. The same paper reported Peron seen walking on Benevolent Street, and Mr. Spitzer walked there the next evening. Catherine cannot remember her wedding or her groom, and sometimes thinks she married Death in her sleep. Mr. Spitzer decides that her dreaming is no farther from reality than the routines of people who punch clocks. The chapter ends with him rising to open a door she has asked a human-sized blackbird to open.

Chapter 34

Catherine recalls being married both in white and in black, with a dog reciting the ceremony in Latin and a St. Bernard giving her the last sacrament. Then she remembers Hannah's presence in her room: iron keys at her belt, a metallic energy, sharp wit, contempt for mortal men, and total indifference to Mr. Spitzer. In Hannah's company Catherine felt both flirtatious and guilty, sensing a love she could not name. Hannah always predicted that Joachim would fail Catherine at the crucial moment, as Peron had by his suicide.

Chapter 35

Catherine imagines inviting Hannah back for a winter of card games. She reviews Hannah's suffrage career on every front, her conquests and her refusal of any memorial window, and all the things Hannah was not: no dancer, no hostess, no keeper of private confessions. She concludes that Hannah was married to the suffrage movement as if it were her husband. Mr. Spitzer notes Catherine's habit of always taking the opposite view.

Chapter 36

Catherine wants to know exactly where Hannah is now. Since she lives among both the living and the dead, she sees no reason she should not be told. She blames Mr. Spitzer, the last person to see Hannah alive, for his useless vagueness. Roused for once, he accuses Catherine of never having loved any living being. Catherine wonders whether Hannah is a man or a woman on the other side, and suspects she would turn heaven to chaos within a week.

Chapter 37

Vera describes the world as Catherine experiences it: drifting mirrors, ceilings that become floors, clocks striking impossible hours. She asks who the dreamer is. Catherine declares that order is a madman's dream. She still schemes to bring Hannah back, but Hannah will not return. Death has throttled her, and silence follows.

Chapter 38

Catherine's memories of Hannah are vague and clouded by her dreams of an immortal love. She has reinterpreted Peron's neglect as a devotion too large to express. We learn that Catherine never believed in women's suffrage, the cause of the woman who tried hardest to save her. Mr. Spitzer adds his anecdotes, and Hannah appears in memory with keys and jeweled sword. Vera names the house for what it has become: a loony bin by the sea.

Chapter 39

Ironically, Hannah the rationalist became for Catherine a figure of dead romance. Vera maps her mother's mind as an infinite house, visited by figures such as Lord Murdock Burdock Thrushmorton and Mr. Res Tacamah. Catherine admits that opium has poisoned her pleasures at their source. Vera describes their meeting as "the perpendicular pronoun I" coming to see "my horizontal mother." The chapter ends asking whether God, too, dreams opium dreams, and whether dream and reality are the same.

Chapter 40

Catherine remembers Hannah's voice, a husky baritone like iron bells, which seemed to speak every language of the living and the dead. In Catherine's mind time runs backward and sideways, her many selves wander the house, and the living and the dead mingle freely. Mr. Spitzer watches from the edge of the room. The chapter closes with Catherine's belief that a ship's figurehead knows more than its captain, because it always goes first.

Chapter 41

Catherine believes only a hair's breadth separates life from death. Her selves leave her body and may not return, furniture drifts, and windows face different seas. She imagines herself an engineer, a builder of cities, a traveler across deserts, yet she cannot walk. Mr. Spitzer complains that she never saw him as a unique person, and so he never learned who he was. Catherine does not envy Hannah's single, steady purpose.

Chapter 42

Catherine rides in a glass carriage behind four alabaster horses, driven by the black coachman, through the sleeping house toward Hannah's deathbed. Mr. Spitzer, a keeper of lists of lost ships, keeps vigil beside the dying suffragette. He finds her crippled, wrapped in an old army coat, sobbing and delirious in a white tent of a room. He wonders what her battles truly won, and she cries out to be hidden from the eyes of men.

Chapter 43

In silk hat and black cape, Mr. Spitzer keeps watch, unsure whether he himself is living or dead. Hannah's dying becomes a dance of death. She raves through deserts, mountains, and snow, speaks to Caesar, a blacksmith, and a figure she calls her love, asks for a mirror, and longs for silk skirts and parasols she scorned all her life. She asks whether she is the anvil or the hammer, and at the end she cries out for a lost beloved under the snow.

Chapter 44

Over the years, Mr. Spitzer pieces together fragments of Hannah's hidden life from her papers: letters, unreadable languages, evidence of a double existence. He recalls her deathbed talk of skirts and petticoats, strange from the great champion of bloomers, and her cry that false modesty killed her love. He wonders whether heroes are driven by a secret cowardice, and whether those who never loved know most of love.

Chapter 45

Reading Hannah's faded letters by candlelight, Mr. Spitzer vows to keep her great secret until long after her death. He sees that her public and private lives cannot be fitted into one portrait. He recalls her asking whether she was always falling, and her sense that she aspired higher and so fell farther. He considers what her grave should say, and how public memory turns the greatest lives into monuments and loses the secret heart.

Chapter 46

Mr. Spitzer realizes that Hannah was also a secret mountain climber, of peaks real and of the mind. As her executor he takes her alpenhorn for his collection of instruments. He wanders a fog-bound city asking strangers whether they have seen her, and people take him for mad. A steeplejack answers him in paradoxes. The chapter ends with Hannah's last cry to the bright star of her love.

Chapter 47

Mr. Spitzer remembers Hannah's last words about the mountains moving, and lays her skirt over her body. He wonders whether her love was that skirt, covering her wound. He reflects on what he owes his twin, on materialism and spirit, on everything in the world seeming conscious, and on the rejected possibility, the life that was never lived. He decides it is better that lost things sink whole than drift scattered.

Chapter 48

In Hannah's house of whispering sounds and keys that fit no lock, Mr. Spitzer performs his duties as a messenger of the dead. He contrasts Peron, who burned brightly and lit matches, with himself, who always feared catching fire. He imagines himself a lamplighter of the stars and is jealous of anyone who might take his place. He sees life as a long rehearsal for the silence.

Chapter 49

In the longest of his meditations, Mr. Spitzer moves through a frozen cosmos of the mind. He mourns Martha, the last passenger pigeon, who died at the Cincinnati Zoo in 1918, weeping for others who did not weep for him. He lists the contradictions of his existence, dying through life and living through death. He wonders whether he is one man or two, bound so closely to his brother that he cannot tell which he is.

Chapter 50

The universe seems off key to Mr. Spitzer: two golden chairs where there should be one, his life a dream dreamed by someone else, himself a blank page between testaments. He considers what he absorbed from Catherine and concludes that "all that happened happened only once." He thinks of Hannah's hyphenated name, Freemount-Snowden, and how the hyphen joining the two names mattered more to her than either name. He writes a nocturne for her. Volume One ends.

Chapter 51

A mouse has gotten into Mr. Spitzer's papers, eating his chords and nesting in old lawsuits. Mr. Spitzer catalogs the selves he might be: the trumpeter or the trumpeter's cry, a conductor, a lamplighter, a lawyer to starfish, a man who has stamped documents with the wrong seals for years. In his wanderings he meets people lost in their own illusions, such as a woman who believes she is a birdcage. He concludes that every dream of life must leave something unrealized, or all would be complete.

Chapter 52

Going through Hannah's estate, Mr. Spitzer opens trunk after trunk of wedding gowns, with no bride's slippers among them. He finds letters in languages he cannot read, her lost skirt embroidered with forget-me-nots, and evidence that she lived as Hannah in Boston and as Hamid in the East. Her book lies open to the tale of Tristram and Iseult. He resolves to keep her secret. He closes her eyes and weights them with two pennies, one for the sun and one for the moon.
