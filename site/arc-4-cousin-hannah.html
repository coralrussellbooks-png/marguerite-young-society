Arc 4: Cousin Hannah (Chapters 30–52)

Part of Story Arcs, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated November 12, 2026

← Mr. Spitzer · Story Arcs Home · Mr. Spitzer's Grief →

Spoiler note: This page covers Chapters 30 to 52. If you are reading along, read it after you finish Chapter 52.

Overview

Catherine's memories summon Cousin Hannah, the suffrage captain, and with her Catherine's own spectral marriage and the architecture of her opium mind. The arc then moves to Hannah's deathbed and its long aftermath, as Mr. Spitzer, her executor, pieces together a hidden life, finds the trunks of wedding gowns, and keeps her secret.

Central figures: Joachim Spitzer · Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Vera Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer

Chapter by chapter

Chapter 30

Catherine and Mr. Spitzer discuss unreal persons as if they were real, especially Eustace, an invisible visitor who takes many shapes. Then Cousin Hannah Freemount-Snowden enters through memory. She was a Boston suffragette, archer, duelist, climber, and traveler who crossed deserts disguised as an Arab king, and she seemed to foresee assassinations. She is now dead and buried. She never cared for the Spitzer twins. Hannah once rode in Arab disguise against Spanish soldiers to defend a caravan of Riffs, and she was dedicated to “the overthrow of the dream and of the dreamer.” Catherine longs for her company and imagines sending her to search the Alps for Jock, Catherine’s husband, who vanished while mountain climbing.

Chapter 31

Catherine and Mr. Spitzer admire Cousin Hannah, whose great deeds made their own lives seem unfulfilled. Her secret grief is circled but never named: the mirrors of her house draped in black, her failed battle to rescue Catherine from opium, her indifference to both Spitzer brothers. She sent not one flower to Peron’s grave and ignored Joachim “as if it were he who had died.” Mr. Spitzer remembers his long faithfulness to her, knocking at a door that never opened. He struggles to write her epitaph, drafting and discarding version after version.

Chapter 32

Catherine remembers Cousin Hannah as a storm of a woman, a suffrage captain with a saber scar and burning eyes who rode through deserts and snowfields. She died not in battle but in bed, after years of frostbite and semi-invalidism, confessing that “the greatest battles were fought in the domestic wars by those who had never left home.” Catherine visited her deathbed only in a dream, and she knew the day Hannah died when a riderless white horse came through the window. Rumor said Hannah once left a suitor at the church door. At Catherine’s own wedding she beat the bridegroom with her white umbrella and tried to carry the bride away. Catherine had married a phantom whose face she never saw: “For weddings are funerals, and funerals are weddings.” She always wanted to die while travelling, for “Death is that which is caused by our partial knowledge.”

Chapter 33

A Boston newspaper once called Catherine “the late Mrs. Catherine Cartwheel,” widow of Jock, and Mr. Spitzer never told her. The same paper reported Peron seen on Benevolent Street, and Mr. Spitzer walked there the next evening. Catherine cannot reconstruct her wedding. Her bridegroom was a white camel, a church steeple, a key, the wind, perhaps Death while she slept. Mr. Spitzer cannot remember the wedding at all. He writes harp music to imitate the wind in a bridge’s cables, and day after day he asks the post office to trace a blank letter he mailed to himself years ago. He decides her dreaming is no farther from reality than the lives of those who punch time clocks: “They, too, could live only while they dreamed.” When she tells someone to open the door and a human-sized blackbird answers that it is already open, Mr. Spitzer gets up and opens it.

Chapter 34

Catherine remembers her wedding many ways: in white and in black, in a cathedral during a royal funeral, at home with only the black coachman as witness, as an elopement with the robber bridegroom pursued by Cossack horsemen. Most often she was married by a St. Bernard dog who spoke church Latin, gave her the Viaticum, and foretold that her husband would be lost between two frozen peaks. Cousin Hannah told her that marriage was “the death of a woman’s soul” and that no man had ever loved her. Catherine remembers Hannah’s visits: the iron keys jangling at her belt, her wit and metallic energy, her weird dance whenever a cock crowed, her contempt for Joachim. In her company Catherine felt “both very flirtatious and very guilty, trying to resist this man who was no man.” She longs to learn arithmetic and geometry so that she might know whether the great geometer of the universe “was sane or insane.” Hannah always predicted that Joachim would fail Catherine at the crucial moment, as Peron had by his apparent suicide.

Chapter 35

Catherine imagines Cousin Hannah returning from the dead for a long winter of cards in the empty house, with Mr. Spitzer banished so that he cannot remind her that he outlived her. Mr. Spitzer, who cannot tell a heart from a spade, has always believed Hannah a card cheat and death “itself a great deceiver.” Catherine reviews Hannah’s suffrage campaigns through deserts, emirates, and harems, the bribes she refused, the stained glass window she declined, and her lack of time for courtly graces, small talk, or the dance. Hannah could never have been made to confess that “she had yearned for marriage, or she had loved a man,” for the suffrage movement was “her awesome husband.” Catherine admires in Hannah the “splendid unity of character” she lacks in her own fragmented selves, and wonders who brought the faded rose Hannah clutched when she died in bed. Hannah once told of a eunuch who became a father and was strangled for love. The chapter ends on Catherine’s distrust of anything predictable and her habit, as Mr. Spitzer says, of taking the opposite view.

Chapter 36

Mr. Spitzer, roused for once from his lethargy, accuses Catherine of never having loved any living being. He lives “at this second remove from reality,” hearing every sound a minute late, a counterfeit of himself who fears that any change of habit would bring the skies down. He is conservative where she is radical, and she has never distinguished imagination from memory. Catherine’s mind is on Cousin Hannah, whom she would woo back from death for a winter’s visit, though Hannah never answers her invitations, which Catherine has probably not yet sent. She blames Mr. Spitzer, the last to see Hannah alive, for refusing to say where Hannah has gone, and suspects he is jealous of a returning spirit. She imagines Hannah’s travels from Moscow to Babylon and across Al Sirat into a Moslem paradise without women, and wonders whether Hannah has become a man or a woman. If a woman should be in heaven, she thinks, it would not be heaven long.

Chapter 37

Vera describes the heaven Catherine expects, as disordered as this world: drifting mirrors, ceilings that become floors, clocks striking inept hours. Catherine has always said that order is “a mad man’s dream,” and that Mr. Spitzer, “wishing to be agreeable, had made a false report as to what might be upon the other side.” Drugged and nearly inert, she cannot tell life from death, and asks who the dreamer is. She sees the reversals of dream: man becoming woman, God become dog, trees with their roots in the sky. Mr. Spitzer insists that Hannah is dead and buried, gone where there is no consciousness and no memory, but Catherine cannot admit her extinction. What she fears is a grey land where none makes reply, and she stays horizontal on her mother-of-pearl boat bed so as not to take that step. She recalls Hannah’s adventures in the harems, unveiling her bearded face to a sheik who went mad, and bowing to a sheik who proved to be a beautiful woman. The riderless horse has already come, and Hannah will not return.

Chapter 38

Catherine’s memories of Cousin Hannah are clouded by opium and by her dream of an immortal love, and Mr. Spitzer, being as one dead, must be prodded to remember anything at all. Catherine thought the lonely cause of suffrage unworthy of Hannah’s adventurous spirit and a waste of time. She would have been happy, she insists, if only Peron had lived, and she explains his neglect as a love too great to express: “This dead love which never was and never would be real was the one undying love.” Hannah had invited her to Egypt to show that a woman could be both beautiful and independent of man, and Catherine imagines the life she might have had, picketing Buckingham Palace, sleeping in silken tents, escaping from moated castles. She remembers Hannah’s sharp, masculine virtues with envy and guilt, wonders whom Hannah had loved, and is sorry that Hannah was not a knight or a Prince Charming come to wake her. Mr. Spitzer observes that most people are disoriented as to time, place, and person, and Vera calls the house what it has become, “this loony bin.”

Chapter 39

Vera reflects on the irony that Cousin Hannah, a rationalist in the avant-garde of suffrage, became for Catherine a figure of dead romance, a knight errant who could not be held to mortal law. Catherine, lonely and drugged, half believes she imagined Hannah’s visit: “It was as if the perpendicular pronoun I had come to see my horizontal mother.” Her “I” means many things. She is the chair, the lamp, Brooklyn Bridge, a great hostess and a recluse at once, travelling everywhere without leaving the house. Her soul is a house of endless rooms, and in every room someone is missing. She knows that opium has poisoned all pleasures at their source, and that knowledge of so many selves has only added to her torment. Her sanity, she believes, is simply knowing that she is insane, and she would not trade her beautiful false selves for one dull true one. She greets her phantoms, Lord Murdock Burdock Thrushmorton, who twitters like a cage of canaries, and Mr. Res Tacamah, the drug bottle with very large ears, and wonders whether God, too, is dreaming opium dreams, and whether dream and reality are the same.

Chapter 40

Catherine remembers Cousin Hannah’s voice, a husky baritone like iron bells, and believes Hannah taught her every language of the living and the dead, from Saracen and Provençal to the speech of seals and Platan rails, though Catherine herself had studied nothing but the French of a Peacock Alley menu. In her room she still converses with Alcibiades, Dante, Daniel Boone, and Darius, who answers the kitchen telephone. Her memories come without sequence, the echo before the song, the death before the life. She once greeted every stranger as a cousin and went to bed to escape the embarrassment, and now she introduces the living among the dead, to Mr. Spitzer’s patient wonder. She suspects that Hannah’s adventures, like her own, may have been dreams. She had no faith to lose, and she cannot say whether opium caused her somnolence or her somnolence the opium. She is many selves at once, the horse and the rider, and she believes that a ship’s figurehead knows more than its captain, because it always goes first.

Chapter 41

After death, Catherine still travels, and she insists that only a hair’s breadth lies between life and death. Her selves leave her body on errands in the void, and she counts her pearls, afraid that when she has counted them all she will be lost. She walks naked through warm snow among footprints that may be hers or a gopher’s, and she is lost for years inside her own ear, a continent of stairways, echoes, and grand pianos, crying, “Let me out of my ear!” The house moves in the night. She takes the night train from Boston to New York through the Alps and the Gobi, followed by brigands, and returns as if she had never gone. She believes she commands winds, tides, and stars, yet she cannot walk. Mr. Spitzer keeps his sorrow alive, and Catherine thinks him less tragic than Peron, who leapt to his death. She remembers that Hannah paid a price for her lack of sorrow, and now, with Hannah dead, she keeps up their rivalry alone. Things left by imaginary visitors, a purple feather fan, riding gloves, an emperor’s white umbrella, prove to her that someone came.

Chapter 42

Catherine dreams that she rides in her glass carriage behind the black coachman and his alabaster horses, through the sleeping house and the sleeping city, to bid farewell to the dying Cousin Hannah, though both ladies are bedridden and the visit can have happened only in a dream. Mr. Spitzer, who goes back and forth between the two dying ladies, is grateful for her dream. He keeps lists of lost ships, the Racehorse, the Arcturus, the Ptarmigan, and searches census rolls from which one person, he suspects himself, is always missing. In Hannah’s house of war trophies he finds pictures of her glory, the bloomer girl, the strike leader, the Arab king, and he learns that in the East she was Al Hamid, that she once left a bridegroom at the church door, and that a sheik sent her gold. He expects to find a secret man in her life and finds instead “a secret woman, a bride.” Hannah lies in a white room like a desert tent, her face covered and her feet bare. She does not know him, and she cries out for a pavilion to hide her from the storm.

Chapter 43

In silk hat and black cape, Mr. Spitzer keeps watch at Cousin Hannah’s deathbed, unsure whether he himself is living or dead. Hannah’s dying becomes a dance of death. She raves through deserts, mountains, and snow, asks whether the lid of the sky is raising or lowering, and speaks to the Emperor Tiberius, a blacksmith, a golden archer, and the Black Moor she calls her love, asking whether she is the anvil or the hammer. The suffrage captain who scorned feminine frivolity longs now for silk skirts, gold-fringed parasols, and her mirror, and Mr. Spitzer wonders whether she had always been “this pale Don Juan beneath her mask.” She searches for “my nymph, my lovely girl,” “my wild swan girl,” and Mr. Spitzer sees her going down like an old ship that no calker can mend. At the end she cries for “my skirt I lost so long ago” and asks, “should love be extinguished by death?”

Chapter 44

Over the years Mr. Spitzer pieces together the truth of Cousin Hannah’s last days. The great bloomer girl, who spent her life urging ladies to take off their skirts, called for her skirt as she died. When he brought her snowdrops, they melted in her hand, and from that moment she began to fade. In her delirium she relived a climb in the snowbound mountains: her love, “my lovely bride,” turned back to find her skirt, out of a modesty Hannah calls false since there was no one to see, and was lost in the snow. Hannah blamed herself for letting go, and Mr. Spitzer understands at last that this lost love drove her suffrage battles and her wanderings. He wonders whether great heroes are driven by secret cowardice, whether the burning moth is braver than the star, and whether suffrage was only her mask. In her papers he finds letters in languages no one can read, many like love letters to a great love, and he remembers a sea shell he once found, written over: “For all dead loves and all remembered things. I have travelled through many seas.” Perhaps he knows most of love who never loved.

Chapter 45

Reading by candlelight the letters strangers wrote to Cousin Hannah, asking about lost loves, dead brides, and vanished bridegrooms, Mr. Spitzer marvels that one who never loved should have been taken for an authority on love. From her last ravings he pieces together her secret. She had climbed the snowbound mountains with “her acolyte, her beautiful girl,” who went back over the peak to find her skirt and never returned. Hannah believed she had let go the girl’s hand and killed her love, and that guilt drove all her suffrage battles, yet at the end she cries that it was the girl who let go: “We were two lone women. There was no man.” She dies asking forgiveness, a sea shell roaring at her feet, and is buried in a grey New England churchyard. Mr. Spitzer resolves to keep her secret and wonders what image could mark her grave, perhaps a milestone reading “Twelve Miles.” He mourns how quickly the world forgets, thinking of the pigeons of Boston and Venice and the vanished passenger pigeons, and he is no longer sure whether he is Joachim or Peron.

Chapter 46

Mr. Spitzer comes to understand that Cousin Hannah had also been a secret mountain climber, perhaps the greatest ever known among ladies, of the Matterhorn, Mont Blanc, and mountains of consciousness, though she was ashamed of it and spoke of it only when dying. When she died, the room grew colder than the Arctic Alps. As her executor he takes her alpenhorn for his collection of instruments, and on the foggy beach he blows a few notes like a ghost calling his lost love. A ram steps out of the fog, and sheep follow him, and ever after lost men follow him through the grey cities. Her dying has brought him to reality and taught him the power of a dead love. He wanders the fog-bound city asking strangers whether they have seen her, and people take him for mad, until an old steeplejack lurches out of the fog and answers him in paradoxes: “I live because I am dead.” The chapter ends with Hannah’s last cry to the bright star of her love.

Chapter 47

Mr. Spitzer remembers Cousin Hannah’s last words, that the mountains moved, that her love lay white under the snow, and that if they could have had a child, “red as her blood, white as the snow,” this great battle never would have taken place. Walking the foggy shore, he thinks of what he owes his dead twin, who robbed death of its terror and will be a “Welcoming Committee of One” on the other side, and he wonders which brother he is. He keeps a count of all things and finds that something is always missing: “Among all things possible, there was the rejected possibility.” He dreams of music never to be played, fears marriage as he fears change, and is agonized by attributing consciousness to everything. He wonders whether Hannah was the bride or the bridegroom and whether her love was her skirt, for which her love had died in the mountains. Afraid to look on her dying face lest beauty return to it, he lays a skirt over her body, and as her attorney, closing her house before it is torn down, he decides it is better that lost things sink whole than drift scattered.

Chapter 48

Alone in Cousin Hannah’s empty house before dawn, Mr. Spitzer, a messenger in the world of the dead acting in his self-appointed legal office, sorts her papers and boxes and fumbles with the forty keys she kept in her bed, among which there is no master key. The lights have gone out, and he lights matches, though he has always been afraid of catching fire. He remembers Peron, who burned like the brightest star and lit match after match, tempting fate long before his apparent suicide. All things happen twice to Mr. Spitzer, perhaps even his death and his love, and he wonders whether one may live for years with a love who died long ago, as with a stranger. He cannot find the way out of the house of windows and doors, and he hopes the watchman will not take him for a thief. He wonders whether the lamplighter is also the one who puts out the light, as the evening star and the morning star are one, and is angry that anyone else might take his place. He doubts that he is a lawyer or a musician, imagines himself a stowaway asleep in a ship that went down long ago, and decides it is better not to know the unknowable. Noticing a lost silver button and his threadbare cape, he asks whether the boundary lines between life and death are fixed, or whether he might cross them backward as well as forward.

Chapter 49

In a frozen cosmos where ice covers the rivers, the boats, the archer, and the constellations, Mr. Spitzer lives when he is dead and wakes only when he sleeps. He weeps for others who do not weep for him, and above all for Martha, the last passenger pigeon, whose death he dates to September 14, 1918, “in a war-torn world” (she died September 1, 1914). He arrived too late to see her alive, rode all night on the train that carried her body to Washington, and begged the old caretaker of the Washington Museum to set a mirror before her so that she would not know she was alone; the caretaker refused, saying she had no soul and that it was the incapacity to be deceived which caused extinction. Mr. Spitzer would have indicted America before the Senate for what it had done to the pigeons and to the Indians, but he would have been threatened with the Federal pen. He remembers meeting an old sailor, the sole survivor of a shipwreck, and telling him that the greatest shipwreck is to sink when there is no storm. He comes to trust only the false, the blind, and the deaf, all who have lost, and walking through the rain among the pigeons he cannot tell whether he is one or two, or which brother he is.

Chapter 50

Everything seems off key and out of course to Mr. Spitzer, the universe tacking like the wild white snow bird and every room doubling, so that when he sees two golden chairs in the clouds he does not know which is the dream. He steps lightly, breathes softly, and pauses before every door lest it open on the void, for he believes he is living his life after death and may be the one dreamer who dreams not. It was not from the opium lady, “the invalid lady flourishing on the enchantment of nightshade,” who waits for him as for his dead self and mistakes life for death, that he learned that all that happened happened only once. Life is a moment’s fading luminosity, and he is not the Holy Writ but the flyleaf stuck between the testaments, ground between the zenith stone and the nadir stone, leaving no water-mark, no signature, no name. His little firefly is his key of gold and perhaps all there is. He walks like the tide, advancing as he retreats. He learned much from the old suffragette captain, for whom the hyphen in her name was more important than the two names it joined; she died before he came, and for her he wrote this nocturne.

Chapter 51

Puffing along the shore with his mantle growing around him, Mr. Spitzer cannot tell whether he is the conductor lifting his baton, the lamp-lighter, or the oarsman striking light from the waves of darkness. He is the doctor who has lost his patient, the lawyer who has lost his client, lawyer to a starfish, a dead coachman, and the moon in the cloud, and the old postman knocking at every door. He fears being right more than being wrong, removes the stones from a road and puts one back lest he change rivers in their course, and trusts the past more than the future. He signs illegal documents as if they were legal and stamps wills with the wrong seals, once with the seal of the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad, while Mr. Mouse gnaws his lawsuits and eats his chords. Respecting other people’s mirages because he is a mirage himself, he meets a lady whose twirling zodiac umbrella moves time, an old woman who thinks she is a bird cage, deaf music critics who hear every error, two pianists who play in perfect time only when a thousand miles apart, and an old woman who believed she was a reindeer and was found with a golden arrow through her breast. He lives by mourning the death of the deathless one, and he concludes that the dream of life, like that of death, must ever contain the unrealized, else all would be complete.

Chapter 52

Mr. Spitzer remembers closing the estate of the old suffragette, who reminds him of his own duplicity and duality. From old newspapers he learns that her wildest legends were true: she lost her lady on a mountain peak when the girl went back for a white skirt embroidered with forget-me-nots, challenged a prime minister to a duel, chained herself to a lamp-post, flew over the Alps in a plane with canvas wings until the Swiss drew up claims against her for the League of Nations, fought a golden eagle, and came back from the Holy Lands in black skirts and a turban. Searching her house by lamplight, he finds ten thousand shoe horns, an acorn in an iron safe, telephones that do not work, and forty trunks that open only after long trials with forty keys, with always one key or one lid left over. The trunks hold wedding gowns and veils, and no bride’s slippers. Believing she had wished to be the bride, he leaves the gowns out of his inventory to keep her secret. At dawn he finds she has turned a page of the book she was reading when she died, perhaps Eloise et Abelard, and he closes her eyes with two pennies, one in memory of the sun and one in memory of the moon.
