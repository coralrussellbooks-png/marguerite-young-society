I. The Philosophy: The Politics of the White Ground

We are trained to treat the ground as neutral: the primed white canvas, the rectangle, the level horizon, the frame, the clean edge where the picture stops. We act as if these are just the empty stage your creativity walks onto. Radical Immanence says they are not neutral at all. The white ground, the standard rectangle, the salable size, the tidy framed edge are habits built by industry and institutions, then handed down as if they were laws of nature.

What we call reality is mostly the version enough powerful people agreed to enforce and sell. Our language helps hold that version in place. It cuts the flowing world into solid, separate things, called nouns, that perform neat actions, called verbs. Painting obeys the same grammar. We say a portrait, a landscape, a still life, a picture, as if each were a fixed object. But the real world is not made of separate nouns. It is made of crossings, processes, and constant change.

When you work on a clean white ground toward a sealed, framed edge, you quietly agree that the world is tidy and settled. The studio can be a place to argue with that. By breaking the clean edge of the picture, you pull yourself and the viewer out of the enforced, sellable version of reality and into the fluid truth of direct experience.

II. Deep Journal Prompts

The Ground Inspection. List three painting nouns you take for granted, such as ground, composition, frame, subject, or finish. Now strip the noun away and rewrite each one as a verb or a demand, to expose what it actually does to you:

The white ground becomes the-blank-that-pretends-nothing-came-before-my-hand.

The composition becomes the-arrangement-that-keeps-the-eye-comfortable-and-the-picture-sellable.

The finish becomes the-sealed-skin-built-to-hide-where-the-struggle-was.

The Unnamed Friction. Name a feeling you get while painting that the art world has no clean word for: the small grief when a loose, alive first mark gets buried under a corrected one, the hollow feeling when a surface reads as too resolved, the strange shame of an accident that looked better than the plan. Describe its texture and its weight without solving it. Who benefits from keeping that feeling unnamed?

III. Studio Exercises

Dissolving the Picture

Take a small, familiar subject and paint it under one strict rule: nothing is allowed a clean edge, a settled composition, or a sealed border.

Phase 1. The flooded ground. Do not start on white. Tone the surface first with whatever is left on the palette, an ugly mixed grey, the scrapings of the last session, so the picture begins on ground that already has a past. Work with no plan. Let the light, the sound in the room, and your own uneven breath set the pace instead of a drawing.

Phase 2. The drifting mark. Let the marks wander. Let color run past the shape it was meant to fill. Let edges soften, bleed, and refuse to close. Never let an edge harden into a clean, photographable line.

Phase 3. The crossing. Let the picture bleed off its own boundary. Paint past the edge of the canvas onto the wall, the table, the floor, or the next surface over. Let the image pull on the room instead of sitting alone inside its rectangle.

The studio rule. Leave the toned ground showing through. Leave the marks that ran over the edge. Do not frame it, do not crop it clean. The open edge is the point.

IV. Cross-Medium Interference

The Hyphenated Title

Write a title for this piece using no plain nouns. String verbs and states together into one unbroken run, like the-thin-grey-bleeding-down-seeking-the-cold-floor-pulling-against-the-torn-corner. Write it directly onto the surface or onto the wall beside it, so the words are part of the work and cannot be peeled off for a clean photo.
