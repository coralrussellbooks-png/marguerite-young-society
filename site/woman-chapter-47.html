Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 47

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

The two shores organize this chapter, and the crossing between them is the pillar. Cousin Hannah, the "great suffrage captain," lies dead with the mountains for a tomb, her blood on the snow, and the narration wonders about a child "red as her blood, white as the snow" and then about itself: "Could I have been a woman, could I have been a man." The suffrage struggle raised to myth and the crossing point arrive together, gender coming loose at the graveside of the woman who fought hardest against its fixities. Mr. Spitzer defers his own life to the next, imagining a "night of love" that makes "no distinction between living and dead, lover and loved," the dissolving boundary and the illusion of the individual folded into one wish.

The doubling runs the chapter. Mr. Spitzer and his dead brother Peron are twins, and Peron's laughter keeps breaking into "his music of total harmony or his silence," the brother's disorder intruding on the brother's order the way it always does. The brother stands on the far shore as a "Welcoming Committee," greeting the captain into a "colony under no flag," and the flagless colony is the book's utopia past every sorting nation and category, the place where the crossings the living forbid are simply the weather. Plural selfhood is the ground of it: Spitzer cannot say which brother he is, whether he is dead or alive, and the not-knowing is not his failure but the book's truth surfacing in its central man.

His frailty against Peron's idealization of "deathless beauty and purity" is the tension the book keeps between aging flesh and the fantasy that would freeze women young, and Spitzer's blurred vision, his difficulty telling near from far, is the fractured self trying to locate itself among conflicting demands. He grows "transparent," "worn," shedding papers, composing fragmentary music, and the transparency is invisibility and erasure worked on the witness himself, the man who preserves lost histories thinning until he is one of the lost. The music "none had heard," the "dead Troubadour," the "lady never awakening," are the unheard song the book keeps playing for whoever is deaf to it.

Snowflakes whisper human voices and name themselves "kings, queens, clowns, and shahs," the multiplicity and fluidity of the self scattered into weather, and Mr. Spitzer doubts the captain "had ever found her love," calls her "ambiguous and never easily defined," which is unfulfilled feminist longing and the refusal of a fixed self held in one phrase. Death arrives as a "dipping sail," "a moment of rapture forever prolonged," and the captain dies "with none to uphold her," solitary at the end of a public life. The planetary wheel turns over "roads no traveller had ever travelled," there is no "master key" and always a missing "factor," and the chapter closes on marriage as "no by-road" but a passage straight to death, the "opium lady" and the "old corsair" "hacked by many wounds" gathered at the edge of it, every woman's survival scarred and every crossing still open.
