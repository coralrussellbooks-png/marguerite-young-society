Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 43

← Chapter 42 · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 44 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: Not One Self · Grief Doesn't End · Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The Questions

Mr. Spitzer attends the dying Cousin Hannah without knowing “exactly whom he was mourning for.” What is a grief that cannot find its object?

He has “greeted the dead or buried the living, only to find that he had been mistaken.” Where is the line between the living and the dead in him?

“Few could endure the absolute truth. Most must be self-deceived.” If everyone builds, is Cousin Hannah’s dream any different in kind from anyone’s?

What returns to Cousin Hannah at the end that she had spent a life refusing?

This is the Witness at a deathbed, and the deathbed will not resolve. Mr. Spitzer has witnessed “such journeys many times before,” and he attends this one as he attends everything, staying with the dying woman’s wild screamings and citadels and snowstorms without fixing them into an account. What makes the chapter hard is that his witnessing is blind: his grief is “vexed and thwarted…by his not knowing exactly whom he was mourning for.” The Witness usually knows at least the face it attends. Here even that is gone, and what remains is attention without an object, staying-with in the dark, grief that mourns it knows not whom. This is Grief Doesn't End carried past the walled and the material into the unmoored: grief that cannot locate the one it is grieving and grieves anyway.

His own edges are as uncertain as hers. He has “greeted the dead or buried the living, only to find that he had been mistaken,” and he is “not sure who he was, whether living or dead,” always mourning for one or the other without knowing which. Not One Self and the unmoored grief are the same condition in him: a man who cannot tell the living from the dead cannot tell whom he mourns, because the line that would sort them runs through himself. He believes the dead cast no vote and so casts none; he has stepped out of the count of the living as he stepped out of the census, neither fully here nor fully gone.

The chapter makes self-deception universal, and this matters for how the book reads Cousin Hannah. “Few could endure the absolute truth. Most must be self-deceived, perhaps by the very idea of time or memory or that ghostly love which never was and yet endured.” Everyone clothes the dream; everyone builds a workable version of a life too much to hold straight. Cousin Hannah’s sealed certainty is not a special vice against a world of clear-eyed realists. It is the common condition in one of its harder forms, and the chapter refuses to set her apart as uniquely deceived. The difference among people is never whether they build, only how sealed the building is.

And what the deathbed brings back is everything she refused. “All that she had rejected, it had returned, or almost all, all those passions she had subdued, those loves she had denied.” The great climax of a life spent fleeing love is the return of the love fled from. This is the sealed blueprint failing from the inside at the last hour, the walled-out weather coming through the wall exactly when the wall can no longer be maintained. Mr. Spitzer, witnessing, sees it and does not name it to her, cannot even be sure it is her he sees. The returning loves are hers and unreachable, and his grief for her is real and cannot find her.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Mr. Spitzer’s blind mourning for confusion to be cleared up, and the reading that sets Cousin Hannah apart as uniquely self-deceived. Neither the confusion to be corrected nor the special case is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that the Witness can attend even without an object, grief mourning it knows not whom, and this unmoored grief is Grief Doesn't End at its furthest, not a failure to identify the dead but the truth that the line sorting living from dead runs through the mourner himself. Self-deception is universal, so Cousin Hannah’s sealed dream differs from anyone’s only in degree, and the deathbed returns to her all the love she spent a life refusing, the wall failing at the last hour. Everyone builds, the difference is only how sealed, and the walled-out weather comes through in the end. Mr. Spitzer mourns without knowing whom, and the chapter lets the not-knowing stand.

For Reflection

Is it possible to grieve truly for someone one cannot quite locate or name?

If everyone is self-deceived to some degree, what separates a dangerous seal from an ordinary one?

What might return at the last hour that one has spent a life refusing?
