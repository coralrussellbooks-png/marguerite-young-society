Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 38

← Chapter 37 · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 39 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

The Questions

Catherine’s world is timeless and everyone in it immortal, yet “one was always missing.” What loss gets into a world built to have none?

She loves a “dead love which never was,” an “unearthly romance which should suffer no deterioration or change.” Is a love sealed against change still love?

Her memory is “a widely netted seine catching only the biggest star.” What does a net that coarse hold, and what does it lose?

Is Catherine’s withdrawal a rich inner life, or an “evasion” of living?

Catherine built a world to keep loss out, and this chapter shows the loss getting in anyway. In her timeless dream where “all were immortal,” still “one was always missing as if there were time, for there was this loss.” The seal fails at exactly the point it was built to hold. She admitted no theory of loss, and loss arrives without a theory, unbidden, the one who is always missing walking through the wall the dream was raised to keep them behind. This is the book’s quiet verdict on the sealing against grief: it does not work. The weather she locked out is inside the house. You cannot build a world so tight that loss stays out, because the missing one is already in the timeless room.

Her love is grief attached to a beyond that never was. She loves a “dead love which never was,” an “unearthly romance which should suffer no deterioration or change,” and the phrasing gives it away: she wants a love sealed against time, incapable of change or loss because it never lived to be lost. This is the promised elsewhere in the register of romance, the same pull that tempted Mr. Spitzer toward his silent-music heaven, now Catherine’s emotional economy. A love that cannot deteriorate is a love that cannot live, and Catherine has chosen it precisely for its deadness, its safety from the change that living love would suffer. The seal against loss becomes a love of the already-lost, because the already-lost can never be lost again.

Her memory is a coarse construction. It is “a widely netted seine catching only the biggest star or fish,” a net so wide it holds only the largest things and lets the rest pour through. This is memory as building, not retrieval, and building crudely: the past she keeps is a few big catches in a wide-meshed net, the fine detail gone, “the memory of the memory of music,” a copy of a copy. She cannot recall the years. What she has is not the past but a coarse construction of it, and she knows, somewhere, that a finer net, an accurate memory, would catch the loss she cannot afford.

And the chapter names the cost of the withdrawal. Catherine’s inner life is rich, and it is also an “evasion” of “the direct renunciation of life,” a stagnation dressed as depth. Against her the book keeps Cousin Hannah’s engagement, flawed as it was, and Mr. Spitzer’s provisional staying-in-the-world. Catherine’s sealed inwardness is not the model; it is the warning, a life so turned from the world that the world has stopped happening to it. The richness is real and it is not enough, because a construction sealed against loss is also sealed against life, and the missing one who walks through the wall is the proof that the seal has failed at both.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Catherine’s sealed inner world as a rich alternative to living, and the reading that takes her idealized dead love as a deep romance. Neither the rich inwardness nor the great love is what the chapter finally shows.

The footing is that the seal against loss fails, and the missing one walks into the timeless room, because no world can be built tight enough to keep grief out. Catherine’s love of a “dead love which never was” is grief attached to a beyond sealed against change, a love chosen for its deadness because the already-lost cannot be lost again. Her coarse-netted memory is a crude construction that holds the big things and lets the loss pour through, and her withdrawal is an evasion of life, not a depth beyond it. Sealing against loss does not work, and a love sealed against change is a love sealed against life. The missing one keeps walking through the wall, and the chapter does not seal the leak it has found.

For Reflection

What loss has gotten into a part of one’s life that was built to have none?

Is there a love one keeps precisely because it is safely past, and cannot change or be lost again?

When does a rich inner life become an evasion of the outer one?
