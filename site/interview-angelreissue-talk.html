Young's Interviews Analyzed

A Talk on "Angel in the Forest" (the mid-1960s reissue)

← Previous · Young's Interviews Analyzed · Next →

See also: Angel in the Forest: A Reading in Fourteen Chapters

A reading for the Miss MacIntosh project.

The talk at a glance

This is a public talk, not an interview, and it was cut short when Young ran out of time, so it breaks off in the middle of Robert Owen's story with her apology, "I misunderstood the time." The occasion is the republication of "Angel in the Forest" "after two decades," which places the talk around the mid-1960s, roughly twenty years after the 1945 first edition and close to the 1965 appearance of Miss MacIntosh. An introducer, whom Young names as Mr. Daubier, frames the book through Thomas Carlyle and through Professor Charles Holt's essay in the Columbia University Forum on the nonfiction novel as, simply, "history," and praises "Angel in the Forest" as "history as a prose poem." The talk covers the same New Harmony material as the WBAI interview with Charles Ruas, so its worth lies less in the history it repeats than in the compressed statements Young makes when she has to say, quickly, what the book is about.

Facts from the introduction

The introduction is worth keeping for its hard numbers, which shift across the interviews. It gives Miss MacIntosh as "17 years and 8 months in the writing" and "1198 pages long," and notes that the manuscript was longer still, with "a complete novella within the novel" removed to be "published separately as The Lady and the Centaur." It also reports that Young was then "working on a biography of James Whitcomb Riley," the earlier Indiana poet. That detail helps with dating and with tracking her plans, since the later interviews find her at work on the very different Eugene Debs biography rather than Riley. The precise "17 years and 8 months" and "1198 pages" are the firmest figures in the whole set, against the looser "17 years," "eighteen years," and "1135 pages" she gives elsewhere.

Carlyle as master, and the two illusions

Young opens by seizing on Carlyle. She says the introducer "found out one of the great influences upon my literary life," then explains that "when I determined to write prose, after years of dedication to poetry, I took Carlyle as my master in the transfer of poetry to prose." That is the clearest account in any of these sources of how she thought about turning herself from a poet into a writer of prose. She follows it with the sharpest single line on how her two books rhyme: "as in Miss McIntosh, I pursue the themes of illusion begotten by opium, here I pursue those begotten by utopia in a political state." Rapp, "one of the greatest manufacturers of whiskey," was for her "manufacturing various kinds of illusion," which lets her fold the utopias into the same lifelong subject as the opium lady's dreams.

The same county as the novel

The talk makes the tie between the two books explicit in a way the others only gesture at. The bus journey in Miss MacIntosh, "said to be the longest in literary history," arrives in "Posey County, Indiana," the real county of New Harmony, and the figures met there, "a tattooed landlord" and "a hangman who tells of his excellent fruit orchards," appear in both books "because the dream county to which the bus comes is the same setting as Angel in the Forest." Young repeats her documentary claim, now for the nonfiction: "all that I have told in this story is absolutely true, down to the last butterfly or tree or flower." And she names the people who first drew her, "a kind of group of Steinbeck characters living in tents at the outskirts, wanderers from the Ozarks," the stragglers "who sat at the gates of both utopias and never joined either one," among them the old pioneer who said, "I studies it and I studies it."

Siding with Coleridge: the irrational as the reason she wrote

The center of the talk is Young declaring whose side she is on. She recounts Owen's Manchester debate with Coleridge, in which Owen argued that "man is a machine of which all the parts may be known" while Coleridge defended "angels and unearthly creatures, spirits without bodies, irrational beings." When the deaf, aging Owen began to "hear voices" and "debate with the dead Coleridge, with Thomas Jefferson, with the Duke of Kent, with Shelley," to the embarrassment of the labor movement, Young found her subject: "if he had not lost contact with reality, or gained it, whichever way you like to think, I'm sure I never would have written my book about him." She states the principle without hedging: "I fell in love with Robert Owen simply because he came over to the irrational in the end," and "I take Coleridge's point of view." This is the belief that runs under all her work, said here as plainly as she ever says it.

Women sacrificed in utopia

One interpretive thread is sharper here than anywhere else. Describing Jane Owen's restoration of the maze, Young notes that she "commissioned Frederick Kiesler, the sculptor," whose figure there is "the Madonna with her arms empty and no child." The two women were "delighted" to find "we had seen the same thing in Utopia," and Young draws the lesson: "in Utopias, no matter what they are, women are sacrificed," because "the fantastic, irrational, non-reasoning side of life must go in a perfect society." The Rappite women "may work in the fields, but they don't have babies." Set beside the WNYC interview, where Miss MacIntosh's missing hair stands for "the feminine in life" that "God did not give her," this shows Young binding the loss of the feminine to the loss of the irrational, and treating both as the price a perfected order exacts.

Set against the Ruas interview and the others

This talk is the compressed, performed twin of the WBAI interview, and reading the two together is useful in two ways. First, the drifts. Here Rapp advertises in "the London Times," while in the Ruas interview it is the New York Times; here Owen meets the Tsar "at Geneva," while there it is Aix-la-Chapelle; and Jane Owen is "great-great-granddaughter-in-law" here and "great-granddaughter-in-law" there. These are the small slippages of a story told many times from memory, and they mark the parts Young reconstructs rather than checks. Second, the gains. Because the talk is short, Young says outright what the interview leaves implicit: Carlyle as the master of her prose, the opium book and the utopia book as twin studies of illusion, her allegiance to Coleridge and the irrational, and the sacrifice of women in every utopia. For the compilation, this is the source to quote when the point is Young's own theory of her work, while the Ruas interview remains the source for the New Harmony history itself.
