"Liquescence as Form," a feminist-critical essay by Miriam Fuchs

Part of Young's Interviews Analyzed, a study of what Marguerite Young (1908 to 1995) said in interviews about her work and life.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

A reading for the Miss MacIntosh project.

The source at a glance

This is not an interview or a profile but a work of literary criticism, "Marguerite Young's Miss MacIntosh, My Darling: Liquescence as Form," by Miriam Fuchs, from the anthology she co-edited with Ellen G. Friedman, "Breaking the Sequence: Women's Experimental Fiction" (Princeton University Press, 1989). It is the first theoretical treatment in the set, so it should be weighed as criticism, an argument about how the novel works, rather than as evidence about Young's life or intentions. Fuchs is also the scholar who interviewed Young, and lines from that interview appear in the Neville profile, so she reads the book with knowledge of its author. Her essay reads Miss MacIntosh through French feminist theory, chiefly Helene Cixous, to argue that the novel is a major example of what that school calls feminine writing. For the project its value is a rigorous vocabulary for the form Young describes only intuitively in the interviews, plus close-reading detail, page-anchored facts, and a candid account of the book's critical neglect.

Liquescence: the argument about form

Fuchs builds her title from Bernard Bergonzi's 1965 review, which reported the book at 3,449 typescript pages and about three and a quarter pounds, and she converts text to pages to pounds to gallons of water and oil to set up her governing image: the novel behaves like liquid. Vera Cartwheel narrates by mixing the recluses of her New England childhood with the eccentrics of her Midwestern bus trip, so that separate time frames diffuse, break into "particles" for close view, Miss MacIntosh's bald head and missing breast, Esther Longtree's swollen womb, Mr. Spitzer's double vision, then thicken and resurface. In Fuchs's reading the book's borders and partitions do not divide but collapse, and objects transform into other objects, a basket of grapes into a chair, a swan into a wash basin, a pear into a glove into a basket of peaches. Nothing is fixed; everything is "diffused, mixed, merged," seen only in analogous or related forms. To read the novel, she argues, is to yield to its "laws of baffling equivalences."

Against closure

Much of the essay is a close reading of how the novel refuses to end. Vera claims a traditional quest, to find her childhood nursemaid, yet Miss MacIntosh is almost certainly dead, having walked into the New England sea years before and left her corset and red wig on the shore, so the quest is illogical from the start, a search for her "where she was not." Fuchs tracks how plot resurfaces late only to be skewed further: with a sixth of the book left, instead of settling, the narrative starts over with Esther Longtree across some one hundred and sixty-five pages, then tilts again eight pages from the end with the stone-deaf man, whom Vera abruptly loves and marries in the final pages. When Young uses plot, Fuchs writes, she does so to negate its legitimacy. The two-line ending, Esther's misspelled sign that she is out to lunch and will be back, is a closure that promises reopening, and its conditional tense holds "the indefinite space between action and inaction," so that the novel ends without finishing, just as Esther is forever about to give birth without giving birth.

A feminine aesthetic

Fuchs's central claim is that Miss MacIntosh joins the twentieth-century avant-garde, long treated as male, to twentieth-century women's fiction, long treated as traditional, and so stands as an innovative expression of a female aesthetic. She reads it as the feminine writing theorized by Cixous, prose that wrecks partitions and has no closure, that "starts twenty times, thirty times, over," and she supports this with Barthes on the morality of form and DuPlessis on a nonhierarchical writing that carries "a female signature." The encyclopedic lists, of butterflies, archaic languages, shipwrecks, battles, the dead and the living, become in her account a plenitude in which all things coexist and through which no linear path can be cut. She reads the book's world as matriarchal, "replete with mothers, mother-surrogates, would-be-mothers," while the fathers are absent or impotent: Esther calls herself the passive policeman's only daughter, Catherine's husband vanished under Alpine snow, and Esther cannot name the fathers of her dream children, "all like moths in a storm." Against the masculine question Cixous poses, where children come from, Fuchs sets Young's redefinition of paternity as the desire to nurture, embodied in Mr. Spitzer's devotion to Vera, and of maternity, in Esther, as "breathing and hoping," the equivalent of life itself. Mr. Spitzer becomes her figure for the reader, a man of common sense forced to adjust to a feminine space with no causality or chronology, where "on time" is the wrong time and a whole episode about a jewel thief builds from events that never happened, and who learns that sympathy, nurturing and patience "cross gender lines." Catherine's journey down the canal of her own inner ear, which opens into a vast continent of ballrooms and stone walls, is Fuchs's emblem of that interior feminine space, endless and never fully explored.

Reception and the record it documents

The essay is unusually useful on reception. Fuchs states plainly that despite laudatory reviews the novel has been overlooked by feminist and postmodern critics alike, earning only two MLA citations in the quarter century after publication, and she notes that the existing interviews, Newquist's of 1967 and Ruas's of 1985, offer "little more than basic information." She cites John Gardner's admiring review in The Southern Review, which called the book extraordinary for "breadth of mind and verbal genius," and her notes confirm that Gardner had been Young's pupil and wrote his first two novels in her class, corroborating the account in the complete Ruas interview. Her bibliography also fixes publication facts cleanly: "Prismatic Ground" (Macmillan, 1937), "Moderate Fable" (Reynal, 1944), "Angel in the Forest: A Fairy Tale of Two Utopias" (Reynal, 1945), and Miss MacIntosh (Scribner's, 1965), and a footnote records the familiar discrepancy that Bergonzi gives the writing span as seventeen years while Young says eighteen.

Set against the other sources

As the first critical rather than biographical source, this essay gives disciplined form to what Young only gestures at in conversation. Her talk of a "muralist imagination," of "the many as opposed to the one," of everything becoming everything else and of a prose that lets absolutely everything in, finds here a sustained account under the name of liquescence and feminine writing. It also corroborates and clarifies facts scattered through the interviews: the stone-deaf man marrying Vera in the final pages, the Spitzer and Peron doubling, Esther's dream pregnancy, and Miss MacIntosh's death in the sea. Two points are worth carrying into the compilation. Fuchs names the lawyer twin "Joachim Spitzer," which confirms that the "Joachim" in the 1983 recording is the character's real first name and not a transcription error, and she notes Miss MacIntosh's missing breast, a bodily detail the interviews omit in favor of the baldness but which the Neville profile also records. Above all, her remark that the interviews provide "little more than basic information" names precisely the gap this compilation is meant to close, since the value of the interviews, read together and against the criticism, is exactly the accumulation of detail and variation that no single one of them supplies. This essay should anchor the critical, as opposed to the biographical, side of the eventual portrait, and it pairs naturally with the reading Young herself gives, in the Fuchs interview, of a book about going on without certainty toward a utopia that may not exist.
