Chapter 19

Characters: Miss MacIntosh

The wig lies on the floor of this chapter "like a rare tropical bird," a self shed and left where it fell. Around it is the plain inventory of Miss MacIntosh's room, the knitting needles and sewing baskets and soapstone dishes, the patched clothes, the bust of Martin Luther, the cheesecloth thrown over the broken cherubim. This is female domestic labor and survival, and it is the surface the book has laid over the dissolution all along, the material the real kept scrupulously in order above a woman who is coming apart underneath it.

She sleeps bald, without eyebrows or eyelashes, and the chapter ends on the amputated breast, "no bigger than a baby's," the body as the place the self comes apart shown at last without the wig or the corset to soften it. The playing cards near her, the deuce of clubs read as "the test of tears" and "the omen of death," hold the life-and-death boundary in a card, and her refusal to be a "weathercock" or a "turncoat" is the grounded woman's last stand against a world that will not stay fixed.

The room itself has begun to give way to the sea. The door is barred but crusted with seaweed and barnacles, sea boots stand ready, the umbrella lies open on the floor. The woman who keeps clear of the "greatest waves" has the ocean growing on her own door, the real and the dream as one country closing in on the one character built to keep them apart.
