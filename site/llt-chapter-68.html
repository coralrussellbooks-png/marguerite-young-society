Chapter 68

Characters: Vera Cartwheel

Pillars: Reality Is Constructed · The Past Doesn't Go Away · The Sacred Is Here

The Questions

“To come into a village by night may mean perhaps never to know it” as one would by day. Does the first way of entering a place seal how it is ever after known?

The shadows lie “like a texture or web laid upon the hills.” Are the shadows in the place, or laid over it by the one arriving?

The village is all rusted chassis, broken wheels, “bed-springs like old musical instruments never to be played on again.” What is the ruin doing here?

If one journeyed toward heaven and reached hell instead, has one still “reached one’s goal”?

The chapter opens on perception as a thing that seals the world. “To come into a village by night may mean perhaps never to know it in the same way as if one came in the incandescence of sunlight.” How one first enters a place fixes how the place is ever after known; arrive by night and the night stays, the shadows never lifting even if morning shows everything different. This is Reality Is Constructed at the level of perception itself: the first framing builds the place, and the built place holds, so that Vera’s night arrival is not a temporary condition but a construction that will roof her knowledge of the village for good. The village she gets is the village she came into, and she came into it in the dark.

The shadows raise the question of where the darkness is. They lie “like a texture or web laid upon the hills and river and trees,” and the word laid is the chapter’s point: the shadows are not simply in the village but laid over it, and by whom is left open. Vera arrives carrying her own night, her grief and her weariness and her long journey, and the web of shadow that textures the hills is partly hers, cast rather than found. The book has shown reality built by the one who perceives it; here the builder is grief and darkness, and the village receives the shadow the traveler brings as much as it owns any of its own.

The ruin is The Past Doesn't Go Away made into the village’s substance. The place is “rusted chassis and broken wheels and flat tires, bed-springs like old musical instruments never to be played on again,” loving cups “which would gather nothing but the rain.” Everything is the leaving of a past that will not stay behind, the accumulated wreckage of used-up things, the sediment of other lives risen into the present as junk. This is not a village with a history behind it but a village made of its own remainder, the past not gone but piled in the vacant lots, and Vera walks into a present that is mostly the debris of what came before.

And the chapter refuses to let arrival depend on which destination one reached. “If one had journeyed… to heaven and had come instead to hell… one still had reached one’s goal.” The goal was not the heaven or the hell but the reaching, the arriving, the coming-into a real place at the end of a real journey. This is The Sacred Is Here’s answer to the promised elsewhere: it does not matter that the village is not the shining destination, not heaven, is in fact a ruined night town of rusted junk. It is where the journey actually went, the here one actually reached, and reaching the actual here, heaven or hell, is the arrival that counts, not the imagined elsewhere one set out toward.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that treats Vera’s night arrival as a mood that morning will simply dispel, and the reading that takes the ruined village for a failure of the journey, the wrong destination reached. Neither the passing mood nor the failed arrival is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that how one first enters a place seals how it is ever after known, the night arrival building a village that holds even against morning; that the shadows are laid over the place as much as found in it, cast partly by the grief and darkness the traveler brings; that the ruin is The Past Doesn't Go Away, the village made of its own risen remainder, the past piled in the vacant lots; and that reaching the actual here, heaven or hell, is the arrival that counts, not the imagined elsewhere one set out toward. Perception seals the place, the past is its substance, and the real destination is the one actually reached. Vera comes into the ruined town by night, and the chapter counts that a goal reached.

For Reflection

Does the way one first enters a place, or a relationship, seal how it is ever afterward known?

How much of the shadow on a place is in the place, and how much laid over it by the one arriving?

If one set out for heaven and reached hell, has one still arrived somewhere real, and does that count?
