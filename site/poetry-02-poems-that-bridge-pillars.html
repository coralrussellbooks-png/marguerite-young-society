Exploring Young's Poetry

Poems That Bridge Pillars

← Movement VII. Naming: The Made Word · Exploring Young's Poetry · The Thesis Tested →

A handful of these poems refuse to sit still in one movement, which is not a flaw in the sorting, and it is not a flaw in the poems. The eleven ideas were never separate boxes. They are facets of a single vision, and a vision that holds together will naturally show more than one facet at once. When a poem carries two pillars at the same time, it is doing exactly what the whole argument of this book says Young's work does. The overlap is the proof, not the problem. A seam is where two pieces are joined, not where one is coming apart.

Each of the poems below has a center of gravity, which is why it lands in the movement it lands in. But each also opens onto a second pillar, and reading it with both in view is richer than forcing it to choose.

"Moderate Fable" sits in grief for its desolation, though its title, the modest and unfinished story, belongs just as truly to the open construction of II.

"Noah's Ark" and "Summer Day" hold creation and deep time at once, pulling between the sacred ground of I and the long history of VI.

"The Trains" and "The Whales" carry loss and also the machinery of what a people destroys, bridging IV and VI.

"The Home-Coming" and "Voyager Man" turn on belonging and the passage out, joining III and IV.

"That Cloud" wavers between perception and a dissolving self, holding II and V together.

"Ballad-Loving Woman" is filed under song but is also a portrait of a singular woman, bridging VII and III.

"Death in Lilliput" and "Strange Prophecy" shrink death to the small scale of I while grieving like IV.

"Guide to New Harmony, Indiana" holds The Past Doesn't Go Away, the invented utopia of Reality Is Constructed, and the brown-grass Sacred Ordinary all at once, bridging VI, II, and I.

"The Contravalid" and "Proem to Reality" are late summas that fuse the constructed real of II with Unfinished Is Honest, and "The Contravalid" pulls hard on The Margins Reveal the Truth besides.

"Elegy" closes the whole Collected on The Sacred Is Here while carrying Grief Doesn't End and the memory of The Past Doesn't Go Away.

"Transition" and "Jonathan's House" show the Dubious Ground of II already latent in the early work, one through a broken mirror and one through a dead stone replica.

"Love Mourned" grieves and dissolves the self in the same breath, bridging IV and V.

None of these needs to be resolved. The movement a poem sits in names where its weight falls, not the only thing it is about. Left to carry two or more pillars at once, these poems become small demonstrations of the book's central claim, that the vision is one and its ideas run into each other the way the parts of any living thing do.
