Phenomenology

In the research: Chapter 1, Chapter 7, Chapter 14, Chapter 36, Chapter 68

Definition from research

Chapter 1

  • Phenomenology is a philosophical study and movement largely associated with the early 20th century that seeks to objectively investigate the nature of subjective, conscious experience. It attempts to describe the universal features of consciousness while avoiding assumptions about the external world, aiming to describe phenomena as they appear to the subject, and to explore the meaning and significance of the lived experiences. (Wikipedia)

Chapter 7

  • “Am I seeing with my eyes?”: This reflects the phenomenological tradition of bodily awareness. Moses’s questions are phenomenology’s: what the senses and the body give to consciousness. Vera asked the same in Chapter 1, when the world seemed “the conspiracy of memories and dreams floating without purpose” and “The individual is the one illusion.”

Chapter 14

  • “There should be the same divisions and multiplications”: Multiplicity is a philosophical concept developed by Edmund Husserl and Henri Bergson from Riemann’s description of the mathematical concept. Bergson distinguished two kinds: a countable multiplicity of distinct parts, and a qualitative one whose parts interpenetrate. (Wikipedia)

Chapter 36

  • “heard the musician only when the music had ceased”: Edmund Husserl took a melody as his example of time-consciousness: each note, as it passes, is held in “retention” while the next sounds, so that a melody is never heard all at once (Edmund Husserl, The Phenomenology of Internal Time-Consciousness, 1928).

Chapter 36 · Time and Perception

  • Time is experienced non-linearly; sounds arrive late, events are recalled out of sequence.
  • The narrative reflects a consciousness that is “always in danger” of missing or misinterpreting life’s moments.
  • This theme underscores the instability of human experience and the elusive nature of reality.
  • “Each sound reached his dead ear always a minute late.” “His eyes were separated from his ears.”

Chapter 68

  • “there was only one definition of reality, that it was real”: The line emphasizes a tautological but deeply existential assertion: reality is defined by its own existence as experienced or known, regardless of its form. “even the unreal things were real,” points to an inclusive, perhaps subjective or phenomenological, understanding of reality where things dismissed or marginalized by society (ignored, rejected, unloved) are nonetheless part of the fabric of reality. This echoes themes of illusion versus reality, and the permeability between them, which run throughout the novel. Philosophically, this passage represents a theme often associated with existentialism and phenomenology: that reality is defined by experience and perception rather than by fixed, external definitions. It also touches on the fluidity of reality, blurring lines between dream and waking life, appearance and essence, presence and absence. The passage conveys that reality is not limited to the tangible or socially accepted but includes dreams, illusions, forgotten or rejected beings, and all forms of existence or perception. It challenges rigid dichotomies and invites a broader, more inclusive understanding of what is real.
