Definition from research

Chapter 1

  • Phenomenology is a philosophical study and movement largely associated with the early 20th century that seeks to objectively investigate the nature of subjective, conscious experience. It attempts to describe the universal features of consciousness while avoiding assumptions about the external world, aiming to describe phenomena as they appear to the subject, and to explore the meaning and significance of the lived experiences.

Chapter 7

  • “Am I seeing with my eyes?” - Phenomenological tradition (bodily awareness). This moment is emblematic of Vera’s inner turmoil and quest for truth, where she is caught between what is perceived and what might be the “conspiracy of memories and dreams floating without purpose,” emphasizing the novel’s overarching theme that “the individual is the one illusion” and that reality itself may be elusive or illusory.

Chapter 14

  • Multiplicity is a philosophical concept that refers to the parts of a whole that can be considered separately. It can also refer to an entity that is made up of simple elements that have twisted or folded together. Philosophers who have worked with the concept of multiplicity: Edmund Husserl and Henri Bergson.

Chapter 29

  • The philosophy of conversion explores the concept of a significant and transformative change, often in belief, behavior, or identity. It can be viewed within religious, philosophical, and psychological contexts, examining the nature of this change, its motivations, and its implications for individuals and societies. Religious Conversion Christian conversion, Jewish conversion, Islamic conversion, Hinduism doesn't have a formal conversion process. In Plato's philosophy, the concept of conversion is represented in the allegory of the cave, where a prisoner's awakening involves turning away from shadows and towards the light of truth. Philosophical conversion can also refer to an existential change in the individual, adopting new moral standards or making a fundamental shift in their understanding of reality. Moral conversion involves a change in an individual's moral beliefs, values, and actions, often driven by a critical self-reflection and a new understanding of moral principles. Philosophers like Descartes and Husserl are considered to have undergone a form of philosophical conversion in their pursuit of universal science and phenomenology, respectively. The philosophy of conversion is significant as it encapsulates the novel’s central concerns with change, the instability of identity, and the interplay of life and death. Conversion is not merely a religious or moral act but a profound existential process that embraces ambiguity, transformation, and the continual search for meaning amid uncertainty and flux. This philosophy enriches the novel’s exploration of human consciousness and the elusive nature of reality.

Chapter 36

  • Julia Shaw is a psychologist and an expert in complex autobiographical false memory. She asserts that all memories are illusions which are similar to how quantum physicists describe reality, what Einstein called a persistent illusion. It’s the same way Plato talked about prisoners in a cave confusing shadows on the wall with the reality outside the cave. Current research found a robust false memory effect. However, participants were able to differentiate false memories and actual memories when using specific phenomenological tasks. False memory in the novel is portrayed as a multifaceted phenomenon that shapes and distorts reality for the characters. It is linked to themes of identity, time, and the elusive nature of truth. Memory is not a stable foundation but a shifting, sometimes deceptive, landscape where truth and illusion coexist. Characters struggle to discern reality amid the fluidity and fallibility of memory, and this struggle underscores much of the novel’s psychological depth and philosophical inquiry.

Chapter 68

  • "there was only one definition of reality, that it was real" emphasizes a tautological but deeply existential assertion: reality is defined by its own existence as experienced or known, regardless of its form. "even the unreal things were real," points to an inclusive, perhaps subjective or phenomenological, understanding of reality where things dismissed or marginalized by society (ignored, rejected, unloved) are nonetheless part of the fabric of reality. This echoes themes of illusion versus reality, and the permeability between them, which run throughout the novel. Philosophically, this passage represents a theme often associated with existentialism and phenomenology: that reality is defined by experience and perception rather than by fixed, external definitions. It also touches on the fluidity of reality, blurring lines between dream and waking life, appearance and essence, presence and absence. The passage conveys that reality is not limited to the tangible or socially accepted but includes dreams, illusions, forgotten or rejected beings, and all forms of existence or perception. It challenges rigid dichotomies and invites a broader, more inclusive understanding of what is real.
