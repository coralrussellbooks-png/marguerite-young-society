Research notes: Chapter 20 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 20

Characters: Miss MacIntosh

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The narrator is never singular in this chapter. She is simultaneously the child who worships Miss MacIntosh, the witness who sees her undone, the strategist who plans to pretend she saw nothing, the mourner who grieves what she has seen, and the philosopher who tries to construct meaning from the wreckage. These selves do not resolve into one another. They coexist in the same paragraph, often in the same sentence.

"There, though I saw her clear and plain, I was determined, my childish heart furious with resolutions, to go on as if I had never seen her."

The phrase "my childish heart" is spoken by a voice that is clearly not entirely childish; it is retrospective, ironic, self-aware. The child-self and the knowing-self occupy the same grammatical space without either canceling the other. The narrator does not choose between them. She inhabits both.

Later, the multiplication deepens:

"that she was not my secret lover or husband or cause of the smile playing like a lost sunbeam upon my lips."

The relationship between narrator and Miss MacIntosh is named here as lover, husband, cause: three incompatible categories offered simultaneously, none retracted. The self is constituted by its attachments, and those attachments are irresolvably plural.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter's entire ethical structure rests on the narrator's decision to maintain an illusion: to pretend she has not seen what she has seen. But crucially, Young frames this not as cowardice but as a kind of metaphysical loyalty to the constructed world that made Miss MacIntosh possible.

"It would be simple to pretend that we were simple."

This is one of the most compressed sentences in the chapter. The word "pretend" acknowledges the illusion; the word "simple" is used twice, first as an act and then as a condition, and neither usage is innocent. To pretend to be simple is to knowingly construct a reality, which means simplicity was never natural, only performed. The illusion does not come after reality; it was always the structure.

"I was resolved to close my guilty eyes again that they might open only on the commonplace where nothing should be deranged."

The "commonplace" is not a neutral default; it is a construction requiring active maintenance, guilty labor, deliberate not-seeing. The cave is not escaped by opening the eyes. It is only exchanged for a smaller, more carefully managed cave.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The chapter opens in lyrical invocation of dawn as potential healing, "oh, radiance of heavenly light which shall bind up the wounds in our sides", and then systematically dismantles that promise. The light does not heal. It reveals. And what it reveals is that the wounds are older than the light, older perhaps than the narrator's knowledge of Miss MacIntosh entirely.

"For the light revealed these wounds which would not heal with time, these deprivations which were surely hers and everlasting, and some were older than the pale or burnished light sweeping across her fearful forehead."

"Everlasting" and "older than the light": grief here is not an event with a beginning. It is a condition that precedes consciousness of it. The narrator does not discover Miss MacIntosh's sorrow; she discovers that it was always already there, that she had been living inside someone else's unacknowledged grief without knowing it.

The accumulation continues through the questions about Miss MacIntosh's hair:

"Was it her broken heart which had caused this? Was she in mourning for someone dead, for a dead lover of her youth?"

These questions are not answered, and they are not meant to be. Grief's origin is not recoverable. What matters is its presence, its permanence, its reality as climate rather than event.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The ethical core of the chapter is the act of seeing, and the agonized negotiation about what to do with what has been seen. The narrator cannot unsee Miss MacIntosh's baldness and missing breast. The question becomes: what does one owe to what one has witnessed?

"I was determined... to go on as if I had never seen her, just as she had saltily advised."

The narrator chooses, at least consciously, not to confront Miss MacIntosh with her knowledge. But the text itself refuses this suppression. The chapter does not enact the narrator's resolution; it enacts the opposite. It stays in the room. It looks. It describes with extraordinary precision what it sees:

"There was, livid before my eyes, the missing breast like a door knob, the reality of that which is missing."

"The reality of that which is missing"; this is witnessing at its most exacting. The absence is named as real. The missing thing is not euphemized or turned away from. The narrator's conscious plan to pretend is overridden by the text's deeper commitment to not simplifying.

The chapter also witnesses without flinching the narrator's own complicity and confusion:

"I tried to figure out what should be my attitude, as I tried to organize my own transient point of view."

The witness here is not stable or authoritative. She is trying, in real time, to constitute a response. That trying is itself witnessed and recorded.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The narrator's plan for the morning after is explicitly a return to the beginning: a reconstruction of the ordinary:

"I would awaken upon a day of old, beloved routines repeating themselves."

But this return is not innocent repetition. It is reconstruction with knowledge: the same routines, now freighted with what has been seen. The narrator lists with almost manic specificity what Miss MacIntosh will wear:

"Her false teeth should be in her bony head and not in the ruby-colored glass, and she should wear her salmon-colored corset tightly laced and with stout stays and double reinforcements, her breast pad, her brick-colored wig of bright, metallic curls and waves."

Each item in this list is now understood differently. The false teeth, the breast pad, the wig; these are not simply costumes. They are the materials of a constructed self, and the narrator now knows the construction explicitly. The building will resume, but the builder knows it is building.

The lists of things the narrator will learn (Hebrew measures, weights, coins, the Jewish calendar, the itinerary from Egypt to Canaan) are also recursive constructions: the re-assumption of the student role, the deliberate return to the posture of not-knowing, as a way of managing what is now known.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter is syntactically incompletable. Young's sentences do not end so much as they accumulate and eventually stop, having refused to conclude. Consider:

"this plaintive forehead without brow which was high or low or middle or could be defined, should we even use a mariner's compass or guide ourselves by fading or faded stars."

The conditional clause, "should we even use a mariner's compass", does not resolve the question of the forehead. It multiplies the instruments of measurement while making clear that none of them will suffice. The sentence enacts its own incompletion as a form of honesty.

The narrator explicitly acknowledges the violence of demanding resolution:

"But now this routine would have a different meaning for me, would be already of the elegiac past, and I should hold to every moment as if I lived with a dying man, and I should protect her from the knowledge of herself."

"Protect her from the knowledge of herself"; this is the ethics of incompletion made explicit. To force the conclusion, to make Miss MacIntosh confront what the narrator has seen, would be a kind of violence. The ongoing sentence (the maintained pretense, the unfinished conversation) is the form that care takes.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Miss MacIntosh, as revealed in this chapter, is a figure of accumulated marginalities. She is bald. She has one breast. She wears false teeth, a wig, a breast pad. She is a nursemaid: a working-class woman in a wealthy household. Her body is a site of multiple lacks, multiple departures from the normative female form.

"Should Miss MacIntosh be held responsible for this poor thing who was Miss MacIntosh? Whose work, I vaguely wondered, was this? Whose accomplishment, this sad masterpiece?"

The question of responsibility is the question of the marginal person's relationship to the categories that exclude them. Miss MacIntosh did not choose her baldness or her missing breast. But she is held to account by the normative standard: Ovid's "ugly is a field without grass, a plant without leaves, a head without hair" is cited not as truth but as the violence of the norm made visible.

"Hair adds beauty to a good face and terror to an ugly one, according to the Greek."

The appeal to classical authority here is ironic. The Greek and Ovid are invoked as the voices of the normative, and they are immediately insufficient. Miss MacIntosh exceeds both their categories. She is the evidence that the categories are constructed.

The missing breast is named with similar precision:

"that narrow and intolerable vacancy where there should be the other breast of woman or of man."

"Of woman or of man": the grammar itself refuses the binary. The vacancy is not simply the absence of a female attribute. It is a vacancy in the category of the body as such.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The chapter is saturated with the past pressing up through the present. Miss MacIntosh's baldness is not a present condition; it is the accumulation of something that happened in an unrecoverable past:

"Was it hair by hair she lost, perhaps through many hesitant years, or all at one blow in some troubled, unknowable past escaping my sphere of life's experience?"

"Unknowable past escaping my sphere": the past is not accessible but it is present. It is visible in its effects, in the bald head, in the missing breast, in the face that is "so sublimely peaceful even in its broken, anguished beauties".

The narrator's own relationship to time is similarly sedimentary:

"This was the tree stripped bare of leaves before the tree was. This was the dead tree of life. Perhaps she was older than those whose hair had turned grey."

"Before the tree was": Miss MacIntosh's loss precedes even the existence of what was lost. The past is not behind her; it is beneath her, constituting her. The sediment is the person.

The chapter's opening invocation, "washings of waves", establishes the sea as the figure for sedimentary time: always arriving, always depositing, never the same wave twice but always the same sea.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Young's language in this chapter is self-consciously constructive. The opening lyrical passage, "Oh, diamond dawn and pure, many-prismed, oh, radiance of heavenly light", is not description but invocation, and it is immediately undercut by what the light actually reveals. The language of transcendence is deployed and then shown to be insufficient.

The lists are a particularly important linguistic strategy:

"I would learn the Jewish calendar, Nisan which is March, Jyar which is April, Sivan which is May, Thammuz which is June, Ab, July, Elul, August."

This list does not describe reality. It constructs a particular relationship to knowledge: the student's relationship, the memorizer's relationship, the one who knows the names of things without necessarily knowing the things. The language here is explicitly a performance of not-seeing, a linguistic strategy for maintaining the pretense.

The theological catalogue near the end is perhaps the most explicit instance of language as construction:

"What was God, defined in so many terms—as a fuller’s soap, as a refining fire, as the light of the morning when the sun arises... as a casket, as an ark, as a crown of glory, as a diadem of beauty, as a belt of stars."

Each of these is a metaphor, and the accumulation of metaphors does not add up to God; it adds up to the impossibility of naming God from any single location. The language names its own inadequacy. It speaks from somewhere particular (from the bedside of a bald, one-breasted woman in the early morning) and it knows it.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The chapter opens with the language of transcendence (celestial light, heavenly radiance, the washing away of sins) and then locates all of that transcendence in the body of one sleeping woman in a sparsely furnished bedroom.

"There was, through all this theme of dark negation and flight away from life, her affirmation, strong and positive, that which God Himself should tremble to see should ever the problematical deity be confronted with the problematical creation."

"The problematical deity be confronted with the problematical creation": the sacred is not above the scene. It is in the scene, specifically in the body that the scene reveals. God is "problematical": the same word used for Miss MacIntosh's face earlier: "as if this were the experimental light of the first day of crazed creation lifting upon a face forever problematical." The face and the deity share the same ontological status.

The theological questions (Was God bald? Was God bearded?) are not blasphemous. They are the logical extension of immanent transcendence:

"Was God bald, too, I wondered, or bearded so that birds might hide themselves behind His dreaming hair, behind the cloud?"

The sacred is found by pushing deeper into the immanent (into the bald head, the missing breast, the sleeping face) not by looking away from them toward something else.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The chapter's final movement is explicitly about the narrator's position at the intersection of times:

"only I would remember her, even when I had forgotten her, for only I had seen her clear and plain."

The narrator is constituted as a crossing point: she carries what she has seen into a future in which Miss MacIntosh will be covered over, lost, forgotten by others and perhaps by herself. The obligation is structural; she did not choose to see, but having seen, she is now the carrier.

The sedimentary figures converge here:

"my mind wandered already through far waters and narrow straits and passages, searching for that which was lost."

The search through "far waters and narrow straits" is not a journey forward but a descent, into the layers of what has been deposited. The narrator is not moving through time; she is the place where time crosses.

The chapter's final sentence confirms this:

"Truly it had been said, I had come close to something which none should see and live to relate, the image of life failing before his eyes."

"To live to relate": the narrator's function is testimonial. She is the crossing point between the witnessed and the not-yet-known. She has seen the image of life failing, and she will carry it. This is not chosen. It is what she is now.
