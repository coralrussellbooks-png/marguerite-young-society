Young's Interviews Analyzed

"The Guru," a chapter by David O. Dowling on her Iowa years

Part of Young's Interviews Analyzed, a study of what Marguerite Young (1908 to 1995) said in interviews about her work and life.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Previous · Young's Interviews Analyzed · Next →

A reading for the Miss MacIntosh project.

The source at a glance

This is chapter five, "The Guru: Marguerite Young," of David O. Dowling's "A Delicate Aggression: Savagery and Survival in the Iowa Writers' Workshop" (Yale University Press, 2019), in the book's section on the Engle era. It is a documented scholarly account built from Young's letters to Paul Engle, from Bruce Kellner's recollections of studying with her, and from other students such as Peter Merchant. Its value for the project is that it covers the one long stretch of her life the interviews mostly skip, her career as a teacher and her navigation of the literary profession, and it supplies dated facts where the interviews give only memory. It covers three periods: her Chicago graduate apprenticeship of 1935 to 1936, her Iowa years as a doctoral student and lecturer from 1940 to 1943, and her return as Workshop faculty from 1955 to 1957. As a secondary source it should be weighed as biography rather than as Young's own testimony, though it quotes her letters directly.

The guru at Iowa

Dowling's portrait of Young as a teacher is the fullest anywhere. She arrived in a black woolen cape in ninety-degree heat, and students flocked to her over the other mentors, Ray West and Curtis Harnack, drawn by a novel-in-progress unlike anything since Melville or Proust. Her magnetism bred jealousy, above all from the professor Robert O. Bowen, whose own war novel was ignored and who once brandished a butcher knife at an Iowa City party, prompting Young's whispered "Bob is going to kill me." Against the harsh New Critical methods of the Workshop, and faculty like John Berryman, she gathered a devoted coterie. In class she would summon apparitions, pausing to greet Henry James at the door, and her two-room apartment overflowed with manuscript stacks and, in Kellner's phrase, a clutter of gimcracks and toys, with no discernible bedroom and no memory among friends of her cooking a meal. Dowling dates and firms the setting the interviews leave vague: she was about forty-five, on the faculty from 1955 to 1957, lured back by a Rockefeller fellowship Engle arranged.

Her pedagogy

The chapter gives Young a coherent teaching philosophy that the interviews only imply. She freed students from the tyranny of realism, singing out that "the subject matter doesn't matter," and she worked to defuse the savage criticism that passed for rigor, identifying with Virginia Woolf as a woman writer facing a male critical establishment. The clearest illustration is the student Peter Merchant, who dutifully cut a fantastical line as "nonsense" only to have Young write "As was" beside every deletion, telling him he had crossed out "the best parts" of his writing, since his rational mind had censored his deepest material. Where the workshop preached pruning, she preached restoration of the unconscious. She held that "style is thinking," refused the assumption that talent is innate, and took an Emersonian, democratic view that creative power is common because "most people dream." Asked why her students wrote, she cut through their answers to say they wrote "because you want to be loved." Her method produced results the skeptics did not expect, placing student work, and her own story "The Opium Lady," in Marguerite Caetani's Rome journal Botteghe Oscure.

Chicago, 1935 to 1936: Stein and the opium lady

Dowling documents and dates the two Chicago encounters the interviews return to. Young sat at Gertrude Stein's feet while taking a master's degree, drawing courage from Stein's radical individualism and carrying, like her, a handbag stuffed with food and manuscripts. The deeper figure was Minna K. Weissenbach, "the opium lady of Hyde Park," whom Young called the most remarkable person she ever knew and for whom she worked as reader and secretary after the university employment office sent her looking for money. Weissenbach was a patron who had housed Edna St. Vincent Millay, and Young slept in Millay's bed and sat among relics that later furnish Catherine Cartwheel's house, a silver cup said to be Keats's, a Persian letter set, a bird with a seashell. Instructed to keep reading aloud even when the old woman passed into an opium dream, Young realized she was providing "the verbal passport" to another world, the exact role she wanted as a novelist. Dowling names her "Minna K. Weissenbach," which is worth noting for the compilation, since the interviews call her "Minna" in the 1970s and 1980s and "Mette" in 1983.

Working the system: Young and Paul Engle

The most valuable new material is the record of Young as a shrewd professional, drawn from her letters to Paul Engle, which complicates the unworldly image of the other sources. "Angel in the Forest" began as sixty interrelated ballads in blank verse in 1940, when she was a doctoral candidate at Iowa and met Engle, who saw the commercial potential of her poetry. He arranged a fellowship and a lectureship that brought her back in 1942, and steered the Reynal and Hitchcock editor Frank Taylor to her, who signed the book. She never finished the doctorate, balking at a Greek and Latin requirement she resented as time stolen from writing. Moving to Bleecker Street, she became known in the Village as a "well of energy," unmarried and, in her phrase, with "neither chick nor child," and watched "Angel in the Forest" and the poetry book "Moderate Fable" appear together, "like being the mother of twins." She lobbied Engle openly to win her a Guggenheim, which came in 1948, told him plainly that he had started her "on the right road," and traded promotional reviews with him, once writing what she frankly called a "commercial review" of his book. She prized the money as much as the praise, boasting of a "huge advance" from Scribner's and her new standing as a "Scribner's author with Max Perkins as my editor." After years in New York, Naples, and Rome, where she cultivated the princess Marguerite Caetani and her journal, she returned to Iowa in 1954 to finish the novel. This is the Young the interviews never show, one who understood the fellowship-and-review machinery of American letters and worked it with skill.

Reception, the Debs book, and the end

The chapter also gathers reception and closes the arc. The critic Nona Balakian, a founder of the National Book Critics Circle, gave the keynote at a 1983 celebration of Young by the American Professors of Creative Writing, calling the novel American to its core and predicting that Young's recognition, like Proust's, would take fifty years. Nin called the book the voice of "nocturnal America," as Don Quixote was of Spain and Ulysses of Ireland, and a Nashville paper ranked it the most important American work since Moby-Dick. Dowling records the Algonquin encounter with Sinclair Lewis, grateful that she saw the Middle West on its own terms, and the poet Amy Clampitt's memory of Young as a lingering monument to a vanished world. On the Debs biography he notes what matters most for the record: the manuscript grew even larger than Miss MacIntosh and stood as a nonfictional substitute for a novel of ideas, and "Harp Song for a Radical" appeared posthumously under the editorial care of Charles Ruas, the same interviewer who conducted two of the recordings in this project. Young died in 1995 before completing the three-volume history.

Set against the other sources

This chapter mostly documents and dates what the interviews assert, and adds the professional life they omit. It confirms the from-life method, the Browning creed that "the poet is a reporter" and the refusal to invent characters, with Kellner's gloss that under every fantasy lay a hard truth, "nobody else's but hers." It confirms the excised-novellas regret, Young telling students the book would have sold far better published one volume at a time, and it confirms Maxwell Perkins as her editor and the large advance. It corrects or firms several things the interviews blur: "Angel in the Forest" was a Reynal and Hitchcock book brought about by Frank Taylor, the doctorate was never completed, and the Iowa faculty years were 1955 to 1957. Two links are worth carrying forward. The scholarship names the opium lady "Minna K. Weissenbach," a further data point in the Mette-versus-Minna question, and it shows that Charles Ruas, interviewer in two of these sources, edited her posthumous Debs biography, tying the archive together. Above all, this source supplies the one thing every interview leaves out, the picture of Young as a canny operator inside the literary marketplace, which belongs beside the dreamer she presents in her own voice.
