Movement Two: The Five Pillars in the Traditions

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Titus Bonebreaker · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Faith, God, and the Supernatural

Philosophy: Augustine and Christian Theology · Buddhism · Marguerite Young on Her Craft · Plato and Platonism · Spinoza

Allusions: Angels and Archangels · Genesis · Napoleon and Wellington · Revelation and the Apocalypse · The Bible · The History of Opium

Definition from research

The first sentence Young gives us about God in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is not a prayer or a theological argument or a scene of worship. It is a statement of condition: "God is the loneliest of all, and there is perhaps no God but what we dreamed, and there is no train." Three clauses, and each one performs a different operation on the religious tradition Young inherited. The first extends Christian sympathy to God as a sufferer rather than a sovereign. The second places the entire theological enterprise inside the epistemological framework of illusion as primary. The third collapses the metaphysical into the mundane, the God-question and the train-question arriving in the same grammatical space with the same answer: perhaps neither exists except as the dream of the person asking. This is not atheism. It is something structurally more complex: a religious sensibility that has absorbed the full weight of the tradition and refused to either accept or dismiss it, choosing instead to hold it as a question the novel will spend 1,198 pages not resolving.

Young builds her religious architecture on four distinct registers, each one carried by a different character, each one doing different work inside the five pillars. The registers are: Miss MacIntosh's practical piety (the religion of the verifiable), the mother Catherine's agnostic mysticism (the religion of the interior), Mr. Spitzer's theological pluralism (the religion of accumulated names), and Mr. Bonebreaker's evangelical Christianity (the religion of salvation and judgment). None of these is the novel's position. All four together constitute the novel's argument about what religion is and what it is for.

Miss MacIntosh's piety is the most plainly rendered and the most philosophically interesting precisely because it contradicts itself without embarrassment. She is the woman who insists on truth to nature, rejects illusions and false prophets, refuses the consolations of an imaginary life, and teaches Vera the Pilgrim's Progress alongside the population of Kansas. Her faith is expressed as labor and endurance: "God had been her harness-maker, that He had hung her harness with tinkling bells so that it might always be known where she was, even when she was in the shadows." This is theology as practical ethics, God as the craftsman who makes the harness for the work that must be done, the bells not announcing divine presence but simply making the worker findable in the dark. She also holds, without apparent contradiction, that God "had not created everything," that "He had left many things uncreated," which is the most unsentimental theological statement in the novel: a God with limitations, a creation with gaps, a universe in which the uncreated is as real as the created and the worker's job is to keep working regardless. The witness pillar runs through her entire religious sensibility: she sees the world plainly, she names what she sees, and she refuses to dress the gaps in creation with illusions she cannot personally verify. "God pity God," she says when confronted with the baldness that is her secret, the bald head that will undo her entire performance of normality, and the inversion is theologically precise: the God who did not finish creation deserves the same compassionate attention she extends to any other being facing the consequences of its own insufficiency.

The mother Catherine represents the novel's most paradoxical religious figure: she is the agnostic who lives inside a world more densely populated with the sacred than any orthodox believer in the novel. "She was the agnostic, but the angels surrounded her in her opium paradise, their fleecy wings outspreading, shadowing her cheek, and the harpers harped, and the seas were turned to glass." Young is not being ironic here. The mother's agnosticism is genuine: she does not believe in God as a metaphysical proposition. But her interior world is constituted entirely by the imagery of revelation, by seraphim and cherubim and apocalyptic seas of glass, because the imagery of the Christian tradition, accumulated over centuries, has become the available vocabulary for interior experience at a certain scale of intensity. Her father had set a table for the wandering angel, a salt shaker and a crystal goblet and a plate of gold for whoever might arrive from Tarsus or some far place. She rejected his faith but kept his hospitality: her house is always full, its rooms assigned to Plato and Spinoza and Wellington and the ghost of Cheops, all equally welcome, none more real than another. The illusion as a primary pillar is what permits this: if illusion is the primary mode of experience, then the agnostic's interior world can be more theologically rich than the believer's, because it is not constrained by the requirement of doctrinal consistency. Every figure who arrives is equally invited and equally imaginary and equally present, which is its own kind of sacred.

Mr. Spitzer carries the novel's most formally elaborate religious architecture: the passage in which he catalogs the names of God runs for a full page without pausing, and it is worth attending to as architecture rather than decoration. "God was not God in Himself, Mr. Spitzer believed, but in these pitiful images of God, these images which were but phantoms fading on the air." Then the list: the True Vine, the Bread of Life, the Gate, the Door, the Wine, the Only-Begotten, the Anointed, the Word, the Restorer, the Resurrection, the Great Spirit, the Light of the World, the Man of Sorrows, the Old Man, the King of Glory, the King of Kings, the Prince of Peace, the Good Shepherd, the Narrow Way, the Rose, the Thorn, the Risen, Immanuel, the Word made Flesh, the Advocate, the Intercessor, the Dove. The King of Light opposed "by the necessary King of Darkness, for if there were no darkness, then there should be no conception of light." This is the plural self pillar operating at theological scale: God is not a single being with a stable identity but a being constituted by the accumulation of every name human longing has ever assigned to the divine, each name a particular, the God behind them all a universal that may or may not exist independently of the names. Young also has Mr. Spitzer extend the catalog beyond Christianity to "the third heaven, the seventh heaven, Valhalla, Nirvana, the happy hunting grounds," collapsing the boundary between traditions with the same gesture she uses to assign Plato and Spinoza to the same house: all of it is equally real, equally imagined, equally necessary.

Mr. Bonebreaker is the novel's only practicing evangelical, and Young gives him the most physically specific presentation of any religious figure in the novel. He is "sallow and tall and stoop-shouldered, a fisherman on the waters of this stormy life, casting his wide net," carrying his Bible and his Bible literature and his long black umbrella, raindrops falling from his beaked nose and chin. He finds Miss MacIntosh just after her mastectomy, weak and wandering, and tells her that "for whom the Lord loveth, the Lord chasteneth," and gathers the stray lamb to the fold. Young is not mocking him, exactly. She is showing how evangelical Christianity operates at its most effective: it arrives in the specific moment of maximum vulnerability, it names the suffering as spiritually meaningful, it offers community and certainty and a framework in which the wound becomes evidence of divine love rather than mere biological bad luck. Miss MacIntosh, who has refused every illusion, leans on Bonebreaker's arm in the rain because the wound has undone her ordinary resistance and what he is offering is warmth in a cold district, which is what religion has always offered and what Young never dismisses as simply false.

The preacher who collapses the wedding rite and the burial rite in a single ceremony without anyone present noticing until it is too late is the novel's most condensed theological statement. He moves from "to love, honor, and obey, forsaking all others" to "Man that is born of woman...hath but a short time to live" to "earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust, brother to brother, deep calling to deep" without the flicker of an eyelid, and then ends with "Amen, amen, amen. Strike up the music! On with the dance." The grief as permanent weather pillar and the illusion as primary pillar converge here in a single liturgical form: the Christian ceremonies for the two great thresholds of human life, marriage and death, are revealed as structurally identical, which is Young's most honest theological claim. Both ceremonies ask the participants to stand before the irreversible and say words that acknowledge it and then continue. Both are performed by someone in a robe who does not know the participants personally. Both end with a meal. The difference between them is the direction of the transition, and Young's preacher, moving through both simultaneously, makes the direction seem less important than the ceremony itself, less important than the standing together in the face of what cannot be stopped.

"We were created by the dragonfly dropping upon its silken cable line. We were created by the images of the uncreated creation. We were created by a falling star singing as it fell. We were created by a shadow moving where the shadow increased." This is Mr. Spitzer's counter-Genesis, his alternative to "In the beginning God created," and it is the recursive construction pillar at its most theologically ambitious. Creation in Young is not a single act by a single author but an endless process of being-called-into-being by particular instances of beauty and contingency, the dragonfly, the star, the shadow. God's absence from this account is not denial but redistribution: the creative force is not concentrated in a single sovereign being but dispersed through every particular thing that has ever been beautiful enough or strange enough to make another being suddenly aware of existing. This is the theological claim the whole novel has been building toward, and it arrives not as argument but as list, as the same recursive accumulation by which Young constructs every truth the novel offers: not this or that explanation, but this and this and this and this, until the weight of the particular instances becomes its own kind of faith.
