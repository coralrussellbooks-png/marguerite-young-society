Definition from research

Chapter 5

  • The folk song "Go Tell Aunt Rhody" (also known as "The Old Gray Goose is Dead") has a rich history rooted in both European and American traditions. The melody originated as a gavotte in Jean-Jacques Rousseau's 1752 opera Le Devin du Village. An English version of the opera was produced in London in 1766, and the tune subsequently inspired various English lyrics, including "Sweet Melissa". Around 1825, the tune began appearing in U.S. hymnals, and the Aunt Rhody version, a common American folk song, has appeared in numerous song anthologies. The lyrics tell the story of a gray goose that is being saved to make a feather bed, but dies. The song's lyrics are often interpreted as expressing grief or loss, specifically the death of a loved one, but it's also been suggested that the goose's death is a matter of gain for those who need feathers for their beds. (Wikipedia) In prison slang, "grey goose" refers to the prison bus that transports inmates. "The old gray goose is dead" symbolizes the pervasive presence of death and the fragility of life in the narrative. It underscores the chapter’s mood of desolation, the cyclical nature of existence, and perhaps the inevitability of loss that haunts the characters on their journey.

Chapter 6

  • “the pale girl dancing herself to death”: In the ballet Giselle (1841) the wilis are the ghosts of brides who died before their weddings and dance men to death.
  • “They were playing taps for the World War soldiers”: General Daniel Butterfield set “Taps” in 1862, and it became the bugle call at military funerals.
  • “Jenny Lind”: The “Swedish Nightingale” toured America for P. T. Barnum in 1850-1852.
  • “Little Sir Echo! How do you do?”: The children’s song (1917) was a hit again in 1939. In myth the nymph Echo pined for Narcissus until only her voice was left.
  • “taking them all over the hill and far away”: “Over the Hills and Far Away” is an old soldiers’ song, sung in The Beggar’s Opera (1728).
  • “Death was always the bridegroom”: Death and the Maiden, the skeleton embracing the girl, was a favorite subject of Renaissance painters and of Schubert’s song (1817).
  • “The sunny side of the street”: “On the Sunny Side of the Street” (1930) was a Depression-era hit.

Chapter 7

  • “He said it was Mozart”: Mozart was a prolific and influential composer of the Classical period. Despite his short life, his rapid pace of composition and proficiency from an early age resulted in more than 800 works representing virtually every Western classical genre of his time. (Wikipedia) Mozart died at thirty-five in 1791 while writing his Requiem.
  • “the fringe is frozen on the surrey top”: “The Surrey with the Fringe on Top,” from Oklahoma! (1943).
  • “a hot time in the old town next Saturday night”: “A Hot Time in the Old Town” (1896) became the marching song of the Spanish-American War.

Chapter 8

  • “Where was Miss MacIntosh, my darling, now aloud I cried?”: The novel’s title may echo the old ballad “Oh My Darling, Clementine,” whose Clementine is lost beneath the water.

Chapter 9

  • “Pythagoras who had a golden thigh”: Pythagoras was an ancient Greek philosopher. It is believed his teachings were metempsychosis, that every soul is immortal and after death enters another body, and musica universalis where the planets move according to mathematical equations and create an inaudible symphony of music. Legend says he showed a golden thigh at Olympia as a sign of his divinity.
  • “What makes you think they are not one?”: Love and death as one is the Liebestod, the love-death of Wagner’s Tristan und Isolde (1865).
  • “the Islands of the Dead”: Arnold Böcklin’s painting Isle of the Dead (1880-1886), a rocky island of cypresses with a white-draped figure arriving by boat, inspired Rachmaninoff’s tone poem (1909).

Chapter 10

  • “it was a funeral march which was played at a perfect wedding”: Chopin’s Funeral March (Piano Sonata No. 2, 1839) in place of Mendelssohn’s Wedding March (1842) or Wagner’s “Here Comes the Bride” (Lohengrin, 1850).

Chapter 11

  • “Rimski-Korsakov who never came except in Mr. Spitzer’s absence”: Rimski-Korsakov was a prominent Russian composer, a member of the Mighty Handful, five composers who drew on Russian folk music, and a significant figure in shaping Russian classical music. He is known for his operas, orchestral pieces like Scheherazade (1888), named for the bride who tells a tale night after night to stay alive, and Capriccio Espagnol. He sailed around the world as a young naval officer.

Chapter 12

  • “the reincarnation of Chopin”: Chopin wrote some sixty mazurkas, the Polish dance. The polka is Czech, and he wrote none.

Chapter 14

  • The teaching most securely identified with Pythagoras is the “transmigration of souls” or metempsychosis, which holds that every soul is immortal and, upon death, enters into a new body. He may have also devised the doctrine of musica universalis, which holds that the planets move according to mathematical equations and thus resonate to produce an inaudible symphony of music. (Wikipedia)
  • “the plural melody which was not Mr. Spitzer’s composition or choice”: His “melody moving or attended by one or more related but independent arrangements of sounds” is nearly the dictionary definition of polyphony, or counterpoint, several independent melodies sounding at once.
  • “the funeral gondola with the corrugated angel at its helm”: Venice carries its dead by black gondola to the cemetery island of San Michele. Liszt wrote “La lugubre gondola” (1882) foreseeing Wagner’s death in Venice, and Wagner’s body left by gondola in 1883.

Chapter 19

  • “the bust of Martin Luther”: Martin Luther (1483-1546) was a German professor of theology, priest, Augustinian monk, and hymn writer, and the central figure of the Reformation. (Wikipedia) His “A Mighty Fortress Is Our God” (c. 1529) is still sung in Protestant churches. Luther himself opposed the image-breakers of 1522, but the Reformation he began stripped many churches bare, as Miss MacIntosh strips her room. Miss MacIntosh quoted a line attributed to him at dawn in Chapter 18.

Chapter 24

  • “Sir Christopher Wren and Jenny Lind were to be put into the rose”: Sir Christopher Wren was a highly acclaimed English architect, astronomer, and mathematician, the builder of St Paul’s Cathedral (1675-1710). Jenny Lind, “the Swedish Nightingale,” was a Swedish opera singer of the 19th century who toured America for P. T. Barnum in 1850-52.

Chapter 25

  • “for they were as fickle as wind or water or woman”: The Duke’s aria in Verdi’s Rigoletto (1851): “La donna è mobile / qual piuma al vento” (“Woman is fickle, like a feather in the wind”).
  • “the lost chords, the silent keys, the broken strings”: Adelaide Procter’s poem “A Lost Chord” (1858), set by Arthur Sullivan as “The Lost Chord” (1877): “I struck one chord of music, / Like the sound of a great Amen.”

Chapter 26

  • “A cat had walked up and down on the piano keys, playing a little tune from the works of one of the more obscure eighteenth-century musical composers”: Domenico Scarlatti’s Sonata in G minor, K. 30, has been called “The Cat’s Fugue” since the nineteenth century, after the story that his cat walked across the keyboard.

Chapter 29

  • “Only his silent music”: There is such a thing as “silent music” that was done by several musicians as experimental. John Cage’s 4’33” (1952) is an example. Mr. Spitzer “had written only silent music, that which, for many years now, no one else had ever heard.”
  • “He swore that their chant would be almost Gregorian”: Gregorian chant is a form of monophonic, unaccompanied sacred song in Latin of the Roman Catholic Church. (Wikipedia)

Chapter 33

  • “these dream fugues, these polyphonic compositions”: A fugue is a composition in which voices enter one after another on the same theme, as in Bach’s The Art of Fugue. In psychiatry a fugue is also a flight from one’s own identity.

Chapter 35

  • “the dance of the hours”: In Greek myth the Horae, the Hours, were goddesses of the seasons and of order. Ponchielli’s ballet “Dance of the Hours,” from his opera La Gioconda (1876), shows the hours of dawn, day, evening, and night.

Chapter 36

  • “Not every lost chord could be resolved”: “Seated one day at the organ, / I was weary and ill at ease… But I struck one chord of music, / Like the sound of a great Amen” (Adelaide Anne Procter, “A Lost Chord,” 1858). Arthur Sullivan set it as “The Lost Chord” in 1877.

Chapter 38

  • “a funeral gondola black as a blackbird”: Franz Liszt wrote La lugubre gondola, “the funeral gondola,” in Venice in 1882, and the next year a gondola carried Wagner’s coffin down the Grand Canal.
  • “her grand idée fixe”: An idée fixe is an obsession. Berlioz gave the name to the melody that haunts his Symphonie fantastique (1830).
  • “Mr. Spitzer writing now his nocturne in memory of her”: The nocturne, a night piece for piano, was invented by John Field in 1812 and perfected by Chopin.

Chapter 40

  • “lutenists who had known Mozart who had not known them”: By Mozart’s day the lute had nearly fallen out of use.
  • “the errant somnambulist”: In Bellini’s opera La Sonnambula (1831), the sleepwalking Amina crosses the high, broken bridge of a mill.

Chapter 42

  • “He had sought for the elements of harmony, but this was chaos”: Aristoxenus wrote the Elements of Harmony in the fourth century BC, the oldest Greek treatise on music to survive.
  • “Here comes the bride”: The tune is the Bridal Chorus from Wagner’s Lohengrin (1850).
  • “how fickle, how irresponsible this great lady was”: Francis I, King of France, is credited with saying, “Woman is always fickle; foolish is he who trusts her.” Brantôme says Francis I scratched “Souvent femme varie, bien fol est qui s’y fie” on a window at Chambord. Victor Hugo gave the line to the king in Le roi s’amuse (1832), and Verdi turned it into “La donna è mobile” in Rigoletto (1851).
  • “What mad dance was life, and what madder dance was death”: The dance of death, the medieval Danse Macabre, in which Death leads every rank of the living, became Saint-Saëns’s tone poem Danse macabre (1874).
  • “the strength of this snow maiden”: In the Russian tale of Snegurochka, the Snow Maiden, she melts when love enters her heart (Alexander Ostrovsky’s play, 1873; Rimsky-Korsakov’s opera, 1882).

Chapter 43

  • “this pale Don Juan beneath her mask”: Don Juan is a legendary, fictional Spanish literature character. He first appeared in a 1630 play, Tirso de Molina’s The Trickster of Seville, and probably most famously in the opera Don Giovanni by Mozart. He was a libertine, womanizer, arrogant, unrepentant, and was punished for these sins. Mr. Spitzer wonders whether Hannah had always been “this pale Don Juan beneath her mask,” the great seducer hidden inside the woman who refused love.
  • “my wild swan girl”: In folklore the swan maiden sheds her feathered robe to become a woman, and whoever steals the robe can keep her. Tchaikovsky’s Swan Lake (1877) gives the story its best-known form.
  • “Are those the music-makers”: “We are the music makers, / And we are the dreamers of dreams” (Arthur O’Shaughnessy, “Ode,” 1873).

Chapter 44

  • “a musician whose baton had gotten loose from him”: Goethe’s “The Sorcerer’s Apprentice” (1797) starts a magic he cannot stop. Paul Dukas set it to music (1897), and in Disney’s Fantasia (1940) Mickey Mouse dreams that he conducts the stars and the waves.
  • “Must not the lover die of love?”: See the Liebestod, the love-death of Wagner’s Tristan und Isolde, in the Chapter 9 resources.

Chapter 45

  • “There was a rift in his lute.”: “It is the little rift within the lute, / That by and by will make the music mute” (Alfred Tennyson, “Merlin and Vivien,” Idylls of the King, 1859).
  • “His music was made almost entirely of lost chords.”: See Adelaide Procter’s “The Lost Chord” in the Chapter 25 resources.

Chapter 47

  • “Death was that dark lover who drew her down”: See Death and the Maiden in the Chapter 33 resources.

Chapter 48

  • “Perhaps the greatest music was that which had no meaning”: “All art constantly aspires towards the condition of music” (Walter Pater, “The School of Giorgione,” The Renaissance, 1873), the art in which form and meaning cannot be told apart.

Chapter 49

  • “perhaps only one grace note”: A grace note is a musical ornament, a brief note that precedes and embellishes the main note in a musical passage. It means a small, delicate, and ornamental component of a larger composition of life and death as a metaphor for the subtle, transient experiences and nuances that enrich the human condition but are often overlooked or not fully grasped.
  • “His was this barcarolle heard not by the singer”: A barcarolle is a Venetian gondolier’s song in a rocking rhythm, like Offenbach’s Barcarolle in The Tales of Hoffmann (1881); he “had rocked them on the waves.”
  • “What cenotaph was there for him”: Mozart was buried in 1791 in a common grave at St. Marx in Vienna whose place was soon lost; a memorial stands near the likely spot, and a monument was raised to him in Vienna’s Central Cemetery in 1891.

Chapter 50

  • “So he had written this nocturne”: The Irish composer John Field published the first nocturnes in 1812, and Chopin made the form his own. The chapter closes on this night music.

Chapter 51

  • “two pianos in the silence”: John Cage’s 4′33″ (1952), first played by the pianist David Tudor, is four minutes and thirty-three seconds in which the performer plays nothing, so that the music is the sound of the room.
  • “the squawking of a fire bird”: Igor Stravinsky’s ballet The Firebird (1910) is the tale of a magical glowing bird of Russian folklore.
  • “the most famous music critics were the deaf”: Beethoven was nearly deaf when his Ninth Symphony was first played in 1824, and he had to be turned around to see the applause.

Chapter 52

  • “that old knight Tancred”: Tancred, a hero of the First Crusade, is the knight of Torquato Tasso’s epic Jerusalem Delivered (1581). He loves the pagan warrior maiden Clorinda and, not knowing her in her armor, kills her in single combat at night, baptizing her as she dies. In Canto 13, when the crusaders need timber from an enchanted forest, Tancred strikes a tall cypress; blood flows from the cut, and Clorinda’s voice cries from inside the tree that he wounds her still. Claudio Monteverdi set the duel as Il combattimento di Tancredi e Clorinda (1624). Clorinda was never Tancred’s wife; Young makes her one.

Chapter 53

  • It may refer to the artistic and literary theme of the Danse Macabre, also known as the Dance of Death. This theme, popular in the late medieval period, depicts death, often personified by a skeleton, leading people of all social classes in a dance to their graves. It serves as a reminder of the inevitability of death for everyone. This artistic and literary motif emerged in the late medieval period, particularly following the Black Death, to remind people of their mortality. Death is often portrayed as a skeleton or a figure of decay, leading people from all walks of life in a procession to the grave. The Danse Macabre highlights the idea that death is a great equalizer, affecting everyone regardless of social status or wealth. The theme is expressed in various forms of art, including paintings, woodcuts, and literature. Camille Saint-Saëns wrote the musical composition, “Danse Macabre,” which is a symphonic poem inspired by the theme.

Chapter 54

  • It is an American song published in 1926 and composed by Ray Henderson with lyrics by Mort Dixon. It became popular through early recordings by Gene Austin and was a hit in Europe. The song was later adapted for propaganda during World War II, though the melody remained the same. The song has been recorded by countless artists, including Peggy Lee and Miles Davis, and was covered by Ringo Starr and Paul McCartney on their respective albums. The song was written by American composer Ray Henderson and lyricist Mort Dixon, who were famous in the golden age of American musicals. The song was recorded in 1926 by Gene Austin, Bennie Kruger’s orchestra, and Nick Lucas, with Gene Austin’s version topping the charts. “Bye Bye Blackbird” gained significant popularity in Europe before World War II, partly due to Josephine Baker’s 1927 recording in Paris. The melody was later adapted by German military bands, such as Charlie and His Orchestra, for propaganda purposes during World War II. The lyrics were changed to demolarize Allied troops. The song’s melody became controversial in the context of the American civil rights movement, with some segregationists playing it to harass civil rights marchers. After a period of limited availability in the US after the war, the song regained traction after being featured in the 1954 film The Eddie Cantor Story and the 1955 film Pete Kelly’s Blues. Jazz musician Miles Davis recorded the song numerous times, with his 1956 performance on his album ‘Round About Midnight being particularly influential and a gateway into modern jazz for many musicians. The song continued to be covered by various artists, including Peggy Lee in 1955.

Chapter 55

  • “The other great composers had written only their ninth symphonies”: Curse of the ninth is a superstition connected with classical music. It is the belief that the ninth symphony is destined to be the composer’s last. After finishing it the composer is fated to die before finishing a tenth. Beethoven, Dvořák, and Bruckner each died with nine numbered symphonies, and Gustav Mahler, who feared the curse, left his Tenth unfinished at his death in 1911.
  • “The divine Melba sings”: The divine Melba sings may refer to Dame Nellie Melba who was an operatic soprano.
  • “Jenny Lind will sing tonight”: Jenny Lind may refer to Johanna Maria “Jenny” Lind who was a Swedish opera singer. P. T. Barnum brought her to America for a tour that opened at Castle Garden, New York, in September 1850.
  • “First American performance of Ole Bull”: Ole Bull may refer to Ole Bornemann Bull who was a Norwegian virtuoso violinist and composer. He first toured America in 1843 and in 1852 founded Oleana, a short-lived Norwegian colony in Pennsylvania.
  • “when he had found himself talking to Mozart”: “Mozart” Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart was a prolific and influential composer of the Classical period. Despite his short life, his rapid pace of composition and proficiency from an early age resulted in more than 800 works representing virtually every Western classical genre of his time. (Wikipedia) Many of these compositions are acknowledged as pinnacles of the symphonic, concertante, chamber, operatic, and choral repertoires. Mozart is widely regarded as one of the greatest composers in the history of Western music, with his music admired for its “melodic beauty, its formal elegance and its richness of harmony and texture.” Born in Salzburg, Mozart showed prodigious ability from his earliest childhood. At age five, he was already competent on keyboard and violin, had begun to compose, and performed before European royalty. His father, Leopold Mozart, took him on a grand tour of Europe and then three trips to Italy. At 17, he was a musician at the Salzburg court but grew restless and travelled in search of a better position. Mozart’s search for employment led to positions in Paris, Mannheim, Munich, and again in Salzburg, during which he wrote his five violin concertos, Sinfonia Concertante, and Concerto for Flute and Harp, as well as sacred pieces and masses, the motet Exsultate Jubilate, and the opera Idomeneo, among other works. While visiting Vienna in 1781, Mozart was dismissed from his Salzburg position. He stayed in Vienna, where he achieved fame but little financial security. During Mozart’s early years in Vienna, he produced several notable works, such as the opera Die Entführung aus dem Serail, the Great Mass in C minor, the “Haydn” Quartets and a number of symphonies. Throughout his Vienna years, Mozart composed over a dozen piano concertos, many considered some of his greatest achievements. In the final years of his life, Mozart wrote many of his best-known works, including his last three symphonies, culminating in the Jupiter Symphony, the serenade Eine kleine Nachtmusik, his Clarinet Concerto, the operas The Marriage of Figaro, Don Giovanni, Così fan tutte and The Magic Flute and his Requiem. The Requiem was largely unfinished at the time of his death at age 35, the circumstances of which are uncertain and much mythologised. His elder sister, Maria Anna Walburga Ignatia “Marianne” Mozart, nicknamed Nannerl, was a highly regarded musician from Salzburg, Austria. In her childhood, she developed into an outstanding keyboard player under the tutelage of her father Leopold. She became a celebrated child prodigy and went on concert tours through much of Europe with her parents and her younger brother Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart. At age 17, her career as a touring musician came to an end, though she continued to work at home teaching piano and performing on occasion. At age 33 she married, moved to a village six hours by carriage from Salzburg, and there raised her own and her husband’s children. On her widowhood in 1801, she returned to Salzburg and resumed teaching and performance. She is known to have composed works of music, though no manuscripts survive. In her later years she contributed to the biographical study of her late brother. (Wikipedia)

Chapter 56

  • “though music should describe, his brother had so often reminded him, no image”: The quarrel is real. Eduard Hanslick argued in On the Beautiful in Music (1854) that music represents no images, only “tonally moving forms,” while program music painted scenes: in Saint-Saëns’s Danse macabre (1874), Death plays his fiddle in the graveyard at midnight and the oboe crows the cock’s crow at dawn.
  • “the golden cockerel weathervane”: In Pushkin’s “The Tale of the Golden Cockerel” (1834), a golden cockerel on a spire crows to warn the tsar of danger; Rimsky-Korsakov made it an opera (1909).

Chapter 57

  • “the music of the hurdy-gurdy”: The true hurdy-gurdy is a stringed instrument sounded by a cranked wheel; in America the name went to the barrel organs of street musicians.

Chapter 58

  • “a minor composition by one of the lesser-known nineteenth-century composers”: Young names no piece; the waltz is lost, remembered only by Mr. Spitzer.
  • “embalmed in old music boxes”: The Geneva watchmaker Antoine Favre made the first comb-and-pin music movement in 1796, and Swiss cylinder music boxes carried the waltzes and opera airs of the nineteenth century into parlors everywhere.
  • “such as he might have composed for a black keyboard only”: In Chopin’s “Black Key” Étude (Opus 10, No. 5, 1830) the right hand plays only on the black keys.

Chapter 59

  • “old torch singers singing under the city arc lamps”: A torch song is a lament for a love that is not returned; to “carry a torch” is to go on loving someone who does not love back. Helen Morgan, Libby Holman, and Ruth Etting were famous torch singers of the 1920s and 1930s.
  • “the bells of the drowned cathedrals”: In Breton legend the city of Ys was drowned for its sins, and on calm mornings its cathedral rises from the sea with its bells tolling. Claude Debussy set the legend in “La cathédrale engloutie” (“The Sunken Cathedral,” Préludes, Book 1, 1910).
  • “the sign marking the beginning or the end of a repeat”: Young defines the segno, the sign in a score that marks where a repeat begins or ends; “dal segno” (D.S.) means “from the sign.”
  • “that old cue-ball musician who was writing a symphony for musical billiard balls”: Composers have long played with chance. The Musikalisches Würfelspiel, a dice game for composing waltzes, was published under Mozart’s name in 1792, and John Cage composed his Music of Changes (1951) by tossing coins according to the I Ching. Mr. Spitzer’s verdict: “And if chance, it was not music.”

Chapter 60

  • “whistle like Mozart in the silent hours”: See Mozart in the Chapter 55 resources. At fourteen Mozart heard Allegri’s Miserere in the Sistine Chapel and wrote it out from memory (1770).
  • “like an unfinished fugue running through his mind”: A “fugue” has two main meanings: a complex musical composition where a theme is repeated and interwoven by multiple voices, and a temporary mental state of amnesia and wandering, often associated with dissociative disorders. Musically, it involves a single theme being introduced by one voice and then imitated by others in a structured, imitative style. Psychologically, a dissociative fugue is a condition where an individual forgets their past and may travel, only to have no memory of the period later. Bach’s Art of Fugue breaks off in its last fugue; he died in 1750 before finishing it.
  • “many antiphonies”: “Antiphonies” are a type of responsive singing or music between two groups, creating a “call and response” effect. The term can also refer to a verse sung before and after a psalm or hymn, often used in religious services.

Chapter 61

  • “an Enrico Caruso among the frogs”: Enrico Caruso was a famous Italian operatic tenor. His records made him the first great star of the phonograph; he died in 1921.
  • “an idee fixe”: In Berlioz’s Symphonie fantastique (1830), the idée fixe is the melody that stands for the beloved and returns, changed, in every movement.

Chapter 62

  • “many golden cockerels flapping their wings and crowing on singing weathervanes”: Compare Rimsky-Korsakov’s opera The Golden Cockerel (first performed 1909), whose golden bird crows warnings from its perch.
  • “he was the great conductor conducting the water music”: Handel’s Water Music was first played on a barge on the Thames in July 1717, for King George I, who had it repeated three times.
  • “his barcarolle which was his elegy”: A barcarolle is a folk song of Venetian gondoliers, which is imitated in classical music to create a gently rocking rhythm, often in 6/8 or 12/8 time. The most famous examples are Jacques Offenbach’s “Barcarolle” from the opera The Tales of Hoffmann and Frédéric Chopin’s Barcarolle, Op. 60 for solo piano.
  • “had not many great composers written their works with visual patterns none could play”: Compare eye music, written to be seen rather than heard: Baude Cordier’s chanson “Belle, bonne, sage” (c. 1400) is written in the shape of a heart, and Renaissance composers blackened their notes for words of night and death.
  • “Pavanne for a Dead Lady”: Compare Maurice Ravel’s Pavane pour une infante défunte, “Pavane for a Dead Princess” (1899).
  • “a member of the Philadelphia Symphony”: The Philadelphia Orchestra was founded in 1900 by Fritz Scheel, evolving into one of America’s most prominent symphony orchestras. Key figures in its history include conductors Leopold Stokowski and Eugene Ormandy, who shaped its sound and recorded extensively. The orchestra is known for its history of innovation, including being a leader in orchestral recordings and tours, and for forging a close relationship with composer Sergei Rachmaninoff.

Chapter 64

  • “a barber of Seville”: The Barber of Seville is considered one of the greatest masterpieces of comedy in music known as an opera buffa. Rossini’s opera (1816) is based on Beaumarchais’s play (1775).

Chapter 65

  • “Love lifted me”: The hymn “Love Lifted Me,” written by James Rowe in 1912 with music by Howard E. Smith, tells a story of salvation using the biblical account of Peter walking on water (Matthew 14) as a metaphor for being pulled from sin by Jesus’s love, with Smith composing the melody despite severe arthritis, creating a powerful testament to divine rescue and devotion.

Chapter 67

  • “I can’t give you anything but love, baby”: I Can’t Give You Anything But Love, Baby is an American song from 1928. The idea behind the song came during a stroll Fields and McHugh were taking one evening down Fifth Avenue. They saw a young couple window-shopping at Tiffany’s. McHugh and Fields understood that the couple could not afford to buy jewelry from Tiffany’s. They heard the man say, “Gee, honey, I’d like to get you a sparkler like that, but right now, I can’t give you nothin’ but love!” Within an hour McHugh and Fields came up with the song. The lyricist was Dorothy Fields, one of the first women to succeed as a Broadway and Hollywood songwriter, with Jimmy McHugh; the song was introduced in the all-Black revue Blackbirds of 1928.
  • “Sweet Mary O’Grady”: Moses sings Sweet Mary O’Grady but the famous song is called Sweet Rosie O’Grady. “Sweet Rosie O’Grady” (1896) was written by Maude Nugent, a vaudeville singer, and “down by the old mill stream where I first met you” is from Tell Taylor’s “Down by the Old Mill Stream” (1910); Moses runs the two together.
  • “There was going to be a hot time in the old town tonight”: “There’ll Be a Hot Time in the Old Town Tonight” (1896), by Theodore Metz and Joe Hayden, became the marching song of Theodore Roosevelt’s Rough Riders in 1898.

Chapter 71

  • “the Wabash country”: The Wabash runs across Indiana to the Ohio. Paul Dresser’s “On the Banks of the Wabash, Far Away” (1897) became the state song in 1913.

Chapter 72

  • “The jukebox repairman”: The jukebox, a nickel a record, filled the taverns and diners of the 1930s and 1940s.

Chapter 74

  • “she was only a silent piano with dusty keys”: In the Chapter 72 resources Esther insists, “I am a white piano.”

Chapter 76

  • “a sunken ship of leaden sails and stone hull and stone bells”: See Debussy’s Sunken Cathedral and the drowned city of Ys, whose bells ring under the sea, in the Chapter 59 resources.
  • “the lost music of the lost echo of the lost chord”: “Seated one day at the organ, / I was weary and ill at ease, / And my fingers wandered idly / Over the noisy keys” (Adelaide Anne Procter, “A Lost Chord,” 1858). Arthur Sullivan set it in 1877 as “The Lost Chord,” one of the best-selling songs of the century.

Chapter 78

  • “being a white piano in a tinkling stream”: “I am a white piano,” Esther says in the Chapter 72 resources; in the Chapter 74 resources she is “only a silent piano with dusty keys.”

Chapter 79

  • “a gramophone was playing”: The gramophone, invented by Emil Berliner in 1887, is an early, mechanical, analog sound reproduction device that plays flat, spirally-grooved discs (shellac records). It uses a stylus and horn to amplify sound mechanically, without electricity. As a precursor to the modern turntable, it revolutionized music by allowing mass production of recordings, distinguishing itself from cylinder-based phonographs.

Chapter 81

  • “struck all the silent keys”: See “I am a white piano” in the Chapter 72 resources.
  • “more beautiful than Bach, Beethoven, or Brahms”: Johann Sebastian Bach was a German composer and musician of the late Baroque period. He is known for his prolific output across a variety of instruments and forms, including the orchestral Brandenburg Concertos; solo instrumental works such as the cello suites and sonatas and partitas for solo violin; keyboard works such as the Goldberg Variations and The Well-Tempered Clavier; organ works such as the Schübler Chorales and the Toccata and Fugue in D minor; and choral works such as the St Matthew Passion and the Mass in B minor. He is known for his mastery of counterpoint, as heard in The Musical Offering and The Art of Fugue. Felix Mendelssohn precipitated the Bach Revival with a performance of the St. Matthew Passion in 1829. Ever since, Bach has been known as one of the greatest composers of classical music.
