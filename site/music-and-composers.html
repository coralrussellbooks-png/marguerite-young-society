Chapter 5

  • The folk song "Go Tell Aunt Rhody" (also known as "The Old Gray Goose is Dead") has a rich history rooted in both European and American traditions. The melody originated as a gavotte in Jean-Jacques Rousseau's 1752 opera Le Devin du Village. An English version of the opera was produced in London in 1766, and the tune subsequently inspired various English lyrics, including "Sweet Melissa". Around 1825, the tune began appearing in U.S. hymnals, and the Aunt Rhody version, a common American folk song, has appeared in numerous song anthologies. The lyrics tell the story of a gray goose that is being saved to make a feather bed, but dies. The song's lyrics are often interpreted as expressing grief or loss, specifically the death of a loved one, but it's also been suggested that the goose's death is a matter of gain for those who need feathers for their beds. In prison slang, "grey goose" refers to the prison bus that transports inmates. "The old gray goose is dead" symbolizes the pervasive presence of death and the fragility of life in the narrative. It underscores the chapter’s mood of desolation, the cyclical nature of existence, and perhaps the inevitability of loss that haunts the characters on their journey.

Chapter 26

  • “Musica Universalis” is a metaphysical principle by Boethius that uses mathematical relationships to express ‘tones’ that could be heard by the soul. The "Musica Universalis" here symbolizes the harmonious and eternal cosmic order that underlies the apparent chaos of mortal life and death. It is depicted as a sublime, transcendent music that governs the movements of the stars and planets, reflecting a divine harmony beyond human perception. Mr. Spitzer, a semi-retired lawyer and composer of "silent music," embodies this principle as someone who is attuned to the "music of the spheres" or the eternal, silent harmonies that exist beyond earthly sounds. His compositions, unheard by living ears and played only for the dead or in the celestial realm, metaphorically represent this cosmic music. The chapter emphasizes the contrast between earthly, temporal music and this higher, immutable harmony, suggesting that the tangible world is but a fragment or shadow of a greater metaphysical reality. Catherine's opium dreams expand this theme by blurring the boundaries between the real and the illusory, the living and the dead, reflecting the ineffable nature of the universal music that ties all existence together. The "Musica Universalis" is not just an abstract concept but a living presence in her visions with images of angels harping, celestial choirs, and cosmic rhythms permeating her consciousness. The chapter suggests that this music is intertwined with suffering and mortality (the "music of the perfect silence beyond the chance of the brittle and falling stars") highlighting a metaphysical view where life, death, and cosmic order are inseparable parts of a greater whole. The "Musica Universalis" is used to articulate a metaphysical principle that all existence, from the smallest earthly details to the vast cosmos, is bound by a harmonious, eternal music. This music transcends the limitations of physical reality and human understanding, offering a vision of unity amid fragmentation and a cosmic order beneath the apparent chaos of life and death.

Chapter 49

  • Peter Abelard was a medieval French scholastic philosopher, leading logician, theologian, teacher, musician, composer, and poet. In philosophy he is celebrated for his proposed logical solution to the problem of universals via nominalism and conceptualism and his pioneering of intent in ethics. Often referred to as the "Descartes of the twelfth century", he is considered a forerunner of Rousseau, Kant, and Spinoza. He is sometimes credited as a chief forerunner of modern empiricism. In Catholic theology, he is best known for his development of the concept of limbo, and his introduction of the moral influence theory of atonement. He is considered (alongside Augustine) to be the most significant forerunner of the modern self-reflective autobiographer. He paved the way and set the tone for later epistolary novels and celebrity tell-alls with his publicly distributed letter, The History of My Calamities and public correspondence. In history and popular culture he is best known for his passionate, tragic love affair and intense philosophical exchange with his brilliant student and eventual wife, Héloïse d'Argenteuil. After they secretly married and went to different convents Heloise’s guardian had Abelard ambushed and castrated. Roscellinus and others made fun of him. He did father a son before that event.

Mr. Spitzer's legal practice is described as existing in a liminal space between life and death. He practices "only in the courts which were paved with the moonstones of lost cases under a firmament of lost stars," evoking a sense of decay, loss, and the ephemeral nature of human endeavors. The images of a "dead moth with its wings trembling" and a "mouse in a paper nest" further emphasize the themes of decay and disorder. The mouse, in particular, embodies the chaotic forces that undermine Mr. Spitzer's attempts to impose order. It gnaws on legal documents and musical manuscripts, "jumbling symphonies with old racing forms," and ultimately becoming "the greatest impresario" by shaking "the long handle of the Great Dipper of stars pouring out little stars." Mr. Spitzer's preference for "the seething silence to the sound, preferring mystery to exposure, preferring a cloak to nakedness" is highlighted. Despite his "great sense of order," he is increasingly drawn to the chaotic and irrational aspects of life, symbolized by the "litigious starfish" and the bees buzzing around his head.

Chapter 53

  • It may refer to the artistic and literary theme of the Danse Macabre, also known as the Dance of Death. This theme, popular in the late medieval period, depicts death, often personified by a skeleton, leading people of all social classes in a dance to their graves. It serves as a reminder of the inevitability of death for everyone. This artistic and literary motif emerged in the late medieval period, particularly following the Black Death, to remind people of their mortality. Death is often portrayed as a skeleton or a figure of decay, leading people from all walks of life in a procession to the grave. The Danse Macabre highlights the idea that death is a great equalizer, affecting everyone regardless of social status or wealth. The theme is expressed in various forms of art, including paintings, woodcuts, and literature. Camille Saint-Saëns wrote the musical composition, "Danse Macabre," which is a symphonic poem inspired by the theme.

Chapter 55

  • “Boston Common” is a public park in downtown Boston, Massachusetts. It is the oldest city park in the United States. Boston Common consists of 50 acres of land bounded by five major Boston streets: Tremont Street, Park Street, Beacon Street, Charles Street, and Boylston Street. The Common is part of the Emerald Necklace of parks and parkways that extend from the Common south to Franklin Park in Jamaica Plain, Roxbury, and Dorchester. The visitors' center for the city of Boston is located on the Tremont Street side of the park. The Central Burying Ground is on the Boylston Street side of Boston Common and contains the graves of artist Gilbert Stuart and composer William Billings. Also buried there are Samuel Sprague and his son Charles Sprague, one of America's earliest poets. Samuel Sprague was a participant in the Boston Tea Party and fought in the Revolutionary War. The Common was designated as a Boston Landmark by the Boston Landmarks Commission in 1977.

Chapter 55

  • “minuet” is a social dance of French origin for two people, usually written in 3/4 time. The English word was adapted from the Italian minuetto and the French minuet. The term also describes the musical form that accompanies the dance, which subsequently developed more fully, often with a longer musical form called the minuet and trio, and was much used as a movement in the early classical symphony. While often stylized in instrumental forms, composers of the period would have been familiar with the popular dance.

Chapter 55

  • “Mozart” Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart was a prolific and influential composer of the Classical period. Despite his short life, his rapid pace of composition and proficiency from an early age resulted in more than 800 works representing virtually every Western classical genre of his time. Many of these compositions are acknowledged as pinnacles of the symphonic, concertante, chamber, operatic, and choral repertoires. Mozart is widely regarded as one of the greatest composers in the history of Western music, with his music admired for its "melodic beauty, its formal elegance and its richness of harmony and texture". Born in Salzburg, Mozart showed prodigious ability from his earliest childhood. At age five, he was already competent on keyboard and violin, had begun to compose, and performed before European royalty. His father, Leopold Mozart, took him on a grand tour of Europe and then three trips to Italy. At 17, he was a musician at the Salzburg court but grew restless and travelled in search of a better position. Mozart's search for employment led to positions in Paris, Mannheim, Munich, and again in Salzburg, during which he wrote his five violin concertos, Sinfonia Concertante, and Concerto for Flute and Harp, as well as sacred pieces and masses, the motet Exsultate Jubilate, and the opera Idomeneo, among other works. While visiting Vienna in 1781, Mozart was dismissed from his Salzburg position. He stayed in Vienna, where he achieved fame but little financial security. During Mozart's early years in Vienna, he produced several notable works, such as the opera Die Entführung aus dem Serail, the Great Mass in C minor, the "Haydn" Quartets and a number of symphonies. Throughout his Vienna years, Mozart composed over a dozen piano concertos, many considered some of his greatest achievements. In the final years of his life, Mozart wrote many of his best-known works, including his last three symphonies, culminating in the Jupiter Symphony, the serenade Eine kleine Nachtmusik, his Clarinet Concerto, the operas The Marriage of Figaro, Don Giovanni, Così fan tutte and The Magic Flute and his Requiem. The Requiem was largely unfinished at the time of his death at age 35, the circumstances of which are uncertain and much mythologised. His elder sister, Maria Anna Walburga Ignatia "Marianne" Mozart, nicknamed Nannerl, was a highly regarded musician from Salzburg, Austria. In her childhood, she developed into an outstanding keyboard player under the tutelage of her father Leopold. She became a celebrated child prodigy and went on concert tours through much of Europe with her parents and her younger brother Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart. At age 17, her career as a touring musician came to an end, though she continued to work at home teaching piano and performing on occasion. At age 33 she married, moved to a village six hours by carriage from Salzburg, and there raised her own and her husband's children. On her widowhood in 1801, she returned to Salzburg and resumed teaching and performance. She is known to have composed works of music, though no manuscripts survive. In her later years she contributed to the biographical study of her late brother.

Chapter 58

  • “minor composition” This may refer to Paul Lincke’s Luna Waltz from 1911. Carl Emil Paul Lincke was a German composer and theater conductor. He is considered the "father" of the Berlin operetta. His well-known compositions include "Berliner Luft" ("Berlin Air"), the unofficial anthem of Berlin, from his operetta Frau Luna; and "The Glow-Worm", from his operetta Lysistrata. Luna Waltz is an instrumental piece adapted from the 1899 operetta Frau Luna and published in the United States in the early 20th century. The waltz, originally a dance tune within the original operetta, became a popular, standalone composition that showcased Lincke's success as a composer of Berlin operettas, a genre he helped define with this work.

Chapter 71

  • "After all, he was an Italian thief" By specifying that the thief was Italian, Catherine invokes a common ethnic stereotype: at that time and in many literary works, Italians were often portrayed as charming, passionate, and sometimes criminal or roguish figures. The label "Italian thief" carries connotations of romanticism, cunning, and a certain exotic, theatrical flair. In the story, this adds a layer of color and character to the thief, distinguishing him from a generic burglar and situating the encounter within a broader cultural imagination. Catherine’s world is heavily dreamlike and infused with fantasy, illusions, and theatricality. The thief’s Italian identity fits this atmosphere, lending a sense of drama and exoticism to the otherwise quiet, snow-blanketed house. It underscores the surreal quality of the encounter: where a criminal becomes a guest, sipping sherry and talking about revolutions, dressed in the finery Catherine provided. By choosing to highlight the thief’s nationality and by outfitting him as a "pale, aristocratic gentleman" with a silk opera hat, white motor coat, and white flowers behind his ears, Catherine asserts a kind of control and agency over the narrative. She transforms the thief from a mere criminal into a figure of aristocratic elegance and revolutionary potential, playing with identities and expectations. The Italian thief is not just a criminal but a symbol of possibility, subversion, and transformation. Catherine is herself a figure caught between reality and dream, between past grandeur and present decay. The Italian thief episode reflects her tendency to blur lines between fact and fantasy, to rewrite narratives with theatrical embellishments. By stressing "Italian," she situates the episode in a world of cultural richness and romantic possibility, contrasting with the harsh realities outside her doors. Catherine’s emphasis on "he was an Italian thief" is a deliberate narrative choice to evoke a particular image and mood: that of a charming, exotic, somewhat romanticized figure who becomes part of her dreamlike, theatrical domain. It reflects her complex psychological state and her inclination to infuse her recollections with drama, fantasy, and symbolic meaning, transforming a criminal intrusion into an almost magical encounter.

Chapter 71

  • The scattered women’s shoes, all mateless, are highly symbolic. Shoes generally suggest journeys, paths, or the presence and absence of people, and the fact that these shoes are mateless highlights a sense of incompleteness, loss, or fragmentation. Each shoe appears to come from a different era and place (white canvas tennis shoes, green opera pumps, old women’s laced shoes, buckskin shoes with buttons, gold Grecian sandals) suggesting a collage of many lives, times, and experiences, all disconnected and isolated in this one room. This could symbolize the scattered, fragmented nature of the doctor’s mind or the fragmented realities of the women who come to him: real or imaginary. The shoes' variety and solitary nature imply absence: where are the wearers? The doctor deals with pregnancies and deliveries, but many of his "patients" are imaginary or unreal, and the mateless shoes evoke the idea of incomplete or failed births, lost children, or the ghostly presence of women who are not fully there. The shoes are remnants or traces, much like the doctor’s practice itself: delivering "imaginary babies" in a "barren countryside where nothing grew." This barren setting and the doctor’s madness emphasize infertility, loss, and the failure of creation or procreation. The doctor’s clothes hanging alone, despite his complaint of having only one suit, add to the sense of mystery and absence. He has left behind his physical presence, yet his garments remain, like a ghost or a symbol of an absent self. The idea of the doctor "naked as death" going about delivering imaginary babies deepens the theme of the blurring between life and death, reality and hallucination.

Chapter 81

  • Johann Sebastian Bach was a German composer and musician of the late Baroque period. He is known for his prolific output across a variety of instruments and forms, including the orchestral Brandenburg Concertos; solo instrumental works such as the cello suites and sonatas and partitas for solo violin; keyboard works such as the Goldberg Variations and The Well-Tempered Clavier; organ works such as the Schübler Chorales and the Toccata and Fugue in D minor; and choral works such as the St Matthew Passion and the Mass in B minor. He is known for his mastery of counterpoint, as heard in The Musical Offering and The Art of Fugue. Felix Mendelssohn precipitated the Bach Revival with a performance of the St. Matthew Passion in 1829. Ever since, Bach has been known as one of the greatest composers of classical music.

Chapter 81

  • Ludwig van Beethoven was a German composer and pianist. One of the most revered figures in the history of Western music, his works rank among the most performed of the classical music repertoire and span the transition from the Classical to the Romantic era. Beethoven's early period, during which he forged his craft, is typically considered to have lasted until 1802. From 1802 to around 1812, his middle period showed an individual development from the styles of Joseph Haydn and Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart, and is sometimes characterised as heroic. During this time, Beethoven began to grow increasingly deaf. In his late period, from 1812 to 1827, he extended his innovations in musical form and expression.

Chapter 81

  • Johannes Brahms was a German composer, virtuoso pianist, and conductor of the mid-Romantic period. His music is noted for its rhythmic vitality and freer treatment of dissonance, often set within studied yet expressive contrapuntal textures. He adapted the traditional structures and techniques of a wide historical range of earlier composers. His œuvre includes four symphonies, four concertos, a Requiem, much chamber music, and hundreds of folk-song arrangements and Lieder, among other works for symphony orchestra, piano, organ, and choir.
