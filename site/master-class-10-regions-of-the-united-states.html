Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Titus Bonebreaker · The Chicago Detective · Vera Cartwheel

The novel is built on a journey with a direction, and the direction has a meaning. Vera boards a bus in the East, where she was raised, and travels west, toward What Cheer, Iowa, the plain town that has become in her mind the place where her life will finally make sense. That is not just a route. It is the reality-versus-illusion dialectic laid across the map of the country. In this novel the East is where the past piles up and the dream thickens, and the West is where Miss MacIntosh swears the real is simply there to be seen with the naked eye. The map makes a promise: travel far enough from the dream and you arrive at the plain, given, unillusioned real.

The whole geography of the book exists to break that promise. Vera never reaches What Cheer. She lands in southern Indiana, and the plain Middle West which was her quest turns out to be as fogbound, decayed, and unreal as any haunted house on the New England coast. The country does not divide into a dreaming East and a solid West. The map that promised a solid West is itself the last illusion to go. What follows moves the way the bus moves, from the East that dreams to the West that was supposed to wake her, and it ends where she ends, in a place that keeps neither promise.

The East: Where the Past Will Not Go Away

Boston

Boston is the novel's capital of the past pressing on the present. It is Catherine's world by lineage, the city of elite social circles and inherited norms, and her retreat into opium and phantoms reads as a refusal of everything Boston expects of her. The city carries an intellectual past too; she imagines herself among "proud Bostonians, Platonists of the period of the intellectual flowering of Athens." But the idealized Boston never appears alone. It is always shadowed by its own decay, the forgotten and dispossessed crowding the same streets as the flowering, so that the city's grandeur and its ruin occupy one frame. Boston exists, characteristically, in a state of twilight, day and night indistinguishable, which is the atmospheric signature of a place where the historical weight of what was keeps intruding on what is. It is less a backdrop than a demonstration that in this novel the past does not recede. It accumulates.

New England

New England is Boston's condition extended to a whole coast. Derelict houses, ruined gardens, deserted shorelines: the region is decay made landscape, the fading of a past and its values rendered as broken hedges and salt-blackened wood. Two forces rule it. The sea is the first, a constant presence that is at once beauty and oblivion, drawing characters toward solace and toward drowning, standing in for the unknowable depths the whole book keeps sounding. The weather is the second, fog and mist and rain that blur the line between the seen and the imagined and keep perception unreliable. Crucially, New England is not simply bleak. Its stark natural beauty sits directly against its human ruin, and that tension, loveliness and dissolution in one view, is the coast's version of the novel's refusal to let opposites resolve.

New York

New York is the East's dream-city, a haven for the lost and a place where nothing is quite what it seems. It is where Vera searches for the vanished Miss MacIntosh in "faceless old beggar women huddled in empty parks" and in "old-fashioned saloons and bowling alleys and billiard parlors and under the falling leaves," at street-preacher corners and in packed ballparks. The city stacks wealth against destitution without comment, "tenements crowded with discrowned kings, rabbit warrens and the rabbits dreaming of the rabbit god," and its buildings read less as architecture than as externalized dreams and memories. It offers connection and delivers isolation, holds out the marginal refugees and keeps them marginal. It is the East's promise of arrival that never quite arrives, a goal always one block further on.

Philadelphia

Philadelphia barely appears, and its thinness is its function. It surfaces almost always in passing, in relation to Boston and New York, a point of origin characters have left rather than a place the book inhabits, the landlord's wife who was a circus fat lady from there, a hangman who once heard the Philadelphia Symphony. Against the fantastical coast and the oceanic Middle West, Philadelphia stands for ordinary, occupational, everyday reality, the world the novel's wanderers have consciously turned their backs on. It carries none of the symbolic weight of the sea-blackened house because it is precisely the un-symbolic, the plain place left behind, and it matters mainly as the flat ground the more haunted places are measured against.

The West: The Real That Was Promised

Iowa

Iowa is the promised land of the plain, and it exists in the novel almost entirely as an idea in Miss MacIntosh's mouth rather than a place on the page. What Cheer, her birthplace, is by her own account no different from any neighboring town, and that ordinariness is the whole point. Iowa stands for stark, unadorned reality, common sense, hard work, a useful life, the world where "reality can be seen even with the naked eye" and no "evil imagination" comes between a person and "the clear reality of simple things." It is the moral compass she holds against Catherine's dream. But the novel refuses to let even her promised land stay idealized. Iowa is also hardship, a "granite road" rather than a "sea road," a place of "necessary deprivations," frozen winters and children's frostbitten ears and farmers breaking themselves on the land. It is at once a lost paradise of innocence Vera can never return to and a place of real deprivation, which is already the first crack in the promise that the plain West is simply better than the dreaming East.

The Far West

The Far West generalizes Iowa's promise into a direction of escape. For Miss MacIntosh the West is the anti-East: no inherited decadence, no weight of history, no "subjective star," just "the clear reality of simple things" and room to lead a useful life. It is where the average is honored and she can call herself "a plain middle brow." But the same crack widens here into open warning. The Far West is also "bare and cold," its houses "small as ruined birds' nests," and the fat lady on the train tells Vera plainly that she is heading somewhere "out of this world," full of "desolation and weeds." The promised land of the plain realm is, on closer look, another landscape of the soul, "ethereal," "bloodless," "dismal," mirroring the same inner turmoil the East did. The escape west turns out to lead to the same interior country.

Chicago

Chicago is the hinge between the plain and the haunted, the grounded city that cannot keep the dream out. It functions as a home base, practical and industrial, the world of factories and the working class, of labor disputes and economic injustice, a transportation hub through which characters pass on their way elsewhere. It shapes Miss MacIntosh's sensible Middle Western outlook and supplies the backstories of the disillusioned and morally compromised like Mr. Bonebreaker and the Chicago detective among them. But Chicago is where the West's claim to plainness starts to fail from within. Even this most grounded, industrial place is infiltrated by the novel's dreamlike atmosphere; its characters carry fantasies and distortions through its practical streets, and its "old" self is set wistfully against its "new." Chicago proves that practicality is no defense against illusion, which is the discovery the whole westward journey is about to make in full.

Seattle

Seattle, glimpsed only a few times, is a wound on the map. It is where Miss MacIntosh's nose was broken in a traffic accident, the injury that leaves her unable to see "the purple hairstreak moths among the evening flowers," a small blindness that quietly marks the woman who preaches clear sight. It is also the far northwest edge toward which James, Catherine's ex-convict chauffeur, drives on his endless journey to Alaska. As the outermost West, it is less a destination than a vanishing point, a place associated with damage and with a departure that never resolves, the far corner past which even the promised West runs out.

The Collapse: Indiana, Where the Map Breaks Its Promise

Then Vera arrives, and the whole geography folds. She was traveling toward the clear Western realm. She stops in southern Indiana, "a state as yet unknown to me," and finds not the plain promised land but a landscape as decayed, ambiguous, and dreamlike as anything the East contained. This is where the map's promise breaks, and the novel stages the breaking with care.

Indiana is Young's own birthplace, the state where she taught, and she gives it none of the romance. The land is "drained and bloodless," as if it had suffered a transfusion. Though it is spring, "it might have been winter still, another planet, the face of the dead moon," the earth "bare and cold and the thorn trees without flower." The roadside is a procession of "old Coca-Cola and aspirin signboards, tattered as tramps," and "curious pieces of rural architecture," an enormous coffee urn open to the sky, a wigwam nightclub with a melancholy tethered buffalo, a leaning tower, Noah's Ark, the old woman who lived in the shoe, incongruous relics that turn the plain Middle West into a surreal museum of broken American dreams. Above all it is "oceanic, dotted by pale pools in the vapors of mist," with "no boundary line, no shore." The land has become the sea. The quality that defined the haunted New England coast, water without a boundary, dissolving the line between land and dream, has reappeared in the heart of the plain interior Miss MacIntosh promised was solid ground.

That reappearance is the argument. The Middle West Vera reaches is not the antidote to the Eastern dream; it is the dream in a different costume. Its people are not the plain, useful Iowans of Miss MacIntosh's creed but racists and anti-Semites, the broken and the dirty, the mentally ill, the poorly schooled, small-town gossips, everything her romantic idea of the interior refused to hold. The plain real, arrived at, is neither plain nor reliably real, and Vera stands in it a stranger, on a "plain of silence" where a "double-headed man" shakes his fist at the passing bus, her own soul's "inexactitude" spread across the fields.

The South, folded into this same southern-Indiana ground, sharpens the collapse by adding the moral weight the dream had let Vera ignore. Here the novel's decay stops being atmospheric and becomes social. This is the country of Esther Longtree, where a Black man must flee north to survive fathering her child, where a white detective counts white children's deaths and not Black ones, where a man is hanged in Kentucky for sleeping with a white woman, where the coal villages stand as "bare-ribbed skeleton houses" and the people are "blackened by coal dust, sweat, and sorrow." The romanticized interior Vera was reaching toward turns out to be a place of racial violence, poverty, and exclusion. The promised land is not innocent. It never was. Reaching it does not deliver the clear real; it delivers the thing the dream of the clear real was built to keep out of sight.

So the map completes the motion the whole book keeps making. Young lays down a country divided into a dreaming East and a waking West, sends her seeker the length of it toward the plain place that will finally be real, and then dissolves the plain place into fog and water and injustice the moment the seeker sets foot in it. There was no What Cheer at the end of the line. There was only Indiana, the ordinary here-and-now, as unreal and as real as everywhere else, which is the only ground anyone was ever standing on.

The turn for a writer is that a map can carry a promise, and a book can be built to break it. Young does not simply set scenes in different regions. She loads the whole geography with a single expectation, that west means real and east means dream, and then she drives a character across it to prove the expectation false. A setting divided by a clear line invites the reader to trust the line, and the deepest thing a writer can do with that trust is spend the length of the journey earning it and then, at the destination, take it away, so the reader arrives where the traveler arrives, having believed in a solid West right up to the moment the land turns to water underfoot.

The Author's Own Map

There is one more thing to notice about this geography, and it is that Young built it out of her own life, then ran it backward.

The three places the novel weighs most heavily are the three places that made her. She was born and raised in Indiana and taught high school English there. She did her graduate work at the University of Iowa. She lived and taught in New York for the rest of her working life. Those are the poles of the book: Vera was raised in the East, questing toward Iowa as the birthplace of the woman she loved, and coming to rest at last in southern Indiana. The emotional map of the novel is drawn on the same three points as the map of Young's life.

But the direction is reversed, and the reversal is the interesting part. Young's own motion was outward and eastward. She left Indiana, went to Iowa, went on to New York, and stayed. Vera travels the other way. She starts in the East, aims at Iowa as a destination rather than a way station, and ends where Young began, in the ordinary Indiana the author herself had left decades before. If Vera's route mirrors anything in Young's biography, it mirrors it in reverse: not the ambitious departure from the plain origin, but the return to it. The author who got out sends her seeker back.

That inversion fits the book too exactly to be only coincidence, whether or not Young intended it. The whole novel is about arriving at the plain origin you fled instead of the magical elsewhere you sought. Vera reaches for What Cheer, the luminous center, and lands in the un-magical Middle West that was the starting point all along. It is fitting, then, that the ground she finally stands on is Young's own home state, the place the novel's maker came from and the place her character is delivered to. The crossing point, the exact here-and-now where a life is actually lived, turns out for Vera to be the author's beginning, reached by traveling the author's road the wrong way down.

The claim here is only about coordinates and direction, which are matters of record, not about intent, which is not. Young may have mapped her life onto the country deliberately, or the places that formed her may simply have been the places she had on hand when she needed a country to build. Either way the shape is on the page: a novel whose geography is its author's biography turned around, so that the journey out becomes the journey home, and the plain place left behind becomes the plain place finally, and only, arrived at.
