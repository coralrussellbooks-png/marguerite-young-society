Part One: The Suffragette and the Socialist

Part of a reading of Harp Song for a Radical (1999), Marguerite Young’s life of Eugene Victor Debs.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 25, 2026

The Suffragette and the Socialist: Cousin Hannah and Eugene Debs · Part Two →

Cousin Hannah, Eugene Debs, and the two crusaders at the center of Marguerite Young’s late work

Marguerite Young spent eighteen years writing Miss MacIntosh, My Darling. Then she spent the last long stretch of her life on a second enormous project, a biography of Eugene Victor Debs that she never finished. Most readers treat the two books separately: one a dream novel, one a history. But a figure stands at the center of each, and the two figures are related.

At the exact middle of the novel stands Cousin Hannah Freemount-Snowden, suffragette, explorer, mountaineer, duelist, crusader. Her chapters, 30 through 52, sit at the midpoint of the book, the string around which everything else is tuned. At the center of Harp Song for a Radical stands Debs: railroad fireman, labor organizer, five-time presidential candidate, a socialist who ran for office from a prison cell.

Both are crusaders. Both fought for people their society refused to count. Both were made from real lives. The society’s master class essay on Hannah describes her as “a real person, a real cousin who really lived and really died.” Debs was very real. He was born in Terre Haute, Indiana, on November 5, 1855, to Jean Daniel Debs and Marguerite Mari Bettrich Debs, who had come to the United States from Colmar, in Alsace. His mother’s name was Marguerite. It is a small coincidence, but Young would have noticed it.

What makes the pairing worth exploring is that Young did not write the two crusaders the same way. Hannah is a warning and a love letter at once. Her cause is just, her courage is real, and her certainty is the thing the novel tests until it cracks. Debs comes out of Harp Song as something different: a man of contradictions who refused to stand above the crowd he fought for.

Dreamers, radicals, and the long American argument over how people should live together were Young’s lifelong subjects. Hannah and Debs are two answers to the same question: what does it mean to fight for others, and what does the fight do to the fighter?
