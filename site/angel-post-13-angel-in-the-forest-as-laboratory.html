Angel in the Forest

Chapter 13. Angel in the Forest as Laboratory

Part of a reading of Angel in the Forest (1945), Marguerite Young’s history of the two failed utopias at New Harmony, Indiana.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 12 · Angel in the Forest: A Reading in Fourteen Chapters · Chapter 14 →

There is a temptation, with a writer’s first book, to read it as a warm-up. Angel in the Forest came out in 1945. Twenty years later Young published Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, a novel of some twelve hundred pages that took eighteen years to write and that a certain kind of reader considers one of the great American books of the century. It is easy to file the first book as apprentice work for the second. That would be a mistake. Angel in the Forest is not a rough draft. It is a laboratory. In it, working with the real history of two failed utopias, Young develops the questions and the methods that Miss MacIntosh will later carry into the interior of a single mind. The history is where she learned how to do it. The novel is where she did it at scale. Nothing essential changes between the two books except the size of the experiment.

You can see this in a single question, because it is the same sentence in both books. In his introduction to Angel in the Forest, Mark Van Doren quotes a line and says it comes from the earlier book but could just as well be in the later one, and probably is: what is the nature of experience, “what dream among dreams is reality?” The sentence is there in Angel, in the opening pages, asked of New Harmony. In Angel the question is asked about history. Which of these visions was real, the Rappite heaven or the Owenite machine, and was either of them any more real than the mud they were built in? In Miss MacIntosh the identical question is asked about consciousness, about one woman’s memory and one woman’s search. The question does not deepen or develop between the books. It is already fully formed in the first one. What changes is only what Young points it at. First history, then a mind.

The catalog

The first method the laboratory produces is the catalog, and it is the most visible thing about Young’s prose. In Angel, the lists organize the historical material. She does not summarize the goods that came out of Rapp’s Harmony. She pours them: wheat, corn, oats, hides, furs, butter, wax, deerskins, hogskins, bearskins, on and on until the sheer quantity of the world defeats any attempt to add it up. This is not a stylistic tic. It is an argument in the shape of a sentence. What Young discovers, writing the histories, is that accumulation is more honest than synthesis. A summary tells you the world adds up to a single point. A list refuses to. The list says: here is more, and more, and more, and it does not resolve into a tidy total, because the world does not. When she comes to write Miss MacIntosh, the same method is waiting, and she turns it on the inside of a person. The catalog that once held deerskins now holds memories, sensations, the endless furniture of a consciousness, and it does the same work it did in the history. It keeps the interior from freezing into a single tidy self, exactly as it kept the history from freezing into a single tidy meaning.

The past beneath the present

The second thing the laboratory produces is a way of handling time. Angel in the Forest is built on the discovery that the past is not behind the present but beneath it, pressing up. The book opens in 1940, on a ferry, and the utopian centuries are not gone from that scene. They are underneath it, in the golden rain trees still growing, in the re-carved footprint, in the Indian graves one layer below the Rappite ones. Past and present are simultaneous, stacked, weighing on one another. This is the eighth pillar, and Angel is where Young learns to write it. Miss MacIntosh then applies the same structure to a single life. Vera Cartwheel, who narrates the novel, stands in the same relation to her own past that New Harmony in 1940 stands to its utopian history. Her memory is not a record of things that are over. It is a set of layers pressing up through the present moment, exactly as the two utopias press up through the filling-station town. Young worked the structure out on a place first, then moved it into a person.

The witness

The third thing, and the deepest, is an ethical position, and Radical Immanence calls it the witness. In Angel, Young trains herself to stay in the room with contradiction and not force it shut. The faith is real and the folly is real, and she will not let one cancel the other. Rapp was a tyrant who castrated his son and a genuine spiritual leader who built a place of real intensity, and both are true, and she holds them both. Owen was a generous visionary and a man whose rational program let children die in the mud, and both are true, and she holds them both. This is very hard to do, and Angel in the Forest is her learning to do it, refusing the historian’s usual move of settling the account. Miss MacIntosh is that stance extended to enormous length, more than a thousand pages of staying present with a single consciousness and its contradictions without once forcing them to resolve. The witness is not a natural talent. It is a discipline, and you can watch her acquire it in the first book so that she can sustain it through the second.

The philosophy she was building

Here is the thing worth saying, at the end of a study that has been circling it. What Young is doing across these two books is not describing a philosophy. She is building one. The eleven pillars this study has mapped onto Angel in the Forest are not a grid pressed down onto her from outside. They are the shape of the thing she constructed, first in the laboratory of the history book and then at full scale in the novel. The plural self. The constructed reality. The grief that accumulates. The witness. The building without foundations. The unfinished that is not failed. The margin that tells the truth. The past beneath rather than behind. The language that decides what is real. The sacred located here, in the ordinary. And the crossing point, the self as the place where everything that made it possible meets everything it will make possible. Radical Immanence is not a lens borrowed to read Young. It is, more nearly, the name for what Young spent her life making, and Angel in the Forest is where she made the first working model.

So the two books are one project at two scales. Angel in the Forest asks what dream among dreams is reality, and asks it of a place and its history. Miss MacIntosh asks the identical question of a mind. The catalog, the simultaneous past, the witness who will not resolve the contradiction, all of it is worked out in the history and then carried, intact, into the fiction. The laboratory was not a lesser thing than the experiment it prepared. It was the place where the experiment became possible. To read Angel in the Forest as a warm-up for Miss MacIntosh is to miss that the questions were already whole, the methods already sharp, the philosophy already standing. What the twenty years bought was not a better set of tools. It was room to use them on the largest possible subject, which for Young was always the same subject: a single dreaming mind, trying to learn which of its dreams is real, never quite arriving, and building anyway.
