Chapter 1: The Nostalgia Trap

Part of The Creative Radical Collection, on Radical Immanence, the philosophy Coral Russell draws from the work of Marguerite Young (1908 to 1995).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

"Memory sometimes provides the one flower more than ever blossomed. Memory sometimes omits the only flower there really was."

Before a demagogue can control your future, he has to rewrite your past.

This is always the first move. Not policy. Not theology. Not even fear. The first move is a photograph, held up like an icon: a factory town in 1958, smoke rising from the mill, a clean street, a family walking out of church. "This is who you were," the strongman says. "And they took it from you."

It doesn't matter that the photograph is a lie. It doesn't matter that the air in that town was killing people, that the church enforced the silence of women, that the prosperity on that street was built on systematic exclusion. None of that survives the edit. The strongman doesn't need the full picture. He only needs your hunger for one.

Every high-control system begins here. The political movement that promises to restore a golden age. The evangelical community that demands a return to original, uncorrupted faith. The end-times congregation that tells you the world you remember was pure and the world you live in now is fallen. The sales pitch is identical across all of them: the present is a disaster, the past was paradise, and only submission to this leader, this doctrine, this movement will take you back.

Your brain is not helping you resist this. Human memory is not a recording. It is an editing suite, and it runs constantly, softening pain, amplifying warmth, and cutting the complicated middle. When you are exhausted and the present feels loud and unnavigable, the edited past feels like oxygen. Manipulators understand this. They step into the exhaustion and hand you a story that makes the panic stop. Your brain does not care that the story is false. It only cares that it works.

The political machinery that runs this operation has been documented, tested, and refined. In Hungary, Viktor Orbán has spent fifteen years building a cultural and educational apparatus designed to produce a single, approved version of Hungarian history, one in which national greatness is always interrupted by foreign corruption, and in which the restoration of that greatness requires the suspension of democratic inconveniences. In the United States, the "Make America Great Again" framework did not describe a real historical period. It described a feeling, specifically the feeling of white male industrial centrality, and it weaponized nostalgia for that feeling into a political program. The facts of the actual postwar decades, the segregation, the Cold War terror, the violent suppression of labor, were not part of the image. They were the part that got cut.

At the intimate scale, the same mechanic runs with the same precision. NXIVM, the organization Keith Raniere built over two decades before his 2019 federal conviction on charges including sex trafficking and racketeering, was a masterclass in manufactured nostalgia. New members were wrapped in intense admiration. They were told they had a gift. That they could be leaders. That they had finally found the place where they truly belonged. That early period of warmth was not accidental. It was documented. It was the bait. As former member Sarah Edmondson has said publicly: no one joins a cult. They join something they believe in. What NXIVM sold in its first act was a memory of your best self, a period of clarity, purpose, and belonging that felt more real than anything you had experienced before. Once that memory existed, it became a leash. Every demand, every escalating act of submission, every piece of "collateral" extracted from members, was framed as the price of returning to that original feeling. The golden age of your membership was always just ahead, recoverable, if only you went deeper.

This is the structure. Create a paradise. Then sell it back to you piece by piece.

The action: The next time you feel the pull of a political movement, a religious community, or any group that asks you to long for something you've lost, write down two columns. First, write the feeling they're selling. Second, write who was not in the photograph. Not as an argument. As a discipline. The past is never as clean as the pitch. If it feels simple, it has been edited.
