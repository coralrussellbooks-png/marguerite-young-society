Unit 8, pp. 153-176: Weitling's later years

← Unit 7 · Harp Song for a Radical: A History Companion · Unit 9 →

Background for this unit: The History in Harp Song for a Radical

After Communia fails, Weitling becomes a New York family man, an immigration clerk, an amateur astronomer, and an inventor cheated of his sewing machine patents. He dies in January 1871, the night after a festival of the new International. Young makes his decline a parable: the prophet of the wageless society ends as a worker beaten by the patent wars of the machine age.

Major entries

Weitling's family and inventions (pp. 161-173)

  • What happened: In 1854 Weitling married Caroline Tödt, a young seamstress whose family had come from Mecklenburg-Schwerin and farmed near Utica, New York (pp. 161-162). They had six children. He worked as a tailor and devised improvements for sewing machines, including an automatic buttonhole attachment. Young records that he applied for patents in October 1861 on nine improvements, and five were granted (p. 168).

  • Why it matters: The sewing machine was one of the first mass-produced household machines, and its makers fought the "Sewing Machine War" of patent lawsuits until they pooled their patents in 1856. Weitling complained to his friend August Schilling that he was being cheated by Singer and the others, and that "whoever was without funds in this country had no chance against those who had funds" (p. 171).

  • His son's name: Weitling named his first son William Wangansky, after a Chinese reformer he admired (p. 166). This was probably Wang Anshi, the eleventh-century Song dynasty minister whose state loans to farmers some European socialists admired. The family shortened it to Wangan.

  • Further reading: Wittke, The Utopian Communist (1950), final chapters.

Weitling's death and burial (pp. 174-176)

  • What happened: On January 24, 1871, Weitling attended a festival of the German, French, Czech, and English sections of the International Workingmen's Association in New York (p. 174). He died the next day. He was buried in a poor section of Greenwood Cemetery in Brooklyn.

  • Why it matters: He died two months before the Paris Commune, when the International he had helped foreshadow reached its height of fame. His obituary in the New York Times remembered him chiefly as an inventor (p. 176).

  • How Young uses it: His lapsed dues to the Journeyman Tailors' Protective and Benevolent Union (p. 174) and his pauper's plot make him one of the "nameless" pioneers Debs wanted remembered.

August Willich (pp. 155, 157)

  • Who he was: A Prussian army officer (1810-1878) who turned communist and fought in the Baden uprising of 1849, with Engels as his aide. In London in 1850 his faction split from Marx's in the Communist League. Marx mocked him in letters to Weydemeyer.

  • In America: He presided at the Shakespeare Hotel banquet where Weitling spoke (p. 155) and edited a German paper in Cincinnati. In the Civil War he rose to brigadier general, commanding the 32nd Indiana, an all-German regiment, and later a brigade.

  • Further reading: David T. Dixon, Radical Warrior: August Willich's Journey from German Revolutionary to Union General (2020).

Wilhelm Keil and the Bethel and Aurora colonies (p. 156)

  • Who he was: A German tailor (1812-1877) who emigrated in 1836, became a mystic healer and Methodist preacher, and broke away to lead his own communal flock. Young says Weitling first met him in Kentucky and found his hellfire preaching distasteful.

  • What happened: Keil founded Bethel, Missouri, in 1844, a prosperous communal town. In 1855 he led a wagon train to Oregon, carrying the body of his son Willie in an alcohol-filled coffin, and founded Aurora, south of Portland, in 1856. Both colonies dissolved after his death.

  • Where the record differs: Young calls Keil a tailor "from Darmstadt" (p. 156). He was born in Prussian Saxony, at Bleicherode.

  • Further reading: Robert J. Hendricks, Bethel and Aurora (1933).

Friedrich Adolph Sorge (pp. 174-175)

  • Who he was: A veteran of the Baden revolution (1828-1906) who came to America in 1852, taught music in Hoboken, New Jersey, and became Marx's and Engels's chief American correspondent.

  • Why it matters: When the International moved its headquarters to New York in 1872, Sorge served as its general secretary. He is often called the father of Marxian socialism in America, the organizational link between the 1848 exiles and the Socialist Labor Party of the 1870s.

  • Further reading: Philip S. Foner and Brewster Chamberlin, eds., Friedrich A. Sorge's Labor Movement in the United States (1977).

Castle Garden (p. 169)

  • What it was: A former fort and concert hall at the Battery in lower Manhattan that served as New York State's immigrant landing depot from 1855 to 1890, before Ellis Island. About eight million immigrants passed through it.

  • Connection: Weitling worked there briefly as a registrar of immigrants. Young notes his pay was a tenth of a superior who left him all the work (p. 169).

Shorter entries

  • Governor Thomas Ford and Governor Lilburn Boggs (p. 154): Ford governed Illinois when Joseph Smith was murdered in the Carthage jail in 1844. Boggs, governor of Missouri, issued the 1838 "Extermination Order" driving the Mormons from the state.

  • Joseph Weydemeyer (pp. 155, 157-159): Marx's ally in America, a rival to Weitling among German workers. He joined the Union army in Missouri.

  • Charles A. Dana (p. 171): Former Brook Farm member and managing editor of the New York Tribune. He edited the Chicago Republican briefly after the war, which fits Young's "a Republican then living in the city of Chicago." He was later Lincoln's assistant secretary of war.

  • Elias Howe and Isaac Singer (pp. 168-173): Howe patented the lockstitch sewing machine in 1846. Singer built a practical machine in 1851 and the Singer company came to dominate the world market.

  • Richard Gatling (pp. 168, 171): Inventor of the Gatling gun (Unit 9), whom Young sets beside Weitling as an inventor who prospered.

  • The Fenians (p. 172): The Fenian Brotherhood, founded in 1858, organized Irish Americans for Irish independence. Its members raided Canada between 1866 and 1871.

  • William Dean Howells (p. 153): Editor of the Atlantic Monthly and Twain's friend, who praised Roughing It.

  • Hans Christian Andersen (p. 164): The Danish writer of fairy tales. Young pictures Weitling's crowded household of cats, birds, and babies as an Andersen fairy-tale world.

Allusions

  • Prometheus (p. 156): The Titan who stole fire for humanity and was chained to a rock.

  • Golgotha (p. 156): The hill of the crucifixion.

  • Newton's dog Diamond (p. 163): The legend that Isaac Newton's dog knocked over a candle and burned years of his papers.

  • Copernicus, Ptolemy, Tycho Brahe (pp. 163-171): The astronomers Weitling studied in his hobby of reforming astronomy.

  • Pegasus (p. 167): The winged horse of Greek myth.

  • Hannibal and Carthage (p. 160): The general of Carthage, and a play on Carthage, Illinois, where Smith died.
