Research notes: Chapter 64 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 64

Characters: Lorena · Miss MacIntosh

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

Miss MacIntosh is constituted by irresolvable multiplicities throughout this chapter. She is simultaneously:

  • The stern, practical nursemaid of What Cheer, Iowa
  • The wandering orphan of the human race
  • The woman who loved Lorena
  • The near-wife of a barber
  • The rejected child on the riverbank
  • "George," the man her brothers named her
  • The descendant of a drowned Gaelic prince
  • The woman who almost married the King of Sweden

These are not sequential identities: past selves replaced by present ones. They exist simultaneously, pressing against each other without resolution. When she says "I should have been born a man", she does not resolve her gender identity; she adds another layer to an already multiple self. The narrator observes this directly: "She never would be the same again. We all must change, even while we live. And if we alter not, life changes us".

Even her name participates in this plurality. "MacIntosh" is simultaneously the woman, the raincoat, and the apple: three entirely different ontological categories held together by a single word, and the text exploits this deliberately. She cannot be reduced to one thing because the language that names her refuses reduction.

The wig itself is the emblem of the plural self: it is both disguise and revelation, both the self she performs and the self she conceals, and the chapter is structured around its constant removal and replacement. Identity is not underneath the wig or on top of it. It is the gesture of putting it on and taking it off.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The entire chapter is organized around the collapse of a foundational illusion, the red wig, and the discovery that beneath it lies not a truer reality but simply another surface, the bald dome, which is itself immediately aestheticized, mythologized, and made into a new kind of beauty. The narrator tells us: "it seemed to me that she was more real now than she had been in the days before I saw her bald".

But this "more real" is immediately suspect. The narrator's perception of Miss MacIntosh's reality is itself a construction: an emotional investment, a love story projected onto a bald head. When the phrenologist declares her head "the most beautiful head of all", he is not revealing truth; he is constructing a new illusion over the revealed one. The text notes he "waxed eloquent" and was "quite carried away by his own oratory": the narrator herself registers the performative dimension of his truth-telling.

The chapter's most devastating statement of Pillar 2 comes here: "a map where man might see himself when there were no deceptions, no deceits, no illusions, when the only premise or foundation stone given was the bald dome dreaming waters and clouds and winds, hair, feathers of birds, grass, flowers, all man's imaginings, all woman's loves". The bald head is offered as the place beyond illusion, but what it contains is all of man's imaginings. The escape from illusion leads directly back into it. There is, as Pillar 2 insists, only a larger cave more carefully lit.

Miss MacIntosh's own life confirms this. She constructed herself around common sense and practical reality as a defense against the illusory world of the narrator's opium-dreaming mother, and yet her common sense was itself a costume, as artificial as the wig, a performance of solidity in a world she knew to be dissolving.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Grief in this chapter does not arc. It accumulates. Miss MacIntosh's losses are not sequential tragedies she has moved through and survived; they are a sediment she carries:

  • The loss of Lorena
  • The loss of a breast
  • The loss of hair before birth
  • The loss of belonging in her family
  • The loss of the young men who never asked her to the ball
  • The loss of the orphans who forgot her
  • The loss of the forger at Alcatraz, now dead
  • The loss of any future she might have imagined

These losses do not resolve into acceptance. They coexist. When she speaks of Lorena, she smiles and sighs in the same breath. When she speaks of her childhood baldness, she weeps and then almost immediately laughs about the barber. The emotional weather is mixed because grief here is not a stage but a climate: the permanent condition of a life in which everything loved has already been changing since before birth.

The prose itself enacts this. The sentences do not resolve; they accumulate clause upon clause, loss upon loss, in a rhythm that refuses to arrive anywhere: "I have lost one breast. There is a pain in my right shoulder. My hands grow stiff as pokers, as starfish. Shall starfish feed on me who fed not on them? My light is going out". Each sentence is a new weather front moving through, and none of them clears.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator's primary ethical act in this chapter is to stay in the room and look. She does not flinch from the bald head. She does not rush toward resolution or cure. She touches it: "I brushed with my cheeks against her bald, sweating head so luminous that one might have seen the shadow of a moth". She determines "to familiarize myself with it and to be unastonished by it".

This is Witnessing as ethical practice. The narrator does propose a cure (she asks about medicines, tonics, modern science) but these proposals are gentle, almost ritual, and she herself frames her love as located in the baldness rather than despite it: "as for myself, her baldness was my love". The cure is not what she actually wants. What she wants is to see fully.

The most powerful expression of the Witness stance comes near the chapter's end, where the narrator reflects: "Whose was her face, I must wonder now who knew her and yet did not know her". This is not a failure of attention but its most honest form: the Witness who acknowledges the limits of witnessing, who stays in the room precisely because the room cannot be fully mapped.

Miss MacIntosh herself is also positioned as a kind of anti-Witness: someone who has refused to be witnessed, who has covered her head for decades so that others would not see her plain. Her revelation is therefore a surrender to being witnessed, and the chapter is the record of what that costs her.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Miss MacIntosh's life is structured as recursive construction. She builds, loses, builds again:

  • She constructs a toupee from a doll's head as a child
  • She constructs a wig from horses' tails, wire, gauze, and adhesive tape as a young woman
  • She acquires a proper red wig as a nursemaid
  • She now oscillates between wearing and removing it

Each wig is a provisional building: an attempt to construct a self that can move through the world. Each one fails in some way (the doll's toupee stolen by siblings, the horses' hair wig subject to boys with fishing rods, the red wig now being thrown "almost anywhere"). But she builds again. The building is never finished. "That is not failure. That is how it works."

The narrator's own act of knowing Miss MacIntosh follows the same structure. She thinks she knows her, "backward and forward as the pendulum swings", and then discovers she does not. She returns to the beginning with new information. The chapter itself is this recursive return: the narrator circling Miss MacIntosh's history, adding detail, revising understanding, never arriving at a complete picture.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The prose style is the primary carrier of this pillar. Young's sentences refuse to conclude. They extend, qualify, reverse, extend again. Consider: "So I chose the fog,’ she said, ‘because the fog was like feathers, angels protecting this poor angel who had no feathers, and I chose my black umbrella to cast its shadow in my walk, and may I never lose it, for I was but this phantom which would fade into the light". The sentence builds toward a conclusion, the phantom fading, and then continues, refusing to let the fading be final.

Miss MacIntosh herself embodies the ethics of incompletion. She says: "After a while, we must have a good, long, serious talk — come to an understanding — get everything straightened out, once and for all. I am not as I seem". But this promised conversation never arrives. The "once and for all" is structurally impossible in this text. Every clarification opens new obscurity. Every revelation is followed by "But she stopped herself suddenly as if she were going to tell me too much".

The demand for completion (for the wig to stay on, for the self to be stable, for the past to be settled) is shown throughout as a kind of violence. The boys who tried to hook her wig with fishing rods are the comic-grotesque version of this violence: the world's insistence on exposing, fixing, concluding what refuses to be concluded.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Miss MacIntosh is the marginal person as evidence. She exists at the edges of every category:

  • Neither fully woman nor man ("George")
  • Neither healthy nor sick (one breast missing, hair congenitally absent)
  • Neither working class nor gentry (descended from kings and coffin-makers simultaneously)
  • Neither sane nor mad (she is the "reality principle" in a house of opium dreams, but her own grip on reality is shown to be a performance)
  • Neither lovable nor unloved (forgotten by orphans, loved by the narrator, loved by Lorena, almost married to a barber)

Her body itself is the ontological margin made flesh. "I have no hair anywhere on my body. I am a hairless woman". She lacks the biological markers that organize human bodies into legible categories: hair as gender signal, hair as health signal, hair as age signal. Without it, she is harder to place, harder to read, harder to fix in the social taxonomy.

The phrenologist's response to her head is the text's clearest articulation of Pillar 7: he finds in her marginality not deficiency but visibility, "a map where man might see himself when there were no deceptions". The marginal person does not reveal a different truth. They reveal that truth was always a construction, and at the margins, the construction shows its seams.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This pillar is perhaps the most fully elaborated in the chapter. Miss MacIntosh's baldness is not a present condition with a past cause. It is the past pressing up through the present:

"Dear child, it happened long ago and in the most unlikely place. It happened before I was born. It happened when I was sleeping the long sleep before birth".

The baldness precedes her existence. It is deposited in her before she arrives. This is sedimentary time at its most literal: the condition of the present self was laid down in strata she did not choose and cannot excavate. Her mother's failure to love her father, the drowned Gaelic prince who was her ancestor, the poverty of the shoe-maker and coffin-maker; these are not backstory. They are substrate. They are what she stands on.

The text enacts sedimentary time in its narrative structure. The narrator does not tell Miss MacIntosh's history chronologically. She layers it: the present scene, then a depth of childhood memory, then a deeper layer of prenatal origin, then a mythological layer of the drowned prince, then back to the present. Time in this chapter is geological, not linear. The past does not recede. It accumulates beneath us, layer on layer.

The most beautiful expression of this: "she had been shedding her hair, hair by hair", except she never had any hair to shed. The image is of a loss that preceded itself, a mourning for what was never possessed, a sediment of absence.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Young's prose is relentlessly self-aware about language as construction. The chapter is full of moments where language is shown making reality rather than reporting it:

The phrenologist "waxed eloquent" and "was quite carried away by his own oratory": his praise of Miss MacIntosh's head is shown as a linguistic performance, a construction of value through rhetoric.

Miss MacIntosh's own language is full of self-interruption and qualification: "that was my tragedy, I suppose, or if not a tragedy, for tragedy might be too great a word, then at least a misfortune". She is caught in the act of choosing language, aware that the word shapes the reality it names. "Tragedy" and "misfortune" are not neutral descriptions of the same event; they are different constructions of it.

The MacIntosh name itself is a linguistic construction that the text unpacks with comic seriousness: the woman, the raincoat, the apple, three things named by the same word, each reflecting on the others. The apple is "not those golden apples falling upward"; it is emphatically not the apple of Eden, not the apple of myth, just a plain Iowa apple good for cooking or eating raw. This is language insisting on its own ordinariness, refusing the mythological weight that other language would impose.

And yet the text immediately undermines this refusal, because the very act of insisting on the apple's plainness is itself a rhetorical construction: a performance of common sense that is as elaborate as any mythology.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter lives entirely within the ordinary and the bodily. There is no heaven, no afterlife, no transcendent realm. There is only the bald head, "shining like a lost star", "luminous", "gleaming like mother-of-pearl or like the moon when there are no clouds".

The phrenologist's rapture over Miss MacIntosh's skull is a secular liturgy. He finds in the physical structure of her head (the mosaics of skull-bones, the bumps of common sense and dreaming) the sacred capacity of the human. "Hers was that sea greater than the Sea of Azov". The transcendence is entirely immanent: it is in the bone, in the flesh, in the hairless dome of a poor Iowa farm girl.

Miss MacIntosh's own spirituality is resolutely immanent. She objects to Emperor Constantine for making God rest on Sunday, for imposing a sacred calendar on the working week, for weaving "this golden tapestry" over the plain march of days. Her sacred objects are the hoop, the wheel, the hasp, the nail, the board, the tarpaulin: things that work, things that cover, things that hold. The macintosh raincoat is more sacred to her than any golden crown, because it actually keeps the rain off.

And yet, and this is where the pillar becomes most complex, her baldness is consistently described in the language of the sacred: pearl, moon, planet, the earth before God moved over the face of the waters. The ordinary becomes transcendent not by pointing elsewhere but by being looked at long enough, closely enough, honestly enough. This is The Sacred Is Here: the grey bus, the bald head, the old plaid mackintosh; this is where the sacred lives.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The chapter's final movement, the long prose-poem meditation beginning "Where there was only water, the firmament laid upon the firmament", is the fullest expression of Pillar 11.

Miss MacIntosh is explicitly positioned as a crossing point between what came before and what comes after: "Bald I came into this world, and bald I will go out of it". She is constituted by what was deposited before her arrival (the drowned Gaelic prince, the mother's failure of love, the poverty of generations) and she is constituting the ground the narrator will stand on.

The narrator understands this: "I mistook the elegy for my love". Her love for Miss MacIntosh is already an elegy, already a mourning for what is passing, because Miss MacIntosh is herself a crossing point: a place where the past and future intersect in a present that is already becoming past.

The most structurally important expression of this pillar: "the baldness of the universal mourning which was hers as she sat in the grey fog blowing, at last having no desire for shelter or protection of any nest". The baldness is not Miss MacIntosh's private condition. It is universal mourning: the condition of all selves at the crossing point, stripped of the coverings that made them legible, reduced to the bare dome that was there before birth and will be there after death.

Miss MacIntosh is where time crosses itself. The ancestors are in her sediment: the Gaelic prince, the shoe-maker, the coffin-maker, the horses whose tails she stole. The carrying continues into the narrator, who will carry Miss MacIntosh's baldness and loneliness and common sense and grief forward into a future the old nursemaid will not see.
