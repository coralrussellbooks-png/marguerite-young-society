Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Why a second edition

Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · The Route Down →

The first edition of this book did a straightforward thing. It took Miss MacIntosh, My Darling seriously as a novel about women, placed it among the women experimentalists whose work had been pushed off the record, and traced the ways the book refuses to hold its shapes still. That work was right, and this edition keeps it. A reader who owns the first edition has lost nothing.

What sent me back to the book was a suspicion that the first reading had stopped too soon. It saw that the novel dissolves things. It named some of what dissolves: character running into character, scene into scene, the living into the dead. But it treated that dissolving as one feature among many, a matter of style and form, something the prose does on its way to its real subject. The longer I stayed with the novel, the more that got the order backward. The dissolution is not a feature of the book. It is the book. Everything else, the women, the reproductive range, the suffrage captain, the opium dreams, is the route Young takes to reach it.

The thing she reaches is a depth of consciousness that most fiction does not go near. She goes down into a mind and keeps going, past the point where the ordinary supports hold, until the categories a reader carries into the book stop working. Living and dead, waking and dreaming, man and woman, one self and the many selves inside it: each of these comes apart under the pressure of how far she is willing to go. This is not something the novel argues. It is something it does, sentence by sentence, across the eighteen years of its writing.

Once that is taken seriously, it cannot be added to the first edition as one more chapter. It changes what the other chapters are about. The book had been sorted into themes, women's bodies, marriage, grief, madness, and each theme had been read as a social condition the novel documents. All of that is still here, and all of it is still true. But under each theme is the same floor, and the floor is the same in every case: a self that will not stay single, in a world that will not stay sorted. The themes are the doors. The dissolution is the room they all open onto. To read the book that way is to read it again from the beginning, which is what a second edition is for.

This is why the novel's own line has moved to the center of this edition. "Every heart is the other heart. Every soul is the other soul. The individual is the one illusion." In the first edition that line could sit among the others, one striking sentence in a book full of them. Here it is the thesis, stated by the book about itself. The individual, the bounded single self that can be counted and sorted and owned, is the one thing the novel does not believe in, and everything it does to its women, and later to its men, is in the service of showing that the individual was never there to begin with.

The vocabulary this edition brings for that floor is new, and I want to be clear about its status before the chapters begin.
