Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Pillars: The Past Doesn't Go Away

The Questions

In the warped looking glass Vera can “hardly see my face,” only her eyes, “as if they had swam out of my face like two silver fishes.” What is happening to Vera’s own self?

The room is full of a stranger’s leavings, and Vera wonders “Whose hair?” What is it to sleep in the traces of someone one will never know?

The door is marked “Fire Escape,” a red light burning through the glass. What kind of room is one always half-ready to flee?

Why does the book turn its dissolving gaze, at last, on the seeker herself?

The chapter turns the plural, dissolving self on Vera, and this is new. Through the book Vera has watched others come apart, Catherine, Mr. Spitzer, Miss MacIntosh, learning to see the seams in them; here she comes apart herself. In the “warped looking glass” she can “hardly see my face,” and “only my eyes were reflected in its depths, as if they had swam out of my face like two silver fishes detaching themselves from a porous underwater flower.” The self that observed the plurality of others now undergoes it, the face dissolving, the eyes swimming free. The seeker’s education has reached the point where the truth she learned about others arrives in her own mirror.

She sleeps in the traces of a stranger she will never know. The room holds “wads of blonde, streaked hair combings,” “a piece of lipstick covered with tobacco,” the leavings of some previous occupant, and Vera wonders, “Whose hair?” This is The Past Doesn't Go Away at the scale of a rented room: the past of an unknown person risen into Vera’s present as residue, a stranger’s remainder she lies down among. She cannot know whose hair, whose lipstick, whose life left these; she can only sleep in their sediment, the anonymous past pressed into the room she has for the night, one more layer she adds herself to.

The door marked “Fire Escape,” with its red light burning “even when I had turned off the light,” makes the room a place of permanent half-flight. To sleep in a room that is also an exit, under a light that will not go out, is to be always partly ready to leave, never fully arrived, never able to seal the room shut and rest in it. This suits the condition the chapter describes: Vera, her face dissolving, among a stranger’s traces, in a room that is also an escape route, is nowhere she can settle, nowhere sealed, held in the open unrest that the book has made its truest condition. The unput-outable red light is the sign that there is no closing this door.

And the book turns its dissolving gaze on the seeker deliberately, as the completion of her long apprenticeship. Vera set out wanting a truth that does not fail, a solid answer to reach and hold, and she has spent the book learning that the solid selves around her are constructions, plural, unsealed. It would be a failure of the book’s honesty to exempt her, to let the one who learns the lesson stay solid while everyone she studies dissolves. So the mirror takes her too. The eyes swim out of her face, and Vera joins the condition she has been witnessing, no longer only the student of the plural self but an instance of it, dissolving in a warped glass in a room she cannot seal.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that treats Vera’s dissolving reflection as mere atmosphere, a spooky mirror in a seedy room, and the reading that keeps Vera exempt from the plurality she has studied in everyone else. Neither the atmosphere nor the exemption is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that the plural, dissolving self arrives at last in Vera herself, the face she can hardly see and the eyes swimming free, the seeker undergoing what she learned to see in others; that she sleeps in the risen traces of a stranger she will never know, an anonymous past sedimented into her borrowed room; and that the room marked as an exit, under a light that will not go out, is a place of permanent half-flight, nowhere sealed, nowhere settled. The one who studies the plural self becomes an instance of it. Vera dissolves in the warped glass among a stranger’s leavings, and the chapter refuses to let her stay the solid exception.

For Reflection

Has the truth one learned about others ever arrived, at last, in one’s own mirror?

What is it to rest among the anonymous traces of people one will never know?

Is there a room in one’s life that is also an exit, that one can never quite seal shut and settle into?
