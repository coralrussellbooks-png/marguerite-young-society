Harp Song for a Radical

Unit 9, pp. 177-200: Terre Haute and the coming war

← Unit 8 · Harp Song for a Radical: A History Companion · Unit 10 →

Background for this unit: The History in Harp Song for a Radical

Part Two opens with the Debs family's journey from New York to Cincinnati to Terre Haute, where Eugene was born on November 5, 1855. Young sketches the town's frontier founding, then the tensions of the 1850s: the Dred Scott decision, a southern Indiana full of Confederate sympathizers, and an Indianapolis doctor inventing the Gatling gun.

Major entries

The Debs family's arrival and Eugene's naming (pp. 181-186)

  • What happened: Daniel and Marguerite first lived in Williamsburg, then a separate town, now part of Brooklyn, where their first baby died at birth (p. 181). They tried Cincinnati, then settled in Terre Haute. Daniel worked in a slaughterhouse and laid railroad ties (p. 183) before the family opened a grocery.

  • The names: Eugene Victor was named for two French novelists of the poor: Eugène Sue, whose The Wandering Jew (1844-1845) attacked the Jesuits, and Victor Hugo (p. 186).

  • A note on dates: Young mentions Les Misérables in this passage (p. 186). That novel appeared in 1862, seven years after Eugene's birth. Daniel's admiration for Hugo rested on earlier work such as Notre-Dame de Paris (1831).

  • Further reading: Salvatore, Eugene V. Debs: Citizen and Socialist (1982), chapter 1.

The founding of Terre Haute and Fort Harrison (pp. 187-190)

  • What it was: Terre Haute ("high land" in French) sits on a bluff over the Wabash. William Henry Harrison built Fort Harrison nearby in 1811 during his campaign against the Indian confederacy. Abraham Markle and Joseph Richardson scouted the site in 1815 (p. 187), and the town was platted in 1816 in Vigo County, named for the fur trader Francis Vigo.

  • Abraham Markle: Born in Ulster County, New York, he moved to Upper Canada. During the War of 1812 he switched sides and served as a major in the Canadian Volunteers, a unit of Canadians fighting for the United States. Losing his Canadian property, he was compensated with western land and helped found Terre Haute.

  • Growth: The Wabash and Erie Canal reached the town in 1849, and railroads from Indianapolis arrived in 1852. The line later known as the Vandalia gave Daniel Debs work and Eugene his first job.

  • Further reading: Mike McCormick, Terre Haute: Queen City of the Wabash (2005).

Tecumseh, the Prophet, and Tippecanoe (p. 187)

  • What happened: The Shawnee leader Tecumseh and his brother Tenskwatawa, called the Prophet, built a pan-Indian confederacy to stop the sale of Native land. On November 7, 1811, while Tecumseh was away in the South, the Prophet's warriors attacked Harrison's army camped near their town, Prophetstown, at Tippecanoe Creek. Harrison held the field and burned the town.

  • Why it matters: The battle made Harrison a national hero and later president ("Tippecanoe and Tyler Too," 1840). He died a month after taking office in 1841. Tecumseh allied with Britain and was killed in 1813.

  • Where the record differs: Young describes "the cowardly attack upon the utopian philosopher Tecumseh and his sleeping red men when they had already surrendered" (p. 187). In fact Tecumseh was absent, and the Indians attacked Harrison's camp before dawn. Her sympathy for Native Americans runs through the book, but here the facts are reversed.

  • Further reading: Peter Cozzens, Tecumseh and the Prophet (2020).

The Dred Scott decision (pp. 198-200)

  • What it was: Dred Scott, an enslaved man, sued for freedom because his owner had taken him to live in a free state and a free territory. On March 6, 1857, the Supreme Court under Chief Justice Roger Taney ruled that Black Americans could not be citizens and that Congress had no power to bar slavery from the territories.

  • Why it matters: The ruling voided the Missouri Compromise, outraged the North, and pushed the country toward war. It was overturned by the Fourteenth Amendment in 1868.

  • Connections Young draws: Scott and his family were freed in May 1857 by Taylor Blow, a son of Scott's original owner (p. 200). Roswell Martin Field, the St. Louis lawyer who took Scott's case into federal court, was the father of the poet Eugene Field, later Debs's friend (p. 200).

  • Where the record differs: Young places the decision on "the very day after" Buchanan's inauguration (p. 198). It came two days after, on March 6.

  • Further reading: Don E. Fehrenbacher, The Dred Scott Case (1978).

Richard Gatling and the Gatling gun (pp. 193-197)

  • Who he was: A North Carolina-born physician and inventor (1818-1903) living in Indianapolis. He patented his hand-cranked, multi-barrel rapid-fire gun in November 1862. The Army adopted it in 1866.

  • The Union Repeating Gun: Before Gatling, the salesman J. D. Mills promoted the "coffee mill" Union Repeating Gun as "an army in six square feet" (p. 194). Lincoln watched a demonstration and ordered ten in October 1861.

  • Why it matters: After the war, Gatling guns were deployed against strikers and miners. Young lists the Pullman strike of 1894 and the mining wars at Coeur d'Alene, Idaho (1892, 1899), Cripple Creek, Colorado (1894, 1903-1904), and Goldfield, Nevada (1907) (p. 197).

  • Further reading: Julia Keller, Mr. Gatling's Terrible Marvel (2008).

Oliver P. Morton and the Knights of the Golden Circle (pp. 194-196)

  • Morton: Indiana's Republican war governor (1861-1867), later a senator. When Democrats won the legislature in 1862 and refused to pass a budget, he ran the state for two years on federal money and private loans, outside the law.

  • The Knights: Founded by George Bickley in Lexington, Kentucky, on July 4, 1854 (p. 195), the Knights of the Golden Circle first aimed at a slave empire around the Gulf of Mexico. During the war the name was attached to Copperhead networks in the Midwest, the Order of American Knights (1863) and the Sons of Liberty (1864). Young says the Hoosier council began in Terre Haute in 1863 and spread to sixty counties (p. 195), a detail that is unverified.

  • Why it matters: Southern Indiana was settled largely from the upland South. Many residents opposed the war and emancipation. Morton used the secret societies, real and exaggerated, to justify arrests and military trials (Unit 10).

  • Further reading: Frank L. Klement, Dark Lanterns: Secret Political Societies, Conspiracies, and Treason Trials in the Civil War (1984).

Shorter entries

  • Franklin Pierce (pp. 184-185): Democratic president from 1853 to 1857. His only surviving son, Benjamin, was killed in a train wreck two months before the inauguration. He signed the Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854, which repealed the Missouri Compromise.

  • The Missouri Compromise (p. 185): The 1820 law admitting Missouri as a slave state and barring slavery north of 36°30' in the Louisiana Purchase.

  • Butternuts (p. 192): A nickname for settlers of southern origin in the lower Midwest, from clothing dyed brown with butternut hulls. It came to mean Confederate sympathizers.

  • The Terre Haute State Bank (p. 189): The Greek Revival branch of the State Bank of Indiana on Ohio Street, built in the 1830s. It still stands.

  • Bill Nye (pp. 189-190): Edgar Wilson Nye (1850-1896), a humorist and Riley's touring partner. Young says he and Debs, both tall and bald, were taken for twins.

  • Vigo County Court House (p. 190): Where Debs later served as city clerk (1879-1883).

  • Helen Keller (p. 198): The deaf and blind author was a Socialist and a supporter of Debs, and wrote to him in prison.

  • Karl Marx and the Pullman strike (p. 197): Newspapers in 1894 blamed the strike on foreign radicalism. Young notes that few Americans had heard of Marx.

Allusions

  • St. Bartholomew's Day (p. 181): The 1572 massacre of French Protestants in Paris, used for New York's mass killings of stray dogs.

  • Mammon (p. 181): Wealth as a false god, from the Gospels.

  • Infant Hercules and River Queen (p. 182): Nicknames for Cincinnati, the fast-growing river city.

  • Hades (p. 184): The Greek underworld.

  • The Mason-Dixon Line (p. 191): The boundary surveyed in the 1760s, taken as the line between North and South.

  • Methuselah (p. 200): The oldest man in the Bible.
