Research notes: Chapter 13 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 13

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The narrator Vera Cartwheel is not one person in this chapter but several at once: the child who knew Miss MacIntosh, the adolescent crossing into knowledge on her fourteenth birthday, and the retrospective consciousness narrating from some later, destabilized position. These selves do not resolve into one another.

"farewell to the last certitude, even that tender night of my fourteenth birthday when I trembled on the margin of sentient life"

The phrase "margin of sentient life" is precise: she is not yet fully constituted as a self, and the chapter suggests she never will be. The self is perpetually at the margin. Meanwhile, Miss MacIntosh herself is revealed as plural: the ordinary woman Vera thought she knew and the "strangest being on earth" she turns out to have been. Two incompatible versions of the same person coexist without synthesis. The mother adds a third register of selfhood: a consciousness so multiple it has evacuated the physical world entirely, hosting garden parties of the dead while lying in bed.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The Lake Constance passage is the chapter's central philosophical statement, and it is entirely about the constructedness of the real:

"who did not realize that he had left terra firma long ago...that he had done the impossible, that he had crossed that waste expanse which was said to be impossible to cross...had dropped dead with the shock of the after-knowledge"

The rider's survival depended on not knowing what was real. The solid ground was ice over water. Terra firma was illusion. And crucially, the revelation of truth, the sunlit world outside the cave, kills him. Young is making a radical epistemological claim: illusion is not a deficiency of knowledge but its operating condition. The larger cave is not escape; it is only a "larger cave more carefully lit." Vera's ignorance of Miss MacIntosh's true nature was, as she says explicitly, "perhaps my salvation". Reality, when it arrives, does not liberate. It annihilates.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The chapter is structured as a series of farewells, but these farewells do not move toward acceptance. They accumulate:

"farewell to the past and its illusion, and farewell to the reason, the purpose, the goal, the future, and farewell to What Cheer, the cherry blossoms billowing like surf, the peach orchards, the globed fruit, the dusty lanes, for all had been as if they were a lie"

Notice the grammar: each farewell does not diminish the loss but adds to it. The list grows. This is grief as accumulation, not as process. The losses do not cancel each other; they layer. And the final clause, "as if they were a lie", does not mean the past was false, but that the knowledge of its falseness retroactively recolors everything, which is a different and more devastating operation. The cherry blossoms are still beautiful and still gone and now also uncertain. The climate of grief is total.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator's fundamental stance throughout is one of sustained, unflinching attention. She does not look away from the disintegration of her certainties. She witnesses Miss MacIntosh's transformation in her own understanding without rushing to condemn or forgive:

"I had thought of her as probably the only ordinary person I knew, as far from mysterious, no one to compete with the moon and the waves, and all the while — who had she been, who was she?"

The double question, past and present tense, refuses conclusion. The witnessing extends to the garden sequence, where Vera moves through surreal imagery without interpreting it, simply cataloguing:

"Delicate vapors of colors drifted like scarves and veils and plumes before my eyes...there were congregations of pagodas and umbrellas, and there were flutes and choral voices"

She does not explain the garden. She stays in it. This is attention as ethical practice: the refusal to simplify what is genuinely strange.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The chapter's prose itself enacts recursive construction. Sentences begin, extend, loop back through subordinate clauses, and then extend again: the Lake Constance passage runs for nearly a full page before completing its thought. This is not ornamental. The syntax performs the epistemology: you build a structure of meaning, discover it is provisional, incorporate that discovery, and build again.

Vera explicitly frames her relationship with Miss MacIntosh as something she will have to rebuild from the beginning:

"Everything, as I looked back, would be different from what it had been, the simplest gesture having a different meaning, the smallest word being weighted with a different message"

The entire archive of their shared life must be re-read. The building is never finished. That is not failure; that is the structure of consciousness encountering time.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter does not conclude. It does not arrive at a new stable understanding of Miss MacIntosh. It arrives at a series of farewells and then moves into the garden sequence, which is itself unresolved, which then moves into the mother's conversation with Mr. Spitzer about the dead ear, which ends mid-argument. The chapter's formal incompletion is its honesty.

"I should not be sure of anything, not even of that old creature who had seemed...so very average and unassuming"

Certainty is not recovered. The demand for resolution (for knowing who Miss MacIntosh really was, for the story to arrive somewhere) is refused. The ongoing sentence, grammatically and philosophically, is the form integrity takes here.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Miss MacIntosh is the chapter's central marginal figure: the nursemaid, the servant, the woman who asks rote questions about North Dakota and monsoons, the one person assumed to be ordinary in a house full of the extraordinary. And it is precisely her assumed ordinariness, her marginality to the drama of the household, that makes her the most destabilizing figure:

"the one person who was not chimerical and fading...the one person who was not the dreamer and the mistaken"

She was defined by negation, not mad, not dreaming, not mysterious. Her identity was constructed as the absence of the qualities everyone else possessed. And now that construction has collapsed. The marginal person (the nursemaid, not the opium-dreaming mother, not the distinguished dead guests) turns out to be the evidence that the entire category system was constructed. Her ordinariness was the fiction that held everything else in place.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The past does not recede in this chapter. It presses upward:

"Never again should I see Miss MacIntosh whom I had known so well in daily life...never again, except in memory's dim or golden light, should I see that simple-minded, fussy old nursemaid"

The memories that follow (the black umbrella, the dripping whaler's hat, the question about North Dakota) are not being left behind. They are being re-sedimented under new knowledge. They remain, but their meaning has shifted. The Lake Constance rider is the perfect image for this: he crossed the ice, the crossing happened, it is part of the ground he stands on, and the knowledge of what he crossed does not undo the crossing; it recontextualizes it catastrophically.

The mother's garden compounds this: it is populated by the genuinely dead (Galen, Harvey, Goldsmith, Jonson) who are not behind her but present, circulating through the garden party. The past accumulates as living company.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Young's prose is relentlessly self-conscious about language's constructive power. The rote questions Miss MacIntosh asked, "Where is North Dakota, and what is a monsoon, and what is a mirage", are now revealed as deeply ironic. Miss MacIntosh, who taught Vera to ask what a mirage is, was herself a kind of mirage. The language of the nursery (rote, catechistic, authoritative) was "that fabric of self-deception we two persons had lived by".

The farewell passage is also a farewell to a particular language system:

"farewell to the old moralities, the proverbial wisdom, the rote questions and answers like the rote tide"

The rote tide: language that moves by habit, not by truth. The chapter performs the dismantling of that language even as it names it, replacing it with a prose that refuses to speak from nowhere, that is located in a specific consciousness at a specific moment of destabilization.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter is not elsewhere. It is in the umbrella, the whaler's hat, the shirts and collars Vera ironed:

"I had even reluctantly washed and ironed her shirts and collars and handkerchiefs as I would have done most cheerfully had I known the facts"

The mundane domestic act, ironing, is retrospectively charged with significance. The sacred was already there, in the ordinary labor, unrecognized. Similarly, the garden sequence is not a vision of heaven but a deeper immersion in the immanent world, strange and saturated:

"there were marble statues trembling on the brink of supernumerary consciousness...many familiar things were increasingly unfamiliar as if, until I had seen them in the illuminated darkness, I had never seen them"

The illumination is not from outside. It is the darkness itself that illuminates: the immanent made strange enough to be seen. The sacred lives in the frog that screams, the fish hawk that dives, the flowers that fly away like luna moths.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The chapter's most explicit statement of this pillar is the Lake Constance passage, but the crossing point is not only spatial. Vera at fourteen is precisely where time crosses itself: the child she was (who ironed Miss MacIntosh's shirts without knowing why) and the adult she is becoming (who will never see Miss MacIntosh the same way again) intersect at this moment of revelation. She did not choose to be at this crossing. It is structural.

"After this night of change, that which should take place in myself if not in another"

The change is in her, not in Miss MacIntosh. Miss MacIntosh was always what she was. Vera is the crossing point where that truth finally arrives. And the carrying continues: the chapter does not end with resolution but with the mother's insistence that the ear is dead and Mr. Spitzer cannot hear his own voice, which is itself a transmission, a depositing of this knowledge into the ground of the narrative, for whoever comes after.
