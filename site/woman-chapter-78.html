Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 78

← Chapter 77 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 79 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Esther Longtree · Rosemary

"Little Jackass" is the name Esther carries from birth in this chapter, given for her stubbornness and her unwillingness to be born, and the resistance is a girl's refusal of the roles set for her before she has drawn a breath. Her loneliness, her natural enemies among the woodpeckers and bluejays, mark her as the outsider the world punishes, and the mother's reproach of "ingratitude" for resisting birth is the pressure to accept a motherhood and a womanhood unquestioningly, laid on Esther from the first.

The household built "to her mother's size," where everything is "breakable," is the domestic smallness a woman is fitted into, and Esther's harsh childhood, the "deformed foot," the "crossed eyes," the bee that stings her while her mother disbelieves the pain, is the body as the place the self comes apart and the minimizing of female suffering both. Her father's lineage runs prolific and "natural," his mother giving birth "many times, often without knowing it," an instinctive fecundity Esther cannot access, and her mother stands as the "needle's eye" through which the "camel shall not pass," the narrow, excluding femininity Esther is measured against and found wrong.

Rosemary, the stillborn twin, is idealized as "perfect," "considerate," "grateful," the compliant daughter the world would have wanted, and Esther is accused of having "killed a little child before she was ever born." The twin is the illusion of the individual worn by two sisters who are versions of each other, plural selfhood reaching across a womb, and Esther's fixation on Rosemary, her forbidden digging for the grave, is a woman trying to recover the silenced double the family buried, the self she is told she destroyed and cannot stop mourning.

Esther's question of whether she is herself or "the shadow," "the other," is the fracturing of a self the book keeps performing, and her insistence that she is "all flesh, all blood" is an embodied feminism claiming the lived female body against the idealized dead one. Her laughter "shaking the snow from the clouds," her sense that "non-being" is more beautiful than "being," close the chapter on the dissolving boundary between life and death embraced rather than feared, a woman who carries her dead twin and her own contested existence and goes on, defiant and alive, at the threshold the book keeps for its erased.
