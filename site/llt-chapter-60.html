Chapter 60

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: Not One Self · Unfinished Is Honest · The Sacred Is Here

The Questions

Is Mr. Spitzer a musician or a gambler, and can the book, or he, decide?

There is “a meaning of the music even when he had forgotten the score.” What survives when the notes are gone?

He is “always at one remove from that sad musician who was himself.” How is a man at a remove from himself?

If his musical life was a self-deception, “a lost musical career which should have taken place in a better world,” what is left of him?

The chapter will not decide which brother Mr. Spitzer is, and neither can he. He is a musician “if he was not mistaken,” and a gambler “on the other hand, if he was not a musician but had merely deceived himself.” The two selves, the composer and the gambler, the brother he was and the brother he buried, will not resolve into one answer, and the chapter holds the undecidability open rather than settling it. This is Not One Self at the end of its long development in him: not many selves he can survey, but two he cannot choose between, a man who genuinely does not know whether his life’s work is real or a self-deception, whether he is the artist or the gambler who dreamed an artist.

What survives the uncertainty is a meaning without its content. There is, the chapter says, “a meaning of the music even when he had forgotten the score,” even when he “had never known the arrangements of notes.” The music can be lost, the notes gone, the score forgotten, and still a meaning of it remains, a germinal music in the head that outlasts every particular of the piece. This is Unfinished Is Honest turned inward on art: what matters is not the finished, remembered, notated work but the meaning that persists when the work is gone, the sense of a music that survives the loss of the music. Mr. Spitzer may not have his score, may never have had it, and the meaning is there regardless.

He stands, the chapter says, “always at one remove from that sad musician who was himself.” Even his own identity as the musician is held at a distance, watched rather than inhabited, so that the self he might be is a figure he observes across a gap. This is the counterfeit self the book found earlier, the passing-for with no original, here grown gentle and habitual: Mr. Spitzer is not the musician but at one remove from him, beside the self he is, never quite occupying it. The remove is not a failure to be himself. It is what being a self has come to mean for him, a standing-beside rather than a standing-in.

And the chapter roots the undecidable question in the ordinary. Whether musician or gambler, Mr. Spitzer is among the mundane props of a life, the ace of clubs falling from his sleeve, the dice like his rattling bones, the horses’ hooves on the oval, the germinal music in his head that needs no concert. The meaning he keeps is not in a better world where the lost career should have taken place; it is here, in the falling card and the remembered-forgotten music and the elegiac mood of an ordinary afternoon. The Sacred Is Here holds even the undecidable self: whichever brother he is, the meaning of him is in here, the card and the dice and the music with no score, and not in the better world the career was supposed to belong to.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that wants Mr. Spitzer settled as either musician or gambler, the question answered, and the reading that takes his possible self-deception for a sad reduction, a man who was nothing he thought. Neither the settled identity nor the reduction is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that the two selves, musician and gambler, will not resolve, and the undecidability is Not One Self at the end of its course in him, held open rather than settled; that a meaning of the music survives the loss of the score, the germinal sense outlasting every note, so what matters is not the finished work but the meaning that persists without it; that Mr. Spitzer stands always at one remove from himself, beside the self he might be rather than in it; and that the meaning of him, whichever brother he is, is in the ordinary here, the falling card and the scoreless music, not in a better world. The self stays undecided, the meaning survives its content, and the sacred is in the ordinary afternoon. The chapter lets Mr. Spitzer be both brothers and neither, and asks for no better world to hold him.

For Reflection

Is there a question about who one really is that has no answer, and can be lived without one?

What meaning of a thing survives when the thing itself, the score, the notes, the content, is gone?

What would it be to live always at one remove from the self one might be, beside it rather than in it?
