Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 26

← Chapter 25 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 27 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer

The chapter runs on the machinery of law, and law is the pillar: the instrument by which a woman's property, her illness, and her existence pass into the keeping of men. Mr. Spitzer administers "the dead" and "hidden pasts," and the estates he settles are full of people "who were rich and imagined they were poor, those who were poor and imagined they were rich," a confusion of status that falls hardest on women, whose class is never their own but arrives through a man. The women who write "many wills to baffle even the most conscientious attorney" are practicing the only authorship the system leaves them, scrambling the record so that no clean line of inheritance can be drawn through their hands. This is women's dispossession under inheritance law and it is the failure of the record itself to hold a woman's life, the two being the same failure.

Against that failure Young sets the Boston lady who believes she is Cleopatra reborn and leaves everything to her Siamese cats. She reclaims a female power the histories have maligned and she routes her legacy outside the male line entirely, to creatures with "nine lives" who own her house after her death. Legacy passes without lineage: the cats persist where daughters and heirs do not, and female continuity survives in a form the law was not built to recognize. Mr. Spitzer, "never amused by life," cannot follow her into that magic; his rational, conservative custody stands for everything that measures and forecloses, and her feline immortality escapes his ledgers.

Catherine is the case that gathers all of this. She is "already dead" in the eyes of the men who manage her, kept and controlled through debts and sedatives while her brother's rivalry and neglect compound her suffering. Mr. Spitzer's loyalty to her, unreturned, and his "ideal and incorruptible marriage" that "never could have been" in the flesh, name marriage and law together as institutions that promise women shelter and deliver confinement. His nightwatch over the "lower world" of thieves and "street women," the passage one must cross to reach "the upper world," admits the whole marginalized economy of women the respectable order refuses to see. The chapter closes on that admission and does not soften it: to reach any legitimate world one goes through the streets where women are least protected and most surveilled, and the law that files the wills has no page for them.
