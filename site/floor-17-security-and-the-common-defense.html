What Security Actually Means and How to Build It

A Companion Document to the Constitution

The Philosophical Foundation

Security means the protection of people's actual ability to live full lives. It means food, housing, healthcare, education, bodily autonomy, environmental safety, and freedom from violence. It does not mean the projection of military or economic dominance over other countries. A nation that can destroy any enemy but cannot house its own people is not a secure nation. It has confused the instruments of security with security itself.

The United States currently spends more on its military than the next ten countries combined. It has been in continuous armed conflict somewhere in the world for most of its existence. Its veterans return home to inadequate healthcare, high rates of suicide, and difficulty accessing the economic floor the country sent them to defend. The communities that send the most people into the military are often the communities with the fewest economic alternatives. The construction of military spending as national security, presented as obvious and necessary, is a political choice. This document examines what that choice has cost and what a different choice would look like.

The Use of Military Force

The decision to use military force in the name of the nation belongs to the nation's representatives, accountable to the nation's people. The Constitution of 1787 gave Congress the power to declare war for exactly this reason: the people who bear the cost of war should have a voice in the decision to wage it. That principle has been eroded steadily since World War II. The United States has fought major sustained conflicts in Korea, Vietnam, Iraq, and Afghanistan without a formal declaration of war. The Authorization for Use of Military Force passed after September 11, 2001 has been used to justify military operations in countries never contemplated when it was written, without further Congressional approval.

This constitution restores the original principle with specific mechanisms. The use of military force requires a declaration of war by Congress. Emergency deployments are permitted for up to thirty days without Congressional approval, after which Congress must vote to authorize the continuation of the deployment or it ends. The people may trigger a referendum on any sustained military commitment through the initiative process established in Article I. The President who commits forces to sustained combat without Congressional authorization is in violation of this constitution and subject to removal.

This is not a pacifist provision. It is a democratic one. The nation may defend itself. The nation may honor treaty obligations to allies. The nation may respond to genuine emergencies. All of these things can happen within a framework that requires democratic accountability for the decision to use violence in the nation's name.

The Military-Industrial Complex

Companies that profit from military contracts have a financial interest in the expansion of military spending regardless of whether that expansion serves the national interest. The relationship between those companies and the elected and appointed officials who make decisions about military spending is one of the most thoroughly documented sources of corruption in American public life. Defense contractors fund congressional campaigns. Retired military officers move into defense industry employment. The officials who approve contracts have financial relationships with the companies whose contracts they approve.

This constitution's transparency provisions apply to military contracting with full force. Every lobbying contact between defense contractors and elected or appointed officials is a matter of public record within forty-eight hours. Campaign contributions from defense contractors are subject to the same caps and disclosure requirements as all other contributions. The movement of military leaders directly into employment with defense companies they previously worked with is prohibited for a defined period after leaving government service. Contracts are awarded through a transparent competitive process subject to independent audit.

The argument that these relationships are necessary because defense contracting is too complex for ordinary transparency requirements is the same argument every concentrated interest makes for its own exemption. It is not a sufficient argument. The public interest in knowing how military spending decisions are made outweighs the industry's interest in making them quietly.

Peace-Building as a Constitutional Obligation

Article IX establishes peace-building, conflict prevention, and the systems that support negotiation and peaceful resolution of conflict as constitutional obligations, funded at a defined ratio relative to military spending. This document defines what that means in practice.

The research on conflict prevention is consistent: the conditions that produce armed conflict are knowable and addressable before violence begins. Poverty, resource scarcity, political exclusion, historical grievances without legitimate outlets, and the presence of armed groups with more resources than the governments they operate alongside: these are the documented precursors of armed conflict. The investment in addressing these conditions before they produce violence is less expensive by every measure than the investment in military response after violence has begun.

The United States currently spends a small fraction of its national security budget on the diplomatic and development tools that address conflict precursors. The State Department and the United States Agency for International Development together receive less than one-tenth of what the Department of Defense receives. This allocation reflects a construction of what security requires that this constitution rejects: the assumption that military capacity is the primary instrument of security and everything else is supplementary.

The rebalanced allocation this constitution requires does not eliminate military capacity. It funds the full range of instruments that genuine security requires: diplomacy, development assistance, conflict mediation, support for democratic institutions in fragile states, and the international legal frameworks that provide alternatives to violence for the resolution of disputes between nations.

The United States' relationship to international law and international institutions has been inconsistent at best. It has selectively participated in international frameworks when they served its interests and withdrawn from them when they did not. This constitution establishes consistent engagement with international legal frameworks as a constitutional obligation, not a policy preference subject to reversal by each administration. The nation that helped build the post-World War II international order and then periodically undermined it when the order constrained its own behavior has not been a reliable partner in the project of replacing war with law. That changes here.

The Just War Principle

Not all use of force is prohibited by this constitution. The just war tradition, developed over centuries of moral and legal philosophy, provides a framework for distinguishing the use of force that can be justified from the use of force that cannot. The core principles are: the cause must be just; the decision must be made by legitimate authority accountable to the people; force must be a last resort after other means have been exhausted; the harm caused must be proportionate to the good achieved; and the means used must distinguish between combatants and civilians.

These principles are not new. They are incorporated into the international humanitarian law that the United States has ratified. What this constitution adds is the enforcement mechanism: military actions that violate these principles are subject to constitutional challenge, the officials who ordered them are subject to accountability under the due process provisions of Article VII, and the people may use the referendum mechanism to review any sustained military commitment.

The drone strike program, which has killed documented numbers of civilians in countries with which the United States is not formally at war, fails the proportionality and civilian distinction tests of the just war framework. The detention of people without charge or trial at Guantanamo Bay fails the due process provisions of Article VII. These are not novel legal conclusions. They are the application of principles this constitution makes enforceable that were previously aspirational.

The Conversion Framework

Article IX establishes that existing military capacity is progressively redirected toward the obligations this constitution has already established. This document defines what that conversion looks like in practice.

The United States military has capabilities that the country desperately needs applied to non-military problems. The Army Corps of Engineers has the capacity to build infrastructure at scale. The military's logistics systems can move people and supplies to disaster areas faster than any civilian agency. Military research and development has produced technologies, including the internet, GPS, and many medical advances, that transformed civilian life. The military's medical infrastructure is among the most sophisticated in the world.

Conversion means directing these existing capabilities toward the constitutional obligations this document has established. Specifically:

The Army Corps of Engineers and military construction capacity are redirected toward climate resilience infrastructure: the sea walls, the flood management systems, the water infrastructure, the grid modernization that the environmental rights provision requires. The communities most at risk from climate change, which are disproportionately the communities with the least resources to protect themselves, receive priority.

The military's logistics and transportation capacity is integrated into the federal disaster response system, with clear protocols for deployment that do not require the declaration of martial law or the suspension of civilian authority. The inadequacy of the federal response to Hurricane Katrina in 2005, when the military's capacity sat largely unused while people died, is the failure this integration is designed to prevent.

Military medical infrastructure is integrated into the universal healthcare system, beginning with the Veterans Administration, which is brought to full funding and full staffing and opened to the communities surrounding military installations where the VA is often the only substantial medical facility.

Military research and development is reoriented toward the clean energy transition. The military already has strong institutional reasons to pursue energy independence: fuel supply lines are a significant source of military vulnerability, and installations that can generate their own power are more resilient. The research investment that pursues these goals for military reasons produces civilian applications that accelerate the broader clean energy transition.

Conversion is not the elimination of military capacity. It is the recognition that the country has invested enormous resources in capabilities that can serve the full range of constitutional obligations rather than only the military ones. The question is whether those capabilities are deployed in the public interest. This constitution says they are.

Veterans

The people who serve in the military bear the direct personal cost of the security decisions this document is redesigning. The treatment of veterans by the country that sent them to war is the clearest measure of whether the country's stated values about service and sacrifice are genuine or ceremonial.

The Veterans Administration healthcare system is chronically underfunded and understaffed relative to the need it is supposed to serve. Veterans wait months for appointments. Mental health services are inadequate to the documented need. The suicide rate among veterans is significantly higher than among the general population. The country that sends people to war and then provides inadequate care for the psychological and physical damage war produces is not honoring its obligations. It is managing them at the minimum level consistent with political viability.

This constitution's universal healthcare provision covers veterans with the same comprehensive coverage it provides to everyone, with the VA system expanded and fully funded as the delivery mechanism for veterans who prefer to use it. The economic floor provisions apply to veterans with full force: the veteran who returns from service to unemployment, housing instability, and inadequate healthcare is not a personal failure story. They are the evidence of what happens when the country treats the cost of service as a private problem rather than a collective obligation.

Nuclear Weapons

The United States maintains a nuclear arsenal capable of destroying human civilization multiple times over. The cost of maintaining that arsenal, the research, the delivery systems, the command and control infrastructure, the ongoing modernization program, runs to hundreds of billions of dollars over the current planning period. The doctrine governing the use of these weapons has varied across administrations and is not subject to democratic oversight in any meaningful way. The President of the United States can order the launch of nuclear weapons without the approval of Congress, without judicial review, and without the possibility of recall once the order is given.

This constitution does not unilaterally disarm the United States. It establishes that nuclear weapons policy is subject to the same democratic accountability as all other security policy: Congressional authorization for any change in nuclear posture, full transparency about the doctrine governing use, and active participation in international frameworks for nuclear reduction and eventual elimination. The goal of a world without nuclear weapons is a constitutional commitment, not an aspiration. The path toward it is through negotiated multilateral reduction, pursued with the same seriousness the country applies to military expansion.

The Transition

The transition from the current security framework to the one this document describes is politically difficult because the current framework has powerful institutional defenders. The defense industry, the military services, the members of Congress whose districts host military installations and defense contractors: all have interests in the continuation of the current allocation of resources. Those interests are not illegitimate. The people who work in defense industries and military communities have real stakes in how the transition is managed.

The transition requires a timeline that allows communities dependent on military spending to develop alternative economic bases, with the federal investment in conversion providing the economic activity that replaces what the drawdown removes. The communities that have hosted military installations and defense industries have often done so at significant cost to their environments and their civic life. They are owed a transition that takes that cost seriously.
