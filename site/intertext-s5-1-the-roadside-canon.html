1. The Roadside Canon

Part of Intertextuality in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Previous · Intertextuality in MMMD Home · Next →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Madge Capehorn · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Allusions: Dante · Don Quixote · Genesis · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest

Definition from research

The first strategy appears on the novel's first pages and governs everything after. Vera recalls what the bus has passed on its way through fog: "an enormous coffee urn with its lid opened against the sky, a wigwam nightclub," then "a windmill, a leaning tower, Noah's Ark, the old woman who lived in the shoe" (chapter 1). Scripture, a Tuscan landmark, a nursery rhyme, and the windmill Don Quixote charged have all become novelty architecture on an Iowa road.

Young repeats the move throughout. In chapter 10 a litany of Israel's tribes runs straight into American states without a pause: "Where is Manasses? Where is Kansas? Where is Iowa?" The names of Genesis and the names on a road map share one breath and one grammar. Miss MacIntosh's hometown is called What Cheer, a real Iowa town, and she remembers it as a place "where there were no graven images" (chapter 3). The Second Commandment has been settled there, in a town named for a cheerful greeting.

The inherited names keep turning up on small things. Esther Longtree names her lovebirds "Sweetie Petie, Mr. Bubbles, Martin Luther, Beau Geste" (chapter 72). Catherine's one yellow canary answers to "Juniper, Tulip, Cellini, Solomon, Methuselah, Marco Polo, Admiral Peary, Shackleton, Dante" (chapter 2). In chapter 6, the townspeople remember their favorite girl as "Abraham Lincoln's sweetheart and Lincoln's mother afterwards and Jenny Lind." One of them explains the process plainly: "We elected her to be immortal."

That sentence is the strategy in small. In Young's Middle West the great names are not inherited by birth. They are elected, borrowed, painted onto buildings, and given to pets. Sometimes they arrive damaged. Madge insists the girl was "no Venus Adonis, no handsome girl with a broken arm" (chapter 6). She has fused the armless Venus de Milo with Adonis and kept the broken arm. Young does not correct her. The heritage reaches the interior of the country half-remembered and still loved, and the mistake is part of how it lives there.

This strategy is where Young's intertextuality meets the principle the radical immanence notes call "Reality Is Constructed." Culture is shown as something people build and keep putting up. The canon is not a monument behind glass. It is a coffee urn on a roadside, and the people who built it meant it.
