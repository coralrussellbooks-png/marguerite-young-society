Arc 7: The Tavern (Chapters 67–71)

Part of Story Arcs, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated November 10, 2026

← Miss MacIntosh's Last Month · Story Arcs Home · Esther Longtree →

Spoiler note: This page covers Chapters 67 to 71. If you are reading along, read it after you finish Chapter 71.

Overview

The bus reaches Indiana. Madge and Homer walk out of the novel, and Vera spends a sleepless night at the Tavern among the Christian hangman Mr. Weed, Dr. O'Leary raging at the dead Sarah through the wall, and the remembered Mrs. Hogden, before a grey morning of inventory.

Central figures: Vera Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Catherine Cartwheel · Moses Hunnecker · Justice O’Leary · Mr. Weed

Chapter by chapter

Chapter 67

The bus reaches the Tavern at the end of its journey and cannot wedge into the space between a leaking milk truck and a hearse. Moses Hunnecker, drunk or so Vera thinks, curses old Doc, the mad Dr. Justice O’Leary, whose invisible ghost buggy, an old Ford full of the children Moses jeers at, he believes is taking the space. He sings scraps of old songs, boasts of the buzzards returning to Rush Creek, and threatens to scatter Doc all over the street. Vera, Madge, and Homer stay in their seats out of infinite weariness, for they have come home to no home. Madge laughs at a misery that is nothing new to her and likens them to people trapped in a crazy house of mirrors with no doors, and Homer wonders which door Jackie found.

Chapter 68

Night has fallen when the bus comes to rest in a desolate Indiana village before the Tavern, “built in 1827 by God’s servants who were believers in the lamb.” Moses bounds inside to the mother who thinks he is her bridegroom, and Madge and Homer walk off toward the river. Waiting in a rocking chair to sign in, Vera dreams herself old in an instant and remembers Miss MacIntosh’s galoshes thrown up by the tide. At the desk the deaf, tattooed landlord, who says he has been on four ships that went down, talks with Mr. Weed, the Christian hangman, on his way home from a hanging. Mr. Weed tells of his first wife, Marie, who hanged herself in the orchard when she feared she had conceived again; of his children, who play at hanging and hang their dolls and a blind dog from the same peach tree; of murders he has heard of; and of a Black man in Kentucky, hanged for sleeping with a white woman, as he surely never did, and cut down alive. Mr. Weed finds in his pocket the black poker chip from an unsolved murder, and the two men climb the stairs while no clock strikes.

Chapter 69

Vera’s room is marked Fire Escape: a corn-husk mattress, a “Forget me not” picture of a church steeple, and a slop jar beside someone else’s blonde hair combings. On the stairs the tattooed landlord, once a magician, a ventriloquist, a sailor, a soldier in the Intelligence, and a hunter of wild animals for Hagenbeck’s, now married to the former circus fat lady who owns the hotel, tells her that the hangman’s talk came from his shadow and that Mr. Weed is in truth a prosperous dairy farmer paid for his work. Her neighbors include Tillie, a very young girl who is also an old woman keeping turkey buzzards, or canaries, in chains, and a legless newspaper man who rides a sled with rusted sleigh bells. All night, through the thin wall, old Dr. Justice O’Leary rages at Sarah, a silent woman who may be his little sister, dead at the age of one, or the love who ran off with a travelling salesman. He lists every disease he knows and speaks of his strangest case, a woman always pregnant with an imaginary baby. At last Vera screams at Sarah to go.

Chapter 70

In the silence after the doctor’s quarrel, Vera half dreams of Mrs. Hogden, a woman she never saw, whose name slides from Hogden to Logden, Fogden, Dogden, and Bogden. During Vera’s absence Mrs. Hogden arrived at Catherine’s house in four coats, hung with temple bells and packages, carrying an empty bird cage and a basket of wormy apples. She had sold her Dutch windmill on Long Island to become the arithmetic teacher Catherine says she never hired. Lisa Lunde, the would-be physicist who replaced Vera as Catherine’s companion, carries messages between the two women and interprets neither to the other. Lodged in the deserted playroom, Mrs. Hogden explores the house, plays chess alone with an atomizer for the missing bishop, drinks, and at last takes to bed as a rival invalid, dreaming the household’s dreams out loud. Mr. Spitzer offers to restore her windmill and more, and she rides away in the mist. In the empty playroom Vera finds, on the little blackboard, a message in red chalk ending, “and therefore, dear heart, all a’s are c’s. You know me.”

Chapter 71

Vera has not slept. She wears her mother's black velvet, meant as Catherine's shroud and never used, along with her lace and her pearls, which Catherine believed multiplied when lost. She thinks of Mr. Weed guarding his children through the night. She enters Dr. O'Leary's room, a clutter of mismatched women's shoes, medical instruments, a Yale diploma, and a calendar twenty years out of date. A woman shouts prejudices on the stairs. The Tavern Landlady, in thick dialect, tells her about everyone in town, including a waitress always expecting a baby. The maid, Tabitha, whispers good morning.
