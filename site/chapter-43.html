Summary

In silk hat and black cape, Mr. Spitzer keeps watch, unsure whether he himself is living or dead. Hannah's dying becomes a dance of death. She raves through deserts, mountains, and snow, speaks to Caesar, a blacksmith, and a figure she calls her love, asks for a mirror, and longs for silk skirts and parasols she scorned all her life. She asks whether she is the anvil or the hammer, and at the end she cries out for a lost beloved under the snow.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with vivid descriptions of Cousin Hannah’s struggle through dreamlike, stormy landscapes symbolizing her life’s battles and approaching death. The imagery of crossing deserts, climbing mountains, and clouds emphasizes the arduous journey of life and death, and the suffragette’s wild screams indicate confusion and loss of self-identity near death.
  • It is revealed that even this great lady “wept if a wandering snowflake touched her hand” and was "fragile as were all mortal things," prone to “wild dreams, visions, fantasies, presentiments, forebodings, fears of the unknown.” This humanizes her, showing vulnerability despite her public strength and ideals.
  • Mr. Spitzer himself is uncertain of his own state, “not sure who he was, whether living or dead,” and reflects on the confusion about who is dying and the nature of death. His belief that “the dead cast no vote” and his abstention from voting reflects his detachment from worldly affairs.
  • The narrative explores death as both an ending and a beginning, a “great crucible” where opposites meet, and where “death was that which blew us all along.” It describes Cousin Hannah’s cries and visions as part of a larger metaphysical process, suggesting death is not peace but a storm or dance of contradictory forces.
  • Cousin Hannah speaks to various mythic and historical figures like Caesar, a blacksmith, a golden archer, a blackamoor. asking profound questions about identity and fate (“Am I the anvil which is struck or the hammer which strikes?”). This symbolizes her grappling with agency, power, and the unknown as life fades.
  • The blackamoor, a mysterious figure she calls her love, and the “great lamp-lighter” who might light her way, appear repeatedly in her delirium. This imagery suggests a guide or companion in the transition from life to death, blending cultural and mystical symbols.
  • Mr. Spitzer reflects on how Cousin Hannah’s ravings mix ancient history, myth, and personal memories, with cities, armies, and landscapes blending into a timeless dreamscape. There is uncertainty whether these are real memories, dreams, or hallucinations, emphasizing the blurred boundary between life and death, past and present.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • A somber, enigmatic figure who carries a high silk hat and black cape, symbolic of mourning and ritual.
  • He is both witness and participant in the suffragette’s dying experience.
  • Reflects deeply on life, death, identity, and the nature of existence.
  • Struggles with his own sense of being alive or dead, voting or not voting, and the meaning of his presence.
  • His observations are philosophical, often pondering the illusions of life and the mysteries beyond death.

Cousin Hannah

  • A once powerful figure, now fragile and approaching death.
  • Engaged in a fierce, chaotic battle against death, manifested in vivid, surreal visions.
  • Experiences wild dreams, hallucinations, and shifting identities in her final hours.
  • Struggles with recognition of self and the meaning of life and death.
  • Exhibits vanity, fear, ecstasy, confusion, and moments of clarity.
  • Her final moments are a “dance of death,” a paradoxical mix of seriousness and frivolity.

Research: Themes

Life, Death, and the Transition Between

See: Death and Mortality

  • Death is depicted not as an end but a complex transformation, a journey through storm, cloud, snow, and desert.
  • The suffragette’s battle with death is fierce and confusing; death is both a closing and an opening (“Is the lid lowering or raising?”).
  • The ambiguity of death and existence is central, with Mr. Spitzer unsure if he or the suffragette is alive or dead.
  • Death is associated with loss, revelation, and paradox, often blending into life’s experiences.

Memory, Identity, and Illusion

See: Illusion and Reality · Memory and the Past · Identity and the Self

  • The suffragette’s past is fragmented and elusive, mixing real and imagined events.
  • Both characters wrestle with who they are and who others have been.
  • Dreams and memories flow outside of linear time, suggesting the permeability of identity.
  • Reality and illusion are intertwined; the suffragette’s visions may be symbolic rather than literal truth.

Vanity and Human Frailty

See: Decay and Ruin

  • Despite the gravity of death, vanity persists (“vanity of silken skirts,” “great parasols fringed by gold”).
  • The suffragette clings to images of beauty and love even as she fades.
  • The fragility of human existence is highlighted, especially in the face of death.

Love and Loss

See: Love · Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • The suffragette expresses deep, eternal love in her delirium, though the identity of lover and beloved is unclear.
  • Love and death blur together, raising the question if death extinguishes love or transforms it.
  • The paradox of love’s endurance beyond death is a poignant motif.

The Role of the Observer

  • Mr. Spitzer’s role as witness is complicated by his own uncertain status.
  • He balances grief with detachment and observation with participation.
  • He questions the reliability of memory and perception, acknowledging possible error.
