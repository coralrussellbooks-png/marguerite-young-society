Summary

In silk hat and black cape, Mr. Spitzer keeps watch, unsure whether he himself is living or dead. Hannah's dying becomes a dance of death. She raves through deserts, mountains, and snow, speaks to Caesar, a blacksmith, and a figure she calls her love, asks for a mirror, and longs for silk skirts and parasols she scorned all her life. She asks whether she is the anvil or the hammer, and at the end she cries out for a lost beloved under the snow.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with vivid descriptions of Cousin Hannah’s struggle through dreamlike, stormy landscapes symbolizing her life’s battles and approaching death. The imagery of crossing deserts, climbing mountains, and clouds emphasizes the arduous journey of life and death, and the suffragette’s wild screams indicate confusion and loss of self-identity near death.
  • It is revealed that even this great lady “wept if a wandering snowflake touched her hand” and was "fragile as were all mortal things," prone to “wild dreams, visions, fantasies, presentiments, forebodings, fears of the unknown.” This humanizes her, showing vulnerability despite her public strength and ideals.
  • Mr. Spitzer himself is uncertain of his own state, “not sure who he was, whether living or dead,” and reflects on the confusion about who is dying and the nature of death. His belief that “the dead cast no vote” and his abstention from voting reflects his detachment from worldly affairs.
  • The narrative explores death as both an ending and a beginning, a “great crucible” where opposites meet, and where “death was that which blew us all along.” It describes Cousin Hannah’s cries and visions as part of a larger metaphysical process, suggesting death is not peace but a storm or dance of contradictory forces.
  • Cousin Hannah speaks to various mythic and historical figures like Caesar, a blacksmith, a golden archer, a blackamoor. asking profound questions about identity and fate (“Am I the anvil which is struck or the hammer which strikes?”). This symbolizes her grappling with agency, power, and the unknown as life fades.
  • The blackamoor, a mysterious figure she calls her love, and the “great lamp-lighter” who might light her way, appear repeatedly in her delirium. This imagery suggests a guide or companion in the transition from life to death, blending cultural and mystical symbols.
  • Mr. Spitzer reflects on how Cousin Hannah’s ravings mix ancient history, myth, and personal memories, with cities, armies, and landscapes blending into a timeless dreamscape. There is uncertainty whether these are real memories, dreams, or hallucinations, emphasizing the blurred boundary between life and death, past and present.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • A somber, enigmatic figure who carries a high silk hat and black cape, symbolic of mourning and ritual.
  • He is both witness and participant in the suffragette’s dying experience.
  • Reflects deeply on life, death, identity, and the nature of existence.
  • Struggles with his own sense of being alive or dead, voting or not voting, and the meaning of his presence.
  • His observations are philosophical, often pondering the illusions of life and the mysteries beyond death.

Cousin Hannah

  • A once powerful figure, now fragile and approaching death.
  • Engaged in a fierce, chaotic battle against death, manifested in vivid, surreal visions.
  • Experiences wild dreams, hallucinations, and shifting identities in her final hours.
  • Struggles with recognition of self and the meaning of life and death.
  • Exhibits vanity, fear, ecstasy, confusion, and moments of clarity.
  • Her final moments are a “dance of death,” a paradoxical mix of seriousness and frivolity.

Research: Themes

Life, Death, and the Transition Between

See: Death and Mortality

  • Death is depicted not as an end but a complex transformation, a journey through storm, cloud, snow, and desert.
  • The suffragette’s battle with death is fierce and confusing; death is both a closing and an opening (“Is the lid lowering or raising?”).
  • The ambiguity of death and existence is central, with Mr. Spitzer unsure if he or the suffragette is alive or dead.
  • Death is associated with loss, revelation, and paradox, often blending into life’s experiences.

Memory, Identity, and Illusion

See: Illusion and Reality · Memory and the Past · Identity and the Self

  • The suffragette’s past is fragmented and elusive, mixing real and imagined events.
  • Both characters wrestle with who they are and who others have been.
  • Dreams and memories flow outside of linear time, suggesting the permeability of identity.
  • Reality and illusion are intertwined; the suffragette’s visions may be symbolic rather than literal truth.

Vanity and Human Frailty

See: Decay and Ruin

  • Despite the gravity of death, vanity persists (“vanity of silken skirts,” “great parasols fringed by gold”).
  • The suffragette clings to images of beauty and love even as she fades.
  • The fragility of human existence is highlighted, especially in the face of death.

Love and Loss

See: Love · Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • The suffragette expresses deep, eternal love in her delirium, though the identity of lover and beloved is unclear.
  • Love and death blur together, raising the question if death extinguishes love or transforms it.
  • The paradox of love’s endurance beyond death is a poignant motif.

The Role of the Observer

  • Mr. Spitzer’s role as witness is complicated by his own uncertain status.
  • He balances grief with detachment and observation with participation.
  • He questions the reliability of memory and perception, acknowledging possible error.

Further notes from the text

  • Mr. Spitzer carries his high silk hat, “almost his only God,” and keeps watch unable to help, since he is as one already dead.
  • He wonders whether Hannah’s sudden longing for feminine frivolities is a last great joke, a parody of life at the moment life is fading. See Comedy in Chapter 43.
  • He had supposed that bodily death and real death would come together. Watching her, he begins to doubt it, and wonders whether the person dying now is someone he has not really seen since his forgotten youth.
  • Hannah’s delirium crosses the ancient world, Ur of the Chaldees, Nineveh, Crete, Babylon, and asks for her little boat and her figurehead, linking her dying to Catherine’s image of the figurehead in Chapter 40.
  • She cries out to the Black Moor, “beloved of woman,” the figure Mr. Spitzer later connects with the black coachman of Catherine’s dreams.
  • Her ravings repeat a paradox that sums up the novel’s method: look not into the things that are, to see the things that are not.
  • The chapter ends with her going down like a ship in a whirlpool, and Mr. Spitzer certain he will be on the other shore, not where the wreckage lands.

  • Eternalism (philosophy of time) is an approach to the ontological nature of time, which takes the view that all existence in time is equally real, as opposed to presentism or the growing block universe theory of time, in which at least the future is not the same as any other time. Some forms of eternalism give time a similar ontology to that of space, as a dimension, with different times being as real as different places, and future events are “already there” in the same sense other places are already there, and that there is no objective flow of time. It is sometimes referred to as the “block time” or “block universe” theory due to its description of space-time as an unchanging four-dimensional “block”, as opposed to the view of the world as a three-dimensional space modulated by the passage of time.
  • “this great emir of suffrage”: Emir comes from the Arabic amir, meaning commander or prince. It was the title of military leaders and rulers across the Islamic world. Calling Hannah an emir makes her a desert commander, which fits the armies, tents, and camel bells of her deathbed visions.
  • “So like a palmer she had wandered through this world”: A palmer was a medieval pilgrim who had traveled to the Holy Land and carried back a palm frond as proof. Some palmers had no home and spent their lives traveling between holy sites. The word suits Hannah, whose great cause kept her always on the road and never settled.
  • “the auk’s egg”: The great auk was a large flightless seabird of the North Atlantic. Hunters killed the last confirmed pair on the island of Eldey, off Iceland, in June 1844. After its extinction, its eggs became prized collector’s items that sold for large sums at Victorian auctions. Placing the egg among Hannah’s vanities joins beauty to extinction. The Iceland connection also ties it to Mt. Hekla, which appears later in the chapter.
  • “the glass barouche”: A barouche was a fashionable four-wheeled carriage of the nineteenth century. It had a folding half-hood and two double seats facing each other, and it was built for being seen in. A barouche made of glass could not exist, which puts it among the vanities that veil and display at once.
  • “the next horse race at Churchill Downs when the track was muddy”: Churchill Downs is the racetrack in Louisville, Kentucky, that opened in 1875 and has hosted the Kentucky Derby ever since. A muddy track favors certain horses and upsets the odds, which is exactly the uncertainty that drew Mr. Spitzer’s gambling brother. Mr. Spitzer judges that this talk of the future would be tactless at a deathbed. See On the Kentucky Derby and Black Jockeys (Ch 80).
  • “join the vast majority, just as the Romans said”: The Romans used the phrase “abiit ad plures,” meaning “he has gone to the many,” as a polite way to say someone had died. It appears in Petronius’s Satyricon. The English poet Edward Young used the same idea in his 1721 play The Revenge: “Death joins us to the great majority.” Mr. Spitzer turns the saying around and thinks the city of the dead might be “a smaller city.”
  • “Watchman, what hour?”: This is a version of Isaiah 21:11, “Watchman, what of the night?” In the prophecy, someone calls out from the darkness asking how long the night will last, and the watchman answers that “the morning cometh, and also the night.” The verse inspired John Bowring’s 1825 hymn “Watchman, Tell Us of the Night.” Hannah asks it as an old clock strikes, as if the hour of her death were a question someone on the wall could answer.
  • “what lazar demanding alms”: A lazar is a beggar with a disease, especially leprosy. The word comes from Lazarus, the beggar covered with sores in Luke 16, and it gave its name to the lazaretto, a hospital for contagious diseases. Lazarus of Bethany, who was raised from the dead, also haunts Mr. Spitzer’s world of the living dead. See Lazarus of Bethany (Ch 4).
  • “gardens of beautiful odalisques”: An odalisque was a servant woman in an Ottoman harem. The word comes from the Turkish odalık, meaning chambermaid. European painters turned the odalisque into a fantasy of the reclining, veiled woman, most famously in Ingres’s Grande Odalisque of 1814. These are women no man sees “unless he died,” hidden the way Hannah has hidden herself.
  • “came but with the neap tide”: A neap tide happens twice a month, when the sun and moon pull at right angles to each other at the first and third quarter moon. Neap tides have the smallest difference between high and low water. Young reverses this: Hannah’s “highest tide” arrives with the weakest tide of the month, and her life follows its own rhythm, “harnessed to no moon.”
  • “Is there no meeting place, place where two parallel lines meet?”: Euclid’s fifth postulate held that parallel lines never meet. In the nineteenth century, mathematicians such as Lobachevsky and Riemann built non-Euclidean geometries in which that rule no longer holds, and in projective geometry parallel lines meet at a point at infinity. Hannah’s question asks whether two lives that ran side by side without touching, like hers and her lost love’s, can meet at the end.
  • “ram’s horn”: The shofar is a trumpet made from a ram’s horn and blown in Jewish worship, especially at Rosh Hashanah. In Joshua 6, priests blow seven ram’s horns and the walls of Jericho fall. Hannah’s “great angel blowing his horn upon a lonely mountain top” also recalls the angels with trumpets in Revelation 8, who announce the end of the world. See On the Battle of Jericho (Ch 59).
  • “Was she the burning phoenix or the star?”: The phoenix is the mythical bird that lives for centuries, burns itself on a pyre, and rises again from its own ashes. Herodotus wrote that the Egyptians said it came to Heliopolis every five hundred years. Christians later took it as a symbol of resurrection. For Hannah, who believed in no future life, the question of rising again stays open.
  • “Then put out the light”: This echoes Othello, Act 5, Scene 2, where Othello stands over the sleeping Desdemona and says “Put out the light, and then put out the light,” before smothering her in her bed. Several things in Cousin Hannah’s deathbed cries point to the play: she calls to a “Black Moor, beloved of woman,” she asks whether the turban-winder has come “to strangle me so that I cannot cry out,” and she lies in a bed that has become a deathbed. The suffrage captain, who spent her life refusing the role of wife, casts herself at the end as Shakespeare’s most famous murdered wife. See Shakespeare.
  • “None should see the face of love and live”: This echoes Exodus 33:20, where God tells Moses, “Thou canst not see my face: for there shall no man see me, and live.” Hannah replaces God with love, which suggests that for her, love was the force too great to look at directly.
  • “Comes he not to the dying king?”: In 1 Samuel 16:23, the young David plays the harp for King Saul, and the troubling spirit leaves the king. Hannah calls for her court musician to come with “great gold harp strings,” which places her in the role of a tormented king in need of music. Mr. Spitzer, the musician who hears “the music of the dead,” sits beside her unrecognized.
  • “In heaven the black rooster lays the egg, and the white hen crows”: An old proverb says “A whistling woman and a crowing hen are neither fit for God nor men.” It was used against women who spoke out of their expected place, which makes a crowing hen a fitting image for a suffragette. A rooster laying an egg was considered unnatural and dangerous. Medieval legend held that a rooster’s egg hatched the basilisk, and in Basel in 1474 a rooster accused of laying an egg was tried and burned. Hannah’s heaven reverses the order of the barnyard. The hen crows, “and none shall stop her.”
  • “Is that the tent-maker? Is that the tent?”: The name of the Persian poet Omar Khayyam means “tentmaker.” Edward FitzGerald’s 1859 translation of his Rubáiyát made him famous in English as a poet of mortality, and in one quatrain the body is “but a Tent where takes his one day’s rest / A Sultan to the realm of Death addrest.” The apostle Paul was also a tentmaker by trade (Acts 18:3), and he called the body “our earthly house of this tabernacle,” a tent that will be taken down (2 Corinthians 5:1). Hannah, whose visions are full of desert tents, asks where the tent-maker sets his tent “upon what star or cloud” as her own tent is being struck.
  • “Look not into the things that are to see the things that are not”: This inverts 2 Corinthians 4:18, “While we look not at the things which are seen, but at the things which are not seen: for the things which are seen are temporal; but the things which are not seen are eternal.” Paul is urging faith. Hannah, who believed in no afterlife, is giving a warning.
  • “not even the greatest calker could calk her now”: A calker, now usually spelled caulker, forces fibers soaked in pitch or tar into the seams between a wooden ship’s planks to make it watertight. Mr. Spitzer’s “maritime memories derived from a remote forefather” turn Hannah’s dying body into an old ship coming apart at its seams.
  • “Is it the will-o’-the-wisp”: A will-o’-the-wisp is a ghostly light seen at night over marshes and bogs. It is also called ignis fatuus, Latin for “foolish fire.” In folklore it leads travelers off safe paths and into the swamp. It is now thought to come from burning marsh gases. Hannah asks whether she “must follow her who followed me,” which makes her lost love a light that is both a guide and a lure.
  • “Do I come again to that dark tower? Is that the winding horn?”: These questions recall Robert Browning’s 1855 poem “Childe Roland to the Dark Tower Came,” whose title comes from a line Edgar speaks in King Lear. Roland crosses a ruined wasteland to the dark tower where all the knights before him were lost, and the poem ends as he raises his horn: “Dauntless the slug-horn to my lips I set, / And blew.” Hannah, too, has come to the end of a long quest where others fell. See Childe Roland to the Dark Tower.

Helm
The wheel or tiller that steers a ship.
Ibis
A long-legged wading bird with a downward-curved bill, sacred in ancient Egypt to Thoth, god of writing and wisdom.
Immured
Shut in or imprisoned, as if walled up.
Imponderable
Something that cannot be measured, weighed, or judged.
Inscrutable
Impossible to understand or interpret.
Inveterate
Long settled in a habit and unlikely to change, as in “inveterate gambler.”
Keel and list
To keel over is to capsize, with the keel, the ship’s backbone along the bottom, turning upward. To list is to lean to one side, usually from water taken on.
Lagoon
A stretch of shallow water separated from the sea by a reef or sandbar.
Lazar
A diseased beggar, especially one with leprosy. More under Resources.
Lethargy
Sluggishness, drowsiness, a lack of energy.
Livid
Bluish-gray, the color of a bruise. Also furiously angry.
Lunar month
The time from one new moon to the next, about 29.5 days.
Lute
A plucked string instrument with a rounded back shaped like half a pear, popular in Europe from the Middle Ages through the 1700s.
Maharajah
A great king or prince in India. From the Sanskrit for “great king.”
Mandarin
A high-ranking official of imperial China.
Medusa
A jellyfish in its free-swimming, bell-shaped stage, named for the Gorgon of Greek myth whose hair was snakes.
Mercurial
Quick and changeable in mood. From the swift Roman messenger god Mercury and the liquid metal named for him.
Metamorphic
Having to do with a change of form. In geology, rock that heat and pressure have changed.
Mica
A mineral that splits into thin, shiny, nearly transparent sheets and makes rock glitter.
Minaret
A tall, slender tower of a mosque, from which the call to prayer is given.
Mirage
An optical illusion, common in deserts, in which hot air bends light so that distant water or cities seem to appear.
Monsoon
A seasonal wind in South Asia that brings months of heavy rain.
Mosaic
A picture or pattern made by setting small pieces of colored stone, glass, or tile together.
Nag
An old or worn-out horse.
Neap tide
The tide with the smallest difference between high and low water, twice a month at the quarter moons. More under Resources.
Negligee
A woman’s light, flowing dressing gown.
Nepenthe
In Homer’s Odyssey, a drug from Egypt that Helen puts in the wine to banish grief and sorrow. Anything that brings forgetfulness of pain. See On Nepenthe (Ch 82).
Odalisque
A servant woman or concubine in a Turkish harem. More under Resources.
Pagoda
An Asian tiered tower with multiple eaves, usually a Buddhist temple or shrine.
Palmer
A medieval pilgrim who brought back a palm frond from the Holy Land. More under Resources.
Paradox
A statement or situation that seems to contradict itself and yet may be true.
Parasol
A light umbrella carried for shade from the sun.
Parterre
An ornamental garden of flower beds laid out in a pattern. A parterred floor is inlaid in patterned sections.
Perambulations
Walks or strolls, especially leisurely ones.
Peregrinations
A journey, especially a long or meandering one.
Phantasmal
Ghostly; like a phantom or an illusion.
Pinnacle
The highest point; a tall, pointed mountain peak or the slender spire on a building.
Portfolio
A flat case for carrying loose papers and documents.
Presentiment
An intuitive feeling that something is about to happen.
Principality
A territory ruled by a prince.
Prism
A clear piece of glass with flat sides that splits light into the colors of the rainbow.
Promontory
A high point of land jutting out into the sea.
Roseate
Rose-colored. Also hopeful or overly optimistic.
Rubicon
A small river in northern Italy. Julius Caesar crossed it with his army in 49 B.C., starting a civil war, so “crossing the Rubicon” means passing a point of no return. See Crossing the Rubicon (Ch 23).
Sandalwood
A fragrant wood from India and the Pacific, burned as incense and used in perfume.
Sartorial
Having to do with clothing, tailoring, or style of dress.
Seer
A person who sees the future; a prophet.
Shah
The title of the king of Persia, now Iran.
Shell game
A swindle in which a pea is hidden under one of three shells or cups and shuffled, and bettors guess where it is. The operator usually palms the pea, so no one can win.
Skiff
A small, light boat for rowing or sailing. Young’s “skiff moon” is a crescent shaped like one.
Spar
A pole used as a ship’s mast, boom, or yard.
Steeplechase
A horse race over fences, ditches, and water jumps, first run cross-country from one church steeple to another.
Suffragette
A woman who campaigned for women’s right to vote. The word was coined by a London newspaper in 1906 for militant British activists. Most American activists called themselves suffragists. See Women’s Suffrage.
Sultanate
A country ruled by a sultan.
Supernumerary
Extra; more than the number needed. In the theater, an extra with no lines.
Surrey
A light four-wheeled horse-drawn carriage with two seats, often with a fringed canopy on top.
Testimonial
A formal statement given as evidence or proof of something.
Tonnage
The size or carrying capacity of a ship.
Torpor
A sluggish, heavy, half-asleep state.
Touch
Slang for asking someone for money, especially a small loan. To “make a touch” is to borrow or beg cash.
Transact
To carry out or carry through. Young uses it for crossing a distance, as in “transacted the gulf between two worlds.”
Transmutation
A change from one form or substance into another, especially the alchemists’ dream of turning base metal into gold.
Turret
A small tower on a building or castle.
Untenable
Impossible to defend, hold, or live in.
Vigil
A period of staying awake to keep watch, especially at the bedside of someone sick or dying.
