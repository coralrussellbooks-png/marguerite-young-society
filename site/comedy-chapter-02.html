Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Allusions: Ancient Egypt · Dante · Exploration and the Poles

Definition from research

The comedy of the pregnant girl on the bus is satire by accumulation. Young dresses her in the entire world of athletics at once, a silk print of "black sail-boats sailing over purple trees and red football players playing over steeples and white skiers skiing over sail-boats," with tennis players on her scarf, footballs and golf clubs on her charm bracelets, and, crowning it all, a hat holding "one stuffed yellow canary with a moth-eaten wing." The effect is a portrait of identity assembled entirely from cheap sporting motifs, a person who is all surface trophy and no interior. The bus driver's gruff running commentary belongs here too, his self-satisfied rant delivered as "a drunken bus-driver, rolling along here in my own feather bed," a man who narrates his own bravado to an audience of no one.

The mother's dead chauffeur, James, is one of the chapter's steadiest comic runners. An ex-convict who long ago absconded with her automobile, he lives on in her mind as a driver perpetually en route to the Arctic, "still driving to Alaska, still evading, of course, the stupid police." She rides beside this absence for years, issuing orders through an imaginary speaking tube, "Home, James, she would say, helplessly, for where is home?" and then, indifferent to every signal, "Go through the red light. Pay no attention to the other drivers with cars and horses." The dark comic turn is that she knows he means to kill her once they reach that frozen country and still cannot stop giving directions, a passenger commanding her own murderer down streets that never end.

The canary is comedy built on a con the invalid never sees. Because her life depends on the little yellow bird outliving her, Mr. Spitzer keeps a supply of spares and quietly swaps a living one for each that dies, so that she believes a single bird has lived through all the years. She renames this immortal creature as the mood takes her, a rotating roll call of explorers and poets, "Juniper, Tulip, Cellini, Solomon, Methuselah, Marco Polo, Admiral Peary, Shackleton, Dante," imagining she may yet keep an "octogenarian canary." The joke lands when she watches Marco Polo escape through an open transom and, rather than grieve, concludes with perfect calm that she must now live forever, since no one could find one lost canary in a hurricane.

The gold-turbaned Egyptian is the chapter's best single scene of mistaken identity. Announced as a spirit from the underworld and received as one, he proves to be an old horse-racing acquaintance whose sacred document, a "papyrus which might have come from the tomb of a pharaoh," turns out under candlelight to be "a race dope sheet listing those Arabian horses no longer running." The comedy is Mr. Spitzer's laborious, lawyerly effort to convince the dancing visitor that he is dead and has come to the wrong man, an argument he nearly has to settle by "lifting him by his shirt-tail." It is the purest instance of the book's running gag, a literal man trying to reason a dream back out the door.

The dead grandfather supplies a strain of religious absurdity. A self-made manufacturer, he ran his household "like a church although perhaps he was its only member," kept "a place set at his table for the wanderer angel," and turned no beggar from his door "for fear it might be an angel in disguise." The comedy sharpens in his blindness, when he is led on his garden rounds by a Seeing Eye dog that is itself blind "yet never lost his path," the two of them pacing the dark walks together, each trusting the other to see. The joke and the pathos arrive in the same image, which is the chapter's whole method in miniature.

A smaller recurring gag attaches to Mr. Spitzer's cane. The suspicion follows him that he carries "a small whiskey flask inside the sphere of his ivory-headed cane," stopping on the long sea road to take "a nip like some old Pan among the silver reeds." He denies it with wounded dignity, insisting the cane holds only a slip of parchment with instructions to be opened at his death, a solemn cover story for what may be nothing grander than a hidden drink.

One tiny bookend joke is worth catching. Among the mother's furnishings are "twelve grand pianos in the house, and no one ever played them," and the number refuses to hold still, for by the book's end the flood has carried off the twelve pianos and "the thirteen pianos, one of which never was." The inventory of her dreams keeps gaining an instrument that never existed, a small running comic accounting of things that were never there.
