Definition from research

What Her Life Proposes

Cousin Hannah Freemount-Snowden never appears in the novel alive. She is remembered by Catherine and investigated by Mr. Spitzer after her death, and her story fills much of the middle of the book. A close reading shows that her life proposes a system distinct from all the others: the life of action. She is a suffragette, an explorer, a mountain climber, a duelist, a marksman. She “had gone by camel into places where no woman had ever gone before” (ch. 30). Where Catherine dreams and Mr. Spitzer hesitates, Hannah acts.

Her system rests on certainty. “She had compensated for her superficial views by a marvelous certainty that was set above the peaks of time” (ch. 30). She lived “by her inscrutable choices as if there had been no alternative, no road except the road she had taken, no ship except the ship in which she had sailed.” She “had always believed in being direct” (ch. 31). And her certainty made further inquiry unnecessary. She acted as if she had “already seen into all the dark abysses of human nature,” and “so she had had no use for further experience or knowledge” (ch. 30).

This is the philosophy of the deed: the conviction that thought is secondary, that the world is changed by those who move rather than those who wonder. It has noble forms, and Hannah’s cause, the vote for women, was a just one. The novel never mocks the cause. It tests the certainty.

The Loading

Young loads Hannah’s life with a secret that her certainty was built to cover. The narrator hints at it early: “Because of the loss of a skirt, it was known now, her entire life had been perhaps an act of sorrow and redemption” (ch. 30). As Mr. Spitzer’s researches continue, the story comes into view. On a mountain long ago, Hannah climbed with a young woman she loved, and in a snowstorm she “had let go her girl’s hand, her dove-white fluted hand in a snow storm long ago as in another life” (ch. 45). The girl was lost. The crusade that followed, the forty voyages and the battles against men, grew from that one moment of letting go.

The loading also comes from her own divided nature. Catherine calls her “a soul of vast duplicity” and imagines she could play two hands at cards, being “both herself and a gentleman” (ch. 30). The woman who lived by directness was never simple. And her certainty about the future turns out to be dreamlike too. She was said to have “known of the murder of the archduke at Sarajevo several hours before the news had reached the rest of the world” (ch. 30), knowledge as uncanny as anything in Catherine’s opium visions.

The Break

The break comes at her death, and it comes twice. First, there is the reported deathbed confession: “that she no longer believed in the glory of these foreign conquests” and that “The greatest battles were fought in the domestic wars by those who had never left home” (ch. 32). The woman of action, at the end, turns against action. But the novel immediately undercuts even this: “it was impossible to know how the great captain could have made this death-bed confession,” since she had not answered her telephone in years. The confession may be Catherine’s invention. Even the collapse of the system cannot be verified.

Second, there is the delirium Mr. Spitzer overhears as she dies, and here the Nietzschean images gather. She cries, “Shall I fall into the great abyss of which there is no floor, no ceiling? Shall I fall into love or death? Are both one?” (ch. 43). And in the forty-fifth chapter, climbing in her fever through mountains of snow, she sees the dwarf “a turret on his head.” Nietzsche’s Zarathustra climbed with the spirit of gravity on his back and, at the summit gateway, overcame it with the thought of eternal return. Hannah climbs with her grief and her lost girl, and the dwarf she meets wears the tower of the house of illusion. The heroic ascent ends not in affirmation but in the question of whether love and death are one.

Catherine delivers the final verdict, and it is devastating in its gentleness. “Cousin Hannah’s life, just like her own, had been largely imaginary, transient, unknown, unexplored, passing like a dream” (ch. 41). Then comes the question that dissolves the whole distinction on which Hannah built her life: “how should action differ from meditation?” The woman who acted and the woman who dreamed turn out to have lived in the same condition. Time and sorrow, the narrator adds, were “all she had ever denied.”

What Is Left

Mr. Spitzer’s researches uncover one more image, and it carries Young’s politics. According to an old newspaper story, Hannah once “skied, according to one old newspaper reporter dreaming of her,” with “blind skiers following a leader’s bell, and none had known that which Mr. Spitzer knew, that the leader was blind” (ch. 52). The leader of the crusade was as blind as the followers. This does not make the cause wrong. It makes the certainty dangerous.

What remains of Hannah is her love, which her crusade never mentioned and never healed, and her forty unopened trunks. Mr. Spitzer tries every key and opens none, and he does not break them. The life of action leaves behind a store of experience that no single key can open, and a man patient enough to leave it closed.
