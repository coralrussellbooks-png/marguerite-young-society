Chapter 71

Characters: Esther Longtree · Vera Cartwheel

The Questions

A Christian hangman has spent the night in the inn. Is his guilt real, or a “fancy” Vera builds in the dark?

The hangman has “hanged so many… wife murderers, child murderers.” What is a man whose office is the final closure of others?

Vera imagines “the hangman should hang himself at last.” What happens when the agent of the last seal turns it on himself?

After the long night of voices, Vera is “wide awake… ready to go forth.” What is she ready for?

The chapter closes the long night of the inn on a figure of final closure. Somewhere in the “desolate Indiana hotel” has slept a Christian hangman, a man whose office is the last seal there is, the ending of others by rope, and Vera lies awake wondering which room held him, whether the feet she heard “dancing upon the floor above” were his or the mice’s. The hangman is the book’s image of finality itself, the one who closes the account of a life for good, and the chapter sets him among the night’s uncertain sounds so that even he, the agent of the definite end, arrives wrapped in the same doubt as everything else. His guilt, his wife’s death, the very sound of his tread, are all “fancy” and question, unsealed like the rest.

Vera’s waking thought turns the final seal on itself. It seems to her “the extension of logic” that “the hangman, having hanged so many men… should hang himself at last.” The one who deals the last closure to others should deal it to himself, the sealer sealed, the judge become the judged. This is the book’s deepest suspicion of finality played out on its purest agent: the impulse to close, to end, to pronounce the last sentence, turns in the end upon the one who wields it. A man whose life is the sealing of other lives is a man logically bound, at last, to seal his own, and the chapter follows that logic to its grim and fitting end.

The uncertainty around the hangman unsettles even judgment. The chapter cannot say whether his guilt is real or fancied, whether his wife hanged herself or the memory is distorted, and this doubt reaches the one figure who is supposed to embody certain judgment, the man who decides who is guilty enough to die. If even the hangman’s own guilt cannot be settled, then the apparatus of final judgment, of guilt pronounced and closure enforced, rests on the same shifting ground as everything the book has unsealed. The judge is as uncertain as the judged, and the rope that looks like the one sure thing hangs over a doubt as deep as any other.

And Vera is ready to go forth. After the night of “voices, cryings, rustlings, doors opening,” she is “wide awake, alert, watchful, ready to go forth into a cold, grey, silent morning.” She does not sleep in the heart of the mystery, does not wait for the denouement; she rises and goes on. This is the seeker at the threshold of the book’s last movement, leaving the inn of final closures, the hangman and his rope and his uncertain guilt behind her, walking out into the grey morning that will bring her to Esther Longtree. She is ready, and what she is ready for is the counter-figure to every seal she has met, the one who does not close at all.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes the hangman for a figure of settled, righteous judgment, and the reading that takes his imagined self-hanging for mere morbid fancy. Neither the certain judge nor the idle morbidity is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that the hangman, the agent of the final seal, arrives wrapped in the same doubt as everything else, his guilt and his history unsettled, so that even the pure figure of finality is unsealed; that the logic of closure turns on its own wielder, the sealer bound at last to seal himself, the judge become the judged; and that Vera rises from the night of endings ready to go forth, leaving the inn of final closures for the morning that brings her on. Even finality is uncertain, and the seal turns on the one who deals it. Vera walks out into the grey toward the figure who does not close, and the chapter sends her forth rather than settling her.

For Reflection

Is the agent of final judgment any more certain, underneath, than the ones judged?

Does the impulse to seal and close and pronounce the last word tend, in the end, to turn on the one who wields it?

What is one ready to go forth toward, once the night of endings is over?
