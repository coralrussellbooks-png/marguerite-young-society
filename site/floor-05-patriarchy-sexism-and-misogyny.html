The System, the Ideology, and the Enforcement

A Companion Document to the Constitution

The Philosophical Foundation

The provisions in this constitution's companion documents on women and children, healthcare, criminal justice, and economic rights address specific harms: the wage gap, maternal mortality, domestic violence, reproductive coercion, the exclusion of women from medical research. This document addresses the system that produces those harms and the mechanisms that maintain it.

That system is patriarchy: the organization of social, economic, and political life around the interests and authority of men, sustained across generations not only by law but by culture, by institutions, and by the enforcement of roles that the system requires women to fill. Formal legal equality, the removal of explicit legal barriers to women's participation in public life, does not dismantle patriarchy. It removes one layer of its enforcement while leaving the others intact. The woman who is legally permitted to hold any job but is passed over for promotion because she is perceived as too aggressive, too ambitious, or insufficiently likable is experiencing patriarchy without experiencing explicit legal discrimination. The law cannot see what happened to her. The system is working exactly as designed.

This document draws on the framework developed by philosopher Kate Manne, whose work distinguishes between two components of the patriarchal system that are often conflated: sexism and misogyny. Understanding the distinction is essential to understanding why formal legal equality has not produced substantive equality and what substantive equality would actually require.

Sexism and Misogyny: The Distinction

Sexism is the ideology that justifies gender hierarchy. It is the set of beliefs about what women are, what they are naturally suited for, what they are naturally unsuited for, and why the current arrangement of social roles reflects something true about human nature rather than something constructed by people who benefited from it. Sexism says: women are more nurturing, so they naturally belong in caregiving roles. Women are less rational, so they naturally belong outside positions of authority. Women are more emotional, so their testimony is naturally less reliable. Sexism presents the hierarchy as natural order. It is the explanation the system gives for itself.

Misogyny is the enforcement mechanism. Where sexism explains why women occupy subordinate roles, misogyny polices the boundaries of those roles and punishes women who deviate from them. Misogyny is not primarily about hatred, though hatred is one of its expressions. It is about enforcement: the social, institutional, and sometimes physical punishment of women who fail to fulfill what the system expects of them, who claim what the system has not allocated to them, or who refuse what the system requires them to provide.

The woman who is likable and accommodating and does not seek authority or recognition beyond what is offered to her experiences relatively little misogyny. The system has no need to punish her because she is complying. The woman who seeks power, makes claims, refuses care work, expresses anger, reports harm, or occupies space the system has not assigned to her experiences misogyny as the system's correction. She is told she is too aggressive, too difficult, too emotional, not credible, not likable. She is passed over, talked over, pushed out, threatened, and in the most extreme cases subjected to violence. The punishment is proportional to the degree of deviation from what the system requires.

This distinction matters for policy because it identifies two different kinds of work that dismantling patriarchy requires. Addressing sexism requires changing beliefs: the cultural work of challenging the ideological justifications for gender hierarchy, the educational work of teaching the history of how those justifications were constructed and what purposes they served. Addressing misogyny requires changing enforcement mechanisms: the institutional work of removing the systems that punish women for deviating from prescribed roles, the legal work of making the enforcement visible and accountable, and the cultural work of recognizing misogynistic enforcement for what it is rather than accepting the system's framing of it as natural response to women's deficiencies.

What the System Requires Women to Provide

Manne's framework identifies a set of things the patriarchal system positions women as providers of and men as recipients of. These are not arbitrary. They map onto the specific harms documented in the other companion documents.

Care and emotional labor are the most fundamental. Women are positioned as the natural providers of care: for children, for aging parents, for sick family members, for the emotional needs of the men in their lives, for the social fabric of communities. The expectation is not only that women will perform this care but that they will do so willingly, without complaint, and without requiring equivalent care in return. The woman who provides care without complaint is fulfilling her role. The woman who refuses care work, resents it, or demands reciprocity is failing at something the system has told her is natural to her. The economic consequences of this positioning are documented in the women and children companion document: the unpaid labor, the wage penalty for caregiving, the retirement poverty that follows a life of underpaid or unpaid care work.

Testimony and credibility are allocated unequally by the system. Women's testimony about their own experiences, their pain, their symptoms, their reports of harm done to them, is systematically discounted relative to men's testimony about the same events. The healthcare companion document documents one dimension of this: the woman whose pain is attributed to anxiety, whose symptoms are dismissed, whose report of her own body is treated as less reliable than the provider's external assessment. The criminal justice system documents another: the woman whose report of sexual assault is treated with skepticism disproportionate to the skepticism applied to reports of other crimes, whose credibility is assessed against standards applied to no other category of crime victim.

The discounting of women's testimony is not random. It is structural. It reflects the ideology that women are less rational, more emotional, more likely to be mistaken or dishonest about their own experiences. That ideology has no empirical basis. It is the sexist justification for a misogynistic practice: the practice of disbelieving women in ways that protect the men their testimony implicates and the institutions their testimony challenges.

Space and authority are allocated by the system in ways that position women as occupying them on sufferance rather than by right. The woman in a position of authority who is described as bossy, shrill, aggressive, or unlikable for behavior that in a man would be described as decisive, confident, or authoritative is encountering the enforcement mechanism. The qualities required for authority, directness, confidence, the willingness to make decisions and defend them, are coded masculine by the ideology. The woman who displays them is deviating from her role and is punished for it. The punishment discourages other women from seeking authority and maintains the gender composition of powerful institutions in ways that formal legal equality cannot address because the mechanism is not legal.

Misogyny and the Credibility Economy

The credibility economy is Manne's term for the system by which credibility, the social resource of being believed and taken seriously, is distributed unequally along gender lines. In the credibility economy, men begin with more credibility than women by default. A woman's claim to credibility requires demonstration in a way that a man's does not. Her testimony is presumed less reliable until proven otherwise. His is presumed reliable until proven otherwise. The asymmetry is not neutral. It has consequences for every domain in which women make claims: in medicine, in law, in the workplace, in personal relationships, and in public life.

The credibility economy is most visible in how reports of sexual violence are treated. The woman who reports sexual assault faces a scrutiny applied to no other crime victim: her prior sexual history, her relationship to the accused, her behavior before and after the assault, her emotional presentation, her delay in reporting, and her motivations for reporting are all treated as relevant to the credibility of her account. The accused's credibility is not subjected to equivalent scrutiny. The asymmetry is so normalized that it is often invisible to the people applying it. It presents itself as the reasonable assessment of competing claims. It is the enforcement mechanism applied to women who make the specific claim of being harmed by men.

The credibility economy extends beyond sexual violence. The woman who reports workplace discrimination, harassment, or retaliation faces the same asymmetry: her account is assessed against a presumption of unreliability that the account of the person she is reporting is not. The woman whose medical symptoms are attributed to anxiety rather than investigated is encountering the credibility economy in the examination room. The woman whose anger at genuine harm is described as disproportionate, hysterical, or threatening is encountering it in the domain of emotional expression. In each case the mechanism is the same: her account of her own experience is treated as less reliable than the system's account of what her experience should be.

This constitution's provisions address the credibility economy through specific institutional requirements rather than through direct regulation of belief. The requirement that medical providers document and investigate reported symptoms rather than attributing them to psychological causes without investigation is a credibility economy intervention. The requirement that reports of sexual violence be investigated with the same seriousness as reports of other crimes of comparable severity is a credibility economy intervention. The requirement that workplace discrimination complaints be adjudicated by processes that do not place the burden of proof on the complainant at a level higher than other civil claims is a credibility economy intervention. None of these requirements can change what individuals believe. All of them can change what institutions are required to do regardless of what individuals believe.

Misogyny Directed at Specific Groups of Women

Misogyny does not operate uniformly. The enforcement of patriarchal roles is shaped by race, class, disability, sexual orientation, and gender identity in ways that produce different and compounding harms for different groups of women.

Black Women

The misogyny directed at Black women operates within and through racism in ways that have a specific history and specific contemporary expressions. The controlling images that Patricia Hill Collins documents in her work, the mammy, the jezebel, the angry Black woman, the strong Black woman who does not need care, are the ideological constructions that justify specific patterns of mistreatment: the dismissal of Black women's pain in medical settings, the hypersexualization that affects how Black women's reports of sexual violence are received, the penalization of Black women's anger in workplaces and courtrooms, the expectation that Black women will provide emotional labor for everyone around them while receiving none in return. The maternal mortality disparity documented in the healthcare companion document is one outcome of this intersection. It is not the only one.

Women with Disabilities

Women with disabilities encounter misogyny shaped by the assumption that disabled women are asexual, that their reproductive choices are less legitimate, that their testimony about their own experiences is less reliable because disability is presumed to compromise judgment. The history of forced sterilization documented in the historical grief companion document falls disproportionately on disabled women. The assumption that disabled women's reports of abuse are less credible, combined with their frequent dependence on caregivers who may be the source of the abuse, produces a specific vulnerability that the system has historically not addressed.

Queer Women and Trans Women

The misogyny directed at lesbian and bisexual women is shaped by the specific threat that women who do not organize their lives around men represent to the system: the woman whose primary relationships, economic arrangements, and emotional investments are not centered on men is not providing what the system expects her to provide. The enforcement takes forms including the erasure of lesbian relationships as less real or less serious than heterosexual ones, the hypersexualization of bisexual women, and the specific violence directed at queer women that the research documents as distinct from the violence directed at straight women.

Trans women encounter misogyny that is compounded by transphobia in ways that produce specific and severe harms. The denial that trans women are women is itself a form of misogyny: it defines womanhood in ways that police who is permitted to claim it and punishes those who claim it without permission. The violence directed at trans women, which falls disproportionately on Black trans women, is among the most severe expressions of the enforcement mechanism this document describes.

Institutions That Enforce Patriarchy

Misogyny is not only a set of individual attitudes. It is enforced through institutions whose structures, practices, and cultures reproduce the hierarchy regardless of the individual attitudes of the people within them.

The Legal System

The legal system has been built predominantly by men, from the common law traditions that treated women as legal non-persons, through the constitutional interpretations that excluded women from the rights the constitution was understood to protect, to the current doctrines that make specific forms of gender-based harm difficult to litigate. The evidentiary rules that allow a rape defendant to introduce the complainant's prior sexual history, the standing doctrines that make it difficult to bring structural discrimination claims, the damages frameworks that undercompensate emotional and relational harm relative to economic harm: these are not neutral technical legal rules. They are the accumulated product of a legal system built without women's interests at the center.

The reform of the legal system's enforcement of patriarchy requires more than the addition of specific protections, though specific protections are necessary. It requires the examination of which doctrines, rules, and practices have the effect of making gender-based harm invisible or non-compensable, and the deliberate redesign of those doctrines to make the full range of harm visible and accountable.

The Workplace

The workplace enforces patriarchy through structures that appear neutral but produce gendered outcomes. The definition of the ideal worker as someone without caregiving responsibilities outside work is a structural enforcement of the expectation that women will absorb caregiving so that men can meet the ideal worker standard. The penalty for taking parental leave, documented across industries, falls more heavily on men who take leave than the ideology would predict and more heavily on women than formal equality would predict. The performance evaluation systems that rate women lower for the same behaviors that earn men higher ratings are enforcing the credibility economy and the role expectations simultaneously.

The specific workplace protections in the women and children companion document address some of these structural enforcement mechanisms. This document names the underlying system they are part of so that the protections can be understood as doing more than addressing isolated unfairness. They are interventions in a system of enforcement that will find new mechanisms if the underlying logic is not named and challenged.

Religious Institutions

Religious institutions occupy a specific position in the enforcement of patriarchy because their authority claims are not subject to the same legal accountability as other institutions and because their ideological function in justifying gender hierarchy is explicit rather than implicit. The religious justifications for women's subordination, their exclusion from religious authority, their assignment to domestic roles, and the restrictions on their reproductive and sexual autonomy, are the sexist ideology in its most direct form: the claim that the hierarchy reflects divine order rather than human construction.

This constitution does not regulate the internal doctrines of religious institutions. It protects religious liberty as a fundamental right. It also establishes that religious liberty does not extend to the use of religious doctrine as justification for denying women the constitutional rights this document guarantees. The religious institution that discriminates in its internal governance is exercising religious liberty. The religious institution that uses its resources and political influence to restrict the legal rights of people who are not its members is doing something else. That distinction is the line this constitution draws.

What Dismantling Looks Like

Dismantling patriarchy is not a single policy intervention. It is the ongoing work of identifying and changing the mechanisms that enforce gender hierarchy across every domain of social, economic, and political life. This constitution's companion documents address specific domains. This document names the system they are all responding to and identifies what the work looks like at the level of the whole.

At the level of law and policy, dismantling means the specific provisions in the companion documents: equal pay, universal childcare, universal healthcare including reproductive care, the credibility reforms in the legal system, the workplace protections, the domestic violence provisions. These are necessary. They are not sufficient.

At the level of institutions, dismantling means the examination of which institutional structures, practices, and cultures enforce gender hierarchy and the deliberate redesign of those structures. This includes the legal reforms described above, the medical training reforms described in the healthcare document, the curriculum reforms described in the education document, and the workplace structure reforms described in the economic document. It also includes the accountability mechanisms that make it possible to identify when institutions are enforcing patriarchy and to require them to stop.

At the level of culture, dismantling means the long work of changing the beliefs that justify the hierarchy: the educational work, the media work, the public discourse work of naming sexism and misogyny for what they are rather than accepting the system's framing of them as natural responses to women's characteristics. This constitution's public media provisions are relevant here: a public media system obligated to represent the full range of human experience, including women's experience on its own terms rather than in relation to men's, is doing cultural work that the market-driven media system does not do.

None of this work has an end point. The ideology that justifies gender hierarchy is old and deeply embedded. The enforcement mechanisms are adaptive: when one is removed, the system finds others. The work is iterative. Each pass finds what the previous one walked past.
