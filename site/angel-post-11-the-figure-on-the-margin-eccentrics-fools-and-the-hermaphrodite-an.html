Angel in the Forest

Chapter 11. The Figure on the Margin: Eccentrics, Fools, and the Hermaphrodite Angel

← Chapter 10 · Angel in the Forest: A Reading in Fourteen Chapters · Chapter 12 →

In the first pages of Angel in the Forest, before she has told you much of anything about history, Young gives you a list of the people who once walked the streets of New Harmony. It is one of the strangest sentences in the book. She lists, among others, an angel like a hermaphrodite butterfly, a Daniel Boone of the infinite, a finite Elijah, a herb doctor, a celibate breeder of horses, the Spirit of Nature, rational man, irrational men. She does not stop to explain any of them. She sets them down, one after another, and lets them stand, and then she tells you that the list does not begin to exhaust the possibilities. This is not a decorative flourish. It is a thesis about where truth is found. The people who do not fit are not the exceptions to New Harmony. They are the clearest description of it.

This is the seventh pillar of Radical Immanence, and Young builds a method on it. The idea is easy to state and hard to live by. Categories look natural from the inside. It is only at the edges, where someone fails to fit, that you can see the category was drawn by hand. A person who is neither fully male nor fully female shows you that the line between male and female was a decision. A person who is at once celibate and a breeder shows you that abstention and fecundity were only ever placed on opposite sides of a border someone invented. The eccentric is not a footnote to the system. The eccentric is the exposed seam, the place where the stitching shows.

The hermaphrodite butterfly angel

Look at the figures Young chose, because each one collapses a distinction the community depended on. The hermaphrodite butterfly angel folds together the angelic and the animal, the male and the female, the celestial and the earthly. A butterfly is an insect. An angel is a spirit. A hermaphrodite is neither one sex nor the other. Stack all three into a single figure and you have a being that cannot be filed under any of the community’s headings. It is heaven and dirt at once. Later Young turns the same figure against the Owenites, when Owen, speaking from Rapp’s old pulpit, declares the worship of “the hermaphrodite butterfly, exaggerated to the status of angel” happily finished. But you cannot legislate a figure like that out of existence. It survives precisely because it refuses the category. The thing both utopias wanted gone is the thing that reveals what both utopias were built to exclude.

The celibate breeder of horses

Rapp’s community practiced total celibacy and yet ran, in part, on the breeding of animals. The order that would not reproduce itself made a living reproducing horses. Hold that contradiction and you can see the shape of the Rappite economy in one impossible person: abstention for the people, fecundity for the livestock, the reproductive drive forbidden in humans and harnessed in the barn. The celibate breeder is not a joke. He is the Rappite economy walking around on two legs.

The Daniel Boone of the infinite

Young takes the great figure of American frontier expansion and aims him at metaphysics. Boone went looking for elbow room in the wilderness. The Daniel Boone of the infinite goes looking for elbow room in eternity, in the endless spiritual real estate of a New Jerusalem or a New Moral World. He collapses the frontier and the afterlife, the surveyor and the mystic. He shows you that the utopian and the pioneer are the same person facing two different directions, and that the promised land of the soul and the promised land of the map were always the same appetite.

The Fool of Nature

Young gives a chapter the title “The Fool of Nature,” and the fool keeps returning throughout the book, not as one man but as a recurring presence. He is the figure who, by refusing or failing to keep the social grammar, exposes how arbitrary that grammar is. When the fool speaks, the sensible people around him look, for a moment, no more sensible than he is. Near the end, Young lets the Fool rise like Triton from the waves and turn into an octopus with a golden book in every hand, and a different writing in every book. That is the fool’s function. He holds a different reading of the world in each hand and refuses to settle on one. The universe, from where he stands, is a series of illusions of grandeur, and he is right, and everyone treats him as mad. The community needs him to be mad. If he is sane, then its single official reading of the world is only one book among many.

Count Maximilian de Leon

And then there is the false messiah, who earns his own section because Young’s handling of him is a small masterpiece. Into Rapp’s community, which had waited decades for the second Coming of One, there arrives a letter announcing Count Maximilian de Leon, “the Great Ambassador and Anointed One of God,” descended from “the Stem of Judah and the Root of David,” child of Moses and Abraham and Solomon, the Lion of the Tribe of Judah. The Rappites line the streets. The band plays from the church tower. The herald blows his trumpet. And out of the curtained carriage steps a corpulent, blond gentleman in purple and green broadcloth, a golden book open in his green-gloved hands. It is very funny. But Young is doing more than comedy. De Leon is not simply a con man who fooled a gullible sect. He is the logical product of the sect’s own grammar. A community that organizes its entire existence around awaiting a single anointed figure has, in effect, issued an open invitation. When you build a world that requires a messiah, a messiah will arrive, and he will look exactly like this: fat, blond, green-gloved, and completely certain. The fraud is not a failure of the system. It is the system working as designed. The margin, once again, says the thing the center cannot say about itself.

What the self actually looks like

These figures share one more thing, and it points at the first pillar as much as the seventh. The hermaphrodite angel, the celibate breeder, the Daniel Boone of the infinite are all figures of plural, unstable selfhood. Each of them is two or three things at once that are supposed to be mutually exclusive. Each is exactly what a self looks like once you drop the demand that a person be one consistent thing. Both utopias ran on that demand. Rapp needed his people single-mindedly bound for heaven. Owen needed his uniformly rational. The eccentrics are the ones who would not collapse into a single identity, and Young loves them for it, because they are the most honest people in the book. They are not failing to be whole. They are showing you that a person was never one thing to begin with.

It is hard, in the end, not to see Young herself in the hermaphrodite butterfly angel. She is a writer who will not be filed. Angel in the Forest is history and poetry and fiction and philosophy, all at once, and it refuses to settle into any one of them. Readers have always struggled to say what kind of book it is, which is another way of saying that Young, like her own angel, crosses the categories the reader arrives with. The figure at the margin, impossible to classify, turns out to be a self-portrait. She stationed her own image at the edge of her own book, where, by her own theory, the truth is most legible.
