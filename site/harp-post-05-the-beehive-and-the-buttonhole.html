Chapter 5. The Beehive and the Buttonhole

Part of a reading of Harp Song for a Radical (1999), Marguerite Young’s life of Eugene Victor Debs.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 4 · The Unfinished Harp: Reading Harp Song for a Radical · Chapter 6 →

Joseph Smith, Brigham Young, Mark Twain, and the death of Wilhelm Weitling at the close of Part One

Part One of Harp Song for a Radical ends on page 178. Its last sixty pages do two things. They build a portrait of the Mormon Zion, from Joseph Smith's golden plates to Brigham Young's desert beehive, seen partly through the eyes of a young Mark Twain. Then they follow Wilhelm Weitling, the wandering tailor who has carried Part One on his back, to his grave in Brooklyn.

Eugene Debs is still not born, at least not on these pages. By the time Weitling dies in 1871, the real Debs is fifteen and already working in the railroad shops of Terre Haute. Young never mentions this, but the dates carry the point. One dreamer is being buried while the next is shoveling coal.

Plates of sand and gold

Young's Joseph Smith is a comic figure, and she tells his story in a mock-epic voice that neither believes nor quite debunks. The golden plates, in one version she passes along, "had started out as about forty pounds of gleaming wet sand wrapped in his shirt" (120). In the retelling they grow heavier and heavier, at one point reaching "twelve hundred tons," then shrink until they "might shrink to the size of a watch or a moth or an eyelid" (120). The Book of Mormon, she writes, was a book "which some people thought was real and some people thought was a fraud" (121). She leaves both camps standing.

This is Not One Self applied to a prophet. In the previous section, Young called Smith's gift "the pluralistic art of infinite camouflage" (94). She does not try to peel the masks away to find the real man underneath. The masks are the man.

She also sets Smith beside the real gold hunters of his century. "For with all the sand in the world," she writes, "Joseph Smith had nothing to see but an idea in his mind" (122). The true builders of empire were the rising tycoons of coal and oil and steel, who "were well on the way to building their great pyramids of power which no poor gypsy could possibly overturn" (122). The contrast cuts both ways. Smith's gold was an idea, and so, in the end, was theirs. Money, property, and corporate power are also agreements held in place by belief and repetition. The tycoons' myth simply had the law and the militia on its side. This is Reality Is Constructed, told as a comparison between two kinds of treasure hunt.

The wife who doubted

The most striking pages in the Smith chapters belong to a minor figure. Martin Harris, a prosperous farmer, financed the translation of the plates and hoped to grow rich publishing them. His wife, Lucy, did not believe a word of it. Young reports, from Lucy Harris's own later account, that her husband beat her for her doubt, striking her with the butt end of an ox whip and chasing her around the garden (126–127).

Young takes her side in a single sentence: while some angels are angels of forgetfulness, "others may be the angels of memory, particularly if they are in communication with an abused wife who cannot forget one iota of his assaults upon her" (127).

When Harris lost the first pages of the manuscript, suspicion fell on Lucy, who was thought to have burned them. Young clearly enjoys her reply. She would neither confirm nor deny it, and she told her husband that Joseph Smith "should put on his magical spectacles and go peek for it" (129).

This is The Margins Reveal the Truth at its sharpest. In a story crowded with prophets, angels, and seer stones, the clearest eyes belong to the skeptical wife nobody listened to. Young gives her the last word.

Then, in the middle of the lost-manuscript story, Young breaks into a passage that sounds like a sermon of her own:

"Have a care about the next dead dog you may see on the road. Do not give it a kick. It may be the lost bible and may suddenly whine." A few lines later: "Have a care about the next pile of dung. It may speak with an angel's voice to a poor man, for it is also made with gold" (129).

It is The Sacred Is Here stated as Young ever states it. If there is holiness anywhere, it is in the roadside and the barnyard, not in golden plates hidden in a hill. Young does not mock the hunger for revelation. She simply points it at the ground.

The beehive

Brigham Young gets gentler treatment. Young gives him a hard childhood. He had "not exceeded a period of eleven days" of formal schooling (148). He carried his shoes in his hands to church so as not to wear them out on the road, putting them on only at the door. Left alone with his sister while their father searched for grain, the small boy shot a robin in the snow and baked it into a pie to keep them both alive (149).

As leader, Young's Brigham is practical to the bone. He told the handcart immigrants they "should not search the sky for the angelic beings who actually, of course, thickened the air about them but should dig ditches for irrigation and dig holes for the planting of the turnip" (144). Arriving in the desert, he announced, "We have been kicked out of the frying pan into the fire, out of the fire into the middle of the desert floor, and here we are and here we will stay" (144). In one of the book's sweetest scenes, a little girl on his knee tells him, "Mr. Young, your eyes look exactly like the eyes of my father's sow" (145). He gets up and goes out to the pig shed with her to study the sow's face.

Young does not hide the darker side of the kingdom, the violence, the polygamy, the power of one man. She does locate the source of the persecution precisely: "It was always because of their success that the Mormons were threatened by the Gentiles" (153). This echoes her earlier point that utopias die either because they fail with money or because envious neighbors destroy them when they succeed.

She also names the method of their destruction in a phrase that looks backward and forward at once. Joseph Smith's lynching in the Carthage jail, carried out while officials stood aside, followed "this tool pattern for the illegal lynch murder which was permitted or instigated by the legal authorities themselves and which had been used before and would be used again" (154). A page later, Weitling travels home through the Allegheny Mountains, which Young notes will be the scene of the Molly Maguire hangings in the 1870s. This is The Past Doesn't Go Away working as foreshadowing. The frame-up of Tom Mooney in the opening chapter, the Molly Maguires to come, and Debs's own prosecutions all rest on this layer.

Twain at the gate

In August 1861, Samuel Clemens rode into Salt Lake City by stagecoach with his older brother Orion, whom Lincoln had just appointed secretary of the Nevada Territory. Clemens would write it up a decade later in Roughing It, and Young follows his account closely.

Twain found the city "a land of enchantment and goblins and awful mystery" (141). He was struck that there were no loafers or drunkards, that clear streams ran down every street, and that everyone was busy. The Mormon emblem fit perfectly, he thought: "It was a representative of a Golden Bee Hive, with the bees all at work" (142). The brothers called on Brigham Young, who talked politics with Orion and ignored the younger brother's attempts to join in. As they left, he patted Sam on the head and asked Orion, "your child, I presume? Boy or girl?" (146).

Young enjoys all this, and then she quietly corrects it. Twain's jokes about Brigham's "homely" wives, she says, were an "acidulously comic portrait, which showed largely his own biases" (146). She adds what Twain left out: that in this city orphans were taken in rather than left to sleep in gutters, and that Brigham reportedly adopted two starving Native children he found in the desert and raised them as his own (141). She calls Twain's narrator, Mr. Johnson, a "comic mask concealing the tragic mask of the future Mark Twain" (150).

The chapter closes with William Dean Howells's review of Roughing It, and his sentence could describe Young's own book. Out West, Howells wrote, "all existence there must have looked like an extravagant joke, the humor of which was only deepened by its nether side of tragedy" (153). In the opening chapter, Young said Debs had a "comic sense which must sustain a larger tragic sense." Here she finds the same double vision in Twain. The joke and the grief are not opposites. Each makes the other deeper.

The tailor's last years

The final four chapters of Part One return to Weitling, and they are among the most tender in the book.

Communia collapsed. The dam he had built with such joy washed out. Accused of misusing the workers' funds, he cleared himself before a judge in Dubuque, never went back to the colony, and returned to New York as a jobbing tailor. "Upon the cobwebbed window of his shop in New York was hung a sign advertising Zion" (160). He still wore his red cap and red shoes at the bench. Young calls him "the unforgettable forgotten man" (160).

Engels, looking back years later, said Weitling "carried a recipe for the realization of heaven on earth ready-made in his pocket and was convinced that everybody intended to steal it from him" (161). Young quotes it and does not dispute it. Then she shows what the rest of his life was actually like.

He married Caroline Todt, a locksmith's daughter half his age who made straw bonnets and nursed the sick. They had five sons and a daughter. He named the boys after his heroes, including the French revolutionary Gracchus Babeuf and the astronomer Tycho Brahe, and they all quietly shortened their names. The daughter, Caroline Johanna, grew up to head a nursing program at Hunter College (167). He rescued a street dog that boys were stoning to death, and his wife stitched its torn ear back on. They named it Filou (164–165). He spent years on a universal language and a new map of the heavens.

He also invented a buttonhole and embroidery attachment for sewing machines, meant to spare the eyesight of women who punched eyelets by hand. He patented several improvements in 1861, and the big sewing machine companies used the ideas without paying him. Young notes that no investor could be found who saw "a future in saving the eyes of poor women" (168). Meanwhile, the government was backing a different kind of invention: "The eye puncher which was promoted by the government in the never-ending Civil War was, of course, the great Gatling gun" (171). Which machines get built and which get stolen is not a natural fact. It reflects who holds the money. Weitling put it in a letter to a friend: "Heaven save humanity from such republics of the money bags" (171).

For a while he worked at Castle Garden, the immigration station, writing letters home for new arrivals who could not write. He refused to take part in the practice of steering newcomers into the Union Army (169). This is Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act in its humblest form. The failed prophet spends his days guiding the hands of strangers as they sign their names with an X.

In 1868 he fell on the ice in his yard. The injured big toe was amputated, and his health never recovered. On January 24, 1871, he went out to a banquet of the Workers' International, hosted by his old friend Friedrich Sorge. Twenty years earlier, Sorge had played the fiddle under streetlamps to draw crowds for Weitling's pamphlets (175). That night Weitling suffered a stroke. He died the next day.

His union, the Journeyman Tailors' Protective and Benevolent Union, had kept up his dues when he could not, and it paid for the hearse and the grave at Green-Wood Cemetery in Brooklyn, which spared him a pauper's burial. Young calls it "A surely admirable example of human solidarity" (176).

What the ending shows

The shape of Part One now becomes clear. Weitling began as a messiah who knew the way to paradise. He ended as a tailor buried by his union's burial fund. That is exactly what the Philadelphia workers had set up back in 1847 with their dues of three cents a week. Utopia never arrived. The coffin money did. For this study, that is The Sacred Is Here made concrete. The real achievement of the utopian century was not the colony on the prairie. It was ordinary people pooling pennies so that none of them would go into the ground alone.

Young also lets Weitling's work stay unfinished. The New York Times notice mentioned "his unfinished treatise on moon and sun and stars" (176). It is hard not to hear an echo of Young herself, who died with her own great book unfinished. The obituary she quotes from a German-language paper could stand as her epitaph for every dreamer in Part One: "Men ceased to believe in him, and the waves tossed him upon the beach." And then: "Throw no stone at him, for he proved to be a man amidst the storms of life, and his driving motive was something higher than ambition and selfishness" (177).

Marx gets his say too. In an 1877 letter to Sorge, he dismissed the returning tide of utopian socialism as "silly, stale, and basically reactionary" (178). Young lets the verdict stand in the record, but she gives Weitling the more important line: "Perhaps his travels were not over and there were metamorphoses yet to come" (175). That is Unfinished Is Honest in one sentence. A life that ends unfinished may still be passing something on. Part Two begins with the boy who will receive it.

Checking the record

Young follows her sources closely in these chapters. The scenes in Salt Lake City come directly from Roughing It, and Weitling's last years follow Carl Wittke's 1950 biography, which she names (166). Marx's letter to Sorge of October 19, 1877, and Engels's 1885 recollection of Weitling are real. Weitling died in New York on January 25, 1871, the day after the International's banquet, as she describes.

Several points need correcting or flagging:

  • Twain's trip. Young dates it 1862 (139). The Clemens brothers crossed the plains in the summer of 1861 and reached Salt Lake City that August.

  • The lost manuscript. Young gives it "one hundred and seventeen pages" (129). The usual count is 116.

  • The two Harmonies. Young places Emma Hale's family home "in Economy" and describes Smith's digging ground as "the former Rappite stamping ground" (117). Emma Hale grew up in Harmony, in Susquehanna County in northeastern Pennsylvania. Rapp's Harmony was in Butler County, in the western part of the state, and Economy was the Rappites' later town near Pittsburgh. Young, or someone preparing her manuscript, merged two places with the same name. Given how much of her life she spent writing about Harmony, the slip is almost poignant.

  • "Dialectical Materialism." Young twice treats this as the title of a book by Marx and Engels, saying it was published in the year Smith died (154, 157). No work of theirs bears that title, and the phrase came into wide use only later.

  • Governor Boggs. Young describes the Carthage killing as arranged through the agreement of Governor Ford of Illinois and Governor Boggs of Missouri (154). Lilburn Boggs issued his notorious order against the Mormons in 1838, but he had left office by 1844.

  • The wrong Bryan. Young writes that "William Jennings Bryan of the New York Evening Post" worked in his garden in Roslyn, Long Island, during the Civil War (171). The poet and editor who lived in Roslyn and ran the Evening Post was William Cullen Bryant. William Jennings Bryan, whom Debs would campaign for in 1896, was born in 1860. It is the kind of error an editor would have caught easily, and it shows how thinly this manuscript was checked.
