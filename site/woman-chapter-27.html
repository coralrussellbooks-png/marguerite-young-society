Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 27

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer

Catherine and Mr. Spitzer are set against each other as two ways of knowing, and the gap between them is the chapter's pillar. She lives inside "opium dreams" and "phantasmagoric facts," inventing his life for him out of fragments; he lives by the watch, timing "the music of the dying wind" and the vibrations of bridges, forecasting the natural world into obedience. Her intuitive, dreamlike mode and his measured rational one divide along the old line that assigns reason to men and irrationality to women, and Young turns the assignment over. Catherine's "lack of faith" that "made all things possible to her" is both her powerlessness, the drugged inertia of a woman society has sedated and shelved, and a genuine epistemology, a way of holding the world that his forecasts cannot reach.

Time is the second pillar and it is broken along the same seam. Mr. Spitzer's watches, the ones that keep time and the ones that do not, stand for a male order of measurement, and Catherine's "great coffin of stars" that holds love, her fluid and fragmented sense of duration, refuses to be counted. The shattering of the watch is the shattering of linear time, and it falls on her the way disrupted life-courses fall on women: pregnancy, aging, waiting, the biological and social clocks that mark a woman's body while a man's time runs unburdened. Her opium suspends her between life and death, a "watchful, wide-eyed wakefulness," and this is the boundary held open, neither surrender nor escape but a sovereign province where she rules by seeing what was not there.

Mr. Spitzer's dread of the "fat man" he fears becoming, his anxiety about ridicule and bodily exposure, extends the surveillance of appearance that usually pins women; here it catches him too, and the chapter lets the vulnerability spread across the line rather than stay on one side of it. The closing dissonance, traffic lights "all wrong," the "red light turned to green" at the wrong moment, his near accidents, is the disordered world both of them must cross, and it reads as the mistimed and thwarted chances that meet a woman inside patriarchal time and space. Catherine stays central even while the chapter follows him: her inert body preserves a female consciousness that his rationality can neither wake nor comprehend, and that incomprehension is the point.
