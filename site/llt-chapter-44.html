Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 44

← Chapter 43 · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 45 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Pillars: Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The Questions

The great enemy of skirts, the bloomer captain, calls for her skirt as she dies. What blueprint is collapsing in that one word?

Mr. Spitzer resolves to “draw the veil,” to hide from any future biographer that she “had perhaps not died as a hero.” Is that protection, or the completing of her seal?

Her public life dissolves at the end into crying for a lost love in the snow. What was the public certainty a wall against?

“If we cannot be true together, then let us be false together,” she says dying. What has happened to true and false at the end of a life?

Here the sealed blueprint collapses in a single word. Cousin Hannah, “that great bloomer girl who had been the enemy of fashion-mongers,” who spent a life urging women out of the skirts that dragged them down, calls at the last, “as one who was going over to her mysterious bridegroom,” for her skirt. The public identity, the reformer, the enemy of encumbrance, comes apart on the thing she had defined herself against. This is the concrete content of what the earlier chapters could only name: the great battles “as nothing,” the domestic war she never fought. The war was a lost love and a longing she had dressed, for a lifetime, in the armor of a cause.

Her dying reveals what the cause was built over. She cries for a lost love blinded in the snow, a “little snowdrop” who “turned away” saying “I must find my skirt,” and searches for that love “through all the after-years.” The suffrage captain’s public certainty, seen from the deathbed, was a wall against a private grief, a love lost in the mountains that she spent her loud life outrunning. The book has shown three sealed figures fail because they finished, and here it shows, in the most exact way yet, what a finished blueprint is finished against: not the world, but one’s own unbearable loss, sealed over with a life of purpose.

Mr. Spitzer’s response is the Witness’s hardest act. He resolves to “draw the veil,” to “never reveal to any possible future biographer” that she had “perhaps not died as a hero.” This is tender, and it is the completing of her seal. It is exactly what Vera did for Miss MacIntosh and what he himself did for Catherine: protect the sealed self-image by keeping the truth from those who would unseal it. The book sets this third instance beside the others without absolving any of them. To draw the veil is to guard her dignity and to keep the door locked, and the chapter will not tell us whether the mercy or the locking weighs more.

And true and false come apart at the end. “If we cannot be true together, then let us be false together,” she cries; “was I false, and were you true? Was I true, and were you false?” The categories that ran her certain public life, right and wrong, true cause and false fashion, dissolve at the deathbed into a tangle she cannot sort. This is the sealed grammar of her convictions failing the way the nouns failed at the dream-wedding: the woman who knew, all her life, exactly what was true and false, dies unable to say which of them, in the one thing that mattered, she was.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that keeps Cousin Hannah the vindicated hero her public life claimed, and the reading that makes Mr. Spitzer’s veil-drawing a clean and simple kindness. Neither the hero nor the pure mercy survives the deathbed.

The footing is that her public certainty was a wall built over a private lost love, and it collapses in the one word she had defined herself against, the enemy of skirts calling for her skirt, which is the most exact picture the book has given of what a finished blueprint seals against. Mr. Spitzer’s choice to draw the veil is the third instance of the Witness’s protective sealing, tender and door-locking at once, set beside Vera’s and his own without absolution. And true and false dissolve at the deathbed for the woman who lived surest of them. The certainty was a wall against a grief, and protecting the legend keeps the seal. Cousin Hannah dies unable to say whether she was true or false, and the chapter lets the veil fall over her rather than lifting it.

For Reflection

What might one’s firmest public certainty turn out to have been built over?

When one protects a dead person’s legend, is that mercy to them, or the completion of a seal they can no longer open?

Is it possible to live surest of true and false and die unable to tell which one was?
