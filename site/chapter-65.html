To All My Darlings

Chapter 65

On Chapter 65 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 10, 2026

← Chapter 64 · Home · Chapter 66 →

Summary

As August turns to September, Miss MacIntosh, bald and half dressed, plays solitaire and mends a pair of black gloves; Vera reveals that these are the gloves she wore when she walked into the ocean one September morning, leaving her wig, her corset, and her umbrella on the beach. The umbrella was Mr. Bonebreaker’s, and Miss MacIntosh tells the story. Weak after losing her left breast and setting ninepins in a Chicago bowling alley, she was found reeling in the street by Titus Bonebreaker, a vegetarian Gospel salesman from the Ozarks who preached the end of the world through a trumpet and believed his grandmother’s story that he was the true King of Sweden. He called her the little sister who had no breasts, praised the hair she did not have, gave her his watch, and kept postponing their wedding. On the night before it, she stood before him naked and bald under an electric bulb. He fled, leaving his umbrella, and later married a foreign girl to give her child a name. Miss MacIntosh worked among the blind, drifted east, spent her savings in Buffalo on white clothes and an ashen wig, and stood alone among the honeymooners at Niagara Falls.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, Vera reflects on Miss MacIntosh’s many moods and the symbolic nature of her baldness. Her bald head, described as a “prism or a flame,” becomes a metaphor for her essential loneliness and mystery, a symbol of life and death intertwined. The passage emphasizes her unique and solitary existence, her strength amid frailty, and the profound loneliness she embodies.
  • Miss MacIntosh expresses disdain for “old kings of gold and silver towers,” traditional dreams of grandeur, and religious or royal symbolism such as the King James Bible and king crabs. She values the natural world and simple facts, rejecting illusions and pretenses. This passage highlights her grounded perspective and her rejection of false hopes or societal conventions.
  • A large part of the chapter focuses on Mr. Bonebreaker, the Gospel salesman. His character is described vividly: a man driven by religious zeal, preaching the end of the world, carrying a long black umbrella, and possessing a complex personality filled with contradictions. His interaction with Miss MacIntosh, his religious fervor, and his vision of salvation and judgment are central to understanding the chapter’s themes of faith, hope, and disillusionment.
  • The narrative details the courtship and engagement of Miss MacIntosh and Mr. Bonebreaker, which is fraught with difficulties. Mr. Bonebreaker’s inability to accept Miss MacIntosh’s physical reality, her baldness and mutilation, is a critical moment. When she reveals herself without her wig and shows her wounds, he reacts with shock and flees, leaving behind his umbrella and Bible literature. This moment symbolizes the ultimate failure of their relationship and Mr. Bonebreaker’s inability to fully love or accept her true self.
  • Throughout the chapter, Miss MacIntosh contemplates mortality and the nature of existence. She plans simply to live as long as she can and then to die without ceremony (no coffin, no forwarding address) accepting life and death on her own terms. Her quiet strength and acceptance contrast with Mr. Bonebreaker’s fiery religiosity.
  • After Mr. Bonebreaker leaves, Miss MacIntosh supports herself by working with the blind in Chicago, finding some solace among those who cannot see her physical wounds. Eventually, she flees Chicago, drifting eastward with a sense of loneliness and the painful memory of her failed romance. This passage portrays her resilience and the painful solitude she endures.
  • The black umbrella and the luminous watch given by Mr. Bonebreaker serve as symbols of their relationship and of faith and protection. After he flees, she keeps these items, but they are reminders of a lost and failed connection. The umbrella especially symbolizes shelter and salvation that proved illusory.
  • Throughout the text, there is a striking contrast between Miss MacIntosh’s sober, pragmatic view of the world and Mr. Bonebreaker’s apocalyptic and evangelical fervor. He preaches the end of the world, judgment, and salvation, while she quietly endures and accepts life’s hardships, reflecting a tension between religious hope and human reality.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s visit to Niagara Falls, a place of honeymooners and new beginnings, is poignant. She stands alone, no bridegroom or escort, with only her black umbrella. The noise and joy around her contrast sharply with her solitude and lost dreams. This scene symbolizes the end of her hopes with Mr. Bonebreaker and her solitary path forward.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “It was the one and only time a man possessed her, body and soul.” What does “possessed” mean for an embrace that lasts a moment?
  • Mr. Bonebreaker calls her “that little sister who had no breasts.” How does he turn Scripture into a wound?
  • “She was neither sinful nor beautiful.” How does Miss MacIntosh measure herself against his compliments?

Characters

  • Mr. Bonebreaker eats no meat, “but he would eat an egg, never supposing that it was the unborn he ate with such gusto.” What do his inconsistencies show?
  • Miss MacIntosh “could not agree that the Jews should be singled out for the brightest, biggest stars,” and at her mission “People of all races were to be admitted.” Where does she stand apart from him?
  • When she stands before him, “He was like a crazy, snared bird when he saw her.” Why does the man who preached the end of the world flee from one bald woman?

Connections

  • The gloves she mends are “those very black gloves she must have worn” into the sea. How does Vera’s foreknowledge change this chapter?
  • “The sun and the moon shone in the same pearly sky when she walked into the ocean.” Where did Miss MacIntosh foretell this in Chapter 63?
  • On the beach lay “her black umbrella, Mr. Bonebreaker’s umbrella.” How does knowing where the umbrella came from change it?

Reflection

  • “Only in love does one notice these things.” Is that true?
  • “Life is never in accordance with what we think it is.” When has that been true?
  • “There is surely a man somewhere in every woman’s life.” What does Miss MacIntosh, whose one love was Lorena, mean by this?

Research: Characters

Miss MacIntosh

  • A woman of many moods and contradictions, both physically and emotionally marked by her ailments.
  • She is bald, missing a breast, and wears wigs to mask her reality.
  • Profoundly lonely and marked by a sense of loss, she is simultaneously resigned and hopeful.
  • She embodies a “pilgrim’s progress from nowhere to nowhere,” with a simple, austere view of life and death.
  • Her internal conflict centers on her past, her physical condition, and her tenuous relationship with faith and society.
  • She is portrayed as both vulnerable and strong, practical yet mystical.

Mr. Titus Bonebreaker

  • A passionate, zealous Gospel salesman from Missouri, characterized by his fervor and eccentricity.
  • He preaches about the impending end of the world, salvation, and the need for repentance.
  • He is physically gaunt and stoop-shouldered, with distinctive features like a beaked nose and bright eyes.
  • Despite his religious zeal, he is inconsistent and flawed (e.g., his diet, brushes with police, personal hygiene).
  • He represents religious fanaticism and the human desire for meaning and redemption amid decay and despair.
  • His relationship with Miss MacIntosh is complex: he idealizes her, but ultimately fails to accept her fully.
  • His first name may refer to Titus the Roman emperor from 79 to 81 AD, who was also a celebrated military general. He is known for leading the Roman army to victory in the First Jewish-Roman War, which resulted in the destruction of Jerusalem and its temple. His brief reign as emperor is notable for the eruption of Mount Vesuvius and the opening of the Colosseum. Titus is also the companion to whom Paul wrote the Epistle to Titus.

Richard MacIntosh

  • He is Miss MacIntosh’s brother, a primitive Christian bishop from Iowa who went to the Easter Islands to convert the heathen but disappeared, presumably a victim of head-hunters. He represents a failed attempt to impose a particular belief system onto a different culture, and his fate is a source of anxiety for Miss MacIntosh.

John Knox MacIntosh

  • He is Miss MacIntosh’s father.

Mercy MacIntosh

  • She is Miss MacIntosh’s mother.

Research: Themes

Loneliness and Isolation

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Miss MacIntosh’s life is defined by solitude, both physical and emotional.
  • Her baldness and physical wounds symbolize her vulnerability and societal alienation.
  • She finds some solace among the blind, where her own differences are less visible.

Faith, Salvation, and Doubt

See: Faith, God, and the Supernatural

  • The chapter explores the tension between religious fervor, spiritual hope, and personal doubt.
  • Mr. Bonebreaker’s apocalyptic preaching contrasts with Miss MacIntosh’s pragmatic and skeptical attitude.
  • Her rejection of his God and his ultimate flight from her when confronted with her full reality illustrates this tension.

The Body and Physical Affliction

See: Hair, Wig, and Baldness · Women, Gender, and Marriage

  • The physical condition of Miss MacIntosh (her baldness, lost breast, and scars) are central to the narrative.
  • They symbolize both human frailty and inner strength.
  • Her mending of black gloves carries guilt. Young’s psychiatrist friend explained “all the meanings of the word black and gloves in relation to psychology and guilt” (Marguerite Young, interview with Charles Ruas, WBAI radio, New York), and she told Studs Terkel, “I finally understood why it was again a symbol of guilt” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).

The End of Time and Apocalypse

See: Time

  • Mr. Bonebreaker’s constant preaching about the “Last Day,” the destruction of Chicago, and the fall of creation permeates the narrative.
  • This apocalyptic vision frames the personal drama within a cosmic scale.

Identity, Appearance, and Reality

See: Illusion and Reality · Identity and the Self

  • Miss MacIntosh’s wearing of wigs and her attempt to hide her baldness reflects issues of identity and societal perception.
  • The contrast between appearances and inner reality is a recurring motif.
  • Mr. Bonebreaker’s idealized view of her contrasts sharply with her own self-perception.

Research: Resources

  • “the old King James version which was, in her pale esteem, befogged”: The King James Bible (1611) was made at the order of James I, the Stuart king; Miss MacIntosh objects to it as she objects to all royalty, “even to king crabs, kingfishers, king posts.” See The Bible.
  • “just as she had done to the King of Sweden”: See the King of Sweden in the Chapter 64 resources, and Mr. Bonebreaker below. See Mr. Titus Bonebreaker.
  • “playing solitaire with a pack of the old playing cards”: Solitaire originated in Europe in the late 18th century, likely in Germany or Scandinavia, with early versions potentially used for fortune-telling. The game, also known as Patience, spread to France and England in the 19th century, with the first rule books appearing around the 1870s. See the solitary player in the Chapter 60 resources. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “In Chinese gambling dens”: Vera, recounts Miss MacIntosh’s memories and opinions, which include several stereotypes about Chinese people: Miss MacIntosh states, “Did I know that the Chinese are a nearly hairless people, that Chinese men prefer a hairless woman with small breasts?” This perpetuates a stereotype about the physical characteristics of Chinese individuals. Miss MacIntosh mentions Chinese gambling dens where men play solitaire and are watched to prevent cheating the “invisible partner.” This associates Chinese culture with gambling and implies a need for surveillance to ensure fair play. Miss MacIntosh claims to have learned solitaire in San Francisco’s Chinatown, suggesting a connection between the game and Chinese culture.
  • “called by many the Windy City”: Chicago is known as the “Windy City” due to a combination of factors, primarily a 19th-century political jab, rather than just its weather. The primary theory suggests the name was coined by journalists, particularly from New York and Cincinnati, to mock Chicago’s boastful politicians and promoters. During the competition to host the 1893 World’s Columbian Exposition, a reporter for the New York Sun, Charles A. Dana, reportedly editorialized that the city’s advocates were “full of hot air.” The derogatory term was meant to suggest that Chicagoans were all talk and bluster. The term appeared in print as early as 1876 in the Cincinnati Enquirer during a heated baseball and economic rivalry between the two cities. The Cincinnati papers used the term in a double-edged way, referring both to a recent tornado and to Chicago’s verbose (or “windy”) speakers. Despite its initial use as an insult, the nickname became popular and was eventually embraced by Chicagoans as a badge of honor, reflective of the city’s dynamic and resilient spirit. Chicago’s location on the shores of Lake Michigan creates distinct wind patterns, especially along the waterfront, due to temperature differences between the land and the water. A cold, harsh winter wind in Chicago is often referred to as “The Hawk” or “Mr. Wind.” See Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest.
    • Chicago’s history began with Indigenous peoples, followed by French fur traders and the first permanent settler, Jean Baptiste Point du Sable, in the late 1700s. The city was officially founded in 1837 and grew into a major transportation hub for canals and railroads, becoming an industrial and commercial center by the late 19th century. The city experienced rapid expansion, and key events include the Great Chicago Fire of 1871, which destroyed much of the city but led to a period of robust rebuilding, and the 1893 World’s Columbian Exposition, which boosted the city’s reputation.
    • Chicago’s history spans from Native American roots as Shikaakwa (wild onion place) through its rapid 19th-century growth into a major industrial hub, the Gilded Age’s steel and meatpacking dominance, the devastating Great Fire of 1871, its rise as a cultural center (World’s Fairs, jazz), the Prohibition-era crime, the political machine era under Mayor Richard J. Daley, and its emergence as a modern metropolis, all while grappling with significant immigration, racial tension, and urban development leading up to the turbulent late 1960s.
  • “the old playing cards with the Dutch windmills flying on their backs”: Waddingtons in England, produced playing cards featuring Dutch windmills on the backs, often in sealed, faux-leather boxes, with designs popular in the 1950s and 1960s for their quaint, scenic appeal. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “Chasing windmills—Miss MacIntosh had always been chasing windmills”: Miss MacIntosh told Vera she had been chasing Dutch windmills. Another character, Mrs. Hogden, claims to have lived in a Dutch windmill. Moses Hunnecker, the bus driver of Chapter 1, is also imagined as “some mad Don Quixote chasing windmills.” Windmills in the Dutch style stand in the United States too, among them the Fabyan Windmill outside Chicago and the two in San Francisco’s Golden Gate Park (1903, 1908), the two cities of Miss MacIntosh’s solitaire. Windmills symbolize a pursuit of illusionary goals, a theme that is explored through the characters and their desires. Tilting at windmills is an idiom for attacking imaginary enemies from the novel Don Quixote by Miguel de Cervantes. It is a founding book in Western literature and is labeled the first modern novel. This book is an example of the need for literature, fiction and imagination. According to Mark C. Aldrich, Don Quixote is the story of an older man who has lost his mind over his obsession with literature and decides to go and live these adventures of time past. Little by little through his friendship with his companion he recovers his mind. It is ultimately the journey of one man finding out who he really is. Quixote is the highest aspirations of human beings. It pits the world of imagination against the terrible reality of modern life. It highlights the challenges of understanding one’s identity. There is a parallel between Don Quixote and Miss MacIntosh My Darling in that it is not a book of issues but of tissues, like an onion, you find layer after layer upon subsequent rereadings. Don Quixote stands for change and renovating society. He wants to help the poor and the oppressed. See On tilting at windmills. See Don Quixote and Moses Hunnecker.
  • “her brother Richard who had disappeared among the islands of Oceania”: Oceania is a vast geographical region in the Pacific Ocean, encompassing Australia, New Guinea, New Zealand, and thousands of smaller islands, traditionally divided into Melanesia, Micronesia, and Polynesia, plus Australasia (Australia/NZ). It’s defined by the ocean, featuring diverse climates and landforms, from large continental islands like Australia to tiny coral atolls, with cultures shaped by unique island isolation and later European colonization. See On Richard MacIntosh and the Easter Islands.
    • Richard MacIntosh is Miss MacIntosh’s brother.
    • “Polynesian head-hunters” Polynesian headhunting, particularly prominent in places like the Solomon Islands (Melanesia, often grouped with Polynesian cultures in historical accounts) and among the Māori of New Zealand, wasn’t just about violence; it served deep cultural, spiritual, and economic purposes, often tied to ancestor worship, capturing soul matter, avenging deaths, enhancing warrior status, and controlling land, with practices evolving and often intensifying or changing due to European contact and the trade in preserved heads (mokomokai).
  • “in old San Francisco’s old Chinatown”: Chinatown, San Francisco is the oldest in North America and was founded in the 1840s by Chinese immigrants seeking opportunity during the Gold Rush but found refuge from discrimination. It grew into a tight-knit community that preserved Chinese culture and provided mutual support, despite facing prejudice that led to events like the 1882 Chinese Exclusion Act. The neighborhood was rebuilt after the 1906 earthquake and has since become a vibrant cultural hub, officially renamed “Chinatown” by the press in 1853.
  • “just before the Golden Gate was built”: Miss MacIntosh claims to have been there right before the Golden Gate Bridge was built. This would place her there before the bridge opened in 1937; construction began in 1933. She also claims it may have been foggy so she didn’t see it.
  • “And the Arabs had their topknots by which God might fish for them”: The topknot hairstyle has ancient origins in various cultures, often symbolizing status, maturity, or spiritual significance. Historically, it was adopted by samurai in Japan for practical reasons, such as securing their helmets, while in ancient China it was worn by scholars and nobility. In South Asian traditions, it holds religious meaning, and in Korean society, it was used to signify adulthood.
    • Western travelers told of Muslim men, Turks especially, who shaved the head but for one lock by which they would be drawn up into paradise; Young gives the belief to “the Arabs.”
  • “that there is a great hole at the North Pole”: In 1818 John Cleves Symmes Jr. announced that the earth is hollow and open at the poles. In the 1880s Cyrus Teed’s Koreshan Unity in Chicago taught the reverse, that we live on the inside of a hollow earth. See Exploration and the Poles.
  • “No matter how many explorers draw how many maps of the Himalayas”: The Himalayas are Earth’s highest and youngest mountain range, forming a massive arc across Asia, known as the “Abode of Snow,” containing Mount Everest and over 110 peaks over 24,000 feet, creating a major climatic barrier and source of major rivers like the Ganges and Brahmaputra, vital to billions in India and beyond. Formed by the collision of the Indian and Eurasian tectonic plates, the range features three main parallel ranges (Great, Lesser, Outer) and supports unique ecosystems, with glaciers providing water to billions.

  • “her work in the bowling alley where, every evening, it was her duty to set ninepins up”: Bowling alleys evolved from ancient games, gaining indoor structure in 19th-century America, particularly among German immigrants, shifting from nine-pin to ten-pin to circumvent gambling bans, and transforming into modern entertainment venues with automatic pinsetters and widespread media appeal, especially after the 1950s, though they’ve always been associated with socializing and gambling. Pinsetters began as young boys and girls (“pinboys”) manually resetting pins and returning balls, a common, low-paying job in early 20th-century alleys, often enduring harsh conditions. It was hard, hot work, requiring speed and agility to avoid fast-moving balls and pins, as recounted by both men and women who did the job. See ninepins in the Chapter 64 resources.

  • “why should she not see Niagara Falls”: Niagara Falls near Buffalo, New York was a popular honeymoon destination. The falls began forming approximately 12,000 years ago, at the end of the last ice age. As the glaciers of the Wisconsin glaciation receded, vast amounts of meltwater filled the basins they had carved, creating the Great Lakes. The water from the newly formed Lake Erie poured over a steep slope known as the Niagara Escarpment on its way to Lake Ontario. Over millennia, this powerful torrent of water eroded the rock layers, carving out the seven-mile-long Niagara Gorge and causing the falls to retreat upstream to their present-day location. The retreat of the falls is a result of a hard, erosion-resistant layer of limestone and dolomite (Lockport Formation) that sits on top of softer, more easily worn away shale (Rochester Formation). The river erodes the softer rock underneath, causing the hard caprock to break off in large chunks. The rate of erosion, once several feet per year, has been significantly slowed by water diversion for hydroelectric power. The first inhabitants of the region were the Neutral and other Iroquoian-speaking peoples who settled in the area between 1300 and 1400 A.D.. The name “Niagara” is believed to be derived from an Iroquoian word, “Onguiaahra,” which means “thundering noise.” The French were the first Europeans to explore the Niagara region in the 17th century. Jesuit priest Louis Hennepin is credited with the first documented eyewitness account in 1678, publishing his discovery in Europe and inspiring further exploration. Following the construction of the Erie Canal in 1825 and the development of the railroad in the 1800s, Niagara Falls became a popular tourist destination. Its association with honeymooners began in the early 19th century. The surge in tourism led to unbridled commercialism, with unscrupulous entrepreneurs charging visitors for views of the falls and turning the area into a chaotic carnival. In response to the over-commercialization and industrial threat to the falls’ natural beauty, a conservation movement arose. Frederick Law Olmsted, co-designer of Central Park, and others advocated for state protection. Their efforts resulted in the creation of New York’s Niagara Reservation, the first state park in the U.S., and Ontario’s Queen Victoria Niagara Falls Park, both established in 1885. The falls’ power potential was recognized as early as 1750, when a small canal was built to power a sawmill. In 1882, the first hydroelectric generating station was built on the American side to provide direct current (DC) electricity to local mills. A group of investors backed the development of a large-scale alternating current (AC) power system, designed by Westinghouse Electric with Nikola Tesla as a consultant. In 1896, power generated at Niagara was sent over 20 miles to Buffalo, proving the feasibility of long-distance AC transmission. Today, a significant portion of the Niagara River’s flow is diverted through tunnels to power plants on both the American and Canadian sides, which generate millions of kilowatts of electricity. A treaty between the U.S. and Canada ensures enough water remains flowing over the falls to preserve its scenic beauty during tourist hours. For over a century, the falls have attracted thrill-seekers performing dangerous stunts for fame and fortune. In 1901, Annie Edson Taylor became the first person to successfully go over the falls in a barrel. Many other daredevils, including tightrope walkers and those in homemade contraptions, have attempted to conquer the falls, some successfully and others tragically. In 1969, the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers temporarily diverted the water from the American Falls to study the effects of erosion. In 1848, an ice jam in the river above the falls completely cut off the water flow for a period of up to 40 hours, leaving the falls dry. An iron scow carrying two men became stuck on the rocks upstream from the falls in 1918. Despite the rescue of the crew, the vessel remains wedged in the river to this day. Legend makes Jérôme Bonaparte, Napoleon’s brother, the first honeymooner there, in 1804. See On Niagara Falls and honeymooners.
    • The Maid of the Mist has carried visitors in oilskins to the foot of the falls since 1846.
  • “so many Aphrodites and Apollos”: Aphrodite: see Chapter 42, Resources, for the full entry.
    • Apollo is one of the Olympian deities in Greek mythology. Apollo has been recognized as a god of archery, music and dance, truth and prophecy, healing and diseases, the Sun and light, poetry, and more.
  • “Romeo and Juliet, Paul and Virginia, Eloise and Abelard, Tristram and the white Yseult”: Romeo and Juliet is a tragedy written by William Shakespeare early in his career about two young Italian star-crossed lovers whose deaths ultimately reconcile their feuding families. See Shakespeare.
    • Paul and Virginia is a novel by Jacques-Henri Bernardin de Saint-Pierre, first published in 1788. The novel’s title characters are friends since birth who fall in love. It records the fate of a child of nature corrupted by the artificial sentimentality of the French upper classes in the late eighteenth century.
    • Eloise and Abelard refers to Heloise who was a French nun, philosopher, writer, scholar, and abbess. She is famous in history and popular culture for her love affair and correspondence with the leading medieval logician and theologian Peter Abelard, who became her colleague, collaborator and husband. More in the Chapter 52 resources.
    • Tristan and Iseult or Yseult is a chivalric romance told in numerous variations since the 12th century. The tale is a tragedy about the illicit love between the Cornish knight Tristan (Tristram) and the Irish princess Iseult (Isolde, Yseult).
  • “handing out cards promising quick and easy divorces”: In 1931 Nevada cut its residency for divorce to six weeks, and Reno became the divorce capital. See Weddings and Funerals.
  • “a Moody’s Bible chained to a post”: Young’s “Moody’s Bible” recalls Dwight L. Moody, the Chicago evangelist whose Bible Institute opened in 1886; the Gideons began placing Bibles in hotel rooms in 1908. See American Religious Movements.
  • “the serpent bite without enchantment”: “Surely the serpent will bite without enchantment” (Ecclesiastes 10:11, KJV). See Ecclesiastes.
