Chapter 65

On Chapter 65 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 30, 2026

Summary

Miss MacIntosh tells Vera about her one romance, with Mr. Titus Bonebreaker, a street preacher from Missouri who proclaimed the end of the world. They were engaged, but when she showed him the truth of her body, he fled, leaving his umbrella behind. She kept it, and the luminous watch he gave her. She worked among the blind in Chicago, where her differences mattered less, and later stood alone on a boat full of honeymooners, resolved to forget him.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, Vera reflects on Miss MacIntosh’s many moods and the symbolic nature of her baldness. Her bald head, described as a "prism or a flame," becomes a metaphor for her essential loneliness and mystery, a symbol of life and death intertwined. The passage emphasizes her unique and solitary existence, her strength amid frailty, and the profound loneliness she embodies.
  • Miss MacIntosh expresses disdain for "old kings of gold and silver towers," traditional dreams of grandeur, and religious or royal symbolism such as the King James Bible and king crabs. She values the natural world and simple facts, rejecting illusions and pretenses. This passage highlights her grounded perspective and her rejection of false hopes or societal conventions.
  • A large part of the chapter focuses on Mr. Bonebreaker, the Gospel salesman. His character is described vividly: a man driven by religious zeal, preaching the end of the world, carrying a long black umbrella, and possessing a complex personality filled with contradictions. His interaction with Miss MacIntosh, his religious fervor, and his vision of salvation and judgment are central to understanding the chapter’s themes of faith, hope, and disillusionment.
  • The narrative details the courtship and engagement of Miss MacIntosh and Mr. Bonebreaker, which is fraught with difficulties. Mr. Bonebreaker’s inability to accept Miss MacIntosh’s physical reality, her baldness and mutilation, is a critical moment. When she reveals herself without her wig and shows her wounds, he reacts with shock and flees, leaving behind his umbrella and Bible literature. This moment symbolizes the ultimate failure of their relationship and Mr. Bonebreaker’s inability to fully love or accept her true self.
  • Throughout the chapter, Miss MacIntosh contemplates mortality and the nature of existence. She plans simply to live as long as she can and then to die without ceremony (no coffin, no forwarding address) accepting life and death on her own terms. Her quiet strength and acceptance contrast with Mr. Bonebreaker’s fiery religiosity.
  • After Mr. Bonebreaker leaves, Miss MacIntosh supports herself by working with the blind in Chicago, finding some solace among those who cannot see her physical wounds. Eventually, she flees Chicago, drifting eastward with a sense of loneliness and the painful memory of her failed romance. This passage portrays her resilience and the painful solitude she endures.
  • The black umbrella and the luminous watch given by Mr. Bonebreaker serve as symbols of their relationship and of faith and protection. After he flees, she keeps these items, but they are reminders of a lost and failed connection. The umbrella especially symbolizes shelter and salvation that proved illusory.
  • Throughout the text, there is a striking contrast between Miss MacIntosh’s sober, pragmatic view of the world and Mr. Bonebreaker’s apocalyptic and evangelical fervor. He preaches the end of the world, judgment, and salvation, while she quietly endures and accepts life’s hardships, reflecting a tension between religious hope and human reality.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s visit to Niagara Falls, a place of honeymooners and new beginnings, is poignant. She stands alone, no bridegroom or escort, with only her black umbrella. The noise and joy around her contrast sharply with her solitude and lost dreams. This scene symbolizes the end of her hopes with Mr. Bonebreaker and her solitary path forward.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Miss MacIntosh

  • A woman of many moods and contradictions, both physically and emotionally marked by her ailments.
  • She is bald, missing a breast, and wears wigs to mask her reality.
  • Profoundly lonely and marked by a sense of loss, she is simultaneously resigned and hopeful.
  • She embodies a "pilgrim’s progress from nowhere to nowhere," with a simple, austere view of life and death.
  • Her internal conflict centers on her past, her physical condition, and her tenuous relationship with faith and society.
  • She is portrayed as both vulnerable and strong, practical yet mystical.

Mr. Titus Bonebreaker

  • A passionate, zealous Gospel salesman from Missouri, characterized by his fervor and eccentricity.
  • He preaches about the impending end of the world, salvation, and the need for repentance.
  • He is physically gaunt and stoop-shouldered, with distinctive features like a beaked nose and bright eyes.
  • Despite his religious zeal, he is inconsistent and flawed (e.g., his diet, brushes with police, personal hygiene).
  • He represents religious fanaticism and the human desire for meaning and redemption amid decay and despair.
  • His relationship with Miss MacIntosh is complex: he idealizes her, but ultimately fails to accept her fully.
  • His first name may refer to Titus the Roman emperor from 79 to 81 AD, who was also a celebrated military general. He is known for leading the Roman army to victory in the First Jewish-Roman War, which resulted in the destruction of Jerusalem and its temple. His brief reign as emperor is notable for the eruption of Mount Vesuvius and the opening of the Colosseum.

Richard MacIntosh

  • He is Miss MacIntosh's brother, a primitive Christian bishop from Iowa who went to the Easter Islands to convert the heathen but disappeared, presumably a victim of head-hunters. He represents a failed attempt to impose a particular belief system onto a different culture, and his fate is a source of anxiety for Miss MacIntosh.

John Knox MacIntosh

  • He is Miss MacIntosh’s father.

Mercy MacIntosh

  • She is Miss MacIntosh’s mother.

Research: Themes

Loneliness and Isolation

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Miss MacIntosh’s life is defined by solitude, both physical and emotional.
  • Her baldness and physical wounds symbolize her vulnerability and societal alienation.
  • She finds some solace among the blind, where her own differences are less visible.

Faith, Salvation, and Doubt

See: Faith, God, and the Supernatural

  • The chapter explores the tension between religious fervor, spiritual hope, and personal doubt.
  • Mr. Bonebreaker’s apocalyptic preaching contrasts with Miss MacIntosh’s pragmatic and skeptical attitude.
  • Her rejection of his God and his ultimate flight from her when confronted with her full reality illustrates this tension.

The Body and Physical Affliction

  • The physical condition of Miss MacIntosh (her baldness, lost breast, and scars) are central to the narrative.
  • They symbolize both human frailty and inner strength.
  • Her mending of black gloves carries guilt. Young’s psychiatrist friend explained “all the meanings of the word black and gloves in relation to psychology and guilt” (Marguerite Young, interview with Charles Ruas, WBAI radio, New York), and she told Studs Terkel, “I finally understood why it was again a symbol of guilt” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).

The End of Time and Apocalypse

See: Time

  • Mr. Bonebreaker’s constant preaching about the “Last Day,” the destruction of Chicago, and the fall of creation permeates the narrative.
  • This apocalyptic vision frames the personal drama within a cosmic scale.

Identity, Appearance, and Reality

See: Illusion and Reality · Identity and the Self

  • Miss MacIntosh’s wearing of wigs and her attempt to hide her baldness reflects issues of identity and societal perception.
  • The contrast between appearances and inner reality is a recurring motif.
  • Mr. Bonebreaker’s idealized view of her contrasts sharply with her own self-perception.

  • Waddingtons in England, produced playing cards featuring Dutch windmills on the backs, often in sealed, faux-leather boxes, with designs popular in the 1950s and 1960s for their quaint, scenic appeal.
  • Chicago is known as the "Windy City" due to a combination of factors, primarily a 19th-century political jab, rather than just its weather. The primary theory suggests the name was coined by journalists, particularly from New York and Cincinnati, to mock Chicago's boastful politicians and promoters. During the competition to host the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition, a reporter for the New York Sun, Charles A. Dana, reportedly editorialized that the city's advocates were "full of hot air". The derogatory term was meant to suggest that Chicagoans were all talk and bluster. The term appeared in print as early as 1876 in the Cincinnati Enquirer during a heated baseball and economic rivalry between the two cities. The Cincinnati papers used the term in a double-edged way, referring both to a recent tornado and to Chicago's verbose (or "windy") speakers. Despite its initial use as an insult, the nickname became popular and was eventually embraced by Chicagoans as a badge of honor, reflective of the city's dynamic and resilient spirit. Chicago's location on the shores of Lake Michigan creates distinct wind patterns, especially along the waterfront, due to temperature differences between the land and the water. A cold, harsh winter wind in Chicago is often referred to as "The Hawk" or "Mr. Wind”.

  • “the language of baseball” or "baseballese," was rich with colorful, evocative slang like "banjo hitter" (soft contact) or "daisy cutter" (hard grounder), alongside foundational terms such as "ace" (pitcher) and "dish" (home plate). It featured unique terms for plays like "around the horn" (infield relay) and players like "baller" or "hurler," reflecting a simpler, more traditional game before extensive media exposure amplified modern jargon. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries some Christian leaders saw the sport as a moral threat. Many communities had laws that banned baseball games on Sundays to encourage church attendance and prevent "rowdy behavior" often associated with saloons and alcohol consumption. State laws even forced some professional teams, like the Boston Red Sox, to play Sunday games at different fields away from churches. Some religious groups viewed baseball as a frivolous activity that distracted from intellectual and spiritual pursuits. Debates emerged about whether good Christians should play or watch baseball, especially on the Sabbath. Individual Jewish players, notably Hank Greenberg (in the 1930s) and Sandy Koufax, gained national attention and became heroes to many in their community by choosing not to play on the Jewish high holiday of Yom Kippur, a public declaration of faith that highlighted the choice between religious observance and professional demands. Simultaneously, baseball became a powerful cultural force in America, developing many characteristics that paralleled religious institutions. The game was given a deliberate creation myth in the early 1900s, attributing its invention to Abner Doubleday in Cooperstown, NY, which became a "sacred" origin site (now the home of the Baseball Hall of Fame). The sport developed its own rich repository of legends, rituals, and traditions (e.g., the seventh-inning stretch song "Take Me Out to the Ball Game," the "Curse of the Bambino," the "infield fly rule") that fostered a sense of continuity and shared experience. Iconic players like Babe Ruth were treated with reverence, their personal artifacts (bats, uniforms, gloves) becoming the "relics" of modern-day fandom, housed in the "shrine" of the Hall of Fame. The shared experience of fandom, with fans gathering in stadiums or living rooms, created strong bonds of community and belonging, much like a religious congregation. The language and imagery of baseball encouraged spiritual metaphors, with scribes and fans finding lessons of faith, sacrifice, and redemption in the game.

  • St. Louis's "German Beer Kings" were immigrant brewers like Eberhard Anheuser, Adolphus Busch, the Lemp family, and the Griesediecks, who built brewing empires in the mid-19th century by introducing lager beers and leveraging St. Louis's German population and unique limestone caves for brewing, transforming local businesses into national brands like Anheuser-Busch (Budweiser) and creating a rich brewing heritage.
  • Chicago's Union Stock Yards, opened in 1865, became the world's largest meatpacking center, transforming America's food system by centralizing livestock processing through rail, making Chicago the "hog butcher for the world," attracting waves of immigrant labor, and spurring related industries, but its reign ended in 1971 due to industry shifts, leaving behind legacy sites like its famous entrance gate and a lasting impact on Chicago's economy and immigrant communities.
  • "great Chicago hotel fire" may refer to the near-destruction of the Grand Pacific Hotel during the massive 1871 Great Chicago Fire, which destroyed much of the city, and later, tragic incidents like the 1946 LaSalle Hotel Fire.
  • Almanach de Gotha is a directory of Europe’s royalty and higher nobility.
  • The Habsburgs were a powerful German royal dynasty that ruled vast European territories for centuries, rising from Swiss origins in the 10th century to dominate the Holy Roman Empire and control Spain, Austria (as Austria-Hungary), and extensive colonies through strategic marriages and shrewd politics, eventually ending with World War I. Their empire peaked under Charles V, uniting Spain, the Netherlands, and Austria, but the dynasty's vast reach and complex succession eventually led to internal strains, culminating in the dissolution of their empire after WWI.
  • The Stuart dynasty ruled Scotland from the late 14th century and later England, Scotland, and Ireland from 1603 to 1714, unifying the crowns under James VI of Scotland (James I of England) and overseeing a turbulent era of civil war, religious conflict, plague, and the Great Fire of London, ultimately ending with Queen Anne and leading to the Hanoverian succession, establishing England as a constitutional monarchy.
  • “shield of the unicorn” In medieval art, unicorns were tamed only by virgins, making them symbols of purity, often featured on shields alongside figures like the Virgin Mary. The unicorn, often chained and rearing, appears in heraldry, notably with the lion in the British Royal Arms and Canadian symbols, representing royal power.
  • Bar sinister was a heraldic charge that was used supposedly to designate legitimate from illegitimate children.
  • Mr. Bonebreaker often preached that he had put away childish things. 1 Corinthians 13:11 Mr. Bonebreaker's frequent assertion that he had "put away childish things" reflects his self-perception as a man who has transcended his past sins, weaknesses, and worldly attachments to embrace a higher, spiritual calling. This phrase, borrowed from biblical language, suggests a mature renunciation of immature behaviors and desires, symbolizing his transformation from a sinful, indulgent man to a devout Gospel salesman committed to preaching about the imminent end of the world and salvation. Despite his claims of having overcome childishness, the narrative reveals a persistent childlike frailty within Mr. Bonebreaker. Miss MacIntosh observes that, like all humans, he retains a vulnerable, human "child" inside. This is evident in his inability to face the harsh reality of his poverty and his enduring dream of a heavenly crown and royal status, which reflects an ongoing attachment to grandiose illusions rather than complete spiritual maturity. While Mr. Bonebreaker preaches strict renunciation, including abstaining from meat, alcohol, tobacco, and other indulgences. He exhibits contradictions and inconsistencies in his behavior. His erratic and passionate preaching style, his brushes with the law, and his occasional violence towards those who oppose him further illustrate that his "putting away of childish things" is incomplete, marked by ongoing struggles and imperfections. His intense fervor, wild gestures, and sometimes illogical or scattered thoughts indicate that his spiritual transformation is intertwined with a kind of fanaticism or mania rather than calm, settled wisdom. The childlike elements (his hope, dreams, and emotional volatility) persist even as he claims to have overcome them.
  • Tobit’s dog may refer to a dog mentioned in Christianity. In Christianity, dogs represent faithfulness. A dog is mentioned in the deuterocanonical Book of Tobit, faithfully accompanying Tobias, Tobit's son and the angel Raphael on their journeys.
  • The Medici were a powerful Italian banking family and political dynasty that ruled Florence and Tuscany for centuries, becoming synonymous with the Italian Renaissance through their vast wealth, strategic political maneuvering, and unparalleled patronage of arts and sciences, sponsoring figures like Michelangelo and Leonardo da Vinci and producing four Popes before their dynasty ended in 1737. Starting as wool merchants, they built a massive bank, used financial power to gain political control, and fostered an explosion of Florentine culture, making Florence the heart of the Renaissance.
  • The House of Bourbon is a major European royal dynasty, originating from France as a branch of the Capetians, ruling France from 1589 to 1792 and again from 1814–1848, and later providing monarchs for Spain (since 1700, with interruptions, and currently), Naples, Sicily, and Parma. Key figures include France's Henry IV, the Sun King Louis XIV, and the ill-fated Louis XVI, whose execution ushered in the French Revolution, ending Bourbon rule in France until the restoration. Their legacy is tied to absolute monarchy, French cultural dominance, and widespread European influence, continuing today in Spain and Luxembourg.
  • The history of the Navarres involves the ancient, powerful Kingdom of Navarre in the Pyrenees, which produced French royalty like Henry IV of France, and distinct lines of American families, notably the French-Canadian Navarres who settled in Michigan (like Robert Navarre in Detroit) and the Potawatomi Navarre family in Kansas/Indian Territory, tracing back to early settlers like Pierre Navarre in Indiana.

  • The saying "Love is blind," meaning love makes people overlook faults, has ancient roots but was popularized in English by Geoffrey Chaucer in the late 14th century ("The Merchant's Tale") and later cemented in language by Shakespeare in plays like The Merchant of Venice, where it means lovers can't see flaws, even their own, and often highlights how love obscures reality and judgment, a concept supported by scientific studies on brain function during attraction.
  • “The blind would rub noses like the Chinese when they met” People from cultures like the Inuit (using the kunik), Māori (the hongi), and peoples in some Persian Gulf countries (like the UAE and Saudi Arabia) use nose rubbing as a greeting, often to exchange breath or scents, signifying affection, respect, or a spiritual connection, rather than a Western-style kiss, with different cultural meanings attached.
  • Ohio's history spans from its Native American roots, through European exploration and settlement (leading to statehood in 1803, though officially recognized later), its crucial role in the Civil War as a Union state with significant anti-slavery sentiment, rapid industrialization in the late 19th century fueled by cities like Cleveland and Akron, its identity as the "Mother of Presidents," and into the mid-20th century, solidifying its industrial might and diverse cultural influences from New England, New York, and the South.

  • “Polynesian head-hunters” Polynesian headhunting, particularly prominent in places like the Solomon Islands (Melanesia, often grouped with Polynesian cultures in historical accounts) and among the Māori of New Zealand, wasn't just about violence; it served deep cultural, spiritual, and economic purposes, often tied to ancestor worship, capturing soul matter, avenging deaths, enhancing warrior status, and controlling land, with practices evolving and often intensifying or changing due to European contact and the trade in preserved heads (mokomokai).
  • Niagara Falls near Buffalo, New York was a popular honeymoon destination. The falls began forming approximately 12,000 years ago, at the end of the last ice age. As the glaciers of the Wisconsin glaciation receded, vast amounts of meltwater filled the basins they had carved, creating the Great Lakes. The water from the newly formed Lake Erie poured over a steep slope known as the Niagara Escarpment on its way to Lake Ontario. Over millennia, this powerful torrent of water eroded the rock layers, carving out the seven-mile-long Niagara Gorge and causing the falls to retreat upstream to their present-day location. The retreat of the falls is a result of a hard, erosion-resistant layer of limestone and dolomite (Lockport Formation) that sits on top of softer, more easily worn away shale (Rochester Formation). The river erodes the softer rock underneath, causing the hard caprock to break off in large chunks. The rate of erosion, once several feet per year, has been significantly slowed by water diversion for hydroelectric power. The first inhabitants of the region were the Neutral and other Iroquoian-speaking peoples who settled in the area between 1300 and 1400 A.D.. The name "Niagara" is believed to be derived from an Iroquoian word, "Onguiaahra," which means "thundering noise". The French were the first Europeans to explore the Niagara region in the 17th century. Jesuit priest Louis Hennepin is credited with the first documented eyewitness account in 1678, publishing his discovery in Europe and inspiring further exploration. Following the construction of the Erie Canal in 1825 and the development of the railroad in the 1800s, Niagara Falls became a popular tourist destination. Its association with honeymooners began in the early 19th century. The surge in tourism led to unbridled commercialism, with unscrupulous entrepreneurs charging visitors for views of the falls and turning the area into a chaotic carnival. In response to the over-commercialization and industrial threat to the falls' natural beauty, a conservation movement arose. Frederick Law Olmsted, co-designer of Central Park, and others advocated for state protection. Their efforts resulted in the creation of New York's Niagara Reservation, the first state park in the U.S., and Ontario's Queen Victoria Niagara Falls Park, both established in 1885. The falls' power potential was recognized as early as 1750, when a small canal was built to power a sawmill. In 1882, the first hydroelectric generating station was built on the American side to provide direct current (DC) electricity to local mills. A group of investors backed the development of a large-scale alternating current (AC) power system, designed by Westinghouse Electric with Nikola Tesla as a consultant. In 1896, power generated at Niagara was sent over 20 miles to Buffalo, proving the feasibility of long-distance AC transmission. Today, a significant portion of the Niagara River's flow is diverted through tunnels to power plants on both the American and Canadian sides, which generate millions of kilowatts of electricity. A treaty between the U.S. and Canada ensures enough water remains flowing over the falls to preserve its scenic beauty during tourist hours. For over a century, the falls have attracted thrill-seekers performing dangerous stunts for fame and fortune. In 1901, Annie Edson Taylor became the first person to successfully go over the falls in a barrel. Many other daredevils, including tightrope walkers and those in homemade contraptions, have attempted to conquer the falls, some successfully and others tragically. In 1969, the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers temporarily diverted the water from the American Falls to study the effects of erosion. In 1848, an ice jam in the river above the falls completely cut off the water flow for a period of up to 40 hours, leaving the falls dry. An iron scow carrying two men became stuck on the rocks upstream from the falls in 1918. Despite the rescue of the crew, the vessel remains wedged in the river to this day.
