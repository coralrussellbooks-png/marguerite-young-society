Research notes: Chapter 3 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 3

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

Miss MacIntosh is constructed as the anti-plural self; she insists on a single, stable, unambiguous identity. "She was neither a high brow nor a low brow but just, as she was pleased to admit, a plain middle brow, a Middle Westerner, trying to steer her middle course". She is always in her harness, her bells always tinkling so that "it might always be known where she was".

And yet the text quietly, devastatingly multiplies her. Mr. Spitzer hired her believing she was a brunette: "he had rather been under the impression that she was a brunette, that her forehead was overhung by cliffs of darkness, that her eyebrows were dark and beetling cliffs". He sees her again and finds a different woman. Her past is irrecoverable, contradictory, full of gaps: she had been connected with lighthouses for the blind, asylums for the mad, homes for old sailors, and yet "she was always, it was true, sufficiently cryptic when she mentioned what, precisely, she had done with her life". She has a lost brother consumed by the very head-hunter she despises. She is simultaneously the woman of absolute certainty and a woman whose history cannot be assembled into a coherent self.

The narrator's title, "Miss MacIntosh, my darling", is itself a plural act: it names her as both the plain nursemaid of Iowa and as an object of profound, almost mythological love, "truer than the North star". These two Miss MacIntoshes cannot be fully reconciled. The self she insists upon (singular, knowable, direct) is exactly the self the text refuses to grant her.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Miss MacIntosh's entire project is the destruction of illusion in favor of unmediated reality. She dismisses phantoms, imaginary moose, hallucinations, the head-hunter's dreams, the mother's opium visions. "She was under no influence of that hallucinatory atmosphere in which she lived, and she had never seen these phantoms thickening in the air around us".

But the chapter performs a slow, relentless argument that her "reality" is itself a construction: simply a more aggressively maintained one. Her heaven, if any existed, "should be bare and plain and unfurnished"; she constructs an afterlife in the image of her own preferences. She cannot see "the purple hairstreak moths among the evening flowers": her vision has its own selective blindness. She dismisses her mother's pearls as probably imaginary, "not real", collapsing the distinction between what is owned and what is dreamed: "it was all the same to her".

Most strikingly, the text observes that she "seeming not always to distinguish between what my mother possessed and what she thought she possessed". This is a devastating inversion: the woman who exists to enforce the real/illusory distinction cannot herself reliably make it. Her What Cheer, Iowa (plain, unadorned, the ground of all her certainties) is never seen; it exists in the novel only as her assertion of it, a constructed origin story no more verifiable than the mother's opium paradise. The line between illusion and reality is, exactly as Pillar 2 insists, itself a construction, and Miss MacIntosh's construction is simply louder and more aggressively policed than the others.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Miss MacIntosh explicitly refuses grief its proper weather. "There was never an unoccupied moment, and there was no time for the luxury of idle tears, for vague griefs". She insists that "each day's business should be finished and left behind us", that grief "was no creation" and "nothing of which one should be proud".

And yet grief saturates her. Her brother disappeared among the cannibals she despises. Her nose was "broken in a traffic accident and never correctly mended, she having gone to no doctor": a small, terrible detail of a pain deliberately left unhealed, unacknowledged, the body carrying what the will refuses to mourn. She has "no friends". She carries two pennies for closing her own eyes at death. She is "always alone, single and single-minded, accompanied by no guardian angel".

The chapter's closing movement reveals grief that has been accumulating beneath the entire narrative: the narrator's grief at Miss MacIntosh's disappearance. "Who but I had caused this terrible thing... who but I had killed her?". This grief does not resolve; it is the emotional climate of the entire novel, the reason the narrator is on the bus, the reason the chapter exists. The very length and recursiveness of the prose, its refusal to end a sentence when another clause can be added, is the formal enactment of grief that "accumulates" rather than concludes. Miss MacIntosh tried to teach the child that grief was nothing; the novel's form is the child's answer.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator is the chapter's supreme ethical figure, not Miss MacIntosh. Miss MacIntosh acts; the narrator witnesses. The narrator watches Miss MacIntosh "searching through the waves at the shore's edge, searching for something she had not lost and had not found": a perception Miss MacIntosh herself would refuse, but which the witnessing child holds without resolving.

The narrator witnesses without flinching the full contradiction of Miss MacIntosh: her robustness and her mortality, her certainty and her unknowability, her practicality and her hidden grief. The narrator does not simplify her into either a saint of common sense or a figure of pathos. Both are held simultaneously.

The act of witnessing extends to the disappearance itself. The narrator catalogs the "fugitive details" (the eye-glasses rose in the sunlight, the folded handkerchief, the one remaining button) with the precision of someone who has refused to look away: "Such trivial things by which to remember her, yet now more precious than crowns of silver and crowns of gold!". This is witnessing as ethical practice: to hold the small real thing against the erasure of death, to refuse the comfort of abstraction.

Mr. Spitzer, by contrast, fails as witness. He "had never noticed precisely what Miss MacIntosh wore". He picks up things from the beach that were never hers. His memories are "baffled by his tendency to remember false memories". He is the novel's anti-witness, and his failure is explicitly moral; it is why he cannot search for her, cannot find her, cannot grieve her properly.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The chapter is itself a recursive construction. The narrator is on a bus, moving away from the world of Miss MacIntosh, reconstructing her from memory, but the reconstruction is not linear. It circles, returns, adds, qualifies. The sentence structure enacts this: clauses accumulate, loop back, modify what was just said. "Her marcelled red hair fitting close like a helmet and lighting the dark days like a second sun or moon": the description is never finished, always being revised with another "or," another "and."

Miss MacIntosh herself practices recursive construction in her activities: "We would work all day pulling nails out of an old box, straightening the nails, then driving them into another box made of old boards". This is labor that returns to its own beginning, that builds provisionally, that does not arrive at a final structure. She sweeps footprints from the sand before the tide erases them anyway. She mends the fence, caulks the boat, hammers the raft, always returning to maintenance, to the ongoing work that is never finished.

The narrator reads Pilgrim's Progress "straight to the end" and then from "the end, reading straight to the beginning, working from redemption backward": a perfect image of recursive construction, of the building that is never finished because it can always be entered from another direction.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Miss MacIntosh demands completion obsessively. "One finished task should be preferred to a hundred started". She insists that "each day's business should be finished and left behind us". She stamps out the last ember before the waves come. She folds clothes "as carefully as if we never expected to put them on again".

But the chapter itself is the argument against her. Its sentences refuse to end. Its paragraphs accumulate subordinate clauses that defer closure endlessly. The description of Miss MacIntosh walking the beach runs for pages without arriving at a conclusion about who she is. This is not stylistic excess; it is the ethics of incompletion enacted in prose form. The demand for completion that Miss MacIntosh enforces is exposed as "its own violence against experience."

Her disappearance is the ultimate incompletion: no body, no proof, no conclusion. "Without the dead body, there could be no proof of death in a legal sense". She who demanded that everything be finished is herself unfinished: her story has no ending, her death is unconfirmed, her past is irrecoverable. The novel refuses to grant her the completion she demanded of everything else.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Miss MacIntosh is herself a marginal figure: "this comparatively colorless person", ignored by the visitors to the house, unseen by the surf fishermen and clam-diggers as she passes, hired without inquiry, never looked at twice. She is the nursemaid, the servant, the plain woman, the woman from What Cheer, Iowa: the margins of every social category.

But she is also the evidence that the constructed nature of reality becomes most visible at the edges. In the context of the mother's opium household (where imaginary butlers, phantom moose, and head-hunters are the norm) Miss MacIntosh's radical ordinariness becomes extraordinary, "more peculiar than flower-crowned satyrs and moon-eyed minotaurs". Her marginality to the household's reality-system makes her the most visible demonstration that reality-systems are constructed: she simply refuses to participate in the household's construction, and this refusal is itself a kind of strangeness.

The head-hunter, another marginal figure, is her mirror: he comes "each afternoon in a black-curtained cab for fear of startling the simple country people", hidden at the margins of the road, just as she is hidden at the margins of the house. Both are evidence of the constructed categories they transgress.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The past does not recede in this chapter; it presses upward through everything. The house itself is sedimentary: "many rooms which had not been visited for years", coins "unearthed from graves", the garden full of "broken marble or porous statues sloping toward the edge of the wild sea", the beach strewn with "rotted masts and canvas sails", "the hull of an old fishing boat swept up".

Miss MacIntosh tries to live only in the present, but the beach she walks is composed entirely of the past's wreckage. She throws driftwood back into the sea; the sea returns it. She sweeps footprints from the sand; the tide makes new ones. The past is not behind her; it is beneath her, in the sediment of the shore she walks.

Her own past presses up through her in ways she cannot control: the brother lost to cannibals, the broken nose never set, the cryptic references to asylums and lighthouses and a "Slough of Despond". She refuses to excavate these layers, "she did not believe in delving into the past, blowing up a cold spark among the dead ashes", but the layers are there regardless. The text excavates them for her, against her will, through the accumulation of small revelations across the chapter.

The narrator's grief is itself sedimentary: "Seldom could I bring myself to think of her unless I saw a shadow moving far away in the whistling wind or the faded colors of an old plaid raincoat laid out upon the sand". The past is not behind the narrator; it is in every shadow, every plaid coat, every faded color. It is the ground the narrator stands on.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Miss MacIntosh's language is aggressively positional; it speaks from What Cheer, Iowa, from Protestant plainness, from the tradition of practical virtue, and it pretends to speak from nowhere, from simple common sense. "She was always right". "She was bare-faced and clear of vision". Her speech has no nuance, no qualification: "she would argue with the world's greatest preacher if she knew him".

The narrator's language is the counter-argument. It is explicitly located, in memory, in grief, in the child's perspective, in the retrospective reconstruction of a woman who has disappeared. It names its own uncertainty: "it would always seem", "perhaps", "as she might say", "in her opinion". The narrator's language knows where it speaks from; Miss MacIntosh's does not.

The chapter also enacts the constructive power of language in its treatment of Miss MacIntosh's origin. "She had originated, quite simply, in What Cheer, Iowa, a small town like any other, as she frequently pointed out, and no different from its neighbors. If anything, it was more like its neighbors than its neighbors were". This is language performing its own absurdity: the insistence on typicality so extreme it becomes a kind of myth-making. What Cheer, Iowa is not a neutral fact; it is a constructed origin, a language-act that grounds Miss MacIntosh's authority in a place that is itself only ever present in the text as her assertion of it.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Miss MacIntosh explicitly rejects transcendence: no heaven, no angels, no afterlife, no God who has not left "many things uncreated". And yet the sacred keeps erupting through the ordinary in her world. When she and the child are lost in darkness, "Miss MacIntosh found an old glass jar at the edge of the dark surf and made a lamp by filling it with fireflies": an image of immanent transcendence so precise it is almost unbearable: the sacred light made from the ordinary world's own creatures, carried like a lighthouse over dark waters.

She nurses a wounded seagull "carrying it against her bosom under her faded mackintosh until it had mended its wing and recovered its strength": the sacred act of care performed with no sacred language, no ritual, just the body's warmth and the faded coat. Her "idea of heaven" is simply "that no man should have a hole in his coat here on earth, that all should have enough to eat": a heaven entirely immanent, entirely within the ordinary world.

The grey feather she wears, "a seagull feather which she had found on the beach, something which had drifted through a drifting cloud. That was something which was already lost. That was something which did not cost money", is the perfect emblem of immanent transcendence: the sacred object is the found, the lost, the free, the ordinary. The transcendent is not elsewhere; it is in the feather already falling through the cloud.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is perhaps the deepest pillar in the chapter, and it operates most powerfully through the narrator's relationship to Miss MacIntosh. The narrator is not simply remembering her; the narrator is constituted by her. "There was only then, after all, to lead the bewildered child with her own dark problems of that childhood which groped toward light, one who could be trusted". Miss MacIntosh is the sediment upon which the narrator's self was built: the one stable point in the opium paradise, the "North star" by which the child navigated.

But the narrator has also constituted Miss MacIntosh: has preserved her, has made her visible, has refused to let her disappear into the sea that took her. "Such trivial things by which to remember her, yet now more precious than crowns of silver and crowns of gold!". The narrator carries Miss MacIntosh forward in time; Miss MacIntosh deposited the narrator's capacity for witness, for grief, for attention to the real. The obligation is structural, not chosen. "Who but I had caused this terrible thing... who but I had killed her?". The narrator cannot escape the crossing point: cannot stand outside the relationship to claim innocence or distance. The ancestors are in the sediment. Miss MacIntosh is in every shadow the narrator sees. The carrying continues.

And Miss MacIntosh herself stands at a crossing point she refuses to acknowledge: between the world she came from (What Cheer, Iowa, the asylums, the Slough of Despond) and the world she inhabits (the opium house, the sea, the child who will carry her). She is "constituted by what was deposited before she arrived" (the brother lost to cannibals, the broken nose, the two pennies for closing her own eyes) and she is "constituting the ground the not-yet-born will stand on" through every lesson she gives the child. She insists she is only passing through, only doing her job; the novel insists she is a crossing point, and the crossing continues long after she has walked into the sea.
