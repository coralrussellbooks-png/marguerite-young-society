Thus Spoke Zarathustra

← Previous · Intertextuality in MMMD Home · Next →

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Philosophy: Nietzsche

Definition from research

Category: optional. Nietzsche is never named, but his central teaching is, and his great image runs through the book.

Friedrich Nietzsche's Thus Spoke Zarathustra, published between 1883 and 1885, follows a prophet who comes down from the mountains to teach. Two of its ideas matter here. The first is eternal recurrence, the thought that all things return again and again. The second is the image of man as "a rope over an abyss," stretched between what he has been and what he might become.

Mr. Spitzer, grieving his twin brother, asks, "Should not eternal recurrence be the one law of life" (chapter 42). The word "abyss" appears in thirty-three chapters of the novel and gathers around the Spitzer brothers. Mr. Spitzer pictures Peron's last hours as a long hovering at the edge: "not suddenly had he come to the great abyss, the yawning chasm" (chapter 58).

Young's use of Nietzsche is gentle. Eternal recurrence, which Zarathustra offers as a hard test, is for Mr. Spitzer a hope: that the lamp that went out might be lit again. The abyss is not the ground of a new man but the place where a brother was lost. The named teaching and the abyss make it an optional reference.
