Movement II. Dubious Ground: The Constructed and Uncertain Real

Characters: Miss MacIntosh

What feels most solid turns out to be the least trustworthy, and this is the one movement whose principle Young had to grow into rather than carry from the start. The early poems barely touch it. It arrives in force in the Middle Years, when the poems begin to think their doubt out loud. "Snow in the Mountains" sets Plato's ideal snow against the actual snow and cannot say which is the more real, "And even the wild goat of Ephesus" cannot pry the knower from the known, and "But where come the innuendos" finds a pattern only in the accidental. By Moderate Fable the doubt is a full method. "Dubious Ground" walks a sleepwalker across a field that is not firm, "A High Subjectivity" states it flatly, "The idea of the universe is inconclusive," and "Bishop Berkeley of Cloyne" hands the movement its patron, the philosopher who held that a thing exists only in being perceived. The late "Proem to Reality" closes the line: "There is no whole." The honest response to a built and shifting world is not to pretend it is bedrock but to keep questioning and keep revising. This is the country the novel lives in, a thousand-plus pages where waking and dreaming trade places without warning.

"Sequence."

A woman stands between "waves of the meadow" and "the sea's crying," two worlds that will not resolve into one, and the closing question goes unanswered on purpose. The poem lives in the Invention, treating the solid categories of land and sea, past and present, as fluid and provisional. The novel's dream-and-waking country begins here.

"Dubious Ground."

A sleepwalker crosses "the dubious texture of the ground," aware even in sleep that the field is not firm. The poem is the movement's namesake because it makes uncertainty a form of perception rather than a failure of it. To feel the ground's dubiousness is to have woken up to the Invention.

"Whorl."

"Waxen leaves" and a "clear-cut bloom" look preserved, artificial, a little too perfect, a made surface passing for a natural one. The poem catches the built quality of what claims to be given. Seeing the wax is the first step out from under the spell.

"Daniel."

The speaker accepts that regret is unavoidable and keeps questioning anyway, refusing both false certainty and despair. This is Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary as a temperament: build a life provisionally, revise it, distrust the finished answer. The seeking is the faith.

"Heroics."

"Wind and foam" dominate a turbulent, shifting world where nothing human holds against the elements. The poem sets the self inside forces it cannot fix or found itself on. Solid ground is exposed as the illusion it always was.

"A Crystal Principle."

Crystal stands for a fragile, evolving purity that nature reaches toward and never secures, undone by "accident unknown / And least departure." Perfection is provisional, always one small deviation from breaking. The poem holds the ideal open rather than closing it.

"A High Subjectivity."

The poem states the movement's thesis outright: "The idea of the universe is inconclusive." Reality cannot be finished or fully known, only entertained. This is Reality Is Constructed pushed to the scale of the cosmos, the whole of things left unsettled on principle.

"That Chance."

Existence itself is founded on chance, and yet chance can "propose a system like a sphere," order arising from the accidental. The poem refuses both pure chaos and fixed design, holding the built world as contingent all the way down. Nothing here had to be, which is why nothing here is inevitable.

"Null Class."

The unicorn and the "non-existent" hover on the line between real and imagined, entities that defy proof yet insist on presence. The poem makes the boundary between fact and fiction porous, which is the Invention's home ground. What we agree to imagine has its own kind of being.

"Bishop Berkeley of Cloyne."

The poem hands the movement its patron, the idealist who held that "to be is to be perceived," and pairs him with the severed head of John the Baptist, thought and martyrdom together. If a thing exists only in being perceived, then perception builds the world, and the builder is answerable for it. The novel, a thousand pages of consciousness making and unmaking reality, is Berkeley's principle turned into a book.

"Failing Perspective."

The bright earth turns and drags along colored elements, a child and a balloon and a plover pulled through ceaseless change. Nothing in the frame stays fixed long enough to be certain of. The poem treats perspective itself as unstable, a view always failing and reforming.

"The Time Is Not Now."

"Thin blood needling in the brain" and a flight of "ashen swallows" scramble past and present until time will not stay put. The poem lives in the seam where the clock loses authority. When now is not now, the ordered timeline is revealed as one more arrangement.

"Speculative Evening."

"If there were no past now, but specious present only" opens a world where time is disjointed and possibly illusory, made by a "heavenly geometer" no one can see. The poem questions whether a design means anything if no mind can grasp it. It keeps the construction open by refusing to trust the blueprint.

"Slow Motion."

The heart becomes "a camera of slow motion" and "a stellar eye," an organ that does not simply beat but perceives, holding time at a deliberate delay. The poem relocates perception into the body and makes it strange. What we call seeing is a built and lagging thing.

"That Apple Was Mental."

The apple is "mental, a constellation of ideas," and the poem asks whether the real can be invisible, whether sound can be inaudible and still exist. The line between physical fact and mental construct dissolves. This is Berkeley's question again, put to a single piece of fruit.

"That Cloud."

Love, identity, and perception all turn out to be mutable here, selfhood interchangeable and dissolving under a passing cloud. The poem wavers between the unreliability of perception and the unreliability of the self, which is why it also belongs to the Many Selves. Two pillars meet in one cloud, and neither cancels the other.

"The Process."

The poem imagines clock hands running backward as easily as forward, questioning whether time has any fixed direction at all. Causality is treated as a convention rather than a law. Once time is up for revision, so is everything built on it.

"Figures of Space."

Nature declares "a pact of unaltered peace" and lets sleep enter softly, a surrender to a process no one controls. Sleep stands in for death, the ultimate uncontrollable, met not with terror but with acceptance. The poem keeps the construction open right to the edge of oblivion.

"At the Cinema."

Time unwinds as "mechanic timelessness" on "the waste expanse of canvas," a dead lover held as a spectral frame of film. The poem makes time itself a projected illusion, a reel that can be paused and rerun. Reality flickers, and the flicker is the truth of it.

"Snow in the Mountains"

Plato arrives: "Plato's snow more real than the shadow of snow," the mountains "expanding with the theme / Of absolute," the travelers "twin souls / Of real and unreal." The poem sets the actual snow against its ideal and cannot say which is more real, which is Reality Is Constructed lifted into open metaphysics. This is where the Dubious Ground of the later work first thinks out loud.

"But where come the innuendos"

"The causeless winds, the illogical tear," accidents "Happening so, because it has happened so," until "how dark, / Dark is its responsibility." The poem turns chance and causelessness over the way "That Chance" will, finding a pattern only in the anomaly. Reality is contingent all the way down, and the mind that notices carries the weight of it.

"And even the wild goat of Ephesus"

"We are both subject thinking and object / thought of," and "we cannot know how much of us is involved in the act / of knowledge," with "all things are process." The poem pushes perception to its limit, unable to pry the knower from the known. It is Dubious Ground as pure epistemology, the same question "That Apple Was Mental" asks of a single fruit.

"Flea Circus on Times Square"

A prose letter to a child: from a skyscraper's height people are "just dolls or spiders," yet "to themselves each is very different from the other," and the circus fleas are people to themselves too. The poem turns the scale over until every vantage is provisional, the giant's view and the flea's equally partial. The bald showman "with a face just as round as the moon's" is the Miss MacIntosh of the flea world, and the novel's play of scale and perception is here in a nursery voice.

"The Priesthood of Motion"

A sea gull wheels over cotton fields where an old Black field worker looks up in tears, and everything becomes "a process wayfaring in stellar space," "No goal ... but a tide." The poem dissolves a fixed scene into pure motion without destination, the flux that "Process" will name outright. Its grieving laborer keeps a foot in the world the Crossing Point watches, but the poem's argument is that nothing arrives.

"Proem to Reality"

"Polaris will not be always the north North Star," "There is no reference but to shifting fixity," "There is no whole," reality "perceived partially / By particles," "the putting together of names." The poem is a late summa of Dubious Ground, the built and shifting real stated without a hedge, and it folds in the naming of Language Shapes What's Possible and the immanent present of The Sacred Is Here. Its closing admission, that "god was the only project admitting of completion," is Unfinished Is Honest signed at the bottom.
