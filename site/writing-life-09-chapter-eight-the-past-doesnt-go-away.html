Chapter Eight: The Past Doesn’t Go Away

Part of Radical Immanence Across Marguerite Young's Writing Life, on Radical Immanence, the philosophy Coral Russell draws from the work of Marguerite Young (1908 to 1995).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Characters: Esther Longtree · Miss MacIntosh

The eighth pillar addresses the relationship between the past and the present, between what has been lost and what continues to carry it.

The third pillar establishes that grief is permanent weather, that the losses built into the foundation of individual and collective life do not resolve. The eighth pillar extends this into a full account of time: time is not linear but sedimentary. The past does not recede. It accumulates.

Walter Benjamin's "Theses on the Philosophy of History," written in 1940 shortly before his death, offers the most compressed and powerful account of sedimentary time available in the philosophical tradition. Benjamin's image of the angel of history, facing the wreckage of the past while being blown forward into the future by the storm of progress, is the image of a being for whom the past does not recede. The angel sees the accumulation of catastrophe, layer upon layer, the wreckage piling up at its feet, and cannot turn away to face the future because the past is always already there, demanding attention.

Benjamin's argument is that the progressive view of history, the view that the past is behind us and the future is ahead and we are moving from worse to better, is not a description of how time actually works. It is an ideology that serves the interests of those who benefit from the current arrangement by naturalizing it as the outcome of a progress that cannot be reversed. This is Young's permanent grief extended to the scale of history. The grief does not resolve not only because the individual self cannot put down its losses but because the losses are real and cumulative and the passage of time does not undo them. What was lost is still lost. The wreckage is still there. The angel of history is still facing it.

The geological record is sedimentary time made literal. Each layer of rock is a moment of the past that has not receded but accumulated, pressing down on the layer before it, all of it still present, none of it gone, none of it resolved. The geological record does not tell the story of progress. It tells the story of accumulation: extinction after extinction, transformation after transformation, nothing resolved, everything carried forward in a different form.

This has direct implications for how we understand the relationship between historical injustice and present political life. The wealth extracted from enslaved labor is not a historical fact that can be acknowledged and moved past. It is a layer in the sediment, still present, still pressing down, still part of the substrate on which current economic arrangements stand. The land taken from indigenous peoples is not a past event. It is a geological fact about the present landscape, carried forward in the soil and the water and the names of places and the arrangements of property that are still in effect.

Young drew this layered ground herself, literally, in "Angel in the Forest". The Rappites lie “only one layer above an Indian burial ground,” and she asks, “When resurrection comes, will both racial groups emerge, with tomahawks and hallelujahs?” A storekeeper in the town of 1940 puts it plainly: “You don’t have to do anything to get into history. You are just there.” Her last published poem, “Elegy” (1945), carries it to the scale of the universe: “Reality with universal memory is haunted.”

The sedimentary view of time does not produce guilt as a permanent condition. It produces the honest acknowledgment that the present is built on the accumulated past, that the past is not behind us but beneath us, and that building justly requires attending to what is in the substrate rather than pretending the ground is neutral.

"Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" is itself a sedimentary text. The novel does not progress from beginning to middle to end in the linear sense. It accumulates. Images and characters and weathers return, each time with the weight of all their previous appearances pressing down on the new encounter. The novel moves downward, into the sediment, toward the layers that are oldest and most fundamental. This is the formal expression of the eighth pillar: the novel that moves like geology rather than narrative, accumulating rather than progressing, finding depth rather than arrival.

This pillar was not settled early. “Lot’s Wife” (1930) pities the backward look and still warns that “he who lives / In what has flown / Is less than salt.” The poems of 1943 to 1945 argue both sides, imagining a world created a moment ago with its memories built in, before “Elegy” settles on universal memory. The novel makes the past psychic and drains it of names. "Harp Song for a Radical" brings named history back, from Sand Creek to Ludlow. The pillar moves from myth toward history.

Phase One, Childhood and the Early Poems (1908 to 1934)

Pillar 8: The Past Doesn’t Go Away

"Song of Moving Away" (1929) carries the past forward: "Into another street I take / These old, old days and my old friends / And all that I cannot forsake." In "Jonathan's House" the "old, familiar shadows" are "true."

Complication: "Lot's Wife" (1930) pities her backward look and still ends: "he who lives / In what has flown / Is less than salt, / Is less than stone." From the start, Young both honors the past and fears being held by it.

Phase Two, The Chicago Years, Prismatic Ground, and the Graduate Essays (1935 to 1937)

Pillar 8: The Past Doesn’t Go Away

In "The Return," the land "will / Be perhaps forever in pause, / Recollecting its child still." "Lesser Magellan" follows an explorer whose Holy Grail is the past's small leftovers: "a curl, a bag of saffron, / Swan's throat time has blurred." "Heart Who Stays" remembers what cannot go. The settlers of "Currin Valley" cannot leave the past "or / Leaving in sorrow, to forget."

Complication: the Patmore essay notes that the past, "in being known, still keeps its distance."

Phase Three, The Philosophical Poems (1938 to 1945)

Pillar 8: The Past Doesn’t Go Away

"Elegy" (1945), Young's last published poem: "Reality with universal memory is haunted, / Throughout the universe is memory." In "Legend in Grass," a drunken general leers "vastly underground / As if he were unburied still." A draft: "Man has eaten history."

Complication: this phase argues hardest against the pillar. "Speculative Evening" (1943) imagines a world created a moment ago with memories built in: "who would realize the colossal joke?" "Proem to Reality": "That which has been and is not exists perhaps no more / As little as that which has never been." "The Time Is Not Now" calls the remembered past of Keats and Chatterton "illusory." Only "Elegy" settles on universal memory.

Phase Four, "Angel in the Forest" and “Complex Life and Complex Letters” (1945 to 1946)

Pillar 8: The Past Doesn’t Go Away

This is the first full layered ground. The Rappites lie "only one layer above an Indian burial ground," and the narrator asks, "When resurrection comes, will both racial groups emerge, with tomahawks and hallelujahs?" The Owenites "felt haunted by their Rappite predecessors." The storekeeper "is always kicking against history in the dark": "You don't have to do anything to get into history. You are just there." Young later recalled the town still split between Rappite and Owenite sympathies "like between Republicans and Democrats."

Complication: the failed Owenites sit on corners remembering what they left behind. The book treats this nostalgia gently, as an escape.

Phase Five, "Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" (written from the mid 1940s, published 1965)

Pillar 8: The Past Doesn’t Go Away

Miss MacIntosh cries "There was no past," and the narration answers, "the true lover remembers everything." Esther's past returns as accusation, "the A, the B, the C." Historical injustice sits at the book's core. Esther's one real child is a mixed-race baby killed in the Deep South, whose father is a lynched man who survived, and Young took the image from a rope "still hanging" in a Southern town where she had lived. The detective shows how a past stays buried, since the system will not look.

Complication: Strehle notes that Young drains named history from the novel. New Harmony enters "with all utopian references removed," and the places go unnamed. The past here is psychic and mythic.

Against "Angel in the Forest", where the past was laid down as graves under graves, the novel moves the past inward. It places historical injustice at the book’s emotional core and at the same time removes its names. The pillar is fully lived here as memory but only partly present as history, which returns in "Harp Song for a Radical".

Phase Six, The Occasional Prose, Interviews, and Self-Interpretation (1940s to 1995)

Pillar 8: The Past Doesn’t Go Away

The Van Doren speech: his poems knock "upon the door of a house which is already gone," and it ends on "a house where the dead live." "Reality may be hidden but is not lost." "Old James" shows nostalgia hiding the widows he profits from and the armies gathering. "The Doll People": the rag doll stands for "a past which may never have been what we think it was."

Phase Seven, "Harp Song for a Radical" (written in her last decades, published 1999)

Pillar 8: The Past Doesn’t Go Away

Named history returns in full. Sand Creek is "historical precedent for the slaughter of the strikers in the tent city of Ludlow." The Central Park Indian hunter is "a memorial to a species that was doomed to extinction." The McGuffey readers passed through the Civil War with no "mention of a Fugitive Slave Act or a St. Louis porter named Dred Scott." The sentimental, neutral past is named as a school curriculum.
