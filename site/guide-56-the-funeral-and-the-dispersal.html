Chapter 56: The Funeral and the Dispersal

← Chapter 55 · Guide Home · Chapter 57 →

Research notes: Chapter 56

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Themes: Fate and Chance · Madness and Sanity

Symbols: Clothing, Hats, and Skirts · Clouds and Storms · Keys, Doors, and Locks · Weddings and Funerals

Philosophy: Augustine and Christian Theology

Allusions: Gambling and Playing Cards · Napoleon and Wellington

Peron is buried with his poker chips, his dice, and a playing card.

Joachim arranges the funeral with the care and precision he brings to everything: the corpse dressed with sartorial elegance, the best suits, the formal correctness that Peron in life would have found hilarious. A violinist with an empty sleeve plays. An official track bugler performs: someone who has played at Pimlico and Suffolk Downs, at the great racetracks where Peron spent much of his life. The ceremony is perfect. Joachim leaves only the red rose in the grave, the recognition at “the portals of the great city.”

And then, over the years that follow, he gives away everything else. Peron's wardrobe (the extraordinary, bewildering accumulation of a man who could be anything and dressed accordingly) goes to the homeless, the poor, the tramps on the street. Homburgs. Stetsons. “Trilbys, pork-pies, brown bowler derbies.” Jockeys' silks. Cantons and merinos and astrakhans and Irish cambrics and paisleys. All of Peron's identity, all the costumes of the chameleon, scattered into the city. Joachim, keeping only his own threadbare suit, watches the dispersal and wonders which of them is still alive.

What actually happens in Chapter 56

Chapter 56 is a chapter of aftermath: the aftermath of Peron's death that Joachim has been living inside for all these years, rendered now in its most direct and least oblique form. He describes the funeral. He describes the dispersal of the wardrobe. He describes his own philosophical collapse: the inability to believe in reality as a stable foundation, the sense of being a puppet controlled by unknown forces, the question of who tailored him in this strange life, the mad tailor who cut his existence from this particular cloth.

His grief is not personal grief only. Young has always given Mr. Spitzer a grief larger than any individual loss: he mourns flowers, moths, tides, stars, moments. He mourns the impermanence of all things. This chapter gives that grief its most explicit form: the cosmic mourning of a sentimentalist who cannot stop noticing what passes. The funeral is for Peron. The mourning extends, in concentric rings outward, to everything that will not last.

The chapter closes with Joachim lamenting the absence of a guiding author or architect of being. He carries “the golden key to the door that” is no more. The door is gone. The key remains, perfectly made, opening nothing. The dead musician who lives despite attempts to silence him, that last image, encapsulates what Mr. Spitzer has been all along: a persistence that continues past the point where persistence makes obvious sense, because the alternative is the silence that no one should choose for themselves.

The funeral rites for the gambler

Poker chips, dice, and a playing card in the grave.

This is Joachim's version of love: knowing his brother well enough to bury him with the tools of his trade, the instruments of the life he actually lived rather than the life the conventional ceremony would prefer to acknowledge. Most funerals pretend that the dead person was better behaved than they were. Joachim buries Peron as Peron was: a gambler, a man of chance, a person whose relationship to fate was always a wager rather than a plan.

The red rose he leaves is the recognition, at “the portals of the great city,” the entrance to wherever Peron has gone. The red rose for Peron, the white rose worn by Joachim himself: life passing beyond life. The two brothers each assigned their color at the threshold. Peron's red is vitality, passion, the gambler's flush. Joachim's white is the pale replica, the mourner, the one who remains behind in the register of the barely living.

Young presents this funeral as a performance of love: an act of witness and recognition that required Joachim to know Peron completely and to honor that knowledge rather than correct it. He gave Peron the funeral Peron would have wanted, not the funeral convention prescribed. This is the most intimate thing he has ever done. He does it alone.

The wardrobe dispersed

Peron's wardrobe was a collection of identities: every hat and suit a different version of the chameleon's display, a different costume for a different occasion in a life organized around becoming whatever the moment required. The homburgs for the formal occasions. The Stetsons for the Western affect. The trilbys for jazz clubs and Hollywood glamour. The jockeys' silks for the racetracks. The elaborate layers of fabric (canton flannels, merinos, astrakhans, paisleys) each one the costume of a different Peron.

Joachim gives all of it away. To the poor. To tramps. He scatters Peron's identity through the city the way the wind scattered the elegy in the previous chapter. He keeps only his own threadbare suit. He keeps being himself, in the suit that is too thin and too worn and too obviously the suit of a man who values his dignity more than his comfort, because the suit is what he has and the suit is what he is.

The dispersal is grief and it is liberation and it is the opposite of accumulation. Peron accumulated. He collected identities the way Joachim collected stamps, except that Peron wore his collection rather than filing it. When Joachim gives the wardrobe away, he is undoing the collection: sending the identities back into circulation, letting them become the clothing of whoever needs them next. The tramp in Peron's homburg is wearing a piece of Peron. The tramp does not know this. The homburg does not care. The identity disperses into the city like everything else.

The mad tailor

Who tailored him in this strange life?

Mr. Spitzer's question (who is the author or architect of his existence, who cut and stitched him into this particular shape) is the philosophical question of theodicy rendered as a sartorial complaint. If there is a God who designed the universe, why did this God design it as it is? If there is a principle of order underlying existence, why does the order keep failing? Who made Mr. Spitzer the pale replica of his brother, the man who arrives a minute late, the cautious chess player who cannot take the final action?

The mad tailor is the answer Young provides, not as theology but as image. The creator of this particular existence was mad. The stitching is wrong. The basting stitches (the temporary stitches meant to hold the garment together until the real work is done, the stitches that are supposed to be removed) have never been removed. Mr. Spitzer is being held together by temporary fastenings that were never meant to be permanent. He is, in this sense, perpetually provisional: a garment that was not finished, assembled from pieces that do not quite match, basted into the shape of a person and then left to wear the basting.

Puppet, dummy, cloud with unraveling seams

Young gives Mr. Spitzer three self-images in rapid succession: puppet, tailor's dummy, and cloud with seams unraveling.

The puppet has no agency; it moves when the strings move it. The tailor's dummy has no self; it exists only to display the garments placed upon it. The cloud with unraveling seams has no coherence; it is a shape that is actively losing the stitching that held it together, becoming formless as it moves. Three versions of a self that has been emptied of what makes a self: agency, interiority, coherence.

And yet Mr. Spitzer is not simply these things. He is also the person who arranged Peron's funeral with love. Who gave the wardrobe to the poor with intention. Who wanders the city finding the lost heirs and the forgotten legacies. Who keeps the great secret. Who carries the alpenhorn. Who composes the elegy in fragments and watches it scatter in the wind. The puppet moves when the strings move it and also writes music. The dummy displays the garments and also loves. The cloud is unraveling and is also still a cloud, still holding its shape against the dispersal.

The golden key to the door that is no more

The chapter's final image: Joachim carries “the golden key to the door that was no more.”

He has the key. The key is perfectly made: golden, precise, the right instrument for the right lock. The door it was made for no longer exists. The house that held the door was lost in the bankruptcy. The life organized around the door (the twin house, the music as tomb, the careful conservative investments) is gone. The key remains, opening nothing, carried in his pocket through every chapter of his wandering, in the silk hat and the threadbare suit, through the fog-bound city and the wrong carriage and the missed connection and the beachcomber's house.

Young's key that opens nothing is among her most exact images for Mr. Spitzer's condition. He is perfectly equipped for a life that no longer exists. He has the instrument for the situation he prepared for, and the situation has been dismantled. He carries the key anyway because the key is his. Because having it is the only form of relation to the lost house that remains available. Because you do not throw away a golden key simply because the door is gone. The door may come back. The door may appear somewhere else. He is still looking.

What to hold onto

Chapter 56 is the most direct account of Peron's actual funeral and its aftermath that the novel offers, and it is organized around the central paradox of Joachim's grief: he mourned his brother better than his brother ever mourned him. He gave Peron the perfect burial. He dispersed the perfect wardrobe. He kept the single suit and the golden key and the threadbare dignity and went on.

What to carry forward: the poker chips in the grave, the intimate act of honoring a person as they were rather than as convention required them to be. The wardrobe scattered through the city as Peron's identity dissolving into general circulation. The mad tailor who basted instead of sewing and left the temporary stitches in place. And the golden key, carried through every chapter, opening nothing, never thrown away.
