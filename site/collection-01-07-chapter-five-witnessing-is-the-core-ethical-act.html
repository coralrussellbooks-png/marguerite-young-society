Chapter Five: Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act · Across every book

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

The fourth pillar is the one that does the ethical work of the system.

The first three pillars describe conditions: the self is plural, reality is constructed, grief is permanent. They tell you what the world is. The fourth pillar tells you what to do about it, which is not what you might expect from a philosophical system that has spent three chapters dismantling the foundations of certainty.

What you do is witness.

Not act, not fix, not resolve, not transcend. Witness. Stay in the room with what is actually there. See it without flinching and without concluding. Maintain the full attention that the thing in front of you deserves, the attention that does not look away when the looking becomes painful and does not rush toward resolution when the contradiction becomes uncomfortable. The witness is the one who can hold the wig and the baldness at the same time, without requiring that one of them be more real than the other, without needing the scene to resolve into something more manageable than it actually is.

This sounds passive. It is not. It is the most demanding ethical stance Young's system makes available, and it is demanding precisely because it refuses the satisfactions that make ethical action feel complete. The hero acts and the problem is solved and the story ends. The witness stays and the problem remains and the story keeps going and the witness keeps staying. There is no arrival. There is only the quality of attention brought to the continuing.

Young's novel is itself an act of witnessing. The narrator does not judge Catherine. She does not diagnose her, does not position her opium world as inferior to the world outside it, does not offer the reader the comfort of a clinical or moral frame that would explain Catherine and thereby contain her. She witnesses Catherine. She gives Catherine's world the full weight of her attention and renders it with the same density and care she gives everything else, and she does not conclude.

The length of the novel is not excess. It is the time required to witness properly. You cannot witness in summary. You cannot witness at a distance. Witnessing requires duration and proximity and the willingness to keep looking after the looking has become difficult, which it becomes almost immediately in a novel this committed to not looking away.

Vera is also a witness, though an imperfect one in the way that all actual witnesses are imperfect. She witnesses her mother. She witnesses Miss MacIntosh. She witnesses the community of characters the novel accumulates around its central journey, each one rendered with the same insistence on their full interior reality. The characters who fail in Young's novel are not the ones who suffer. Suffering is not failure in Young. The characters who fail are the ones who stop witnessing, who replace genuine attention with a frame that makes the thing in front of them manageable at the cost of its actual complexity. Miss MacIntosh herself is the clearest example. She witnessed the solid world with tremendous attention and refused to witness anything else. Her pragmatism was a witnessing of one reality and a deliberate not-witnessing of everything that reality couldn't contain. The wig was the boundary of her attention.

Young's fourth pillar is in deep, unacknowledged conversation with Simone Weil's ethics of attention. Weil argued that the foundation of ethics is not duty or utility or virtue but attention: the capacity to truly see the person in front of you, to give them the full weight of your regard without the distortions of projection or self-interest or the desire to make them into something more manageable than they actually are. For Weil, attention is an act of love, and love is not a feeling but a practice, the practice of keeping your eyes open when everything in you wants to look away or simplify or conclude.

Young arrives at the same place through fiction rather than philosophy, which means she arrives there with different tools and different evidence but the same essential claim. The 1,198 pages of Miss MacIntosh, My Darling by Marguerite Young are 1,198 pages of practiced attention, of keeping the eyes open, of refusing the simplifications that would make the novel shorter and the ethics easier.

The difference between them is instructive. Weil's ethics of attention operate between persons: you attend to the other, the one in front of you, the one whose suffering makes a claim on your regard. Young's witnessing operates at a different scale. It is not only interpersonal but intrapersonal: you witness yourself, your own plurality, your own grief, your own constructed reality, with the same attention you owe the other. Young's witnessing is Weil's attention made systemic. It is attention not as an individual ethical act but as a philosophical stance, a way of being in the world that shapes everything the self does and makes and builds.

A political culture organized around the witness would look radically different from what we have. What we have is organized around action: the decisive leader, the transformative policy, the revolution that changes everything, the election that finally gets it right. The political imagination is saturated with the fantasy of arrival. Young's system says: the story does not end. The problem is not the kind of thing that gets solved. The question is the quality of attention brought to the continuing. This does not mean political action is pointless. It means political action that is not grounded in genuine witnessing is dangerous. Every authoritarian movement in history has depended on the refusal of witnessing: the reduction of the complex plural self to a simple sortable category, the replacement of genuine attention with ideology, the decision that what cannot be processed by the frame does not need to be seen.

That is the fourth pillar. Stay in the room. Keep looking. Do not conclude.
