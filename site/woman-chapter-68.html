Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 68

← Chapter 67 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 69 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Weed · Vera Cartwheel

Vera reaches the village at night, and real and dream as one country and the dissolving boundary between life and death organize what she finds. A place known only by daylight, the text says, is never known as it is, and this village of dilapidated buildings and "vacancy" and iridescent winter weeds is the ordinary town opening onto a dreamed one, the seam gone between the seen and the dreamed. The Tavern's "God's servants who were believers in the lamb," the sleeping man in a miner's hat "with no light in his bulb," the graveyard where the coal mines should be, hold the boundary open before anyone speaks, a village where the living and the dead keep the same address.

The Christian hangman, Mr. Weed, talks of tying knots that "none shall untie until Gabriel blows his horn," and the hangman's noose and the marital knots run together in his mouth so that marriage becomes a binding as final as an execution, the wedding and the funeral fused the way the book has fused them since Chapter 6. He calls death itself a "marriage ceremony" with life, and the metaphor carries marriage as social death and the boundary between living and dead giving way in a single figure, the rope and the ring the same knot.

His first wife, Marie, hanged herself while pregnant with a child she did not want, and his children "playing at hanging themselves" carry the cycle of female despair and reproductive dread down a generation, the interrupted and unwanted birth ending at the rope. His second wife wears the first wife's clothes, and the two women folded into one set of garments are the crossing and the doubling the book keeps performing, one woman worn over another, the dead wife present in the living one's dress, held at the threshold rather than let go.

The hangman's talk of "undiscovered crimes," of murderers who move like "sleep-walkers," of the "bedroom murders" that leave no clear author, is the diffusion of agency the book stated at the start, a death with no single hand behind it, the same logic that would not say who killed Miss MacIntosh. The woman who killed a man "for pleasure" and was spared the rope for her beauty, the man who murdered his wife over her cats and unraveled her identity by misspelling her letters, the black poker chip left at an unsolved killing, are the violence against women and the erasure of their voices the book keeps exposing, hidden in bedrooms and unaccounted for on the record. The hangman and the landlord climb the stairs, and Vera is left "rocking" below in a house with no clock to strike, suspended time closing the chapter on a woman waiting at the threshold while the men go up to their business.
