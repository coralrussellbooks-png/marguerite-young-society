To All My Darlings

Chapter 28

On Chapter 28 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 27 · Home · Chapter 29 →

Summary

Mr. Spitzer admits that he may be only another of Catherine’s hallucinations, and that he truly lives only during these evenings in her room. Peron, who loved “the wastrel wheels of chance,” had no patience “for the caterpillar to become the butterfly.” Mr. Spitzer recalls the butterfly collection he abandoned after Peron’s funeral, his correspondence with fellow collectors, and the births and deaths of butterflies he watched. If one were called away at the crucial moment, he observes, one could never tell whether one had seen a beginning or an end.

Key passages

From the research

  • The opening passage describes Mr. Spitzer’s nightly visits as a kind of faithfulness, drawing “him each evening here as to a brilliant star,” despite his life feeling like a “dark and empty plain” except for these “delusive evenings.” This passage highlights the blurred boundary between his corporeal reality and the narcotic, hallucinatory world of Catherine. He is portrayed as a “living ghost” caught between life and death, reality and fantasy, which underscores the chapter’s exploration of existence, memory, and identity. The imagery of “the vastness of this trembling flesh enclosing his feeble spirit like a flame” poignantly captures the fragility of his being.
  • There is a significant reflection on Mr. Spitzer’s dead twin brother, who had a very different approach to life. While Mr. Spitzer was thinking “only of the impossible future,” his brother “had loved the cruelty of actual life” and seemed to live only in the present moment. The brother’s death profoundly affected Mr. Spitzer, dividing his soul almost to lunacy. This contrast illuminates themes of mortality, memory, and the divergent ways individuals confront existence and loss. It also sets up a tension between rationality and passion, foresight and immediacy.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s passion for collecting butterflies is portrayed not merely as a hobby but as a meditative and symbolic pursuit. The butterflies are described with poetic richness. This imagery conveys a mystical quality, indicating that these ephemeral creatures represent moments of beauty, transformation, and perhaps spiritual truth. His care for butterflies is contrasted with Catherine’s visions of “imaginary sea bishops,” “mermaids,” and other fantastical figures, suggesting a shared yet distinct engagement with the invisible or transcendent world.
  • The chapter notes that after Peron’s “devastating funeral,” many aspects of Mr. Spitzer’s life ended, including his butterfly collecting and his “own capacious heart.” Peron’s pragmatic, earthly interests are contrasted with Mr. Spitzer’s more spiritual and impersonal passions. This marks a turning point, emphasizing loss not only of a person but of a way of life and a kind of inner vitality. Peron’s skepticism about the afterlife and the significance of spiritual phenomena positions him as a foil to Mr. Spitzer’s contemplative nature.
  • The closing passage reflects on the indistinguishability of birth and death moments: “if one were suddenly called away in the midst of these proceedings, one should never know whether it was death or birth, beginning or end.” This philosophical observation encapsulates a central motif of the chapter, the fluid boundary between beginnings and endings, life and death, seen through the metaphor of the butterfly’s emergence and expiration. It also explains the impetus for Mr. Spitzer’s studies: the search for meaning in “impalpable butterflies,” or elusive spiritual truths.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “He might well have been… among my mother’s irresponsible hallucinations.” Why does the thought not frighten him?
  • Why does Mr. Spitzer find “his only sense of his unbroken continuity” in a room where “no chair had changed its place for years”?
  • “One should never know whether it was death or birth.” What does the butterfly teach him?

Characters

  • Peron had no patience “to wait for the caterpillar to become the butterfly.” What did he love instead?
  • Mr. Spitzer gave up his butterflies after Peron’s death. Why give up the thing that consoled him?
  • Catherine sees “all these beauties which he could no more see than if he were a mole.” What does each of them see that the other cannot?

Connections

  • The butterfly on the flower was “as identical to the flower as twin to twin.” How does that echo the twins?
  • In Chapter 24, Catherine imagined “a unicorn wearing a necklace.” Here the unicorn is “enclosed by a picket fence.” What has changed?
  • The “painted ladies fluttering through the darkness” are butterflies and street women at once. Where else does Young let one word carry two worlds?

Reflection

  • Is a beginning ever distinguishable from an end?
  • What do we collect, and why?
  • Does the dead person return “except in our thoughts”?

Research: Characters

Joachim Spitzer (Peron’s twin brother)

  • The central figure of this chapter, haunted by his brother’s death and Catherine’s narcotic-induced visions.
  • A collector and contemplator of butterflies, symbolizing his search for meaning and beauty.
  • A man of solitude, haunted by existential questions about life and identity.
  • He may be, he admits, “among my mother’s irresponsible hallucinations,” and only in her room is he “still renewed.”
  • He gave up his butterflies “two or three years after his brother’s irrational death,” and much else ended “on the day of Peron’s devastating funeral.”
  • He finds “his only sense of his unbroken continuity in life” in her room, “perhaps because no chair had changed its place for years.”

Peron Spitzer (Joachim’s twin brother)

  • A loudmouthed, pragmatic man who embraced the cruelty of actual life and seemed indifferent to spiritual or afterlife concepts.
  • His untimely death deeply affects Mr. Spitzer’s psyche and way of life.
  • He loved “the wastrel wheels of chance, the games of loaded dice, the croupiers, the billiard hall artists… the drugged horses.”
  • He “would most certainly have seen no value, no unearthly significance as to butterflies or any other spirits.”

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • A figure whose narcotic dreams create the enchanted, surreal world Mr. Spitzer visits each evening.
  • Her “obtuse faith that he would always return” and her hallucinations sustain Mr. Spitzer’s sense of renewal and continuity despite his personal losses.
  • She sees “all these beauties which he could no more see than if he were a mole under the ground,” even when her eyes are closed.

Research: Themes

The Intersection of Life and Death

See: Death and Mortality

  • Mr. Spitzer exists in a liminal space, described as “this living ghost,” caught between corporeal reality and spiritual or hallucinatory realms.
  • The chapter reflects on how death and memory interact, emphasizing that the dead do not return except in the thoughts of the living.
  • The novel suggests a tenuous grasp on identity after death or loss, especially in relation to Mr. Spitzer’s dead twin brother, whose absence profoundly impacts him.

Memory and Identity

See: Memory and the Past · Identity and the Self

  • Mr. Spitzer’s struggle to understand “the actual man he was” highlights the elusive nature of self-knowledge and the effect of loss on personal identity.
  • Memory serves as a bridge to the past and as a means to preserve continuity, especially through his recollections of his brother.
  • The chapter suggests that memory may be deceptive or limited, with “things not possible” entering the mind through “the deceived memory of the unknown.”

The Role of the Narcotic and Hallucinatory World

See: Dreams and Imagination · Opium

  • Mr. Spitzer is drawn each evening to a place shaped by Catherine’s narcotic dreams, a surreal, enchanted world filled with fantastical figures (e.g., Russian bishops, lighthouse keepers, moon-faced duchesses) and imagery (snow owls, serpents, music boxes).
  • This dreamlike space is where, though “still denied and turned away,” he is “still renewed,” suggesting a complex relationship between reality and illusion.

Butterflies as a Symbol

See: Butterflies and Moths

  • Butterflies serve as a powerful metaphor for fleeting beauty, transformation, and the ephemeral nature of life and memory.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s passion for collecting butterflies reflects his attempt to capture and preserve moments of beauty and meaning amid loneliness and loss.
  • The novel details the vividness and variety of butterflies he treasured, symbolizing spiritual messengers or incarnations of beauty in various realms.
  • His butterfly collection and memories also contrast with the pragmatic and mundane approach of his brother Peron, who dismissed such ethereal concerns.

Contrast Between Mr. Spitzer and His Brother Peron

See: Twins and Doubles · Peron Spitzer

  • Peron is portrayed as a man focused on the immediate, material world, indifferent to spiritual or afterlife concepts, and unconcerned with the delicate beauty Mr. Spitzer values.
  • Peron’s death, “by accident or suicide,” marks a turning point for Mr. Spitzer, leading to the end of many of his pursuits and a deepening of his introspection.

Philosophical Reflections on Birth, Death, and Time

See: Death and Mortality · Time · Motherhood, Pregnancy, and Birth · The Search for Meaning

  • The chapter explores the ambiguity between birth and death, suggesting moments in life where beginning and end are indistinguishable.
  • Mr. Spitzer muses on how one could never be sure if a certain moment signifies death or birth, reflecting on the cyclical and interconnected nature of existence.
  • This ambiguity first set him on his study of the “impalpable butterflies,” symbolizing ineffable and transient moments in life.

Research: Resources

  • “perhaps the Antarctic Queen Maud Mountains”: The Queen Maud Mountains are a major group of mountains in the Antarctic. Roald Amundsen named them in 1911 for Queen Maud of Norway, on his way to the South Pole. See Exploration and the Poles.
  • “perhaps Queen Charlotte Islands in a storm”: The Queen Charlotte Islands, now called Haida Gwaii, are an archipelago off the coast of British Columbia in Canada. Captain George Dixon named them in 1787 after his ship, the Queen Charlotte, which was named for George III’s queen.
  • “perhaps the headless horseman fording a brook”: Washington Irving’s headless horseman. See the Chapter 26 resources.
  • “that spindle of creative flame which is said to sleep in the eternal darkness, weaving our bodies”: Weaving plays a significant role in the mythologies of various cultures, often symbolizing creation, destiny, and even the weaving of life itself. In many stories, weaving is attributed to deities or individuals who control fate or have supernatural abilities. For example, the Greek Moirae (Fates) spin, measure, and cut the thread of human lives, and the Norse goddess Frigg is associated with weaving and seiðr magic, which involves weaving destiny. Greek mythology: Athena, Arachne, Moirae (Fates), Penelope, Philomela. Norse mythology: Frigg, The Norns. Other mythologies: Neith, Laima, Päivätär and Kuutar, Spider Woman, Arianrhod. In Plato’s Myth of Er (Republic 10), the Spindle of Necessity turns the spheres on the knees of Necessity, and her daughters, the Fates, spin each life. Weaving has symbolic meanings: creation, fate, communication, transformation, supernatural abilities. Weaving carries deep symbolic significance related to creation, life, consciousness, and fate in the chapter. It explores metaphors of weaving as a cosmic and existential process, intertwining themes of life and death, illusion and reality, and the complex interrelations of souls and identities. Weaving is portrayed as a divine or cosmic act, with references to a “spindle of creative flame” that sleeps in eternal darkness, weaving bodies, souls, and dreams. This suggests that life and identity are constructed like a tapestry or garment, woven from countless threads representing experiences, memories, and spiritual elements. The chapter implies that individual identity is not singular but composed of many intertwined selves, memories, and possibilities. The act of weaving also relates to the tension between illusion and reality. This weaving of illusions is necessary but also a source of existential uncertainty. The chapter suggests that life and death, beginnings and endings, are not linear but cyclical and intertwined like threads in a weave. Death is not simply an end but part of the ongoing process of creation and dissolution, much like the continuous interlacing of threads in fabric. The weaving is often hidden, complex, and beyond full human comprehension, alluding to the mysterious forces (possibly divine) that govern existence and the unknowability of ultimate truths. It is a rich, multifaceted symbol that encapsulates the novel’s exploration of existence as a complex, interwoven pattern of life, death, illusion, and identity, where boundaries between these concepts blur and human understanding is limited. This symbolism aligns with many other images in the novel related to threads, webs, and patterns, emphasizing the intricate, often elusive nature of reality and self. See Plato and Platonism, Fate and Chance, and Greek Mythology.
  • “deserts suddenly flowering”: “The desert shall rejoice, and blossom as the rose” (Isaiah 35:1). See The Bible.
  • “no patience to wait for the caterpillar to become the butterfly”: The Greek psychē means both “soul” and “butterfly.” In Apuleius’s The Golden Ass (2nd century AD), Psyche is the soul who marries Cupid. See Greek Mythology and Butterflies and Moths.
  • “as of the great shell of Venus”: Botticelli’s The Birth of Venus (c. 1485), the goddess rising from the sea on a scallop shell. See Greek Mythology.
  • “a booming sound like that of the loud sea bittern”: The bittern, a marsh heron, is known for its deep, booming call. See Birds.
  • “these candle flames reflected in dark Venetian mirrors”: The mirror-makers of Murano, near Venice, held the secret of fine glass mirrors from the sixteenth century. See Mirrors and Reflections.
  • “the Hesperides like spirit messengers”: Hesperides in Greek mythology are nymphs of the evening. They guarded the golden apples at the western edge of the world. The skippers, a family of butterflies, are the Hesperiidae, named for them. See Butterflies and Moths.
  • “brimstone-colored butterflies”: The brimstone (Gonepteryx rhamni), a sulphur-yellow butterfly. Its butter color is one guess at the origin of the word “butterfly.”
  • “bejeweled butterflies as precious to him as the most precious jewels or mosaic emperors”: The emperors are butterflies too, among them the Purple Emperor (Apatura iris).
  • “more precious than the cuckoo singing as it flies”: Wordsworth’s “To the Cuckoo” (1807): “No bird, but an invisible thing, / A voice, a mystery.” See Romantic Poets and Birds.
  • “the griffins flying over the roof-tops”: The griffin, with an eagle’s head and wings and a lion’s body, guarded gold in Greek legend (Herodotus, Histories 3.116). See Greek Mythology.
  • “the imaginary sea bishops with mitre-shaped heads and vestments of silver scales”: The Sea Bishop, a fish-man in a bishop’s mitre, was said to have been caught off Poland in 1531. It is pictured in Conrad Gesner’s Historia animalium (1558). See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “walking in the cool of the evening shade”: “They heard the voice of the Lord God walking in the garden in the cool of the day” (Genesis 3:8). See Genesis.
  • “the mermaids feeding their infants at their breasts”: Mermaids nursing their young. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “the milk-white unicorn enclosed by a picket fence”: The Unicorn in Captivity, the last of the Unicorn Tapestries (c. 1495-1505), shows the unicorn inside a round wooden fence. It hangs at The Cloisters in New York. See the Chapter 24 resources.
  • “as she had cared for things invisible made visible to her”: “For the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen” (Romans 1:20). See The Bible.
  • “the wastrel wheels of chance, the games of loaded dice, the croupiers”: Roulette (“little wheel”), whose croupier spins it. See Gambling and Playing Cards, Fate and Chance, and Peron Spitzer.
  • “the harlequins, the painted ladies fluttering through the darkness”: Harlequin was the patched, masked trickster of the commedia dell’arte. The Painted Lady (Vanessa cardui) is a butterfly, and “painted lady” was also slang for a woman of the streets. Young lets both meanings fly at once. See Butterflies and Moths.
  • “a large correspondence with other meditative butterfly hunters”: The great collectors corresponded and traded specimens. Vladimir Nabokov, novelist and lepidopterist, worked on butterflies at Harvard’s Museum of Comparative Zoology (1942-48).
  • “the phenomenal world of the butterfly in all its illusive manifestations”: For Kant, the phenomenal world is the world as it appears to us, as against the thing in itself (Critique of Pure Reason, 1781). See Kant and Hegel and Illusion and Reality.
  • “the butterfly perching upon the flower had seemed as identical to the flower as twin to twin”: Zhuangzi (c. 300 BC) dreamed he was a butterfly, and woke to write: “Now I do not know whether I was then a man dreaming I was a butterfly, or whether I am now a butterfly, dreaming I am a man” (trans. Herbert Giles, 1889). See Taoism and Zhuangzi and Twins and Doubles.
  • “watching the amazed birth of a butterfly, the first tremblings of the feeble, dew-lit wings”: The emergence of the butterfly from the chrysalis is called eclosion. See Butterflies and Moths.
