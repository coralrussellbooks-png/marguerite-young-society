The Philosopher of the Will

Arthur Schopenhauer's central claim is that the world we perceive, the world of objects and individuals and events, is a representation: a surface, an appearance, the veil of Maya. Behind it and beneath it and constituting it is the Will, a single blind irrational force that drives all existence without purpose or destination. The Will is not conscious. It does not desire any particular thing. It simply strives, endlessly, and every act of striving produces suffering because striving is by definition the experience of a lack that cannot be permanently filled. The individual self is the principle of individuation: the illusion that you are a separate entity with a separate fate. You are not. You are a temporary form through which the Will expresses itself before dissolving back into the underlying unity from which you were never really separate.

The ethical consequence is compassion. If the other's suffering is the same as your own because the underlying will is one, then the recognition of another person's pain as your pain is not sentiment but metaphysical accuracy. The artist's consequence is music: Schopenhauer placed music at the apex of the arts because unlike painting or poetry, which represent ideas or copies of the world, music bypasses representation entirely and speaks directly from the Will itself. It is not about the world. It is the world's innermost nature made audible.

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is not a philosophical novel in the sense of dramatizing arguments. But Arthur Schopenhauer's philosophy is woven into the fabric of Mr. Spitzer so completely that it functions less as a reference and more as a diagnosis: the novel is describing what it actually looks like to live inside Schopenhauer's world, inside the Will and its suffering, inside the partial penetration of the veil, inside the impossible position of a man who has glimpsed the unity beneath individuation and cannot unsee it.

The Will-to-Live Kills Itself

Schopenhauer's most counterintuitive claim about suicide is that it is not the denial of the will-to-live but its most extreme expression. The person who kills themselves does so because the will-to-live has found its current circumstances intolerable. They are not negating the will; they are acting from it, trying to escape its suffering by destroying its vessel while leaving the underlying drive intact. Suicide affirms the will because it is the will refusing to accept its conditions. It is not liberation. It is the will in its most desperate form.

The novel dramatizes this argument through Peron. Mr. Spitzer works through the logic: "For what was fear, he had often asked himself, but the will to immortality, the aversion to death, either as the idea of an overwhelming fate or the extinction of the individual, and might not a man kill himself simply because of his overwhelming fear of death, his will to live, to live forever, and might he not seek that death which he had feared and embrace her as his love in an everlasting love... Suicide might be, far from the negative impulse it seemed, the most creative act, even like sexual love begetting another person, for suicide might be the desire to live, to live beyond the grave, beyond the limits of this life, to seek that consuming love which he had feared, so Peron had died, had died early."

This is not Mr. Spitzer rationalizing his grief. It is the novel engaging Schopenhauer's argument directly and extending it: what if suicide is not just an expression of the will but its most creative expression, the will's attempt to be born again into a form where it can finally get what it wants? Peron leaps from the tallest building in a cloud as a star flashed. He does not deny the will. He is the will at its most extravagant, most fearful, most reaching. Joachim stays. He is the will denying itself: the hesitant suicide who takes his luxurious time, the musician who writes only silent music, the man who lives on only to prolong the agony or in memory of his dead brother. The twins are not opposites in temperament. They are the will's two modes: the explosive affirmation that destroys its vessel, and the slow negation that outlives everything it loved.

Music as the Will Made Audible

Schopenhauer argued that music does not represent the Will's objects, its desires and their satisfactions. Music directly embodies the Will: the rising melody is striving, the resolution is momentary satisfaction, the dissonance is unsatisfied longing, the minor key is grief without consolation. Hearing music, we hear the Will itself, stripped of its representations, speaking in its own language. This is why music can move us to emotions we cannot name: we are hearing something prior to emotion, the drive from which all emotion comes.

The novel names Mr. Spitzer's music explicitly in Schopenhauerian terms: the music of the spheres, filling all space with its sound as of the human will, music his dead brother heard but which Mr. Spitzer could not hear, could only imagine in this silence. The music as of the human will: Young places the attribution directly. The music that Mr. Spitzer writes but cannot perform, the silent music that no living ear can hear, is music in Schopenhauer's sense: it is the will's direct expression, which is why it cannot be played on any instrument. Instruments play music about the will. Mr. Spitzer's music is the will itself, which has no instrument adequate to it.

The division between the brothers is the division between the will heard directly (Peron) and the will imagined in silence (Joachim). Peron heard the music of the will as of course he did, he who was the will's most extravagant expression, who lit fires wherever he went so he might follow himself. Joachim can only imagine it. He lives in the silence where the music should be, writing it down in notation that no orchestra can play, listening for it in the space between sounds, in the dying tide, in the lost musician with the withered string and the unuttered word. He is the man positioned one step outside the Will's direct language, the man for whom the world's innermost nature is permanently just beyond hearing.

Compassion Without Limit, Compassion With Limit

Schopenhauer's ethics rests on compassion as the recognition that the other's suffering is the same as your own, because beneath the illusion of separate selfhood, the will that suffers in them is the same will that suffers in you. The person who has partially seen through the principle of individuation, who has glimpsed the unity beneath individuation, feels the other's pain directly, without mediation, not because they are sentimental but because they are accurate.

Mr. Spitzer is described as sheltering by his compassion all the poor creatures incomplete and helpless and unknown: the derelicts, the hobo camp inhabitants, the Lady Macbeth actress drifting through the camps, the banker who does not know he is a banker, the cancer specialist who has crawled away to hide among the faceless ones. His compassion is not charity. It is the recognition that they are him, that he is them, that the differences of identity and history and social standing are the veil and the suffering beneath is one. He pays his dead brother's debts. He attends the opium lady for years without requiring anything from her. He cannot stop. Compassion of this kind is not chosen; it is the consequence of having seen, however briefly and incompletely, that the boundary between self and other is an illusion.

The novel also shows compassion with its limit, which is the point at which the veil reasserts itself. Mr. Bonebreaker has accepted Miss MacIntosh with her barren breast, her barren heart. The novel notes his compassion that had accepted her with these absences. But when she reveals her bald head he runs, slamming the door, causing the entire building to shake. His compassion had a bottom, an edge, a point at which the particular body before him reasserted its particular strangeness and he could no longer hold the unity. This is Schopenhauer's description of partial ethical development: the compassion that extends some distance through the veil and then, at some threshold of the strange or the unexpected, snaps back into the principle of individuation. He had counted on that compassion which had already accepted her barren breast, her barren heart. The acceptance was real. The limit was also real. He ran.

Individuation as Illusion: The Characters Who Blur

Schopenhauer argued that the sense of being a separate individual, distinct from all other individuals, is the fundamental illusion. It is not that you are wrong about most things and right about your own existence. The principle of individuation is itself the veil: you are not a separate entity with a separate will. You are the Will temporarily expressing itself through a particular form that will dissolve. Death is not the end of the Will; it is the end of one of the Will's temporary individuations. The Will continues in everything else that was ever alive and ever will be.

The novel's characters consistently blur into each other in exactly the pattern Schopenhauer describes. Mr. Spitzer wonders whether he and his brother were one and the same, though recognizing that this might be his own extinction. The heart specialist, testing in a midnight street, fears he might find the beatings of two hearts inside one body. The characters Jock, the Black Coachman, Joachim, and Peron may all be the same person: different individuations of the same underlying figure, the will expressing itself through multiple temporary forms that happen to coincide in one man's history.

Mr. Spitzer often wonders about his identity being confused with his dead brother's and this is applied to a man who was too literalist to be confused about his own identity, cuts through the literal reading. Mr. Spitzer is literally not confused. But the novel is telling us something the literal man cannot tell himself: he has become his brother because he was always his brother, because the principle of individuation that separated them was always an illusion, because the two hearts the physician would have found beating inside him are the same heart heard from two sides of the veil.

All who had taken their last shot were all the same. The billiard hall characters, the gamblers, the men who have reached the end of their particular individuation: at the level of the Will they are all the same, all expressions of the same striving that differs only in its temporary form. There was a place where all worlds, the highest and the lowest and the middle, should certainly be one, and no one should certainly be able to tell them apart. This is the place behind the veil of Maya. Mr. Spitzer has been glimpsing it all his life and cannot quite arrive there and cannot quite leave it behind.

The Opium Lady's Failed Renunciation

Schopenhauer admired the saints and ascetics who achieved genuine denial of the will: who withdrew from desire, who renounced the pleasures the will craves, who reached a state analogous to Buddhist nirvana through the systematic negation of the will's demands. This is the path he thought most worthy, not because it produces happiness but because it is the only honest response to a world that is constitutively suffering.

The opium lady looks like a Schopenhauerian denial of the will. She has withdrawn from the world. She lies in her bed behind drawn curtains. She has refused ordinary life, marriage, social engagement, and the demands of the present. She has gone by all other roads rather than the direct renunciation of life, preferring never the immediate moment of realization but an unearthly romance which should suffer no deterioration or change. She has renounced the world of ordinary time and its losses.

But the novel then shows that this is not the denial of the will. It is the will wearing the costume of renunciation. "Surely, my mother had evaded, wherever possible, the direct renunciation of life—going by all other roads—and had preferred never the immediate moment of realization but an unearthly romance which should suffer no deterioration or change brought by harsh experience inasmuch as it was not subjected to these mortal laws of change and chance and these accidents like the great snow avalanches falling over the leaden roofs with the sound of church bells tolling for the lost crystal-hunter, and surely she had loved that love which never was, yet was more powerful than any love which should be realized in this mortal sphere, the heavenly bridegroom none should see, the lover never to be discovered on this clouded earth—never, unless he should come in death’s guise, carrying her away." The love for the dead husband is the will-to-live at maximum intensity directed toward an object that cannot provide resistance. The will needs resistance; it strives against something. Her ghost-husband provides no resistance, no deterioration, no change, no disappointment. She can want him forever without the wanting ever being satisfied or frustrated in the ordinary sense.

She had evaded, wherever possible, the direct renunciation of life. The key word is evaded. Schopenhauer's renunciation is direct: you confront the will and say no to it. The opium lady's apparent renunciation is indirect: she says no to the world by saying yes, endlessly and without limit, to something the world cannot provide. This is not the saint's path. This is the will finding the one strategy that allows it to strive infinitely without exhausting itself. The drugs sustain the dreaming that sustains the love that sustains the will that keeps her breathing for the decades of the novel.

Two Watches: One for the Living, One for the Dead

Mr. Spitzer wears "two wrist watches, one which he wore on his right wrist, one which he wore on his left wrist which had no perceptible pulse beats, less than the quiverings of a dead moth or sparrow, his dead side, one which kept no time and was for the living so that he would miss his appointments, one which kept time and was for the dead so that he would not miss his appointments."

This is the AND Philosophy's grief as permanent weather pillar in its most Schopenhauerian form: the watch that keeps accurate time is the watch for the dead, the watch for Peron, the watch that was Peron's. The will-to-live expressed itself through Peron in time: he kept appointments, he raced, he gambled, he left fires so he could follow himself. The watch keeps time for him still, ticking on Mr. Spitzer's dead wrist, keeping the time of the will's most vital expression on the arm of the will's most subdued one.

The watch for the living keeps no time and is therefore useless for living. Mr. Spitzer misses his appointments. He walks the streets as if on thinnest ice, not knowing what hour it is, arriving too late or too early or at the wrong address. The world of the living, the world of the principle of individuation with its schedules and appointments and social engagements, is the world he has been losing his grip on since Peron died. The world of the dead, the world beneath the veil, the world where the will is one and all hearts beat together: that world he keeps perfect time for.

The AND Philosophy Reading

Schopenhauer's philosophy ends in the denial of the will: the sage who negates the will's demands, achieves equanimity, sees through the veil. The AND Philosophy does not end in denial of the will. It ends in the continued movement of the one who remains, still pulled by grief, still extending compassion, still wearing the dead brother's watch on the dead wrist, still hearing the music as of the human will in the silence where no instrument can play it.

The difference is this: Schopenhauer treats the denial of the will as an achievement, a state one can reach by sufficient philosophical clarity and sufficient ascetic practice. Young's novel treats it as a permanent condition of incompletion. Mr. Spitzer has seen through the principle of individuation far enough to know that the boundary between himself and his brother is illusory, far enough to know that the derelicts in the hobo camps are him, far enough to feel their suffering as his own and to extend his compassion without limit. But he has not reached the saint's total negation. He still wants Peron back. He still asks whether eternal recurrence might be the one law of life. He still wears both watches.

The AND Philosophy's plural self pillar is what Young takes from Schopenhauer: the self is not one, the boundary between selves is an illusion the will maintains for its own purposes, and the grief that crosses the boundary between the living and the dead is not pathology but accuracy. The grief as permanent weather pillar is what Young adds: the partial penetration of the veil is not a stage on the way to full enlightenment. It is the permanent condition. You see through the veil enough to suffer for everyone. You do not see through it enough to stop.

"And he was covered by wild goose barnacles as are those who have dwelled long in the waters, the waters of a dream. And indeed, though Mr. Spitzer had not consulted a physician now for so many years, being his own physician, he believed, and diagnosing his own ills and taking his own medicine, a mixture of several powders, elixirs of secret life, yet a great physician had consulted him in a dark midnight street, under a dim lamp suddenly gleaming like an already extinguished star, had introduced himself and had shown Mr. Spitzer his calling card and several charts of the heart, the upbeat and the downbeat and the movements as of waves, the long surges and the quick steps, and Mr. Spitzer had at first thought he was a musician, but he was this great heart specialist, and he had told Mr. Spitzer that he made his living testing the hearts of minotaurs and sea lions and whales, of all those who had been submerged, and he had asked if he might make a test of Mr. Spitzer’s heart, for he could see that he was only surfacing, coming to the surface of life, that he had been a long time submerged, longer years than anybody knew. And he was only coming up for air. But Mr. Spitzer had naturally refused, for what good would it have done to have tested his heart, and what could it possibly have proved, no matter what the physician had heard, whether he had heard the silence or the murmur? Perhaps he would have found that Mr. Spitzer’s was the beating heart, and perhaps he would have found that his was the resting heart, the eternally resting heart, that which beat no more, or perhaps, and this was what Mr. Spitzer had so greatly feared, he would have heard the beatings of two hearts."
