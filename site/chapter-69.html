To All My Darlings

Chapter 69

On Chapter 69 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 10, 2026

← Chapter 68 · Home · Chapter 70 →

Summary

Vera’s room is marked Fire Escape: a corn-husk mattress, a “Forget me not” picture of a church steeple, and a slop jar beside someone else’s blonde hair combings. On the stairs the tattooed landlord, once a magician, a ventriloquist, a sailor, a soldier in the Intelligence, and a hunter of wild animals for Hagenbeck’s, now married to the former circus fat lady who owns the hotel, tells her that the hangman’s talk came from his shadow and that Mr. Weed is in truth a prosperous dairy farmer paid for his work. Her neighbors include Tillie, a very young girl who is also an old woman keeping turkey buzzards, or canaries, in chains, and a legless newspaper man who rides a sled with rusted sleigh bells. All night, through the thin wall, old Dr. Justice O’Leary rages at Sarah, a silent woman who may be his little sister, dead at the age of one, or the love who ran off with a travelling salesman. He lists every disease he knows and speaks of his strangest case, a woman always pregnant with an imaginary baby. At last Vera screams at Sarah to go.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a vivid description of the protagonist’s room marked “Fire Escape,” featuring a narrow bed stuffed with corn husks, a picture of a silver church steeple with the words “Forget me not,” and unsettling details such as a slop jar with sediment and blonde hair combings. This sets a bleak, eerie atmosphere and introduces motifs of decay, memory, and forgotten lives. The reflection of only the eyes in the warped mirror symbolizes fragmented identity or perception. The creaking rocking chair suggests unseen presences, hinting at the spectral or psychological elements in the narrative.
  • The landlord is depicted as a colorful, loquacious man with tattoos, a rich past, and contradictory traits: strong yet faded in color, worldly yet tied to this dilapidated hotel. His monologues reveal a layered personality and provide backstory on Mr. Weed and other characters like Moses and “Tillie,” a young girl who knits baby hoods. The landlord’s tales weave together local myths, histories, and the odd coexistence of the living and dead in the hotel. His perspective contrasts with Mr. Weed’s silence and introduces themes of memory, reputation, and the passage of time.
  • The landlord’s comments about Mr. Weed and his “shadow” who speaks for him reveal a symbolic duality. Mr. Weed himself is cautious and reserved, while his shadow is outspoken and more intriguing. This duality might represent the tension between public persona and hidden truths, or between the living and the spectral. Mr. Weed’s reputed success in his enterprises and his role in the local community add complexity to his character.
  • The most emotionally intense passages are the doctor’s anguished soliloquies and interactions with “Sarah,” a ghostly, haunting figure who disrupts his life. The doctor mourns the loss and disgrace Sarah represents, reflecting on death, memory, and human frailty. His repeated curses (“God damn it, Sarah!”) and the description of Sarah as a “baby” and “senile” with cold breath and sores evoke themes of decay, madness, and the inescapability of death. The doctor’s anguish over his professional failures and the burden of his memories gives a tragic depth to his character.
  • Across these passages, there is a pervasive meditation on death, both literal and symbolic. The doctor’s reflections on Sarah’s death, Mr. Weed’s shadow, and the hotel’s other inhabitants (like the chained turkey buzzards and the silent old people) create a liminal space where life and death intermingle. The chapter explores how memory can be both a blessing and a curse, how the past haunts the present, and how human dignity is fragile in the face of time and mortality.
  • The room’s designation as a “Fire Escape” and the landlord’s references to the hotel as a “good enough port for a man in a storm” symbolize refuge and escape but also confinement and decay. The hotel is a microcosm of a decaying world where ghosts, literal or metaphorical, linger, and where the characters’ histories are inscribed in the physical and social environment.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “Whose hair, I wondered as I undressed in the red-streaked dark—whose hair?” Why does a stranger’s hair matter to Vera?
  • The landlord speaks of Tillie, young and old, “always of one person —for as if in ocean depths, the two were one.” How can one woman be both?
  • The landlord says it was “the hangman’s shadow, who got loose after every hanging and said the things the hangman never would have said.” Which is the real Mr. Weed?

Characters

  • “God had never been in the hotel business.” What does the landlord know that God does not?
  • “I am the only one who remembers you, Sarah,” the doctor says, and then, “I have forgotten you.” Which is true?
  • “Many a woman was brought to child-bed against her will.” What does the old doctor reveal about the women of the town?

Connections

  • Over Vera’s bed are the words “Forget me not,” and the doctor tells Sarah “that is why your eyes are blue forget-me-nots.” What links them?
  • The doctor cries, “My God! Why hast thou deserted me?” What does he change from the words on the cross?
  • The doctor’s strangest case is “a woman who was always pregnant with an imaginary baby.” How does she echo Vera’s stone child in Chapter 68?

Reflection

  • “Who could endure the sight of his love when his love was old?” Who in the novel has?
  • He tells Sarah to go so that “she would seem beautiful, beautiful because distant and forgotten.” Is love easier from a distance?
  • “Go, Sarah!” Vera screams. Why does she join the doctor’s quarrel?

Research: Characters

Vera

  • A reflective observer who notices the eerie attributes of the room and the unfolding drama next door.

The Landlord

  • A loquacious, colorful figure with a tattooed, lion-like chest, who shares stories of Mr. Weed, the hotel’s history, and his own varied past as a sailor, performer, and soldier. He is knowledgeable but somewhat cynical, providing a bridge between reality and the supernatural elements.
  • His wife, a former circus fat lady, owned the hotel. He tells of being a candy merchant at ten, a magician, a ventriloquist, a sailor, a soldier in the Intelligence, and a hunter of wild animals for Hagenbeck’s, and of having killed, “very reluctantly, against his will,” “a very little black man who had no tongue but had swallowed a diamond.”

Mr. Weed and His Shadow

  • Mentioned as mysterious figures, Mr. Weed is a man of few words, while his shadow is more outspoken and unsettling. The landlord distinguishes between the two, adding layers to the mythos of the hotel.
  • The landlord says Mr. Weed is in truth “a prosperous farmer and the soul of respectability” with a model dairy farm, and that “The reason he tied the knot was that he got paid to tie it.”

Dr. Justice O’Leary

  • An aging, embittered physician who is haunted by “Sarah,” a ghost or symbolic figure representing death, disgrace, and the burden of his past. He is portrayed as mad, desperate, and filled with sorrow and rage.

Sarah O’Leary

  • A ghostly, troubling figure who haunts Dr. O’Leary’s room and mind. She is described variously as a dead woman, a baby, a spiteful presence, and a symbol of decay and disgrace. Her interactions with the doctor reveal themes of memory, loss, and the inescapable presence of death.

Other Figures

  • The bus driver, Imadaisy (Imadaisy appears only here; asked who she was, the landlord answers, “Nobody.” The name reads as “I’m a daisy,” old American slang for someone first-rate.), and various clients of the doctor add depth to the setting’s community. Tillie is a young girl who lives in the hotel where the narrator, Vera, is staying. The landlord describes her as “a very young girl” who will knit a baby hood for Vera. He mentions her casually while showing Vera the corridors of the hotel, specifically pointing to the door opposite Vera’s room. The landlord’s description suggests that Tillie is a quiet presence in the hotel, possibly involved in simple, caring tasks like knitting. But the landlord insists that she and the old woman who keeps canaries as big as turkey buzzards are one person: “as if in ocean depths, the two were one.” The landlord seems to treat Tillie as a benign, almost innocuous figure within this strange and lively environment.

Research: Themes

Death and Haunting

See: Death and Mortality

  • The omnipresence of death is felt through the spectral figure of Sarah, Mr. Weed’s shadow, and the decaying hotel environment.
  • Dr. O’Leary’s confrontation with Sarah is a meditation on mortality, loss, disgrace, and the persistence of the past.

Memory and Forgetting

See: Memory and the Past

  • Dr. O’Leary claims to be the only one who remembers Sarah, highlighting the tension between remembrance and societal forgetting.
  • The landlord’s stories and Mr. Weed’s shadow serves as conduits for local history and legend, preserving memory in a decaying place.

Decay and Corruption

See: Decay and Ruin

  • Physical decay (the mattress, the slop jar, Sarah’s sores) mirrors moral and psychological decay, especially in the doctor’s life and the hotel’s atmosphere.

Isolation and Madness

See: Isolation and Connection · Madness and Sanity

  • The doctor’s madness and isolation are emphasized through his anguished monologues and the strange, disruptive presence of Sarah.
  • The landlord and Vera observe these events as somewhat detached witnesses, enhancing the sense of alienation.

The Supernatural and Reality

See: Illusion and Reality · Faith, God, and the Supernatural

  • The narrative blurs lines between reality and the supernatural with ghostly figures, haunted rooms, and symbolic presences.
  • The landlord’s tall tales and Mr. Weed’s shadow complicate the story’s realism.

Research: Resources

  • “was marked Fire Escape”: Fire escapes emerged in the U.S. from the 1800s, driven by deadly urban fires, initially as simple ladders and later evolving into complex external stair systems, with Anna Connelly’s 1887 patent for a bridge-like design becoming foundational; they became common after laws following disasters like the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire but faded in new construction after the 1960s as modern sprinklers, alarms, and improved firefighting equipment made them less essential, though they remain iconic features in older cityscapes.
  • “the mattress stuffed with corn husks”: Corn husks were used from the Colonial era to the 19th century.
  • “Forget me not”: The forget-me-not is the flower of remembrance. In the German legend a knight, gathering the flowers for his lady by a river, was dragged down by his armor and threw them to her as he drowned, crying “Forget me not.” See Death and Mortality.
  • “There was a slop jar with big handles like ears”: Slop jars were used along with a pitcher and basin in hotels until the 1950s.
  • “as loquacious as the hangman”: Loquacious means talkative or tending to talk a great deal.
  • “as many colors as Joseph’s coat”: Joseph’s coat of many colors is from the Book of Genesis. It was a special, richly decorated robe given to him by his father Jacob, symbolizing his favoritism, which fueled his brothers’ jealousy, leading them to sell Joseph into slavery and present the bloody coat to Jacob as proof he was dead. The garment, likely a regal tunic with long sleeves, signified Joseph’s special status and set him apart, though it also provoked envy, highlighting themes of favoritism, betrayal, and God’s guiding purpose through hardship. “Now Israel loved Joseph more than all his children… and he made him a coat of many colours” (Genesis 37:3, KJV). See Genesis.
  • “the landlord’s wife who had been a circus fat lady”: The “fat lady” in the U.S. circus was a prominent sideshow attraction from the 19th to mid-20th centuries, embodying spectacle, curiosity, and sometimes exploitation, with famous figures like Lottie Whitlock, billed as the original “Fat Lady,” and later stars like Alice from Dallas and Celesta Geyer (Dolly Dimples) who gained fame and fortune before some dramatically lost weight, highlighting societal views on size and health. These performers, part of the “freak show” tradition, used their size to build successful careers, often achieving celebrity status within the circus world, though facing exploitation and objectification. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “He had seen lamer ducks than the hangman”: A lame duck is an official (especially the president) in the final period of office, after the election of a successor; an ineffectual or unsuccessful person or thing.
  • “Barnum’s seven wonders of the universe”: Phineas Taylor Barnum was an American showman, businessman, and politician, remembered for promoting celebrated hoaxes and founding the Barnum & Bailey Circus with James Anthony Bailey. Barnum went on to create America’s first aquarium and to expand the wax figure department of his museum. His Seven Grand Salons demonstrated the Seven Wonders of the World which claimed 850,000 curiosities. (Wikipedia) His circus was billed as the Greatest Show on Earth.
  • “old people with hardening of the arteries”: Hardening of the arteries, or atherosclerosis, is a widespread issue in the U.S., a slow-developing disease where plaque (fat, cholesterol) narrows arteries, significantly contributing to heart attacks and strokes, the leading causes of death, affecting roughly half of Americans aged 45-84, often silently until serious events occur, with risk factors including high blood pressure, smoking, high cholesterol, and diabetes.
  • “If you hear sleigh bells ringing”: More than four thousand American soldiers came home from World War I as amputees. The rusted sleigh bells Vera dreaded in Chapter 68, jingling all night in the corridors, are his. See the Chapter 68 resources.
  • “He was coming home on a furlough during the Last War”: The landlord’s Last War is World War I: he remembers the Marne and the Lusitania, steps on the face of a “Hun,” and goes hunting for Hagenbeck’s after it ends.
  • “Maybe I was surprised by the hangman’s confessions”: The passage explores themes of death, violence, and respectability, highlighting the banality and repetitiveness of horror, and the unsettling coexistence of brutality and normalcy in the hangman’s life. The gruesome crimes serve as a backdrop to the hangman’s enigmatic identity and the eerie atmosphere of the setting.
  • “the mad butcher who sold human beings as hams”: Compare Fritz Haarmann, the “Butcher of Hanover,” executed in 1925 for murdering young men and long suspected of selling their flesh as black-market meat.
  • “a model dairy farm”: The modernization of model dairy farms started in 1879 and continued to World War II.
  • “a very little black man who had no tongue but had swallowed a diamond”: At the Kimberley diamond mines in South Africa, from 1885 Black miners were locked in closed compounds, searched naked, and held and dosed with purgatives before they left, to recover any diamonds they had swallowed. See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “I was in the Intelligence during the Last War”: In World War I the United States built its first real intelligence services: the Army’s Military Intelligence Division (1917), its Corps of Intelligence Police, and MI-8, Herbert Yardley’s codebreakers.
  • “I can say parlez vous to little Frenchies”: “Frenchies” is an informal and sometimes derogatory term used describing French people. American soldiers in France sang “Mademoiselle from Armentières,” with its refrain “Hinky dinky, parlez-vous.”
  • “Remember the Marne”: The Marne was the river of two great battles of World War I. In 1914 the French and British stopped the German advance on Paris there, and in 1918 American troops held the line at the Second Battle of the Marne. “Remember the Lusitania” was a recruiting cry of 1915.
    • Remember the Marne may refer to Remember the Maine! To hell with Spain! after the USS Maine. It was a United States Navy ship that sank in Havana Harbor on February 15, 1898, contributing to the outbreak of the Spanish-American War in April 1898. Although the USS Maine explosion was not a direct cause, it served as a catalyst that accelerated the events leading up to the war.
  • “His sacred pictures were on his back”: Sailors’ lore held that a crucifixion tattooed across the back would stay the hand of an officer ordering a flogging.
  • “a supposedly dead Hun”: “Hun” was the Allied name for German soldiers in World War I, revived in World War II, linking them to the barbaric, nomadic Huns of antiquity known for cruelty under Attila, especially after Kaiser Wilhelm II’s 1900 speech encouraging similar savagery in China; Allied propaganda effectively used this to portray Nazis as barbaric, though terms like “Fritz” or “Jerry” (British) and “Heinie” (American) were also common for German soldiers.
  • “Hagenbeck’s out of Berlin”: Hagenbeck’s out of Berlin may refer to The Tierpark Hagenbeck zoo in Stellingen, Hamburg, Germany. The collection began in 1863 with animals that belonged to Carl Hagenbeck Sr., a fishmonger who became an amateur animal collector. The park itself was founded by Carl Hagenbeck Jr. in 1907. It is known for being the first zoo to use open enclosures surrounded by moats, rather than barred cages, to better approximate animals’ natural environments. Among his collections, however, were also human beings whom he exhibited in “human zoos.” The private zoo is still run by the Hagenbeck family. Hagenbeck’s was based in Hamburg, not Berlin. From 1874 it exhibited Sámi, Nubians, Inuit, and other peoples in its “ethnological shows.” See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “had seen elephants copulating”: The belief is not from the Bible but from Pliny, who wrote that elephants mate in secret, out of modesty (Natural History, Book 8), and from the medieval bestiaries that followed him.
  • “Sarah, you are dead, this town’s little sister”: See Sarah O’Leary.
  • “a canker in the rose immortal”: “And loathsome canker lives in sweetest bud” (Shakespeare, Sonnet 35). Compare Blake’s “The Sick Rose” (1794), destroyed by “The invisible worm.” See Shakespeare and Romantic Poets.
  • “We must put away our childish loves”: “When I was a child, I spake as a child… but when I became a man, I put away childish things” (1 Corinthians 13:11, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “He was as mad as a March hare”: “Mad as a March hare” is an idiom meaning extremely silly, eccentric, or crazy, originating from the frenzied, unpredictable behavior of European hares during their spring breeding season (March), which includes frantic chases, high leaps, and “boxing” matches where females swat males away to discourage unwanted advances. This phrase was popularized by Lewis Carroll’s Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland, featuring the eccentric March Hare at the Mad Hatter’s tea party, though the behavior itself is a real, albeit misunderstood, part of hare mating rituals, not actual madness. See Alice in Wonderland.
  • “Dr. Justice O’Leary, for so he called himself”: In Chapter 67, Moses believed old Doc dead at the wheel of his ghost buggy. See the Chapter 67 resources and Justice O’Leary.
  • “He had blown his breath into the mouths of dead men”: “And he went up, and lay upon the child, and put his mouth upon his mouth… and the flesh of the child waxed warm” (2 Kings 4:34, KJV), Elisha raising the Shunammite woman’s son. See The Bible.
  • “he had seen something steal out of the room”: Compare Dr. Duncan MacDougall of Haverhill, Massachusetts, who in 1907 weighed six dying patients and claimed that the soul weighed about twenty-one grams.
  • “The outside of the cup was shining”: “For ye make clean the outside of the cup and of the platter, but within they are full of extortion and excess” (Matthew 23:25, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “a wormwood”: Culturally wormwood has been used in the Christian Bible to connote something incredibly bitter. In the Book of Revelation it is used as the name of the star. In Hamlet, he uses the word wormwood to describe the situation he believes is going on with his family. “And the name of the star is called Wormwood” (Revelation 8:11, KJV). See Revelation and the Apocalypse and Shakespeare.
  • “Let this cup pass from him”: From Matthew 26:39 where Jesus is asking God if he has to go through with this. This moment is part of a larger, surreal and symbolic interaction filled with themes of death, memory, disgrace, and the passage of time, as the doctor grapples with his own mortality and the ghostly reminders of a troubled past. “O my Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me” (Matthew 26:39, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “Better a stone cherub than a baby now”: Compare Vera’s stone child, “a marble statue in your womb,” in the Chapter 68 resources.
  • “For God knew he was no abortionist”: By 1900 abortion was a crime in every state. In 1930 abortion was the recorded cause of death of nearly 2,700 American women, almost one in five maternal deaths. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “as God is not the great physician”: “They that are whole have no need of the physician, but they that are sick” (Mark 2:17, KJV); revival hymns hailed Jesus as “The Great Physician.” See The Bible.
  • “Why hast thou deserted me?”: From Psalm 22, according to the Gospels, Jesus spoke these words during the Crucifixion, after he had suffered on the cross for three hours. It is a cry of abandonment and despair, reflecting Dr. O’Leary’s inner turmoil as he confronts death, loss, and the haunting past. The King James reads, “My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?” (Psalm 22:1; Matthew 27:46). The doctor makes it “deserted.” See The Bible.
  • “Could anyone get blood out of a turnip”: The phrase refers to the idiom, “You can’t squeeze blood from a turnip,” meaning you can’t get something from someone or something that doesn’t have it to give, like trying to get money from someone with no money, or forcing an impossible result. It’s similar to “You can’t get blood from a stone” and signifies the futility of demanding something unavailable.
  • “Jerusalem, Jerusalem”: “O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, thou that killest the prophets” (Matthew 23:37, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “Mayo, Mayo!”: Mayo Clinic originated with the medical practice of William Worrall Mayo, M.D. who immigrated to the United States in 1846. He had two sons. The Mayo brothers were financially successful and professionally prominent. The Mayo Clinic is still in the US and has opened branches in several countries. The clinic grew from St. Marys Hospital in Rochester, Minnesota, which Mother Alfred Moes and her Sisters of St. Francis built after the tornado of 1883 and opened in 1889, with the Mayos as its doctors. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “a woman who was always pregnant with an imaginary baby”: The doctor says his strangest case is a woman who believes she is always pregnant with an imaginary baby and suffers from many miscarriages. This woman seems to refer to Esther Longtree who will be prominent towards the last chapters of the novel. See Esther Longtree.
  • “that penny he stole off a dead man’s eyes”: Coins were laid on the eyes of the dead to keep them closed, and in Greek custom a coin was left for Charon, the ferryman of the dead. See Death and Mortality.
  • “his house was his Englishman’s castle”: “The house of every one is to him as his castle and fortress” (Sir Edward Coke, Semayne’s Case, 1604).
  • “naked, we come into this world, and naked, we go out of it”: The line comes from the Bible, found in both Ecclesiastes 5:15 and Job 1:21. The phrase reflects the inevitability of human vulnerability at birth and death, stripping away all pretenses or material concerns, which is a poignant theme running through the novel’s exploration of mortality, identity, and human frailty. “Naked came I out of my mother’s womb, and naked shall I return thither” (Job 1:21, KJV). See The Book of Job and Ecclesiastes.
  • “Oh, my Lady Gonorrhea!”: Gonorrhea was first documented in ancient Greece by Galen, who used the term from the Greek words gonos (semen) and rhoia (flow) to describe what he mistakenly called an “involuntary ejaculation of semen.” The name “the clap” emerged in the 1500s, originating from the French “les clapiers,” a term for brothels where the disease was common. While medieval and early modern records describe symptoms, the specific bacterium, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, wasn’t identified until 1879 by Albert Neisser, though the connection to women’s health was sometimes misunderstood.
