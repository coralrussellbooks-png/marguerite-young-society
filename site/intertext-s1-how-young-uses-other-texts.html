Section 1. How Young Uses Other Texts

Allusions: Angels and Archangels · Don Quixote · Fairy Tales and Folklore · Genesis · Greek Mythology · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · Judaism · Milton · Shakespeare · The Bible · The History of Opium

Definition from research

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling opens on a Grey Goose bus crawling through fog somewhere in the interior of the country. Before the first chapter has gone far, Vera Cartwheel reaches for a book to explain a stranger. The bus-driver whistles, drinks, calls on the angels, and talks about a wife waiting for him at home. Vera has no way of knowing whether that wife exists. She wonders whether he is "perhaps even some mad Don Quixote chasing windmills, a virgin spirit, nobody." Then she catches herself and admits that his whole family life may be "an emanation of my over-active imagination" (chapter 1).

The allusion arrives as a guess, and the guess exposes the person who makes it. Cervantes's knight read too many books and mistook them for the world. Vera is a reader too, and within a sentence she confesses to doing the same thing. The reference explains the bus-driver no better than any other guess would. What it explains is the young woman on the bus who needs him explained.

A page later the windmill turns physical. Vera recalls what the bus has already passed on its way through the fog: "an enormous coffee urn with its lid opened against the sky, a wigwam nightclub," and then "a windmill, a leaning tower, Noah's Ark, the old woman who lived in the shoe" (chapter 1). These are roadside buildings, novelty architecture standing in what she calls "the watery greyness of the Middle West." Quixote's windmill, the tower of Pisa, the ark from Genesis, and a nursery rhyme have all been rebuilt in lumber and paint beside an Iowa road.

There is nothing sneering in the passage. Someone built those things, likely with pride, and travelers drove past them on the way to somewhere else. Young shows the great traditions of the West already arrived in the middle of the country, already rebuilt cheaply and sincerely, long before her narrator came along to notice them. The inheritance is not locked in a library in Boston. It stands in a cornfield, weathered and a little absurd, and still recognizable.

The Thesis

These two moments, placed side by side at the very start, hold the method of the whole novel. Young's allusions arrive worn and secondhand, filtered through a character's need. A reference in this book rarely tells what its source means. It tells what a person needed the source to do.

That turns every allusion into a question about the person holding it, not only the text it points to. Why does Vera reach for Quixote? Why does a nursemaid prefer Bunyan to the Bible? Why does an Iowa bus-driver recite the tribes of Israel? The answers are different each time, and they add up to a portrait of how people in this novel furnish their minds.

The question also fits the scale of the book. An index built for this volume finds about two thousand references across the novel's 82 chapters. They draw on Scripture, Greek myth, Mother Goose, the English poets, European history, American presidents, hymns, almanacs, and the names of stars. No reader can hold all of that at once, and the novel does not ask anyone to. It asks a smaller and more useful question of each reference: who is holding this text, and what did they need it to do?

Before the first chapter ends, Young names the myth that governs the rest. Vera says, "All my life I had been reaching for the tangible, and it had evaded me, much like the myth of Tantalus" (chapter 1). Tantalus stood in water that drained away whenever he bent to drink, under fruit that rose out of reach whenever he stretched for it. Vera's long search for Miss MacIntosh, the plain nursemaid who seemed the one solid thing in a house of dreams, follows that pattern exactly. Every time the real seems close enough to hold, it recedes. The allusion is not ornament. It is the plot, stated in one sentence.

What the Index Shows

Sorting the references by the character who carries them brings three findings into view.

First, each character holds the inheritance in a different way.

  • Miss MacIntosh carries reference books, not stories: Pilgrim's Progress, Franklin's almanac maxims, the genealogies of the Bible, the Hebrew calendar. She prefers Bunyan to Scripture because "old Bunyan was fairly safe even though not altogether trustworthy" (chapter 3). She treats the great men of history as personal opponents. Every reference to Benjamin Franklin in the novel belongs to her, and she never forgives him: "it was he who had written that early to bed makes us wise" (chapter 23), and then he brought the electric light.

  • Catherine Cartwheel, Vera's mother, holds history as a crowd of houseguests. In her opium dreams she is "entertaining the P's, the Q's, the R's" (chapter 11), and her rooms fill until "Spinoza and Shakespeare and Mrs. Sydington were already in the green" (chapter 24).

  • Cousin Hannah takes the parts of conquerors and questers and fails at the quest. She is "that Jason who had not found the golden fleece" (chapter 42).

  • Mr. Spitzer keeps his references in pairs and in the geography of the afterlife. He is the one who concludes, after listing the names of God, that "God was many things to many people" (chapter 29).

  • The bus-driver turns Scripture into oaths and barroom litany.

  • Esther Longtree builds an imagined motherhood out of sacred and fairy-tale material, down to "the Song of Songs of the sterility" (chapter 77).

  • Vera, who narrates nearly all of it, owns surprisingly few references herself. The ones she owns, like Quixote and Tantalus, are about seeing without grasping.

Second, Young's handling of her sources repeats in a small set of strategies that cut across the whole cast.

  • She flattens the canon into roadside buildings and pet names.

  • She stores it in catalogs and alphabets that cannot rank what they hold.

  • She lets one text change its meaning by changing who recites it. The genealogies of Israel begin as Miss MacIntosh's schoolroom drill. The bus-driver recites them as a lament for "the poor humanity that was dead before it was ever born" (chapter 7). They end as Miss MacIntosh's own cry of loss, "Where is Issachar, my dear, my dear of dears?", and among the patriarchs she slips a living man: "Where is Mr. Spitzer?" (chapter 18).

  • She hands famous roles to the wrong people. The bus-driver, guarding his uncut hair, becomes "Penelope weaving this web, not Ulysses" (chapter 5), and two Boston spinsters each claim to be Cleopatra (chapter 26).

  • She lets the great enter through their injuries: "crippled Mr. Alexander Pope" and "blind Milton dictating Paradise Lost to those indifferent daughters" (chapter 2).

  • She treats the calendar as something people built. Miss MacIntosh returns again and again to the Emperor Constantine for changing the day of rest, and 10 of the novel's 11 references to Constantine are hers.

  • She gives borrowed crowns to people with little else.

  • She lets one shared picture of death, Charon's ferry and Gabriel's horn, pass across every class, from a Boston lawyer to an Iowa bus-driver.

Third, these strategies all do the same underlying work: they keep the inheritance while refusing it final authority. Nothing is thrown away. Nothing keeps its original rank for long.

  • The Bible is doubted by its most devoted reader, who admits in the end that "The Bible exaggerates" (chapter 63).

  • Pilgrim's Progress, Miss MacIntosh's chosen guide, turns up on the beach as "her Pilgrim's Progress water-logged with marks of sea shells on its pages" (chapter 3). Near her own end she names the journey for what it has become: "ours is a Pilgrim's Progress from nowhere to nowhere" (chapter 63).

  • Calderón's famous line that life is a dream is spoken not by a sage but by Miss MacIntosh in her collapse (chapter 18) and by a drunken country doctor. He delivered a real baby while believing he was dreaming, and afterward he announces, "Life is a dream, and I have been wrong" (chapter 77).

  • Even Miss MacIntosh's scorn softens into pity for the great men who failed as she has: "Oh, my poor Canute who could not stay the tide although he bid the tide stand still!" (chapter 63).

The inheritance survives, soaked through, relabeled, and spoken by unlikely mouths.

Why Young Called Her Work Classic

Critics often called Miss MacIntosh, My Darling experimental. Young insisted that it was classic, and her use of other texts supports her. Her sources are the common stock of Western reading: the Bible, Homer, Virgil, Cervantes, Bunyan, Milton, Calderón, Grimm, and Mother Goose. A reader in 1965 would have met most of them in school, in church, or at bedtime. Nothing in her borrowing is obscure for its own sake.

What makes the novel feel strange is not the sources but the way they are kept. Young treats the Western inheritance the way a family treats an attic, not the way a museum treats a collection. Things are stored there because someone once needed them. They come down worn and relabeled, and they are put to uses their makers never pictured. A nursery rhyme becomes a roadside building. A royal genealogy becomes a grandmother's promise to an illegitimate boy in the Ozarks that he could win any throne "if he believed in America" (chapter 65). A town remembers its prettiest girl as Lincoln's sweetheart and explains, without irony, "We elected her to be immortal" (chapter 6).

This is what Young meant by classic. She took the tradition whole and set it down in the American interior among ordinary people. There it is used, misremembered, loved, and argued with. It is classic in the sense of being common property.
