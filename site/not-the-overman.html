Definition from research

Who is that dwarf, a turret on his head, a rose at his collar? It is the rose of consciousness.

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling

The Philosopher of Overcoming and the Novel That Does Not Overcome

Friedrich Nietzsche's philosophy is organized around the figure of the overman: the being who has confronted the death of God, faced the abyss without flinching, borne the heaviest thought (eternal recurrence), overthrown the spirit of gravity that rides the back and whispers you cannot bear this, and arrived at amor fati: the love of fate, the willingness to will the eternal return of every moment of one's life. The overman does not inherit values; he creates them. He does not need the afterlife to justify the present. He does not need the dwarf removed from his back by a doctor or a priest. He overcomes the dwarf himself, in the open air, on the mountain, at the gateway called Moment where two eternal paths meet.

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling contains no overman and is not interested in producing one. What it contains instead are three precise interventions in Nietzsche's philosophical system: Young takes three of his most characteristic figures, the Spirit of Gravity, eternal recurrence, and the abyss, and does something specific to each of them that Nietzsche did not do. She does not defeat the dwarf. She does not affirm eternal recurrence. She does not emerge from the abyss strengthened. These are not failures to understand Nietzsche. They are AND Philosophy corrections of him: what happens when you apply the overman's philosophy to a man who is constitutionally incapable of being the overman, and discover that the incapacity is itself the truth.

The Spirit of Gravity Becomes the Rose of Consciousness

In Part Three of Thus Spoke Zarathustra, in the section titled 'On the Vision and the Riddle,' Zarathustra is climbing a mountain with the dwarf, the Spirit of Gravity, riding on his back. The dwarf is the heaviness of conventional thinking, the weight of the past, the internal voice that says: you cannot bear what must be borne. He rides Zarathustra's back and whispers that Zarathustra will fall. They reach a gateway called Moment where two paths stretch in opposite directions to eternity: one path forward into the future, one path backward into the past. This is the gateway at which eternal recurrence must be confronted. Zarathustra tells the dwarf to get off his back. He must face the vision alone. The dwarf is the obstacle between Zarathustra and his greatest thought.

Young's novel places a figure climbing through mountains of snow in Chapter 45, a grieving voice searching for a dead love, ascending nakedly through storm clouds, crying that the soul should go with the body, that she lived on when she should have died. It is Cousin Hannah's voice, or the opium lady's voice, or the voice of any consciousness that has survived what it loves. She climbs. The dogs bark at her heels. The sea shell roars. And then, mid-climb, mid-storm: Who is that dwarf, a turret on his head, a rose at his collar? It is the rose of consciousness.

The identification is not Nietzsche's. Zarathustra identifies the dwarf as his adversary, the Spirit of Gravity, the thing that must be overcome. Young identifies the dwarf as consciousness itself. She does not tell the dwarf to get off. She does not overcome him. She names him. She gives him a turret on his head, another small tower, another architectural enclosure housing whatever is most guarded, and a rose at his collar: the most mortal and most beautiful of emblems, the thing that is already dying as it opens.

The rose of consciousness: this is the AND Philosophy's most compressed statement of what awareness actually is. Not the weapon of the overman. Not the faculty to be honed into philosophical mastery. Consciousness is the small, armored, flowering thing you carry up the mountain with you, not riding your back as an adversary but present at your collar as your own nature. It is turret-headed, it encloses and guards itself, it moves through the storm with you. It is also a rose: temporary, fragrant, in the process of its own dying even as it blooms. You do not overcome it. You wear it.

Cousin Hannah, the novel's greatest figure, is explicitly described as not superhuman. She was not superhuman, she had encountered greater dangers than most men know, certainly greater dangers than most women know in this life. The qualifier is doing exactly the work Young intends: the greatest human being in the novel, the one who crossed into Mesopotamia and bearded lions in their dens and rode against great turbaned Turks, is not the overman. She is human. Her heart is human. She has the rose of consciousness at her collar like everyone else, blooming and dying, and she carries it up the mountain and climbs naked through the snow, and she does not arrive at the gateway called Moment and confront the eternal return. She arrives at the opium lady's dark tower and cannot get in. She dies in her own old tent bed with no aide-de-camp to see her go. The greatest figure is not the overman, and this is not presented as failure. It is presented as the condition of being alive.

Eternal Recurrence as Grief's Deepest Wish

Nietzsche's eternal recurrence is a thought experiment and a test. The question is: if you were condemned to live this life again, exactly as it has been, every moment recurring eternally, would you affirm it? Would you will it? Could you love your fate enough to say yes to its infinite repetition? The test is designed to be almost unbearable. Most people, confronted with it honestly, find they cannot say yes. The one who can say yes, who can amor fati, who can will the eternal return of their own existence, has become something close to the overman.

In the novel, Mr. Spitzer asks the question differently. "Perhaps he could not say farewell to anyone because he had not bidden good-bye to his brother whose memory was now the only life to him—sustaining him through many weary hours—and surely, as he would often sighingly whisper, his brother had not said farewell to life. Should not eternal recurrence be the one law of life, and should not the great lamp-lighter going his rounds through darkened heavens light that lamp which had gone out and was himself shining through clouds?"

This is not Nietzsche's thought experiment. Mr. Spitzer is not asking whether he would affirm his life's eternal repetition. He is asking whether the dead return. He is asking whether the lamp that went out gets relit. He wants Peron back. He wants the opium lady's lamp to be turned on again by some great lamp-lighter who goes his rounds through darkened heavens. He is using Nietzsche's most demanding proposition as a wish: the wish that the universe is organized around return rather than loss, that what has gone keeps going, that the dead brother comes back because that is the law.

This is the AND Philosophy's grief as permanent weather applied to Nietzsche directly. Eternal recurrence, in Mr. Spitzer's hands, is not a test of life-affirmation. It is grief's most philosophical form: the refusal to accept that the dead stay dead, dressed in the language of cosmological law. He is not strong enough for Nietzsche's eternal recurrence, which demands that you affirm even the worst moments, even the deaths, even the losses, even the cruelty of what has happened, and will it all to return. He wants only the good parts back. He wants the lamp. He wants his brother. He asks the question not as a hammer but as a lamp-lighter's prayer.

This is not a misunderstanding of Nietzsche. It is Young's deliberate retuning of the concept to the frequency at which Mr. Spitzer actually lives. He is the most philosophical figure in the novel, the one most likely to encounter Nietzsche's thought, and he encounters it as a man of grief encounters a philosophy of affirmation: he takes from it what he can use, which is the structure of the wish that things return, and leaves the demand for affirmation where he cannot reach.

The Abyss That Stares Back Every Day

Nietzsche wrote in Beyond Good and Evil: if you gaze long enough into the abyss, the abyss gazes also into you. The aphorism is a warning about the cost of sustained confrontation with darkness: the person who spends their life investigating cruelty, meaninglessness, or nihilism risks becoming what they study. The abyss does not remain passive under scrutiny. It returns the gaze. The overman, by implication, is the one who can sustain this mutual gaze without being destroyed by it.

“A fat man, well protected by his dreaming flesh, this corporeal body he could not depart from, he was one who, walking always as if on thinnest ice, tread softly where angels feared to rush ahead, for he never knew, wrapped in his many ethereal abstractions, what the next step would bring, whether he would step on nothing, whether it would be a stairway or a dissolving stairway or a chasm filled with the songs of dead birds, whether he would stare at that wild, precipitous abyss which would stare at him.” The novel's deployment is not a warning and not a test. It is a description of ordinary movement. Mr. Spitzer does not gaze long and deliberately into the abyss. He walks his daily route and does not know if the next step is solid ground or a chasm filled with the songs of dead birds. The abyss is not somewhere he goes to confront his limits. It is the condition of every step.

This is Young's most direct correction of Nietzsche's spatial metaphor. The abyss is not a place you travel to for philosophical confrontation, from which you may return strengthened or destroyed. It is the permanent condition of walking at all. Every step Mr. Spitzer takes is a step on what might be thinnest ice, over what might be the abyss that will stare back. He is not gazing into it. He is living above it. The distinction matters: Nietzsche's warning assumes you have the option of not gazing. Mr. Spitzer has no such option. He cannot choose not to be above the abyss. He can only choose how carefully he walks.

What Young Is Not Doing

It would be easy to read these three deployments as Young simply transposing Nietzsche's philosophy into narrative. The dwarf is an obstacle to consciousness. Eternal recurrence as Mr. Spitzer's philosophical framework. The abyss as the existential condition of the modern novel. These readings are accurate as far as they go but they miss what is most specific about AND Philosophy and Young's use of Nietzsche.

She is not illustrating his philosophy. She is applying it to people who cannot live by it and showing what happens. What happens is not failure. What happens is that the philosophy reveals, under the pressure of actual human beings with actual grief and actual mortality, a different set of truths than the ones it was designed to produce.

The Spirit of Gravity is not overcome; it becomes the rose of consciousness, which is something more accurate and more mortal and more beautiful than what Nietzsche made of it. Eternal recurrence is not affirmed; it becomes the wish that the dead return, which is something more honest about grief than Nietzsche's hammer. The abyss is not confronted for philosophical growth; it is walked above every day, which is something more like actual courage than the dramatic mutual gaze of the aphorism.

Cousin Hannah, the person in the novel who comes closest to Nietzschean heroism, is explicitly not superhuman. She carries her rose of consciousness up the mountain with her. She dies in a domestic war. She is not the overman. She is the greatest human figure the novel can produce, which is something both lesser than the overman and, in Young's terms, more real.

The AND Philosophy Reading

The AND Philosophy's plural self pillar is what Young deploys against Nietzsche most directly. Nietzsche's philosophy requires a unified subject: the overman is a singular will that overcomes, affirms, creates values, and bears the heaviest thought. The AND Philosophy's self is constitutively plural, fragmented, distributed across grief and loss and the dead and the past and the body and the spirit and the twin who is gone. Mr. Spitzer cannot be the overman because there is no single Mr. Spitzer to be him. He is Joachim and Peron simultaneously, the living brother wearing the dead brother's watch, the musician of silent music, the hesitant suicide who lives on, the lawyer with his brief-case and his old laundry bills. The overman requires integration. Mr. Spitzer is permanently disintegrated and has found that this is not a problem to be solved.

The grief as permanent weather pillar is what Young deploys against the eternal recurrence. Nietzsche wants you to affirm the return of everything, including the grief, as part of the life you are willing. Young shows a man who cannot affirm the grief because the grief is not a moment that can be affirmed or refused; it is the weather, the atmospheric condition, the permanent press of the loss that does not resolve. You cannot will the eternal return of something that never left. The grief of the dead brother is not a moment to be returned to. It is the medium in which every moment occurs.

The witness pillar is what Young deploys against the abyss. Nietzsche's gazer into the abyss is alone: it is the individual will confronting the individual void. Young's witness is always watching something other than themselves. Mr. Spitzer does not gaze into the abyss. He watches the opium lady. He watches the mute. He watches the derelicts in the hobo camps. The abyss gazes back at him not because he has looked into it directly but because it is present in everything he witnesses: in the woman who was the greatest Lady Macbeth, in the hobo who does not know he was a banker, in the opium lady dying with the sun and the moon weighted on her eyes. The witness is always already above the abyss because the people being witnessed are above it too. It gazes back at everyone equally and no one overcomes it. You only walk carefully, and witness each other, and keep walking.
