Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

A closing word

← Decolonization in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

A book about dissolution should be careful how it ends, since an ending is one more boundary, one more line drawn where the argument has spent all its pages rubbing lines out. So this is not a conclusion in the usual sense. It gathers nothing into a fist. It sets the tools down where a reader can pick them up.

Read Miss MacIntosh, My Darling as a book that goes down. Not across, through its famous length, and not around, through its catalog of women's lives, but down, into a mind, until the categories a reader carries in stop holding. Living and dead, waking and dreaming, man and woman, one self and the crowd inside it: the novel does not argue these apart. It stays inside a consciousness long enough that they come apart on their own, the way a word repeated too many times stops meaning anything and becomes only sound. Young found she could reach that depth through any door a woman was handed, the pregnancy, the marriage, the wig, the grief, and that every door opened on the same room. The women are the route. The floor at the bottom of the route has no sex, which is why Mr. Spitzer can stand on it too.

The eleven pillars were never meant to be believed. They are reading tools, and a tool that will not let itself be set down has stopped being a tool and become a doctrine. A reader can grant the premise of this edition, that Young wrote from the seam where the fixed categories give way, and still argue with every single reading in the chapters above. That is the right use of the book. The premise is the ground. The readings are one person's path across it, and other paths are there to be walked.

What stays, when the argument is set aside, is the novel itself, and the novel does not need this edition or any other to be what it is. It was written across eighteen years by a woman who said she thought of herself as neither woman nor man, only as someone doomed to write, and who was met for saying so with the particular rage a culture keeps for those who will not stay sorted. She wrote from that rage's target and did not flinch from it. She put down what happens to a woman, all of it, and then went underneath what happens to a woman to the structure of a self under pressure, and there she wrote what the whole book keeps saying to anyone who will hear it. “Every heart is the other heart. Every soul is the other soul. The individual is the one illusion.”

That is not a comfort and it was not offered as one. It is a finding, arrived at through fiction before there was a settled language to name it, and it belongs now to whoever reads far enough to reach it. The women Young kept at the threshold, the erased and the unfinished and the unheard, are still there in the book, still enduring, still refusing to disappear. This edition has tried only to say, in the plainest terms it could find, where to stand to see them, and then to get out of the way.

The links that follow lead further into the reading. They are there for the reader who wants to keep going down.
