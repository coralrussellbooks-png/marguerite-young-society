Section 3. How the References Were Found and Checked

← Previous · Intertextuality in MMMD Home · Next →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Titus Bonebreaker · Vera Cartwheel

Philosophy: Nietzsche · Plato and Platonism · William James and Pragmatism

Allusions: Ancient Egypt · Edgar Allan Poe · Romantic Poets · Shakespeare · The Bible

Chapters discussed: 65

Definition from research

The text. The novel was first published by Charles Scribner's Sons in 1965 and reissued in two volumes by Dalkey Archive Press in 1993. So every citation here gives a chapter number instead of a page. A reader holding any edition can find a passage by its chapter and a phrase from it.

Named references. Every capitalized name in the novel was reviewed by hand, about two thousand distinct forms in all. Each was sorted into one of three groups:

  • a reference to something outside the novel, such as a person, book, myth, song, place, or event;

  • an invented character or place;

  • an ordinary word that happened to begin a sentence.

Each reference was then searched through the full text, and every place it appears was recorded with its chapter and a line of surrounding text.

Unnamed allusions. Many of Young's borrowings do not name their source, so a second search looked for the wording of known sources: Bible verses, Shakespeare, Virgil, Coleridge, Poe, Franklin's maxims, hymns, and nursery rhymes. Each possible match was read in context before it was kept.

A reading pass then found allusions that no search would have caught. Two examples show the range:

  • At the old doctor's bedside, the novel slips in Othello's "men whose heads do grow beneath their shoulders" without naming the play (chapter 8).

  • When the Gospel salesman Mr. Bonebreaker warns that "there would be stink in Chicago on that Last Day" (chapter 65), he is borrowing the prophet Isaiah's promise that "instead of sweet smell there shall be stink."

Categories. After the essays were drafted, the categories were reviewed a second time. That review moved several texts into the optional category, once their anchors were found. These were Nietzsche, the Egyptian Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor, Paine and American pragmatism, the pastoral poems, and Plato's Symposium. For example, the novel names eternal recurrence outright: "Should not eternal recurrence be the one law of life" (chapter 42).

Carriers. Each reference was assigned to its carrier, the character whose voice or mind produces it. When one character reports another's visions, the one who imagines or speaks is the carrier. For example, Mr. Spitzer watches Catherine's imaginary guests, and those guests are counted as Catherine's references. Every literary, biblical, mythic, folk, musical, religious, and historical reference was read in context to settle its carrier, about 860 entries in all.

Quotations. Every quotation in this volume was checked word for word against the novel's text, and its chapter was confirmed. The results appear in Section 9.

Who carries the most. The finished index holds about two thousand entries, and the counts say something about the novel.

  • Mr. Spitzer carries the most references by far, about 735. His chapters hold the book's longest catalogs: butterflies with the names of gods, the names of God himself, and twin portraits in his brother's house.

  • Catherine Cartwheel carries about 360, most of them imaginary guests and dreamed travels.

  • Miss MacIntosh carries about 310.

  • Vera's own narration carries about 230.

The mix matters as much as the totals. Miss MacIntosh holds more biblical references than any other character, which fits a woman who drills genealogies and distrusts the Bible's stories at the same time. Scripture makes up nearly half of Mr. Bonebreaker's references, since he sells it for a living. It makes up more than a third of the bus-driver's, who uses it for oaths.

Where references cluster. The densest chapters are 65, 22, 54, and 29. Chapter 65 alone holds about 120 entries, most of them Mr. Bonebreaker's preaching and the story of Miss MacIntosh's past in Chicago. At the other extreme, chapters 25 and 75 hold almost none. The density follows the catalogs, not the plot: wherever a character begins to list, the references pour in.

Limits. Not every carrier could be settled by reading. Places, historical events, and objects of commerce were assigned by the chapter's point of view and the names nearest them, and those assignments should be treated as approximate. No search can find every allusion either, because some of Young's borrowings are echoes of rhythm or image with no shared wording.

Standards. This volume holds to a few plain rules:

  • Every quotation from the novel or any other source appears in quotation marks, with a chapter for the novel.

  • No reference is counted unless it was found in the text.

  • Connections a reader brings to the novel are welcome, but they are labeled as reader connections and kept apart from Young's own references.

The full index, with every entry, chapter, source, carrier, and category, is kept in a companion spreadsheet alongside this volume. It is built to grow. When a reader finds an allusion the index missed, it can be added as a new line, and the counts update on their own.
