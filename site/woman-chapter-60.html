Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 60

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Musician or gambler, the protagonist cannot say which he is, and the undecided self is this chapter's pillar. Music as transcendent and spiritual against the mundane obsession with gambling, the "elegiac mood" over slipping cards and decayed jockeys, is the fragility of a role and the fragility of identity itself, a self that will not settle into one calling. His ignorance of high society and finance, his indifference to whether "old ladies wear dresses of bone lace or bird-cages in paradise," carries the fading feminine decorum the book keeps noting, women defined by appearance and social ritual, and the "obituary columns" that "level" all classes and statuses are the dissolving of hierarchy the book welcomes, mortality the one force that unsorts what patriarchy sorts.

The moose call is the chapter's opening into another way of knowing. He calls and the moose answers, an "auditory hallucination" that crosses the line between natural and supernatural, and the answered call is voice and agency granted to a self outside societal roles, a recognition the ordinary world withholds. His limited musical knowledge set against the "basso profundo" of the moose and the "polysyllabic horns" of the wild ducks contrasts the formalized male-dominated art, the "musical composition," the "Stock Exchange" heard as a "long surf," with an untamed communal music, the non-hierarchical knowing the book keeps crediting to the feminine and the marginalized. His brother, dead and "confined to silence," marks the patriarchal limit, and the wild sounds are the diverse voices that resist classification the way the book's selves resist it.

The wild ducks give the chapter its crossing. He whispers to them and they respond, a "dialogic relationship" that defies patriarchal dominance and control, communication across the boundaries of species and worlds and states of being, and the crossing of those boundaries is the crossing point the book runs through gender, vulnerability and openness credited as strength rather than weakness. He stands still amid the chaos as a "fat gentleman," his stillness and trembling the precariousness and resilience of a marginalized self, and the lighthouse the birds dash against "as a star" is the danger that meets anyone seeking visibility and truth, the patriarchal gaze that blinds and destroys the ones drawn to its light.

The chapter ends attuned to the ducks as "exotic Pilgrims," "beautiful Asiatics," lightness and color and grace flying through "great clouds," and the fear of moving that keeps him still is the balance of presence and invisibility the book's women navigate, seen and unseen at once. His conclusion that his "elegy was this wild duck's horn" is the reclaiming of voice and narrative through connection with the natural and the marginalized, a music made outside the male tradition and the patriarchal record, an alternative legacy sounded on a wild horn where the composed elegy will not come. The undecided self finds its song not in the concert hall or the ledger but in the answered call of a creature that crosses every line the book means to dissolve.
