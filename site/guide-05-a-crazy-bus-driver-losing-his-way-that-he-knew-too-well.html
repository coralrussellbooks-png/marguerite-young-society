Chapter 5: A Crazy Bus-Driver Losing His Way That He Knew Too Well

← Chapter 4 · Guide Home · Chapter 6 →

Research notes: Chapter 5

Characters: Justice O’Leary · Miss MacIntosh · Moses Hunnecker · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Society, Class, and Race

Symbols: Hair, Wig, and Baldness

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Greek Mythology · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · Music and Composers · The Bible

Welcome back to the bus.

If you felt, in Chapters 3 and 4, that you had moved away from the bus and deep into Vera’s past, you were right. That is how this novel works. The bus is always there, waiting, while Vera’s memory does its long and spiraling work. And now, in Chapter 5, we return to it. Night has fallen. The roads are washed out. And Moses Hunnecker, bus driver, is holding forth.

Prepare yourself. Moses has a lot to say.

What actually happens in Chapter 5

It is nighttime on the bus. Moses Hunnecker is driving through flooded, broken roads in the dark: no stars, no signposts, the landscape dissolved into rain and mist. He is drinking. He is talking: to himself, to the old doctor, to whoever will listen.

He refuses to cut his hair. This is the chapter’s great fixed point: Moses’s hair, which has never been cut, which has grown to enormous proportions, which he treats as his last and most personal possession. He will not let anyone touch it. Not the barber. Not the Democrats. Not anyone.

He rails against the Democrats and the New Deal, against FDR, against what he sees as the government’s steady encroachment on his freedom. He is a lifelong Republican. He has never voted, he never has time, but his politics are ferocious.

He talks about the old doctor, Justice O’Leary, who sits among the passengers in a state of advanced dementia, imagining he is still making house calls, still delivering babies, his rusted forceps and broken stethoscope still tools of a living practice. Moses alternates between mockery and a kind of rough tenderness toward him.

And then, quietly, the chapter asks a question it does not answer: is Moses actually driving this bus? Or is he dreaming it? Is any of this happening?

What Vera is thinking about

Vera watches all of this with her characteristic mixture of empathy and detachment. She is the observer, the recorder, the consciousness through which we receive everything, but she does not intervene. She listens to Moses and understands, on some level, that he is caught in the same kind of loop she is: a man driving a road he knows too well, going somewhere he may never arrive, in a landscape that has stopped making sense.

There is a line Young gives us early in the chapter: “a crazy bus-driver losing his way that he knew too well.” Sit with that. You can know a road so well that you lose it, know it so well that it stops being a road and becomes something else entirely, a groove worn into habit, a dream of motion without destination.

This is Vera’s condition too. She has been circling Miss MacIntosh’s absence for so long that the circling has become its own kind of journey. Moses is her mirror in this chapter, whether he knows it or not.

Moses and his hair

The hair needs its own explanation, because Young gives it so much weight and it might otherwise seem merely eccentric.

Moses has never cut his hair. It has grown to extraordinary length and, in his telling, has taken on a life of its own, full of “burning eyes like the eyes of Argus,” twisting and growing like something alive. He talks about it the way other men talk about their souls. It is his “only virtue.” His “angel.” The one thing about himself that no one has managed to take from him.

This sounds strange until you understand the context. Moses is a man who feels that everything else has been stripped away: his country is declining, the government is encroaching, the roads are washed out, the old certainties are gone. His hair is the last thing that is purely, irreducibly his. No barber. No government. No tax on it.

There is a long tradition, across many cultures, of hair carrying the self: its strength, its identity, its spiritual power. The Biblical Samson is the most famous example: his hair was the source of his power, and when it was cut, he was undone. Moses (and Young chose this name deliberately) is a version of that story: a prophet-figure driving through the wilderness, his identity intact in his uncut hair, raging against the forces that would diminish him.

The novel also makes a quietly darkly comic point: hair and nails do not actually continue to grow after death. It only looks that way, the skin dehydrates and pulls back. If Moses is, as the chapter hints, possibly dead or dreaming rather than truly driving, his hair’s continued growth is itself a kind of illusion. The last thing he believes is real might not be real either.

The old doctor, and the ghosts he carries

Justice O’Leary has been on this bus since Chapter 1, and now we understand him better. He was once a country doctor who made house calls, who delivered babies, who was a figure of authority and care in his community. Now he is old and lost, his mind running on rails that no longer connect to the present world. He thinks he is still practicing. He sees patients who are not there. His instruments are rusted, his stethoscope broken.

Moses is not entirely wrong to mock him. But watch how Young holds both things at once: the doctor is deluded, yes, but his delusion is that he is still helping people, still bringing life into the world. Of all the delusions available to a person, this one has a certain dignity. He has not retreated into pleasure or comfort or oblivion. He has retreated into service, even if the service is imaginary.

The pregnant women he tends, his “patients”, are ghosts. Their cries and their suffering are echoes of births that may never happen, futures that may never arrive. Young is showing us a world in which the business of bringing new life into being has become spectral, uncertain, haunted.

Is any of this real?

Chapter 5 does something the earlier chapters have gestured toward but not said outright: it openly questions whether Moses is actually driving the bus or dreaming the whole thing from his four-poster bed.

Young never resolves this. She is not going to. The bus is real and the bus is a dream and the bus is a passage between worlds and all of these are true simultaneously. Moses himself wonders if he has died and is continuing out of habit, driving the same road because he doesn’t know how to stop.

This is not a puzzle you need to solve. It is a condition you need to inhabit. The novel is asking you to hold uncertainty the way its characters hold it, not as a problem to be fixed, but as the weather you’re moving through.

What matters is that Vera is on the bus. Whatever the bus is (real, dream, passage) she is in it, moving forward, searching for something that may or may not be findable.

The political weather

Moses’s tirades against the Democrats and the New Deal might seem like local color, but they are doing something more precise. Young is placing us in a specific American moment: the 1930s and ‘40s, the era of FDR, when the question of what the government owed its citizens was being fought over furiously. Moses represents one side of that argument: the man who sees government help as government control, who feels the state reaching into his last freedoms and taxing even his hair.

You do not have to agree with Moses to understand him. Young doesn’t ask you to agree. She asks you to feel the genuine fear underneath the bluster: the fear of a man who has watched his country change in ways he didn’t choose, who has no power over any of it, and who has found the one thing, his hair, his body, his self, that he can still refuse to surrender.

That fear is real. It has not gone away. Look around.

What to watch for as you keep reading

The four passengers named Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John. Young names four passengers on the bus after the four Evangelists, the authors of the four Gospels. They appear quietly here, but they give the bus journey a spiritual dimension that will deepen as the novel continues. Moses is not just a bus driver on a midwestern road. He is a prophet-figure navigating a passage. Notice who is bearing witness to the journey.

The folk songs. Moses sings “Go Tell Aunt Rhody” (the old gray goose is dead) and “The Old Gray Mare.” Both are songs about something vital that has died or declined. Young is not being subtle here. The dead country Moses is driving through is America or a version of it, an America that has stopped being what it was supposed to be.

Wormwood, boxwood, cedar. Three woods mentioned in the chapter, each carrying Biblical weight: cedar for sacred endurance, boxwood for divine provision, wormwood for bitterness and judgment. When Young reaches for this kind of language, she is building a register of meaning that accumulates slowly. Don’t worry about tracking every reference. Just notice that the landscape has this kind of depth.

The question of whether Moses is dead. Hold it. Don’t try to answer it. Let the novel show you what it wants to show you.

What to hold onto

Chapter 5 is wild and funny and dark and a little operatic with Moses raging about his hair and his politics in the rainy dark while an old doctor tends to invisible patients. It can feel like a departure from the intimacy of Vera’s grief in earlier chapters.

But here is the thread: Vera is watching a man who, like her, has refused to let go of something. Moses will not cut his hair. Vera will not give up on Miss MacIntosh. Both of them are holding onto the one thing that still feels like themselves, in a landscape that has done its best to wash everything away.

They are more alike than either of them knows. This bus is exactly the right place for both of them.
