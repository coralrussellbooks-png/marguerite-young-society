Chapter 1

Listed as: Miss Georgia MacIntosh

  • Vera’s lost companion and moral anchor, embodying simplicity, truth, and steadfastness.

Chapter 3

Listed as: Miss (Georgia) MacIntosh

  • A nursemaid who embodies practicality, common sense, and moral certainty.
  • Described physically with "marcelled red hair," "greyish, greenish eyes," and a robust constitution.
  • She is a symbol of stability, reality, and groundedness amidst chaos and confusion.
  • Rejects illusions, fantasies, and idle dreams; values labor, discipline, and the present moment.
  • Wears simple, durable clothing; disdains luxury and extravagance.
  • Has a brusque, rough demeanor, often compared to an old sailor.
  • She is industrious, always busy, and advocates physical exercise and self-forgetfulness.
  • Holds a strict moral code, emphasizing honesty, self-control, and acceptance of life's hardships.
  • Originates from What Cheer, Iowa, a simple town, and contrasts sharply with the opulent, dreamlike environment of the household.
  • Maintains distance from others, avoids intimacy, and does not indulge in self-pity.
  • Ultimately disappears under mysterious circumstances, leaving behind only her clothes and belongings on the beach.
  • Mentions she has a brother Richard, who disappeared while being a missionary.

Chapter 4

  • The lost love, symbolizing a "normal life" and idealized domesticity.
  • Her disappearance is mysterious and traumatic.
  • Associated with innocence and unattainable dreams.

Chapter 9

  • Vera’s guardian, strict and practical, embodying discipline and austere values.
  • Holds strong beliefs about women’s roles, marriage, and responsibility.
  • Comes from a harsh, unprivileged background in What Cheer, Iowa.
  • Acts as a maternal figure but also as a controlling authority.
  • Is caring yet unyielding, knowledgeable yet evasive.

Chapter 10

  • An older woman, practical and resigned to a life without love or marriage.
  • She is portrayed as colorless, ordinary in appearance, yet capable and domestically inclined.
  • In the dream, she assumes the role of bridegroom, further blurring gender and identity.

Chapter 11

  • Her denial of “those things which did not exist” is the lesson Vera leans on to tell dream from reality.

Chapter 12

  • The practical, no-nonsense caretaker who keeps the "Old Reliable" clock.
  • Represents pragmatism, moral clarity, and a grounded worldview.
  • Cautions against illusion and flight of fancy; emphasizes duty and reality.
  • Her perspective contrasts with Vera’s imaginative nature.
  • “The most beautiful ladies of history had been noted for their hair”: Queen Berenice gave her hair to the gods for her husband’s safe return, and it was set among the stars as Coma Berenices.
  • “the first vision would also be the last”: Foreshadowing of Chapter 17, when Vera finds Miss MacIntosh bald.
  • “her lips turned blue and her skin lost its natural color, turning to saffron”: Blue lips and short breath are signs of a failing heart or lungs, a warning Vera does not read.
  • Her short breath, blue lips, and stone-grey skin are signs Vera does not yet read.
  • She threatens to retire, taps Vera with a silver thimble carved “Juliet, 1899,” and saves soap coupons “to exchange for crockery.”

Chapter 13

  • Miss MacIntosh, once a fixed star in Vera’s life, becomes like a "perfect stranger."
  • “a perfect stranger I had lived with for seven years”: “From my seventh to my fourteenth birthday” (Chapter 9).
  • Lips blue and face “pale and twisted with unuttered pain,” she walked through cloudbursts on her constitutional.
  • Her rote questions: “Where is North Dakota, and what is a monsoon, and what is a mirage, Vera Cartwheel?”

Chapter 13 · Miss MacIntosh

  • Initially a symbol of stability and ordinariness, she transforms into a mysterious figure, representing the unknowability of others and the fragile nature of relationships.

Chapter 15

  • Represents the adult world’s order, prudence, and caution, but also coldness and emotional distance.
  • She is methodical, practical, and somewhat resigned, embodying a worldview that emphasizes survival and control over passion or mystery.
  • Her repeated warnings about danger and death show concern but also a limiting worldview.
  • Her peculiar habits suggest underlying fragility or unresolved fears.
  • “She could not swim a stroke”: Foreshadowing of Miss MacIntosh’s death in the sea.
  • She cannot swim, keeps her windows open in winter, and sleeps in a bare room under a harp-framed bed with “no angels to play these harps.”
  • She answers every personal question with a date, such as “the sinking of the Titanic.”

Chapter 16

  • Miss MacIntosh is her caretaker, described as an “old witch” by Vera, who holds a controlling influence over Vera’s life.
  • “Miss MacIntosh was an old witch who kept me enchained in a dark tower”: Rapunzel, locked in a tower by a witch.
  • Her door, “usually locked and barred from the inside,” opens of itself, and she stands by the window, “greatly changed… unbelievably not herself.”

Chapter 17

  • Initially described as a familiar and trusted figure, a nursemaid, a guardian.
  • Transforms into a bald, expressionless, ghostly figure, described as “bald as the egg or as the rock where nothing grows,” symbolizing loss, change, or an unsettling void.
  • Portrayed with contradictory imagery: both fragile and monstrously strong, spectral yet physically violent.
  • Vera struggles to reconcile this apparition with the Miss MacIntosh she knew, suspecting deception and a hidden identity.
  • Symbolizes a “bald pretender,” a false self, or a shadowy force challenging Vera.
  • “she was bald. She was bald as the egg”: Alopecia, the total loss of hair, was popularly blamed on “some sudden shock or amazement or illness.” The hair passage of Chapter 12 prepared for this, and in Chapter 19 Vera finds Miss MacIntosh’s red wig.
  • “I knew her by her broken nose”: The broken nose from the Spokane bus.
  • “this old man who had only one breast”: Halsted’s radical mastectomy (1894), which removed the breast and the muscles beneath it, was the standard treatment for breast cancer for most of the twentieth century. Chapter 19 confirms the loss. In Chapter 10, Vera imagined “claws upon her forehead like the claws of a lobster,” and cancer takes its name from the crab.
  • She has “only one breast,” and Vera knows her only “by her broken nose” from the Spokane bus (Chapter 9).

Chapter 18

  • Initially perceived as a plain, red-headed nursemaid.
  • Transforms into a bald, frightening figure symbolizing raw truth and loss.
  • Acts as a guide or interlocutor, mixing sternness with intimacy.
  • Represents both nurturing and harsh realities of life, death, and change.
  • Embodies themes of charity, mortality, and the limits of human understanding.
  • “Who was that old rapist if not Miss MacIntosh”: Imagery in this chapter hints that either physically, emotionally, or psychologically a rape took place. Vera because of her age and experience does not have the words/vocabulary to fully explain what has happened to her.
  • “The left breast is already gone”: Miss MacIntosh talks about losing her left breast. A mastectomy appears in history as far back as 548 AD. In the United States the first mastectomy was performed in 1882. There is no clear record of how the women survived or for how long after the operation. Halsted’s radical mastectomy was described in 1894.
  • “the heavy, claw-shaped mark of her hand upon my forehead”: In Chapter 10, Vera imagined “claws upon her forehead like the claws of a lobster.” Now the claw marks Vera.
  • “that hers was that knob where the breast was missing”: In the AMC series Dietland (2018), based on Sarai Walker’s novel, the character Julia (Tamara Tunie) reveals to Plum that she has had a double mastectomy, after a life of wearing “a mask,” as Tunie put it: “the hair, the makeup, the clothes.”
  • Her red wig hangs on the chair post, and she mourns the left breast “already gone.”
  • She threatens to kill Vera if she tells, then says she lost her hair in Vera’s service.

Chapter 19

  • Bald head cold as marble, veined limbs, a missing breast (amputated), thin and ascetic face without any facial hair, red wig resembling a tropical bird, gnarled hands.
  • Wears a baseball cap or old caps in the morning and a green ribbon in the evenings.
  • Always carries a whistle around her neck.
  • Wears a veil-tied hat outdoors, sometimes layered with heavy veiling.
  • Carries an old black umbrella indoors and outdoors, like a shield.
  • Sleeps lightly, alert to the slightest noise.
  • Has a strong Christian faith, critical of gambling and superstition.
  • Exhibits physical and emotional contradictions: shifting skin color, blushing for no apparent reason, and trying to resist external forces (“buck[ing] against the broncho wind”).
  • The playing cards, especially the “deuce of clubs,” symbolize fate, death, or a trial by tears, despite her rejection of gambling.
  • Her patched clothing and worn items reflect hardship and resilience.

Chapter 20

  • A figure of strength and common sense, yet marked by profound physical and existential deprivation.
  • Described as “hale and hearty, a red-headed nursemaid” in the past, yet now revealed in a state of vulnerability and loss.
  • Her condition is a mystery to Vera, who struggles to reconcile her external robustness with the internal and physical ravages revealed by the light.
  • She embodies the tension between resilience and suffering, denial and revelation.

Chapter 21

  • Portrayed as a strong yet vulnerable figure, marked physically by baldness, scars, and loss, which symbolize deeper suffering.
  • Despite her condition, she maintains a robust and kind demeanor, showing resilience. She is depicted with vivid details like her red hair, green ribbon, and practical attire.
  • She embodies the tension between appearance and inner reality, strength and vulnerability.

Chapter 22

  • A forthright, practical woman with a strong sense of duty, she is Vera’s caretaker and disciplinarian.
  • She is resistant to illusions and ghosts, skeptical of sentimentality, and focused on maintaining order.
  • Despite her harshness, she harbors her own doubts and vulnerabilities, reflected in her philosophical musings and physical frailty.

Chapter 23

  • Miss MacIntosh is portrayed as fiercely grounded in what she calls "common sense," valuing the "middle brow," the "middle way," and the "average life", a disposition she prides herself on as her "greatest love," even if others despise it.
  • This common sense coexists with a pervasive skepticism toward accepted truths such as history, geography, and law, which she dismisses as unstable, uncertain, or outright falsehoods.
  • She challenges grand narratives, like that of Caesar crossing the Rubicon or the veracity of historical figures, suggesting that time has neither accumulated nor taught wisdom.
  • This skeptical realism reveals a character who questions the foundations of knowledge and human pretensions but does so without despair; rather, she seems to accept human limitations with a certain weary humor and irony.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s discourse centers on her criticism of modern inventions, especially electric lights. She associates electricity, notably Benjamin Franklin’s experiments, as a disruptive force that has destroyed natural rhythms, privacy, and the quietness of night. She laments electric lights for erasing darkness, which she sees as essential for sleep, privacy, and even mystery in human relationships, particularly marriage. Electric lights, she argues, expose flaws and imperfections that were once safely hidden in darkness, thereby damaging marriages and social harmony.
  • Her detailed critique extends to "electric trains," "electric irons," "fluorescent lamps," and "two-way lighting systems" as unnatural intrusions into life’s natural order.
  • This stance encapsulates her resistance to tampering with "old, damaged creation" and her preference for simpler, older ways such as "bayberry candles," "snuffers," and natural starlight. Her view is that the modern world’s brightness is a form of extravagant excess that impoverishes human experience and dignity.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s practical nature is emphasized by descriptions of her appearance and habits. She dresses plainly, in worn, practical clothing: a grey baseball sweater with a ravelling sleeve, stout shoes, and a patched salt-and-pepper tweed coat.
  • She carries her "rigging" everywhere, including an old black umbrella likened to a sail and a whale-boned ice-breaker corset, symbolizing her preparedness and no-nonsense approach to life on a “rough shore.”
  • She rejects frivolities such as filmy dresses or adornments, even on significant occasions like weddings or funerals, signaling her seriousness and focus on the immediate, tangible world rather than on dreams or illusions.
  • Despite her talk of cosmic and remote issues like glaciers, submerged continents, and the first days of creation, Miss MacIntosh reveals little of her personal emotions or vulnerabilities.
  • She claims her consciousness ceases during sleep and that she cannot remember dreams, not even her own, suggesting a detachment from inner emotional life or a guardedness.
  • Her conscience "kept guard" as a "lonely vigil," indicating solitude and perhaps a self-imposed emotional isolation.
  • She evades personal disclosures and remains impassive, almost austere, in her demeanor.
  • This emotional reserve contrasts with the vastness of the cosmic themes she invokes, highlighting a tension between the grand and the personal.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s reflections often shift from the mundane to the cosmic and from daily life and societal critiques to the “first starfish upon the first beach” and “the first moon drawing the first tide.” This oscillation suggests that she perceives human life as part of a vast, impersonal natural order that transcends immediate existence.
  • She uses cosmic imagery to mask what she does not want to reveal: her fears, losses, or inner life.
  • This duality makes her a somewhat enigmatic figure, connecting the earthly and the universal while remaining personally elusive.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s thoughts about the coming winter, the colorlessness of the world under snow and ice, and the need to prepare with warm clothing and supplies reveal a keen awareness of mortality and the cycles of life.
  • Winter symbolizes death or hardship, and her sober preparation indicates acceptance rather than fear. Yet, she also acknowledges the subtle beauty of winter’s muted colors, reflecting a nuanced appreciation of life’s phases and transitions.
  • Her concern with time’s passing, the loss inherent in change, and the persistence of “dimmed colors” in hardship underscore a mature, grounded philosophy.
  • Throughout her discourse, Miss MacIntosh repeatedly condemns human vanity, illusions of grandeur, and false hopes.
  • She refers to "vain man" in multiple historical and contemporary contexts, from Emperor Constantine and Caesar to Benjamin Franklin, pointing out the futility of human pretensions to immortality or mastery.
  • She warns against illusions, falsehoods that may mask other truths, and the human tendency to stumble blindly in the "broad daylight which God has lighted."
  • Her repeated invocation of vanity and illusion underscores a theme of human limitation and the importance of humility and realism.

Chapter 24

  • Practical, grounded, and coarse-grained, she offers contrast to Catherine and the servants’ fantastical world.
  • She is a companion to Vera and represents the voice of reality amidst chaos.

Chapter 63

  • Miss MacIntosh is described as the "Rock of Ages" in a crumbling world, a symbol of stability, clarity, and common sense.
  • She embodies straightforwardness, rejecting illusions and fantasies, and insists on living within a "framework" of reality.
  • Her physical appearance symbolizes her internal struggle: her ruddy hair like a "sunset burning with bees," a broken nose, and eventually revealed baldness, all reflecting her life's hardships and resilience.
  • The black umbrella she carries is a protective shield, symbolizing her defense against the "fog" of confusion, dreams, and illusions.
  • Miss MacIntosh is a moral mentor and nursemaid figure who values practicality over imagination.
  • She is skeptical of dreams, illusions, and modern distractions, such as electric lights, which she believes disturb natural life rhythms and privacy.
  • She is combative and sharp, not hesitating to scold even divine figures like Emperor Constantine or God Himself for what she sees as excess or failure.
  • Despite her stern exterior, she has a deep, incorruptible sense of humor and reveals moments of vulnerability, especially when discussing her baldness and personal losses.

Chapter 64

  • The central figure of the chapter, an orphanage matron who has suffered lifelong baldness.
  • She is portrayed as a strong yet vulnerable woman, bearing the weight of loneliness and social alienation.
  • Her baldness is both a physical condition and a metaphor for her isolation and difference from others.
  • Miss MacIntosh evolves from a figure shrouded in mystery to a deeply human character marked by resilience and vulnerability.
  • Her storytelling reveals layers of her personality, from pride and courage to sadness and regret.

Chapter 65

  • A woman of many moods and contradictions, both physically and emotionally marked by her ailments.
  • She is bald, missing a breast, and wears wigs to mask her reality.
  • Profoundly lonely and marked by a sense of loss, she is simultaneously resigned and hopeful.
  • She embodies a "pilgrim’s progress from nowhere to nowhere," with a simple, austere view of life and death.
  • Her internal conflict centers on her past, her physical condition, and her tenuous relationship with faith and society.
  • She is portrayed as both vulnerable and strong, practical yet mystical.

Chapter 66

  • A strong, eccentric woman who dresses for storms and long voyages, symbolizing her preparedness for life's harshness. She is bald, always wearing a red wig, which is a hidden truth only discovered later. Her voice is booming and manly, and she exhibits contradictory traits: a resolute socialist with disdain for wealth and extravagance but also an astute student of finance.
  • Represents resilience, the burden of loneliness, and the struggle for self-identity. Her baldness symbolizes vulnerability and a unique form of naked truth.
  • She harbors dreams of love, adventure, and escape from societal norms, but also suffers from deep isolation and self-awareness of her physical and emotional amputations.
  • Acts as a strict, demanding teacher who pushes Vera to learn relentlessly, even amid storms and symbolic death.
