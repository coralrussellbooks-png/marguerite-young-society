Chapter Seven: Young's Horizontal Lady

← Chapter Six: The Medieval Wheel · Radical Immanence Collection · Chapter Eight: The Turn →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Marguerite Young spent eighteen years on the horizontal axis.

This is not a criticism. The horizontal axis is where the ten pillars live. It is where the plural self spreads its full width, where the sedimentary layers accumulate, where the permanent weather of grief moves through the atmospheric conditions of a life that refuses to resolve. The horizontal axis is where Miss MacIntosh, My Darling was built, page by page, sentence by sentence, the long recursive construction of a philosophical system that chose fiction as its only honest form.

But Young knew about the vertical. The novel is reaching toward it in every sentence that circles back on itself, every image that returns with the weight of all its previous appearances pressing down on the new encounter. The wig on the back of the crab is not only a horizontal image, a lateral spread of symbol and meaning across the surface of the text. It is also a vertical one: the weight of everything the wig has meant, all the layers of Miss MacIntosh's constructed solidity and Vera's childhood faith and the collapse of the opposition between Catherine's illusion and the solid world, pressing down through the image into the sediment of the novel, depositing something that will be in the ground of every subsequent reading.

Young's sentences are crossing points. They are the places where the horizontal spread of the plural self meets the vertical line of transmission, where the accumulation of the present moment is intersected by the weight of what came before and the weight of what will come after. The sentences that circle back and refuse to finish are sentences that are trying to hold both axes at once, to stay in the horizontal spread of the present moment while feeling the pull of the vertical line.

The novel never arrives at What Cheer. But it arrives, repeatedly, at the crossing point. It arrives at the place where Vera is most fully herself: not moving toward a destination, not accumulating toward a resolution, but standing at the intersection of everything she has received and everything she will deposit, holding the weight in both directions, witnessing without concluding.

The Wheel of Fortune turn in Young's work is the moment when a character who has been resting in the horizontal spread of consciousness is rotated into the vertical line of transmission. Miss MacIntosh's turn is the most dramatic: the wig comes off, the horizontal accumulation of her constructed solidity collapses, and she is suddenly visible as a vertical figure, a person constituted by what she received and what she deposited, a carrier rather than a destination. The solid world she represented was not solid. It was alluvial. It was made of what was carried to her and deposited in her and carried forward by her, and the wig was the pretense that the carrying had stopped, that she had arrived somewhere stable rather than being, like everyone else, in the middle of the transmission.

Catherine's turn is subtler and more philosophically interesting. Catherine is already on the vertical axis in a sense: her opium world is a world in which the horizontal accumulation of consensus reality has been suspended and the vertical line of what she has received and what she is depositing is more visible. Her interior world is not a retreat from the transmission. It is a different orientation within it, a way of being at the crossing point that the horizontal world cannot process and therefore pathologizes.

Young does not pathologize it. Young witnesses it with the same density and care she gives the Indiana plains. In doing so she is enacting the eleventh pillar before the eleventh pillar had a name: she is treating the vertical orientation as a legitimate way of being at the crossing point, not as a failure of the horizontal but as a different relationship to the same structure.

The Grey Goose bus is the crossing point made mobile. It is moving through the horizontal landscape of Indiana while carrying its passengers along the vertical line of their individual transmissions, each one oriented differently, each one at a different point in the rotation, each one carrying a different weight in a different configuration. The bus is not going to What Cheer in any simple sense. It is going to the crossing point, which is always already here, which is where the passengers already are, which is where the novel was from the first sentence.
