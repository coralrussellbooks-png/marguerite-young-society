Chapter 14: The Black Coachman

← Chapter 13 · Guide Home · Chapter 15 →

Research notes: Chapter 14

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Symbols: Horses and Carriages

Philosophy: Astrology, Alchemy, and Esoterica

Allusions: Gambling and Playing Cards · Suicide in History and Literature · The History of Opium

Chapter 14 is the longest chapter we’ve encountered so far, and the most demanding. It asks you to sit inside two minds at once (Catherine’s hallucinatory one and Mr. Spitzer’s bewildered, sorrowful one) for a very long time, as they talk past each other, toward each other, and sometimes through each other in the dark.

If you find yourself losing the thread, that’s fine. You’re supposed to. Catherine loses the thread too. So does Mr. Spitzer. The chapter is partly about what happens to conversation when one person is tethered to reality by the thinnest cord and the other is barely tethered at all.

Stay with it. By the end you’ll know things about Mr. Spitzer that will change how you read him for the rest of the novel.

What actually happens in Chapter 14

Vera is still wandering the estate grounds, and the chapter begins at “the grave of the black coachman”: the old family servant who drove Catherine’s father’s carriage. From there, the chapter pivots into a long reconstruction of Catherine’s obsession with locating the black coachman, her endless interrogations of Mr. Spitzer about where he was buried, what happened to the four white horses, whether her instructions for his funeral were properly carried out.

Around and beneath this obsession, the chapter slowly delivers what we’ve been circling since the novel began: Mr. Spitzer’s brother fell from a tall building. It was not an accident. His brother had a suicidal streak. Mr. Spitzer has been living in the aftermath of that death ever since: wearing his brother’s cufflinks, finding his brother’s cigarette still warm in his coat pocket, being mistaken for his brother on city streets, sometimes not correcting the mistake.

The chapter ends with Catherine still talking after Mr. Spitzer has quietly left the room, addressing his empty chair, unable to register his absence. She will talk until the stars fade and sleep takes her. The night, she says, is the only light.

The black coachman, and why Catherine cannot let him go

The black coachman is not a person Catherine knew intimately. He was a servant: the man who drove the carriage, who knew the roads and the horses and the directions of the winds. He survived everyone in the family, just by a shade. Catherine had arranged, years ago, that he should be buried by the sea with “the carriage and the four white horses,” in his livery and high silk hat, so that he could go on driving under the earth.

Why does this matter so much to her? Young gives us the answer in a single sentence: “If she could find him, she could perhaps find herself, the bewildered passenger.”

The black coachman is Catherine’s figure for orientation. He is the one who knew which way to go, which road to take, how to bring the carriage safely around the long avenue. In a life that has dissolved entirely into hallucination (where even Mr. Spitzer’s reality is uncertain, where time has stopped on the bedside clocks, where the guests are all dead or imaginary) the black coachman is the last possible landmark. Find him, and the carriage stops spinning. Find him, and you know which city you’re in.

She cannot find him. Mr. Spitzer maintains his silence. So she keeps asking, evening after evening, year after year, circling the question the way a carriage circles an avenue with no end.

What we learn about Mr. Spitzer’s brother

Chapter 14 is where Young finally lays out, in full, the story of the twin brothers.

Peron, the brother, was the quick one, the brilliant one, the gambler. He moved in racing circles and shady company. He kept a double life that Mr. Spitzer is still discovering, years later, when strangers on city streets call out to him with debts to settle or old love affairs to resume. He was capable, Young tells us, “of far greater variations on one theme than” Mr. Spitzer, who is cautious and slow and literal-minded and has, in his own assessment, led “the most retired, the most unassuming life imaginable.”

Peron fell from a tall building. There is almost no doubt in Mr. Spitzer’s mind that it was deliberate. He had a suicidal streak. The day of his death was, according to the dream books Mr. Spitzer has been secretly consulting, a lucky day: a great day for new adventures, “an excellent day for beginning a new relationship or ending an old love.” It was, for Mr. Spitzer, the unluckiest day of his life.

And here is the thing Young gives us quietly, almost in passing: Mr. Spitzer had dreamed, long before his brother’s death, that he himself had fallen. He had climbed a rope toward heaven, heard silver trumpets and the fading voices of violins, and then lost his footing and fallen. He woke. He was fine. He was still sitting at Catherine’s bedside, in plain view. And yet; something of Mr. Spitzer died when his beloved brother was buried, he will say later. The clods of earth fell on that other face, and they fell on something in Mr. Spitzer too.

He has been wearing his brother’s cufflinks, double horses’ heads carved in mother-of-pearl, to his brother’s funeral and every day since. He carries his brother’s ivory-headed cane. The cigarette still warm in his coat pocket. The playing card dropping from the sleeve of his brother’s purple dressing gown. These are not supernatural occurrences. They are the texture of grief that has nowhere to go.

The case of Spitzer versus Spitzer

Catherine, in one of her sharpest moments in the chapter, names what has consumed Mr. Spitzer’s entire adult life: “the case of Spitzer versus Spitzer.”

He is both the defense and the accusation. He is the lawyer who has outlived the man he should have been, or who has lived the life the other was supposed to live, and he cannot settle the case. He does not know which brother survived and which one died. He does not know if the thoughts he thinks are his own or his brother’s. He finds things in his pockets that are not his. He is recognized on streets he has never walked. He sometimes does not correct people who call him by his brother’s name, and in not correcting them, he lets his brother live a little longer.

Which is shadow and which is man? Catherine asks this, and it is not a hallucination. It is the most accurate question anyone has ever posed to Mr. Spitzer, and he has no answer.

Catherine as the unexpected authority

One of the most interesting things Young does in this chapter is establish Catherine (the opium addict, the hallucinatory invalid, the woman who has not left her bed in decades) as the person who most clearly sees Mr. Spitzer.

It is Mr. Spitzer who walks about in the world, but it is “my mother who knew, through her absence from the world, the world.”

She knows he has been reading dream books in poor neighborhoods. She knows he sometimes wears another’s high silk hat by mistake and doesn’t realize it’s his own. She knows that his music stopped before his brother died, not after, even though Mr. Spitzer has been telling himself and everyone else that the death was the cause. She knows “he placed a bet on a dead horse” at a parimutuel window without knowing what he was doing. She knows, in Catherine’s precise and devastating phrase, that he is the only living person she has ever found it necessary to doubt, because everyone else in her life is imaginary, and Mr. Spitzer is stubbornly, confusingly real.

The person most disconnected from ordinary reality is the one who sees the real man most clearly. This is one of Young’s great recurring propositions, and Chapter 14 is its fullest demonstration.

What to watch for as you keep reading

Light spoilers: This section hints at what happens later in the novel. Skip it if you want to meet the story fresh.

The black coachman will not be found. That’s not what the search is for. The search is Catherine’s way of insisting that something in her life was once oriented, was once moving purposefully through the world in a known direction. The carriage keeps circling because there is no destination, but there was a coachman, and he knew the roads.

Mr. Spitzer’s relationship to his brother will deepen and complicate across the entire novel. What Young has established here is the basic grammar of it: one quick, one slow; one dead, one surviving; one who took the last chance, one who is still waiting. Every time you see Mr. Spitzer hesitate, fumble, arrive too late, maintain his silence, remember this chapter.

Notice that Catherine sets a place at the table for the dead. She says this plainly, as a domestic fact: the table is “always laid for twelve or thirteen or fourteen guests,” and there is “a place set for the dead.” This is one of the most quietly extraordinary sentences in a novel full of them. She is not being morbid. She is being hospitable. In Catherine’s world, the dead are expected.
