Exploring Young's Poetry

The Thesis Tested

← Poems That Bridge Pillars · Exploring Young's Poetry · The Vision At Its Most Concentrated →

The early poems were the honest test, and the result holds with a real and useful qualification. The felt principles are there from the start. The Sacred Is Here, Grief Doesn't End, The Margins Reveal the Truth, Not One Self, and Language Shapes What's Possible all appear in the 1927 to 1934 work, including "I Heard a Bird," written at nine, and "Worship," a teenager's creed that finds God "In stars and flowers and shining eyes" and nowhere near a church. That a nine-year-old and a teenager were already doing this is the strongest possible evidence for Young's claim that her preoccupations were fixed by the age of nine.

What is not there early is just as telling. The Dubious Ground philosophy, the metaphysics of perception and the constructed real, barely registers in the early poems and then floods the Middle Years, where "Snow in the Mountains," "And even the wild goat of Ephesus," and "But where come the innuendos" think the problem out loud for the first time. The Crossing Point, the political and war principle, arrives with the war years. By analyzing Young’s poems the emotional core was innate and the intellectual and political apparatus grew onto it. The vision is one, but it was felt before it was argued.
