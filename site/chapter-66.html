To All My Darlings

Chapter 66

On Chapter 66 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 12, 2026

← Chapter 65 · Home · Chapter 67 →

Summary

Through a winter storm, Miss MacIntosh, dressed like a whaler, fires questions at Vera about the life of the eel, the calendar, the North Pole, and the states adjoining Kansas. In her last month she tells Vera her secrets: that her ovaries were removed when she was young; that on her vacations in New York she made an imaginary fortune at the Stock Exchange, for which she was sure Vera’s mother had her followed by a Pinkerton; and that there she met a wooden-legged Danish explorer who begged her to give him her money and come with him to the North Pole, then vanished without leaving his name. Vera wonders whether everyone, from Lorena to her own mother, always knew that Miss MacIntosh was bald. The chapter closes on her walk into the sea at dawn, her clothes and wig strewn on the beach, and Vera’s elegy for her, while old Friday barks by the waves.

Key passages

From the research

  • At the outset, Miss MacIntosh is described as dressed for a long voyage (whaler’s hat, sea boots) ready for protection amid a storm, though she insists they are not going anywhere. Her booming voice and strong presence contrast with the child narrator’s perception of her eccentric normalcy. She expresses pity for fishermen, sailors, fishes, and even stars, acknowledging nature’s cruelty toward the weak. This passage sets a tone of resilience, foreboding, and her deep connection with nature’s harshness.
  • Despite the storm and darkness, Miss MacIntosh insists on continuing lessons, asking questions about the heart, geography, history, and more. She shows impatience but also a dedication to education. Vera later reflects that these lessons were far from orthodox and took on new significance after Miss MacIntosh’s disappearance.
  • A turning point is the revelation that Miss MacIntosh was bald all her life, wearing a red wig that Vera had not noticed before. Her loneliness is profound; she loathes children despite being a nursemaid, yearns for adult companionship, and feels disconnected even in bustling cities like New York.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s interactions with a nameless wooden-legged Arctic explorer at the Stock Exchange are important. She admires Captain Cook, debates the discovery of the North Pole, and contemplates socialism and finance. The explorer’s persistent proposals to take her to the Arctic, contrasted with her preoccupation with finance and her own cautious independence, reveal two isolated souls longing for escape and meaning.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s reflections about her amputations (physical and metaphorical), her lost femininity (due to baldness and removed ovaries), and her unfulfilled love life deepen the sense of tragedy. She contrasts her fate with old traditions and societal expectations of women, expressing a complex relationship with identity and desire. She hopes for a world without colonialism, extravagance, and selfishness.
  • The chapter culminates with a haunting depiction of her disappearance: her clothes strewn on the beach, the red wig torn, and only remnants left behind. Vera contemplates the metaphor of amputated feet walking on their own, the sea as her coffin, and the endless cycles of nature and memory. The chapter closes with the image of old Friday (the dog) barking by the sea.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “It is a bad night,” she says of the storm, “and I almost pity the stars.” What does her pity reveal about her?
  • She calls the Stock Exchange “a palace of evil,” yet “she had her own seat there, for she had appropriated it.” Why does imaginary money suit her?
  • “Money was the root of all evil, she did believe.” The King James Bible says “the love of money” (1 Timothy 6:10). What does her version leave out?

Characters

  • “Greater dog, he said, was man.” What does the explorer mean, and what does it tell us about him?
  • “He patted her long black umbrella—he patted his wooden leg—he made love to wood and cloth but not to her.” What does he want from her?
  • “Never could she kneel before man, and no man knelt before her.” What does Miss MacIntosh refuse, and what does it cost her?

Connections

  • “Who had not known that Miss MacIntosh was bald?” How does Vera’s list change the secret she discovered?
  • Miss MacIntosh was “like that wounded doe who stands with its blind eye toward the sea.” Where did her danger come from?
  • “Yet once more, Miss MacIntosh, my darling, and once more.” Why does Vera borrow the music of Milton’s elegy for a drowned friend?

Reflection

  • “The ultimate of fear was that there was nothing to be afraid of.” What can that mean?
  • “If only she could have known one person well.” Is that the loneliest wish in the chapter?
  • “Sometimes far from the tree from which it had fallen, the apple was found.” What does she mean, and of whom is she thinking?

Research: Characters

Miss MacIntosh

  • A strong, eccentric woman who dresses for storms and long voyages. She is bald, always wearing a red wig, which is a hidden truth only discovered later. Her voice is booming and manly, and she exhibits contradictory traits: a resolute socialist with disdain for wealth and extravagance but also an astute student of finance.
  • Represents resilience, the burden of loneliness, and the struggle for self-identity. Her baldness symbolizes vulnerability and a unique form of naked truth.
  • She harbors dreams of love, adventure, and escape from societal norms, but also suffers from deep isolation and self-awareness of her physical and emotional amputations.
  • Acts as a strict, demanding teacher who pushes Vera to learn relentlessly, even amid storms and symbolic death.

The Arctic Explorer at the Stock Exchange

  • A wooden-legged, talkative man with a haunting past who invites Miss MacIntosh to join him on an expedition to the North Pole. His character is mysterious and symbolic of isolation, survival, and the lure of the unknown.
  • Embodies the exploration of the unknown, physical and emotional scars, and the harsh reality beyond civilization.
  • A complex connection based on shared loneliness and amputations; he proposes a life of silence and isolation, contrasting with Miss MacIntosh’s attachment to her worldly concerns.
  • He wants her money: she must “draw out her money from the bank and put it all into his name,” though her fortune is imaginary and she has never placed her first ten cents.
  • He calls himself “Greater Dog who had once eaten his dead master,” the same story Miss MacIntosh tells of a dog in What Cheer.

Catherine Cartwheel

  • She is described as being “pent up in bed” and dreaming in an “over-heated room.” This suggests she is physically unwell and possibly confined to her bed for an extended period.
  • Vera mentions that her mother sometimes dreams “opium dreams” and entertains “too many hallucinations” for her health’s sake. This implies a detachment from reality and a possible reliance on drugs or other means to escape her physical condition.
  • Vera notes that her mother has strange dreams where she never recognizes living men, only the dead ones. This indicates a preoccupation with the past and a possible inability to connect with the present.
  • Miss MacIntosh criticizes her mother for lacking common sense, specifically citing the gift of an Eskimo cookbook as an example. This suggests a disconnect between the mother’s actions and practical reality.
  • The gifts Vera’s mother gives Miss MacIntosh (a silk nightgown and the Eskimo cookbook) point to a certain level of wealth and privilege.
  • Vera wonders if her mother always knew Miss MacIntosh was bald, despite it being a secret. If true, this could indicate a deeper understanding or perception than she lets on.

Vera Cartwheel

  • Vera is deeply influenced by Miss MacIntosh, to the point of reflecting her moods and taking on “every color except my own.” This suggests a lack of a strong, independent identity in her childhood, making her highly susceptible to Miss MacIntosh’s unusual worldview.
  • Vera is often confused by Miss MacIntosh’s lessons and pronouncements. She admits to being “very stupid, undeveloped” and unable to answer basic questions. However, this also implies a certain level of intellectual curiosity, even if she struggles to grasp the concepts.
  • As a child, Vera takes Miss MacIntosh’s eccentricities for granted, considering them “commonplace.” This acceptance highlights her innocence and the way children often normalize the unusual behavior of adults in their lives.
  • The narrative is framed as a recollection from “long years after Miss MacIntosh’s disappearance.” This distance allows Vera to view the lessons with “a new light,” recognizing their unorthodox nature. The nostalgic tone suggests that Miss MacIntosh, despite her strangeness, had a significant impact on Vera’s life and development.
  • Vera seems haunted by Miss MacIntosh’s disappearance and the circumstances surrounding it. The recurring imagery of storms, drowning, and the sea suggests a deep sense of loss and unresolved questions. She can still hear Miss MacIntosh’s voice “roaring above the storm,” indicating a lasting psychological impact.
  • Vera’s observation that Miss MacIntosh wished to tell her all her secrets during the last month of her life suggests a growing awareness and understanding of her nursemaid’s complexities. She begins to perceive Miss MacIntosh’s loneliness, her longing for adult companionship, and her hidden vulnerabilities, such as her baldness.
  • Even as a child, Vera seems to possess a keen eye for the absurd. She notices the incongruities in Miss MacIntosh’s behavior, such as her wearing a whaler’s hat during lessons or receiving an Eskimo cookbook as a gift when she never cooks. This suggests an underlying sense of humor and an ability to recognize the strangeness of the world around her.
  • In retrospect, Vera reflects on her own shortcomings and tendencies, such as her proneness to hallucinations and her lack of a distinct identity. This introspection suggests a desire to understand herself and her relationship with Miss MacIntosh on a deeper level.

Mr. Bonebreaker

  • Miss MacIntosh reflects that Mr. Bonebreaker belonged to an old religion where women must kneel before men, similar to the customs of What Cheer, Iowa, where women wash men’s feet. She contrasts this with her own situation, noting her baldness and lack of traditionally feminine features, which prevent her from fulfilling such a role.
  • Mr. Bonebreaker seems to represent a lost opportunity for love and connection in Miss MacIntosh’s life. He is associated with the idea that she could never kneel before a man, implying a rejection of traditional gender roles and a sense of isolation.
  • It is said that Mr. Bonebreaker fled leaving his umbrella behind.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s baldness is a significant factor in her perception of her relationship with Mr. Bonebreaker. She believes her lack of hair and other physical attributes made her unattractive and contributed to the failure of their relationship.
  • Mr. Bonebreaker is mentioned as one of the people who knew Miss MacIntosh was bald.

Old Friday (pet dog)

  • Old Friday is consistently present at Miss MacIntosh’s heels, even after her disappearance. He barks “much as if he might still be aware of her, a moving shadow, a shadow of a black umbrella moving over water and sand —as if perhaps she had never gone or had returned.” This suggests that Old Friday retains a memory of Miss MacIntosh and remains loyal to her, even when others have moved on.
  • The text implies that Old Friday might perceive something beyond what others can see. Vera questions, “had he not known the denudation and the dream, the reality and the illusion, how each must live by some pretense?.” This suggests that Old Friday is aware of the underlying truths and illusions that define Miss MacIntosh’s existence.
  • Even after Miss MacIntosh’s apparent drowning, Old Friday continues to bark by the sea, suggesting a continuation of life and memory even in the face of loss. The waves return “with all but her, waves swollen with winds, the waves like sheep with a thousand bells, aye, while she and the shores and sounding waves wash far away in echo and response.”
  • Old Friday’s barking by the sea after Miss MacIntosh’s disappearance can be interpreted as a symbol of inconsolable grief and mourning. He is a constant reminder of her absence.

Lorena

  • Lorena appears only briefly but her presence is significant in relation to Miss MacIntosh and the broader themes of the novel.
  • In Chapter 64, Lorena is described as a beautiful dancing girl with dark hair, described as fat, who ran away and became a strip-tease dancer. This characterization contrasts sharply with Miss MacIntosh’s practical, no-nonsense, and grounded nature. Lorena represents a more sensual, flamboyant, and perhaps socially transgressive aspect of femininity, set against Miss MacIntosh’s sobriety, austerity, and emotional reserve.
  • Lorena’s knowledge of Miss MacIntosh’s baldness before Vera discovers it suggests an intimacy or at least a closer social awareness of Miss MacIntosh’s vulnerability and true self.
  • Lorena’s laughter behind her fan and her awareness of this physical fact highlight the difference in how women in the novel perceive and interact with each other, often marked by a mixture of judgment, awareness, and perhaps envy or pity.
  • Lorena helps underscore Miss MacIntosh’s loneliness and isolation. While Lorena embodies escape, sensuality, and perhaps a more conventional form of social engagement (albeit in a marginal way), Miss MacIntosh remains solitary, bald, and burdened by her practical concerns and emotional detachment.

Research: Themes

Isolation and Loneliness

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Miss MacIntosh’s baldness and social detachment highlight profound loneliness, despite her physical presence among others.
  • The Arctic explorer’s life and invitation symbolize a complete withdrawal from society into the harsh, silent wilderness.
  • Vera, as a child, reflects this isolation by absorbing the moods and eccentricities of Miss MacIntosh without fully understanding them.

Truth and Illusion

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Miss MacIntosh claims to always speak the “bald truth,” a pun on her physical baldness and metaphorical truthfulness.
  • Her imaginary financial dealings on the Stock Exchange represent the illusion of control and wealth in a chaotic world.
  • The explorer’s tales mix reality with fantasy, blurring the lines between truth, myth, and personal delusion.

The Harshness of Nature and Fate

See: Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos · Fate and Chance

  • The recurrent imagery of storms, drowning, and the sea as a coffin symbolize the inevitability of death and nature’s indifference.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s preoccupation with the fishermen, drowned sailors, and drowned things on the beach emphasizes vulnerability.
  • The Arctic setting and references to icebergs, glaciers, and the North Pole evoke an unforgiving environment that mirrors internal struggles.

Identity and Physical Amputation

See: Identity and the Self

  • Both Miss MacIntosh and the Arctic explorer carry physical and emotional amputations (her baldness and removed ovaries; his lost leg), symbolizing loss and survival.
  • Their shared disabilities forge a silent kinship and mutual understanding of alienation.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s lost hair and wig symbolize the loss of traditional feminine identity and seduction.

Education and Knowledge

See: The Search for Meaning

  • Miss MacIntosh’s relentless questioning, despite her indifference to answers, reflects a ritualistic pursuit of knowledge.
  • Vera’s difficulty in answering symbolizes innocence and underdevelopment, contrasting with Miss MacIntosh’s eccentric wisdom.
  • Subjects range from geography and history to biology and social science, showing a broad but fragmented understanding of the world.

Research: Resources

  • “What is the altitude of Tibet?”: The Tibetan Plateau averages more than 14,000 feet above sea level, the highest plateau on earth, called the Roof of the World.
  • “Who was Martin Luther?”: Martin Luther (1483-1546), the German monk whose Ninety-five Theses of 1517 began the Protestant Reformation.
  • “What is the life of the eel?”: The eels of Europe and America are born in the Sargasso Sea, drift thousands of miles to the rivers, and return to the sea to spawn and die. The Danish biologist Johannes Schmidt traced their birthplace in the early 1920s. See The Sea and Water.
  • “what were the states adjoining Kansas”: Nebraska, Missouri, Oklahoma, and Colorado. The geographic center of the forty-eight contiguous states lies in Kansas, near Lebanon.
  • “Who changed the calendar”: Julius Caesar reformed the Roman calendar in 46 BC, and Pope Gregory XIII gave us the Gregorian calendar in 1582; Britain and its colonies adopted it in 1752, when September 2 was followed by September 14. Constantine’s change was to time itself: in 321 he made Sunday a day of rest, and his Council of Nicaea (325) set the rule for the date of Easter. See Constantine and the calendar in the Chapter 22 resources and the Chapter 63 resources.
  • “Who was Captain Cook?”: Miss MacIntosh’s “Captain Cook” is Dr. Frederick A. Cook (1865-1940), the American who claimed to have reached the North Pole in April 1908, a year before Peary. The University of Copenhagen rejected his records in 1909. In 1923 he was convicted of mail fraud for promoting oil stocks in Texas, served his sentence at Leavenworth until his parole in 1930, and was pardoned by President Franklin Roosevelt in 1940. Young merges him with Captain James Cook, the British navigator. See Exploration and the Poles.
    • Captain Cook or James Cook was a British explorer, navigator, cartographer, and captain in the British Royal Navy, famous for his three voyages between 1768 and 1779 in the Pacific Ocean and to New Zealand and Australia in particular. Recently his voyages were criticized for the promotion of colonialism. (Wikipedia)
  • “it was Captain Cook and not Commander Peary”: Commander Peary or Robert Peary was an American explorer and officer in the United States Navy who made several expeditions to the Arctic in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. He is best known for, in April 1909, leading an expedition that claimed to be the first to have reached the geographic North Pole. Explorer Matthew Henson, part of the expedition, is thought to have reached what they believed to be the North Pole narrowly before Peary. (Wikipedia) Peary’s backers were the Peary Arctic Club of wealthy New Yorkers, and the National Geographic Society endorsed his claim. See Exploration and the Poles.
    • In 1897 Peary brought six Inuit from Greenland to New York, where four of them died, among them Qisuk; the American Museum of Natural History staged a false burial for his son, Minik, and kept Qisuk’s skeleton. Its remains were returned to Greenland in 1993. See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “We will now skip the sinking of the Armada”: The Spanish Armada of 1588, beaten in the Channel, was wrecked by storms off Scotland and Ireland, where thousands of sailors drowned.
  • “Lecturing on socialism”: Socialism is a left-wing political, social, and economic philosophy encompassing a range of economic and social systems characterized by social ownership of the means of production, as opposed to private ownership (Wikipedia). Vera recalls Miss MacIntosh lecturing on socialism while walking in the surf with her skirts pinned up and her black umbrella billowing above her head. Miss MacIntosh expresses disdain for the unequal distribution of wealth, stating that in New York City, “The rich threw dollar bills out of windows, and the poor did not catch the money.” She hopes for a reformed world without rich people by the time Vera grows up. Despite her disapproval of wealth, Miss MacIntosh visits the Stock Exchange, which she calls “a palace of evil.” She claims she goes there to level the rich so they should all be poor. Miss MacIntosh teaches Vera “the beauty of deprivation.” She mentions her desire to improve the human race and her search for “simple humanity.” She visits the Stock Exchange to make imaginary money but saves real money for her old age. She also mentions a past relationship with an Arctic explorer. The Arctic explorer, whom she met at the Stock Exchange, echoes some of Miss MacIntosh’s criticisms of society, viewing it as empty and artificial. He suggests that she abandon her worldly goods and join him on an everlasting trek to the North Pole.
  • “should I do with an Eskimo cook book”: Many Inuit and Yupik people now consider “Eskimo” an outsider’s name; Canada and Greenland use Inuit. A real Eskimo Cook Book, gathered by the students of the Shishmaref Day School in Alaska, was published in 1952 by the Alaska Crippled Children’s Association.
  • “Where is the Bering Sea?”: The Bering Sea and Strait are named for Vitus Bering (1681-1741), a Dane in Russian service who sailed them in 1728 and died on what is now Bering Island in 1741. The explorer of this chapter is a Dane too. See Exploration and the Poles.
  • “who is John L. Sullivan”: John L. Sullivan was an American boxer dubbed the “Boston Strong Boy” by the press and recognized as the first heavyweight champion of gloved boxing. (Wikipedia) He was the last bare-knuckle heavyweight champion and lost his title to James J. Corbett in 1892; he died in 1918.
  • “Going to New York on her occasional vacations”: New York City’s history spans from Lenape settlements, Dutch colonization (New Amsterdam), English rule, Revolutionary War, becoming a major port and immigration hub, skyscraper boom, and finally a global cultural and economic powerhouse, peaking in post-WWII influence with thriving jazz, arts (Abstract Expressionism), and Broadway before the significant changes of the late 60s began to emerge, solidifying its unique, fast-paced identity.
  • “she had often visited the Aquarium”: The New York Aquarium is the oldest continually operating aquarium in the United States, located on the Riegelmann Boardwalk in Coney Island, Brooklyn, New York City. It was founded at Castle Garden in Battery Park, Manhattan in 1896, and moved to Coney Island in 1957. (Wikipedia)
  • “Money was the root of all evil”: “For the love of money is the root of all evil” (1 Timothy 6:10, KJV). Miss MacIntosh drops “the love of.” See The Bible.
  • “followed by this Pinkerton agent”: The Pinkerton National Detective Agency, founded in Chicago by Allan Pinkerton in 1850, hired Kate Warne in 1856, the first woman detective in America. Pinkertons were also hired by mill and mine owners to spy on and break strikes, as at Homestead in 1892. See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “Grant’s Tomb, she had always visited it”: Grant’s Tomb or the General Grant National Memorial, is the final resting place of Ulysses S. Grant, 18th president of the United States, and his wife, Julia Grant. It is a classical domed mausoleum in the Morningside Heights neighborhood of Upper Manhattan in New York City. (Wikipedia) It was dedicated in 1897. See The Civil War.
  • “in the shadow of Brooklyn Bridge”: The Brooklyn Bridge opened in 1883. When its engineer, Washington Roebling, was crippled by caisson disease, his wife Emily Warren Roebling carried his instructions to the site for years and learned the engineering herself; she was the first to cross the finished bridge. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “she herself had been an ugly duckling”: Hans Christian Andersen’s “The Ugly Duckling” (1843), the tale of another Dane.
  • “an end of the British Empire and of all forms of Colonialism”: The British Empire’s history is a centuries-long process of expansion driven by exploration, trade, and colonization, beginning with settlements in North America and growing to encompass vast territories across the globe. Key events include the loss of the American colonies, direct British rule over India after the Indian Mutiny, the Scramble for Africa, and the eventual decline of the empire following the two World Wars as independence movements gained momentum. The empire’s legacy includes the spread of the English language and British political and educational systems, but also the violent suppression of resistance, exploitation of resources, and the enslavement of millions of Africans through the triangular trade.
    • Colonialism is a historical practice where one country dominates and controls another, often exploiting its resources and people. While it existed in antiquity, the modern era of colonialism, driven by European powers from the 15th to the 20th century, saw empires expand across the Americas, Africa, and Asia. The Spanish and Portuguese initiated colonization in the Americas, followed by the French and British who established empires in North America, the Caribbean, and other parts of the world. This process frequently involved violence, the subjugation of indigenous populations, and the imposition of the colonizer’s culture, language, and economic systems, with lasting negative impacts still felt today.
    • The history of Jewish return to Palestine is marked by two major periods: ancient exiles and returns, and the modern Zionist movement. Ancient returns occurred after the Babylonian exile around 538 BCE and were driven by religious and political events. The modern era saw a mass return fueled by the late 19th-century Zionist movement, which aimed to establish a Jewish homeland in Palestine amidst rising antisemitism, culminating in the establishment of the State of Israel in 1948. Around 587 BCE, the Babylonians destroyed the First Temple in Jerusalem and exiled many Jews. In 538 BCE, Cyrus the Great of Persia conquered Babylon and allowed the Jews to return to Judah and rebuild the Temple, which was completed in 515 BCE. The Romans destroyed the Second Temple in 70 CE during the First Jewish-Roman War, leading to a major Jewish diaspora and the loss of sovereignty. The Zionist movement, which began in the late 19th century, aimed to re-establish a Jewish homeland in Palestine. Organized immigration to Palestine began in 1882, with immigrants motivated by a combination of religious ideals and practical/economic considerations, though not yet modern Zionism. The movement gained momentum due to rising antisemitism and pogroms in Europe, which led to increased Jewish immigration to Palestine throughout the 1930s. The British limited Jewish immigration to Palestine with the “White Paper of 1939,” which disappointed Zionists, though many Jews still arrived during this period. Following World War II and the liberation of concentration camp survivors, the United Nations partitioned Palestine. On May 14, 1948, the modern State of Israel was proclaimed, re-establishing Jewish sovereignty after nearly 2,000 years.
  • “For otherwise, where would they go?”: At the Évian Conference of July 1938, thirty-two nations met over the Jewish refugees fleeing Nazi Germany, and nearly all refused to take more. In 1939 the ship St. Louis, carrying 937 passengers, most of them Jewish refugees, was turned away by Cuba, the United States, and Canada. See Judaism.
  • “an end to the Tong wars”: The Tong Wars were a series of violent disputes beginning in the late 19th century among rival Chinese Tong factions centered in the Chinatowns of various American cities, in particular San Francisco. Tong wars could be triggered by a variety of inter-gang grievances, from the public besmirching of another Tong’s honor, to failure to make full payment for a “slave girl,” to the murder of a rival Tong member. Each Tong had salaried soldiers, known as boo how doy, who fought in Chinatown alleys and streets over the control of opium, prostitution, gambling, and territory. In San Francisco’s Chinatown district, the Tong Wars lasted until 1921, with the various criminal Tongs estimated between nineteen and as many as thirty at the peak of the conflict, though the actual number is uncertain, with frequent splintering and mergers between the various Tongs. While a loose alliance, consisting of the Chinatown police, Donaldina Cameron, the courts, and the Chinese community itself tried to stem the tide of the fighting Tongs, it was the 1906 San Francisco earthquake and subsequent fires caused by the earthquake that was the death knell for the Tongs in San Francisco, as it destroyed the brothels, gambling dens, and opium houses that the criminal organizations had used for the majority of their revenue. Recovery was slow for most Tongs, so they abandoned the old Chinatown. (Wikipedia)
  • “China, the Sleeping Dragon, would awaken”: China has been referred to as “the sleeping Dragon.” In China, the dragon stands for power, strength, and good luck. In the past, the Chinese emperor was referred to as the Dragon. China’s economy covers thousands of years with cycles of prosperity and decline. “Let China sleep, for when she wakes up, she will shake the world” is often credited to Napoleon, but no source has been found in his speeches or writings; the earliest known attribution is from 1911. See Napoleon and Wellington.
    • The Qing dynasty, with its mandarins, fell in the revolution of 1911, and the last emperor abdicated in 1912.
  • “She once knew two dwarves”: It’s possible and actually quite common for two parents with dwarfism, especially achondroplasia, to have an average-sized child, as there’s a 25% chance for a child to inherit a “normal” height gene from each parent, resulting in an average-sized stature, while still having a 50% chance of a dwarf child like themselves, and a 25% chance for a severe, often fatal, form of dwarfism (homozygous).
  • “the seed pods, her ovaries, had been removed”: Removing the ovaries is an oophorectomy, not a hysterectomy. In 1872 the Georgia surgeon Robert Battey began removing healthy ovaries to treat menstrual pain, “hysteria,” and “insanity,” and by the 1890s thousands of women in America and Europe, many of them young, had undergone the operation. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
    • Hysterectomies are rarely performed on women younger than thirty-five years old. It is a mystery as to what happened to Miss MacIntosh’s ovaries and difficult to pinpoint her age or anyone’s age except Vera in the novel.
  • “She did hope that, before she died, the King of England would lose his crown”: Attempts to overthrow the English monarchy span centuries, from medieval power struggles like the Anarchy and Wars of the Roses, to the successful republican revolution during the English Civil War (leading to Charles I’s execution and the Commonwealth), the Glorious Revolution (limiting monarchical power), and the Jacobite Risings seeking Stuart restoration, all demonstrating persistent challenges to royal authority.
    • A King of England did lose his crown: Edward VIII abdicated in December 1936 to marry Wallis Simpson, an American divorcée.
  • “She was not crowned by virtue”: “A virtuous woman is a crown to her husband” (Proverbs 12:4, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “Had she not once had a love affair with the King of Sweden”: Mr. Bonebreaker, who believed he was the true King of Sweden. See the Chapter 65 resources. See Mr. Titus Bonebreaker.
  • “that a path would open through the sea for her”: “And Moses stretched out his hand over the sea… and made the sea dry land, and the waters were divided” (Exodus 14:21, KJV). See The Bible and Ancient Egypt.
  • “if she had no breast, then he would build a tower upon a wall”: See the little sister who “hath no breasts” (Song of Solomon 8:8-9) in the Chapter 65 resources. See The Bible.
  • “that there would be balm in Gilead”: “Is there no balm in Gilead; is there no physician there?” (Jeremiah 8:22, KJV). The balm of Gilead was a healing resin from the hill country east of the Jordan, carried by traders: “a company of Ishmeelites came from Gilead with their camels bearing spicery and balm and myrrh, going to carry it down to Egypt” (Genesis 37:25, KJV). The African American spiritual answers Jeremiah’s question: “There is a balm in Gilead / To make the wounded whole.” See The Bible.
  • “a pillar of clouds by day, a pillar of fire by night”: “And the LORD went before them by day in a pillar of a cloud, to lead them the way; and by night in a pillar of fire, to give them light” (Exodus 13:21, KJV). See The Bible and Ancient Egypt.
  • “But Mr. Bonebreaker had belonged to that old religion”: This passage critiques patriarchal traditions by illustrating the extreme expectations placed on women to be subservient to men, using vivid, almost grotesque imagery of kneeling and hair used as a towel for washing men’s feet. “that old religion” and its description here refers to patriarchy of which Christianity is a part and instances of the changes from a matrilineal society and traditions to a patrilineal one are in the Bible (Marilyn French, From Eve to Dawn: A History of Women in the World, 2002). Patriarchy entitles men to admiration, sex, consent, and domestic labor from women. Men also receive better medical care and pain management and overall focus of health science for their bodies. Men also are able to practice bodily control which is often and routinely denied women. Misogyny and sexism go hand in hand with patriarchy although it is possible for men to practice one or the other and both need not be present at the same time. Sexism is the theoretical and ideological branch where beliefs, ideas and assumptions are used to rationalize and naturalize patriarchal norms and expectations. Misogyny is the “law enforcement” branch of patriarchy which polices and enforces gendered norms and expectations for girls and women (Kate Manne, Down Girl, 2017, and Entitled, 2020). Gerda Lerner documented men’s withholding knowledge and power from women through denying education for centuries (The Creation of Feminist Consciousness, 1993). Young upends many of these gendered norms in the novel. See Women, Gender, and Marriage and Mr. Titus Bonebreaker.
  • “wash his dirty feet in a tin basin and dry them with her hair”: “And stood at his feet behind him weeping, and began to wash his feet with tears, and did wipe them with the hairs of her head” (Luke 7:38, KJV). Feet washing, after John 13:14, is still practiced by the Brethren, Mennonites, and Primitive Baptists. See The Bible, Hair, Wig, and Baldness, and American Religious Movements.
  • “there had been a rival love”: Miss MacIntosh could never kneel before a man, and no man knelt before her, “although there had been more than one man in her life, of course, and there had been a rival love.” She still hopes there “might still be a path opening for her in the sea.”
  • “the opium dreams my mother dreamed”: Psychoactive substances and paranormal activity have been reported for over a thousand years. In the documentary Super High Me in 2007 Doug Benson smokes pot for thirty days straight. He took a series of tests before and after. His Psychic ESP test went from 1/25 to 7/25. See The History of Opium.
  • “Once she went to an exhibit of kitchen robots”: At the 1939 New York World’s Fair, Westinghouse showed Elektro, a seven-foot talking robot, and staged a “Battle of the Centuries” in which a woman washing dishes by hand raced an electric dishwasher.
    • The American National Exhibition of 1959, held in Moscow, not New York, which featured a “miracle kitchen” that included a radio-controlled proto-roomba-like device. As part of a Cold War-era exhibition, the U.S. showcased a futuristic kitchen featuring a radio-controlled, two-way mirror device that resembled a proto-roomba. This exhibit was a counter-exhibit to a Soviet exhibition in Moscow, part of a propaganda battle.
  • “At the head of Wall Street”: From 1711 to 1762 New York’s municipal slave market stood on Wall Street. See the Wall Street broker in the Chapter 54 resources. See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “she would sit among the tombs at Trinity Churchyard”: Trinity Churchyard, is located in Lower Manhattan near Wall Street and Broadway. Alexander Hamilton, Albert Gallatin, and Robert Fulton are buried in the downtown Trinity Churchyard.
  • “in the graveyard at St. Mark’s”: St. Mark’s Church-in-the-Bowery is an Episcopal Church in the East Village neighborhood of Manhattan in New York City. The property has been the site of continuous Christian worship for more than three and a half centuries, making it New York City’s oldest site of continuous religious practice. (Wikipedia)
    • Peter Stuyvesant, the Dutch director-general of New Amsterdam, is buried there (1672). In 1654 he tried to turn away twenty-three Jewish refugees from Brazil, and the Dutch West India Company overruled him; he also held enslaved Africans. See Judaism and Society, Class, and Race.
  • “in a bullish or a bearish market”: A bull expects prices to rise, a bear expects them to fall; both terms come from the London exchanges of the eighteenth century. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
    • Since the Great Recession in 2008 there has been a great deal of investigative journalism on American and international financial institutions and their manipulation. Matt Taibbi has written a series of articles in the Rolling Stone magazine. Jon Stewart has recently done a series of video podcasts on financial concerns. Mark Blyth, a professor at Brown University, has also published books and articles.
  • “if she had only had more than ten cents to her name”: John D. Rockefeller, whom she names below, handed out shiny new dimes to children and strangers in his old age.
  • “she knew the shiftings of Anaconda”: “Anaconda” here likely alludes to the Anaconda Copper Mining Company, a well-known mining corporation historically traded on the stock market. Miss MacIntosh, though a poor nursemaid, imagines or studies the financial shifts and trends of such stocks, demonstrating her keen interest and understanding of finance despite her practical and modest lifestyle. It is a symbol of the volatile financial markets that Miss MacIntosh follows with astute observation and imagination, representing the unpredictable nature of investments where the infrastructure (rails, trains, lights) remains constant but the market values (rails being “up” or “down”) fluctuate unpredictably.
  • “how American tin can might go up or down”: American Can Company, formed in 1901 by merging more than a hundred can makers, was called the Tin Can Trust.
    • American tinware began with colonial tinsmiths making pierced lanterns and household goods. The Civil War’s demand for canned food made the tin can cheap and common, carrying food from the industrial East to the frontier, and pressed tin ceilings became an affordable imitation of ornate plaster.
  • “that six feet of earth which would be his”: Compare Tolstoy’s “How Much Land Does a Man Need?” (1886), whose greedy peasant runs himself to death trying to claim more land and is buried in a grave six feet long.
  • “the coffin-maker’s child had not his coffin”: Compare the proverb “The shoemaker’s children go barefoot.”
  • “She learned what intangible property was”: Intangible property refers to assets that lack physical form but hold significant economic value, representing legal or financial rights, ideas, or reputation, rather than objects like land or inventory: intellectual property (patents, copyrights, trademarks), brand names, trade secrets, licenses, franchises, customer lists, goodwill, and digital assets like software and databases. These properties derive value from their ability to generate income or provide exclusive use, making them crucial in business valuation.
  • “or could corner all the gold”: On Black Friday, September 24, 1869, Jay Gould and Jim Fisk’s attempt to corner the gold market collapsed, ruining many speculators. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “she was richer than Rockefeller or the King of Sweden”: John D. Rockefeller (1839-1937), founder of Standard Oil, was the richest American of his age.
  • “she had her own seat there, for she had appropriated it”: In 1870 Victoria Woodhull and her sister Tennessee Claflin opened the first brokerage on Wall Street owned by women. No woman held a seat on the New York Stock Exchange until Muriel Siebert bought one in December 1967. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “Her man’s watch ticking on her shoulder”: Mr. Bonebreaker’s watch. See the Chapter 65 resources. See Clocks and Watches.
  • “piling up a vast fortune in American Anthracite”: Anthracite is hard coal, mined in the United States almost entirely in northeastern Pennsylvania, and it heated American homes and powered industry well into the twentieth century. Its fortunes came at a steep human cost, marked by mine disasters and bitter labor conflicts such as the Anthracite Coal Strike of 1902.
  • “She would not invest in zippers”: Miss MacIntosh would not invest in zippers because in What Cheer they were considered a modern invention and against the will of God. This kind of restriction sounds similar to the Amish and Mennonites who have lived in Iowa for over 160 years. The zipper began as Whitcomb Judson’s “clasp locker” (1893) and Gideon Sundback’s “hookless fastener” (1913); B. F. Goodrich named it the zipper in 1923. See Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest and American Religious Movements.
  • “sweeping up ticker tape”: The stock ticker, printing prices on paper tape, came to Wall Street in 1867. The first ticker-tape parade, in 1886, celebrated the dedication of the Statue of Liberty.
  • “until the street was a sea of glass”: “And before the throne there was a sea of glass like unto crystal” (Revelation 4:6, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “He told her that he had swept with his broom in all the great streets of the world”: The old broom-sweeper, not the Arctic explorer, describes his presence in many important and symbolic places around the world. This conveys his wide-ranging journey through the world, both literally and figuratively, and his connection to both the extraordinary and the ordinary.
    • Trafalgar Square evolved from the King’s Mews into a grand public space, commemorating the 1805 Battle of Trafalgar with Nelson’s Column (1843) and bronze lions (1867), featuring the National Gallery, fountains (1939), and serving as London’s premier site for political demonstrations, rallies, and public gatherings, solidifying its role as a cultural and political heart of the city.
    • Kremlin Square (Red Square) evolved from a medieval market/shantytown cleared by Ivan the Great into a ceremonial center, marketplace, execution site, and Soviet focal point for parades, marked by structures like St. Basil’s Cathedral (1550s) and the GUM store (late 19th century), becoming a stage for state power, revolution (1917), and post-war military displays, with Lenin’s Mausoleum and Kremlin Wall Necropolis established in the Soviet era before the major 1950s-60s renovations and public opening.
    • Golden Square London, evolved from a plague pit (Pesthouse Field) and horse grazing land (Gelding Close) into a fashionable aristocratic address in the 1700s, hosting diplomats and artists, then shifted to professionals and businesses by the 1800s, becoming a literary landmark featured in Dickens and known for its wool trade.
    • St. Mark’s Square (Piazza San Marco) began in the 9th century as a small space by the original St. Mark’s Basilica, evolving from a muddy field to Venice’s grand public heart, paved in brick by 1267, enlarged and adorned with natural stone patterns by the 18th century, and featuring significant Renaissance architecture like the Doge’s Palace and Campanile, solidifying its role as Europe’s “drawing room” for centuries.
  • “she did not own even a share in General Electric”: General Electric (GE) began in 1892 from a merger of Edison General Electric and Thomson-Houston Electric Company, becoming an early tech giant known for pioneering lighting (Edison’s bulb), appliances (electric stoves), and industrial equipment. GE expanded into radio, TV, jet engines (WWII), nuclear power, and established its famous research lab, driving American industrial growth through innovations like the first commercial power station (1882) and early home appliances, becoming a key Dow Jones component.
  • “some other potters’ field”: Paupers’ graves (also potter’s fields) in the US were common, unmarked plots for the poor, unknown, or unclaimed, often managed by poorhouses or asylums, reflecting a societal stigma where a cheap, dignified burial was a mark of last resort, with burials sometimes in communal trenches and lacking individual headstones, a practice evolving from English poor laws and continuing through periods like the Civil War era’s hardship. “And they took counsel, and bought with them the potter’s field, to bury strangers in” (Matthew 27:7, KJV). New York’s potter’s field has been on Hart Island since 1869. See The Bible and Death and Mortality.
  • “It would be a natural death, not a suicide”: Miss MacIntosh is describing the myth that men jumped out of office windows after the market crash in 1929. John Kenneth Galbraith found that the suicide wave of 1929 was a legend; suicides in New York in the weeks after the crash were fewer than the year before (The Great Crash, 1929, 1955). See Suicide in History and Literature.
  • “Every investment she made was hedged around by Ivory soap”: Ivory soap is a flagship personal care brand created by the Procter & Gamble Company. Ivory’s first slogan, “It Floats!,” was introduced in 1891.
  • “a wilted carnation at her feet or a wilted rosebud”: The wilted carnation or rosebud is a poignant image of impermanence and the quiet sadness that accompanies Miss MacIntosh’s life and her reflections on human nature and society.
  • “America, she always said, was still the Land of Opportunity”: The Land of Opportunity is a nickname for the United States and refers to socioeconomic mobility between social classes or economic levels.
  • “this explorer who was wooden-legged”: Compare Peter Freuchen (1886-1957), the Danish Arctic explorer who lost his left foot to frostbite in 1926, settled in New York, and won The $64,000 Question in 1956. See Exploration and the Poles.
  • “born in Denmark hard by the castle of Elsinore”: The description situates his origin geographically and culturally: he is from Denmark, specifically near Elsinore Castle, which is famously known as the setting of Shakespeare’s play Hamlet. Elsinore Castle is a real fortress located in Helsingør, Denmark. Like Hamlet, he is a Dane marked by tragedy: he “had left home because of a great tragedy.” See Shakespeare.
  • “had killed twin sisters who had been hiding naked in the bushes”: Compare Actaeon, who came upon Diana bathing naked, was changed into a stag, and was torn apart by his own hounds (Ovid, Metamorphoses, Book 3). The “great huntsman” of the sky is Orion, whom the Greater Dog follows. See Greek Mythology and Astronomy.
  • “Greater dog, he said, was man”: The Dog Star is a nickname for Sirius, a star in the constellation Canis Major (Greater Dog). See Astronomy and Dogs.
  • “He told her that he was a Great Dane”: “Great Dane” is a German breed of large mastiff-sighthound, which descends from hunting dogs of the Middle Ages used to hunt bears, wild boar, and deer. This expresses the Arctic Explorer’s tough, solitary character marked by loss and endurance, a man who carries dog-like loyalty and survival instincts but is also physically and emotionally wounded. The explorer is a pun: a great Dane. See Dogs.
  • “fringed like that of a Wilderness Scout”: “Wilderness Scout” in the U.S. wasn’t a formal program but emerged from early youth groups like Ernest Thompson Seton’s Woodcraft Indians (1902) and Daniel Carter Beard’s Sons of Daniel Boone (1905), which emphasized nature skills and American Indian lore, eventually merging into the Boy Scouts of America (BSA), which itself developed older youth programs like Senior Scouts and Explorers (1935) for advanced wilderness camping and challenging outdoor pursuits, exemplified by trips to Philmont and Canadian wilderness canoe expeditions. Wilderness scouts, frontiersmen, and explorers frequently wore fringed garments, like the “hunting frock” or buckskin jackets, because the fringe served practical purposes, shedding rain and helping the leather dry faster, in addition to being a cultural style adopted from or shared with Native Americans, making it ideal for frontier life.
  • “they would both have done better as fiction writers”: Neither claim is accepted without doubt today. Cook’s records were rejected in 1909, and Peary’s own navigation and speeds have been questioned since. See Exploration and the Poles.
    • Polynesian voyagers may have reached Antarctic waters as early as the seventh century, more than a thousand years before Europeans. Māori oral traditions tell of Hui Te Rangiora and his crew sailing the vessel Te Ivi o Atea far south to “a foggy, misty and dark place not seen by the sun,” with icy summits “piercing the sky.” Ovens and middens found on subantarctic islands show Polynesians in far southern waters. The study argues that these oral histories, often overlooked by Western scholars, show Polynesians were probably the first humans to see Antarctica (Priscilla Wehi and others, “A short scan of Māori journeys to Antarctica,” Journal of the Royal Society of New Zealand, 2021). Cook crossed the Antarctic Circle in 1773 without sighting land, the first confirmed European sightings came in 1820, and Peary’s expeditions were in the Arctic. Flag: after 1964, the 2021 study.
  • “slightly less than two moons’ breadth from Polaris”: In the mid-twentieth century Polaris stood about one degree from the celestial pole, and the full moon is half a degree wide, so the explorer is right. See Astronomy.
  • “their amputations and their sense of having killed something young and helpless”: Miss MacIntosh, bald and missing a breast, and the explorer, who broke his leg fording a stream in Newfoundland and cut it off himself when gangrene set in, share literal amputations. He believes they share a guilt as well, which the novel leaves unexplained.
  • “Fording a shallow stream in Newfoundland”: The history of Newfoundland and Labrador covers the period from habitation by Archaic peoples thousands of years ago to the present day. Prior to European colonization, the lands encompassing present-day Newfoundland and Labrador were inhabited for millennia by different groups of Indigenous peoples. (Wikipedia)
  • “Among the Eskimos, she would do as the Eskimos did”: The proverb “When in Rome, do as the Romans do” goes back to St. Ambrose’s advice to St. Augustine, which Augustine recorded in a letter.
  • “they, the two of them, would walk to Russia”: Big Diomede, in Russia, and Little Diomede, in Alaska, lie about two and a half miles apart in the Bering Strait, and in hard winters the ice between them can be walked.
  • “There was nothing to fear. Where nothing was known”: The phrase philosophically explores how fear depends on distinctions and knowledge, and how in a place of pure mystery or complete ignorance, those distinctions vanish, and with them, fear.
  • “Far above King William Land, at the Magnetic Pole”: King William Land, also called King William Island, is an island in the Kitikmeot Region of Nunavut, which is part of the Arctic Archipelago. (Wikipedia) Franklin’s lost expedition perished near King William Island in 1845-1848, and James Clark Ross first reached the North Magnetic Pole nearby, on the Boothia Peninsula, in 1831. See Exploration and the Poles.
    • A Magnetic Pole is the planetary magnetic poles. On Earth it is the North and South magnetic poles where on the planet’s surface the planet’s magnetic field lines are vertical.
  • “she would find that there is no word for corset”: The old claim that the Eskimo have many words for snow goes back to the anthropologist Franz Boas (1911); the explorer offers the reverse, a word they lack.
  • “the spirit without the flesh”: “The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak” (Matthew 26:41, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “the nights were six months long where they would go”: At the North Pole itself the sun sets near the September equinox and does not rise again until the March equinox, so the night there is about six months long. See Exploration and the Poles.
    • Alaska is not actually dark for six months of the year, even in the northern cities. The entire state experiences varying hours of sunlight and darkness all year long. They do get longer and longer hours of darkness until winter solstice, usually around December 21st.
  • “It was pear-shaped”: On his third voyage Columbus wrote (1498) that the world is not round but shaped like a pear. In 1958 and 1959 measurements of the orbit of the satellite Vanguard 1 showed that the earth is in fact very slightly pear-shaped. See Astronomy.
  • “for he reminded her of her father”: Miss MacIntosh was fond of the Arctic Explorer because he reminded her of her father who had to have his leg cut off when he was older.
  • “her brother, who had gone far away to live among the head-hunters”: Richard MacIntosh. See the Chapter 65 resources.
  • “women were like icebergs”: Compare Hemingway on writing: “The dignity of movement of an ice-berg is due to only one-eighth of it being above water” (Death in the Afternoon, 1932).
  • “he promised not to harm a hair of her head”: “But there shall not an hair of your head perish” (Luke 21:18, KJV), promised to a woman who has none. See The Bible and Hair, Wig, and Baldness.
  • “she had known a dog who had been Greater Man”: The explorer calls himself harmless, “Greater Dog,” and Miss MacIntosh recalls a dog in What Cheer who had been “Greater Man,” for he had eaten his drunken master, to whom he was chained and who had frozen to death in the Arctic cold. See Dogs.
  • “The ultimate of fear was that there was nothing to be afraid of”: Compare Franklin Roosevelt’s first inaugural address (1933): “the only thing we have to fear is fear itself.”
  • “who have no social security”: The Social Security Act of 1935 created old-age pensions in the United States. Miss MacIntosh, always saving for her old age, gives the poor old fish none. See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “Lorena had known, laughing behind her fan”: See Lorena.
  • “not knowing whether he was Peron or Joachim”: See Peron Spitzer and Joachim Spitzer.
  • “old Friday, barking at her heels”: See Old Friday.
  • “orchards of golden apples”: See the golden apples in the Chapter 64 resources. See Greek Mythology.
  • “far from the tree from which it had fallen, the apple was found”: Miss MacIntosh reverses the proverb “The apple never falls far from the tree.”
  • “when moon and sun were shining in the same firmament”: See the sun and moon in one sky in the Chapter 63 resources and the Chapter 65 resources.
  • “her corset draped over a rock in a salt pool”: See her walk into the sea in the Chapter 65 resources. See Suicide in History and Literature.
  • “she was like that wounded doe”: In Aesop’s fable of the one-eyed doe, a doe grazed by the sea with her blind eye toward the water, fearing only the land; hunters came by boat and shot her. Trouble comes from where it is least expected.
  • “She went not by coach with the black coachman”: See The Black Coachman. Compare Emily Dickinson: “Because I could not stop for Death — / He kindly stopped for me —” (1890).
  • “Yet once more, Miss MacIntosh, my darling, and once more”: Vera’s elegy echoes Milton’s “Lycidas” (1637), his lament for a friend drowned at sea: “Yet once more, O ye laurels, and once more”; “He must not float upon his watery bier”; “Whilst thee the shores and sounding seas / Wash far away”; “Visit’st the bottom of the monstrous world”; “So sinks the day-star in the ocean bed”; “With nectar pure his oozy locks he laves.” See Milton, Grief, Loss, and Mourning, and The Sea and Water.
  • “the other faces mourning as over the universal baldness”: See the universal baldness as a sign of mourning in the Chapter 64 resources. See Hair, Wig, and Baldness and Grief, Loss, and Mourning.

Vocabulary

Anthracite
Hard coal that burns with little smoke.
Artifice
Clever trickery, or the art of making things seem other than they are.
Astuteness
Shrewdness; keen judgment.
Bier
A stand on which a corpse or coffin is carried.
Blubber
The fat of whales and seals.
Cold-water flat
A tenement apartment with no hot running water.
Corset
A close-fitting undergarment, stiffened with whalebone or steel and laced tight, worn to shape the waist.
Crusader
A soldier in the Crusades, the Christian military campaigns to take the Holy Land, fought between 1096 and 1291.
Day-star
The morning star; also the sun.
Demented
Out of one’s mind.
Denudation
The act of stripping bare.
Deprivation
The lack or loss of something needed.
Faugh
An exclamation of disgust.
Firmament
The sky or the heavens, imagined as a solid dome overhead, as in Genesis 1.
Gangrene
The death and decay of body tissue.
Hallucination
Something seen or heard that is not there.
Hurricane lamp
An oil lamp with a glass chimney to shield the flame from wind.
Igloo
An Inuit house built of snow blocks.
Lave
To wash or bathe.
Leghorn
A breed of chicken.
Limpid
Clear and transparent.
Mackintosh
A raincoat of rubberized cloth, named for its inventor, Charles Macintosh.
Mandarin
A high-ranking official of imperial China.
Mirage
An optical illusion, common in deserts, in which hot air bends light so that distant water or cities seem to appear.
Monsoon
A seasonal wind in South Asia that brings months of heavy rain.
Myrrh
A fragrant resin used in incense and embalming.
Nebula
A cloud of gas and dust in space; once any hazy patch of light among the stars, including galaxies.
Obtrusive
Pushing oneself forward; unwelcome.
Oozy
Wet with soft mud or slime.
Parlay
To build a small stake into a large one by betting the winnings again.
Periphery
The outer edge.
Perjury
Lying under oath.
Phantasmagoric
Like a phantasmagoria, a shifting series of dreamlike or nightmarish images. A phantasmagoria was an eighteenth-century magic-lantern ghost show.
Pigmentation
The natural coloring of skin, hair, or other tissue.
Potter’s field
A burial ground for the poor and the unknown.
Proximity
Nearness.
Sand dollar
A flat, round sea urchin whose white skeleton is found on beaches.
Shades
Ghosts; the spirits of the dead.
Spinster
An unmarried woman, especially an older one. The word first meant a woman who spun thread.
Traipse
To walk about wearily or aimlessly.
Whaler
A whaling ship or a man who hunts whales.
