Summary

Dressed for a long voyage in whaler's hat and sea boots, Miss MacIntosh teaches Vera through a storm. She fires questions about geography, finance, and the body, describes her imaginary raids on the Stock Exchange, which she calls a palace of evil, and her Arctic explorer, and speaks of her removed ovaries. Then, one cold September morning, she walks into the sea. Her clothes are found on the beach and her red wig is torn in the sand, while old Friday, the sheep dog, barks at the waves.

Key passages

From the research

  • At the outset, Miss MacIntosh is described as dressed for a long voyage (whaler’s hat, sea boots) ready for protection amid a storm, though she insists they are not going anywhere. Her booming voice and strong presence contrast with the child narrator’s perception of her eccentric normalcy. She expresses pity for fishermen, sailors, fishes, and even stars, acknowledging nature’s cruelty toward the weak. This passage sets a tone of resilience, foreboding, and her deep connection with nature’s harshness.
  • Despite the storm and darkness, Miss MacIntosh insists on continuing lessons, asking questions about the heart, geography, history, and more. She shows impatience but also a dedication to education. Vera later reflects that these lessons were far from orthodox and took on new significance after Miss MacIntosh’s disappearance, hinting at her mysterious fate and the deeper layers beneath her teaching methods.
  • A turning point is the revelation that Miss MacIntosh was bald all her life, wearing a red wig that Vera had not noticed before. This physical detail symbolizes her alienation and difference. Her loneliness is profound; she loathes children despite being a nursemaid, yearns for adult companionship, and feels disconnected even in bustling cities like New York. This passage humanizes her, exposing vulnerability behind her strong exterior.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s interactions with a nameless wooden-legged Arctic explorer at the Stock Exchange are important. She admires Captain Cook, debates the discovery of the North Pole, and contemplates socialism and finance. The explorer’s persistent proposals to take her to the Arctic, contrasted with her preoccupation with finance and her own cautious independence, reveal two isolated souls longing for escape and meaning. This dialogue also highlights themes of isolation, survival, and the clash between harsh reality and dreams.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s reflections about her amputations (physical and metaphorical), her lost femininity (due to baldness and removed ovaries), and her unfulfilled love life deepen the sense of tragedy. She contrasts her fate with old traditions and societal expectations of women, expressing a complex relationship with identity and desire. Her hope for a world without colonialism, extravagance, and selfishness reflects her idealism and disillusionment.
  • The chapter culminates with a haunting depiction of her disappearance: her clothes strewn on the beach, the red wig torn, and only remnants left behind. Vera contemplates the metaphor of amputated feet walking on their own, the sea as her coffin, and the endless cycles of nature and memory. The chapter closes with the image of old Friday (the dog) barking by the sea, symbolizing continuity amid loss and the enduring mystery of Miss MacIntosh’s life and death.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Miss MacIntosh

  • A strong, eccentric woman who dresses for storms and long voyages, symbolizing her preparedness for life's harshness. She is bald, always wearing a red wig, which is a hidden truth only discovered later. Her voice is booming and manly, and she exhibits contradictory traits: a resolute socialist with disdain for wealth and extravagance but also an astute student of finance.
  • Represents resilience, the burden of loneliness, and the struggle for self-identity. Her baldness symbolizes vulnerability and a unique form of naked truth.
  • She harbors dreams of love, adventure, and escape from societal norms, but also suffers from deep isolation and self-awareness of her physical and emotional amputations.
  • Acts as a strict, demanding teacher who pushes Vera to learn relentlessly, even amid storms and symbolic death.

The Arctic Explorer at the Stock Exchange

  • A wooden-legged, talkative man with a haunting past who invites Miss MacIntosh to join him on an expedition to the North Pole. His character is mysterious and symbolic of isolation, survival, and the lure of the unknown.
  • Embodies the exploration of the unknown, physical and emotional scars, and the harsh reality beyond civilization.
  • A complex connection based on shared loneliness and amputations; he proposes a life of silence and isolation, contrasting with Miss MacIntosh’s attachment to her worldly concerns.

Catherine Cartwheel

  • She is described as being "pent up in bed" and dreaming in an "over-heated room". This suggests she is physically unwell and possibly confined to her bed for an extended period.
  • Vera mentions that her mother sometimes dreams "opium dreams" and entertains "too many hallucinations" for her health's sake. This implies a detachment from reality and a possible reliance on drugs or other means to escape her physical condition.
  • Vera notes that her mother has strange dreams where she never recognizes living men, only the dead ones. This indicates a preoccupation with the past and a possible inability to connect with the present.
  • Miss MacIntosh criticizes her mother for lacking common sense, specifically citing the gift of an Eskimo cookbook as an example. This suggests a disconnect between the mother's actions and practical reality.
  • The gifts Vera's mother gives Miss MacIntosh (a silk nightgown and the Eskimo cookbook) point to a certain level of wealth and privilege.
  • Vera wonders if her mother always knew Miss MacIntosh was bald, despite it being a secret. If true, this could indicate a deeper understanding or perception than she lets on.

Vera Cartwheel

  • Vera is deeply influenced by Miss MacIntosh, to the point of reflecting her moods and taking on "every color except my own". This suggests a lack of a strong, independent identity in her childhood, making her highly susceptible to Miss MacIntosh's unusual worldview.
  • Vera is often confused by Miss MacIntosh's lessons and pronouncements. She admits to being "very stupid, undeveloped" and unable to answer basic questions. However, this also implies a certain level of intellectual curiosity, even if she struggles to grasp the concepts.
  • As a child, Vera takes Miss MacIntosh's eccentricities for granted, considering them "commonplace". This acceptance highlights her innocence and the way children often normalize the unusual behavior of adults in their lives.
  • The narrative is framed as a recollection from "long years after Miss MacIntosh’s disappearance". This distance allows Vera to view the lessons with "a new light," recognizing their unorthodox nature. The nostalgic tone suggests that Miss MacIntosh, despite her strangeness, had a significant impact on Vera's life and development.
  • Vera seems haunted by Miss MacIntosh's disappearance and the circumstances surrounding it. The recurring imagery of storms, drowning, and the sea suggests a deep sense of loss and unresolved questions. She can still hear Miss MacIntosh's voice "roaring above the storm" 829, indicating a lasting psychological impact.
  • Vera's observation that Miss MacIntosh wished to tell her all her secrets during the last month of her life suggests a growing awareness and understanding of her nursemaid's complexities. She begins to perceive Miss MacIntosh's loneliness, her longing for adult companionship, and her hidden vulnerabilities, such as her baldness.
  • Even as a child, Vera seems to possess a keen eye for the absurd. She notices the incongruities in Miss MacIntosh's behavior, such as her wearing a whaler's hat during lessons or receiving an Eskimo cookbook as a gift when she never cooks. This suggests an underlying sense of humor and an ability to recognize the strangeness of the world around her.
  • In retrospect, Vera reflects on her own shortcomings and tendencies, such as her proneness to hallucinations and her lack of a distinct identity. This introspection suggests a desire to understand herself and her relationship with Miss MacIntosh on a deeper level.

Mr. Bonebreaker

  • Miss MacIntosh reflects that Mr. Bonebreaker belonged to an old religion where women must kneel before men, similar to the customs of What Cheer, Iowa, where women wash men's feet. She contrasts this with her own situation, noting her baldness and lack of traditionally feminine features, which prevent her from fulfilling such a role.
  • Mr. Bonebreaker seems to represent a lost opportunity for love and connection in Miss MacIntosh's life. He is associated with the idea that she could never kneel before a man, implying a rejection of traditional gender roles and a sense of isolation.
  • It is said that Mr. Bonebreaker fled leaving his umbrella behind.
  • Miss MacIntosh's baldness is a significant factor in her perception of her relationship with Mr. Bonebreaker. She believes her lack of hair and other physical attributes made her unattractive and contributed to the failure of their relationship.
  • Mr. Bonebreaker is mentioned as one of the people who knew Miss MacIntosh was bald.

Old Friday (pet dog)

  • Old Friday is consistently present at Miss MacIntosh's heels, even after her disappearance. He barks "much as if he might still be aware of her, a moving shadow, a shadow of a black umbrella moving over water and sand– as if perhaps she had never gone or had returned". This suggests that Old Friday retains a memory of Miss MacIntosh and remains loyal to her, even when others have moved on.
  • The text implies that Old Friday might perceive something beyond what others can see. Vera questions, "had he not known the denudation and the dream, the reality and the illusion, how each must live by some pretense?". This suggests that Old Friday is aware of the underlying truths and illusions that define Miss MacIntosh's existence.
  • Even after Miss MacIntosh's apparent drowning, Old Friday continues to bark by the sea, suggesting a continuation of life and memory even in the face of loss. The waves return "with all but her, waves swollen with winds, the waves like sheep with a thousand bells, aye, while she and the shores and sounding waves wash far away in echo and response".
  • Old Friday's barking by the sea after Miss MacIntosh's disappearance can be interpreted as a symbol of inconsolable grief and mourning. He is a constant reminder of her absence.

Lorena

  • Lorena appears only briefly but her presence is significant in relation to Miss MacIntosh and the broader themes of the novel.
  • Lorena is mentioned as a beautiful dancing girl with dark hair, described as fat, who ran away and became a strip-tease dancer. This characterization contrasts sharply with Miss MacIntosh’s practical, no-nonsense, and grounded nature. Lorena represents a more sensual, flamboyant, and perhaps socially transgressive aspect of femininity, set against Miss MacIntosh’s sobriety, austerity, and emotional reserve.
  • Lorena’s knowledge of Miss MacIntosh’s baldness before Vera discovers it suggests an intimacy or at least a closer social awareness of Miss MacIntosh’s vulnerability and true self.
  • Lorena’s laughter behind her fan and her awareness of this physical fact highlight the difference in how women in the novel perceive and interact with each other, often marked by a mixture of judgment, awareness, and perhaps envy or pity.
  • Lorena helps underscore Miss MacIntosh’s loneliness and isolation. While Lorena embodies escape, sensuality, and perhaps a more conventional form of social engagement (albeit in a marginal way), Miss MacIntosh remains solitary, bald, and burdened by her practical concerns and emotional detachment. Lorena’s figure acts as a foil to Miss MacIntosh, illuminating the nursemaid’s deeper struggles with identity, acceptance, and the harsh realities she faces.

Research: Themes

Isolation and Loneliness

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Miss MacIntosh’s baldness and social detachment highlight profound loneliness, despite her physical presence among others.
  • The Arctic explorer’s life and invitation symbolize a complete withdrawal from society into the harsh, silent wilderness.
  • Vera, as a child, reflects this isolation by absorbing the moods and eccentricities of Miss MacIntosh without fully understanding them.

Truth and Illusion

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Miss MacIntosh claims to always speak the "bald truth," a pun on her physical baldness and metaphorical truthfulness.
  • Her imaginary financial dealings on the Stock Exchange represent the illusion of control and wealth in a chaotic world.
  • The explorer’s tales mix reality with fantasy, blurring the lines between truth, myth, and personal delusion.

The Harshness of Nature and Fate

See: Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos · Fate and Chance

  • The recurrent imagery of storms, drowning, and the sea as a coffin symbolize the inevitability of death and nature’s indifference.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s preoccupation with the fishermen, drowned sailors, and drowned things on the beach emphasizes vulnerability.
  • The Arctic setting and references to icebergs, glaciers, and the North Pole evoke an unforgiving environment that mirrors internal struggles.

Identity and Physical Amputation

See: Identity and the Self

  • Both Miss MacIntosh and the Arctic explorer carry physical and emotional amputations (her baldness and removed ovaries; his lost leg), symbolizing loss and survival.
  • Their shared disabilities forge a silent kinship and mutual understanding of alienation.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s lost hair and wig symbolize the loss of traditional feminine identity and seduction.

Education and Knowledge

See: The Search for Meaning

  • Miss MacIntosh’s relentless questioning, despite her indifference to answers, reflects a ritualistic pursuit of knowledge.
  • Vera’s difficulty in answering symbolizes innocence and underdevelopment, contrasting with Miss MacIntosh’s eccentric wisdom.
  • Subjects range from geography and history to biology and social science, showing a broad but fragmented understanding of the world.

  • Colonialism is a historical practice where one country dominates and controls another, often exploiting its resources and people. While it existed in antiquity, the modern era of colonialism, driven by European powers from the 15th to the 20th century, saw empires expand across the Americas, Africa, and Asia. The Spanish and Portuguese initiated colonization in the Americas, followed by the French and British who established empires in North America, the Caribbean, and other parts of the world.   • The history of Jewish return to Palestine is marked by two major periods: ancient exiles and returns, and the modern Zionist movement. Ancient returns occurred after the Babylonian exile around 538 BCE and were driven by religious and political events. The modern era saw a mass return fueled by the late 19th-century Zionist movement, which aimed to establish a Jewish homeland in Palestine amidst rising antisemitism, culminating in the establishment of the State of Israel in 1948. Around 587 BCE, the Babylonians destroyed the First Temple in Jerusalem and exiled many Jews. In 538 BCE, Cyrus the Great of Persia conquered Babylon and allowed the Jews to return to Judah and rebuild the Temple, which was completed in 515 BCE. The Romans destroyed the Second Temple in 70 CE during the First Jewish-Roman War, leading to a major Jewish diaspora and the loss of sovereignty. The Zionist movement, which began in the late 19th century, aimed to re-establish a Jewish homeland in Palestine. Organized immigration to Palestine began in 1882, with immigrants motivated by a combination of religious ideals and practical/economic considerations, though not yet modern Zionism. The movement gained momentum due to rising antisemitism and pogroms in Europe, which led to increased Jewish immigration to Palestine throughout the 1930s. The British limited Jewish immigration to Palestine with the "White Paper of 1939," which disappointed Zionists, though many Jews still arrived during this period. Following World War II and the liberation of concentration camp survivors, the United Nations partitioned Palestine. On May 14, 1948, the modern State of Israel was proclaimed, re-establishing Jewish sovereignty after nearly 2,000 years.
  • “She hoped that there would be an end to the Tong wars. She hoped that China, the Sleeping Dragon, would awaken”
  • The Tong Wars were a series of violent disputes beginning in the late 19th century among rival Chinese Tong factions centered in the Chinatowns of various American cities, in particular San Francisco. Tong wars could be triggered by a variety of inter-gang grievances, from the public besmirching of another Tong's honor, to failure to make full payment for a "slave girl", to the murder of a rival Tong member. Each Tong had salaried soldiers, known as boo how doy, who fought in Chinatown alleys and streets over the control of opium, prostitution, gambling, and territory. In San Francisco's Chinatown district, the Tong Wars lasted until 1921, with the various criminal Tongs estimated between nineteen and as many as thirty at the peak of the conflict, though the actual number is uncertain, with frequent splintering and mergers between the various Tongs. While a loose alliance, consisting of the Chinatown police, Donaldina Cameron, the courts, and the Chinese community itself tried to stem the tide of the fighting Tongs, it was the 1906 San Francisco earthquake and subsequent fires caused by the earthquake that was the death knell for the Tongs in San Francisco, as it destroyed the brothels, gambling dens, and opium houses that the criminal organizations had used for the majority of their revenue. Recovery was slow for most Tongs, so they abandoned the old Chinatown.

GE expanded into radio, TV, jet engines (WWII), nuclear power, and established its famous research lab, driving American industrial growth through innovations like the first commercial power station (1882) and early home appliances, becoming a key Dow Jones component.
  • Paupers' graves (also potter's fields) in the US were common, unmarked plots for the poor, unknown, or unclaimed, often managed by poorhouses or asylums, reflecting a societal stigma where a cheap, dignified burial was a mark of last resort, with burials sometimes in communal trenches and lacking individual headstones, a practice evolving from English poor laws and continuing through periods like the Civil War era's hardship.

  • "born in Denmark hard by the castle of Elsinore" refers to the Arctic explorer character. This description situates his origin geographically and culturally: he is from Denmark, specifically near Elsinore Castle, which is famously known as the setting of Shakespeare's play Hamlet. Elsinore Castle is a real fortress located in Helsingør, Denmark.
  • “Great Dane” is a German breed of large mastiff-sighthound, which descends from hunting dogs of the Middle Ages used to hunt bears, wild boar, and deer. This expresses the Arctic Explorer's tough, solitary character marked by loss and endurance, a man who carries dog-like loyalty and survival instincts but is also physically and emotionally wounded.
  • “Wilderness Scout” in the U.S. wasn't a formal program but emerged from early youth groups like Ernest Thompson Seton's Woodcraft Indians (1902) and Daniel Carter Beard's Sons of Daniel Boone (1905), which emphasized nature skills and American Indian lore, eventually merging into the Boy Scouts of America (BSA), which itself developed older youth programs like Senior Scouts and Explorers (1935) for advanced wilderness camping and challenging outdoor pursuits, exemplified by trips to Philmont and Canadian wilderness canoe expeditions. Wilderness scouts, frontiersmen, and explorers frequently wore fringed garments, like the "hunting frock" or buckskin jackets, because the fringe served practical purposes, shedding rain and helping the leather dry faster, in addition to being a cultural style adopted from or shared with Native Americans, making it ideal for frontier life.
  • “Ah, my dear, said he, they would both have done better as fiction writers.” Polynesian voyagers, who predate the Maori people, may have reached Antarctica as early as the 7th century, long before Europeans like Cook and Peary. This evidence includes oral histories, legends like that of Hui Te Rangiora, and archaeological findings on subantarctic islands. Maori oral traditions speak of Polynesian explorers like Hui Te Rangiora, who sailed to the southern ocean in the 7th century and encountered areas of ice and a "dark, misty, foggy place". Archaeological evidence, such as artifacts from ancient ovens and middens found on subantarctic islands, suggests Polynesian presence in waters further south than previously understood. The study argues that these oral histories and traditional records, often overlooked by Western scholars, provide evidence that Polynesians were the first humans to see Antarctica. European exploration of Antarctica was not confirmed until the 19th century, with Cook and Peary's expeditions occurring much later than the proposed Maori voyages.
  • "He just knew that they had something in common—their differences from others—and their amputations and their sense of having killed something young and helpless" This passage suggests that both Miss MacIntosh and the explorer share a profound sense of isolation and loss that sets them apart from ordinary people. Their "amputations" are both literal and metaphorical: Miss MacIntosh physically has an amputated breast and is bald, while the explorer has a wooden leg, symbolizing bodily loss. But more importantly, these amputations symbolize emotional or existential wounds, each carries a deep internal absence or sacrifice. The phrase "their sense of having killed something young and helpless" reflects a psychological or spiritual burden they both bear, perhaps guilt or sorrow over lost innocence, lost potential, or something vulnerable within themselves or their past. This shared feeling of having caused or endured damage to something fragile adds to their alienation and mutual understanding.
  • Miss MacIntosh was fond of the Arctic Explorer because he reminded her of her father who had to have his leg cut off when he was older.
  • The phrase "Greater Man" plays on the idea that this dog, by its actions, displayed a kind of grim, practical "manliness" or survival instinct that transcended ordinary human behavior. It suggests the dog embodied qualities of toughness, endurance, and survival that are traditionally associated with manhood, but in a raw, animalistic form. This story is used to evoke themes of survival, loneliness, and the blurred boundaries between humanity and animal nature, as well as the harsh realities faced by both the Arctic Explorer and Miss MacIntosh herself.

  • A Magnetic Pole is the planetary magnetic poles. On Earth it is the North and South magnetic poles where on the planet's surface the planet's magnetic field lines are vertical.
  • “piling up a vast fortune in American Anthracite” Anthracite is hard coal, mined in the United States almost entirely in northeastern Pennsylvania, and it heated American homes and powered industry well into the twentieth century. Its fortunes came at a steep human cost, marked by mine disasters and bitter labor conflicts such as the Anthracite Coal Strike of 1902.
