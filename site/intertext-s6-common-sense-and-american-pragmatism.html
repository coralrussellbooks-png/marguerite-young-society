Common Sense and American Pragmatism

By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Order, Chaos, and Discipline

Philosophy: William James and Pragmatism

Definition from research

Category: optional. Paine's pamphlet is not named, but "common sense" is Miss MacIntosh's watchword in eighteen chapters, and her creed belongs to the American line of plain reason and practical results.

Thomas Paine's Common Sense (1776) argued for American independence in plain language addressed to ordinary readers. More than a century later, William James's Pragmatism (1907) gave a whole lecture to "Pragmatism and Common Sense," and judged ideas by their practical results in lived experience.

Miss MacIntosh stands in that line. "Common sense is the finest sense, she had always said" (chapter 1). She wants Vera to "lead a useful life" and to face "the clear reality of simple things" (chapter 1). She judges every idea by what it does, and "never would she secede her common sense to the illusion" (chapter 63). Her teaching is practical to the point of comedy. She asks what the best time is to plant corn, in the middle of reading Bunyan (chapter 3).

The novel honors her creed and tests it. The woman of common sense wears a wig, keeps a secret past, and walks into the sea. The creed survives her failure, and the novel lets both stand.
