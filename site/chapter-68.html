To All My Darlings

Chapter 68

On Chapter 68 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 10, 2026

← Chapter 67 · Home · Chapter 69 →

Content note: This chapter tells of a Black man hanged in Kentucky on a false charge of sleeping with a white woman, and of the hangman’s wife, who hanged herself; the hangman sees “a bevy of little bodies hanging” from the same tree and nearly beats his children with a branch from it.

Summary

Night has fallen when the bus comes to rest in a desolate Indiana village before the Tavern, “built in 1827 by God’s servants who were believers in the lamb.” Moses bounds inside to the mother who thinks he is her bridegroom, and Madge and Homer walk off toward the river. Waiting in a rocking chair to sign in, Vera dreams herself old in an instant and remembers Miss MacIntosh’s galoshes thrown up by the tide. At the desk the deaf, tattooed landlord, who says he has been on four ships that went down, talks with Mr. Weed, the Christian hangman, on his way home from a hanging. Mr. Weed tells of his first wife, Marie, who hanged herself in the orchard when she feared she had conceived again; of his children, who play at hanging and hang their dolls and a blind dog from the same peach tree; of murders he has heard of; and of a Black man in Kentucky, hanged for sleeping with a white woman, as he surely never did, and cut down alive. Mr. Weed finds in his pocket the black poker chip from an unsolved murder, and the two men climb the stairs while no clock strikes.

Key passages

From the research

  • The opening passage reflects on how coming into a village at night changes one’s perception permanently, with shadows that never lift and reality that must be accepted no matter what it is. Vera emphasizes that reality, whether joyful or bleak, is real and must be confronted. This sets a tone of melancholy and philosophical reflection about the nature of existence and perception.
  • The detailed description of the Tavern and the surrounding town conveys a sense of decay, emptiness, and abandonment. The Tavern, despite its sign proclaiming it was built by “God’s servants,” appears as just another commercial establishment, run-down and shadowy. This contrasts with Vera’s later realization of the Tavern’s deeper, more mysterious interior life.
  • Moses’s complex relationship with his old mother, who is sick and deluded, is a poignant metaphor for duty, denial, and the boundaries between love and obligation. Moses obeys “traffic rules” even in the “bridal chamber” with his mother, symbolizing his refusal to engage fully with her fantasies or to take her place.
  • Vera reveals a deep inner fear of her own “defective character,” which loves imperfections, distortions, and the dead more than the living reality. This passage underscores a theme of alienation and longing for something unattainable or lost.
  • The arrival and conversation with the professional hangman introduces a major symbolic figure who embodies death, justice, and mercy. Mr. Weed’s reflections on his job, the nature of crime, punishment, and the human conscience provide a grim but humane perspective on life and death.
  • Mr. Weed’s narrative about his first wife who hanged herself, his children who play at hanging themselves, and his deep internal conflict reveals the toll his work takes on his personal life and psyche. His dreams of cooking live pigs, his vegetarianism, and his haunted conscience highlight the paradox of his profession.
  • Mr. Weed’s observations about criminals acting like sleepwalkers, the subtlety of some murders, and the idea that many crimes remain undiscovered underscore the themes of human fallibility and the complexity of justice. His story about the man who killed his wife because of jealousy over cats is a striking example.
  • The black poker chip, found near a severed woman’s head but ultimately meaningless, symbolizes the confusing and often contradictory clues in the search for truth and justice. Mr. Weed carrying the chip unknowingly for years suggests the persistence of unresolved mysteries.
  • The dialogue between Mr. Weed and the landlord, including the landlord’s impatience and Mr. Weed’s acceptance of his monotonous life, humanizes both characters and closes the chapter with a mix of resignation and quiet dignity.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “For there was only one definition of reality, that it was real.” What does Vera include in reality, and whom?
  • “The bald woman was the woman I loved.” Why does Vera say it here, in a strange hotel lobby?
  • “There seems to be no perfect hanging but only a perfect knot.” What is the difference for Mr. Weed?

Characters

  • Mr. Weed “was a vegetarian, he protested,” and “Off the line of his duty, he would never kill anything.” How does he keep his work and his conscience apart?
  • The landlord says, “I tie lovers’ knots.” What do his knots and the hangman’s have in common?
  • Mr. Weed “had stopped to put on his galoshes” and was “a split second too late” to cut Marie down. Why does Miss MacIntosh’s drowning come into the same chapter as galoshes?

Connections

  • The Black man in Kentucky was hanged “for sleeping with a white woman as he surely never did,” and it was “the tale of what never happened and yet happened, happened every day in America.” What is Young saying about lynching?
  • “Only innocent children would think of blindfolding the blind.” What do Mr. Weed’s children understand about him?
  • “Who wants Death at a wedding?” the landlord asks. Where else in the novel do death and marriage meet?

Reflection

  • Mr. Weed believes “most men did not know what they were doing when they committed a crime or why.” Does that excuse anyone?
  • “The insignificant object was the important one.” Is that true of the black poker chip?
  • Vera says she “had loved only the dead.” Can loving the dead keep us from the living?

Research: Characters

Vera

  • Vera is introspective, haunted by memories and dreams, struggling with the boundary between reality and illusion.
  • Vera reflects on personal loss, especially of Miss MacIntosh, and experiences fear of her own imperfect character and fate.

Moses

  • A figure representing avoidance and detachment, Moses goes home to a mother who believes he is her bridegroom, but he maintains distance, obeying “traffic rules” metaphorically to avoid emotional entanglement.
  • The bus driver feels obligated to care for his mother, who he says thinks he is “a baby crying in her arms.” He made a promise to never leave her. This sense of responsibility, whether real or imagined, weighs heavily on him and contributes to his anxieties.
  • The bus driver’s mother exists in a dreamlike state, much like the characters Vera encounters throughout her journey which blurs the lines between reality and fantasy.
  • The bus driver’s mother is sometimes depicted as young and beautiful, with breasts filled with milk. This suggests a longing for an idealized past, a time of innocence and maternal comfort. She is associated with the potential, yet ultimately unfulfilled, union.
  • The bus driver’s relationship with his mother can be interpreted as a symbol of his internal conflict between his desire for freedom and his sense of obligation. He dreams of escaping his mundane life as a bus driver. However, his commitment to his mother, whether self-imposed or externally enforced, keeps him tethered to his present circumstances.
  • The bus driver says that he will not cut his hair as long as “the Democrats were running us,” and that he has made a promise to his mother to never cut his hair. It seems as though the bus driver’s long hair is a symbol of his commitment to his mother and his abstinence from the more modern world.

Mr. Weed (The Christian Hangman)

  • Central to the chapter, Mr. Weed is a professional executioner who views his work as a mission to provide a painless death.
  • He is a deeply complex character: a good father, haunted by personal tragedy (his wife Marie’s suicide), and tormented by memories and dreams.
  • He discusses his profession with a mix of bitterness, humor, and philosophical insight, revealing his tolerance and humanity despite his grim role.
  • Mr. Weed recounts various cases of crime, murder, and the inefficiency or cruelty of some executions, highlighting the moral ambiguities of justice.
  • He is associated with symbolic objects (a Jew’s harp, a Parker fountain pen, and a black poker chip) each with narrative significance.

Parker Fountain Pen

  • The pen symbolizes death. Mr. Weed says the pen was given to him by the condemned man’s grateful relatives. Additionally, the hangman is described as signing the hotel register with the pen.

Black Poker Chip

  • The black poker chip is found near the head of a murdered woman wearing rose-colored sunglasses, but it’s a clue that can never be connected to the crime.
  • Even though it seems like it should be important, it ultimately has no bearing on solving the murder. The black poker chip plays a great part in the case but is a clue which led nowhere, nowhere at all, like a business man’s address book which was found in the hollow of a nearby oak tree. It was simply confusing.
  • Mr. Weed later finds a black poker chip in his coat pocket, but he claims he never gambles and has no idea how it got there. This adds another layer of mystery and confusion.
  • The Hangman finds the poker chip in his oldest suit, the suit he said that he wears to hangings and to church.
  • The black poker chip represents the often confusing and ultimately meaningless details that can arise in investigations, and it highlights the limitations of human understanding in the face of complex and tragic events.

Jew’s Harp

  • The hangman is described as always playing his Jew’s harp, particularly when taking the shortcut home through the orchard. This suggests the Jew’s harp is part of his routine, a habit he performs after a hanging.
  • The Jew’s harp, often associated with folk music and simple pleasures, contrasts sharply with the hangman’s profession. This incongruity highlights the hangman’s attempts to reconcile his role as an executioner with his self-perception as a good, peace-loving man.
  • Despite his constant playing, the Jew’s harp ultimately fails to console the hangman from the troubles and darkness within his life, particularly the disturbing events involving his children. The music seemingly cannot penetrate the deeper issues plaguing him.
  • The hangman makes a point of saying, “A Jew’s harp is not Jewish.” This statement may symbolize the way the hangman himself is often misunderstood. He is a man whose profession is death, yet he sees himself as a humanitarian who brings a painless end to suffering.
  • Mr. Weed’s profession as a hangman directly symbolizes capital punishment and the administration of justice. He embodies the often-unseen figure who carries out the state’s ultimate punishment.
  • Despite the grim nature of his work, Mr. Weed is portrayed as an ordinary, even respectable, individual, a family man who attends church. This highlights the moral ambiguity and societal complicity in the death penalty, suggesting that even those who carry out such acts can be integrated into mainstream society.
  • Mr. Weed’s constant connection to death and crime, as well as his troubled dreams, symbolize the burden of the past and the lingering presence of violence and injustice in American history.
  • The juxtaposition of Mr. Weed’s morbid occupation with his mundane, everyday life (his family, his farm) reflects a common theme in American literature and art: the grotesque and the mundane coexisting, often uneasily, in the same space.
  • Mr. Weed’s interest in the innocence of children and his fear of corrupting them could symbolize a lost sense of American innocence, the idea that the nation has been tainted by violence and injustice.
  • Mr. Weed’s wide-brimmed Western hat and his matter-of-fact attitude may evoke the spirit of the American frontier, where violence and justice were often intertwined.
  • Just as Mr. Weed is the man who makes it all painless, the rest of the people are silent accepters of death.
  • Mr. Weed’s story of the Black man in Kentucky, hanged for sleeping with a white woman “as he surely never did,” names the racial terror of lynching outright: it was “the tale of what never happened and yet happened, happened every day in America.” See Lynching and Racial Violence.

Marie Weed, his first wife who hanged herself

  • Marie Weed, the first wife of the hangman, is a character defined by her tragic end and the haunting impact she has on her husband’s life.
  • Marie dies by suicide, hanging herself from a peach tree in their orchard. This act is driven by her fear of another pregnancy.
  • Her death profoundly affects her husband, the hangman, turning him into a tormented figure. He is haunted by the memory of cutting her down “a split second too late,” and he sees her children playing at hanging themselves, symbolizing his guilt and the inescapable presence of death in his life. The grief and guilt transform him into a man who, despite his profession, is deeply averse to violence.
  • Marie’s character is primarily revealed through the hangman’s recollections and descriptions, creating a sense of mystery and incompleteness. Vera pieces together her image through the hangman’s stories of his past. She is characterized by her absence and the powerful effect she has on those left behind.
  • Marie’s death and subsequent presence in the hangman’s memories symbolize the destructive power of despair and the enduring consequences of loss. The image of her hanging from a tree laden with peach blossoms represents the juxtaposition of beauty and tragedy, life and death.
  • The hangman’s stories of Marie may be connected to Vera’s quest for meaning in the lives of marginalized people. The hangman himself is a marginalized character who is haunted by the past, similar to Vera and other characters she encounters.
  • Marie, while not directly present in the narrative, is a crucial figure in understanding the hangman and the themes of death, guilt, and the search for meaning.

Julia Weed, his second wife

  • Julia Weed is the second wife of the hangman. Her relationship with him is marked by a sense of his past deeds and the shadow they cast on their present life.
  • Julia’s position as the second wife is complicated by the circumstances of the first wife’s death, which was a suicide. She is sometimes found wearing the clothes of his first wife, Marie.
  • Julia is described as having “the mind of a child.” Her understanding of the hangman’s work and the events surrounding his first wife’s death seem limited.
  • She is seen as someone who does not “think things through.” This suggests a lack of depth or introspection, contributing to the perception of her as somewhat naive or unaware of the darker aspects of her situation.
  • She is connected to a history of sorrow and death, not only through her husband’s profession but also through the tragic end of his first wife. This association highlights the theme of the past’s inescapable influence on the present.
  • There’s a contrast between her apparent innocence and the knowledge she possesses, or lacks, about her husband’s profession and the events of the past. This tension adds to the complexity of her character.
  • The narrative suggests that Julia, in her innocence and limited awareness, is a victim of circumstances, a woman drawn into a situation she may not fully comprehend.
  • Julia Weed is portrayed as a simple, somewhat naive woman caught in the web of her husband’s dark profession and the tragic history of his past. Her character serves as a vehicle to explore themes of death, guilt, and the burden of memory.

The Landlord

  • The Tavern’s landlord is pragmatic, skeptical, and somewhat cynical.
  • He interacts with Mr. Weed with a mixture of humor and impatience, grounding the scene in everyday reality.
  • His references to “turkeys and chickens” and dismissive attitude toward Mr. Weed’s stories add a humanizing, mundane contrast to the heavy themes.
  • In Chapter 69, the “Remember the Marne” tattoo suggests he is a veteran who experienced the horrors of World War I.
  • His world-weariness and the suggestion that his hotel “sinks every night” reflect the disillusionment and cynicism that followed World War I.
  • In later chapters, his background in intelligence implies knowledge of hidden truths and clandestine operations.
  • He listens to the troubles of all kinds of people, suggesting that he represents the common man and those who have been forgotten by society.
  • In Chapter 71, the mix of old and new, the presence of both a “lieutenant governor of Indiana” and “freaks” at the hotel, allude to a changing and increasingly diverse America.
  • He is a complex figure whose symbolism reflects the disillusionment, hardships, and changing social landscape of America in the first half of the 20th century.

Research: Themes

Reality and Perception

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Vera reflects on the nature of reality, emphasizing that whatever is experienced or perceived is “real,” whether it is a dream, a memory, or a physical object.
  • Reality is not confined to conventional or positive experiences but includes shadows, decay, and the unreal or rejected (e.g., abandoned objects, shadows on the hills).
  • This theme is introduced early on, where the village at night is described as a place where shadows never fade, suggesting persistent darkness or hidden truths.

Death and the Inevitability of Fate

See: Death and Mortality · Fate and Chance

  • The chapter dwells heavily on death, especially through the experiences and reflections of the Christian hangman, who symbolizes the finality of mortality.
  • Mr. Weed’s profession is portrayed with a paradoxical humanity; he is portrayed as a missionary of painless death, emphasizing efficiency and compassion within a grim role.
  • Death is also symbolized as a “marital knot” or a binding connection that cannot be undone, suggesting an eternal union with mortality.

Memory, Loss, and the Past

See: Memory and the Past · Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • Vera recalls memories of lost loved ones, such as Miss MacIntosh, whose disappearance haunts Vera.
  • Memories blur with dreams and reality, suggesting a struggle to reconcile the past with the present.
  • Mr. Weed’s personal history, including the hanging of his first wife and his children’s strange behaviors, deepens the motif of loss and haunting memories.

Human Imperfection and Guilt

See: Guilt, Innocence, and Justice

  • Characters are deeply flawed and haunted by guilt, failures, and secrets (e.g., Mr. Weed’s reflections on the imperfect hanging, criminals who act unknowingly, and the failure to prevent tragedies).
  • Mr. Weed discusses the subtlety of crime and how even well-planned acts can betray the perpetrator by small, unnoticed details.
  • The narrative suggests a tension between the desire for justice and the complexity of human nature.

  • The atmosphere is filled with silence, shadows, and a slow, suffocating sense of inevitability, preparing the reader for the philosophical and existential meditations that follow.

Research: Resources

  • “there was only one definition of reality, that it was real”: The line emphasizes a tautological but deeply existential assertion: reality is defined by its own existence as experienced or known, regardless of its form. “even the unreal things were real,” points to an inclusive, perhaps subjective or phenomenological, understanding of reality where things dismissed or marginalized by society (ignored, rejected, unloved) are nonetheless part of the fabric of reality. This echoes themes of illusion versus reality, and the permeability between them, which run throughout the novel. Philosophically, this passage represents a theme often associated with existentialism and phenomenology: that reality is defined by experience and perception rather than by fixed, external definitions. It also touches on the fluidity of reality, blurring lines between dream and waking life, appearance and essence, presence and absence. The passage conveys that reality is not limited to the tangible or socially accepted but includes dreams, illusions, forgotten or rejected beings, and all forms of existence or perception. It challenges rigid dichotomies and invites a broader, more inclusive understanding of what is real. See Existentialism and Phenomenology.
  • “real as Mr. Spitzer’s brother who might be the musician or the gambler”: See Peron Spitzer and Joachim Spitzer.
  • “Josh’s Place, a wreath of fuzzy blue neon lights”: Georges Claude showed the first neon sign in Paris in 1910, and neon came to American roadsides from 1923, when a Los Angeles car dealer bought two signs.
  • “obviously what was meant was Bridge Ahead”: Young saw the sign: “By the way, I saw that in a small town” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See Moses Hunnecker.
  • “Between two scrofulous white pillars”: Scrofulous (adjective) is an historical term for tuberculosis affecting the lymph nodes (especially in the neck, causing swelling) or, figuratively, describing something or someone morally corrupt, degenerate, or having a sickly, unhealthy appearance. It’s often used metaphorically to imply decay, corruption, or poor quality.
  • “The Tavern, built in 1827 by God’s servants who were believers in the lamb”: The town is New Harmony, Indiana, the setting of Angel in the Forest (1945). “Very astute readers of people who have been there know that it is the same town to which Vera arrives. I left out all utopian references. It’s the same town exactly” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983). The bus “stops before an old hotel where there is a tattooed landlord. And my heroine meets her first encounter with rural reality as a hangman who tells of his excellent fruit orchards. These characters appear in both books” (Marguerite Young, talk on the reissue of Angel in the Forest, 1966). George Rapp’s Harmony Society founded the town in 1814 and sold it to Robert Owen in 1825. See The Tavern Landlord and Mr. Weed. The Lamb is the Christ of Revelation: “Worthy is the Lamb that was slain” (Revelation 5:12, KJV). See Revelation and the Apocalypse and Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest.
  • “stayed off the soft shoulder, the narrow neck, the crumbling breast of the hill”: The road signs in the marriage bed came from “a comedian’s joke.” When a reviewer predicted the book’s jokes would end up on the nightclub circuit, Young said, “how little does he dream that a lot of it came from that in the first place?” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See Moses Hunnecker.
  • “a hotel on Twelfth Street for white-sheeted Hindu sailors”: Sailors from India, known as lascars, crewed British merchant ships in large numbers from the eighteenth century onward and were a familiar sight in Atlantic port cities, often lodging in boarding houses set aside for them between voyages.
  • “I should have inquired at the Seamen’s Institute”: The Seamen’s Church Institute of New York, founded in 1834, opened its great building at 25 South Street in 1913, with the Titanic Memorial Lighthouse on its roof. See The Sea and Water.
  • “Oh, have you seen my love, Miss MacIntosh, my darling”: “Saw ye him whom my soul loveth?” (Song of Solomon 3:3, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “This, Mr. Spitzer would say, is Exhibit A in the strange case of the disappearing woman”: Mr. Spitzer’s phrase echoes Stevenson’s Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde (1886).
  • “her little Pilgrim’s Progress is swollen with salt water”: John Bunyan’s The Pilgrim’s Progress (1678), Miss MacIntosh’s book.
  • “if there was no corpus delicti”: Corpus delicti, “the body of the crime,” means the proof that a crime was committed, not the corpse itself, though without a body courts were long unwilling to find a murder. See Guilt, Innocence, and Justice.
  • “I would see her shining through every cloud”: Compare Milton’s Comus (1634): “Was I deceived, or did a sable cloud / Turn forth her silver lining on the night?,” the source of every cloud’s silver lining. See Milton.
  • “like parallel lines which are said to meet in infinity”: In projective geometry, begun by Girard Desargues in 1639, parallel lines meet at a point at infinity.
  • “Pandora’s”: In Hesiod’s Works and Days, Pandora opens a jar and lets loose every evil on mankind, leaving only hope inside; Erasmus turned the jar into a box in 1508. See Greek Mythology.
  • “Parker fountain pen”: Parker fountain pen may refer to the Parker Pen Company, an American manufacturer of luxury writing pens, founded in 1888. Its Duofold of 1921 made Parker’s name.
  • “Hair, hair, that was the great deception, hair, a woman’s crowning glory, hair which keeps growing in the grave”: “But if a woman have long hair, it is a glory to her: for her hair is given her for a covering” (1 Corinthians 11:15, KJV). Hair does not grow in the grave; the skin draws back as it dries, so that hair and nails seem longer. See The Bible and Hair, Wig, and Baldness.
  • “I had no children except a foetus who had turned to stone”: A lithopedion, or stone child, is a fetus that dies outside the womb and calcifies, sometimes carried for decades. The most famous was found in Madame Colombe Chatri of Sens in 1582, twenty-eight years after her pregnancy. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “Who is the father? asked Doctor O’Leary”: Dr. Justice O’Leary, the old doctor of Chapter 67. See Justice O’Leary.
  • “A torn black umbrella was blowing like a crow against the surf”: Miss MacIntosh’s umbrella on the beach. See the Chapter 65 resources and The Umbrella.
  • “signing in, was the Christian hangman, Mr. Weed”: “There was a Christian hangman, exactly like this, upon whom I based this story” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983). Young had heard a local hangman discussed at “a dinner party in a rural district” and “had read many books on hangmen and their psychology” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965), and “every single criminal case he speaks of is based upon a real case” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983). See Mr. Weed.
    • Compare G. Phil Hanna (died 1948) of Epworth, Illinois, across the Wabash from New Harmony, a farmer called the humane hangman. He is said to have taken up the work after seeing a botched hanging at McLeansboro, Illinois, in 1896, so that hangings would be done without pain; he took no pay and assisted at the hanging of Rainey Bethea in 1936. See Mr. Weed.
  • “I hope you find your second wife and your children by your first wife well”: See The Tavern Landlord and Julia Weed.
  • “his heels had been nipped by an old hound pursuing him through oceanic storms”: Compare Francis Thompson’s “The Hound of Heaven” (1890), on a soul pursued by God: “I fled Him, down the nights and down the days.” See Dogs.
  • “sure as if I were the Jolly Roger”: The Jolly Roger is the iconic pirate flag, typically a skull and crossbones on a black background, used to intimidate ships into surrendering during the “Golden Age of Piracy,” symbolizing death and defiance, though real pirate flags varied and sometimes were just black; the name might come from “Jolie Rouge” (pretty red) or a pirate named Bartholomew Roberts. It served as a fearsome warning, but its specific skull-and-bones design was popularized by pirates like Edward England and became a cultural symbol of rebellion and pirate lore.
  • “Four times I have been on ships that went down”: Compare Violet Jessop, a stewardess for the White Star Line, who survived the Olympic’s collision in 1911, the Titanic in 1912, and the sinking of the Britannic in 1916, and was called Miss Unsinkable. See Women, Gender, and Marriage and The Sea and Water.
  • “I was on the Titanic”: The RMS Titanic was a British passenger liner, operated by the White Star Line, which sank in the North Atlantic Ocean on 15 April 1912 after striking an iceberg during her maiden voyage from Southampton, UK, to New York City. (Wikipedia) About 1,500 people died.
  • “I was on the Lusitania”: The RMS Lusitania, a British ocean liner operated by the Cunard Steamship Company sunk by a German U-boat in World War I. The U-20 torpedoed her off Ireland on May 7, 1915, and 1,198 people died, 128 of them Americans.
  • “I was on the White Star Castle”: No ship called the White Star Castle existed. The landlord blends the White Star Line with the Union-Castle Line, whose ships all bore castle names.
    • The White Star Castle may refer to the RMS Majestic which was a White Star ocean liner working on the North Atlantic run, originally launched in 1914. She served the Royal Navy as the training ship HMS Caledonia before catching fire in 1939 and sinking. She was subsequently raised and scrapped in 1943.
  • “I was on the Prince Edward which was hounded by the wreck of the White Star”: No record has been found of a Prince Edward struck by a drifting wreck; the landlord’s tale grows with the telling.
    • Prince Edward may refer to the fourth HMS Black Prince, after Edward, the Black Prince, the eldest son of King Edward III of England, launched in 1904 and was a Duke of Edinburgh-class cruiser sunk with all hands during the Battle of Jutland in 1916.
  • “until Gabriel blows his horn”: The Bible does not name Gabriel as the trumpeter of Judgment Day: “For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God” (1 Thessalonians 4:16, KJV). The tradition of Gabriel’s horn came down through African American spirituals such as “Blow Your Trumpet, Gabriel.” See Angels and Archangels and The Bible.
  • “Bends, hitches, splices”: The history of knots is ancient, predating tools like the wheel, with evidence of rope-making and knots from 15,000-17,000 years ago for hunting, fishing, and shelter, evolving into essential technology for sailing, construction, record-keeping (like the Inca Khipu), and art (Chinese knots), and later formalized in mathematics (Knot Theory) and modern uses from surgery to climbing. Knots developed independently across cultures out of necessity, becoming fundamental to civilization’s advancement and symbolic meaning. The landlord is contrasting his own experience and understanding of “knots” with that of the hangman. While the hangman knows literal knots used in hangings, the landlord speaks metaphorically of “lovers’ knots”: the bonds of marriage that are meant to be unbreakable until the end of time, “until Gabriel blows his horn” (symbolizing Judgment Day), or even beyond, suggesting that even death should not part true lovers. The landlord, who runs a hotel that has endured floods and fire, is well-versed in various types of practical knots (overhand, figure-of-eight, running bowline, and more) that have specific uses in sailing and daily life. He questions the hangman’s ability to understand the “marital knot,” implying that the hangman’s knowledge is limited and that he has not even mastered the basics of tying his own bow-tie properly, a task he suggests is better suited to a wife. The landlord’s metaphorical description emphasizes the strength and permanence of the marital bond, contrasting it with the hangman’s grim profession and the fleeting nature of life and death. The whimsical reference to dolphins sporting over his head as his ship went down and the sinking hotel that “sinks every night” adds a layer of imagery connecting the fragility of human constructs and relationships to the natural world and its constant changes: oceans, ships, drifting continents. This passage reflects the novel’s recurring themes of impermanence, human bonds, and the interplay between reality and metaphor. It also highlights the characters’ different perspectives on life, love, and death: the landlord’s practical yet poetic view versus the hangman’s somber, fatalistic outlook.
  • “even be he the Ghostly Reaper coming with his scythe”: The Grim Reaper, Death with his scythe, harvesting souls as a farmer mows wheat. See Death and Mortality.
  • “the long drop”: The long drop, which breaks the neck instead of strangling, was introduced in England by the hangman William Marwood in 1872, and drops were later set from tables by the weight of the condemned.
  • “a handful of dust”: “For dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return” (Genesis 3:19, KJV). Compare T. S. Eliot: “I will show you fear in a handful of dust” (The Waste Land, 1922). See Genesis.
  • “The Christian Science Monitor”: The Christian Science Monitor commonly known as The Monitor, is a nonprofit news organization that publishes daily articles in electronic format as well as a weekly print edition. It was founded in 1908 as a daily newspaper by Mary Baker Eddy, the founder of the Church of Christ, Scientist. (Wikipedia) Mary Baker Eddy was the founder of Christian Science. See American Religious Movements and Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “Marie, she was an Oliver”: Mr. Weed said his first wife’s name was Marie and her maiden name was Oliver and that they were known for hanging themselves. There are three famous Olivers this may refer to:
    • Andrew Oliver was a merchant and public official in the Province of Massachusetts Bay. Born into a wealthy and politically powerful merchant family, he is best known as the Massachusetts official responsible for implementing the provisions of the Stamp Act, for which he was hanged in effigy. He never actually carried out those duties, and was later commissioned as the province’s lieutenant governor. (Wikipedia)
    • Oliver Cromwell, Lord Protector and ruler of the English Commonwealth after the defeat and beheading of King Charles I during the English Civil War, died on 3 September 1658 of natural causes and was given a public funeral at Westminster Abbey equal to those of the monarchs who came before him. When King Charles II was recalled from exile, his new parliament, in January 1661, ordered the disinterment of the elder Cromwell’s body from Westminster Abbey, for a posthumous execution. He was left hanging until the afternoon before being cut down and beheaded. (Wikipedia)
    • Oliver Moore was lynched in North Carolina in 1930. Oliver Moore was accused of assaulting two young white girls, Ethel, age 5, and Lucile Morgan, age 7, daughters of Mr. and Mrs. E. F. Morgan, who lived on the county line between Wilson and Edgecombe Counties, on the night of 18 July 1930. Moore evaded capture for nearly a month but was finally caught and arrested on 15 August 1930 and jailed in Tarboro, NC, in the county jail. At around midnight on 18 August 1930, a mob of masked men broke Moore out of jail and took to a wooded area on the Wilson-Edgecombe County line just inside Wilson County not far from the scene of the alleged assault, hung him from a tree with a rope around his chest, and shot him repeatedly until he was dead. By the next morning when it appears this photo was taken, thousands of people from all around the area had gathered to view the lynching aftermath and Moore’s body hanging riddled with bullets from the tree. See Lynching and Racial Violence.
  • “Water, water everywhere, and not a drop to drink”: Coleridge wrote “Water, water, every where, / Nor any drop to drink” (The Rime of the Ancient Mariner, 1798); the line is nearly always misquoted, as here. The mad rich man, sailing with his guilt, is a Mariner of his own. See Romantic Poets.
    • “Water, water everywhere, and not a drop to drink” is a line from the poem by Samuel Taylor Coleridge, The Rime of the Ancient Mariner.
  • “a dead man’s hat”: Mr. Weed mirrors Mr. Bonebreaker, who also wears dead people’s clothes: a dead waiter’s coat and “a dead man’s shoes.” See the Chapter 65 resources. See Mr. Titus Bonebreaker.
  • “she feared she had conceived again”: The Comstock Act of 1873, named for the anti-vice crusader Anthony Comstock, made it a crime to mail contraceptives or information about them. This was challenged by Margaret Sanger in 1914. The Supreme Court protected contraception for married couples in Griswold v. Connecticut (1965) and for the unmarried in Eisenstadt v. Baird (1972). See Women, Gender, and Marriage and Suicide in History and Literature.
    • Suicide is a leading cause of death during pregnancy and the postpartum period, often linked to underlying mental health conditions. Mental health conditions, including postpartum depression and psychosis, can lead to increased risk of maternal suicide. While not directly a cause of suicide, the inability to control the number of children a woman has can be a major stressor that contributes to or worsens mental health conditions, which in turn can increase the risk of suicide.
  • “Something fell and rolled along the ground, and yet the bough broke not”: The hangman’s riddle turns the nursery rhyme “Rock-a-bye, baby,” printed in Mother Goose’s Melody (about 1765): “When the bough breaks, the cradle will fall.” Marie feared she had conceived again. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “A Jew’s harp is not Jewish”: The Jew’s harp, a twanging mouth instrument known in Asia for more than two thousand years, has nothing to do with Jews; the English name dates from the sixteenth century, and no one knows its origin. See Music and Instruments.
  • “when he saw a bevy of little bodies hanging from the peach tree”: See Marie Weed.
  • “if you give a man enough rope, he hangs himself”: This proverb means if you give people the opportunity to do something wrong or detrimental to themselves, they will usually do it; one does not need to interfere to bring about someone’s downfall.
  • “There were a great many missing women, apparently”: For the US current statistics show there are more females missing under the age of 21 than males. Over the age of 21 there are more males missing than females. Half of all missing persons are minorities.
  • “They were playing blind man’s buff behind the hill”: In blind man’s buff, a blindfolded player gropes to catch the others, as the children had blindfolded the blind dog. See Dogs and Eyes and Blindness.
  • “He was a vegetarian, he protested”: Christian vegetarianism is the practice of abstaining from meat for faith-based reasons, focusing on themes like stewardship of creation, compassion, health, and honoring God, though the Bible permits meat-eating, with varied interpretations on animal rights and the ideal diet (some pointing to Genesis’s plant-based start, others to Jesus eating fish). While not a universal command, it’s seen as a way to live out Christian values. The Bible Christian Church, which forbade meat, came to Philadelphia from England in 1817; the Seventh-day Adventists later urged a meatless diet, as Mr. Bonebreaker kept. See the Chapter 65 resources. See American Religious Movements.
  • “we are being displaced by the electric chair”: The methods for execution in the US are lethal injection, electrocution, firing squad, and the gas chamber. An offender can choose an alternate method such as hanging. 1996 was the last time a person was executed by hanging in the US. The first man put to death in the electric chair was William Kemmler, at Auburn, New York, in 1890, and Nevada first used a gas chamber in 1924.
  • “I am almost the last leaf left hanging on the bough”: Compare Oliver Wendell Holmes’s “The Last Leaf” (1831): “And if I should live to be / The last leaf upon the tree.”
  • “race-horse tout”: A race-horse tout offers racing tips for a share of any resulting winnings.
  • “a race-track in Bermuda”: Bermuda’s main horse racing venue is the Bermuda National Equestrian Center (NEC) in Devonshire, formerly Vesey Street Racetrack, hosting popular harness pony racing (driving) and show jumping from September to May, with races often held under lights or Sunday afternoons. The island’s unique, small, oval track provides challenging races for ponies pulling carts.
  • “Dark Lady was winning”: The racehorse’s name recalls the “Dark Lady” of Shakespeare’s Sonnets 127 to 152, the mysterious woman of the poet’s desire. See Shakespeare.
  • “as a young man in Kentucky”: Rainey Bethea, a young Black man, was hanged in Owensboro, Kentucky, on August 14, 1936, for the rape of Lischia Edwards, a seventy-year-old white woman; he was charged with rape and not murder, which under Kentucky law allowed a public hanging. About 20,000 people came, many because the sheriff, Florence Thompson, a widow who had succeeded her husband, was to spring the trap; in the end a former policeman, Arthur Hash, did it. It was the last public execution in the United States. See the Equal Justice Initiative’s report Lynching in America (2015). See Lynching and Racial Violence.
  • “The Negro was hanged for sleeping with a white woman”: This is a story told by the Christian hangman Mr. Weed about a botched lynching of a Black man in Kentucky. According to the tale, the man was hanged for the accusation of sleeping with a white woman, an accusation that, as the hangman himself says, was false: “as he surely never did.” The hangman describes how the man was cut down from the noose a split second too soon, meaning he was still technically alive and was later seen pushing a baby buggy, frightening the local white community. The hangman reflects on the “inefficiency” of the hanging, suggesting that his role was to make death as swift and painless as possible, but here the man suffered unnecessarily by surviving the hanging. This passage echoes real historical accounts of lynching in the United States, particularly in the South, where Black men were often falsely accused of crimes, especially of violating racial codes like alleged relations with white women. Lynchings were extrajudicial killings by mobs, commonly by hanging, sometimes with brutal torture. There are documented cases where victims were cut down prematurely or survived the initial hanging, leading to further suffering before death or even escape, heightening the terror of such acts. The passage metaphorically highlights the cruelty and injustice of these acts, along with the hangman’s conflicted role: he sees himself as a Christian bringing mercy through a swift death, yet is part of a brutal system that perpetuates racial violence and injustice. The “unofficially alive” status of the man symbolizes the liminal, tortured state of lynching victims: condemned by society, yet clinging to life in a world that refuses them justice or dignity. This passage serves as a fictional reflection on historical racial violence in America, underscoring the themes of injustice, suffering, and moral ambiguity surrounding lynching and the societal roles that participated in or failed to prevent such atrocities. See Lynching and Racial Violence.
    • Young said the rope was real: “that rope was hanging, still hanging, in a little southern town where the last black man had been lynched, where I lived for a while. And then there had been a black man who did walk away and push the baby carriage up hill. Now, he died, but I used that image” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983).
    • In Southern Horrors: Lynch Law in All Its Phases (1892), Ida B. Wells showed that the charge of rape or of relations with a white woman was the usual pretext for lynching Black men, and was usually false. See Lynching and Racial Violence and Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “Who would want to be unofficially alive?”: In 1946 Louisiana’s portable electric chair failed to kill Willie Francis, a Black teenager of seventeen; the Supreme Court allowed the state to try again (Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 1947), and he was executed that May. See Lynching and Racial Violence.
  • “most men did not know what they were doing when they committed a crime or why”: In the US over 50% of inmates out of the 2.2 million people incarcerated are believed to be suffering from some kind of mental illness.
  • “The Bluebeards, he was speaking of them”: In Charles Perrault’s “Bluebeard” (1697), a wife finds the bodies of the wives before her in a locked room. Henri Landru, the “French Bluebeard,” was guillotined in 1922 for murdering ten women. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “buried her under a cement pavement in the basement”: Compare Dr. Hawley Harvey Crippen, an American in London, who buried his wife Cora’s remains under the cellar floor in 1910 and told their friends she had gone back to America and died in California. He was caught by wireless at sea and hanged that November.
  • “It is the death of our Christian civilization”: Those who speak of the death of Christian civilization often point to the rise of secularism, “dechurching,” and declining Christian influence in the West, viewing it as the end of Christendom, a historical era where Christianity dominated public life, leading to new worldviews like postchristianity; this perspective highlights major societal shifts, but Christianity remains a significant global faith, though its role in Western culture is changing dramatically. Decolonizing Christianity is a movement challenging Eurocentric, white-dominated theology and practices, aiming to dismantle systems of oppression (like racism, capitalism, patriarchy) that became intertwined with the faith during colonialism, and instead centering marginalized voices, indigenous knowledge, and a pursuit of justice, often through practices like reading the Bible from the margins and reclaiming faith from a non-white, non-Western perspective. It involves critically examining Christianity’s historical complicity in colonial violence, repenting for injustices, and fostering a faith rooted in solidarity with the oppressed for true liberation, moving beyond “white” or nationalist versions of Christianity.
  • “Away down in Mississippi, they are using a portable electric chair”: Mississippi’s portable electric chair, carried by truck from county to county so that the condemned died in the county of conviction, was used from 1940 until a gas chamber replaced it in 1954. Willie McGee, a Black man convicted of rape on doubtful evidence, died in it in 1951. See Lynching and Racial Violence.
  • “The Barbados Negroes were those who were burned”: The Barbados Slave Code of 1661, officially titled as An Act for the better ordering and governing of Negroes, was a law passed by the Parliament of Barbados to provide a legal basis for slavery in the English colony of Barbados. It marked the beginning of the legal codification of slavery. Mr. Weed claimed that the Barbados Negroes were executed using an electric chair in a truck that went from village to village. No direct references could be found for this however there were rebellions against slavery which the government ended by publicly executing all those involved. The term “Barbados Negroes” refers to the Black population of Barbados, who are predominantly of West African descent and were brought to the island as enslaved people. The term is now outdated, and the modern and more accurate term for the population is Afro-Barbadians or Black Barbadians. The legacy of slavery and resistance is a central part of the history of these people and the island itself, with key events including the Bussa rebellion in 1816. From the 17th century onwards, enslaved Africans were brought to Barbados to work on sugar plantations, with the English establishing a permanent settlement in 1627. Enslaved people continuously resisted their bondage through various acts, including rebellion, sabotage, and other forms of protest. Slavery was abolished in 1834, but this did not immediately resolve the social and economic challenges faced by the formerly enslaved population. The May 1675 Barbados rebellion plot was a planned uprising by enslaved people that was discovered before it began. An enslaved woman named Fortuna or Anna alerted a planter, Gyles Hall, to the conspiracy. In response, Governor Sir Jonathan Atkins acted swiftly, leading to the arrest of many conspirators, with at least 17 initially executed by beheading or burning. The plot, which involved enslaved people from the Akan ethnic group and aimed to establish a slave government led by a man named Cuffee, was discovered only eight days before its scheduled execution.
  • “a German music box with a horn and a Yale pennant”: Young said every case the hangman tells is based on a real one; this one has not been identified.
    • The woman whose jail cell was furnished with “satin hangings and a German music box with a horn and a Yale pennant” may refer to Ilse Koch, the notorious Nazi war criminal known as “The Witch of Buchenwald.” Ilse Koch was the wife of Karl Koch, the commandant of the Buchenwald concentration camp. She was known for her exceptional sadism and cruelty towards prisoners. After World War II, she was arrested by American authorities and stood trial for war crimes. Ilse Koch did not personally have a Yale pennant or a Yale tattoo; rather, it was a piece of tattooed human skin featuring a Yale-related design that was allegedly found at the Buchenwald concentration camp and attributed to her collection. Koch was infamous for reportedly selecting prisoners with unique or artistic tattoos to be murdered so their skin could be harvested for decorative household objects, such as lampshades, book bindings, and gloves. Testimonies and post-liberation reports described a piece of tanned skin among the camp’s “pathology specimens” that featured a Yale University pennant or similar collegiate crest. It belonged to an unknown prisoner who had likely been a student or traveler before the war. Despite widespread media coverage of these “human skin” artifacts, prosecutors in her 1947 and 1951 trials were unable to conclusively prove that Koch herself ordered the creation of these items or possessed them. General Lucius D. Clay later reduced her initial life sentence partly due to a lack of physical evidence linking her directly to the “lampshades.” She was eventually sentenced to life imprisonment and died by suicide in prison in 1967.
  • “He had never hanged a woman”: Mary Surratt, hanged in 1865 for her part in the plot to kill Lincoln, was the first woman executed by the federal government. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “the mandrakes screaming when they are pulled up by the roots”: This folklore is based on the mandrake plant which is a hallucinogenic and narcotic. It is said that it grows from the blood of a hanged man and the tuber can resemble a human form. Forensic medicine does record erection and the discharge of semen in some deaths by hanging, and the folklore of the gallows mandrake grew from it. Juliet fears “shrieks like mandrakes’ torn out of the earth” (Romeo and Juliet 4.3), and in Genesis Rachel trades for Leah’s mandrakes to bear a child (Genesis 30:14-16). See Shakespeare, Genesis, and Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “The man who found it was a Jehovah’s Witness”: The Bible Students took the name Jehovah’s Witnesses in 1931. See American Religious Movements.
