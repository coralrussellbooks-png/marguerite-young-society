Chapter 68

Summary

At night the bus finally stops. Vera watches Madge and Homer walk away, never to be seen again. She waits to check into the Tavern, a decaying hotel built in 1827, while the landlord talks at the bar with Mr. Weed, a Christian hangman. Mr. Weed's first wife, Marie, hanged herself from a peach tree for fear of another pregnancy. His second wife, Julia, has the mind of a child. His children play at hanging. He carries a pen given to him by a condemned man, a Jew's harp, and a black poker chip of unknown origin, and he considers himself a humanitarian. No clock strikes.

Key passages

From the research

  • The opening passage reflects on how coming into a village at night changes one's perception permanently, with shadows that never lift and reality that must be accepted no matter what it is. Vera emphasizes that reality, whether joyful or bleak, is real and must be confronted. This sets a tone of melancholy and philosophical reflection about the nature of existence and perception.
  • The detailed description of the Tavern and the surrounding town conveys a sense of decay, emptiness, and abandonment. The Tavern, despite its sign proclaiming it was built by "God’s servants," appears as just another commercial establishment, run-down and shadowy. This contrasts with Vera's later realization of the Tavern's deeper, more mysterious interior life.
  • Moses’s complex relationship with his old mother, who is sick and deluded, is a poignant metaphor for duty, denial, and the boundaries between love and obligation. Moses obeys "traffic rules" even in the "bridal chamber" with his mother, symbolizing his refusal to engage fully with her fantasies or to take her place.
  • Vera reveals a deep inner fear of their own "defective character," which loves imperfections, distortions, and the dead more than the living reality. This passage underscores a theme of alienation and longing for something unattainable or lost.
  • The arrival and conversation with the professional hangman introduces a major symbolic figure who embodies death, justice, and mercy. Mr. Weed’s reflections on his job, the nature of crime, punishment, and the human conscience provide a grim but humane perspective on life and death.
  • Mr. Weed’s narrative about his first wife who hanged herself, his children who play at hanging themselves, and his deep internal conflict reveals the toll his work takes on his personal life and psyche. His dreams of cooking live pigs, his vegetarianism, and his haunted conscience highlight the paradox of his profession.
  • Mr. Weed’s observations about criminals acting like sleepwalkers, the subtlety of some murders, and the idea that many crimes remain undiscovered underscore the themes of human fallibility and the complexity of justice. His story about the man who killed his wife because of jealousy over cats is a striking example.
  • The black poker chip, found near a severed woman’s head but ultimately meaningless, symbolizes the confusing and often contradictory clues in the search for truth and justice. Mr. Weed carrying the chip unknowingly for years suggests the persistence of unresolved mysteries.
  • The dialogue between Mr. Weed and the landlord, including the landlord’s impatience and Mr. Weed’s acceptance of his monotonous life, humanizes both characters and closes the chapter with a mix of resignation and quiet dignity.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Vera

  • Vera is introspective, haunted by memories and dreams, struggling with the boundary between reality and illusion.
  • Vera reflects on personal loss, especially of Miss MacIntosh, and experiences fear of their own imperfect character and fate.

Moses

  • A figure representing avoidance and detachment, Moses goes home to a mother who believes he is her bridegroom, but he maintains distance, obeying "traffic rules" metaphorically to avoid emotional entanglement.
  • His “old mother”
  • The bus driver feels obligated to care for his mother, who he says thinks he is "a baby crying in her arms". He made a promise to never leave her. This sense of responsibility, whether real or imagined, weighs heavily on him and contributes to his anxieties.
  • The bus driver's mother exists in a dreamlike state, much like the characters Vera encounters throughout her journey which blurs the lines between reality and fantasy.
  • The bus driver's mother is sometimes depicted as young and beautiful, with breasts filled with milk. This suggests a longing for an idealized past, a time of innocence and maternal comfort. She is associated with the potential, yet ultimately unfulfilled, union.
  • The bus driver's relationship with his mother can be interpreted as a symbol of his internal conflict between his desire for freedom and his sense of obligation. He dreams of escaping his mundane life as a bus driver. However, his commitment to his mother, whether self-imposed or externally enforced, keeps him tethered to his present circumstances.
  • The bus driver says that he will not cut his hair as long as "the Democrats were running us", and that he has made a promise to his mother to never cut his hair. It seems as though the bus driver's long hair is a symbol of his commitment to his mother and his abstinence from the more modern world.

Mr. Weed (The Christian Hangman)

  • Central to the chapter, Mr. Weed is a professional executioner who views his work as a mission to provide a painless death.
  • He is a deeply complex character: a good father, haunted by personal tragedy (his wife Marie’s suicide), and tormented by memories and dreams.
  • He discusses his profession with a mix of bitterness, humor, and philosophical insight, revealing his tolerance and humanity despite his grim role.
  • Mr. Weed recounts various cases of crime, murder, and the inefficiency or cruelty of some executions, highlighting the moral ambiguities of justice.
  • He is associated with symbolic objects (a Jew’s harp, a Parker fountain pen, and a black poker chip) each with narrative significance.

Parker Fountain Pen

  • The pen symbolizes death. Vera learns that the condemned man gave the pen to the hangman's grateful relatives. Additionally, the hangman is described as signing the hotel register with the pen.

Black Poker Chip

  • The black poker chip is found near the head of a murdered woman wearing rose-colored sunglasses, but it's a clue that can never be connected to the crime.
  • Even though it seems like it should be important, it ultimately has no bearing on solving the murder. The black poker chip plays a great part in the case but is a clue which led nowhere, nowhere at all, like a business man’s address book which was found in the hollow of a nearby oak tree. It was simply confusing.
  • Mr. Weed later finds a black poker chip in his coat pocket, but he claims he never gambles and has no idea how it got there. This adds another layer of mystery and confusion.
  • The Hangman finds the poker chip in his oldest suit, the suit he said that he wears to hangings and to church.
  • The black poker chip represents the often confusing and ultimately meaningless details that can arise in investigations, and it highlights the limitations of human understanding in the face of complex and tragic events.

Jew’s Harp

  • The hangman is described as always playing his Jew's harp, particularly when taking the shortcut home through the orchard 854. This suggests the Jew's harp is part of his routine, a habit he performs after a hanging.
  • The Jew's harp, often associated with folk music and simple pleasures, contrasts sharply with the hangman's profession. This incongruity highlights the hangman's attempts to reconcile his role as an executioner with his self-perception as a good, peace-loving man.
  • Despite his constant playing, the Jew's harp ultimately fails to console the hangman from the troubles and darkness within his life, particularly the disturbing events involving his children. The music seemingly cannot penetrate the deeper issues plaguing him.
  • The hangman makes a point of saying, "A Jew's harp is not Jewish". This statement may symbolize the way the hangman himself is often misunderstood. He is a man whose profession is death, yet he sees himself as a humanitarian who brings a painless end to suffering.
  • Mr. Weed's profession as a hangman directly symbolizes capital punishment and the administration of justice. He embodies the often-unseen figure who carries out the state's ultimate punishment.
  • Despite the grim nature of his work, Mr. Weed is portrayed as an ordinary, even respectable, individual, a family man who attends church. This highlights the moral ambiguity and societal complicity in the death penalty, suggesting that even those who carry out such acts can be integrated into mainstream society.
  • Mr. Weed's constant connection to death and crime, as well as his troubled dreams, symbolize the burden of the past and the lingering presence of violence and injustice in American history.
  • The juxtaposition of Mr. Weed's morbid occupation with his mundane, everyday life (his family, his farm) reflects a common theme in American literature and art: the grotesque and the mundane coexisting, often uneasily, in the same space.
  • Mr. Weed's interest in the innocence of children and his fear of corrupting them could symbolize a lost sense of American innocence, the idea that the nation has been tainted by violence and injustice.
  • Mr. Weed's wide-brimmed Western hat and his matter-of-fact attitude may evoke the spirit of the American frontier, where violence and justice were often intertwined.
  • Just as Mr. Weed is the man who makes it all painless, the rest of the people are silent accepters of death.
  • While not explicitly stated, Mr. Weed's statement that he does not hang women and the reference to a Negro who was wrongly accused and hanged in Kentucky may allude to the racial injustices prevalent in American history, particularly in the South.

Marie Weed, his first wife who hanged herself

  • Marie Weed, the first wife of the hangman, is a character defined by her tragic end and the haunting impact she has on her husband's life.
  • Marie dies by suicide, hanging herself from a peach tree in their orchard. This act is driven by her fear of another pregnancy and the overwhelming sense of a "meaningless woe expressed by flesh and blood".
  • Her death profoundly affects her husband, the hangman, turning him into a tormented figure. He is haunted by the memory of cutting her down "a split second too late", and he sees her children playing at hanging themselves, symbolizing his guilt and the inescapable presence of death in his life. The grief and guilt transform him into a man who, despite his profession, is deeply averse to violence.
  • Marie's character is primarily revealed through the hangman's recollections and descriptions, creating a sense of mystery and incompleteness. Vera pieces together her image through the hangman's stories of his past. She is characterized by her absence and the powerful effect she has on those left behind.
  • Marie's death and subsequent presence in the hangman's memories symbolize the destructive power of despair and the enduring consequences of loss. The image of her hanging from a tree laden with peach blossoms represents the juxtaposition of beauty and tragedy, life and death.
  • The hangman’s stories of Marie may be connected to Vera's quest for meaning in the lives of marginalized people. The hangman himself is a marginalized character who is haunted by the past, similar to Vera and other characters she encounters.
  • Marie, while not directly present in the narrative, is a crucial figure in understanding the hangman and the themes of death, guilt, and the search for meaning.

Julia Weed, his second wife

  • Julia Weed is the second wife of the hangman. Her relationship with him is marked by a sense of his past deeds and the shadow they cast on their present life.
  • Julia's position as the second wife is complicated by the circumstances of the first wife's death, which was a suicide. She is sometimes found wearing the clothes of his first wife, Marie.
  • Julia is described as having "the mind of a child". Her understanding of the hangman's work and the events surrounding his first wife's death seem limited.
  • She is seen as someone who does not "think things through". This suggests a lack of depth or introspection, contributing to the perception of her as somewhat naive or unaware of the darker aspects of her situation.
  • She is connected to a history of sorrow and death, not only through her husband's profession but also through the tragic end of his first wife. This association highlights the theme of the past's inescapable influence on the present.
  • There's a contrast between her apparent innocence and the knowledge she possesses, or lacks, about her husband's profession and the events of the past. This tension adds to the complexity of her character.
  • The narrative suggests that Julia, in her innocence and limited awareness, is a victim of circumstances, a woman drawn into a situation she may not fully comprehend.
  • Julia Weed is portrayed as a simple, somewhat naive woman caught in the web of her husband's dark profession and the tragic history of his past. Her character serves as a vehicle to explore themes of death, guilt, and the burden of memory.

The Landlord

  • The Tavern’s landlord is pragmatic, skeptical, and somewhat cynical.
  • He interacts with Mr. Weed with a mixture of humor and impatience, grounding the scene in everyday reality.
  • His references to “turkeys and chickens” and dismissive attitude toward Mr. Weed’s stories add a humanizing, mundane contrast to the heavy themes.
  • The "Remember the Marne" tattoo suggests he is a veteran who experienced the horrors of World War I.
  • His world-weariness and the suggestion that his hotel "sinks every night" reflect the disillusionment and cynicism that followed World War I.
  • His background in intelligence implies knowledge of hidden truths and clandestine operations.
  • He listens to the troubles of all kinds of people, suggesting that he represents the common man and those who have been forgotten by society.
  • The mix of old and new, the presence of both a "lieutenant governor of Indiana" and "freaks" at the hotel, allude to a changing and increasingly diverse America.
  • He is a complex figure whose symbolism reflects the disillusionment, hardships, and changing social landscape of America in the first half of the 20th century.

Research: Themes

Reality and Perception

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Vera reflects on the nature of reality, emphasizing that whatever is experienced or perceived is "real," whether it is a dream, a memory, or a physical object.
  • Reality is not confined to conventional or positive experiences but includes shadows, decay, and the unreal or rejected (e.g., abandoned objects, shadows on the hills).
  • This theme is introduced early on, where the village at night is described as a place where shadows never fade, suggesting persistent darkness or hidden truths.

Death and the Inevitability of Fate

See: Death and Mortality · Fate and Chance

  • The chapter dwells heavily on death, especially through the experiences and reflections of the Christian hangman, who symbolizes the finality of mortality.
  • Mr. Weed’s profession is portrayed with a paradoxical humanity; he is portrayed as a missionary of painless death, emphasizing efficiency and compassion within a grim role.
  • Death is also symbolized as a "marital knot" or a binding connection that cannot be undone, suggesting an eternal union with mortality.

Memory, Loss, and the Past

See: Memory and the Past · Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • Vera recalls memories of lost loved ones, such as Miss MacIntosh, whose disappearance haunts Vera.
  • Memories blur with dreams and reality, suggesting a struggle to reconcile the past with the present.
  • Mr. Weed’s personal history, including the hanging of his first wife and his children’s strange behaviors, deepens the motif of loss and haunting memories.

Human Imperfection and Guilt

See: Guilt, Innocence, and Justice

  • Characters are deeply flawed and haunted by guilt, failures, and secrets (e.g., Mr. Weed’s reflections on the imperfect hanging, criminals who act unknowingly, and the failure to prevent tragedies).
  • Mr. Weed discusses the subtlety of crime and how even well-planned acts can betray the perpetrator by small, unnoticed details.
  • The narrative suggests a tension between the desire for justice and the complexity of human nature.

It also highlights the characters' different perspectives on life, love, and death: the landlord’s practical yet poetic view versus the hangman’s somber, fatalistic outlook.

  • “The Negro was hanged for sleeping with a white woman” describes a story told by the Christian hangman Mr. Weed about a botched lynching of a Black man in Kentucky. According to the tale, the man was hanged for the accusation of sleeping with a white woman, an accusation he likely did not deserve. The hangman describes how the man was cut down from the noose a split second too soon, meaning he was still technically alive and was later seen pushing a baby buggy, frightening the local white community. The hangman reflects on the "inefficiency" of the hanging, suggesting that his role was to make death as swift and painless as possible, but here the man suffered unnecessarily by surviving the hanging. This passage echoes real historical accounts of lynching in the United States, particularly in the South, where Black men were often falsely accused of crimes, especially of violating racial codes like alleged relations with white women. Lynchings were extrajudicial killings by mobs, commonly by hanging, sometimes with brutal torture. There are documented cases where victims were cut down prematurely or survived the initial hanging, leading to further suffering before death or even escape, heightening the terror of such acts. The passage metaphorically highlights the cruelty and injustice of these acts, along with the hangman's conflicted role: he sees himself as a Christian bringing mercy through a swift death, yet is part of a brutal system that perpetuates racial violence and injustice. The "unofficially alive" status of the man symbolizes the liminal, tortured state of lynching victims: condemned by society, yet clinging to life in a world that refuses them justice or dignity. This passage serves as a fictional reflection on historical racial violence in America, underscoring the themes of injustice, suffering, and moral ambiguity surrounding lynching and the societal roles that participated in or failed to prevent such atrocities.

An enslaved woman named Fortuna or Anna alerted a planter, Gyles Hall, to the conspiracy. In response, Governor Sir Jonathan Atkins acted swiftly, leading to the arrest of many conspirators, with at least 17 initially executed by beheading or burning. The plot, which involved enslaved people from the Akan ethnic group and aimed to establish a slave government led by a man named Cuffee, was discovered only eight days before its scheduled execution.
  • The woman whose jail cell was furnished with “satin hangings and a German music box with a horn and a Yale pennant” may refer to Ilse Koch, the notorious Nazi war criminal known as "The Witch of Buchenwald". Ilse Koch was the wife of Karl Koch, the commandant of the Buchenwald concentration camp. She was known for her exceptional sadism and cruelty towards prisoners. After World War II, she was arrested by American authorities and stood trial for war crimes. Ilse Koch did not personally have a Yale pennant or a Yale tattoo; rather, it was a piece of tattooed human skin featuring a Yale-related design that was allegedly found at the Buchenwald concentration camp and attributed to her collection. Koch was infamous for reportedly selecting prisoners with unique or artistic tattoos to be murdered so their skin could be harvested for decorative household objects, such as lampshades, book bindings, and gloves. Testimonies and post-liberation reports described a piece of tanned skin among the camp's "pathology specimens" that featured a Yale University pennant or similar collegiate crest. It belonged to an unknown prisoner who had likely been a student or traveler before the war. Despite widespread media coverage of these "human skin" artifacts, prosecutors in her 1947 and 1951 trials were unable to conclusively prove that Koch herself ordered the creation of these items or possessed them. General Lucius D. Clay later reduced her initial life sentence partly due to a lack of physical evidence linking her directly to the "lampshades". She was eventually sentenced to life imprisonment and died by suicide in prison in 1967.
  • “...a man experiences, at the instant his neck is broken and for an instant afterward, a convulsion, an agony of life. A ghostly progeny is always shot forth into the empty air. Ten thousand sons and daughters fall like a cobweb into everlasting nothingness, nothingness forever. Or else his seed takes root, and his children spring up around the scaffold—flower-faced children winged like birds and rooted in earth, the mandrakes screaming when they are pulled up by the roots, for they are the children of the hanged man, and they grow like the little weeds. That was why the hangman thought of hanging as a marriage ceremony with death.” This folklore is based on the mandrake plant which is a hallucinogenic and narcotic. It is said that it grows from the blood of a hanged man and the tuber can resemble a human form. It is considered a myth that men ejaculate when they are executed.
  • “Dark Lady was winning, and Dark Lady was the horse who was supposed to lose.” The racehorse’s name recalls the “Dark Lady” of Shakespeare’s Sonnets 127 to 152, the mysterious woman of the poet’s desire.
  • “a hotel on Twelfth Street for white-sheeted Hindu sailors” Sailors from India, known as lascars, crewed British merchant ships in large numbers from the eighteenth century onward and were a familiar sight in Atlantic port cities, often lodging in boarding houses set aside for them between voyages.
