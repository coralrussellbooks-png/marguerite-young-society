Pillar: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth · Across every book

Or, Why the Margins Are an Anti-Fascist Blueprint

From the time we are small, society loves to organize the world into neat, tidy boxes. We have rigid categories for almost everything: boy or girl, sane or crazy, productive worker or outcast, citizen or criminal, the person who belongs and the person who does not. We are taught to look at the center of these categories to understand what is normal, and we treat anyone who does not fit into the pre-made slots as a weird exception, an awkward remainder, a problem to be managed or corrected or hidden.

But Marguerite Young flips the script completely. She populates her entire world with characters who live right on the ragged edges of social expectation. And she does not treat them as cautionary tales or tragic exceptions. She treats them as the most reliable witnesses to the truth.

Where the Categories Show Their Seams

Young focuses her deepest attention on people who refuse to fit neatly into the system's boxes. They are not dismissed as eccentric side notes or colorful background characters. Young insists that these people are not distractions at all. They are the absolute center of her philosophical message.

Why? Because the margins are exactly where the categories show their seams. When you look closely at the edges of any box, you see the glue, the staples, the artificial joins, and the enormous amount of pressure required to hold the construction together. The person who does not fit the social mold is not a broken exception. They are the living evidence of how the mold was made, and who made it, and what it costs to maintain it.

This is a profound claim about what actually exists and what is real. Young is arguing that the margins are not outside reality. They see more than the center sees, precisely because they are not inside the system looking out. They are outside the system looking in, and from that vantage point the whole machinery becomes visible in a way it never is to those it was built to accommodate.

Think about what it means to fit perfectly into a social category. It means the category was built with you in mind. The doors are the right width for your body. The forms have a box for your name. The language has words for your experience. The history books include people who look like you doing things that matter. When the system fits you well, you tend not to notice the system at all. It feels like reality. It feels like the natural order of things.

But when the system does not fit you, you notice everything. You notice the width of the doors. You notice which boxes on the form do not apply to you and which ones erase you. You notice whose experience the language was built to hold and whose it was built to make unspeakable. You notice the gap between what the system claims to be and what it actually does to the people it cannot accommodate.

That noticing is not a wound, though it often feels like one. It is knowledge. It is the clearest possible view of how power actually operates, what it protects, what it discards, and where its seams are showing. Young builds her entire philosophy on that view. She trusts the margins more than the center because the margins have seen the construction from the outside. They know it is a construction. They have the evidence in their own bodies.

The Moving Bus

To illustrate what a community built on this principle actually looks like, Young gives us a beautiful and deceptively simple image: the passengers on a cross-country bus.

They are not a group of people who share the same background, the same politics, the same story, or the same destination. They are a collection of different, complex individuals, each carrying their own unique realities, their own permanent grief, their own inner crowd of contradictory selves. They did not choose each other. They are not bonded by ideology or agreement or shared identity. They are together because they are all, for this particular stretch of road, in transit through the same complicated world.

What holds them together is not forced sameness. It is the shared condition of movement, of being on the way, of witnessing each other's differences without demanding that those differences be resolved or erased. The bus does not require you to be the same as the person sitting next to you. It only requires that you share the road for a while.

This is Young's model for genuine community: not a group of identical people unified around a single identity, but a collection of irreducibly different people held together by the honest condition of being alive in the same world at the same time. The margins are not a threat to that community. They are its most essential members, because they are the ones who can see where the road actually goes and where the system's promises fall apart.

Why the System Needs You to Look Away from the Edges

So how does shifting our gaze to the margins help us resist authoritarian control?

Authoritarian movements grow by executing a very specific strategy: they destroy plurality. They demand that every person become legible, sortable, and if possible identical in their loyalty and their silence. They need the center to hold, which means they need the edges to disappear. They need the people who do not fit to be invisible, institutionalized, criminalized, or dead. Not because those people are dangerous in themselves, but because their existence is dangerous to the fiction the system depends on.

The fiction is this: the categories are natural. The boxes were not built by anyone. The people who fit inside them are normal, and the people who do not are aberrations. If you can keep that fiction intact, you can keep the center from asking why the boxes were built the way they were, who benefits from the current arrangement, and what would happen if the walls came down.

The person at the margin blows the fiction apart simply by existing visibly. They do not have to say anything. They do not have to make an argument. Their presence alone is evidence that the box is a construction, that normal is a choice, that the categories require maintenance and enforcement and the constant, exhausting work of making the seams invisible.

This is why authoritarian systems spend so much energy on the margins. They do not ignore them. They obsess over them. They pass laws against them. They build institutions to manage them. They invest enormous resources in making sure the people who do not fit stay out of sight, because the sight of them is destabilizing to everyone the system has convinced that the current arrangement is natural and inevitable.

When we center the margins, when we look at the edges and take seriously what is visible from there, we protect our communities from being captured by that fiction. We build a way of seeing that cannot be taken in by the system's claim to naturalness. We build people who know, from direct evidence, that the boxes are built and can be rebuilt differently.

The Ground That Knew Her: Harriet Tubman

To understand what it means to live at the margin and use that position as a source of power rather than a site of defeat, consider Harriet Tubman, who spent her entire life operating at the edges of every category the system tried to enforce, and who used that position to see things the center could not see and do things the center could not imagine.

Tubman was born into slavery in Maryland sometime around 1822. From the beginning, she existed at the intersection of every category the system used to control people: she was Black, she was a woman, she was enslaved, she was considered property. The law did not recognize her as a person. The social order did not have a box for her humanity. She was, in the system's terms, entirely outside the category of those whose lives counted.

She also had a neurological condition, likely narcolepsy caused by a severe head injury inflicted by an overseer when she was a child, that caused her to lose consciousness without warning and experience vivid, involuntary visions. By any standard the system recognized, this made her less valuable, less reliable, less predictable. It marked her further outside the category of the useful and the normal. It was one more way the system had of dismissing her.

But from the margin where all of this placed her, Tubman could see things that the center was structurally incapable of seeing.

She knew the land. She had worked it, moved through it, slept on it. She knew which swamps were passable and which were not, which roads were watched and which were forgotten, which farms were safe and which were dangerous. She knew the geography of the system from the outside, from the position of someone the system had never bothered to hide itself from, because it had never imagined she would use what she saw.

In 1849 she escaped north, alone, through nearly ninety miles of hostile territory. She arrived free. And then she went back.

She went back thirteen times. She led approximately seventy people out of slavery through the network that became known as the Underground Railroad. She used the landscape the system had built to contain people as the very infrastructure of their liberation. She used the darkness, the swamps, the forgotten roads, the edges of the map where the system's attention did not reach. She operated entirely in the margins, in the spaces the center had decided were not worth watching, and from those spaces she dismantled the system's hold on person after person after person.

The center could not have done this. A person operating from inside the system's categories, trusted and legible and accommodated by the existing order, would not have known where the seams were. Would not have known which roads were unguarded. Would not have had the intimate, embodied knowledge of how the system actually operated as opposed to how it claimed to operate. The margin was not a disadvantage. It was the source of everything Tubman knew and everything she could do.

The visions the system used to dismiss her, the involuntary losses of consciousness that marked her as unreliable, she understood as guidance. She trusted them. She said she never lost a passenger, and she credited those visions as part of how she navigated. What the system read as a defect, she read as information. What the center saw as the evidence of her limitation, she used as a tool.

This is what Young means when she focuses on the margins. It is not that suffering is good, or that exclusion is a gift, or that the violence of the margin should be romanticized. It is that the margin produces a specific and irreplaceable kind of knowledge that the center cannot generate. It is that the people the system has placed outside its categories are the people who can see the categories most clearly, who know where the walls are and how they were built and where the gaps are large enough to move through.

Tubman did not wait for the center to invite her in. She did not petition the system to recognize her humanity or revise its categories to accommodate her. She used what she knew from where she stood and she moved. She kept moving. She treated the margin not as a place of defeat but as a position of clarity, and from that clarity she did what the center had declared impossible.

After the Civil War, she continued. She worked for women's suffrage. She established a home for elderly African Americans in Auburn, New York, where she had settled. She kept operating at the edges of what the system recognized as legitimate political action, and she kept making things happen that the center had not authorized and could not fully account for.

She died in 1913, surrounded by people she loved, having spent her entire life using the view from the edges to see what the center refused to look at. She is buried in Auburn, on the ground she chose, in the place she made her own.

The system never found a box for her. That was never the point. The point was that she could see the boxes from the outside, and seeing them clearly was what made her free.

The Practice

Pay close attention to the places in your own life, your workplace, or your community where you or someone else does not fit into a standard social box. Instead of treating that mismatch as an awkward problem to fix, look at it as a moment of clarity. The edge is where the artificial seams of the system are showing. The person who does not fit is not the problem. They are the evidence.

Notice also when you are being asked to look away from the margins. When a system or a culture directs your attention toward the center, toward the normal, toward the people and stories that fit neatly into the established categories, ask what is being kept out of frame. Ask who is at the edge and what they can see from there that the center cannot.

And notice the knowledge you carry from your own experience of not fitting. The places where the system's categories did not hold you, where the boxes were the wrong shape for your life, where you had to find your own way through terrain the map did not acknowledge. That knowledge is not a wound to be healed. It is information. It is a view of the seams that most people never get. Treat it as the irreplaceable resource it actually is.
