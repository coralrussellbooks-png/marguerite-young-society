Exploring Young's Poetry

Radical Immanence Explained

← The Ground Underfoot · Exploring Young's Poetry · The Poems in Movements →

Immanence is the plainer half. It means that whatever matters is here, in the actual world, and not stored somewhere above it or beyond it. The opposite belief, that the true meaning of things waits in a heaven, a hereafter, a golden age, a final arrival, is the old and familiar one. Immanence refuses the wait. It holds that meaning is in the dirt and the weather and the body, or it is nowhere at all. Radical names the reach of that refusal. At its root the word means “of the root,” thoroughgoing, carried all the way down. Radical Immanence is the conviction that everything worth having is already here, in the world underfoot, all the way down, with nothing kept back in a beyond.

From that one conviction the rest follows because the same words run through all of her work.

Start with the self and the world it moves in. A person, in Young, is a crowd and not a single fixed identity. The many selves are the normal condition of being alive and not a fault to be corrected. This is Not One Self. The world those selves move through is, for the most part, built. Money, borders, laws, rank and class: these feel like bedrock, but they are agreements held in place by repetition, not facts dug out of the earth. Reality Is Constructed is the recognition of that, and seeing the construction is the first step out from under its spell. Even grammar is part of the building. Nouns freeze things into fixed identities and verbs keep them moving, and any voice that claims to speak from nowhere, in pure objectivity, is quietly hiding where it actually stands. That is Language Shapes What's Possible.

If the world is built, then time is not a clean line leaving the past behind. The past is beneath the present, pressed into it like layers of rock, and calling any arrangement inevitable is a way of hiding the people who actually laid it down. This is The Past Doesn't Go Away. It gives every person a particular place to stand where everyone who came before meets everyone who will come after, and where every choice packs down the ground the next generation will stand on. This is the Crossing Point. Trading that future for a present advantage is, in this vision, the one unforgivable move. And the people best placed to see all of this are the ones who do not fit the categories, since the margins are where the seams show. That is The Margins Reveal the Truth.

The vision also asks for a way to stand inside a built and grieving world without lying about it. Grief, here, is not a stage to be passed through on the way to recovery. It is permanent weather, and refusing to perform a tidy healing is a way of staying truthful about what was lost. That is Grief Doesn't End, and its companion is the Witness, the hard discipline of staying in the room with something painful and unresolved without rushing to fix it or wrap it up. Because nothing is ever truly finished, the honest move is to build anyway, provisionally, and keep revising, distrusting the frozen blueprint. That is Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary, and its ethical form is Unfinished Is Honest: the unfinished is not failure but honesty, and anyone who claims to hold the complete and final answer is usually selling something.

At the center of all of it is The Sacred Is Here, which is immanence felt rather than argued. Meaning is here, in the actual world underfoot, and not in the promised beyond. The appeal to a distant beyond, a golden age, a grand destiny, a final arrival, is the oldest tool of anyone who wants to justify a sacrifice in the present. To hold the ordinary as sacred is to take that tool out of their hands.

Eleven ideas, one conviction. They are not a checklist Young worked from. They are the shape that shows up when her work is set side by side, and setting them side by side is what this series does. There are four.

Angel in the Forest is history. It is her account of New Harmony, Indiana, the patch of ground that tried twice to become a paradise and twice came back down to earth. Its very chapters carry the vision in their titles, one of them simply “Paradise Was Lost.” Young measures its heaven against the plainest possible yardstick, calling the promised city “a city as measured by the jimson weed,” a paradise sized against a common roadside plant.

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is the novel, a 1,198 page descent through consciousness, dream, and the porous line between the living and the dead.

Harp Song for a Radical is the biography, the life of Eugene Debs and the labor movement he gave himself to, a book that asks in a real life what a cause is allowed to ask one generation to spend for the next.

And then there are the poems which run from 1927-1945.

Each of the four holds the same vision, but the poems hold it most compressed. What the novel takes a thousand-plus pages to enact, a poem can say in six lines. “Sky of Grasses” watches a person die and reports, without comfort, that “Heaven has shrunk to the dim size / Comprehended by insects’ eyes,” until it is “smaller than the faint far sound / Of a seed pod shaking the grey ground.” That is The Sacred Is Here stated outright and whole. The same idea that Angel walks through the ruins of New Harmony to reach, and that the novel dissolves into over a thousand pages of dream, the poem sets down in the space of a breath. This is the particular work of the poetry volume in the series. It is the vision at its most concentrated, the place where the whole shape can be seen at once before it is watched being worked out at length in the longer books.

Two of the eleven ideas do not appear in this book as a subject. They appear as its method. The Witness is the stance the readings try to keep, staying in the room with each poem, following what is difficult in it without smoothing the difficulty away. Unfinished Is Honest is the book’s own honesty about itself. These readings are offered as provisional, since I am admitting here that I am not an expert at analyzing anything, much less poetry. This is one pass through the poems and not the last word on them, built to be argued with and revised. To read Young any other way, to close the case and hand down a finished verdict, would be to betray the very vision the poems carry. Young herself found the finished thing suspect and the trying beautiful, and put it better than any framing can: “nothing so beautiful as trial and error, this side of paradise.”

Because the vision, and the vocabulary for it, are set out fully here, the series has no required order. A reader can begin with the utopias, the novel, the life of Debs, or the poems, and meet the same eleven ideas under the same names in each. This volume begins with the earliest of her poems, one written at nine, which is the very age Young named as to when her lifelong preoccupations began.

What follows reads them in movements, each movement gathering the poems that press hardest on one or two of these ideas, and reaching, where a passage truly deepens a poem, into the other three books where the same idea is living a longer life. The place to start is the ground underfoot, which is where Young always said the ground her work rested on.
