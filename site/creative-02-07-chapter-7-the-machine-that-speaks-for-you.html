Chapter 7: The Machine That Speaks for You

← Chapter 6: Why Facts Aren't Enough · The Creative Radical Collection · The Creative Radical →

"he was the counterfeit of himself — this face which passed for his face and yet was not his identical face — though others should recognize him and torment him by their mystified recognition"

Every control system described in this book required human labor. A demagogue had to give speeches. A cult leader had to run meetings. An abuser had to be in the room. The reach was always limited by the hours in a day and the number of people one person could touch.

Artificial intelligence removes that limit entirely.

We are not yet living in the science fiction version of this threat, the sentient machine with its own agenda. We are living in something more mundane and more dangerous: a technology that allows human agendas, including the most destructive ones, to scale beyond anything previously possible. The question is not whether AI will become malevolent. The question is who is already using it, for what purpose, and what legal or social infrastructure exists to stop them.

The answer to the last part, as of now, is: very little.

On the evening of January 21, 2024, thousands of registered Democrats in New Hampshire began receiving a phone call. The call opened with the phrase "What a bunch of malarkey," a common Biden expression, and the voice told recipients not to vote in Tuesday's presidential primary, claiming that voting would only enable Republicans. The voice was indistinguishable, to most listeners, from the voice of the sitting President of the United States. It had been artificially generated. The call was spoofed to appear on caller ID as coming from a prominent Biden supporter, to make it appear sanctioned.

The New Hampshire attorney general opened an investigation the following day. A political consultant named Steven Kramer was eventually charged with thirteen felony counts of voter suppression and thirteen misdemeanor counts of impersonating a candidate. In 2025, he was found not guilty. His lawyers argued that the use of deepfake technology was protected speech rather than voter suppression, and that he had not impersonated a candidate because Biden's name was never spoken and he was not on the primary ballot.

Read that outcome carefully. A synthetic presidential voice was used to tell Democratic voters to stay home. The person who created it was acquitted. The legal system could not find language sufficient to name what had happened.

This is where we are. The technology for generating a convincing clone of any public figure's voice costs almost nothing and requires no specialized knowledge. A 2024 report by the cybersecurity firm Recorded Future documented 82 pieces of AI-generated deepfake content targeting public figures across 38 countries in a single year, with a disproportionate number focused on elections. These are only the cases that were identified and tracked. The ones that were not detected left no record of themselves.

The deeper threat is not any single deepfake. It is what the existence of deepfakes does to your relationship with all media. When you know that any voice can be cloned, any image fabricated, any video altered with no visible seam, you face a version of the gaslighting described in Chapter 3, but now operating at civilizational scale. You can no longer trust your ears. You can no longer trust your eyes. The rational response to this environment is epistemic paralysis: the feeling that it is simply impossible to know what is real, and that the effort of trying is not worth the exhaustion.

Epistemic paralysis is enormously useful to people in power. A population that has given up on the possibility of shared truth is a population that can be managed through whatever story is most aggressively distributed. If nothing can be verified, then the loudest voice wins by default. This is not an accident. The liar's dividend, as researchers have named it, means that the existence of deepfakes allows real footage of genuine events to be dismissed as fabricated. A politician caught on video committing a crime says: that's AI. The burden of proof, which used to rest on the person making the accusation, now rests on the person presenting the evidence.

The cognitive offloading problem runs alongside this. When AI can answer any question, summarize any document, and generate any text in seconds, the brain does what brains always do when a shortcut is available: it stops doing the work. This is not laziness. It is the same energy-conservation instinct that makes every other cognitive shortcut appealing. But the consequence of outsourcing your thinking to a system trained on existing data, shaped by the incentives of its creators, and incapable of genuine uncertainty is a gradual narrowing of your own analytical range. You stop cross-examining sources. You stop sitting with ambiguity. You stop developing the tolerance for complexity that is the only real defense against a simplified, manipulated world.

The defense is not to refuse all technology. It is to refuse to let any technology do your thinking for you.

The historical parallel worth sitting with here is the printing press. When Gutenberg's press made text reproducible at scale in the fifteenth century, it produced two things simultaneously: the democratization of knowledge and the industrialization of propaganda. Bibles and pamphlets. Scientific treatises and forgeries. The same technology that made the Reformation possible also made the systematic manufacture of religious disinformation possible. Europe spent the next century and a half in a series of catastrophic conflicts that were fueled in significant part by the weaponization of the new medium.

We are inside a similar moment now, compressed into years rather than centuries because the technology moves faster. AI has already democratized the production of persuasive text, realistic synthetic media, and automated disinformation at a scale that would have been unimaginable five years ago. The same tools that allow a novelist to work through structural problems at midnight or a researcher to synthesize a literature review also allow a political operative to generate ten thousand personalized pieces of targeted misinformation and deploy them before breakfast.

The question that the printing press eventually forced, after considerable violence, was not whether the technology could be controlled. It could not be, entirely, and attempts to control it through censorship mostly entrenched the powerful and suppressed the inconvenient. The question it forced was how citizens develop the capacity to navigate an environment saturated with reproducible text, some true, some false, some weaponized, without a reliable external authority to sort it for them. The answer, worked out slowly and imperfectly over centuries, was a set of practices: source evaluation, cross-referencing, institutional verification, the cultivation of a reading public with enough critical apparatus to make independent judgments.

We are going to have to work out the AI equivalent of those practices. They do not exist yet in anything like a stable form. What we have are instincts, and the most important one is this: your uncertainty is a feature, not a malfunction. A mind that remains genuinely uncertain about AI-generated content, that treats its own first reaction to a synthetic voice or a generated image with suspicion rather than acceptance, is a mind doing exactly what the moment requires. The systems described in this chapter are designed to produce certainty, compliance, and the comfortable feeling of having understood something quickly. Resist the comfort. The discomfort of not knowing is where your judgment lives.

The other instinct worth cultivating is specificity. Generalized distrust of all media, of all technology, of all institutions, is not a defense. It is paralysis, and it is exactly the condition that makes a person susceptible to whoever speaks with the most confidence into the void that distrust creates. Specific, targeted, evidence-based skepticism is different. It asks not "can I trust anything" but "what is the specific chain of verification for this specific claim." That question is harder and slower. It is also the only one that leads anywhere useful.

When a claim is alarming or convenient, find the primary source. Not a summary, not a clip: the full, unedited original. When a voice or image seems designed to produce an immediate emotional reaction, pause before that reaction becomes a conviction. When AI generates an answer that resolves your uncertainty cleanly and quickly, notice the relief you feel and treat it with suspicion. Clean, quick resolution is not a feature of complex reality. It is a feature of systems designed to keep you moving without stopping to look.

The action: This week, choose one topic you have been relying on AI or social media to interpret for you, whether that is a political situation, a medical question, or a historical claim. Find one primary source on that topic: a government document, a peer-reviewed paper, an unedited transcript, a physical book. Read it slowly. Notice how different it feels from the version you had been carrying. The difference between those two versions is the gap that every manipulative system, human or automated, lives inside.
