Definition from research

Chapter 2

  • Molyneux’s problem, posed by the Irish scientist William Molyneux in a letter to John Locke in 1688, asked whether a man who had been born blind and who learned to distinguish and name a globe and a cube by touch, would be able to distinguish and name these objects simply by sight, once he had been enabled to see. Catherine’s blind mother keeps the world of sight in her mind and speaks “of the appearances, the visions, the beautiful world of light.”

Chapter 12 · The Garden of the Blind

  • Represents a sensory world without sight, emphasizing the importance and limitations of perception.

Chapter 12 · Eyes and Eyelashes

  • Represent the function and fragility of vision and perception; Vera meditates on their biological and symbolic significance.

Chapter 12

  • “The eyeball was made to see with”: William Paley’s Natural Theology (1802) made the eye his proof of design: “there is precisely the same proof that the eye was made for vision, as there is that the telescope was made for assisting it.” The book opens with a watch found on a heath.
  • “dead men are usually found with their eyes open”: “Joseph shall put his hand upon thine eyes” (Genesis 46:4). The living close the eyes of the dead and once weighted them with coins.
  • “Leonardo had forgotten to give eyebrows to his Mona Lisa”: The Mona Lisa (c. 1503-1519) is a half-length portrait by Leonardo da Vinci. The painting shows no eyebrows or lashes, though Giorgio Vasari (1550) praised its eyebrows, “the manner in which the hairs spring from the flesh.”
  • “the garden of the blind”: Fragrance gardens for the blind were planted to be touched and smelled. The Brooklyn Botanic Garden opened one in 1955. Vera and Miss MacIntosh walk through this garden in Chapter 3.
  • “the stumbling stone to the blind”: “Thou shalt not curse the deaf, nor put a stumblingblock before the blind” (Leviticus 19:14).
  • “the Braille texture of flowers”: Louis Braille, blinded as a child, published his raised-dot alphabet in 1829.

Chapter 12 · The Role and Limits of the Senses

  • There is an extended meditation on the human senses (sight, hearing, taste, smell, and touch) as the means by which humans experience reality.
  • Vera highlights the design and function of eyes, eyelashes, eyelids, and hair as protective and sensory mechanisms.
  • The garden of the blind symbolizes a world without vision, emphasizing other ways of perceiving such as touch (Braille, texture), and questioning how place and perception influence sorrow and happiness.

Chapter 15

  • “my grandmother’s mahogany dressing table with its three stained mirrors”: The blind grandmother of the garden of the blind in Chapter 12.

Chapter 16

  • “a path that made a circle like an eye through the garden of the blind”: The grandmother’s garden of the blind.
  • “angels who restored sight to the blind and hearing to the deaf”: “Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf shall be unstopped” (Isaiah 35:5). The gardens of the blind and of the deaf were Chapters 12 and 13.

Chapter 19

  • “my grandmother’s rocking chair”: The blind grandmother of the garden of the blind.

Chapter 22

  • “My mother’s mother’s blindness had been only physical”: The blind grandmother of Chapter 12.

Chapter 37 · Eyes and Faces

  • Eyes symbolize perception and identity; the theme of masks and hidden skulls suggests mortality beneath appearances.

Chapter 55 · Visual Impairments

  • Joachim’s double vision and shifting perspectives symbolize his mental confusion and the instability of perception.

Chapter 62 · The Old Oil Lamp with Bull’s-Eye

  • Represents the faint, fragile light of consciousness and creativity amid darkness.

Chapter 68 · The Blindfolded Dog

  • Innocence and cruelty intertwined, reflecting on blindness to suffering.
  • The village is described as dilapidated and eerie, with details like rusted machinery, broken wheels, and a sign "Gentle Incline — Bride Ahead" which is a false reading for "Bridge Ahead," evoking a sense of decay and death.
  • The Tavern, built in 1827 by "God’s servants," is a gloomy, sprawling hotel with mildewed brick, claw-like vines, and a haunting presence. Its interior surpasses Vera’s imagination, filled with human-faced wasps and thin walls that seem to hear everything.
  • The atmosphere is filled with silence, shadows, and a slow, suffocating sense of inevitability, preparing the reader for the philosophical and existential meditations that follow.
