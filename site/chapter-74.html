To All My Darlings

Chapter 74

On Chapter 74 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 13, 2026

← Chapter 73 · Home · Chapter 75 →

Content note: The Esther Longtree chapters (72 to 81) deal with stillbirth and pregnancy loss, rape, infanticide, racist violence including lynching, and cruelty to children. This chapter is one of the hardest in the novel: Esther describes being raped, three winters running, by the Chicago detective, who found her unconscious in the fields.

Summary

Esther dreads the trial she expects next autumn, when the town’s women will crowd the courthouse to accuse her and she will be unable to produce the three children the Chicago detective says she killed. Her worst mistake, she thinks, was writing to him, a father who was not dying. Three winters, hunting a man who had killed several wives, he found her lying in the frozen fields and took her for a corpse; he felt her pulse, found her heartbeat, and raped her, leaving his eye-glasses, his calling card, a bone button, a thread, and “the frozen conception” behind. He came once as a locksmith, once as a soap salesman, once as himself, and told her he was partly wax. When she wrote that she needed an abortion he sent her the name of an abortionist under police protection; now he accuses her of murdering his children. She sets her shame beside a man’s freedom to boast of his bastards, waxes like the slow moon, and speaks of the murder of her heart when she was young. A small, warped child’s hand lies across her womb, and her own childhood reproaches her.

Key passages

From the research

  • Esther expresses her dread and isolation as she anticipates a courtroom trial, where she will be accused and misunderstood by a crowd of women who will judge her harshly without truly understanding her. It underscores her loneliness and the societal scrutiny she faces, as well as the impossibility of defending herself against accusations about her maternity and morality.
  • The detective’s repeated, disguised appearances and his cold, investigative demeanor create a haunting, almost spectral presence in Esther’s life. He is both the father of her stillborn children, by rape, and the figure who pursues her in the law, embodying both violation and threat.
  • The narrative of the detective’s rape of Esther is described with a mixture of detached observation and emotional complexity. The experience is both a physical violation and a symbolic act that precipitates her ongoing “pregnancy” with stillborn children, both literal and metaphorical.
  • The motif of being “always pregnant” with stillborn children recurs throughout this chapter. This is also tied to her physical and emotional condition, which isolates her further.
  • Esther contrasts her own suffering and social condemnation as a woman with the freedom and impunity of men, highlighting societal hypocrisy and the loneliness of her position.
  • Esther traces her sorrows to “the murder of her heart when she was young.”
  • Despite the external pressures, accusations, and her own confusions, Esther claims a mysterious, almost radiant identity, self-sufficient and brooding, unwilling to explain herself to anyone.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “She had felt as if a candle burning had raped her.” What does the candle say about a man who calls himself “partly wax”?
  • “He was a Christopher Columbus in the world of the dead.” What does Esther mean?
  • “What would happen would be worse than what would not happen.” What does Esther fear most?

Characters

  • The detective felt her pulse, found her heartbeat, and then raped her. Why does Esther say “She had not felt violated”?
  • “He had solved all life’s mysteries but his own, the one he was creating then.” What is his own mystery?
  • Esther wrote to the detective “as if she had resisted.” Why does she change the story in her letters?

Connections

  • “A man could have gone off whistling after he had done something wrong.” Where else in the novel do men go off whistling?
  • Esther goes on “dragging the stars after her.” How is she like the woman with child in Revelation 12?
  • “A woman abortionist who enjoyed police protection” is offered by the very detective who now accuses Esther of murder. What does that say about the law?

Reflection

  • “Conception was always a secret to the mother, too, as well as to the father.” Is that still true?
  • “Who was innocent? Who was guilty?” How would you answer for Esther?
  • “Her own childhood reproached her.” What might have happened when she was young?

Research: Characters

Esther Longtree

  • The protagonist, a woman who is perpetually pregnant yet burdened with loss and mystery.
  • She is introspective, lonely, and caught between hope and despair.
  • She fears legal prosecution and societal judgment but is also fiercely protective of her own narrative and secrets.

The Chicago Detective

  • The father of Esther’s children: three winters he found her unconscious in the fields, took her for a corpse, felt her pulse, and raped her.
  • He hunts murderers and now accuses Esther of murdering his children, though he himself raped her and sent her the name of an abortionist “who enjoyed police protection.”
  • He represents authority, suspicion, and the complexities of truth and guilt.

Esther’s Father (the Policeman)

  • Symbolizes familial and societal surveillance and judgment.
  • His passive role highlights Esther’s isolation and the inescapability of the small-town environment.

  • Her womb is described as swollen with “empty waters,” a metaphor for hope and despair coexisting.

See: Isolation and Connection · Guilt, Innocence, and Justice · Society, Class, and Race

Mystery and Ambiguity

See: Faith, God, and the Supernatural

  • The narrative blurs reality and memory. Esther’s story is full of mysteries: the paternity of her children, the nature of her pregnancies, and the actions of the detective.
  • The repeated motif of “stillborn” children and the detective’s ambiguous role deepen this theme.

Powerlessness and Defiance

See: Fear and Courage

  • Despite her vulnerability, Esther exhibits moments of defiance, especially against unfair accusations and societal expectations.
  • Her internal world remains rich and complex, even as she is perceived as “crazy” or “mulish.”

The Passage of Time and Memory

See: Memory and the Past · Time

Gender and Identity

Research: Resources

  • “Esther Longtree had gone on with her expectations”: Esther Longtree in this chapter is portrayed as a profoundly tragic and complex figure, embodying themes of loss, mystery, maternal suffering, and societal judgment. Her character is marked by an overwhelming burden of perpetual pregnancy and stillbirths, an existence both physically and emotionally laden with pain and unanswered questions. Esther’s situation is unique and unprecedented in the legal realm, as she faces the threat of arrest and trial for the disappearance or death of her children: children who may never have been born or who remain unseen by others. She is caught in a web of suspicion and gossip, misunderstood and condemned by her community and the justice system, yet she remains deeply isolated, bearing her sorrow silently and without defense. This isolation is compounded by her physical condition (repeatedly pregnant yet without the relief of childbirth) and by the haunting presence of her absent children, described metaphorically as her “tears” and “stillborn” who haunt her mind and body. Her relationship with the Chicago detective, who is implicated as the father of her lost children, adds layers of complexity and darkness to her narrative. The detective’s repeated appearances (disguised as various characters) are both invasive and inscrutable. Esther’s encounters with him are marked by violence, mystery, and a sense of inevitability: she is raped by him, yet the experience is described with a strange passivity and detachment, as if she were “sleeping in the grass” and indifferent. This rapist-father figure represents a brutal intrusion into her life, one that leaves physical and psychological scars, but also a tangled mystery of conception, conception without life, and lost pregnancies. Esther’s internal world is rich with symbolism and metaphor: the “stillborn” children, the “broken gooseberry bushes,” the “buzzard hanging rounded like a period” all evoke a landscape of death, loss, and unfulfilled potential. Despite her suffering, Esther is radiant and self-sufficient in a strange way, carrying her burden with a stubborn dignity. She is aware of the social judgments (accusations of insanity, immorality, or deceit) but she resists these with a fierce inner knowledge of her own innocence and the mystery of her condition. Her loneliness and estrangement are deeply felt, yet she remains luminous, a figure of both vulnerability and strength. The chapter also explores Esther’s reflections on gender and societal double standards. She contrasts her own experience as a woman burdened with loss and silence to the freedom a man might have in fathering children without consequence or shame. Her pregnancy and stillbirths are not only physical but symbolic of her emotional and existential state: a life marked by “murder of her heart,” repeated disappointments, and unanswered questions about innocence and guilt. Esther Longtree is a character defined by enigmatic suffering, caught between the natural and the supernatural, the legal and the personal. She embodies a tragic maternal figure whose story is one of loss made visible and invisible, a woman burdened by an unexplainable condition and an unresolvable mystery. Her narrative is steeped in poetic imagery and a haunting sense of inevitability and silence, marking her as both victim and enigma within the novel’s broader themes of reality, illusion, and the passage of time. See Esther Longtree.
    • Esther Longtree symbolizes a complex and tragic history of female suffering, loss, and misunderstood maternity that can be read as an allegory for broader historical and social issues in the United States. Her life, marked by repeated pregnancies, stillbirths, and the mysterious disappearance or loss of her children, reflects the harsh realities faced by many women, especially those marginalized or without social support. Esther’s experiences evoke themes of abandonment, societal judgment, and the silencing of women’s voices, particularly in the context of reproductive rights and maternal health. The narrative describes her perpetual state of pregnancy and loss, the accusations she faces from being called an “old maid” to insinuations of infanticide and her helplessness before the law and community gossip. This represents a history of women’s reproductive experiences often ignored or stigmatized by society and legal systems. Her story also touches on the failures of paternal responsibility, as the Chicago detective (a figure of authority) is implicated in her rape and desertion, embodying male cruelty and neglect. Esther’s repeated encounters with the detective, who is seeking evidence and truth in a cold, clinical way, symbolize the invasive and often hostile scrutiny women face from institutions. Her isolation in a small town, where every movement is known, contrasts with the anonymity she imagines in a big city like Chicago, highlighting the oppressive small-town social surveillance prevalent in American history. Her story, which is full of loss, silence, and the mystery of unacknowledged or stillborn children, can be seen as symbolizing the broader historical experiences of women who have suffered reproductive trauma, been marginalized in legal and social systems, and whose lives have been shaped by forces beyond their control. The imagery of barren family trees, repeated stillbirths, and accusations of guilt without proof evokes the historical struggles with fertility, motherhood, and female agency in the US. Esther Longtree’s history symbolizes the hidden, painful legacies of women’s reproductive lives in American history, including issues of maternity, loss, societal judgment, and the often cruel intersection of personal suffering with institutional authority.
  • “Her menses had stopped so long ago”: Menses, commonly known as a period or menstrual flow, is the monthly shedding of the uterine lining (endometrium) that occurs when a pregnancy does not happen. It is a natural part of the reproductive cycle, typically lasting 2 to 7 days, and is characterized by bleeding from the uterus.
  • “to be let alone outside the pale of law”: The Pale was the English-ruled ground around Dublin; to be beyond the pale was to be outside the law and its protection. The Pale of Settlement (1791 to 1917) was the land at the western edge of the Russian Empire where Jews were forced to live. See Judaism.
  • “All the women would flock to the courthouse in droves”: Andrea Dworkin wrote the book Right Wing Women that examined how women are exploited by right wing, conservative politics. They answer women’s fears about their place in the world with offers of chivalrous protection within the domain and under the control of men through patriarchal structures such as the male head of household. It reassures women that they do not need to change the status quo, but accept it, and take whatever access to power is given, almost exclusively along gender defined norms. When these women see other women flaunting these gender norms, they bond with their fathers, husbands or father-figures to form a power base through misogyny, the enforcement branch of patriarchy, to bring the status quo back in force. Right-Wing Women was published in 1983. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.

  • “a Chicago detective and specialist in murder mysteries”: The Chicago Detective can be understood symbolically as a representation of a police state or police corruption through several layers of meaning revealed in the novel. See The Chicago Detective.
    • The Chicago Detective appears as an omnipresent, persistent figure who repeatedly pursues Esther Longtree, the woman at the center of a murder mystery involving unknown children and dark accusations. He comes “three times in three different years” looking for evidence and never finds the murderer but consistently finds Esther herself, lying vulnerable and exposed. His repeated visits and obsessive investigations suggest an invasive, overreaching authority typical of a police state that monitors and controls individuals without clear cause.
    • Esther Longtree’s experience with the Chicago Detective is marked by fear and victimization. She is “raped” by him in a mysterious way, leaving behind “evidence, the symptoms, the signs” (his “eye-glasses, his calling card, a bone button, the frozen conception, a thread, and a single spot of brown blood”) which haunts her. This portrayal aligns the detective with a corrupt or abusive power that violates personal boundaries and fabricates or misuses evidence to persecute the vulnerable.
    • The Detective’s attitude toward “white” versus “black” deaths underscores a racial bias, a hallmark of systemic police corruption and institutional racism. In Chapter 77, “he only dealt with the deaths of little white children, for the deaths of the little black children were not his business, there being so many and so unimportant.” This selective interest in victims based on race symbolizes the partiality and injustice within a corrupt police system.
    • The Detective is described as one who “had solved all life’s mysteries but his own” and “crept in among murderers and had gotten their confessions by confessing his own imaginary crimes.” He is trapped in a cycle of investigating crimes, yet he himself is implicated in the system’s violence and injustice. This reflects the idea of a police state where the enforcers are both agents and victims of systemic oppression, unable to escape the machinery they uphold.
    • The detective’s inability to find the true culprit yet his readiness to implicate Esther Longtree highlights the paranoia and miscarriage of justice prevalent in such systems.
    • The detective’s “many murder cases” and obsession with “quiet mysteries” where “there had never been a body” (Chapters 76 and 77) symbolize not only literal crimes but the suppression or erasure of truths inconvenient to those in power. The recurring motif of “quiet, quiet murders” points to systemic silencing and cover-ups typical in corrupt regimes.
    • The detective is associated with “old evidence staring, new evidence, trampled grass,” and “a clue which led nowhere” (Chapters 76 and 68). This suggests the manipulation or fabrication of evidence which is a central tool of police corruption to maintain control or silence dissent.
    • The detective threatened Esther Longtree with legal persecution and exposed her to the scrutiny of a judgmental society. This intertwines with the theme of social control exercised by authoritarian entities that use fear and punishment to maintain order.
    • The Chicago Detective functions symbolically as an embodiment of police state oppression and corruption. He is a figure of invasive surveillance, racial bias, miscarriage of justice, and systemic violence. His relentless pursuit of a vulnerable woman, selective concern for victims based on race, and role in perpetuating a cycle of unending investigation without resolution all reflect the mechanisms by which a police state enforces control through fear, misinformation, and partiality. The Detective’s character exposes the tragic human consequences of living under such a regime, where truth is elusive and justice is compromised.
  • “a man who had killed several wives”: In Perrault’s Bluebeard (1697) a husband murders wife after wife. Chicago had its own: Johann Hoch, the “Stockyard Bluebeard,” married at least 55 women between 1890 and 1905, poisoning many with arsenic, and was hanged in Chicago in 1906. See Fairy Tales and Folklore and Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “his eye-glasses, his footprints, too, his calling card”: These are all clues used in famous mystery novels. The Golden Age of Detective Fiction (roughly 1913-1939) was an era between the World Wars defined by polite, “whodunit” puzzle mysteries, primarily featuring British writers like Agatha Christie, Dorothy L. Sayers, Margery Allingham, and Ngaio Marsh. These stories favored intellectual, fair-play clues over violence, often set in closed circles like country houses, focusing on solving the murder rather than dark, psychological grit. The “closed circle” of suspects, “fair play” (all clues provided to the reader), and the focus on the “whodunit” puzzle. Iconic characters from this time period: Hercule Poirot, Miss Marple, Lord Peter Wimsey, Perry Mason, and Philip Marlowe. E.C. Bentley’s Trent’s Last Case (1913) is often cited as the beginning, with Christie’s The Mysterious Affair at Styles (1920) setting the standard. The Detection Club, founded in 1930, established “rules” for the genre, such as no faked clues or supernatural solutions. The stories offered escapism, featuring a return to order after a “sanitized” version of murder, often reflecting a desire to move past the trauma of World War I. While often associated with the British “cozy” style, the era also saw the rise of American “hard-boiled” fiction. American hard-boiled detective fiction is a gritty, unsentimental subgenre emerging in the 1920s-30s, defined by cynical private eyes, urban corruption, fast-paced dialogue, and brutal violence. Pioneered by Dashiell Hammett and perfected by Raymond Chandler, these stories moved away from genteel puzzles to realistic crime, often published in pulp magazines like Black Mask.
    • During the Golden Age of Detective Fiction, eyeglasses generally symbolized intellectualism, keen observation, or a specific social, physical, or moral state. They were used to signify a character’s scholarly status, wealth, or, in some cases, to conceal identity or suggest a hidden, vulnerable, or untrustworthy nature.
    • In the Victorian and Golden Age eras, footprints functioned as vital, often clichéd, forensic evidence used to reconstruct a crime scene in the absence of advanced, systematic fingerprinting. They served as a tangible link between a suspect and the crime, often revealing clues about the perpetrator’s physical characteristics, movement, and identity.
    • A calling card, also known as a visiting card, generally refers to two distinct concepts: a social tool of the Victorian/Edwardian era and a signature item left by a criminal at a crime scene.
    • A thread as a clue refers both to a literal, physical object and, metaphorically, to the clue which guided a detective through the “labyrinth” of a crime, originating from the Greek myth of Theseus and Ariadne.
  • “a moss rosebud brown as a mushroom”: The moss rose, its buds furred with green moss, was a favorite of Victorian gardens. See Roses and Flowers.
  • “ringing, ringing, ringing in his ears”: During Esther’s part in the book the repetition of three words appears as if Esther has a stutter. In rhetoric, epizeuxis is the repetition of a word or phrase in immediate succession, typically within the same sentence, for vehemence or emphasis. A closely related rhetorical device is diacope, which involves word repetition that is broken up by a single intervening word, or a small number of intervening words. As a rhetorical device, epizeuxis is utilized to create an emotional appeal, thereby inspiring and motivating the audience. However, epizeuxis can also be used for comic effect.

  • “a bone button, the frozen conception”: A bone button as a clue generally signifies a crucial, mundane, and often overlooked piece of physical evidence that identifies a suspect based on their social standing, clothing, or actions. In the “Golden Age” of detective fiction, such items were used to show how a killer could be convicted by a small, everyday object rather than just fingerprints or bloodstains.

  • “Conception was always a secret to the mother”: In many cultures, the discussion of all sexual issues has traditionally been considered taboo, and adolescents were not given any information on sexual matters. Such instruction, as was given, was traditionally left to a child’s parents, and often this was put off until just before their marriage. However, in the late 19th century, the progressive education movement led to the introduction of sex education as social hygiene in North American school curricula and the introduction of school-based sex education. Despite early inroads of school-based sex education, most of the information on sexual matters in the mid-20th century was obtained informally from friends and the media, and much of this information was deficient or of dubious value, especially during the period following puberty, when curiosity about sexual matters was the most acute. It is considered misogynistic in the way female rape victims continue to be cast as collateral damage and the assault and rape of women is used as a plot point in a narrative that is fundamentally about men. Young is making this about Esther and showing how things would be different if she were a man. Young makes many references to mythology. The struggle to escape from sexual pursuit is one of the most popular motifs of Greek mythology: Persephone, Callisto, Daphne, Europa, Io, etc. Greek mythology depicts women as vulnerable to assault, able to escape only through death, or metamorphosis like Daphne who becomes a laurel tree. The consequence of fleeing sexual violence is another type of force on the body, the loss of the human form where women who survive sexual violence become pregnant, and when gods as the rapists, produce a hero child. The Chicago detective takes Esther for a corpse, then feels her pulse, finds her heartbeat, and rapes her. Chione in Greek mythology was said to be so beautiful that she was the object of a thousand men’s desire. Hermes put Chione into a deep sleep with his wand and proceeded to rape her. Since Young has said Esther is the mother of us all and Mr. Spitzer as the father of us all, these could be archetypes for Adam and Eve and possibly Lilith. In On the Origin of the World, part of the Nag Hammadi Library, the rulers of the world rape the earthly Eve, and she bears their children. In the midrash Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer, Samael, riding the serpent, seduces Eve and fathers Cain; later Kabbalah makes Lilith, Adam’s first wife, Samael’s bride. The Talmud says that in the years Adam was apart from Eve he fathered spirits, demons, and lilin (Eruvin 18b). See Greek Mythology, Genesis, and Angels and Archangels.
