Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 21

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Pillars: Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The Questions

Vera decides to let Miss MacIntosh believe she woke first and knows nothing. Is that mercy, or the sealing completed?

Her pity moves from the baldness to “sorrows greater than baldness.” What does the Witness see once it stops staring at the shocking surface?

Miss MacIntosh warns that the Devil “does not always wear horns.” If evil wears the plain surface too, what surface can be trusted?

The bald head is “an unfinished thing, tentative and unbelievable.” Is unfinished the same as failed?

Vera makes her decision, and the decision is the problem of the Witness in one act. She will deceive Miss MacIntosh into believing she woke first and saw nothing, so that Miss MacIntosh can go on being “but a usual being in the light,” her self-image intact. This is tender, and it is the sealing completed. The two chapters before showed Vera learning to stay with the stripped person; here she chooses to help that person stay sealed, and the book lets the choice stand without absolving it. The protection that began as compassion has become, by its own logic, the keeping of a locked door.

But the chapter also shows the Witness doing its truest work. Vera’s pity, which began fixed on the shocking surface, the baldness, moves past it: she comes to feel “sorrows greater than baldness,” “pity, pity, pity” for the whole person and not the one appalling fact. This is the Witness maturing past its first flinch. To stay with someone is to stop staring at the wound and begin to see the life around it, the larger sorrows the baldness was only the visible edge of. The surface was never the thing; the person was.

Miss MacIntosh’s own warnings unsettle every surface. The Devil “does not always wear horns,” she says; he can quote scripture, wear charming guises, come as the plain and the good. “Evil has many forms, and the good has only one and has no other face.” If even the surface can be a disguise, then no appearance can be trusted to be what it shows, which is the book’s account of the built world turned toward the moral: the surface is made, and the made surface can deceive.

And the baldness itself, read past the horror, is an “unfinished thing, tentative and unbelievable.” The chapter offers this as meaninglessness, but held against the rest of the book it is something else: the unfinished as the truthful shape. Miss MacIntosh’s stripped head is not a completed fact to be filed under tragedy; it is tentative, still, unresolved, and its refusal to be a finished thing is closer to honesty than the sealed surface ever was. The book will later stand a figure on this, the woman forever pregnant and never delivered; here it appears first on an old nursemaid’s bare head.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that makes Vera’s deception a clean mercy, and the reading that makes it a clean betrayal. And it refuses the reading that takes the baldness as simple tragedy, a life reduced to a barren fact.

The footing is that the Witness matures here past the shocking surface into “sorrows greater than baldness,” and in the same chapter it completes the sealing it began, choosing to keep Miss MacIntosh’s self-image intact. The book holds both, the deepening compassion and the locked door, as the true doubleness of witnessing the sealed. And the bald head, read past horror, is the unfinished held as truer than the finished surface it replaced. The Witness sees past the wound to the person, and the unfinished is not the failure it first looks like. Vera’s deception stands unabsolved, and the chapter closes the baldness sequence without deciding whether her mercy was mercy.

For Reflection

Is letting someone keep a comforting self-image mercy, or the completion of their sealing?

Once one stops staring at another’s most shocking fact, what larger sorrows come into view?

What in one’s own life looks like a barren, finished tragedy that might be truer read as unfinished?
