Chapter 55

On Chapter 55 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of The Wheel and the Spokes.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 54 · Wheel and Spokes Home · Chapter 56 →

Research notes: Chapter 55 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 55

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

"his mind did not now so easily differentiate between himself and his brother as in the past, there being now no thinker thinking Mr. Spitzer's thoughts"

Mr. Spitzer is constitutionally multiple. He and Peron are not merely two brothers; they are two simultaneous versions of a single being, divided by temperament (order vs. chaos, composer vs. gambler, introvert vs. extrovert) but sharing a body of patrimony, a house, a face, a comb swept up from the same waves. The plural self is not metaphor here; it is the literal condition of being a twin.

But the plurality deepens after Peron's death. Mr. Spitzer does not become singular; he becomes more fractured:

"After Peron's death, he had dreamed he was two brothers, and one was dead."

He cannot mourn cleanly because he cannot locate the boundary between himself and what he mourns. His retinal rivalry (each eye seeing a different image, the images refusing to fuse) is the somatic expression of the plural self. He sees double because he is double. The self is not one person. The chapter does not treat this as tragedy to be overcome. It treats it as the permanent condition of Mr. Spitzer's existence, as irreducible as his grief.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

"The morning had never come. The morning had been the dream, and the night had been real."

Young's prose enacts this pillar rather than merely stating it. The chapter is saturated with mirages, hallucinations, counterfeit currencies, forged signatures, and false identities. Peron forges Joachim's name on checks. The bank teller insists there is only one brother. Mr. Spitzer finds himself in the wrong carriage, as the wrong passenger.

Most strikingly, the chapter offers this formulation:

"for all money was counterfeit even like the sun, moon, stars turned out at the great Mint"

This is not cynicism; it is ontological. The counterfeit is not the exception to the real; it is the condition of the real. Even the sun is manufactured. The cave has no outside. Mr. Spitzer's investments in sunken Roman galleys and Phoenician traders and kingdoms never marked on any map are not delusions of a failed man; they are the honest investments, the ones that acknowledge their own speculative nature, their dependence on contingencies that may never arrive. The conventional "secure" investments are the real illusions, pretending to stability in a world that has none.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

This pillar may be the chapter's dominant key.

"He had already worn the black mourning cape, how often he must remember now. He had already mourned for the man he once had been, for he had foreseen the empty future."

Mr. Spitzer does not grieve in sequence. He grieves simultaneously and recursively, for his brother, for himself, for the music he never composed, for the life he might have led if his brother had died earlier, for the life his brother led and he did not. He leaves white roses or red roses at his brother's grave:

"a white rose or a red rose depending upon whether he mourned for himself or his brother"

The roses are interchangeable. The mourning is undifferentiated. Grief here is not a response to a specific loss; it is the climate of Mr. Spitzer's entire existence, the weather in which all his other experiences occur. His forehead is "seamed vertically by a line of grief" as a permanent topographical feature, not a temporary expression. The chapter offers no arc toward healing. It is, structurally, a long meditation on grief that does not move, and the prose formally enacts this through its recursive, digressive, non-advancing sentences that circle back endlessly on the same losses.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Young's narrative stance is itself an exercise in this pillar. The prose refuses to judge Mr. Spitzer, to pathologize him, to conclude about him. It stays in the room with his confusion, his double vision, his sleeping-while-waking, his inability to distinguish his own memories from his brother's experiences.

Mr. Spitzer himself practices a version of witnessing:

"he posed his questions in such a way as to receive only the most discursive replies and was also very discursive in his replies... he wished if possible his life to be this continuum"

He refuses resolution. He keeps the questions open. He does not want to "absolve himself completely from the responsibility for his own dark past": meaning he insists on staying in the ambiguity rather than achieving the clean verdict of either guilt or innocence. This is attention as ethics: refusing the simplification that would make things easier but less true.

The architectural design of the house (its twin rooms, its perfect symmetries, its maps and plumb lines) is also a form of witnessing: an attempt to see everything accurately, to leave nothing unaccounted for, to mark every boundary. That it fails does not invalidate the attempt. The witness does not guarantee clarity. The witness guarantees presence.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

"he had made his map and marked his boundary lines before the discovery—had mapped out his entire life"

"Using compasses like those which had moved between two golden-footed stars, also moving, using slide rulers and quadrants and plumb-lines... he had drawn the intricate maps"

Mr. Spitzer builds obsessively and provisionally. The house with its twin gardens, twin portraits, twin staircases, twin chimney pieces is a structure built on the knowledge of its own incompleteness; it accounts for Peron's chaos by trying to contain it within symmetry, but symmetry is always already undermined by the "slight ripple in one which was not in the other".

His musical project is the purest expression of recursive construction:

"he would write the tenth symphony, that which had never been written... that he would begin with the tenth symphony—begin where others had ended—ended their lives."

He does not intend to build on what others completed. He intends to begin at the point of incompletion: to start where the building was abandoned. This is recursive construction as explicit program: return to the unfinished, work from there, knowing the work will itself remain unfinished. The music sheets remain empty. This is not failure. It is the structure of the project.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

"The other great composers had written only their ninth symphonies—perhaps not even these—had never finished the tenth symphony, for they had died in the midst of it"

The tenth symphony, the one that has never been written, is Mr. Spitzer's life work precisely because it cannot be completed. He does not choose an achievable goal and fail to reach it. He chooses the structurally unachievable as his ethical commitment. The demand for completion would be a violence against the truth of musical experience, which always points beyond itself.

His music is described as his tomb, his sarcophagus:

"his music was his tomb. He had wished to be his own sarcophagus."

The work is not separate from the life; it is the life, and like the life, it cannot be sealed. The attempt to "cement his roof before the tide" is the honest acknowledgment that the roof will not hold, that the tide will come, and that building anyway is the only integrity available.

His unfinished manuscripts, his empty music scrolls in every room, his silence that is "more beautiful than music"; these are not signs of failure. They are the honest record of a man who refused to falsify completion.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Mr. Spitzer inhabits multiple marginal categories simultaneously:

  • He is a twin: a person whose identity is structurally divided, never fully singular
  • He is a composer who cannot compose: an artist defined by the work he cannot make
  • He is a lawyer who never practices law: a professional identity held without exercise
  • He is a man who cannot be sure he is alive: suspended between sleep and waking, life and death
  • He invests in kingdoms that never existed, ships that have sunk, bonds of lost empires

"some cancelled postage stamps from kingdoms this world had never seen—republics never marked upon any map"

These marginal investments are not mistakes; they are the evidence. They reveal that all investments depend on contingencies, that all kingdoms are provisional, that the difference between the Republic of Lost Atlantis and the United States Treasury is a matter of duration, not ontology. Mr. Spitzer's marginal position makes visible what the conventional investor's position conceals.

His double vision (the near things seeming small, the far things seeming large, the proportions reversed) is a marginal epistemology that, rather than being corrected, reveals something true: that conventional perspective is also a construction, that the "normal" proportions are not natural but learned.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This pillar is perhaps most beautifully enacted in the chapter's relationship to the past.

"his memory was become like an Aeolian harp, a harp with many strings"

"The past does not recede. It accumulates beneath us."

For Mr. Spitzer, the past does not recede at all. His brother's laughter still sounds:

"he had heard his brother's mocking laughter then as now, for never would his brother's laughter depart from him"

His investments in sunken galleys and Phoenician traders are literally sedimentary; they exist beneath the water, beneath the ground, pressing upward, not yet reclaimed but not gone. His house, now demolished, is still present in his memory as he passes the street. The twin cupids still point their arrows in his mind even though the walls have been knocked down.

Most strikingly, the chapter's very prose style is sedimentary. Each sentence deposits the previous one beneath it rather than moving past it. The accumulation of clauses (clause upon clause, memory upon memory, loss upon loss) formally enacts the sedimentary model of time. You cannot read this chapter quickly because it will not let the past recede.

"He wandered, and the twin house was no more, and he only dreamed that he passed it now—only dreamed that it had once been real and true, stone by stone, glass by glass, bird by bird."

Stone by stone, glass by glass, bird by bird: the granular specificity of accumulation. The past is not a blur. It is a precise deposit.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Young's prose is one of the most radical enactments of this pillar in American literature. The chapter's sentences do not describe Mr. Spitzer's condition from outside; they perform it from inside. The recursive syntax, the endless qualification, the refusal to arrive at a period, the doubling and redoubling of images: all of this is language that names its own location as the consciousness of a man who cannot see one thing without seeing two.

"When he looked at one spot, his eyes saw two spots. When he looked at two spots, his eyes saw four spots."

This is not just description. The prose itself doubles. Every image in the chapter has its mirror: twin portraits, twin gardens, twin moons, twin brothers, twin graves. The language has built the reality it appears to describe.

The legal language scattered through the chapter ("party of the second part," "party of the first part", the law library titles like Authenticity of Will, Will of the Dead, Cases in Chancery) is language that pretends to speak from nowhere, to establish objective categories, to fix identity and ownership. Mr. Spitzer's inability to practice this language, his constant confusion about which party he is, his forgery of his own name; these expose the constructed nature of legal identity itself.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

"Death was silence, though it was also figured like a state of mind, he believed, for there could be no state of mind which was not figured, wrought with its possibilities."

The sacred in this chapter is entirely immanent; it lives in the double vision, the sunken ships, the empty music scrolls, the white rose left at the grave. There is no heaven Mr. Spitzer appeals to, no transcendent resolution he awaits. His music, his "tomb," his "sarcophagus", is the closest thing to the sacred, and it exists in the silence between notes, in the echo that outlasts the sound:

"Silence was more beautiful than music, for the echo was more beautiful than the sound... Sound was life, echo of echo."

The sacred is the echo. The echo is immanent; it exists in the same physical space as the sound, it is made of the same air, it is the sound continuing into its own absence. This is not transcendence upward and out. This is transcendence deeper in.

His investments in the unreachable (sunken galleys, drowned moons, lost empires) are acts of immanent devotion. He worships what is here, beneath the water, beneath the ground, in the sediment. The czar's iron bell, Kolokol, that might ring in Moscow; this is not a fantasy of escape. It is an investment in the sacred possibility latent within the ordinary impossible.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

"He was not omniscient—no heron's blue eye floated out of his forehead—or if so, his vision was in another sphere."

Mr. Spitzer is constituted by what was deposited before he arrived. His patrimony comes from sunken ships. His house was built according to plans that preceded his occupation of it. His musical ambition begins where Beethoven and others ended. He carries his brother's comb on the opposite side of his body: a structural symmetry he did not choose, only discovered.

And he is constituting the ground others will stand on, though he cannot know it. His music, unwritten, is still a presence in the culture, the tenth symphony that defines what has not yet been done. His empty scrolls are the negative space that shapes what comes after.

"He had gone on when he could not go on—that was the great thing, really, in Mr. Spitzer's life."

This is the crossing point's obligation stated plainly. The carrying continues not because it is chosen but because it is structural. Mr. Spitzer did not choose to be Peron's keeper, his alibi, his financial backstop, his mourner. He did not choose to be the survivor. The obligation was deposited in him by the fact of being a twin, by the accident of outliving, by the architecture of the house that divided space into two equal hemispheres neither brother could fully occupy alone.

The ancestors are in the sediment. The sunken ships are the patrimony. The carrying continues.
