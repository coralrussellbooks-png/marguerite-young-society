Summary

Mr. Spitzer's field of law is the dead: wills, deeds, archives, and lost heirs. One case involves two Boston spinsters, each believing she is Cleopatra. One left her fortune to her Siamese cats under his supervision, and the cats seem likely to live forever. He asks what Peron would have done had their places been reversed. He concludes that Peron would never have been faithful to Catherine's bedside, but would have dragged her into the living sunlight even if it killed her. He reflects that to reach the upper world one must first travel through the lower.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, Mr. Spitzer’s role is described as one dealing with "the dead," wills, deeds, secret archives, and old houses haunted by ghosts: both literal and metaphorical. He has encountered many baffling situations, such as lost heirs and numerous wills designed to confuse even the most conscientious attorney. This has led him to doubt the very idea of inheritance and continuity through generations, extending even to physical traits like hair and eyes, which he likens to "properties... of a dream" that fade while observed. This passage underscores the theme of uncertainty and the fragile, illusory nature of identity and legacy.
  • Mr. Spitzer recalls an eccentric case of an old Boston lady who believed herself the reincarnation of Cleopatra and left her estate to her numerous Siamese cats, with the caretaker to live in the house as long as the cats lived. The cats are described almost mystically, with "nine lives," prowling the house and garden as if they owned it, playing piano keys, and embodying a kind of magical, surreal presence. This passage conveys a haunting atmosphere and illustrates Mr. Spitzer’s encounters with the bizarre and uncanny elements of death and legacy. It also reflects his cautious and conservative approach to administering such an unusual estate.
  • This is a deeply poignant section where Mr. Spitzer recalls the shock of identifying his dead brother in a morgue among the homeless and faceless corpses. He reflects on how his life has since been shadowed by this event, feeling as if he lives his brother’s life and dies his brother’s death. The passage reveals Mr. Spitzer’s internal struggle with grief, memory, and the passing of time. It also illuminates the contrast between the brothers: Mr. Spitzer’s “cosmic serenity” versus his brother’s impatience, competition, and instability. This reflection on personal loss and identity is central to the chapter’s emotional core.
  • Mr. Spitzer expresses a belief in a future beyond the law and human institutions where a future of “silent music” and “heavenly harmonies” is fixed beyond time’s fluctuations. He contemplates the idea that perfect life should be perfect death, where there is no rivalry, no immortal derangement, but complete oblivion rather than memory. This is contrasted with Catherine’s opium-induced desire for complete memory. These meditations reveal Mr. Spitzer’s somber, even nihilistic, outlook on existence, death, and what, if anything, lies beyond.
  • The chapter contrasts Mr. Spitzer’s faithful, solemn devotion to Cartherine and the dead with his brother Peron’s impetuous love of life, fear of death, and competitive nature. Mr. Spitzer doubts that his brother would have been so faithful or so enduring in grief, emphasizing Peron’s preference for the “great crowds of Derby Day” and his inability to face death or prolonged farewell. This contrast highlights different ways of confronting mortality and loss, and the complex dynamics of family loyalty and love.
  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates Catherine’s opium-induced visions and her tendency to “narcotize” her memory to avoid facing reality, creating an opaque inner world filled with imaginary beings. He serves as a guardian of her property and life, yet his presence is often ignored or rejected, and he is caught in a paradox of faithfulness and absence. This passage captures the theme of elusive truth, memory, and perception, and the isolating burden of care and love.
  • Toward the end, Mr. Spitzer reflects that to reach the upper world (perhaps a metaphor for peace, truth, or heaven), one must first pass through the lower world: the streets and alleys inhabited by thieves and painted street women. This metaphor suggests a journey through all aspects of life, including its sordid or painful parts, before reaching any transcendence. It ties into the chapter’s exploration of death, memory, and the complexity of existence.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Joachim Spitzer (Peron’s twin brother)

  • Family lawyer, guardian of the dead’s legacy.
  • Scrupulous, conservative, somber, introspective.
  • Struggles with the paradox of his profession and personal grief over his twin brother. He is caught between duty and emotional turmoil.
  • Believes in a final peace beyond life and law, emphasizing oblivion rather than memory after death.
  • His work involves dealing with the hidden past, dusty archives, and eccentric clients, many of whom create baffling legal puzzles through multiple wills or lost heirs.
  • This career, entered reluctantly due to family expectations, keeps him continually close to death and the mysteries of inheritance.
  • A notable case in Mr. Spitzer’s youth involved an elderly Boston lady who believed she was Cleopatra reincarnated. She left her estate and numerous Siamese cats to an ancient caretaker, with Mr. Spitzer supervising. The cats are described almost as immortal beings, with a mysterious presence that haunts the house, symbolizing the blurred lines between life and death, reality and illusion.
  • He also considers Catherine's narcotic-induced oblivion as a form of escape from reality, pondering the value of memory and oblivion in death.
  • Mr. Spitzer philosophizes on the necessity of traveling through the "lower world" (a metaphorical space of moral and social darkness) before reaching the "upper world" or enlightenment. This journey includes encountering thieves, street women, and the underbelly of life, suggesting his awareness of the complex layers of existence beyond his legal profession.

Peron Spitzer (Joachim’s twin brother)

  • Impulsive, competitive, and distrustful of the law.
  • Impetuous, restless, afraid of death.
  • His relationship with Joachim is that their lives and deaths intertwine metaphorically; Peron’s death haunts Joachim.
  • Represents the chaotic, fleeting nature of life and human memory.

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • Bedridden and addicted to opium.
  • Mr. Spitzer handles her estate.
  • Represents illusions of grandeur and the persistence of the past into the present.
  • Her estate is managed by Mr. Spitzer.
  • Central to Mr. Spitzer’s personal life, her illness and narcotic use create a complex emotional dynamic.
  • Embodies memory, oblivion, and the tension between awareness and escape.

Research: Themes

  • Mortality and the Legacy of the Dead The chapter explores how death shapes the living through legal, emotional, and mystical legacies. The dead control the living via wills and memories, but these connections are uncertain and fraught with paradox.
  • Memory vs. Oblivion Mr. Spitzer contrasts Catherine’s desire for memory with his own preference for oblivion after death, questioning what form of afterlife or peace is truly desirable.
  • Illusion and Reality The recurring motif of illusions, reincarnations, the cats’ seeming immortality, and fragmented memories, highlights the fragile, dream-like quality of existence. This is similar to the Catherine’s canary at the beginning of the book.
  • The Law and Its Limits The law is portrayed as an imperfect, sometimes arbitrary system that tries to impose order on the chaos of life and death. Mr. Spitzer hopes for a future beyond law, where harmony replaces discord.
  • Duality of Human Nature Through the contrast between Joachim and his twin brother Peron, the duality of restraint vs. impulsiveness, order vs. chaos, and life vs. death is examined.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

  • The Siamese Cats Symbolize mystery, immortality, and the blurred boundary between life and death. Their dominance in the Boston lady’s house reflects ongoing life amid death.
  • The Pyramid and the Sphinx Represent ancient legacies, riddles of existence, and the enigmatic nature of identity and memory.
  • The Dead Brother A symbol of lost potential, memory, and the haunting presence of the past in the present.
  • Opium and Narcotics Reflect escape, oblivion, and the tension between retaining memory or seeking peace through forgetfulness.

Research: Resources

  • In the US, property can be passed down through generations in perpetuity through methods (Heirs’ Property, Perpetual Trusts, Family Limited Partnerships, Buy-Sell Agreements, Limited LIability Companies (LLC), Living Trusts, Irrevocable Trusts) ensuring a lasting legacy. Promoting Inequality: A significant critique of inherited wealth is that it exacerbates economic inequality, enabling wealth acquisition without labor and hindering social mobility. Critics of these methods create a "Virtual Aristocracy". These unrestricted perpetual trusts, can hold wealth indefinitely, and could lead to the concentration of wealth in the hands of a few families, potentially creating a privileged class. Inherited wealth can create a "glass floor," preventing children from privileged families from falling below a certain economic level, regardless of their own efforts. Inheriting wealth without earning it can undermine a sense of accomplishment and make inheritors feel like mere placeholders for future generations. Support for these methods state passing on wealth to kin is considered a deeply ingrained human desire and is seen as an essential aspect of individual freedom within a system of private property rights. It makes estate tax deductions for charitable bequests easier and provides an incentive for wealthy individuals to donate to charities. Perpetual trusts, when properly structured, can protect assets from potential legal challenges and distributions that may not be in the long-term interest of beneficiaries. The US has a history of enacting estate and gift taxes, though their reach and rates have fluctuated over time, often tied to war financing or efforts to curb wealth concentration. There is ongoing debate about the role and impact of these taxes, with some advocating for their repeal and others for their reform. Traditional legal principles like the Rule Against Perpetuities aimed to prevent perpetual trusts, but many states have modified or abolished this rule, leading to renewed debate about the implications of trusts that can last indefinitely. Other viewpoints have called for the complete abolition of inheritance, asserting that it is incompatible with a fair and equitable society.
  • Women's property rights have expanded over time in the United States, but they have been restricted by gender stereotypes and laws.In medieval times, married women's land became their husbands' property. In 1718, Pennsylvania allowed married women to manage their homes while their husbands were away. In 1848, New York passed the Married Women's Property Act, which allowed women to keep their property when they married. Other states passed similar laws in the 1850s. By 1900, most states recognized women's property rights. However, women of color were not included in these rights. Women faced barriers to accessing credit and mortgages without a male co-signer. In 1974, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act was passed, which helped to address these barriers. Women can apply for home loans and take out lines of credit, which has made it easier for them to become homeowners. Gender stereotypes and ideologies have historically been barriers to women's property rights.

  • “perpetual grief” and two other quotes above may refer to the philosophy of Arthur Schopenhauer who believed that death is a natural part of life and a return to a state of non-existence that existed before birth. He thought that individual consciousness is impermanent and that the life of an individual is meaningless. His key ideas are will-to-live (the driving force behind our attachment to life and our fear of death), knowledge (shows the meaninglessness of life and the worthlessness of living), suicide (pointless because it affirms the will-to-live), palingenesis (suggest that the mind has always existed and will continue to exist), redemption (that death is an opportunity for redemption through the negation of the will). The portrayal of Mr. Spitzer's lifelong sorrow, his recognition of life as a series of losses and unfulfilled desires, and his contemplation of the illusory nature of reality all echo Schopenhauerian themes. The passage reflects a worldview where grief and suffering are continuous and intrinsic to life.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s belief that people need to travel though the lower world to reach the upper world mirrors Pilgrim’s Progress and/or Dante’s writings. There are 7 levels of purgatory with 7 vices. There are 7 virtues. Hell has 9 circles and Heaven has 9 levels of the Moon, Mercury, Venus, Sun, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, the fixed stars, and the primum mobile. According to Dante the cosmos depends on God, who exists in the Empyrean, and immaterial heaven beyond space and time. The Empyrean is made up of God’s love and light, and is home to angels, the blessed, and the souls of the faithful. Interesting note that the woman with the Siamese cats believes she was the reincarnation of Cleopatra and Dante meets Cleopatra in the circle of Hell called Lust.
  • Catherine is expecting a man to save her, which is the damsel in distress. This is a narrative device in which one or more men must rescue a woman who has been kidnapped or placed in other peril. The "damsel" is often portrayed as beautiful, popular, and of high social status; she is usually depicted as a princess in works with fantasy or fairy tale settings. Kinship, love, lust or a combination of those motivate the male protagonist to initiate the narrative. Critics have linked the helplessness of these women to societal views that women as a group need to be taken care of by men and treated nicely. Throughout the history of the trope, the role of the woman as the victim in need of a male savior has remained constant, but her attackers have changed to suit the tastes and collective fears of the period: "monsters, mad scientists, Nazis, hippies, bikers, aliens..."
  • “Musica Universalis” is a metaphysical principle by Boethius that uses mathematical relationships to express ‘tones’ that could be heard by the soul. The "Musica Universalis" here symbolizes the harmonious and eternal cosmic order that underlies the apparent chaos of mortal life and death. It is depicted as a sublime, transcendent music that governs the movements of the stars and planets, reflecting a divine harmony beyond human perception. Mr. Spitzer, a semi-retired lawyer and composer of "silent music," embodies this principle as someone who is attuned to the "music of the spheres" or the eternal, silent harmonies that exist beyond earthly sounds. His compositions, unheard by living ears and played only for the dead or in the celestial realm, metaphorically represent this cosmic music. The chapter emphasizes the contrast between earthly, temporal music and this higher, immutable harmony, suggesting that the tangible world is but a fragment or shadow of a greater metaphysical reality. Catherine's opium dreams expand this theme by blurring the boundaries between the real and the illusory, the living and the dead, reflecting the ineffable nature of the universal music that ties all existence together. The "Musica Universalis" is not just an abstract concept but a living presence in her visions with images of angels harping, celestial choirs, and cosmic rhythms permeating her consciousness. The chapter suggests that this music is intertwined with suffering and mortality (the "music of the perfect silence beyond the chance of the brittle and falling stars") highlighting a metaphysical view where life, death, and cosmic order are inseparable parts of a greater whole. The "Musica Universalis" is used to articulate a metaphysical principle that all existence, from the smallest earthly details to the vast cosmos, is bound by a harmonious, eternal music. This music transcends the limitations of physical reality and human understanding, offering a vision of unity amid fragmentation and a cosmic order beneath the apparent chaos of life and death.
