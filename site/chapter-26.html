To All My Darlings

Chapter 26

On Chapter 26 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 25 · Home · Chapter 27 →

Summary

Mr. Spitzer’s field of law is the dead: wills, deeds, archives, and lost heirs. One case involves two Boston spinsters, each believing she is Cleopatra. One left her fortune to her Siamese cats under his supervision, and the cats seem likely to live forever. He relives the hour he identified his twin in a city morgue, and the day Catherine refused his hand for the last time. He asks what Peron would have done had their places been reversed. He concludes that Peron would never have been faithful to Catherine’s bedside, but would have dragged her into the living sunlight even if it killed her. He reflects that to reach the upper world one must first travel through the lower.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, Mr. Spitzer’s role is described as one dealing with “the dead,” wills, deeds, secret archives, and old houses haunted by ghosts: both literal and metaphorical. He has encountered many baffling situations, such as lost heirs and numerous wills designed to confuse even the most conscientious attorney. This has led him to doubt the very idea of inheritance and continuity through generations, extending even to physical traits like hair and eyes, which he likens to “properties… of a dream” that fade while observed. This passage underscores the theme of uncertainty and the fragile, illusory nature of identity and legacy.
  • Mr. Spitzer recalls an eccentric case of an old Boston lady who believed herself the reincarnation of Cleopatra and left her estate to her numerous Siamese cats, with the caretaker to live in the house as long as the cats lived. The cats are described almost mystically, with “nine lives,” prowling the house and garden as if they owned it, playing piano keys, and embodying a kind of magical, surreal presence. This passage conveys a haunting atmosphere and illustrates Mr. Spitzer’s encounters with the bizarre and uncanny elements of death and legacy. It also reflects his cautious and conservative approach to administering such an unusual estate.
  • This is a deeply poignant section where Mr. Spitzer recalls the shock of identifying his dead brother in a morgue among the homeless and faceless corpses. He reflects on how his life has since been shadowed by this event, feeling as if he lives his brother’s life and dies his brother’s death. The passage reveals Mr. Spitzer’s internal struggle with grief, memory, and the passing of time. It also illuminates the contrast between the brothers: Mr. Spitzer’s “cosmic serenity” versus his brother’s impatience, competition, and instability. This reflection on personal loss and identity is central to the chapter’s emotional core.
  • Mr. Spitzer expresses a belief in a future beyond the law and human institutions where a future of “silent music” and “heavenly harmonies” is fixed beyond time’s fluctuations. He contemplates the idea that perfect life should be perfect death, where there is no rivalry, no immortal derangement, but complete oblivion rather than memory. This is contrasted with Catherine’s opium-induced desire for complete memory. These meditations reveal Mr. Spitzer’s somber, even nihilistic, outlook on existence, death, and what, if anything, lies beyond.
  • The chapter contrasts Mr. Spitzer’s faithful, solemn devotion to Catherine and the dead with his brother Peron’s impetuous love of life, fear of death, and competitive nature. Mr. Spitzer doubts that his brother would have been so faithful or so enduring in grief, emphasizing Peron’s preference for the “great crowds of Derby Day” and his inability to face death or prolonged farewell. This contrast highlights different ways of confronting mortality and loss, and the complex dynamics of family loyalty and love.
  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates Catherine’s opium-induced visions and her tendency to “narcotize” her memory to avoid facing reality, creating an opaque inner world filled with imaginary beings. He serves as a guardian of her property and life, yet his presence is often ignored or rejected, and he is caught in a paradox of faithfulness and absence. This passage captures the theme of elusive truth, memory, and perception, and the isolating burden of care and love.
  • Toward the end, Mr. Spitzer reflects that to reach the upper world (perhaps a metaphor for peace, truth, or heaven), one must first pass through the lower world: the streets and alleys inhabited by thieves and painted street women. This metaphor suggests a journey through all aspects of life, including its sordid or painful parts, before reaching any transcendence. It ties into the chapter’s exploration of death, memory, and the complexity of existence.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “His department was that of the dead.” How has the law shaped what Mr. Spitzer believes about inheritance?
  • The cats “seemed to know they owned the place.” Who owns whom in this house?
  • “Perfect life should be perfect death.” What kind of heaven does Mr. Spitzer want?

Characters

  • Catherine wants “a complete memory,” and Mr. Spitzer wants “a complete oblivion.” Why does she drug her memory, then?
  • Would Peron “have endured my mother’s pleasure in ignoring him”? What does Joachim’s faithfulness cost him?
  • Catherine “held it against him that he did not rescue her.” What would rescue mean for her?

Connections

  • Catherine’s canary “had died already many times without her knowledge” (Chapter 2). How do the immortal cats echo it?
  • In Chapter 25, Mr. Spitzer did not believe in heaven “except as a state of mind.” How does he describe it here?
  • One must “travel through the lower world” to reach the upper. Where has Vera’s own journey gone downward?

Reflection

  • Should the dead control the living through what they leave behind?
  • Is faithfulness without hope a virtue?
  • Would you choose a complete memory or a complete oblivion?

Research: Characters

Joachim Spitzer (Peron’s twin brother)

  • Family lawyer, guardian of the dead’s legacy.
  • Scrupulous, conservative, somber, introspective.
  • Struggles with the paradox of his profession and personal grief over his twin brother. He is caught between duty and emotional turmoil.
  • Believes in a final peace beyond life and law, emphasizing oblivion rather than memory after death.
  • His work involves dealing with the hidden past, dusty archives, and eccentric clients, many of whom create baffling legal puzzles through multiple wills or lost heirs.
  • This career, entered reluctantly due to family expectations, keeps him continually close to death and the mysteries of inheritance.
  • A notable case in Mr. Spitzer’s youth involved an elderly Boston lady who believed she was Cleopatra reincarnated. She left her house and garden to her innumerable Siamese cats, with the ancient caretaker to live there and draw an income as long as the cats lived, and Mr. Spitzer to supervise. The cats are described almost as immortal beings, with a mysterious presence that haunts the house, symbolizing the blurred lines between life and death, reality and illusion.
  • He also considers Catherine’s narcotic-induced oblivion as a form of escape from reality, pondering the value of memory and oblivion in death.
  • Mr. Spitzer philosophizes on the necessity of traveling through the “lower world” (a metaphorical space of moral and social darkness) before reaching the “upper world” or enlightenment. This journey includes encountering thieves, street women, and the underbelly of life, suggesting his awareness of the complex layers of existence beyond his legal profession.

Peron Spitzer (Joachim’s twin brother)

  • Impulsive, competitive, and distrustful of the law.
  • Impetuous, restless, afraid of death.
  • His and Joachim’s lives and deaths intertwine; Peron’s death haunts Joachim.
  • He distrusted the law and wanted only “a crooked lawyer,” one who “would fix the witness and the judge,” and he often tried to fix his brother.
  • He preferred “the great crowds of Derby Day” to any dream, and “had been afraid of death.”
  • Represents the chaotic, fleeting nature of life and human memory.

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • Bedridden and addicted to opium.
  • Mr. Spitzer handles her estate.
  • Represents illusions of grandeur and the persistence of the past into the present.
  • Central to Mr. Spitzer’s personal life, her illness and narcotic use create a complex emotional dynamic.
  • Embodies memory, oblivion, and the tension between awareness and escape.

Research: Themes

Mortality and the Legacy of the Dead

See: Death and Mortality

  • The chapter explores how death shapes the living through legal, emotional, and mystical legacies. The dead control the living via wills and memories, but these connections are uncertain and fraught with paradox.

Memory versus Oblivion

See: Memory and the Past

  • Mr. Spitzer contrasts Catherine’s desire for memory with his own preference for oblivion after death, questioning what form of afterlife or peace is truly desirable.

Illusion and Reality

See: Illusion and Reality

  • The recurring motif of illusions, reincarnations, the cats’ seeming immortality, and fragmented memories, highlights the fragile, dream-like quality of existence. This is similar to Catherine’s canary, which “had died already many times without her knowledge” (Chapter 2).

The Law and Its Limits

See: Guilt, Innocence, and Justice

  • The law is portrayed as an imperfect, sometimes arbitrary system that tries to impose order on the chaos of life and death. Mr. Spitzer hopes for a future beyond law, where harmony replaces discord.

Duality of Human Nature

See: Duality and Doubles · Twins and Doubles

  • Through the contrast between Joachim and his twin brother Peron, the duality of restraint vs. impulsiveness, order vs. chaos, and life vs. death is examined.

Research: Resources

  • “those who were poor and imagined they were rich and dwelled in many castles of illusion”: “Castles in Spain,” castles in the air, are in print in the Roman de la Rose (c. 1230) as chastiaus en Espaigne.
  • “handed down in golden perpetuity through the generations of the dead who thus controlled the living”: In the US, property can be passed down through generations in perpetuity, ensuring a lasting legacy. Promoting Inequality: A significant critique of inherited wealth is that it exacerbates economic inequality, enabling wealth acquisition without labor and hindering social mobility. Critics say these methods create a “Virtual Aristocracy”. These unrestricted perpetual trusts can hold wealth indefinitely, and could lead to the concentration of wealth in the hands of a few families, potentially creating a privileged class. Inheriting wealth without earning it can undermine a sense of accomplishment and make inheritors feel like mere placeholders for future generations. The US has a history of enacting estate and gift taxes, though their reach and rates have fluctuated over time, often tied to war financing or efforts to curb wealth concentration. There is ongoing debate about the role and impact of these taxes, with some advocating for their repeal and others for their reform. The Rule Against Perpetuities began in the Duke of Norfolk’s Case (1682), to limit the “dead hand” of the grave over property. Traditional legal principles like the Rule Against Perpetuities aimed to prevent perpetual trusts, but many states have modified or abolished this rule, leading to renewed debate about the implications of trusts that can last indefinitely. Other viewpoints have called for the complete abolition of inheritance, asserting that it is incompatible with a fair and equitable society.
  • “so many lost heirs, so many lost heiresses”: Salic law barred women from inheriting the French throne, and English succession preferred sons over elder daughters until 2013.
  • “There had been heirs who had been heiresses, heiresses who had been heirs”: Women’s property rights have expanded over time in the United States, but they have been restricted by gender stereotypes and laws. Under the English common law of coverture, a married woman’s property passed to her husband. In 1718, Pennsylvania’s Feme Sole Traders Act let the wives of absent mariners run businesses in their own names. In 1848, New York passed the Married Women’s Property Act, which allowed women to keep their property when they married. Other states passed similar laws in the 1850s. By 1900, most states recognized women’s property rights. However, women of color were not included in these rights. Gender stereotypes and ideologies have historically been barriers to women’s property rights. See Women, Gender, and Marriage and the Chapter 23 resources.
  • “the reincarnation of Cleopatra”: Cleopatra VII (69-30 BC), the last ruler of Ptolemaic Egypt and Mark Antony’s lover. Belief in reincarnation runs through Hinduism, and through Theosophy, founded in New York in 1875. See Ancient Egypt and Hinduism.
  • “a Sphinx in her garden… a greater pyramid than her neighbor could afford”: The Great Sphinx and the Great Pyramid of Khufu at Giza. Egyptian Revival garden ornaments were a fashion after Napoleon’s expedition to Egypt (1798). See Ancient Egypt.
  • “leaving her house and garden to her innumerable Siamese cats”: Siamese cats came from Siam (Thailand). The first known in America was sent to Lucy Hayes at the White House in 1878.
    • There were cases of pets and animals inheriting estates, though some are legends. Gunther VI, a German Shepherd, descends from a dog said to have inherited $80 million from Countess Karlotta Liebenstein in 1992. The Times of London called it “a ruse concocted by the scion of an Italian pharmaceutical company,” Maurizio Mian, and the Countess apparently never existed (Netflix, Gunther’s Millions, 2023). The poodle said to have inherited a fortune from New York heiress Ella Wendel in 1931 is an urban legend: Time reported in 1933 that her poodle Tobey “was not even mentioned in the will.” Blackie, a cat, inherited Ben Rea’s £7 million fortune in 1988 and was named the wealthiest cat by Guinness World Records (Guinness World Records, “International Cat Day,” 2017).
  • “each having nine lives”: “A woman hath nine lives like a cat” (John Heywood, 1546). See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “the windy garden where no birds sang”: “The sedge has withered from the lake, / And no birds sing” (Keats, “La Belle Dame sans Merci,” 1819). See Romantic Poets.
  • “eating from golden dishes fit for the dead pharaohs”: In ancient Egypt, cats were sacred to the goddess Bastet and were mummified by the hundreds of thousands. See Ancient Egypt.
  • “cats walking about like flower-faced sibyls”: The Sibyls were the prophetesses of the ancient world, among them the Cumaean Sibyl who guides Aeneas into the underworld. See Greek Mythology.
  • “less fearful than the raven might have been”: Poe’s “The Raven” (1845), perched “upon a bust of Pallas just above my chamber door.” See Edgar Allan Poe.
  • “A cat had walked up and down on the piano keys, playing a little tune from the works of one of the more obscure eighteenth-century musical composers”: Domenico Scarlatti’s Sonata in G minor, K. 30, has been called “The Cat’s Fugue” since the nineteenth century, after the story that his cat walked across the keyboard. See Music and Composers.
  • “like an old mummified pharaoh wrapped in his body which was his boat”: Pharaohs were buried with boats to carry them through the afterlife. Khufu’s cedar ship was found beside the Great Pyramid in 1954. See Ancient Egypt.
  • “the attitude of a perpetual grief”: This may refer to the philosophy of Arthur Schopenhauer who believed that death is a natural part of life and a return to a state of non-existence that existed before birth. He thought that individual consciousness is impermanent and that the life of an individual is meaningless. His key ideas are will-to-live (the driving force behind our attachment to life and our fear of death), knowledge (shows the meaninglessness of life and the worthlessness of living), suicide (pointless because it affirms the will-to-live, though it destroys the individual), palingenesis (the will, not the individual mind or soul, is reborn in new beings), redemption (that death is an opportunity for redemption through the negation of the will). The portrayal of Mr. Spitzer’s lifelong sorrow, his recognition of life as a series of losses and unfulfilled desires, and his contemplation of the illusory nature of reality all echo Schopenhauerian themes. The passage reflects a worldview where grief and suffering are continuous and intrinsic to life. See also The World as Will and Representation, vol. 2 (1844), chapter 41, “On Death and Its Relation to the Indestructibility of Our Inner Nature.” See Schopenhauer and Suicide in History and Literature.
  • “only his elegiac music”: An elegy is a song or poem of mourning. Mr. Spitzer’s “elegiac music” is music he has written “only for himself or for no mortal ear.” See Music, Sound, and Silence and the Chapter 25 resources.
  • “the heavenly harmonies forever fixed above the floods of time, dark time, those unmoving golden spheres”: “Musica universalis,” the music of the spheres: Boethius, in De institutione musica (c. 500), divided music into musica mundana (the music of the spheres), humana, and instrumentalis. The idea goes back to Pythagoras. The “Musica Universalis” here symbolizes the harmonious and eternal cosmic order that underlies the apparent chaos of mortal life and death. It is depicted as a sublime, transcendent music that governs the movements of the stars and planets, reflecting a divine harmony beyond human perception. Mr. Spitzer, a semi-retired lawyer and composer of “silent music,” embodies this principle as someone who is attuned to the “music of the spheres” or the eternal, silent harmonies that exist beyond earthly sounds. His compositions, unheard by living ears and played only for the dead or in the celestial realm, metaphorically represent this cosmic music. The chapter emphasizes the contrast between earthly, temporal music and this higher, immutable harmony, suggesting that the tangible world is but a fragment or shadow of a greater metaphysical reality. Catherine’s opium dreams expand this theme by blurring the boundaries between the real and the illusory, the living and the dead, reflecting the ineffable nature of the universal music that ties all existence together. The “Musica Universalis” is used to articulate a metaphysical principle that all existence, from the smallest earthly details to the vast cosmos, is bound by a harmonious, eternal music. This music transcends the limitations of physical reality and human understanding, offering a vision of unity amid fragmentation and a cosmic order beneath the apparent chaos of life and death. See Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres, Astronomy, and Music, Sound, and Silence.
  • “He could believe in probably no hell more than this present individual life”: Mephistophilis in Marlowe’s Doctor Faustus (c. 1592): “Why, this is hell, nor am I out of it.”
  • “a state of mind”: Mr. Spitzer’s heaven as “a state of mind,” as in Chapter 25. See the Chapter 25 resources.
  • “united in heavenly mansions”: “In my Father’s house are many mansions” (John 14:2). See The Bible.
  • “by the opium doses, by the needle points”: The hypodermic syringe (Alexander Wood, 1853) brought injected morphine into nineteenth-century medicine and addiction. See The History of Opium.
  • “sleeping car porters with golden roosters on their heads”: Pullman sleeping-car porters were Black men, many of them formerly enslaved, hired by the Pullman Company from 1867 for low wages and long hours, and passengers called every one of them “George.” The Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, led by A. Philip Randolph, was founded in 1925. See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “and headless horsemen”: Washington Irving’s “The Legend of Sleepy Hollow” (1820). See American Writers and Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “he had lost his life, or he had gained his life through death”: “He that findeth his life shall lose it: and he that loseth his life for my sake shall find it” (Matthew 10:39). See The Bible and Suicide in History and Literature.
  • “the great crowds of Derby Day”: The Derby at Epsom, run since 1780. William Powell Frith painted its crowds in “The Derby Day” (1858). See Horses and Carriages and Peron Spitzer.
  • “His brother would not have sat up all night with any corpse”: The wake, the old custom of keeping watch over the dead until burial. See Grief, Loss, and Mourning.
  • “held it against him that he did not rescue her, carry her away as Peron might have done”: Catherine is expecting a man to save her, which is the damsel in distress. This is a narrative device in which one or more men must rescue a woman who has been kidnapped or placed in other peril. The “damsel” is often portrayed as beautiful, popular, and of high social status; she is usually depicted as a princess in works with fantasy or fairy tale settings. Kinship, love, lust or a combination of those motivate the male protagonist to initiate the narrative. Critics have linked the helplessness of these women to societal views that women as a group need to be taken care of by men and treated nicely. Throughout the history of the trope, the role of the woman as the victim in need of a male savior has remained constant, but her attackers have changed to suit the tastes and collective fears of the period: “monsters, mad scientists, Nazis, hippies, bikers, aliens…” (Wikipedia) See Fairy Tales and Folklore and Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “wandering through the intricate streets of a dark city in perhaps the underworld”: Dis, the walled city of Hell in Dante’s Inferno (cantos 8-9). See Dante.
  • “in order to reach the upper world, one must first travel through the lower world”: Mr. Spitzer’s belief that people need to travel through the lower world to reach the upper world mirrors The Pilgrim’s Progress (1678) and/or Dante’s writings. There are the seven terraces of Purgatory with 7 vices. There are 7 virtues. Hell has 9 circles and Heaven has nine spheres: the Moon, Mercury, Venus, Sun, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, the fixed stars, and the primum mobile. According to Dante the cosmos depends on God, who exists in the Empyrean, an immaterial heaven beyond space and time. The Empyrean is made up of God’s love and light, and is home to angels, the blessed, and the souls of the faithful. Interesting note that the woman with the Siamese cats believes she was the reincarnation of Cleopatra and Dante meets Cleopatra in the circle of Hell called Lust (“Cleopatràs lussurïosa,” Inferno 5.63). Virgil wrote “Facilis descensus Averno,” “the descent to Avernus is easy” (Aeneid 6.126), and Heraclitus, “The way up and the way down are one and the same.” See Dante, Heraclitus and Flux, and Greek Mythology.
