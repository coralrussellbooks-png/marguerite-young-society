Chapter Three. Grief Doesn't End: The Earth Took Them Completely

Part of Be Bak in a Whale, on Radical Immanence, the philosophy Coral Russell draws from the work of Marguerite Young (1908 to 1995).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter Two. Reality Is Constructed: Count the Objects · Be Bak in a Whale · Chapter Four. Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act: The Awe Is →

Pillar: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End · Across every book

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

I was a child when I understood what the Earth does with what it takes.

I had found baby rabbits. I took them home because I should not have, because the wanting to care for them was stronger than the knowledge that I shouldn’t, and I tried to keep them alive. Then I had to go to camp. I called my grandmother to come get me rather than stay overnight because I needed to go home and make sure the rabbits lived. I came home and they had died.

I buried them sobbing. I placed a stick on the grave and walked away.

And then I turned around.

I went back to check. Terrified that I had made a mistake. That I had buried them alive and they were still under there and I could still save them if I could just find the place. I dug for the stick. I dug for the hole. I found nothing. The Earth had taken them completely. No marker. No evidence of the grave. No confirmation that what I had buried was truly gone and not still alive underneath waiting for me to find them in time.

The Earth had taken them completely.

I did not know it then but that was the moment I began to understand what Young spends 1,198 pages demonstrating: that grief does not resolve. That the Earth does not give you back the marker. That the turning around is not weakness but the honest response of a self that loved something and cannot simply walk away from the site of the loss, and that the love and the loss and the terror of having made a mistake and the inability to find the evidence and the ground that shows nothing: all of it is permanent. All of it is the weather you live in from that moment forward.

⁂

The third pillar of Young’s philosophy is this: grief is not a stage or a season. It is the permanent atmospheric condition of a world in which everything you love is always already changing into something you didn’t expect.

We have a story we tell about grief. Elisabeth Kübler-Ross gave us the stages and the stages became the normative prescription of denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance. The story of grief is the story of getting through it, of moving toward acceptance which is the destination, the place where grief becomes integrated and the self resumes its forward motion. Grief as temporary weather. Difficult while it lasts, bounded, finite, something that eventually passes.

Young does not believe this.

More precisely: Young does not believe it because it is not true, and she is too honest to reproduce a comfort that falsifies the actual experience it claims to describe. In Young’s system, grief is not temporary weather. It is the climate. The permanent atmospheric condition of a world in which nothing holds still, in which the people you need most are dying or absent or constructed differently than you understood, in which the self that does the loving is itself plural and unstable and not fully available to itself.

The character who carries this pillar most fully is not Vera, though Vera grieves on every page. It is Mr. Spitzer.

⁂

I never quite liked Mr. Spitzer the way I liked Vera and Miss MacIntosh. I want to say that plainly because it matters for what follows. Part of it was the conscious decentering I was doing when I came to Young’s novel of I had made the decision to stop organizing my interior life around men’s perspectives and Mr. Spitzer is a man and I carried that wariness into him.

The second reading changed something. The foreshadowing became visible that I had missed the first time: he is not who he says he is. Catherine knows. Cousin Hannah knows. The women in this novel see through the construction of Mr. Spitzer while he maintains it, and Young builds that dramatic irony carefully across the novel, laying the evidence in plain sight for the reader who is paying full attention.

But here is what Young does that the tradition has no category for: she gives the most breathtaking grief in the novel to the most compromised character.

Mr. Spitzer’s descriptions of the natural world are among the most beautiful passages in the book. His grief over what is lost, what the world has lost, what the natural world loses either through its own processes or through human intervention: it is genuine. It is embodied. It is more fully inhabited than almost anything else Young gives us, and she gives it to a man who is performing his own identity, who is not who he says he is, who the women around him see through.

Young does this deliberately. Grief is not a reward for virtue. It is not given to the characters who have earned it by being honest or good or singular. It is the weather. It falls on everyone. The person who is not who he says he is still loses things genuinely. The loss is real even when the self that carries it is constructed. The grief is among the genuine things even when everything else is performance.

That is the plural self and the grief pillar working together. You can be multiple things simultaneously, some of them false and some of them genuine, and the grief belongs to the genuine register even when the identity containing it is a construction. Mr. Spitzer’s grief over the natural world is real. The man performing the identity of Mr. Spitzer is not entirely real. Young holds both of those truths simultaneously without resolving them into a judgment about which one cancels the other.

⁂

I feel at home outside and in nature. I have always felt this. Camping, hiking, sports, playgrounds, a wild wooded ravine that was my backyard. The natural world has never felt like an escape from the real world. It has always felt like the more real world, the one that was here before the human arrangements and will be here after, the one that operates at a scale and with a patience that the constructed world cannot approach.

Mr. Spitzer gave me language for that feeling before I understood that Young was giving me language for anything. His descriptions of the natural world, the haunting beauty of nature encountered while going about ordinary business, moving through streets, living a circumscribed daily life while something vast and indifferent and gorgeous operates alongside it. I recognized that. I had been living inside that recognition since the baby rabbits. Since the Earth took them completely and left no marker and I stood at the place where the grave had been and understood that the Earth does not grieve what it carries.

It just carries.

That is the distinction Young builds into the novel through the natural world that runs through it constantly, underneath everything, never resolved. The grief in the novel is human. The carrying is geological. Vera grieves. Mr. Spitzer grieves. Catherine grieves in the way that the opium world is itself a form of grief, the interior life built to hold what the exterior world has lost or taken or refused. But the Earth does not grieve. The Earth sediments. It takes the wig and the crab carries it through the water. It takes the rabbits and closes over the grave without a marker. It takes everything that has been lost and carries it forward, transformed but not healed, present but not returned.

Young understood that the human self cannot hold the full weight of permanent grief alone. Individual life lasts decades. The grief she describes accumulates across centuries. The vessel is too small for what it is being asked to carry. But the Earth is not too small. The Earth has been carrying loss since before any human philosophical tradition existed to name what carrying means. The geological record is permanent cumulative grief made literal: layer upon layer, nothing resolved, everything present, the complete record of every loss the world has sustained, sediment by sediment, carried forward without ceremony, without conclusion, without the demand that any of it be processed into something more manageable.

We planted the crab at the end of Chapter One. The crab carries the wig through the water without knowing what it carries. That image is the beginning of this argument. Here is where it becomes explicit: the natural world in Young’s novel is not a backdrop or atmosphere or symbol. It is the original witness. The only archive large enough for what human selves cannot hold.

⁂

Vera Cartwheel is grieving from the first page.

She is grieving Miss MacIntosh, who walked into the water and did not return. She is grieving the childhood organized around a woman who turned out to be a construction. She is grieving Catherine, who is alive but absent in the way that a person absorbed into their own interior world is absent: present in body, unreachable in the ways that matter. She is grieving the self she was before the wig came off, the self that had a foundation even if the foundation was false.

And she cannot stop.

The grief does not process. It does not move through stages toward resolution. It accumulates. Each new understanding of what has been lost adds to rather than replaces the grief that preceded it. Vera at the end of the novel carries everything she has ever grieved, layered and present and unresolved, the way the Earth carries everything in its strata: the old losses and the new losses and the losses that are still in the process of being understood as losses, all of it present simultaneously, none of it gone.

Young presents this not as pathology but as accuracy. As the appropriate response to actual loss in an actual world where loss is permanent and cumulative and does not resolve. The alternative, the grief that resolves, the acceptance that integrates loss into a resumed life, requires at some level deciding that the lost thing was not so irreplaceable after all. Young finds something dishonest in that decision: the lost thing was irreplaceable. Life without it is not the same life. The self that loved it has not found its way forward so much as it has found a way of not looking back.

Not looking back is not the same as arriving somewhere better.

⁂

The turning around.

I keep returning to that gesture because it is the most honest thing I know about grief. You bury what you have lost. You place the marker. You walk away because walking away is what you are supposed to do, what the stage model of grief requires, what the people around you need you to do so that the loss can be categorized and filed and the forward motion resumed.

And then you turn around.

Not because you haven’t accepted the loss. Because the fear underneath grief is not the fear of losing. It is the fear of having made a mistake. Of having buried something alive. Of having walked away from something that still needed you and would have survived if you had stayed. That terror, the terror that you turned your back too soon, is the engine of the turning around, and it is the most honest account of what grief actually feels like from inside it. Not the sadness. The terror that the loss was your fault. That you could have prevented it. That the marker you placed was premature and underneath it something is still waiting.

Young builds this into Vera’s entire journey. The bus is moving toward What Cheer, toward the place Miss MacIntosh came from, and the movement is itself a turning around. Vera cannot stop returning to the loss. She cannot stop looking for the marker, the confirmation, the evidence that what she buried was truly gone and not still alive underneath waiting for her to arrive in time. The journey is the grief. The 1,198 pages are the grief. Not because the novel is depressing but because the turning around is what the honest self does, and Young refuses to abbreviate it into something more manageable than it actually is.

⁂

There is a political dimension to permanent grief that Young builds into the novel without stating explicitly, and it is the dimension that connects the third pillar to the world we are currently living in.

A society that cannot grieve honestly cannot reckon honestly.

The American relationship with its own history is a grief problem before it is anything else. The losses built into the foundation of this country, the theft of indigenous land, the centuries of enslaved labor, the systematic exclusion of women and queer people and immigrants from the promises the country made about itself. These are not losses that have been processed. They have been managed. Periodically acknowledged and returned to the category of the past, the thing that has been gotten over or gotten through, the grief that has supposedly resolved into the acceptance of a nation that has moved forward.

Young’s philosophy says: the grief does not resolve. The losses are permanent and cumulative and the demand that the people most directly descended from those losses perform a recovery they have not experienced is its own violence. The demand for performed recovery is Miss MacIntosh’s threat in a different register: I will kill you if you tell. Keep the wig on. Walk away from the grave and do not turn around. The marker is where we put it and the loss is processed and the forward motion has resumed and if you turn around you are the problem.

The Earth knows better. The Earth has been carrying what the political system demands be called resolved for longer than the political system has existed. The Mahoning Valley carries the grief of deindustrialization in its landscape, in the empty lots and the rusted infrastructure and the particular quality of light in a post-industrial city. It carries the grief of what was taken from the Wyandot people in the land itself, in the river, in the glacial formations that were here before any human arrangement and will be here after all of them. The Earth does not accept the political system’s timeline for grief. It sediments. It carries. It shows nothing where the marker was and that is not erasure. That is the honest account of how long loss actually lasts.

Mr. Spitzer grieves the natural world because the natural world is where the permanent record is kept. His grief over what has been lost to human intervention, the beauty that emerges from that grief, the haunting quality of nature encountered in the middle of ordinary life: Young gives him this because she understands that the grief of the Earth is the scale at which human grief has to be understood if it is to be understood honestly. The individual loss is real and specific and devastating. The Earth carries it alongside everything else it has been given, without hierarchy, without the demand that any of it be resolved before the next one arrives.

⁂

I want to be precise about what Young is not saying.

She is not saying that grief is simply permanent suffering. She is not arguing for despair or for the dissolution of the self into its losses. The third pillar is consistently misread as the novel’s melancholy, its beautiful hopelessness, its maximalist sorrow. That reading mistakes the refusal of false consolation for the refusal of consolation altogether.

What grief gives, in Young’s system, is depth. The self that has not grieved is thinner than the self that has, not morally thinner but experientially thinner, less capacious, less able to hold the full weight of what existence actually contains. Grief is the education that cannot be acquired any other way. It teaches the self the actual scale of what it loves, which is always larger than the self knew before the loss.

I understood this with the rabbits before I had language for it. The grief was proportional to the love, which was larger than I knew I had until the loss measured it. I didn’t know how much I loved those rabbits until I was sobbing at the grave. I didn’t know how much I needed the evidence of the grave until I turned around and went looking for it. The turning around was not a failure to accept the loss. It was the measurement of how real the loss was.

And then the Earth took the marker too, and I stood at the unmarked ground and understood something that I would not have language for until decades later when I read Young’s novel for the first time and drowned and read it again and found Mr. Spitzer and his grief over the natural world and his descriptions of the haunting beauty of what is being lost and understood that the beauty and the grief were the same thing.

You only see the haunting beauty of what is being lost when you have stopped pretending the loss will resolve. When you have turned around enough times to understand that the marker will not be there, that the Earth has taken it, that the carrying is the Earth’s work now and not yours, and that your work is to remain present to what is being lost while it is still being lost, which is always, which is the condition, which is the climate you live in if you are paying honest attention.

⁂

This memory is also what made me empathize with Esther.

I have been thinking about why for a long time and here is what I understand now, the child who sobbed at the grave and turned around to find nothing grew into the reader who recognized Esther as the person who keeps going back in. Who writes the note as best she can. Who cannot spell either, which means she cannot perform the acceptable version of the gesture, can only make the honest version, the one that shows the spelling and the limitation and the going-in-anyway simultaneously.

Esther’s note is the grief pillar compressed into a sign in a restaurant window. It does not resolve. It does not perform recovery. It acknowledges the going out and promises the coming back and admits the whale, which is Jonah’s whale, which is the creature that swallows you and keeps you alive in the dark and eventually returns you, transformed but not healed, to the world.

The whale does not resolve the grief of having been swallowed. It carries it. The way the Earth carried the rabbits. The way the Earth carries everything: without ceremony, without conclusion, without the demand that the carried thing be processed into something more manageable than what it actually is.

Young ends the novel with Esther because Esther is the most honest answer to the grief pillar available in the book. Not the most eloquent. Not the most philosophical. The most honest. The person who cannot spell, who writes the note as best she can, who goes back into the whale not because the whale is where she wants to be but because the whale is where she is and the honest life is the one that acknowledges where it is.

The grief is the climate.

The whale is the Earth that carries it.

The note is what you write when you have stopped pretending either of those things is temporary.

Bee bak in a whale.

For she could not spell either.

Neither could the child at the unmarked grave. Neither can any of us. We write the note as best we can and we go back in and the Earth takes the marker and we turn around anyway and the turning around is the honest life and Young spent 1,198 pages proving it.
