Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 59

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Forlorn musicians fill the street at the start of this chapter, and Mr. Spitzer's alms to them open its pillar: the self as borrowed flesh, a body worn rather than owned. His indiscriminate generosity, and the caution he learns because a beggar may be a king and a king a beggar, is the instability of social status the book keeps exposing and the negotiation with appearance that pins the marginalized, the surface hiding whatever it likes underneath. From there the chapter turns metaphysical, and Mr. Spitzer's alienation from his own body, his sense of being "another" or "not himself," is the constructed, performed self, the masks and cloaks and borrowed flesh that make identity a costume the way gender is a costume.

His fear of losing identity, of thresholds, of silence, is the fear of erasure the book knows women by, and the sound and music that are "ephemeral" and easily lost are the silencing of the unheard voice, silence held as both a loss and a site of power. Mr. Spitzer as a "psychological beggar" and "clown" is the reductive role the book keeps forcing on the marginalized, the "madwoman" or "fool" a person is flattened into, and the deceptive appearances and the "revolving door" are the cyclical entrapment and the charitable paternalism the book meets with suspicion, aid dispensed without touching the system that made the need.

Death and transformation dissolve the chapter's boundaries. Mr. Spitzer as a "cloud" or "shadow," alienated from his own skeleton, is the body as the place the self comes apart, and the "temporary tenant" who does not own the flesh he lives in is the objectified body the book keeps taking from its women and handing, here, to a man, a self renting a skeleton that is not his. The "unholy trinity" of body and soul and spirit as mutable, the fear of losing identity, the thought of having "begotten himself" without human love, is plural selfhood reaching toward the interrupted birth, a creation with no parent and no consummation.

The chapter ends on his admission that he is "already dead," caught in equivocation and ambiguous existence, and the liminal state between life and death is invisibility and erasure worked to its limit on the witness, a man neither present nor absent who names his own condition and cannot resolve it. Though the chapter follows a man, its whole apparatus, the borrowed flesh, the invaded body, the silence that is both erasure and refuge, the self as costume, is the equipment the book built for its women, and Mr. Spitzer inhabits it the way the book means everyone finally to inhabit it, dispossessed of any single, owned self.
