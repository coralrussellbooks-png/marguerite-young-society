To All My Darlings

Chapter 63

On Chapter 63 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 10, 2026

← Chapter 62 · Home · Chapter 64 →

Summary

At breakfast the morning after her dream, Vera tries to see Miss MacIntosh as she always has: the Rock of Ages, the plain, red-haired nursemaid of common sense who scolds the tide, hates the Emperor Constantine for changing the calendar, and rails against Benjamin Franklin and the electric lights that have robbed the poor of the dark. But Vera has seen her bald in the night. She strikes the table and demands the truth: Miss MacIntosh wears a wig and has neither eyebrows nor eyelashes. Miss MacIntosh answers in grief and wild humor, by way of Canute and Constantine, Jonas Hanway and the umbrella that made every man his own coachman, and the brothers who called her George, and at last she lifts off her red wig. She is bald, her left breast is gone, and she was broke when she first came, which is why they saved the electric lights. Vera promises always to love her. Miss MacIntosh tells her to eat her breakfast, that people are never quite what they seem, that nobody knows anybody, and that she suffers especially when there are rainbows.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, Miss MacIntosh is described as a figure of unwavering common sense and moral clarity amid chaos and illusion. She is portrayed as the “Rock of Ages in a land where all things crumbled,” the “surefooted Pilgrim,” and the “one person never dreaming where all might dream.” This passage highlights her steadfastness and practical nature, contrasting with the fog and illusions surrounding them.
  • A substantial and vivid passage details Miss MacIntosh’s intense dislike for electric lights, reflecting her preference for natural darkness and simplicity. She associates electric lights with loss of privacy, artificiality, and moral decay. This passage also reflects her nostalgia and resistance to the encroachment of modern technology and the disruption of natural rhythms.
  • A critical dialogue occurs between Vera and Miss MacIntosh, where Vera confronts her about the “hollow pretense” of their living situation and demands honesty about the nature of their relationship. Vera accuses Miss MacIntosh of deception and questions whether they are truly bound to each other, expressing a desire to understand what she wants and lacks. This exchange is charged with emotional intensity and existential questioning.
  • One of the most poignant moments is when Miss MacIntosh removes her wig, revealing her baldness, which she describes as a source of deep personal embarrassment and symbol of her vulnerability and the “burned wig” shielding her from life’s harsh elements. This act strips away illusion and forces a confrontation with bare reality, emphasizing themes of identity, appearance versus essence, and mortality.
  • Miss MacIntosh offers a bleak yet humorous meditation on life’s disappointments, the inevitability of death, and the “system of mutual deception” that love often entails. She speaks with a mixture of bitterness and dark humor about her past, the failures of God and creation, and the futility of illusions.
  • Miss MacIntosh discusses the umbrella as a symbol of equality and protection, tracing its history from the Orient to England and its social implications. She also mentions the raincoat as a practical item for the poor, underscoring her concern for everyday realities and the working classes. This passage blends historical anecdotes with social commentary.
  • Toward the end of the chapter, despite all the darkness and despair, Miss MacIntosh exhorts Vera to “be of good cheer,” acknowledging that “we are pilgrims” with a long day ahead. She urges living each day as if it were the last, knitting body and soul together even as they unravel. This passage encapsulates the chapter’s tension between despair and endurance, illusion and reality.
  • In the final lines, Miss MacIntosh offers a sober reflection that “people are never quite what they seem” and “nobody knows anybody,” underscoring the chapter’s themes of uncertainty, deception, and the limits of human understanding.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • Vera calls Miss MacIntosh “the Rock of Ages in a land where all things crumbled.” How does the opening portrait prepare for its own undoing?
  • “I would have loved the imperfection because I could not love perfection.” What does Vera discover about love?
  • Miss MacIntosh says love “is a system of mutual deception.” Is she bitter, or honest?

Characters

  • “Most especially, for reasons never quite known, the Emperor Constantine was anathema to her.” Why might Miss MacIntosh hate the emperor who made Christianity the empire’s faith?
  • “My elder brothers called me George.” What does the chapter suggest about how Miss MacIntosh has lived between the roles given to women and to men?
  • Vera says, “I will always love you, and I will never part from you.” Miss MacIntosh answers, “Though I be parted from myself.” What is she telling Vera?

Connections

  • Vera is still thinking of “Miss MacIntosh’s marriage to Mr. Spitzer in that vasty deep.” How does the Spitzer dream carry into this breakfast?
  • Miss MacIntosh hides “the missing breast which gave her the pain over which she long ago had triumphed simply by ignoring the pain.” How does her body tell a history the novel has kept hidden?
  • “I was broke, and that was why we saved the electric lights.” How does this line change the rant against electric lights that came before?

Reflection

  • “Nobody knows anybody.” Is it true?
  • The umbrella, Miss MacIntosh says, “did more, over night, to bring equality than half a dozen revolutions.” Can an ordinary object change a society?
  • Vera says, “We all have to assume a great many things are true when they are not true.” What do people live by that they know is not true?

Research: Characters

Miss MacIntosh

  • Miss MacIntosh is described as the “Rock of Ages” in a crumbling world, a symbol of stability, clarity, and common sense.
  • She embodies straightforwardness, rejecting illusions and fantasies, and insists on living within a “framework” of reality.
  • Her physical appearance symbolizes her internal struggle: her ruddy hair like a “sunset burning with bees,” a broken nose, and eventually revealed baldness, all reflecting her life’s hardships and resilience.
  • The black umbrella she carries indoors and out is, in Young’s words, “a kind of canopy between her and reality. You don’t get too close to her” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). It “always casts a shadow around her and forbids intimacy” (Marguerite Young, interview, WNYC radio, New York).
  • Miss MacIntosh is a moral mentor and nursemaid figure who values practicality over imagination.
  • She is skeptical of dreams, illusions, and modern distractions, such as electric lights, which she believes disturb natural life rhythms and privacy.
  • She is combative and sharp, not hesitating to scold even divine figures like Emperor Constantine or God Himself for what she sees as excess or failure.
  • Despite her stern exterior, she has a deep, incorruptible sense of humor and reveals moments of vulnerability, especially when discussing her baldness and personal losses.

Vera Cartwheel

  • Vera narrates. Having seen Miss MacIntosh bald in the night, she wakes still half in the dream and for once rebels, striking the table so the cups jump and demanding the truth.
  • Her cruelty turns at once to tenderness: once the wig is off she promises, “I will always love you, and I will never part from you,” and she feels she will suffer all her life “the blows of conscience.”

Research: Themes

Reality versus Illusion

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Miss MacIntosh represents a rejection of illusions, emphasizing the importance of common sense and “just what was immediate and near.”
  • Vera struggles with this perspective, feeling trapped between dreams and the harshness of reality.
  • The fog surrounding them symbolizes ambiguity, confusion, and the shifting nature of perception.
  • The electric lights symbolize modern artificiality, disturbing natural darkness and the “dreamless sleep” necessary for authentic living.

Mortality and the Human Condition

See: Death and Mortality

  • The chapter grapples with death, impermanence, and the fragility of life. Miss MacIntosh openly discusses her own mortality, symbolized by her baldness and physical deterioration.
  • She speaks candidly about the “burned-out wick” and the “empty breast,” metaphors for loss and the fading of vitality.
  • The idea that “life is very short” and “the spirit dwells not long in any place” underscores a sense of existential urgency.
  • Vera’s confrontation with Miss MacIntosh about their relationship and existence reflects a deeper search for meaning and honesty in the face of death.

Isolation and Connection

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Despite Miss MacIntosh’s role as a caretaker, there is a profound emotional distance and complexity in their relationship.
  • Vera demands honesty and openness, revealing a desire for genuine connection beyond social roles and appearances.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s admission of her past struggles and loneliness highlights the theme of isolation in human experience.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

The Wig and Baldness

See: Hair, Wig, and Baldness · Identity and the Self · Women, Gender, and Marriage · Marguerite Young on Her Craft

  • Miss MacIntosh’s wig symbolizes the facade of social norms and appearances, which she ultimately reveals as a protective but deceptive covering. Young described the wig as the illusion itself, “the dream by which she lived” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965), and the hair it replaces as “the symbol of the dream and of the feminine in life that she never had” (Marguerite Young, interview with Duncan MacDonald, Observation Point, 1965).
  • Her baldness, exposed in a moment of vulnerability, represents raw truth, mortality, and the stripping away of illusions.

The Umbrella

See: The Umbrella

  • The umbrella protects her “sand-colored skin.” Being hairless, Miss MacIntosh, Young said, “is very exposed to colors, to lights, which burn her skin. That’s why she always carried, even on sunny days, that black umbrella” (Marguerite Young, interview, WNYC radio, New York). It also keeps others at a distance: “You don’t get too close to her” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).
  • It also symbolizes autonomy and control, as she “scolded a wave” or would defend herself against phantoms.

Electric Lights versus Natural Darkness

See: Lamps, Candles, and Light

  • Electric lights symbolize modernity, artificiality, and the loss of privacy and natural rhythms.
  • Miss MacIntosh longs for the natural darkness, the “night of six months,” and the simple light of the moon or candle, reflecting a desire for authenticity and peace.

The Fog

See: Fog and Mist

  • The fog is a recurring image symbolizing ambiguity, uncertainty, and the elusive nature of truth and identity.
  • Miss MacIntosh insists that “never would she yield to the fog,” asserting her steadfastness and clarity.

Research: Resources

  • “as the Rock of Ages in a land where all things crumbled”: Rock of Ages is a Christian metaphor for God or Jesus, representing stability, strength, and a place of refuge from sin and the storms of life. The phrase originates from the Bible and is famously used in the hymn “Rock of Ages,” by Augustus Toplady in 1775.
    • In the King James Bible the margin of Isaiah 26:4 gives “rock of ages” for “everlasting strength.” Toplady’s hymn begins, “Rock of Ages, cleft for me, / Let me hide myself in thee.” See The Bible.
  • “as the surefooted Pilgrim”: A person on a spiritual journey who remains firm and resolute in their beliefs despite hardships or adversity. This interpretation aligns with the idea of a pilgrim drawing on faith to overcome challenges and continue on their path.
    • Later Vera calls their life “a Pilgrim’s Progress from nowhere to nowhere,” after John Bunyan’s allegory The Pilgrim’s Progress (1678).
  • “the simulacrum, the graven image of truth”: Simulacrum is a representation, imitation, or copy of a person or thing. In a postmodern context, it refers to a copy without an original, or a copy that has become detached from reality. (Jean Baudrillard’s Simulacra and Simulation, 1981, is after 1964.)
    • “Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image” (Exodus 20:4, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “She scolded a wave, a drifting sail, an old board brought in by the tide”: Compare William F. Buckley Jr. in 1955: “A conservative is someone who stands athwart history, yelling Stop, at a time when no one is inclined to do so, or to have much patience with those who so urge it.” See King Canute below.
  • “the Emperor Constantine who had changed the calendar”: Emperor Constantine or Constantine the Great ruled Rome and was the first Roman emperor to convert to Christianity. Miss MacIntosh frequently expresses her disapproval of Emperor Constantine because he changed the calendar and converted to Christianity, which she views as a corruption of truth and nature. Constantine is historically known for legalizing Christianity and changing the Roman calendar, including shifting the day of rest to Sunday, the first day of the week. Miss MacIntosh’s disdain for him symbolizes her rejection of these imposed changes to natural cycles and traditional ways of life. She objects to Constantine’s alteration of the calendar, which she sees as confusing and disrupting the natural order established by God. She emphasizes the importance of simple, earnest living connected to nature and common sense, contrasting this with the artificial imposition of religious and societal structures that Constantine represents. Her scolding of him is thus a symbolic condemnation of how Christianity, as institutionalized by Constantine, had altered timekeeping, worship practices, and human experience, creating confusion and distancing people from an authentic connection to the rhythms of life and nature. See On Emperor Constantine. See Augustine and Christian Theology.
    • In 321 Constantine ordered that “the venerable day of the Sun” be a day of rest for city courts and workshops, the first Sunday law.
  • “her old silver-dollar watch”: Compare the Ingersoll “dollar watch” (1896), sold for a dollar as “the watch that made the dollar famous.”
  • “It would be impractical for the infernal spirits to conceive their own damnation”: “The mind is its own place, and in itself / Can make a Heav’n of Hell, a Hell of Heav’n” (John Milton, Paradise Lost, 1.254-255, 1667). See Milton.
  • “the fog that wrapped around us like the principle of ambiguity itself”: The philosophy of ambiguity is not just a theme but a structural and stylistic principle that permeates every level of the novel. Characters defy easy categorization and are often contradictory. Mrs. Hogden embodies both vulgarity and a strange, disconcerting serenity. She seems both real and unreal, present and absent, and Vera struggles to grasp her true nature. Even Miss MacIntosh, who initially appears as a figure of steadfast common sense, is revealed to have hidden depths and a past that complicate her seemingly simple persona. This challenges the reader’s ability to form definitive judgments about these characters. Characters are not fixed entities but rather fluid and shifting identities, reflecting the novel’s broader exploration of the instability of the self. Mr. Spitzer is perhaps the prime example, constantly questioning his identity and blurring the lines between himself and his deceased twin brother. Vera also undergoes a transformation, as she struggles to reconcile her childhood perceptions with the complexities of the adult world. Vera’s narration is filtered through her subjective consciousness, which is itself prone to hallucination, memory distortions, and dreamlike states. This makes it difficult to discern objective truth from Vera’s personal interpretations, further contributing to the novel’s ambiguity. The narrative voice itself is constantly shifting, blurring the boundaries between narrator and character. The novel is steeped in a dreamlike atmosphere, where the boundaries between reality and illusion become increasingly blurred. Events unfold in a non-linear fashion, and the narrative is filled with surreal imagery and symbolic motifs that resist easy interpretation. This dreamlike quality reflects the novel’s exploration of the unconscious mind and the subjective nature of experience. Young employs a rich tapestry of symbolism and metaphor that invites multiple interpretations. Objects, places, and events often carry symbolic weight, but their meanings remain open to interpretation. For example, the sea, a recurring motif in the novel, can represent both the source of life and the threat of destruction, reflecting the inherent ambiguity of nature. The novel’s cyclical structure reinforces its themes of ambiguity and uncertainty. Events and motifs recur throughout the narrative, but with slight variations, suggesting that history repeats itself but never in the same way. This cyclical structure also creates a sense of timelessness, blurring the boundaries between past, present, and future. The novel ultimately rejects the notion of definitive truths or easy answers. The novel suggests that reality is inherently ambiguous and that our understanding of the world is always incomplete and provisional. By embracing ambiguity, Young challenges the reader to question their own assumptions and to accept the inherent uncertainty of human experience. The novel’s exploration of ambiguity is not merely a stylistic choice but a reflection of Young’s philosophical vision. She suggests that embracing ambiguity is essential for understanding the complexities of human existence and for appreciating the richness and diversity of experience. See Fog and Mist and Illusion and Reality.
  • “Because of Benjamin Franklin who should have known better”: Miss MacIntosh views Benjamin Franklin as a well-intentioned but ultimately disruptive figure whose innovations, especially electric lighting, have intruded on natural life, privacy, and morality, reflecting her deep-seated preference for simplicity, natural order, and skepticism toward modernity.
    • Franklin flew his kite into a thunderstorm in 1752 to show that lightning is electricity, and invented the lightning rod.
  • “there were the Elevated above us and the Subway beneath us”: “Elevated” in U.S. transportation refers to elevated railways (or “els”), which are train systems built on steel, concrete, or brick structures high above city streets to avoid surface traffic, with Chicago’s iconic “L” (short for “elevated”) being the most famous example, alongside systems in NYC (largely removed), Philadelphia, and parts of others like Boston and LA, providing crucial, congestion-free urban transit.
    • “Subway” is an urban, high-capacity, electric railway system, often underground (hence the name), moving large numbers of people; it’s also called metro, rapid transit, or sometimes “the L” (elevated) or “the Tube,” operating on exclusive tracks separate from roads, with major examples in NYC, Chicago, DC, and Boston. Boston opened America’s first subway, under Tremont Street, in 1897.
    • “Grand Central Station” is a commuter rail terminal at 42nd Street and Park Avenue in Midtown Manhattan, New York City. Grand Central is the southern terminus of the Metro-North Railroad’s Harlem, Hudson and New Haven Lines, serving the northern parts of the New York metropolitan area. The present Grand Central Terminal opened in 1913.
  • “God Who brings the blackness of the day”: “Wilt thou break a leaf driven to and fro?” (Job 13:25, KJV); “and maketh the day dark with night” (Amos 5:8, KJV). See The Book of Job.
  • “she preferring either the light of the Apocalypse or of Genesis”: “And God said, Let there be light: and there was light” (Genesis 1:3, KJV); “And the city had no need of the sun, neither of the moon, to shine in it: for the glory of God did lighten it” (Revelation 21:23, KJV). See Genesis and Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “she would tell Thomas Edison”: Thomas Edison was an American inventor, businessman, and manufacturer who is considered one of history’s greatest inventors. He was responsible for more inventions than any other inventor, acquiring 1,093 patents in his lifetime. Edison’s inventions, including the light bulb, phonograph, and alkaline storage batteries, revolutionized the world and had a profound impact on everyday life. His first lasting carbon-filament bulb burned in 1879.
    • “War of the Currents” was a bitter commercial and technical battle in the late 1880s and early 1890s between the direct current (DC) system championed by Thomas Edison and the alternating current (AC) system promoted by George Westinghouse and Nikola Tesla. Edison launched a public misinformation campaign, including publicly electrocuting animals with AC, to portray it as dangerous and inferior to protect his DC patents and market share. This was not a public revolution against all electricity, but rather a corporate and technological battle that fueled public anxiety.
  • “Death came like a thief in the night”: “For yourselves know perfectly that the day of the Lord so cometh as a thief in the night” (1 Thessalonians 5:2, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “the electric lights are a monopoly”: Electric utilities are often considered natural monopolies due to massive infrastructure costs (power plants, grid), making it inefficient to have competing lines to every home, leading to government-regulated monopolies for distribution, though generation and retail markets have seen increasing competition, challenging the traditional model. While the infrastructure (wires) remains largely monopolistic, the industry structure is evolving, with debates around breaking up these monopolies for better consumer benefits.
    • Stockholders (or shareholders) are individuals or entities that own shares, representing partial ownership, in a corporation, gaining rights like voting and dividends but limited liability for company debts. They profit from company success through stock appreciation and dividends, while facing risks like investment loss if the company performs poorly. Common stockholders vote on directors, while preferred stockholders get dividends first, and their influence varies by ownership percentage. Criticisms of stockholders often center on shareholder primacy, arguing it fuels short-termism, excessive executive pay, increased inequality (benefiting the wealthy), and can lead companies to neglect other stakeholders (employees, environment, community) for profit, creating agency problems where managers serve themselves or short-term gains rather than long-term value.
  • “We will return to bayberry candles”: New England colonists boiled the waxy berries of the bayberry shrub to make fragrant green candles that burned without smoking. See Lamps, Candles, and Light.
  • “even Eskimos in igloos”: “Eskimo” is a broad, controversial term for the Indigenous peoples of the Arctic (Inuit, Yupik, Iñupiat, Kalaallit) across Greenland, Canada, Alaska, and Siberia, but it’s often considered offensive, with most preferring their specific names meaning “real people,” like Inuit or Yupik. These cultures traditionally thrive in harsh Arctic environments through hunting and fishing, sharing ancient roots from Asia and unique adaptations to the cold, though many now live in modern settlements and practice various faiths.
  • “poor Polynesians in boats”: Polynesians are indigenous peoples from the vast “Polynesian Triangle” of the Pacific (Hawaii, New Zealand, Easter Island), known for incredible ocean voyaging, unique double-hulled canoes, and sophisticated star/swell navigation, originating from Austronesian seafarers who settled the islands over 3,000 years ago, developing shared languages, cultures, and traditions, with major groups including Maori, Samoans, Hawaiians, Tongans, and Tahitians. They are celebrated for their epic migrations, rich oral traditions, art (like Moai statues), and deep connection to the sea, though colonial impacts have affected their traditional ways.
  • “Electric lights, indeed, and great X-rays!”: X-rays began in 1895 with German physicist Wilhelm Conrad Rontgen’s accidental discovery of a mysterious, invisible radiation while experimenting with cathode rays, which he named “X-rays” due to their unknown nature. This breakthrough quickly revolutionized medicine, allowing doctors to see inside the body for the first time without surgery, leading to rapid adoption for diagnosing fractures, though the dangers of radiation weren’t understood early on. Over time, safety measures, digital imaging, and advanced techniques like CT scans evolved, building on this foundational discovery to create modern radiology.
  • “There were all these bright lights of Broadway”: Broadway’s history began with Dutch settlement, named for its width, evolving from a trade route to New York’s entertainment heart, moving uptown with the city, embracing vaudeville, and exploding into a musical powerhouse during its Golden Age (1940s-60s) with Rodgers & Hammerstein, then revitalizing in the 1990s with mega-musicals and Disney, cementing its global fame for polished, diverse productions today, notes Dance Informa Magazine. (The 1990s revival is after 1964.) Broadway’s electric signs made it the Great White Way by about 1902.
  • “all these bright lights of Skid Row”: Skid Row originated from Seattle’s logging days as “skid roads” where logs were dragged (skidded) down hills to mills, evolving into a term for impoverished urban areas where transient, often unemployed men (“down-and-outs”) congregated due to cheap lodging, cheap liquor, and missions, especially during the Great Depression, with Los Angeles’s Skid Row becoming famous for its massive homeless population, cheap hotels, and social services. The history reflects shifts from resource extraction to urban poverty, the impact of economic crises, and evolving policies towards the homeless, becoming synonymous with large concentrations of unhoused people, addiction, and mental health challenges.
  • “the old naphtha flares of the old lamp-lighters”: Naphtha is a flammable liquid hydrocarbon mixture, a key intermediate in petroleum refining, used widely as a solvent, cleaner, and feedstock for gasoline, jet fuel, and petrochemicals, derived from crude oil or natural gas, varying from colorless to reddish-brown with a gasoline-like odor, and known for its volatility and potential health risks like irritation and CNS effects with overexposure. See Lamps, Candles, and Light.
  • “this, the missing breast which gave her the pain”: The Edwin Smith Papyrus, an Egyptian surgical text of about 1600 BC copying older sources, describes bulging tumors of the breast and says there is no treatment. See Ancient Egypt and Women, Gender, and Marriage.
    • In 1804 a Japanese surgeon is recorded as having performed a mastectomy. The surgeon was Hanaoka Seishū, who operated under a general anesthetic of his own making.
    • In 1811 the novelist Frances Burney had a mastectomy in Paris without anesthesia and wrote her sister the most famous account of it (1812). See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
    • In 1882, William Halsted performed the first modern mastectomy. His radical mastectomy, which also took the chest muscles, stayed standard for most of the next century and left many women disfigured and disabled.
  • “Diogenes, in broad daylight, had carried a lantern”: Diogenes of Sinope, also known as Diogenes the Cynic, was a controversial ancient Greek philosopher and founder of the Cynic school of thought. He lived a life of asceticism, rejecting societal norms and material possessions in favor of nature and simple pleasures. Diogenes was known for his provocative actions and sharp wit, which aimed to expose hypocrisy and greed in society and intellectual circles. The story of the lantern comes from Diogenes Laërtius, Lives of the Philosophers (third century). See Lamps, Candles, and Light.
  • “perhaps a man had married a man, and perhaps a woman had married a woman”: Compare the “Boston marriage,” the nineteenth-century name for two women who made a home together, as in Henry James’s The Bostonians (1886); and the life of Murray Hall, a New York Tammany politician who lived as a man for decades, married twice, and was found at death in 1901 to have been assigned female at birth. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “certainly the Franklin stove”: The Franklin Stove, invented by Benjamin Franklin in 1742, was a revolutionary cast-iron heating device designed to be a more efficient, less smoky alternative to open fireplaces, using less wood to provide more heat by circulating air and radiating warmth in all directions, functioning as an early furnace for its era’s climate crisis and wood shortage. It worked by drawing smoke and heat through a hollow baffle and an “inverted siphon” to extract more warmth before exiting, though it was sometimes difficult to draft and required less wood for better comfort.
  • “he had opposed the domination of George III”: George III was King of Great Britain and Ireland from 25 October 1760 until his death in 1820. The Acts of Union 1800 unified Great Britain and Ireland into the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland, with George as its king. George III is often accused of obstinately trying to keep Great Britain at war with the rebels (American Revolution), despite the opinions of his own ministers.
  • “Poor Richard’s Almanack”: Poor Richard’s Almanack was a popular annual publication by Benjamin Franklin (under the pseudonym “Richard Saunders”) from 1732 to 1758, known for its calendar, weather forecasts, and witty proverbs promoting thrift, industry, and self-reliance, which became a cornerstone of American culture and values. It was a bestseller in the American colonies, selling around 10,000 copies yearly, and served as a vehicle for Franklin’s practical wisdom for the common people.
    • “Early to bed and early to rise, makes a man healthy, wealthy and wise” (Poor Richard, 1735).
  • “he had also tampered with God’s nest egg”: Benjamin Franklin “tampered with God’s nest egg and most private creation keeping us up all day and half the night” metaphorically refers to Franklin’s experiments with electricity, which Miss MacIntosh blames for electric lighting. Miss MacIntosh expresses a strong aversion to electric lights, which she sees as an unnatural and disruptive force in human life.
  • “the carriage and the four white horses and the black coachman”: See The Black Coachman.
  • “No old lamp-lighters wanted”: These jobs have evolved or declined significantly due to technological advancements, mass production, changes in consumer habits, and modern logistics.
    • Ship chandlers are specialized suppliers for the maritime industry, providing everything from food (provisions) and cleaning supplies to technical equipment, safety gear, and deck stores (ropes, anchors) for ships and their crews, acting as crucial logistical partners to keep vessels operational and crews supplied globally.
    • An ironmonger (British English) is a retailer or shop that sells hardware, tools, household items, and metal goods, like hinges, locks, pans, and garden tools, essentially a traditional hardware dealer in American English.
    • Many umbrella repairmen, or “mush-fakers,” were mobile, going from house to house or setting up on streets to mend umbrellas. They would often make one good umbrella out of parts scavenged from several broken ones. A popular song in 1938, “The Umbrella Man” recorded by the Kay Kyser Orchestra, highlighted this common occupation. Frankford Umbrellas started in 1898 in the U.S. as a door-to-door repair service and grew into a manufacturing and repair company in Philadelphia. They repaired various types of umbrellas, including patio and beach umbrellas as well as rain umbrellas. Beehler Umbrella Factory was located in Baltimore, Maryland, this was the first umbrella factory in the U.S., founded in 1828. Baltimore became a hub for umbrella manufacturing, shipping over 1.5 million umbrellas annually by the early 20th century. Uncle Sam Umbrella Shop was a New York City staple, founded in 1866, provided sales and repairs for decades, serving notable figures like Charlie Chaplin and President Franklin D. Roosevelt. Umbrella repair was a necessary trade when umbrellas were durable, often made with quality materials like whalebone (early on) and later steel ribs, designed to last and be repaired. The “throwaway society” eventually led to the decline of the repair business, as mass-produced, inexpensive umbrellas made it easier for people to buy a new one rather than fix an old one. (Frankford Umbrellas is a business still running after 1964.)
    • Tin roofers specialized in installing tin-plated iron/steel sheets, a popular, affordable, fire-resistant material that rose in the 19th century with the Industrial Revolution, evolving from imported tinplate to domestically produced, corrugated, and galvanized sheets (tin over iron/steel) for homes, barns, and factories, offering better durability and ease of installation than older methods, though often called “tin” roofs, they were actually steel coated in tin.

The shield bearing a coat of arms.
Eunuch
A man who has been castrated. Eunuchs were employed for centuries to guard harems and to serve as officials in royal courts in the Middle East, China, and Byzantium. See The eunuch (Ch 35).
Faugh
An exclamation of disgust.
Fly-specked
Spotted with the tiny marks left by flies.
Gannet
A large white seabird that dives for fish.
Glow-worm
A beetle whose wingless female and larvae give off a greenish light.
Hackney
A carriage for hire.
Harebell
A wildflower with nodding blue bell-shaped flowers.
Hieroglyph
A picture-sign of a writing system, as in ancient Egypt.
Hieroglyphics
Picture writing, as of ancient Egypt; any writing hard to read.
Hoar-frost
White frost.
Homily
A sermon; a moral saying.
Ice floe
A sheet of floating ice.
Igloo
An Inuit house built of snow blocks.
Imploring
Begging earnestly.
Imponderable
Something that cannot be measured, weighed, or judged.
Impoverished
Made poor.
Inclemency
Harshness, especially of weather.
Incongruous
Out of place; not in keeping with its surroundings.
Incorruptible
Not subject to decay or to bribery.
Intestate
Having made no will.
Ironmonger
A dealer in hardware.
Livid
Bluish-gray, the color of a bruise. Also furiously angry.
Lodestar
A guiding star, especially the polestar.
Lugubrious
Mournful; gloomy.
Mackerel sky
A sky of rippled clouds like the markings of a mackerel.
Mackintosh
A raincoat of rubberized cloth, named for its inventor, Charles Macintosh.
Maelstrom
A powerful whirlpool; any violent, confused situation.
Mauve
A pale purple.
Modicum
A small amount.
Mollusk
A soft-bodied creature, such as a snail or clam, usually with a shell.
Mongoose
A small, quick animal of Asia and Africa that kills snakes.
Monsoon
A seasonal wind in South Asia that brings months of heavy rain.
Nether
Lower; under.
Oblivion
The state of being forgotten, or of being unaware of everything.
Pagoda
An Asian tiered tower with multiple eaves, usually a Buddhist temple or shrine.
Pall
A cloth spread over a coffin; anything dark that covers.
Phosphorescent
Glowing faintly in the dark.
Pinnacle
The highest point; a tall, pointed mountain peak or the slender spire on a building.
Prism
A clear piece of glass with flat sides that splits light into the colors of the rainbow.
Ramification
A branching consequence.
Raucous
Harsh and loud.
Renunciation
The act of giving something up.
Retrospect
A looking back on the past.
Scrutiny
Close, searching examination.
Seer
A person who sees the future; a prophet.
Shades
Ghosts; the spirits of the dead.
Simulacrum
An image or likeness; a mere imitation.
Spar
A pole used as a ship’s mast, boom, or yard. Spar is also the name for certain shiny, easily split crystalline minerals, such as Iceland spar.
Sporadic
Occurring now and then.
Taffeta
A crisp, shiny silk.
Tallow
Animal fat used for candles and soap. Tallow-colored is a dull, yellowish white.
Tentative
Hesitant; not certain.
Terra firma
Solid ground.
Transient
Passing quickly; not lasting. As a noun, a hotel guest who stays only a short time.
Turgid
Swollen; also, pompous in style.
Turtleneck
A high, close, turned-over collar on a sweater.
Twaddle
Silly talk.
Unassuming
Modest.
Vagaries
Unpredictable changes or whims.
Vagary
A whim; an odd notion.
Virginal
A small, legless harpsichord set on a table, popular in sixteenth- and seventeenth-century England.
Vizor
An old spelling of visor, the movable front of a helmet.
Waterproof
An old word for a raincoat, as in Miss MacIntosh’s “plaid, faded waterproof.”
Weathercock
A weathervane shaped like a rooster; also, a person who changes with every wind.
Whaler
A whaling ship or a man who hunts whales.
