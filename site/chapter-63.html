Summary

The novel returns to Vera and Miss MacIntosh. Vera confronts her about the hollow pretense between them. Miss MacIntosh takes off her wig and speaks more openly than ever about her past, her body, her loneliness, and her coming end. She tells Vera that "nobody knows anybody," and that she suffers especially when there are rainbows.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, Miss MacIntosh is described as a figure of unwavering common sense and moral clarity amid chaos and illusion. She is portrayed as the “Rock of Ages in a land where all things crumbled,” the “surefooted Pilgrim,” and the “one person never dreaming where all might dream.” This passage highlights her steadfastness and practical nature, contrasting with the fog and illusions surrounding them.
  • A substantial and vivid passage details Miss MacIntosh’s intense dislike for electric lights, reflecting her preference for natural darkness and simplicity. She associates electric lights with loss of privacy, artificiality, and moral decay. This passage also reflects her nostalgia and resistance to the encroachment of modern technology and the disruption of natural rhythms.
  • A critical dialogue occurs between Vera and Miss MacIntosh, where Vera confronts her about the “hollow pretense” of their living situation and demands honesty about the nature of their relationship. Vera accuses Miss MacIntosh of deception and questions whether they are truly bound to each other, expressing a desire to understand what she wants and lacks. This exchange is charged with emotional intensity and existential questioning.
  • One of the most poignant moments is when Miss MacIntosh removes her wig, revealing her baldness, which she describes as a source of deep personal embarrassment and symbol of her vulnerability and the “burned wig” shielding her from life’s harsh elements. This act strips away illusion and forces a confrontation with bare reality, emphasizing themes of identity, appearance versus essence, and mortality.
  • Miss MacIntosh offers a bleak yet humorous meditation on life’s disappointments, the inevitability of death, and the “system of mutual deception” that love often entails. She speaks with a mixture of bitterness and dark humor about her past, the failures of God and creation, and the futility of illusions.
  • Miss MacIntosh discusses the umbrella as a symbol of equality and protection, tracing its history from the Orient to England and its social implications. She also mentions the raincoat as a practical item for the poor, underscoring her concern for everyday realities and the working classes. This passage blends historical anecdotes with social commentary.
  • Toward the end of the chapter, despite all the darkness and despair, Miss MacIntosh exhorts Vera to “be of good cheer,” acknowledging that “we are pilgrims” with a long day ahead. She urges living each day as if it were the last, knitting body and soul together even as they unravel. This passage encapsulates the chapter’s tension between despair and endurance, illusion and reality.
  • In the final lines, Miss MacIntosh offers a sober reflection that “people are never quite what they seem” and “nobody knows anybody,” underscoring the chapter’s themes of uncertainty, deception, and the limits of human understanding,

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Miss MacIntosh

  • Miss MacIntosh is described as the "Rock of Ages" in a crumbling world, a symbol of stability, clarity, and common sense.
  • She embodies straightforwardness, rejecting illusions and fantasies, and insists on living within a "framework" of reality.
  • Her physical appearance symbolizes her internal struggle: her ruddy hair like a "sunset burning with bees," a broken nose, and eventually revealed baldness, all reflecting her life's hardships and resilience.
  • The black umbrella she carries is a protective shield, symbolizing her defense against the "fog" of confusion, dreams, and illusions.
  • Miss MacIntosh is a moral mentor and nursemaid figure who values practicality over imagination.
  • She is skeptical of dreams, illusions, and modern distractions, such as electric lights, which she believes disturb natural life rhythms and privacy.
  • She is combative and sharp, not hesitating to scold even divine figures like Emperor Constantine or God Himself for what she sees as excess or failure.
  • Despite her stern exterior, she has a deep, incorruptible sense of humor and reveals moments of vulnerability, especially when discussing her baldness and personal losses.

Research: Themes

Reality vs. Illusion

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Miss MacIntosh represents a rejection of illusions, emphasizing the importance of common sense and "what is immediate and near".
  • Vera struggles with this perspective, feeling trapped between dreams and the harshness of reality.
  • The fog surrounding them symbolizes ambiguity, confusion, and the shifting nature of perception.
  • The electric lights symbolize modern artificiality, disturbing natural darkness and the "dreamless sleep" necessary for authentic living.

Mortality and the Human Condition

See: Death and Mortality

  • The chapter grapples with death, impermanence, and the fragility of life. Miss MacIntosh openly discusses her own mortality, symbolized by her baldness and physical deterioration.
  • She speaks candidly about the "burned-out wick" and the "empty breast," metaphors for loss and the fading of vitality.
  • The idea that "life is very short" and "the spirit dwells not long in any place" underscores a sense of existential urgency 784.
  • Vera's confrontation with Miss MacIntosh about their relationship and existence reflects a deeper search for meaning and honesty in the face of death.

Isolation and Connection

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Despite Miss MacIntosh's role as a caretaker, there is a profound emotional distance and complexity in their relationship.
  • Vera demands honesty and openness, revealing a desire for genuine connection beyond social roles and appearances.
  • Miss MacIntosh's admission of her past struggles and loneliness highlights the theme of isolation in human experience.

Electric Lights vs. Natural Darkness

See: Lamps, Candles, and Light

  • Electric lights symbolize modernity, artificiality, and the loss of privacy and natural rhythms.
  • Miss MacIntosh longs for the natural darkness, the "night of six months," and the simple light of the moon or candle, reflecting a desire for authenticity and peace.

The Fog

See: Fog and Mist

  • The fog is a recurring image symbolizing ambiguity, uncertainty, and the elusive nature of truth and identity.
  • Miss MacIntosh resists being "taken in by the fog," asserting her steadfastness and clarity.

The passage of "invisible assassin" and "one’s own heart which has no other enemy" suggests internal conflict, self-doubt, or self-inflicted suffering. It highlights the idea that much of human pain comes from within, from our own hearts or minds. Miss MacIntosh discusses the loss of her hair and wig, symbols of vanity and identity, revealing her head, "naked of all its illusion of planetary glory" is a metaphor for shedding false pride or external appearances. She acknowledges the "vain, shallow pretense" of life that fools might see through, exposing the emptiness behind social facades. She references the Spitzer brothers and their conflicting choices, suggesting that life’s paths are often confusing, contradictory, and beyond full control. The phrase "Time moves us when we move not" captures the inexorable passage of time regardless of human action. Miss MacIntosh feels "rooked" or cheated by God, lamenting the failure of divine promises or plans. She portrays herself as "God’s poorest and lowest," stripped of glory and dignity. This expresses a profound spiritual crisis or disillusionment. Moving from the personal to the social, she recounts the history of the umbrella as a symbol of equality: once a royal privilege, now a common man’s protection. The umbrella represents practical innovation that democratizes protection and privacy, disrupting old hierarchies (e.g., coachmen, nobility). This illustrates her practical, grounded view of life and society amidst the existential turmoil. At the end, Miss MacIntosh calls for "celestial navigation," urging a broader perspective and wisdom to navigate life’s complexities rather than being bogged down in fragmented, superficial knowledge or emotional despair. This suggests a desire to find meaning or direction despite the chaos. This passage reveals Miss MacIntosh’s complex character: a woman deeply aware of suffering, mortality, and the illusions of life, yet also practical, historically informed, and resolute. She embraces her imperfections and losses, including her baldness and physical scars, as part of her identity. She challenges Vera (the narrator) to see beyond appearances and illusions to the stark truths beneath, while also acknowledging the need to carry on with life’s "mournful business" despite the sorrow and emptiness. This passage encapsulates the novel’s themes of death, identity, illusion versus reality, and the struggle to live meaningfully in a world that often seems indifferent or hostile.

  • Charles’ Wain or the Big Dipper. A folk etymology holds that this derived from Charlemagne, but the name is common to all the Germanic languages and the original reference was to the men's wagon, in contrast to the women's wagon, the Little Dipper.

  • Jonas Hanway was an English merchant, writer and philanthropist. He was the first male Londoner to carry an umbrella and was a noted opponent of tea drinking. Of literary importance is the Historical Account of British Trade over the Caspian Sea, with a Journal of Travels, etc. (London, 1753).   • Jonah from the Book of Jonah was swallowed by a large fish where he prays to God for three days. He is then released. The story of Jonah in the Bible teaches about God's universal mercy and grace, showing that his compassion extends to all people, not just the Israelites. It also highlights the dangers of disobedience and prejudice, as the prophet Jonah attempts to flee God's command to preach to the hated city of Nineveh. The story is a lesson for both individuals and nations, showing how even those closest to God must grapple with his love and mercy for those they consider enemies.
  • Noah is the last of the pre-flood patriarchs of the Book of Genesis. He builds an ark and takes pairs of animals in order to survive the great flood. The biblical story of Noah's Ark signifies a profound message about divine judgment, mercy, and salvation. It illustrates that God judges wickedness and sin but also provides a way for salvation for those who are righteous, like Noah, and those who have faith. The story highlights themes of God's faithfulness through the covenant of the rainbow, and in Christian theology, the ark is often seen as a foreshadowing of Christ as the ultimate source of salvation.
  • "Upon a primrose bank I lie. Cheerio, my dear, and fare thee well until we meet again if ever that may be which is not!" This opening line evokes an image of death or rest ("upon a primrose bank I lie") and a farewell that is uncertain ("if ever that may be which is not"), expressing the ephemeral and uncertain nature of life and afterlife. The phrase "cheerio" is a casual, almost ironic goodbye, hinting at resignation or acceptance of mortality. She admits to having come from hardship (sleeping in doorways, lost in the city without guidance) and to having been "broke," both financially and perhaps spiritually. She saved on electric lights, symbolizing her desire for simplicity or darkness over artificial illumination. Her physical appearance (baldness, missing breast, crooked wig) is revealed as a mark of her struggles and losses. This physical unveiling represents a shedding of illusions or pretenses, an invitation to Vera to see her true self beyond facade and social roles. Miss MacIntosh reflects philosophically that words cannot capture the fullness of flesh and blood reality, nor can they "harness the winds." This underscores the themes of communication’s limits and the gap between language and lived experience. She suggests that real life is often silent, and that love, too, is shrouded in silence and mutual deception. She cynically calls love "a system of mutual deception," revealing her guarded and perhaps disillusioned view of intimacy, likely shaped by her personal history of rejection and scorn. This could also reflect her role as a protector and moral guide: aware of human frailty and illusion. Miss MacIntosh muses that life might have been easier had she been a man, reflecting on the limitations imposed on women ("I could have slipped unnoticed through the crowds," "No one would have laughed"). This speaks to gender dynamics and her alienation from the roles expected of her. The removal of her wig is a powerful symbol of vulnerability and honesty. Baldness, a source of personal embarrassment, is revealed as a core truth she can no longer hide. It represents stripping away social masks and confronting reality, no matter how harsh. Miss MacIntosh contemplates mortality with a mixture of resignation and dark humor, acknowledging that life is short and filled with blows ("Life brings many blows"). She speaks of death openly and almost cheerfully, urging Vera to "live each day as if it were the last," emphasizing the preciousness and fragility of life. She calls them "pilgrims," suggesting life is a journey with an uncertain destination. The image of knitting bones together "though they unravel faster than we knit" evokes the struggle to maintain coherence and meaning in life despite decay and loss. Miss MacIntosh asserts that "people are never quite what they seem. Nobody knows anybody," highlighting the inscrutability of human beings and the limits of understanding others even in close relationships, such as marriage. Her closing remark about suffering "especially when there are rainbows" This poignant line suggests that moments of beauty or hope ("rainbows") can intensify her pain or sense of loss, perhaps because they contrast sharply with her own suffering or the stark realities she faces. This passage is a profound meditation on identity, loss, and the human condition. Miss MacIntosh, unveiling her bald head and revealing her painful history, challenges Vera to see beyond surface illusions to the raw truths of life and death. She speaks to the inevitability of disappointment and the limits of understanding others, urging a stoic acceptance of life’s brevity and complexity. Despite its somber tone, she ends with a call to face the day bravely, knitting body and soul for the journey ahead. This moment is crucial as it deepens the emotional and philosophical texture of the narrative, revealing the nursemaid’s inner life and her role as a moral and existential anchor amid the story’s shifting dreams and realities.
  • Poverty is a form of stress that research has shown alters a person’s behavior and development. The Great Depression would have been recent history for the characters in this story and Marguerite Young herself. The average American family lived by the Depression-era motto: “Use it up, wear it out, make do or do without.” Many tried to keep up appearances and carry on with life as close to normal as possible while they adapted to new economic circumstances. Households embraced a new level of frugality in daily life. They kept kitchen gardens, patched worn-out clothes and passed on trips.
  • The philosophy of ambiguity is not just a theme but a structural and stylistic principle that permeates every level of the novel. Characters defy easy categorization and are often contradictory. Mrs. Hogden embodies both vulgarity and a strange, disconcerting serenity. She seems both real and unreal, present and absent, and Vera struggles to grasp her true nature. Even Miss MacIntosh, who initially appears as a figure of steadfast common sense, is revealed to have hidden depths and a past that complicate her seemingly simple persona. This challenges the reader's ability to form definitive judgments about these characters. Characters are not fixed entities but rather fluid and shifting identities, reflecting the novel's broader exploration of the instability of the self. Mr. Spitzer is perhaps the prime example, constantly questioning his identity and blurring the lines between himself and his deceased twin brother. Vera also undergoes a transformation, as she struggles to reconcile her childhood perceptions with the complexities of the adult world. Vera's narration is filtered through her subjective consciousness, which is itself prone to hallucination, memory distortions, and dreamlike states. This makes it difficult to discern objective truth from Vera's personal interpretations, further contributing to the novel's ambiguity. The narrative voice itself is constantly shifting, blurring the boundaries between narrator and character. The novel is steeped in a dreamlike atmosphere, where the boundaries between reality and illusion become increasingly blurred. Events unfold in a non-linear fashion, and the narrative is filled with surreal imagery and symbolic motifs that resist easy interpretation. This dreamlike quality reflects the novel's exploration of the unconscious mind and the subjective nature of experience. Young employs a rich tapestry of symbolism and metaphor that invites multiple interpretations. Objects, places, and events often carry symbolic weight, but their meanings remain open to interpretation. For example, the sea, a recurring motif in the novel, can represent both the source of life and the threat of destruction, reflecting the inherent ambiguity of nature. The novel’s cyclical structure reinforces its themes of ambiguity and uncertainty. Events and motifs recur throughout the narrative, but with slight variations, suggesting that history repeats itself but never in the same way. This cyclical structure also creates a sense of timelessness, blurring the boundaries between past, present, and future. The novel ultimately rejects the notion of definitive truths or easy answers. The novel suggests that reality is inherently ambiguous and that our understanding of the world is always incomplete and provisional. By embracing ambiguity, Young challenges the reader to question their own assumptions and to accept the inherent uncertainty of human experience. The novel's exploration of ambiguity is not merely a stylistic choice but a reflection of Young's philosophical vision. She suggests that embracing ambiguity is essential for understanding the complexities of human existence and for appreciating the richness and diversity of experience.
