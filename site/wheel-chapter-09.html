Research notes: Chapter 9 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 9

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Solomon the Turtle · Vera Cartwheel

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The narrative voice is constitutively fractured. Vera cannot stabilize her own identity because she exists simultaneously as obedient student, secret rebel, dreaming visionary, and retrospective mourner. These are not phases; they coexist.

The most explicit articulation comes in the circus fantasy, where Vera imagines herself as "a bareback rider wearing pink tights and jumping through her exaggerated ivory embroidery hoops" while simultaneously sitting at her sewing. The same body performs obedience and transgression at once. She notes: "I was, back of my dreaming eyes...a bareback rider", the self is layered, not sequential.

Miss MacIntosh herself is a plural figure: the authoritative nurse, the secret bald woman, the self-deceived, the deceiver. The narrative explicitly names this duality: "In seven years, I had lived with a stranger, someone I thought I knew. In one month, I had lived with a dearly cherished friend, someone I would never know". Two Miss MacIntoshes, irreconcilable.

Even the question of guilt is plural and unresolvable: "What terrible deed was this which I had done...that I, even as a little child, had killed Miss MacIntosh, that I had done so only perhaps by opening my eyes". The self that may have killed, the self that mourns, the self that doubts: all present simultaneously, none conclusive.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter is structured around the systematic collapse of the distinction between illusion and reality, and crucially, it does not resolve in favor of either.

Miss MacIntosh's entire project is the enforcement of the real against the imaginary: "there were no fifty white horses struck by lightning in the garden, no sail-boats moored in the lily ponds...and I was simply lying, and there were no wooden eagles. The dream was always the lie". Her epistemology is militantly anti-illusory.

But the chapter systematically undermines her authority to make this distinction. Her red marcelled hair is a wig. Her eyebrows are absent. Her ovaries, asked for directly, are apparently stored "in the bottom drawer...next to the spare corset": an answer so absurd it reveals that Miss MacIntosh herself inhabits a constructed reality, one of performed normality over an absent body.

The deepest irony is structural: Miss MacIntosh, the enforcer of the real, is herself the greatest illusion in the text. Her "What Cheer, Iowa" (the supposed ground of authentic, undeceived life) is itself a mythologized construction, a pastoral of frozen feet and flour-bag underwear that Vera suspects but cannot confirm.

The "larger cave more carefully lit" is precisely What Cheer: not the absence of illusion, but illusion with better lighting.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The chapter opens not with narrative but with grief already in progress, grief that cannot locate its own cause or object: "What terrible deed was this which I had done and which I never should forget, not until all the seas are emptied, until the waves fall away from the sea, not even then?".

This is grief as climate. It does not arrive after loss; it precedes understanding of the loss. It cannot be discharged because its object is uncertain: "Did she kill herself...Did she kill me?". When the object of grief is indeterminate, grief cannot complete its work. It simply continues.

The seagull passage performs this structurally: Miss MacIntosh nurses the wounded seagull "beneath her shawl, against the coldness of her breast", and "the seagull died when its wound was healed". Recovery and death are simultaneous. This is not irony; it is the chapter's meteorological law: healing does not precede loss. They are the same event.

Even Vera's birthday, which should mark the beginning, is saturated with anticipated ending: "that day was the beginning of the end". Joy arrives already grieving itself.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator's primary ethical act is sustained, unflinching attention: to Miss MacIntosh's body, her contradictions, her self-deceptions, without resolving them into judgment.

The question of Miss MacIntosh's eyebrows is paradigmatic. Vera asks, and Miss MacIntosh deflects into a lecture on the Chicago fire. Vera notes: "I did not realize, until long years afterward, that she had never answered mine, that she had evaded the issue". The narrator does not press, does not conclude. She witnesses the evasion and holds it.

Similarly with the ovaries: Vera looks, does not find them, did not "mention the subject again, for I soon forgot it or put it away in the back of my mind as something of no conceivable importance to anyone". This apparent forgetting is actually a form of witnessing: the detail is preserved precisely by being held lightly, without forcing resolution.

The most sustained act of witness is the description of Miss MacIntosh's body: "Her fingers were knotted and yellow-stained...her fingernails were always dirty and cracked". No judgment. No explanation. Pure attention. The body is seen without being interpreted away.

The narrator refuses to simplify the question of Miss MacIntosh's death across the entire opening passage (murder, suicide, disappearance, God's hand, nobody's hand) all held open simultaneously. This refusal is not indecision. It is the ethical stance of the witness.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The chapter enacts recursion formally. It begins with the adult narrator looking back, then moves into childhood memory, then within that memory constructs fantasy (the circus), then within the birthday scene constructs the imagined husband: layer within layer, each built on the previous, none canceling the others.

Miss MacIntosh's housekeeping project is itself recursive: "Everything had to be as it had been in What Cheer, Iowa". She is perpetually rebuilding a lost original, knowing (at some level) it cannot be recovered. The sewing, the hemming, the embroidery hoops that "would roll under the bed as if they were trying to get away from her": the work undoes itself and must be redone.

Vera's birthday reverie is the most explicit recursive construction: she builds an entire marriage, an entire life, an entire geography of adventure, "foreign countries, the European capitals, the Asiatic temples", from within a room where no guests came, where she danced alone with her shadow. She builds knowing it is provisional. That is not pathology. That is how it works.

The chapter's final image, "Were not true marriages made in heaven, and could they ever be dissolved by mortal law or circumstance", is itself a recursive question, returning to the beginning: what is real, what is constructed, what persists?

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter's syntax is its ethics. Sentences accumulate without concluding. Questions proliferate without answering. The opening paragraph alone contains seven consecutive rhetorical questions, none answered, all held open.

Miss MacIntosh's speech is structured as incompletion: "Run, fetch my needle and thread, for we must hem a handkerchief before the sun sets...When, Vera Cartwheel, did the first Viking land upon this bleak New England shore?...Where are my scissors, and what's become of my darning egg?". Tasks and questions pile up. Nothing is finished. The demand for completion, Miss MacIntosh's central demand, is itself revealed as the violence: it produces anxiety, not order.

The embroidery hoops that roll away are the perfect image: the instrument of completion, fleeing completion.

The ethics of incompletion is most explicit in the marriage fantasy: Vera does not conclude her imagined husband's identity, does not fix his complexion, does not resolve whether she will find him. "He must not be ordinary": the definition proceeds by negation, by what he is not, preserving the openness. The unfinished portrait is the honest portrait.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Miss MacIntosh is the chapter's central marginal figure, and her marginality is precisely what makes the constructed nature of normality visible.

She is unmarried in a chapter obsessed with marriage as natural law: "All the creatures of the earth, all the creatures of the air and of the water were married". Her unmarried state is framed as misfortune, "No man was ever tempted by her", but this framing itself reveals the violence of the norm. She is the evidence that the universal is not universal.

Her body is the most radical site of marginality. The absent eyebrows, the probable wig, the missing ovaries: her body does not conform to the female body that marriage discourse requires. She is, in the most literal sense, a woman whose body refuses the categories assigned to it.

Her anti-British oxfords, British shoes worn by an anti-British woman, perform the same logic: she cannot exist outside the categories she rejects. The marginal person is constituted by the categories they exceed.

The blind violinist and the deaf dancer who miss the train extend this: figures defined by the absence of the sense their art requires, who cannot arrive because one "hearing its whistle but not seeing it, one seeing it but not hearing its whistle", marginality as the structural condition of the world, not the exception to it.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The past does not recede in this chapter. It accumulates as pressure.

What Cheer, Iowa is the deepest sediment: "Everything had to be as it had been in What Cheer". Miss MacIntosh's childhood (frozen feet, flour-bag underwear, the broken nose from the bus accident) is not past. It is the ground on which every present action stands. She reproduces it compulsively.

The schoolroom is the chapter's most explicit image of sedimentary time: "The same cracked blackboards lined the walls. There were maps of sunken continents...The same proverbs were framed in frames of peeling gold". Sunken continents on the walls. The past is not behind them; it is beneath them, mapped and present.

The turtle Solomon, "quite ancient, one who might have carried, as in some legendary preadamite time, the whole world on his back", is the image of sedimentary time made flesh: the preadamite past, still moving, still carrying its flower pot, still scraping against Miss MacIntosh's nerves.

The genealogies Vera must memorize, "the sons of Levi, Judah, Er, Onan, Issachar, the sons of Tola, Phuvah, Zebulun", are pure sedimentation: names deposited layer upon layer, required to be carried forward without understanding, pressing up through the present as obligation.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Miss MacIntosh's language is the chapter's most sustained demonstration that language shapes what can exist.

Her speech is a continuous act of world-construction through naming and categorizing: "Locate my scissors, and locate the Grand Canyon, and don't point to the map of Africa, for that's not where it is". Geography is language. To name correctly is to constitute reality. To point wrongly is to be wrong about the world.

Her nautical metaphors, "trimming her sails," "scudding along," "never becalmed", construct her as a sailor who has never been to sea. The language performs a self that her circumstances cannot support. She is "the land-locked sailor": language building what reality denies.

The word "What Cheer" is the chapter's most loaded linguistic construction. It names a real Iowa town, but in the text it functions as a signifier for undeceived normalcy, cheerful practicality, the real. Vera notes: "Cheerful, I supposed she had been in What Cheer, and always skipping a rope", the name generates the fantasy. Language creates the place that justifies the discipline.

Most precisely: Miss MacIntosh insists that "the dream was always the lie", but this is itself a linguistic act, a declaration that one category (dream) maps onto another (lie). Language is not reporting a pre-existing distinction. It is making one.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter is entirely immanent (located in the specific, the bodily, the ordinary) and it is most present in the moments of greatest apparent ordinariness.

The turtle Solomon carrying a clay flower pot with a red geranium "which smelled like tobacco"; this is the sacred in its precise, unglamorous, immanent form. Ancient enough to have carried the world, now carrying a pot that smells wrong. The transcendent is here, in this specific, slightly comic, entirely present object.

The seagull nursed "beneath her shawl, against the coldness of her breast": the sacred is in the coldness of the breast, the specific texture of the shawl, the wounded leg. Not in the symbolism of the bird but in the body of the woman holding it.

Vera's birthday dance is the chapter's central immanent transcendence: "I danced alone. I danced with my shadow. My hair unbraided, whirling, my cheek shadowed, I could imagine any partner I pleased, for no one else would ever see him, and he was entirely mine". No guests, no music, no partner, and yet this is the most alive moment in the chapter. The transcendent is here, in the empty room, in the shadow, in the entirely private imagination.

When Vera says "I am dancing with Death", Miss MacIntosh replies that Death is not yet. But the sacred is precisely in this anticipation, not in death as metaphysical category but in the fourteen-year-old girl alone in a party dress, already in conversation with it.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The chapter's deepest structure is the crossing of times: Vera is constituted by what was deposited before her, and she is constituting the ground for what comes after, and she exists at the crossing point of these two movements.

She is constituted by: her mother's opium dreams, Miss MacIntosh's What Cheer childhood, the genealogies she memorizes without understanding, the turtle given by a dead guest, the Roman coin from excavators, all of these are deposits she did not choose and cannot refuse.

She is constituting: the imagined husband, the imagined marriage, the imagined voyage on "uncharted seas, the crossbeams crackling like frozen twigs above our heads, sea birds screaming in the many winds, and no shore imaginable". This is the ground she is laying for a future she cannot yet inhabit.

The chapter's opening question, "What terrible deed was this which I had done...not until all the seas are emptied, until the waves fall away from the sea, not even then?", is the crossing point made explicit. The adult narrator is constituted by the child's act (opening her eyes, or failing to keep them closed), and that act reaches forward to the end of the seas. The obligation is not chosen. It runs in both directions through time.

Miss MacIntosh's final words in the chapter, "When thou diest, I shall die with you, the flowers of the fields", name this structural entanglement directly. They will cross together. The carrying continues.
