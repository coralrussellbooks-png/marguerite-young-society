Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 54

← Chapter 53 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 55 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

The underwater dream opens this chapter, and the dissolving of every boundary is its pillar. The opium lady's dreams open a "vast subterranean unconscious," a sea of "fertility and transformation," and Mr. Spitzer moves through it as the passive interpreter of a world a woman has dreamed into being, real and dream as one country with a woman at its source. His brother's death and the "alien acquaintances" rattling his conscience, Peron's "chaotic sound and disorder" against Joachim's "silence and order," set the two brothers going again, and the sound effects man who makes "chaos through vocal cords" is the disruptive liminal figure the book keeps placing at the margins, a self performing its own noise.

Insomnia fills the chapter with phantom sounds, bees and birds and natural music, feminine-coded and cyclical, and Mr. Spitzer is "deaf" or "dead" to them, the severed sense that the book uses for erasure turned inward on the man estranged from the living world. He mistakes himself for his dead brother, the reality and the illusion blurring, and the confusion is plural selfhood at its most literal, a self that cannot tell itself from its double. Streets shift and landscapes alter, time and identity go fluid, and all the fixed categories the book keeps refusing, a city that will not hold its shape.

Marginalized figures crowd the chapter, old women and "ragged tramps," and Mr. Spitzer as "finder of lost heirs" and helper of the abandoned carries the book's care for the erased, the relational ethic it keeps setting against the ledger. Women disguised as men, "Indian maidens," a woman "dressing as a man to survive," bring the crossing point and gender as performance into the crowd, surviving wearing the other sex the way the suffragette did. His refusal "to own real jewels," his suspicion as a jewel thief, his self-imposed exile under accusation, are the scapegoating and social ostracism the book knows women and the marginalized by, and the network of "sightings and rumors" is the surveillance that pins a body it cannot recognize.

Death and legacy close it. Mr. Spitzer's admiration for Peron against his own isolation, his search for self and brother, his ambivalence about "love, identity, eternity," are the fragile masculine self the book keeps unmaking, and the "two wrist watches," one for the living and one for the dead, hold the coexistence of multiple selves and times in a single object. Peron's song and Spitzer's fear at hearing it set self against other, life against death, order against chaos, homecoming against loss, and the chapter ends with Mr. Spitzer as the composer of his life's music, "fragmentary and unfinished," the interrupted work the book gives its people in place of any whole.
