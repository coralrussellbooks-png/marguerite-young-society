Chapter Four: The Not-Yet-Born

← Chapter Three: The Ancestors Are in the Sediment · Radical Immanence Collection · Part Two: The Wheel →

The most politically urgent chapter in this book is also the most philosophically difficult, because it requires developing an account of obligation to persons who do not yet exist, who may never exist in the form we imagine, who cannot make claims or express preferences or enter into any relationship of reciprocity with the living. Western ethics has produced frameworks for obligation to contemporaries that are, whatever their limitations, at least grounded in the mutual recognition of existing persons. It has produced much weaker frameworks for obligation to future persons, and the weakness is not accidental. It follows from the foundational commitments of the unified-subject model: obligation requires a specific other, the other must be capable of being wronged or benefited, and the not-yet-born are too indeterminate to fill that role.

The seven generations principle, understood not as a cultural practice to be borrowed from Indigenous traditions but as a philosophical account of the vertical axis in its forward direction, offers a genuinely different structure.

The principle, in its most common articulation, holds that decisions should be made with attention to their effects seven generations forward, roughly one hundred and seventy-five years. This is sometimes treated as an extended version of the same forward-looking obligation that Western ethics already recognizes, just with a longer timeframe. That reading misses the philosophical point entirely.

The seven generations principle is not a longer timeframe for the same kind of analysis. It is a different kind of analysis, one that can only be performed from the crossing point. When you are reasoning from inside a present moment, your decision-making frame is bounded by the horizon of the immediately foreseeable. The consequences you can see are the consequences that fall within that frame. The consequences that fall outside it are invisible not because they are not real but because the frame cannot hold them.

When you are reasoning from inside the vertical axis, from the crossing point that holds both what was given and what will be required, the frame changes. You are no longer reasoning from inside a present moment. You are reasoning from inside a transmission.

The question is not what the consequences of this decision will be in the foreseeable future. The question is what this decision deposits in the sediment. What layer does it add to the ground that the not-yet-born will stand on? What weight does it press down on the layers that will come after?

This is not a more demanding version of consequentialist calculation. It is a different epistemological stance. Consequentialist calculation requires that you be able to trace the causal chains from present decision to future outcome. The seven generation frame does not require that. It requires that you be able to read the structure of the transmission: to understand what kind of deposit you are making, what kind of ground you are contributing to, what kind of weight you are adding to the sediment that will press down on everything that comes after.

The not-yet-born do not need to be specific persons for this obligation to be real. They need only to be the occupants of the ground you are helping to form. And you are always helping to form the ground, whether you are attending to that fact or not. The eleventh pillar says: attend to it. Not because attending to it will make you comfortable. Because attending to it is what honesty requires of a crossing point.

The political implications of this are not abstract. The decisions being made now, in climate, in artificial intelligence, in the dismantling of institutions that took generations to build, in the acceleration of extraction that converts the sediment of millions of years into the atmospheric deposit of a single century, these are decisions being made by people reasoning from inside a present moment with no adequate philosophical framework for the vertical axis. They are making horizontal decisions about vertical matters. They are acting as if the not-yet-born do not exist as a philosophical category, only as a sentimental one.

The not-yet-born are not sentimental. They are structural. They are the forward direction of the transmission line you are already on. They are the occupants of the ground you are already forming. The obligation to them is not chosen. It is constitutional. It arises from the fact of being a crossing point, from being a carrier, from being the place where time crosses itself and the crossing has consequences that extend further than any present moment can see.
