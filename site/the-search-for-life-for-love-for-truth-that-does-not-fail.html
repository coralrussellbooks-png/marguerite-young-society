Quest Traditions Across Four Thousand Years in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling

All chapter references in this document are drawn from direct search of the novel. Where a quest work or figure is identified, the chapter number and passage are given. Interpretive claims are labeled as such.

I. The Frame

Vera states the novel's quest in the first chapter, plainly:

What motive in this quest but the search for life, for love, for truth that does not fail?

This is the frame within which every other quest in the novel operates. Vera's search for Miss MacIntosh, for What Cheer, Iowa, for the stable center of a world organized around practical facts and sensible shoes, is the surface quest. Beneath it, the novel holds nearly every major quest tradition in Western and some Eastern literary history, reaching back approximately four thousand years from Vera's bus journey through Indiana to an Egyptian sailor who arrived in Boston going the wrong way.

A direct search of the novel reveals a more complex architecture: six named literary quests, a second tier of archetypal wandering figures who are not literary titles but named presences in the book, and a structural argument about what quest means that the novel makes through its ending rather than stating outright.

II. The Foundation: The Egyptian Sailor

The oldest quest narrative embedded in the novel appears in Chapter 2, in Catherine's opium world. A drowned Egyptian sailor, gold-turbaned, his face glistening like clouded water, arrives in Catherine's bedroom. He has sailed from Egypt toward New York, gone off course, and arrived instead in Boston. He is, in the novel's own words, looking for Mr. Spitzer, trying to locate him in space if not in time.

Catherine receives him and gives him instructions for finding Mr. Spitzer through the house. The instructions are themselves a quest: she describes a shifting landscape, the schools of faceless dolphins which should follow in his wake, the great birds with teeth, the stars not in their places. She tells him: He should not be afraid of the minotaur, for the minotaur would be afraid of him.

The house has become a labyrinth. Catherine has become the oracle at its entrance. The Egyptian sailor is Theseus given directions by a woman in an opium bed. And the figure he is seeking, Mr. Spitzer, may or may not be findable, in space if not in time.

The Story of the Shipwrecked Sailor is among the oldest narratives in the literary record, dating to Egypt's Middle Kingdom period, approximately 2000 BCE. In it, a sailor is shipwrecked on an island and must find his way home. Young's Egyptian sailor has also been shipwrecked by misdirection: his captain misread the stars, the winds, the auguries of soothsayers and of birds and of horses' heads. He has arrived in the wrong place and is searching for someone who exists in time rather than in space. This is the quest structure at its most ancient: the traveler who has gone off course, who is looking for something or someone in a world that does not organize itself helpfully, who must navigate by stars that have moved from their expected positions.

Young did not name the Story of the Shipwrecked Sailor. She placed the Egyptian sailor, and the instruction not to fear the minotaur, and the labyrinthine directions, and left the reader to hear the resonance. The foundation is the oldest layer. Everything above it is built on top.

III. The Six Named Literary Quests

Young named six quest works directly within the novel. They are distributed across different characters and different chapters, and they are not evenly distributed one per character. The distribution is given here as confirmed from the book.

Pilgrim's Progress: Miss MacIntosh's Book

Pilgrim's Progress is the quest work most deeply embedded in the novel's physical texture. Vera, the narrator, describes her reading habits: "I would begin at the beginning, reading straight to the end or skipping only a few passages, and sometimes I would begin at the end, reading straight to the beginning, working from redemption backward, perhaps skipping the same passages, and she would never seem to notice..." Bunyan's single-direction journey from the City of Destruction to the Celestial City is being read backwards and forwards simultaneously. The quest is being reversed even as it is being transmitted.

Miss MacIntosh also carries Pilgrim's Progress physically through the novel as one of her personal objects, alongside her black umbrella and her fishnet bag. When she disappears, the novel asks: where was her black umbrella, and where was her Pilgrim's Progress? Her copy is last seen waterlogged, swollen by the sea. The guide through the wilderness has been soaked by the same water that took Miss MacIntosh.

Don Quixote: Two Windmill Chasers

Don Quixote appears in Chapter 1 applied to Moses Hunnecker: Was he, after all, a bachelor, perhaps even some mad Don Quixote chasing windmills? The question is Vera's, asked of the bus driver she cannot quite read. He reappears in Chapter 65, where another character says of Miss MacIntosh: Chasing windmills. Miss MacIntosh had always been chasing windmills. The windmill is the emblem of Quixote's error: the giant that was always a windmill, the quest object that was always something more ordinary than the quester believed. Young gives this emblem to both Moses and Miss MacIntosh: the two figures who drive and anchor Vera's journey are both, in someone's assessment, chasers of windmills.

The Odyssey: Two Men Who Did Not Go

The Odyssey appears inverted in Chapter 5. Moses, in his interior monologue, identifies himself as Penelope, not Ulysses: he was Penelope weaving this web, not Ulysses who wandered over the wide seas, he just staying at home and minding his own business. Moses is the one who stays, who keeps the home fires, whose hair grows as Penelope's weaving grew: a perpetual maintenance rather than a journey. In Chapter 23, Mr. Spitzer observes that Ulysses, if ever he had seen Penelope under electric lights, would never have come home. The Odyssey's homecoming is refused: the destination, seen clearly, is not worth arriving at.

Childe Roland: Catherine's Search

Childe Roland to the Dark Tower Came, Robert Browning's poem of a knight who searches through a blasted landscape for a tower whose purpose he does not know, appears in Chapter 30 in the context of Catherine's long search for her lost husband Jock. Mr. Spitzer reports that Catherine implored Cousin Hannah to search for him in dark valleys, search for him where Childe Roland had blown his horn before a dark tower and where no other sound had ever been heard. The Dark Tower quest is the quest whose object is unknown even to the quester: Roland does not know what he will find or do when he reaches the tower. He finds it and blows his horn and the poem ends. Catherine's search for Jock has the same structure: she does not know what she would do if she found him. The quest is the motion, not the arrival.

The Divine Comedy: Mr. Spitzer's Self-Placement

In Chapter 29, in the middle of his long meditation on death and the body and his dead twin, Mr. Spitzer asks: "Or when should he come to the last infernal circle of this Divine Comedy of life and death?" He places himself explicitly within Dante's structure. His life is the Divine Comedy. He is descending through the infernal circles. The last circle is where he is headed. In the Inferno the last circle is the frozen lake of Cocytus, where the worst betrayers are embedded in ice. Mr. Spitzer does not elaborate on what his last circle contains. He asks when he will reach it. The question mark does its work.

The Holy Grail and Jason and the Argonauts: Cousin Hannah

Cousin Hannah receives both the Holy Grail and Jason and the Argonauts in Chapter 42, applied in the same passage: “she had not found the Holy Grail or any object of her quest. She was that Jason who had not found the golden fleece.” Both quest objects are withheld from her. The Holy Grail is the Christian quest object, the cup that holds the blood of Christ; the Golden Fleece is the pagan quest object, the impossible prize that Jason pursued across the known world. Cousin Hannah pursued suffrage for women with the same total commitment that heroes pursued these objects. She did not find either. The sheath had outlived the sword. Young gives Cousin Hannah both the sacred and the classical quest tradition, and both end the same way.

IV. The Second Tier: Archetypal Wanderers

Beyond the six named literary quests, the novel populates itself with archetypal quest figures who are not literary titles but named presences: wanderers who have been going for so long that they have become types rather than characters.

The Wandering Jew

Ahasuerus, the Wandering Jew or, as Catherine preferred to think of him, the Everlasting Jew, appears in Chapter 40 as one of Catherine's opium visitors. He is the figure condemned to wander until the Second Coming, having turned Christ away from his door on the road to Calvary. Catherine listens to him for hours. The Wandering Jew is the oldest Christian quest figure: not someone searching for something, but someone condemned to perpetual motion as punishment, unable to stop, unable to arrive. He is the anti-pilgrim: Pilgrim's Progress reaches the Celestial City; Ahasuerus never reaches anything.

The Northwest Passage

Chapter 32 contains the novel's most direct statement about the universal structure of the quest: For every soul must know finally its Northwest Passage, must find that which cannot be found, the crevice narrow as a thread suddenly opening into those great abysses filled with cities of frozen pinnacles and towers. The Northwest Passage is the quest that killed its questers for centuries: the sea route through the Arctic that explorers searched for and died searching for. Young's use of it here is precise: "For every soul must know finally its Northwest Passage, must find that which cannot be found, the crevice narrow as a thread suddenly opening into those great abysses filled" Not find. Know. The finding is not the point. The knowing of the impossibility is what the quest produces.

Polar Explorers and the Real Questers

Admiral Peary, Shackleton, and the polar expedition tradition appear repeatedly across the novel, most densely in Miss MacIntosh's geography chapters, where she asks Vera: Where is Shackleton Shelf, Enderby Land, Keeling Islands? The polar questers are the ones who went to the edges of the known world and came back having failed to reach the pole, or having reached it and disputed whether they had, or having died on the ice. Cousin Hannah is described as a great balloonist and mountain-climber in the same tradition. Her suffrage quest and the polar quests are held in the same breath because they have the same structure: the journey to an extreme, the attempt to plant a flag at the furthest point, the question afterward of whether anyone actually got there.

The Crusaders

The Crusader figure is applied specifically to Cousin Hannah across multiple chapters. She is that noble Crusader armored by her pride, the great beau sabreur of suffrage brandishing her sword. The Crusades were quests organized around recovering a holy city. Cousin Hannah's quest was organized around recovering the rights of women. Young makes the structural parallel explicit without apologizing for it: the Crusader who sets out to take Jerusalem and the suffragette who sets out to take the vote are both questers who organize their entire lives around a single object and pour everything into the motion toward it.

V. The Labyrinth at the Center

Catherine's instructions to the Egyptian sailor in Chapter 2 contain a structural argument about the novel's entire quest architecture that is worth reading slowly. She tells the sailor he must circle back the way he came, climb the stairs he has already descended. She tells him the doors have changed their places. She tells him not to fear the minotaur, for the minotaur would be afraid of him. The house through which she is directing him is a labyrinth, and the labyrinth does not have a stable architecture: the rooms between the rooms multiply, the doors relocate, the distance between one object and another is illusive.

The Minotaur at the center of the labyrinth is what Theseus was questing for: the thing to be slain at the destination. Catherine tells the Egyptian sailor not to fear it. She does not tell him it is not there. The minotaur exists. It is just afraid of him. The thing at the center of the labyrinth is afraid of the quester who has come all this way to face it.

What is at the center of this novel's labyrinth is the question the quest chapter cannot answer by counting quest references. It is the question Vera has been asking since Chapter 1. The search is for life, for love, for truth that does not fail. The minotaur at the center may be the truth that does fail. It may be Miss MacIntosh's baldness. It may be the realization that the stable center does not exist. Whatever it is, it is afraid of Vera. She has come far enough.

VI. The Ending: Bee Bak in a Whale

The novel's final lines are Esther Longtree's restaurant sign: “Owt to luntsch. bee bak in a whale. For she could not spell either.”

The sign is misspelled. Back is spelled bak. Whale is spelled whale but means while. Except that it also means whale. Jonah went into the whale and came back. The whale is the place of transformation between going and returning: you go in one person and come out another, if you come out at all. Esther Longtree has gone to lunch. She will be back in a while. She will be back in a whale.

The novel ends not with Vera's arrival at What Cheer, Iowa. She does not find Miss MacIntosh. The quest does not reach its stated destination. Instead the novel ends with a promise of return, misspelled, hung in a restaurant window in Indiana, signed by the woman who is the mother of us all and who cannot spell.

This is the quest's resolution. Not arrival. Not the object found. Not the golden fleece retrieved, the Holy Grail discovered, the Celestial City entered, the last infernal circle passed through. The resolution is a sign on a closed door that says: I have gone somewhere. I will come back. The quest continues. The restaurant is closed. Come back later.

This novel is one in which nothing concludes and everything begins over again. The Egyptian sailor is still in the house, waiting for Mr. Spitzer. The bus is still driving through Indiana. Moses's hair is still growing. Vera has arrived somewhere that is not What Cheer. She is going to be married and invite everything alive and dead to the wedding. Esther Longtree would be her. The sign on the door says she will be back in a whale.

Four thousand years of quest literature are gathered in this novel, from the shipwrecked Egyptian sailor reading the wrong stars to the stone deaf man Vera loves at the end. Every tradition Young drew on ends the same way: the quester finds something other than what they sought, and the finding is the quest's true content. What you were looking for was never the point. The looking was.
