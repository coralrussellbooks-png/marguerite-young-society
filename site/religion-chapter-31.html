Chapter 31

On Chapter 31 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Christianity and Religion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 24, 2026

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

“her journeys into forbidden Mecca or Medinah or Fez”: Mecca and Medina are forbidden to non-Muslims. Richard Burton entered both in disguise in 1853. Mecca is Islam’s holiest city in Saudi Arabia. Medinah is the second holiest city in Islam. Fez is an important city in Morocco.

“cutting like a two-edged sword”: “Sharper than any twoedged sword” (Hebrews 4:12). See The Bible.

"plucked up into the bosom of Abraham where sleeps the dove" borrows the resting place of the blessed dead from Jesus's parable of the rich man and Lazarus in Luke 16, Abraham's side as paradise.

“saying that it should reveal the transience of human life”: The transience of human life is a philosophical concept emphasizing the impermanence and fleeting nature of existence, encourages mindfulness, acceptance of change, and a deeper appreciation for the present moment. In Buddhism, impermanence (anicca) is one of the three marks of existence.

“bearded human lions in their dens”: Daniel in the lions’ den (Daniel 6), and Scott’s “to beard the lion in his den” (Marmion, 1808). See The Bible.

“in love with no man on earth but with a fleeting shadow, the mere shadow of a man”: Shadows in scripture and Shakespeare. See The Bible, Ecclesiastes, The Book of Job, Shakespeare, and Love. “Man is like to vanity: his days are as a shadow that passeth away” (Psalm 144:4). Ecclesiastes 6:12 speaks of “all the days of his vain life which he spendeth as a shadow.” “He cometh forth like a flower, and is cut down: he fleeth also as a shadow, and continueth not” (Job 14:2). “For the law having a shadow of good things to come, and not the very image of the things” (Hebrews 10:1). “Which are a shadow of things to come; but the body is of Christ” (Colossians 2:17). “Life’s but a walking shadow” is a line from William Shakespeare’s Macbeth (5.5).

“girding her loins with suffrage like that iron girdle worn by a great Crusader’s wife”: “Gird up now thy loins like a man” (Job 38:3). The chastity belt of Crusaders’ wives is largely a legend of later centuries. See The Book of Job and Women’s Suffrage.

“Or he might imagine that his veiled wife was beautiful”: Veiling and wearing a veil of clothing or hanging cloth has a long history in many societies across the world. It is intended to cover some part of the head or face, or an object of some significance. The practice has been prominent in different forms in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. The practice of veiling is especially associated with women and sacred objects, though in some cultures, it is men, rather than women, who are expected to wear a veil. Besides its enduring religious significance, veiling continues to play a role in some modern secular contexts, such as wedding customs. One view is that as a religious item, it is intended to honor a person, object or space. The actual sociocultural, psychological, and sociosexual functions of veils have not been studied extensively but most likely include the maintenance of social distance and the communication of social status and cultural identity. A veil also has symbolic interpretations, as something partially concealing, disguising, or obscuring. (Wikipedia) In Young’s sentence the veil may conceal “the visage of the naked skull.”

“draped by veils of black chiffon in memory of some earlier death”: Mirrors were covered in houses of mourning, in Victorian custom and in the Jewish shiva. See Grief, Loss, and Mourning and Judaism.

“for what were earthly riches, great castles and lonely towers”: Luke 12:16-21, the Parable of the Rich Fool, shows how wealth can be a distraction from the true purpose of life. “Thou fool, this night thy soul shall be required of thee: then whose shall those things be, which thou hast provided?” (Luke 12:20). Mr. Spitzer’s reflections echo the parable’s warning against greed and misplaced security. See The Bible.

“his calling card embossed with the butterfly which was his sign of resurrection”: Mr. Spitzer’s calling card. See Butterflies and Moths and the Chapter 26 resources.

“Here lies that great Crusader who rode against the Saracens”: Mr. Spitzer’s epitaphs put Cousin Hannah on both sides of the Crusades: she rode against the Saracens, and “She has gone over to the Islamites.” Women did fight and rule on both sides. Women played significant roles in the Crusades, either by leading armies, influencing political decisions, or promoting the cause: Eleanor of Aquitaine, Matilda of Tuscany, Melisende, Queen of Jerusalem, Margaret of Provence, Queen consort of France, Sibyl, Queen of Jerusalem, and Margaret of Beverly, who fought in the defense of Jerusalem against Saladin (1187). Ida of Austria led her own army in the Crusade of 1101, and the legendary Florine of Burgundy was said to have died fighting beside Sweyn of Denmark in 1097. Women resisted during the Crusades: Shajar al-Durr, who ruled Egypt and organized its defense against the Seventh Crusade (1249-50), Anna Comnena, and the women of the Baltic Crusades. Ismat ad-Din Khatun, regent of Damascus, negotiated the end of the Crusader siege of Banias in 1174, and William of Tyre credited her with “courage beyond that of most women” (A History of Deeds Done Beyond the Sea, 20.31). At Shaizar, in Syria, when Assassins stormed the citadel in 1114, during the Crusade years though not by Crusaders, Usama ibn Munqidh’s mother armed the women, and an old servant, Funun, “covered herself with her veil, took up a sword and went into battle” (Usama ibn Munqidh, The Book of Contemplation, trans. Paul M. Cobb, 2008). Khawla bint al-Azwar, the most famous Arab woman warrior, fought the Byzantines at Yarmouk (636), five centuries before the Crusades.

“She has gone over to the Islamites”: In the Islamic tradition burial is a sacred practice. Cremation is strictly forbidden. The body is buried as soon as possible after death, ideally within 24 hours, in a simple shroud, facing Mecca and without a coffin. There are no elaborate markers; simple flat markers are used instead. There is a ritual washing and prayer, and mourners each place three handfuls of soil. Post-mortem examinations are avoided. The general mourning period is three days; a widow mourns four months and ten days (Qur’an 2:234), and many Muslim communities also mark the fortieth day. (Wikipedia)

For Cousin Hannah's epitaph Mr. Spitzer weighs "The rose of Sharon is growing in a desert place," lifting the Song of Solomon's flower, long read as a figure for Christ, and setting it in a wasteland.

“for Joachim, though he pretended to be so true, was weak as water”: “Unstable as water, thou shalt not excel” (Genesis 49:4). See Genesis.

“her search for the external man”: “Though our outward man perish, yet the inward man is renewed day by day” (2 Corinthians 4:16). See The Bible. Internalism and externalism are two opposite ways of integration of explaining various subjects in several areas of philosophy. These include human motivation, knowledge, justification, meaning, and truth. The distinction arises in many areas of debate with similar but distinct meanings. Internalism is the thesis that no fact about the world can provide reasons for action independently of desires and beliefs. Externalism is the thesis that reasons are to be identified with objective features of the world. (Wikipedia)

“The external man, there was no doubt, had been Cousin Hannah’s foe”: Cousin Hannah had scoffed at Catherine’s “search for the external man,” asking “was it flesh which made the man,” his weight, his measure, his girth. Against the outward man stands the inward one (2 Corinthians 4:16).
