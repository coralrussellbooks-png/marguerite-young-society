Part Five: The Vote, the Eight-Hour Day, and the Unfinished

Part of a reading of Harp Song for a Radical (1999), Marguerite Young’s life of Eugene Victor Debs.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 25, 2026

← Part Four · The Suffragette and the Socialist: Cousin Hannah and Eugene Debs

Partial victories and open endings

Cousin Hannah wins real battles. The society’s essay on her warrior lineage, “Married to the Movement,” calls her a general with genuine victories. She helps change laws and break the chains that bound women in public life. But the one battle she most needed to win, freeing her cousin Catherine from grief and opium dreams, she loses. Catherine does not experience her grief as chains. She experiences it as her life. Hannah can change the law. She cannot change the weather inside another person.

Young writes the radicals of Harp Song in the same spirit. The book is crowded with failed communes, cranks, convicts, and prophets who were right too early, what Charles Ruas called Young’s great subjects: “the misfit, the wrecked, the impossible, and the forgotten.” She does not ask them to succeed completely. She said that “out of every dreamer something good comes,” even if “it might only be an eight-hour day.”

The vote and the eight-hour day are the same kind of victory. They are concrete, outward, and hard-won, and they do not cure anyone’s private sorrow. Young honors them without pretending they are paradise. In the language of Radical Immanence, this is the pillar of building anyway, knowing the building is temporary.

Both crusaders are also liberators by temperament. Hannah breaks invisible chains. Debs would later tell the court that sentenced him in 1918, in words Young knew well, “while there is a soul in prison, I am not free.” In the novel that sentence turns against itself. The prisoner Hannah most wanted to free did not want freeing. Young’s fiction asks what a liberator does when the captive loves the cell.

And then there are the endings. Hannah dies slowly, after frostbite and years of semi-invalidism, in bed rather than by a “sharp blow” (Chapter 32). The novel gives her a deathbed confession in which she renounces the glory of foreign conquests and says the greatest battles were fought at home, though it may be Catherine’s invention. Her ascent ends not in an affirmation but in a question: are love and death one? Catherine always insisted that Hannah’s life, “just like her own,” had been “largely imaginary” (Chapter 41). The doer and the dreamer end in the same place.

Debs’s ending in Young’s hands is stranger still. She opens Harp Song at the end of his life, in prison. Debs enters Moundsville in April 1919, is moved to the Atlanta Federal Penitentiary, and walks out on Christmas Day 1921 after Harding commutes his sentence. Prison is the frame. Then the book turns back a century and more, through Colmar, Weitling, the communes, and Terre Haute, and the narrative stops around 1888 with Debs still a young man in Indiana. Pullman, the founding of the Socialist Party, and the five campaigns lie in the gap between the prelude and the place where Young’s pen stopped. The society’s reading of the book, The Unfinished Harp, treats that missing ending as a form of honesty.

Perhaps it is. Young built her late work around two crusaders who were made from real lives, and she closed neither one. Hannah ends in a question. Debs ends in mid-sentence, a young man still in Indiana with most of his fights ahead of him. In both cases the crusade is left open, unfinished, still underway, which may be the truest thing a writer can say about the long American argument over how people should live together.
