Chapter 79

On Chapter 79 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Woman in Young's Magnum Opus.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Characters: Esther Longtree

A silver spoon made Esther in this chapter, and the crossing point runs through the story of her conception. Her mother and father never consummated their marriage, a "blushing bride" who never matured and an immense passive man, and a third person carried the seed between them with the spoon, so that Esther's origin is a thought "rather than a fact," the interrupted birth run backward into the very act of conceiving her. The mother's fragility against the father's bulk, the union that was never a union, is the loveless marriage the book keeps exposing, a honeymoon with no consummation stretched across a life.

The go-between is an "old concubine" who carries the spoon, monstrous and powerful, dressed in masculine attire and foreign, and her ambiguous gender is the crossing point set at the source of Esther's life, the seam the book writes from present at the moment of her making. When the revelation comes that this "other woman" was in fact a man who "deflowered" the mother in disguise, the crossing point turns to violation and deception at once, gender used as a mask over an assault, the man wearing the woman to reach a woman.

The conception takes seven years of pain and dread while the father sits "reading baseball scores," and the gap between his obliviousness and her mother's torment is the uneven burden of reproduction the book keeps weighing, the man untouched while the woman is worked on. The mother's conflicted feelings, her attempts to abort, the violent birth in which Esther is "born kicking," are the interrupted birth turned nearly to its opposite, a delivery resisted from both sides, and the mother's scorn afterward and Esther's guilt for "killing the shadow" carry the fracture forward into the daughter.

Esther's identity stays split between herself and the "shadow," the stillborn potential the book keeps doubling her with, plural selfhood carried from the womb, and the multiplicity of "fathers," the "puny salesmen," scatters her paternity the way her selfhood is scattered. When she confronts the dying go-between who is "the father," the illusions of family and love and origin die with the figure, and Esther's own "gradual death," her talking "of dead grasshoppers," close the chapter on a woman connected to the dying natural world, coming apart slowly and speaking to the dead, at home in the country where real and dream are one.
