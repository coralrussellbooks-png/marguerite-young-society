Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 59

← Chapter 58 · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 60 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act · The Margins Reveal the Truth

The Questions

Mr. Spitzer’s failing eyes cannot keep “the difference between red and white.” When the senses go, what happens to the distinctions they drew?

He keeps “mistaking a beggar for a king or a king for a beggar.” If the eye cannot tell them apart, what held them apart?

He has “learned to be tactfully cautious, not offering his charities until he knew” who stood before him. What is that caution, when he can never quite know?

Why does the book give its clearest sight to a man whose eyes are dimming?

The chapter lets the categories dissolve as the senses fail. Mr. Spitzer’s “eyesight had grown dim,” his eyes “covered by a whirling fog,” and he can “scarcely knew the difference between red and white,” the red rose of the sun from the white rose of the moon. The failing of sight is not only a loss; it is a revelation. The distinctions the eye drew so confidently, red from white, this from that, turn out to have depended on the eye, and when the eye clouds, they come loose. What looked like differences in the world were partly differences the seeing supplied, and a dimming eye is one that stops supplying them, and so sees, in its way, how made they were.

The beggar and the king are the chapter’s sharpest case. Mr. Spitzer keeps “mistaking a beggar for a king or a king for a beggar,” taking the swallow-tailed gentleman for a poker-playing derelict and the derelict for a lord. The social grammar that sorts people into high and low, the categories that seem written into the world, will not hold for a man whose sight has clouded, because they were never written into the world; they were laid over it, a construction of rank the clear eye enforced and the dim eye lets slip. From the fog, the king and the beggar look the same, which is to say they were always, under the costume, the same, and only the sorting eye kept them apart.

His caution is the Witness’s answer to a world he cannot sort. Mr. Spitzer has “learned to be tactfully cautious, not offering his charities until he knew who the starving or sleeping” man was, and yet he can never quite know, so the caution is permanent, a way of moving among people whose rank he cannot read. This is not the caution of suspicion but of respect for an uncertainty he will not force. He will not pin the beggar or the king to a fixed identity his eyes cannot confirm; he stays provisional, tactful, holding the question of who stands before him open, which is the same provisional living he practices with the two chairs and the shifting relationships he refuses to codify.

And the chapter hands its clearest sight to the dimming eye. The Margins Reveal the Truth has been the book’s claim that those who do not fit the categories see the categories best; here it is the man whose sight is failing who sees, better than any clear eye, that the categories are made. His fog is a kind of vision. Where a sharp eye enforces the difference between king and beggar and never notices it is enforcing anything, Mr. Spitzer’s clouded eye lets the difference go and, in losing it, sees it for the construction it was. He is going blind into the truth that the clear-sighted are too clear-sighted to see.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Mr. Spitzer’s failing sight for simple decline, a sad dimming, and the reading that takes his confusion of beggar and king for mere error, a mistake to be corrected. Neither the pathos nor the correctable error is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that the distinctions the senses drew depended on the senses, so a dimming eye lets them come loose and reveals how made they were; that the beggar and the king, the grammar of rank, were laid over the world rather than written into it, and look the same from the fog because under the costume they always were; and that Mr. Spitzer’s permanent caution is the Witness’s way of moving among people whose rank he will not force, holding open the question of who stands before him. The failing eye sees the categories are made, which the clear eye is too clear to see. Mr. Spitzer goes dim into that sight, and the chapter treats the fog as a kind of vision.

For Reflection

How many of the differences one sees so clearly are supplied by the seeing rather than found in the world?

If a clouded eye cannot tell the king from the beggar, what was keeping them apart?

Is there a sight that comes only when the ordinary, confident kind begins to fail?
