Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 82

← Chapter 81 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Woman's Experiences →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

The book ends with Vera unable to confess her losses to Esther, and the illusion of the individual and the dissolving boundary between life and death close it together. She loves "an older love than memory" and mourns "three deaths," and the losses she cannot share, the mother, the ancestral home, the guide, are the grief the book has run on since its first page, a mourning with no bottom and no witness. Her long meditation on the deaf and the blind, the ear that is "dead," is invisibility and erasure gathered for the last time, the severed sense the book has used from the deaf villagers of Chapter 4 to the mute of Chapter 61, women and marginal figures cut off and unheard.

Catherine dies at last, a "beautiful lady sleeping in her swan boat," walking "as if through water" on Mr. Spitzer's arm, and her death is the boundary between life and death the book held open for her since Chapter 2 finally, quietly closing. Mr. Spitzer confesses that he is Peron and not Joachim, that his love for Catherine was hidden all along, and the confession collapses the two brothers the book kept doubling into one man, the "old bride" and the "old bridegroom," the crossing point and the illusion of the individual resolved in a single figure who was never quite two and never quite one.

The storm takes Catherine's property and the house with it, the golden turrets swept into the sea, and the old clam-digger, Spitzer's valet, disappears into the sand, mourned by Mr. Spitzer more than Catherine is, the marginal figure valued at last as the book values all its erased. The clam-digger buried in sand, present and unaccounted for, is the book's practice with the erased performed one final time, the vanished kept at the threshold, and the question of whether physical survival without vitality is survival at all is the book's own question about what it means to be kept and not quite alive.

Esther appears again in her old waterproof, unable to swim, rejecting Vera's illusions about the sea, and her groundedness against Vera's mysticism is the practical woman set against the dreaming one, the two modes the book has run in parallel from Miss MacIntosh to Catherine. In the next room a man addresses a "silent woman," insisting that "Man loves a silent woman," and his loudness against her silence is the silencing of women stated plainly at the book's end, the enforced quiet the whole novel has been listening past, the unheard song still playing for anyone deaf to it.

The man Vera comes to love is stone deaf, hearing "through his eyes and his fingers," awakened to a silent world he fills with an inner life, and his alternative way of knowing is the book crediting the severed sense as a mode of perception rather than only a wound, the deaf man who hears what the hearing cannot. His recognition of shared suffering across race and gender, Vera's vision of lost pearls and clouds merging earth and sky, of marriage and community and love shared with nature and human beings, gather the illusion of the individual into its final affirmation, every heart the other heart made not a loss now but a belonging. Esther Longtree closes the book as the "mother of the stillborn," her handwritten note misspelled and human, "Owt to luntsch. bee bak in a whale," the erased woman leaving her mark on the record at last, imperfect and present, and the novel ends where it began, in a country where the living and the dead, the real and the dreamed, the self and the other are one, and every woman it kept at the threshold is still there, still enduring, still refusing to disappear.
