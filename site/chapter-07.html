Summary

Through rain and flood, Moses spots the wheelless frame of old Doc’s Model T by the road. He imagines the doctor tending patients who are ghosts or were never born. He mocks the old man and is moved by him, but does not stop. Inside the bus, Madge tells Homer the dark stories of their hometown. Josh and Rudolph Hathaway were brothers, and both are dead. Gertrude, who married first one and then the other, is accused by Madge of killing them both, in a murky story of a locked door and a fire. Musidora, Gertrude’s simple-minded sister, was married off to an old man and has too many children, who play in the graveyard. Old Josh burned to death in a coat closet locked from the outside. Homer wants to think the best of everyone. Madge brings the talk back to Jacqueline, and Homer says he is hungry.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a vivid, poetic description of a rain-drenched, misty landscape, setting a somber, almost surreal tone. The bus-driver, Moses, encounters Old Doc’s wrecked, skeletal Ford chassis stranded in a flooded road, illuminated faintly like a firefly or consciousness itself. The driver muses whether what he sees is real or a dream, questioning reality, life, and death, and Old Doc’s tireless but seemingly futile attempts to “start a spark of life.” This passage metaphorically explores mortality, transition, and the persistence of hope or duty even when all seems lost. The imagery of the broken-down car, the flooded road, and the “angels chattering in the tree-tops” suggests a liminal space between life and death, reality and imagination.
  • The bus-driver’s monologue about Old Doc reveals him as a tragic, almost mythic figure: an old doctor who never refuses a call, even when it seems he has lost his faculties, his vehicle, and perhaps his sanity. The doctor’s relentless, futile journeys on icy roads with no proper equipment symbolize stubborn persistence in the face of inevitable decline and death. The narrative illustrates how Old Doc is both mocked and respected, seen as a fool by some but possessing a deeper, perhaps spiritual role in a world where birth and death are blurred or denied. This passage touches on themes of duty, madness, the passage of time, and the elusive boundary between life and death.
  • Moses debates whether to help Old Doc or leave him to his fate, expressing frustration and resignation. He speculates on Old Doc’s patients described as shades, ghosts, or figures who may never have been born or are long dead. Moses also reflects on the futility of Old Doc’s efforts. This passage highlights human helplessness in the face of death and the limits of care or intervention. The bus-driver’s inner conflict personifies the tension between compassion and self-preservation.
  • A shift from the spectral and metaphysical to the more earthly unfolds in the conversation between Homer Capehorn and Madge. They discuss the recent death of Old Josh, a local figure who ran a saloon and who died under suspicious circumstances. Madge accuses Gertrude, Josh’s wife (and widow of his brother), of having killed both brothers, linking the tragedy to a dark family history of violence and secrets. Homer is skeptical, representing denial or disbelief, while Madge embodies a grim acceptance of harsh truths. This passage explores themes of murder, betrayal, community gossip, and the difficulty of uncovering truth in a close-knit but troubled town.
  • Madge tells a haunting story about the Tidings family, focusing on two sisters, Gertrude and Musidora, and the legacy of violence and neglect they endure. Musidora is described as a tragic, possibly mentally ill figure who drags a baby carriage and lives in poverty and squalor, while Gertrude is implicated in violent acts. Their mother is said to have killed one child and sold Musidora to an old man who is both her husband and the father of many children. The narrative paints a bleak picture of generational suffering, broken families, and societal neglect, interwoven with supernatural or symbolic elements like the pale, moth-like children playing in a graveyard. This passage deepens the themes of innocence lost, cyclical violence, and the marginalization of the vulnerable.
  • Towards the end, Madge and Homer discuss the fate of Musidora’s children and the complexities of love, care, and survival in harsh conditions. The dialogue reflects on what constitutes sorrow and happiness in such a bleak existence and the role of the community or authorities in intervening. The mention of the age of twelve as the “age of wisdom” and references to biblical figures add a layer of symbolic meaning, suggesting a loss of innocence and an inevitable confrontation with harsh realities.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • Moses lists everything old Doc’s car lacks: “No steering wheel, no tires, no windshield, no motor.” What is left when everything is missing?
  • “Keys never turn by themselves. Somebody always turns them.” “Keys do turn by themselves.” Which voice does the chapter trust?
  • Moses recites a “broken jeremiad” of names and stones. What is he mourning?

Characters

  • Old Doc has “never refused a call yet,” even when no one calls. Is he a fool or a saint?
  • Madge says, “There are some things you know better through never having been there.” How does she know what she knows?
  • Musidora “never speaks.” What does the town see when it looks at her?

Connections

  • In Chapter 6 Madge drove her father out to drown, and here Gertrude is accused of locking Josh in to burn. Why do Madge’s stories so often accuse women of killing men?
  • The skull on Mrs. Tidings’s mantel and Gertrude marrying her husband’s brother both come from Hamlet. What does the town of Madge’s stories share with Elsinore?
  • In Chapter 5 old Doc practiced “only in his crazy dreams,” and here his patients are “shades.” How has his practice changed?

Reflection

  • “What is sorrow? Is it happiness?” Can Musidora be happy if she does not know she should be sad?
  • Homer says, “You ought not to say anything you cannot prove.” Madge answers that a world needing proof would be silent. Who is right?
  • “Nobody cares. Nobody could prove a thing.” What does a town owe its most helpless members?

Research: Characters

Justice O’Leary (Old Doc)

  • Is described as an enigmatic figure who seems to exist between life and death. He is the former town doctor, now retired.
  • Drives an old, broken-down vehicle with no wheels or steering wheel, symbolizing a loss of control or connection to the physical world.
  • Represents persistence and duty, always answering calls to help, even when no one is calling, suggesting a supernatural or symbolic role as a “doctor” for the dying or unborn.
  • Plays a violin and uses a carriage lantern, reinforcing his ghostly or timeless nature.
  • His practice is described as increasingly mystical, with many patients “as many as the stars.”

Moses Hunnecker (The Bus Driver)

  • Acts as a skeptical observer, rationalizing Old Doc’s strange behavior.
  • Reflects on Old Doc’s past and present, doubts his sanity, and worries about his own survival.
  • Represents the worldly, pragmatic perspective contrasted with Old Doc’s mystical one.

Madge Capehorn

  • A woman who provides a dark, detailed account of local tragedies and suspicions.
  • Reveals complex family histories involving death, murder, and social decay.
  • Portrays a harsh view of morality and survival in a tough town, accusing Gertrude of murder and describing Musidora’s tragic life.

Homer Capehorn

  • Madge’s husband, often skeptical and somewhat detached.
  • Exhibits a more hopeful or resigned attitude toward death and life’s hardships, believing in an afterlife and the persistence of spirits.
  • Struggles to accept Madge’s harsher truths and prefers rational explanations.

Gertrude Hathaway (Tidings)

  • A woman accused by Madge of killing two brothers (Rudolph and Josh).
  • Described as a drinker and a failing housekeeper, living in a state of decay.
  • Twice widowed, and proud of it: “I’ll still be Mrs. Hathaway.”

Musidora (Tidings)

  • Gertrude’s sister, portrayed as mentally impaired or “peculiar.”
  • Lives in poverty and neglect with many children, exhibiting signs of mental and physical deterioration.
  • Symbolizes innocence lost and the cyclical nature of suffering.

Josh and Rudolph Hathaway

  • Brothers who stopped speaking when Gertrude married Rudolph. Rudolph owned the bar and never paid Josh. After Rudolph’s death Josh married the widow and burned to death in a locked closet among his brother’s coats.

Mrs. Tidings

  • Mother of Gertrude and Musidora, said to have killed her husband and the baby she thought “didn’t have its right mind,” and to have sold Musidora for sixteen dollars and a new roof.

Musidora’s Husband

  • A sixty-five-year-old W.P.A. worker who could have been her father.

Jacqueline White

  • At the end Madge reverses herself and says Jacqueline “will see another winter’s snow, another dawn.”

Research: Themes

Death and Transition

See: Death and Mortality

  • Death is depicted as a gradual, inevitable process akin to fading light or the slow recognition of loss.
  • Old Doc’s role as a doctor between worlds symbolizes the liminal space between life and death.
  • The community’s attitudes toward death reflect denial, suspicion, and sorrow.

Decay and Ruin

See: Decay and Ruin

  • Physical decay (flooded roads, broken vehicles, ruined houses) mirrors moral and social decay in the community.
  • The ruined chassis of Old Doc’s car symbolizes a body or life broken down but still carrying a faint spark of consciousness.

Mystery and Suspicion

See: Faith, God, and the Supernatural

  • The deaths of Josh and his brother Rudolph are shrouded in mystery and accusations of murder, highlighting themes of betrayal and concealed violence.
  • The locked door and fire incident suggest murder but remain unproven, emphasizing distrust and ambiguity.

Family and Generational Trauma

See: Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • The Tidings family represents cycles of violence, neglect, and mental illness passed through generations.
  • Musidora’s tragic life and Madge’s narrative of her children illustrate the impact of social neglect and inherited suffering.

Reality vs. Imagination

See: Illusion and Reality · Dreams and Imagination

  • The bus driver’s rational skepticism contrasts with Old Doc’s mystical reality, blurring the line between dream and waking life.
  • Madge and Homer’s differing views represent conflicting ways of understanding harsh truths.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Old Doc’s Vehicle

  • A Model T without wheels or engine parts symbolizes a journey without direction, life without vitality, or a soul trapped between worlds.

The Locked Door and Fire

See: Keys, Doors, and Locks · Fire

  • Symbolizes inescapable fate, hidden crimes, and the destructive power of secrets.

The Graveyard

See: Weddings and Funerals

  • A place of refuge and sustenance for Musidora and her children, representing the blurred line between life and death. One of the last public places where people can gather.

Violin and Lantern

See: Lamps, Candles, and Light · Music and Instruments

  • Old Doc’s violin (sometimes broken) and smoky lantern symbolize fading life, memory, and guidance in darkness.

Feathers and Wings

See: Birds

  • The fragile boundary between life and death, the ephemeral nature of existence, spiritual transition, decay, and the vulnerability of identity and innocence. They create an atmosphere of haunting delicacy and the inevitability of loss and change.

Research: Resources

  • “the mackerel sky”: Cirrocumulus clouds rippled like fish scales, from the old rhyme “Mackerel sky and mares’ tails make lofty ships carry low sails,” a sign of rain.
  • “Transition seemed the only permanence”: Heraclitus taught that everything flows and that change is the only constant. See Heraclitus and Flux.
  • “windows of torn isinglass”: Isinglass, sheets of mica, made the side curtains of early cars.
  • “Am I seeing with my eyes?”: This reflects the phenomenological tradition of bodily awareness. Moses’s questions are phenomenology’s: what the senses and the body give to consciousness. Vera asked the same in Chapter 1, when the world seemed “the conspiracy of memories and dreams floating without purpose” and “The individual is the one illusion.” See Phenomenology.
  • “You’ve lost your fifth wheel”: A fifth wheel is something useless.
  • “There’s a new fool born every minute, and an old fool never dies”: “There’s a sucker born every minute” is attributed to P. T. Barnum, a famous American showman, but there is no strong evidence to support this. Some say that it originated with David Hannum, a Syracuse banker with a share in the Cardiff Giant (1869), a “fake giant,” who was frustrated by the people flocking to see Barnum’s copy of it. There have been variations of this saying in print as early as 1806.
  • “Lucifer and all the angels of creation, the brightest that ever fell”: “How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!” (Isaiah 14:12). See The Bible.
  • “he would need hot water and clean towels”: Semmelweis showed in 1847 that clean hands saved mothers from childbed fever, and Lister brought antiseptic surgery in 1867.
  • “take a headache powder”: Headache powders of acetanilide or aspirin were sold everywhere, and acetanilide poisoned many who took too much.
  • “He said it was Mozart”: Mozart was a prolific and influential composer of the Classical period. Despite his short life, his rapid pace of composition and proficiency from an early age resulted in more than 800 works representing virtually every Western classical genre of his time. Mozart died at thirty-five in 1791 while writing his Requiem. See Music and Composers.
  • “if it was Napoleon Bonaparte being born or George Washington’s mother”: Washington’s mother was Mary Ball Washington, and legend says Napoleon was born on a rug woven with scenes from the Iliad.
  • “the fringe is frozen on the surrey top”: “The Surrey with the Fringe on Top,” from Oklahoma! (1943). See Music and Composers.
  • “old angel Gabriel blowing his tin horn”: Gabriel is an archangel in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. Gabriel announces the births of John the Baptist and of Jesus (Luke 1:19, 26), fit company for a doctor of births. Tradition gave him the last trumpet. See Angels and Archangels.
  • “A fool, Lear in the storm”: In Shakespeare’s King Lear, the “storm” is both a physical and metaphorical force. Lear is exposed to a violent natural storm on the heath, symbolizing his internal turmoil and the chaos of his kingdom after he disowns his loyal supporters and his loving daughter Cordelia. This exposure forces him to confront his own mortality and the suffering of others, leading to a shift in his perspective and, ultimately, a descent into madness. It is a metaphorical description that situates old Doc in the tradition of tragic figures overwhelmed by chaos, emphasizing themes of madness, vulnerability, and existential struggle that run through the novel. See Shakespeare and Madness and Sanity.
  • “It must have been that wounded bank-robber”: John Dillinger, from Mooresville, Indiana, robbed banks across the Midwest in 1933-1934. He had his wounds treated, and his face altered, by doctors who kept quiet. See Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest.
  • “thinking they had been raped, even by their old husbands”: Nebraska first struck the marital exemption from its rape law in 1976, and in 1978 John Rideout of Oregon became the first husband tried for raping his wife while they lived together. By 1993 marital rape was a crime in all fifty states, although some states still treat it very differently than non-marital rape.
  • “Crazier than fifty mad men playing at badminton in an insane asylum”: Despite the shared space and activity, each player struggles individually while staff, guards, and others look on for their amusement. The phrase underscores the sense of profound disorientation and madness that pervades the novel, particularly embodied in old Doc, caught between life and death, reality and dream. See Madness and Sanity.
  • “What said the night watchman to the owl? What said the owl?”: The question echoes Isaiah 21:11, “Watchman, what of the night?” The phrase suggests a silent, symbolic exchange reflecting the novel’s preoccupation with mystery, the unknown, and the interplay between human perception and the hidden truths of the night.
  • “Solomon, Bathsheba! What can I do?”: Moses calls on the wise king and his mother. See The Bible.
    • Solomon was a wealthy and wise king of Israel.
    • Bathsheba was the mother of Solomon. She was the wife of Uriah the Hittite until David, Solomon’s father, saw her bathing and wanted her. David sent her husband to the front during a war in the hopes that he would be killed.
  • “Reuben, Gad, Manasseh, Jahaziel, Eliezer, Luke, Dan, Saul”: Moses recites names the way the Bible’s genealogies do, “begetting, begetting, begetting.” See The Bible.
    • Reuben was the eldest son of Jacob and Leah.
    • Gad was the first son of Jacob and Zilpah.
    • Manasseh was the eldest son of Joseph and the Egyptian Asenath.
    • Jahaziel is the name of five characters in the Hebrew Bible.
    • Eliezer was the name of three individuals in the Bible.
    • Luke, traditionally the author of the Gospel of Luke and Acts, is “the beloved physician” (Colossians 4:14).
    • Dan was the fifth son of Jacob and first son of Bilhah.
    • Saul was the first king of Israel.
  • “in a broken jeremiad for a watery mile”: A jeremiad is a long literary work, usually in prose but sometimes in verse, in which the author bitterly laments the state of society and its morals in a serious tone of sustained invective, and always contains a prophecy of society’s imminent downfall. The word comes from the prophet Jeremiah, and the American jeremiad was the Puritan sermon lamenting a fallen people.
  • “the children of Ham”: Ham was a son of Noah. Noah’s curse fell on Ham’s son Canaan (Genesis 9:25), and defenders of slavery twisted it into the so-called curse of Ham to justify enslaving Africans. It has been used as the origin of the “cursed race” or “Negroes” with regard to white supremacy in the U.S. (and elsewhere). See The Bible.
  • “Old Jesse Ferguson, John Simon, William Williams, Hannegan, Flint, Stein”: Each of these names appears only once in the whole novel, here, and none belongs to a character anywhere else. Elsewhere “Stein” is only part of another word, such as Steinkirk, “flint” is the mineral, and “buck” is a bucket or a buckle. The next sentence gives the key: “It’s a stone without hands. It’s a headstone, a corner stone, a stone of stumbling.” Stein is German for stone, flint is a stone, and Skinflint carries flint inside it, so the list closes on stones and opens into a litany of headstones for men “all gone over the hill.” The names point outward to real people and also stand as the stones of a graveyard. With the biblical names on one side and the titles of Christ on the other, a few modern writers are tucked into a sacred roll call of the dead, given the same weight as the tribes of Israel.
    • Stein: Gertrude Stein (1874-1946), the American modernist whose Paris salon drew Picasso, Matisse, Hemingway, and Fitzgerald. She shares a first name with the chapter’s Gertrude.
    • William Williams: William Carlos Williams (1883-1963), the poet with his middle name dropped. He was also a doctor in Rutherford, New Jersey, fit company for old Doc.
    • Flint: F. S. Flint (1885-1960), the English Imagist poet who ran with Ezra Pound and H.D. With Stein and Williams he makes a tight modernist knot, three writers of the same moment buried together in the driver’s chant.
    • Hannegan: The name has a strong Indiana ring, worth noticing in a Hoosier writer who spent decades on a life of Eugene Debs. Edward A. Hannegan, an Indiana congressman and senator, killed his brother-in-law in a drunken rage and was acquitted.
    • Red Mayhew: This may reach back to Henry Mayhew, whose London Labour and the London Poor (1851) set down the lives of the city’s poor, with “Red” tinting him toward the political.
    • Buck: This could be Pearl S. Buck, author of The Good Earth (1931) and winner of the 1938 Nobel Prize. A buck is also a dollar.
    • John Simon: This could be the critic John Simon (1925-2019).
    • Ferguson: Too many candidates exist to settle on one, among them the preacher and Spiritualist Jesse Babcock Ferguson (1819-1870).
    • Skinflint: Young seems to play off Flint, a pun that feeds the stone motif. A skinflint is also a miser.
    • Tim Dorey: No source has been found for “old Tim Dorey,” which is itself worth noting.
  • “It’s a stone without hands”: Daniel 2:34 reads, “a stone was cut out without hands, which smote the image upon his feet that were of iron and clay,” the same image as Chapter 6’s “feet of clay.” Moses strings together the Bible’s stones, after the names Flint and Stein, German for stone. This phrase contributes to the novel’s broader themes of mortality, helplessness, and the enigmatic nature of existence, where individuals are often caught in forces beyond their control, reduced to passive elements like stones or shadows without the means to intervene. See The Bible.
    • In the Bible, “headstone” primarily refers to the “principal stone in a foundation,” or the cornerstone, signifying strength, stability, and alignment. Specifically, it’s often associated with Jesus Christ, who is described as the cornerstone upon which believers are built. In a broader sense, headstones can also refer to memorial markers at graves, symbolizing remembrance and faith. (Psalm 118:22; Acts 4:11; 1 Peter 2:7; Genesis 28:18-22; Joshua 4:1-8).
    • In the Bible, the cornerstone represents Jesus Christ, serving as the foundational and essential element for the Christian faith, both in the literal building and the spiritual “house” of God. Jesus is described as the cornerstone, the first stone laid in the foundation, which defines the structure and alignment of the entire building. He is also the “chief cornerstone,” signifying his primary and ultimate role. (Psalm 118:22; Matthew 21:42; Ephesians 2:20; 1 Peter 2:6).
    • In the Bible, a “stone of stumbling” metaphorically refers to an obstacle or something that causes people to fall or fail in their spiritual journey. It often signifies a hindrance to faith or understanding, specifically related to the person of Jesus Christ. (Isaiah 8:14; Romans 9:33; 1 Peter 2:8; Matthew 21:43-44; Luke 17:1).
    • The “rock of offense” is a biblical phrase, which refers to Jesus Christ as a stone of stumbling and a rock of offense to those who reject him. The idea is that some will stumble over Christ, while others will be saved by believing in him. (Romans 9:33; Isaiah 8:14; 1 Peter 2:8).
    • In the Bible, “living stones” primarily refers to the concept of believers in Christianity. Jesus is also called the “living stone,” and believers are described as being built together with him into a “spiritual house” or temple. This metaphor emphasizes the idea that believers are part of a living, spiritual community where each person contributes to the whole. (1 Peter 2:4-5).
    • The “tried stone” refers to the foundation laid by God in Zion, which is later identified as Jesus Christ in the New Testament. This stone is described as “a precious cornerstone, a sure foundation.” The term “tried” suggests that this stone has been tested and approved, signifying its strength and reliability as a foundation for faith and salvation. (Isaiah 28:16).
  • “He searcheth out stones in the darkness”: Job 28:3 speaks of “the stones of darkness, and the shadow of death.” This phrase can be interpreted as reflecting the struggle to find meaning, certainty, or “stones” of reality when surrounded by the unknown, illusions, or the intangible nature of life and death. It is part of the larger motif of searching for stability, truth, or guidance in a world filled with shifting shadows, dreamlike states, and elusive realities. See The Book of Job.
  • “The stork knoweth her times”: “Yea, the stork in the heaven knoweth her appointed times” (Jeremiah 8:7). It symbolizes the natural, inevitable rhythms of life and birth that persist despite human resistance or denial, and it resonates with the novel’s broader themes concerning the tension between illusion and reality, the passage of time, and the cycles of life and death. Hans Christian Andersen’s “The Storks” (1838) helped spread the story that storks bring babies, a fit verse for a doctor of births. See The Bible and Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “The footman, the forerunner”: Moses recites the Bible’s titles for Christ and his servants: the forerunner (Hebrews 6:20), the mediator (1 Timothy 2:5), the daysman (Job 9:33), the interpreter (Job 33:23), the advocate (1 John 2:1). See The Bible.
    • Footman is a servant or a soldier in the infantry. Forerunner is a sign or warning of something to come. “If thou hast run with the footmen, and they have wearied thee” (Jeremiah 12:5).
    • Mediator is someone who acts as an intermediary, a go-between, or a peacemaker between two or more parties, particularly between God and humanity. Jesus Christ is the ultimate mediator, reconciling humanity with God through his sacrifice and subsequent exaltation. (Galatians 3:19; 1 Timothy 2:5; Hebrews 8:6, 9:15, 12:24).
    • Daysman is an arbiter, umpire, or day laborer.
    • Intercessor is someone who intervenes on another’s behalf, especially by prayer.
    • The keeper of the seals is the person who is entitled to keep and authorize use of the great seal of a country. In Revelation only the Lamb is worthy to open the book sealed with seven seals (Revelation 5:1-5).
    • The strayed sheep may refer to the Parable of the Lost Sheep where a shepherd leaves his flock of ninety-nine sheep to find the one that is lost (Luke 15:4-7).
    • The unjust steward may refer to the Parable of the Unjust Steward who is about to be fired and seeks favor with his master’s debtors by canceling some of their debts (Luke 16:1-8).
    • The wedding gown may refer to the guest without a wedding garment in the Parable of the Wedding Feast (Matthew 22:11-12).
    • The candlestick may refer to “Neither do men light a candle, and put it under a bushel, but on a candlestick” (Matthew 5:15).
  • “Jack, Jack, be quick”: The nursery rhyme “Jack be nimble, Jack be quick, / Jack jump over the candlestick” follows straight from Moses’s candlestick. See the Chapter 6 resources.
  • “It’s my laburnum”: Laburnum is also known as the Golden Chain Tree. This moment captures the bus-driver’s struggle to maintain control and connection to life despite his deteriorating condition and the hallucinatory nature of his environment. It is an expression of his clinging to what is real and vital for him in a world that is otherwise disorienting and filled with ghosts, illusions, and decay. Every part of the laburnum is poisonous, especially the seeds.
  • “Shall the zebra lose its stripes?”: “Can the Ethiopian change his skin, or the leopard his spots?” (Jeremiah 13:23). See The Bible.
  • “He’s rolling in the flood”: John Milton, Paradise Lost (1.324): “To adore the conqueror, who now beholds / Cherub and seraph rolling in the flood.” The expression metaphorically conveys that old Doc is submerged in a kind of existential or spiritual flood: caught in the overwhelming currents of his own disordered mind or possibly in the waters of death itself. It suggests that Doc is drowning or engulfed by his circumstances, unable to escape his fate. The flood represents a chaotic, engulfing force that traps Doc, reflecting his mental confusion and the dissolution of his medical practice and life. See Milton.
  • “Water on his brain, bats in his belfry”: “Bats in the belfry,” for a crazy head, is American slang from around 1900.
  • “All these old cretins”: Cretinism, stunted growth and mind from a lack of iodine, was common in the Great Lakes “goiter belt” until iodized salt came to Michigan in 1924. See Madness and Sanity.
  • “Hitch your old tin lizzy on to me”: “Tin Lizzie” is another name for the Model T Ford. Henry Ford of the Ford Motor Company built this car, and it was known for its affordability and durability. Ford built it from 1908 to 1927, more than fifteen million of them.
  • “the amateur star-gazers’ association”: Amateur astronomers, many in the American Association of Variable Star Observers (1911), found new stars that sometimes proved to be old ones.
  • “A doctor buries his secrets”: Chapter 5 has “A doctor ought to bury his secrets, bury his mistakes.” See the Chapter 5 resources.
  • “pills, ointments, oils, elixirs, mercury to awaken his vital spirits”: Patent medicines promised everything, and doctors dosed patients with mercury as calomel. “Vital spirits” goes back to Galen.
    • Albumen is egg white.
    • Aqua regia (“royal water”), nitric and hydrochloric acid, is a fuming liquid that can dissolve gold and platinum.
  • “a floating kidney”: The loose kidney was a fashionable diagnosis around 1900, treated with corsets and surgery.
  • “There’s black crape across the windows”: Black crape on the door or window announced a death in the house.
  • “kicked his traces, kicked the bucket, crossed the bar”: A horse kicks over the traces when it breaks free of harness. Tennyson’s “Crossing the Bar” (1889) is the poem of dying, “Twilight and evening bell, / And after that the dark!”
  • “drink together from an old German helmet that was their loving cup”: Soldiers brought home spiked German helmets as souvenirs of the World War.
  • “They should have played taps, but they made a mistake and played reveille”: Reveille wakes the camp at dawn, and Taps ends the day and the soldier. See the Chapter 6 resources.
  • “Rah, rah, rah”: Princeton’s 1869 “Rah, rah, rah! Tiger, tiger, tiger!” was among the first college yells.
  • “go over the river to the mines and be the paymaster, or he might dig coal in the pits”: Southwestern Indiana’s coal fields employed thousands of miners, who wore carbide lamps on their caps. See the Chapter 3 resources on labor and Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest.
  • “What did he expect, marrying his brother’s wife”: Levirate marriage is the custom where the brother of a deceased man is obliged to marry his brother’s widow. Deuteronomy 25:5 commands it, and Leviticus 20:21 forbids it. Henry VIII used Leviticus to end his marriage to Catherine of Aragon, his brother’s widow. In Shakespeare’s Hamlet, Gertrude, Hamlet’s mother, marries her husband’s brother Claudius after the king’s death. Since women were completely dependent on their father, brother, or husband, they did not have much choice. In order to not be destitute they had to be attached. Even now women are poorer at retirement with less savings mainly because of missed years of income raising children. Social Security was partly founded to take care of these widows who wrote letters to FDR.
  • “killing two brothers with one stone”: Madge twists “killing two birds with one stone.”
  • “I know old Gertrude from away back”: Gertrude may be named after the character in Hamlet by Shakespeare. Both Gertrudes embody the tragic consequences of familial and marital complexities: Miss MacIntosh’s Gertrude through literal murder and madness, Shakespeare’s Gertrude through moral ambiguity and political betrayal. They both represent the fragility of women’s positions within patriarchal and violent family systems, caught between roles as wives, mothers, and individuals subjected to forces beyond their control. Their characters explore themes of guilt, innocence, and the ambiguity of human motives, complicating simplistic moral judgments. Both have a presence associated with death and decay, whether physical or psychological, underscoring the novel’s and play’s meditation on mortality. See Shakespeare.
  • “We all have to turn in our checks some time”: Poker players cash in their checks, or chips, when they leave the game, so the phrase came to mean dying. See Cards, Games, and Chance.
  • “I dreamed I was rounding Cape Horn”: Homer Capehorn’s dream is his own name. Cape Horn, at the tip of South America, was the sailors’ graveyard.
  • “no lock which could not be opened at last by the locksmith”: The proverb “Love laughs at locksmiths” comes from Shakespeare’s Venus and Adonis. See Shakespeare.
  • “She was Mrs. Hathaway, and she became Mrs. Hathaway”: Shakespeare’s wife was Anne Hathaway, a fitting married name for a Gertrude straight out of Hamlet. See Shakespeare.
  • “the door of the coat closet where old Josh was locked in was locked from the outside”: Young loved murder mysteries and had a minor in criminology, so she may have included the “locked-room mystery” when Madge gossips about Josh’s death. A locked-room murder is committed in circumstances under which it appeared impossible for the perpetrator to enter the crime scene, commit the crime, and leave undetected. The crime in question typically involves a situation whereby an intruder could not have left; for example the original literal “locked room”: a murder victim found in a windowless room locked from the inside at the time of discovery. Following other conventions of classic detective fiction, the reader is normally presented with the puzzle and all of the clues, and is encouraged to solve the mystery before the solution is revealed in a dramatic climax. The prima facie impression from a locked-room crime is that the perpetrator is a dangerous, supernatural entity capable of defying the laws of nature by walking through walls or vanishing into thin air. The need for a rational explanation for the crime is what drives the protagonist to look beyond these appearances and solve the puzzle. Poe’s “The Murders in the Rue Morgue” (1841) is the first locked-room mystery, and John Dickson Carr’s The Hollow Man (1935) gives a famous “locked-room lecture.” See American Writers.
  • “his brother’s coats with moth balls in their pockets”: Mothballs were made of naphthalene, which burns readily, and Josh struck a match among them.
  • “somebody saying it looked like the phoenix”: The phoenix burns itself and rises from its own ashes. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “pouring down hail and brimstone”: “The Lord rained upon Sodom and upon Gomorrah brimstone and fire” (Genesis 19:24). See The Bible.
  • “the headless horsemen riding with their torches”: Washington Irving’s “The Legend of Sleepy Hollow” (1820). See American Writers.
  • “the drunken sexton who used to dance a merry jig when anybody died”: Hamlet’s gravedigger sings at his work: “Has this fellow no feeling of his business, that he sings at grave-making?” (Hamlet 5.1). See Shakespeare.
  • “a hot time in the old town next Saturday night”: “A Hot Time in the Old Town” (1896) became the marching song of the Spanish-American War. See Music and Composers.
  • “There are bricks with layers of straw”: Pharaoh ordered, “Ye shall no more give the people straw to make brick” (Exodus 5:7). See The Bible.
  • “The better element wanted to close down on old Josh anyhow”: Indiana’s county-option law of 1908 let voters close saloons county by county, and the state went dry in 1918, before national Prohibition. See Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest.
  • “If it is not true, it must be false”: The three laws of logic: the law of identity states that if a statement is true, it must be true; the law of non-contradiction states that a statement cannot be both true and false at the same time; the law of the excluded middle states that a statement must be either true or false. Aristotle set out the law of non-contradiction in his Metaphysics. Homer’s “If it is not true, it must be false” is the excluded middle, and “It cannot be both true and false” is non-contradiction.
  • “because it didn’t have its right mind. It was an idiot”: Idiot, imbecile, and moron were official medical categories, ranked by the eugenicist Henry Goddard in 1910. See Madness and Sanity.
  • “It was that skull the children played with”: “Alas, poor Yorick!” Hamlet holds the jester’s skull (Hamlet 5.1). See Shakespeare.
  • “I don’t know where she got that name”: The name Musidora means “gift of the Muses.” It belongs to the bather in James Thomson’s The Seasons (1727) and to the French silent-film vamp Musidora of Les Vampires (1915).
  • “grew up with the water on her brain”: Hydrocephalus. Before shunts were developed in the 1950s, children with it were often called idiots and put away. See Madness and Sanity.
  • “She lives down there in that old house boat with the holes in the roof”: Shantyboats housed thousands of poor river families on the Ohio and Mississippi during the Depression.
  • “It’s awful the way these common people reproduce themselves”: Eugenics is an immoral and pseudoscientific theory that claims it is possible to perfect people and groups through genetics and the scientific laws of inheritance. Eugenicists used an incorrect and prejudiced understanding of the work of Charles Darwin and Gregor Mendel to support the idea of “racial improvement.” Women judged “feeble-minded,” like Musidora, were the chief targets of the sterilization laws, Indiana’s first among them (1907), and Madge asks why “the county” could not “put her away.” See the Chapter 5 resources and Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest.
  • “He’s sixty-five years old and on the W.P.A.”: The W.P.A. was the Works Progress Administration, which President Franklin D. Roosevelt established in 1935 as part of his New Deal to employ millions of job-seekers on public works projects during the severe unemployment and economic hardship of the Great Depression. Sixty-five was the retirement age the Social Security Act set in 1935. See the Chapter 5 resources.
  • “The old man bought her from her mother for sixteen dollars and a new roof”: In the 1930s many states still let girls of twelve to fourteen marry with a parent’s consent.
  • “Her children played ring-around-a-rosy”: The rhyme was first printed in 1881. The story that it describes the plague is a twentieth-century invention. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “What is sorrow? Is it happiness?”: The exploration of happiness and sorrow may refer to Schopenhauer who provides a Western interpretation of Buddhism which includes: the will to life (why people strive to keep on living no matter what) as a major driving force; it is an error to believe people exist to be happy; his solutions are to become a monk or sage and to spend as much time as possible with art and philosophy. See Schopenhauer.
  • “Little pitchers have big ears”: The proverb is in John Heywood’s collection of 1546. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “Oh, the things that parrot could tell”: Large parrots can live sixty years or more and outlive their owners.
  • “twelve, which is the age of wisdom”: Madge garbles Luke 2:42-47, where Jesus at twelve sits in the Temple among the doctors. The twelve apostles came later. In Jewish law a girl comes of age at twelve. See The Bible.
  • “She’s a pillar of the church”: Paul calls James, Peter, and John “pillars” of the church at Jerusalem (Galatians 2:9). See The Bible.
  • “whether school keeps or not”: “Not to care whether school keeps or not,” meaning not to care at all, is an American saying of the 1880s.
