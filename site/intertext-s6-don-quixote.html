Definition from research

Category: obligatory. Cervantes is named in the first pages, and his knight sets the terms for how the novel's people read the world.

Miguel de Cervantes's Don Quixote, published in two parts in 1605 and 1615, tells of a country gentleman who reads so many romances of chivalry that he mistakes windmills for giants and sets out as a knight-errant. The book laughs at him and loves him at once.

Vera reaches for him on the first page. Watching the bus-driver, she wonders, "Was he, after all, a bachelor, perhaps even some mad Don Quixote chasing windmills, a virgin spirit, nobody" (chapter 1). A page later the bus has passed a real windmill standing beside the road among other roadside buildings. Quixote's error, mistaking what one has read for what is there, becomes the novel's founding question about all its people.

The knight's shape returns in Cousin Hannah, the suffrage captain who rides through storms and mountain passes. She is "a Captain Derring-Do" to Catherine (chapter 30), "a French chevalier" in her own wishes (chapter 38), and "the Black Knight or blackamoor" to the Swiss (chapter 52). Like Quixote, she never finds what she rides for. Vera and her pony Falada form a smaller pair of rider and horse: "we ourselves were the dream, the rider and the horse" (chapter 18). Young shares Cervantes's double view. The dreamer is foolish and the dreamer is also the most human person in the room.
