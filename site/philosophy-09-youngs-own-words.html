Chapter 9. Young’s Own Words

← Previous · Philosophy of MMMD Home · Next →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Philosophy: Augustine and Christian Theology

Allusions: The Bible · The History of Opium

Chapters discussed: 14, 21, 47

Definition from research

Before the principles can be stated, it helps to notice the words Young herself returns to. A philosopher’s system can often be read in the vocabulary she cannot stop using. In Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, “dream” and its forms appear well over a thousand times, and “illusion” and its forms close to two hundred. These are not decorative words. They are the novel’s basic terms, and a few others gather around them.

Illusion, in Young’s usage, does not mean simple error. It means whatever a person lives inside and takes for solid. Catherine’s opium house is an illusion, but so is Miss MacIntosh’s common sense, so is Hannah’s certainty, and so, the novel suggests, is the separate self. Illusion is the condition of being human, not a mistake that clearer thinking could correct.

The unfinished is Young’s word for the condition of the world. God, in Miss MacIntosh’s beach theology, began what He could not finish. Mr. Spitzer’s music is never completed. Vera, seeing her nursemaid bald in the morning light, calls her “this unfinished thing, that which had no business in creation” (ch. 21).

The palimpsest is Young’s image for time and memory. A palimpsest is a manuscript scraped clean and written over, with the old writing still faintly visible beneath the new. Catherine’s world is “a palimpsest of many hieroglyphic writings which had been effaced” (ch. 14), and Mr. Spitzer keeps a “many-written palimpsest or tally sheet” of everything he has seen (ch. 47).

Deep calling to deep is Young’s phrase, borrowed from the Psalms, for the hope that souls might truly answer one another. It appears in the monist passage of the first chapter, first as a promise and then as a lack.

Other people’s mirages is the phrase for Mr. Spitzer’s hard-won tolerance. Once a person knows that everyone lives inside some illusion, including himself, the only decent response is respect.

The principles that follow are drawn from this vocabulary and tested against the trials of Part Two. They owe a debt to an earlier attempt to state a philosophy of radical immanence in plain language, whose categories fit Young’s novel remarkably well. But they are stated here as what remains after the trials, not as a frame laid over them. Each principle is something that survived the breaking of a system. None is offered as a master key.
