Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 2

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer

Catherine's chapter is where the dissolution starts in earnest. Her sleep is "watchful, wide-eyed wakefulness," a state that is neither living nor dead, and the book will keep her there for the rest of its length. This is the dissolving boundary between life and death, and it is Catherine's permanent address, not a symbol of psychological imprisonment. Her bed dressed as a sarcophagus, her powdered birthmark, her belief in her own dual selves, one living and one dead, all say the same thing: she is the book's clearest case of a person held on both sides of the line at once.

Her opium world is the fullest statement so far of real and dream as one country. The imaginary guests, the phantoms, Pope and Byron, the invisible elephant she dares Mr. Spitzer to disprove: none is treated as error. They are a place, with its own weather and rules, and Catherine lives in it. And because the guests are all, finally, versions of her, the chapter is also plural selfhood. She has scattered herself across a houseful of visitors and cannot be found in any single one.

Mr. Spitzer enters here and his first scene is the first note of his own dissolution. Catherine accuses him of not existing, and the accusation is not a woman deflating a man; it is the book beginning to thin him out, the process that ends with him transparent. Even the canary matters. Spitzer secretly replaces each dead bird with a living one so the fiction holds. That is not a man managing a woman's life. It is the book keeping a dream stitched to the real until neither can be told from the other.
