Chapter 33

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act · The Past Doesn't Go Away

The Questions

Catherine’s memory is “beautifully inaccurate,” and “accuracy would have destroyed her.” Is her sealed past a lie, or a survival?

Mr. Spitzer chooses not to correct her “surface impressions.” Is that complicity, or the recognition that some seals keep a person alive?

Everyone has a “predisposition toward illusion.” If everyone builds, what separates Catherine from the rest?

Catherine has “no respect for…the correct order of time.” What is memory once it stops obeying sequence?

Catherine’s memory is a construction, and the chapter says so with unusual tenderness: it is “always quite beautifully inaccurate, as if she knew that accuracy would have destroyed her.” Her past is built, not recalled, and the building is protective. This changes the terms the book has been working in. Catherine’s seal is not only a refusal of loss; it is a structure holding her together, and an accurate memory, the unsealed true past, would not free her but kill her. The invention here is load-bearing. Pull it out and the person comes down with it.

Which puts Mr. Spitzer’s Witness in its hardest light yet. He chooses not to “correct these surface impressions,” not to insist on the accurate past, and by now the reader knows to ask whether that is complicity in her sealing. This chapter answers, at least partly: it may be mercy. If accuracy would destroy her, then to leave her memory beautifully inaccurate is not to keep her comfortably deceived but to keep her alive. The ambiguity that ran through the earlier chapters, kindness or complicity, gains a third possibility here, that some seals are the only thing holding a person up, and the Witness who leaves them intact is neither enabling nor abandoning but sparing.

And the chapter widens the frame until Catherine is not the exception. Everyone, it says, carries a “predisposition toward illusion,” everyone lives in partial unconsciousness, builds a workable past out of a real one too painful or too chaotic to hold straight. Catherine is not a special case of illusion against a world of clear-eyed realists. She is the visible, extreme instance of what everyone does quietly. The difference between her and the rest is not that she builds and they do not; it is that her building shows, and that hers has sealed where theirs stays porous enough to function.

Her time is the sign of the sealing. She has “no respect for logical sequences, none for the relation of cause to event, none for the correct order of time.” Inside the sealed memory, sequence dissolves; the past is not a line she recalls but a space she rearranges. This is The Past Doesn't Go Away inside a single sealed mind, the layers all present at once and reorderable at will. It is what memory becomes when it stops serving accuracy and starts serving survival: not a record but a room, furnished for living in.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that condemns Catherine’s inaccurate memory as simple self-deception, and the reading that lifts her “beautifully inaccurate” building into a wisdom, illusion as the way to live. Neither the condemned liar nor the endorsed dreamer meets the chapter’s tenderness.

The footing is that Catherine’s memory is a load-bearing construction, and accuracy would not free her but destroy her, so her seal is at once her danger and the structure holding her up. Mr. Spitzer’s refusal to correct her may be neither complicity nor abandonment but mercy, sparing a person the accurate past that would kill her. Everyone shares the predisposition to build a workable past; Catherine differs only in that hers shows and hers has sealed. Some seals are load-bearing, and unsealing can kill. Whether to leave such a seal intact is not resolved into a rule, and Mr. Spitzer’s mercy stays a particular, unrepeatable judgment rather than a principle.

For Reflection

Is there a version of one’s own past one keeps because the accurate version would be unbearable?

When a person’s illusion is the only thing holding them up, is it kindness to leave it, or to take it?

If everyone builds a workable past, what makes one person’s building a danger and another’s a survival?
