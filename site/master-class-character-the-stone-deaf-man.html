The Stone Deaf Man: The One Who Heard With His Eyes

← Beatrice Fitzgerald · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Lucie Bell →

Spoiler note: This portrait is written for readers who have finished the novel. It discusses the character’s whole story, including how it ends.

Characters: Esther Longtree · Lisa Lunde · Mr. Weed · The Chicago Detective · The Stone Deaf Man · Vera Cartwheel

He is the answer the whole book has been withholding, the true witness set at last against all the false ones. Vera hears him through the wall before she sees him, a voice "gonging through the dim air" all night, singing to a woman who is not there, and when she meets him at dawn he tells her plainly, "I am a stone deaf man." His face has "the look of one who had already drowned," because "all the waters of creation had smashed against his ears, leaving him stone deaf." And here is the sentence that turns him into the counter-figure for everyone else in these pages: "He heard with his eyes." His ears, he says, "were in his finger-tips, and the light streamed from his fingers."

Everyone else in the book who has the ordinary channels open uses them to avoid seeing. Mr. Weed looks at death for a living and never meets a person. Lisa Lunde sees through people and so never sees them. The Chicago detective looks straight at a living woman and files her as a corpse. The little toy salesman witnesses a drowning world and tips his hat. The stone deaf man has lost the easy way in, and precisely because of that he attends to what no one else will. He hears "what others heard not, the fishes whispering at the bottoms of rivers." He has learned the hard attention that the fully equipped keep refusing.

He came to it through a kind of death. He went deaf at fourteen, after "a sleep of six months, a sleep so near to death that it might almost have been his death," and woke to find that "sound had died when he was sleeping," that the key made no sound in the lock, that the long white curtains blowing made none. "It took him a long time to learn to dream." Then "he became a painter, and he was also the artist of his life." This is the book's Boethius, the one stripped down to the vertical line, who lost the horizontal world of easy noise and found in the silence a whole buried country of attention. What sounds like deprivation is the opening of the deeper sense. He is the sacred ordinary made a person: the silent world, for him, is not empty but full, thick with tongues, "the silences where no speech is," and he is "in love with not awakening," in love with the world that stays still and quiet and complete.

And he shows what the book means when it says language is not where reality actually gets built. He and Vera make their love outside of speech entirely. She "spelled out my name upon my lover's hand, for surely a woman must find some way by which to talk to the silence that she loved." He is "the secret lip-reader"; "thy hand speaks to me." He tells her, "there be words of ours, not in the alphabet of the stars. Flowers spell them out on the earth, and grasses talk." When the official channel is closed, love does not stop being made. It finds the hand, the earth, the flower. An old woman on the road had told him the thing he lives by, that a cut-off white man and a cut-off black woman suffer alike "for something that be not their fault," and that "life is imaginary, and this be an imaginary world," so "dream on." His deafness is not a wall between him and the world. It is the door.

The turn for a writer is the tender one, the single figure in the set who is the maker redeemed instead of the maker indicted. The stone deaf man is what the artist is supposed to become, the one who loses the easy way in and learns to hear with the eyes, to read the hand, to attend to the fishes at the bottom of the river because the surface has gone silent. He "became a painter, and he was also the artist of his life." He misses "much foolish chatter" and catches what the chatterers never will. And Vera, who has spent the whole book among people who could not see her, marries him, the one who "could not hear me" and with whom there is nonetheless "the communication of love." He hears her because he cannot hear. The book offers him as the last correction to all its anti-witnesses, and it plans a wedding at which Esther Longtree, "for she was also our love," and all the stillborn "clapping their hands in clouds," and the flowers and the birds and the old mule are the guests. The child of that marriage, he says, "will be our ear." The future will hear what the present could not, and that, the book says, is the whole hope: to lose the easy sense and gain the hard one, and to pass the hearing forward.
