Chapter Three: The Ancestors Are in the Sediment

Part of Radical Immanence Collection, on Radical Immanence, the philosophy Coral Russell draws from the work of Marguerite Young (1908 to 1995).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter Two: The Transmission Line · Radical Immanence Collection · Chapter Four: The Not-Yet-Born →

Philosophy has two main ways of relating to the dead, and both of them are inadequate.

The first is the way of the archive. The dead are preserved in texts and traditions and cultural memory, and we relate to them through those records. We read what they wrote, we study what they built, we inherit the categories they developed and the problems they left unsolved. This is a real relationship, and it is philosophically important. But it treats the dead as sources rather than as presences. They are available to us as information. They are not available to us as weight.

The second is the way of the supernatural. Many religious and spiritual traditions hold that the dead persist in some form, that they can be addressed or appealed to, that they have interests and can be honored or offended. This is a relationship of presence rather than information, but it requires metaphysical commitments that secular philosophy cannot easily adopt, and it tends to locate the ancestors somewhere other than here, in another realm or another dimension, accessible through special practices rather than through ordinary attention to the world you are already in.

The eleventh pillar offers a third way.

The ancestors are in the sediment.

This is not a metaphor for memory or cultural inheritance, though it includes those things. It is a literal description of where the ancestors are. They are in the ground. They are in the soil that grew the food that made the body you are reading this with. They are in the water that fell as rain on the fields where their fields were, that ran into the rivers that ran into the aquifer that runs under the ground you are standing on. They are in the language you think with, which they deposited layer by layer across generations of use, shaping the grooves along which your thoughts run without your awareness or consent. They are in the grief you carry, which is partly your grief and partly the grief of everyone who held what you are holding before you were born to hold it.

The ancestors are present as deposit. Their presence is geological, not supernatural. You cannot speak to them directly. You can read them in the layers. You are made of them in the most literal sense available to secular philosophy: the ground you stand on is made of their passing through, and you are made of the ground.

This changes the relationship.

When the ancestors are in the archive, you can choose whether to consult them. When the ancestors are in the sediment, you are already in relationship with them whether you choose to be or not. The relationship is not optional. It is constitutional. The question is not whether to relate to the ancestors but whether to relate to them honestly, whether to read the sediment with the attention it deserves, whether to acknowledge the deposit rather than pretending the ground is neutral.

Pretending the ground is neutral is one of the most common and most consequential philosophical errors available. It is the error that allows a political system to treat its current arrangements as natural rather than constructed, as the way things simply are rather than the way things have been deposited by specific people with specific interests over specific centuries. The ground is never neutral. The ground is always the record of what was carried and deposited and pressed down and transformed by the weight of what came after. The ground is always the sediment of a transmission.

To read the ground honestly is to acknowledge the transmission. To acknowledge the transmission is to acknowledge the ancestors, not as supernatural presences to be appeased but as geological presences to be read. The obligation is not to their wishes, which are unknowable. It is not to their memory, which is partial and mediated. It is to the weight they deposited and that you now carry, the obligation to carry it honestly, to not flatten it into a more useful shape, to not pretend it is lighter than it is, to pass it forward with the full acknowledgment of what it is and where it came from.

This is the ancestor relationship the eleventh pillar makes available. It requires no metaphysics beyond the geological. It requires only the willingness to read the sediment honestly, which is the same willingness the ten pillars have been requiring all along, extended now into the vertical dimension.

The ancestors are beneath you. The not-yet-born are above you. You are in the middle, at the crossing point, holding the weight in both directions.

That is the structure. Now for the weight.
