Chapter 26

  • “Musica Universalis” is a metaphysical principle by Boethius that uses mathematical relationships to express ‘tones’ that could be heard by the soul. The "Musica Universalis" here symbolizes the harmonious and eternal cosmic order that underlies the apparent chaos of mortal life and death. It is depicted as a sublime, transcendent music that governs the movements of the stars and planets, reflecting a divine harmony beyond human perception. Mr. Spitzer, a semi-retired lawyer and composer of "silent music," embodies this principle as someone who is attuned to the "music of the spheres" or the eternal, silent harmonies that exist beyond earthly sounds. His compositions, unheard by living ears and played only for the dead or in the celestial realm, metaphorically represent this cosmic music. The chapter emphasizes the contrast between earthly, temporal music and this higher, immutable harmony, suggesting that the tangible world is but a fragment or shadow of a greater metaphysical reality. Catherine's opium dreams expand this theme by blurring the boundaries between the real and the illusory, the living and the dead, reflecting the ineffable nature of the universal music that ties all existence together. The "Musica Universalis" is not just an abstract concept but a living presence in her visions with images of angels harping, celestial choirs, and cosmic rhythms permeating her consciousness. The chapter suggests that this music is intertwined with suffering and mortality (the "music of the perfect silence beyond the chance of the brittle and falling stars") highlighting a metaphysical view where life, death, and cosmic order are inseparable parts of a greater whole. The "Musica Universalis" is used to articulate a metaphysical principle that all existence, from the smallest earthly details to the vast cosmos, is bound by a harmonious, eternal music. This music transcends the limitations of physical reality and human understanding, offering a vision of unity amid fragmentation and a cosmic order beneath the apparent chaos of life and death.

Chapter 35

  • While less widely known, Amanda Vajskop’s work Finding Patterns in the Chaos investigates how meaning, order, and identity can be discerned within apparent disorder, especially in contexts of trauma, fragmentation, and complex social realities. The novel is deeply invested in themes of chaos versus order, illusion versus reality. Vera’s narrative continuously navigates between dreamlike confusion and the search for meaning or “ultimate harmony.” Miss MacIntosh represents order and common sense amid the chaos of Catherine’s opium-induced visions and the fragmented lives of other characters. Vajskop’s focus on identity formation amid chaos parallels the novel’s portrayal of characters with fragmented selves: Vera’s own complex consciousness, Miss MacIntosh’s evolving physical and psychological state (including her baldness and amputated breast), and the multiple personae like Cousin Hannah and Mrs. Hogden. Vera’s act of recounting, reflecting, and seeking patterns in her experiences and the lives around her mirrors Vajskop’s idea of finding coherence in fragmentation. The novel’s richly symbolic and layered narrative demands readers engage in deciphering meaning from a kaleidoscope of images and voices. The novel’s intense focus on female embodiment, sexuality, motherhood, loss, and survival complements critical perspectives on misogyny and sexual politics.

Chapter 39

  • Karl Marx famously described religion as “the opium of the people,” suggesting that it numbs the pain of social inequality and exploitation. Lenin saw it as a way to keep the masses in a state of dependence and conformity. Religion may be used to maintain the status quo and discourage social change. This view might assert that religion offers easy answers to complex questions about the universe, life, and death. It may appeal to those who lack resources and/or education to form different perspectives. The novel suggests that religion, like opium, can be both a source of comfort and a form of escapism or a way to cope with the harshness and uncertainties of mortal life. Catherine’s opium paradise, filled with angels and ethereal visions, embodies this duality: it is a "bountiful enchantment" that extends her life beyond the physical but also confines her to a dreamlike state, disconnected from concrete reality. This resonates with the Marxist critique of religion as "the opium of the people," implying a narcotic that dulls awareness and delays confrontation with real suffering. However, Young also complicates this view by showing that such illusions, whether religious or drug-induced, can be necessary for survival amid profound existential despair. Catherine inhabits a liminal space between life and death, where the opium-induced religious visions provide a fragile structure amid chaos and loss. Miss MacIntosh’s critique highlights the need to face reality soberly and practically, warning against being seduced by illusions that reject nature and the "bare face" of existence. The novel portrays religion as a double-edged phenomenon: a kind of opium that can numb pain and offer transcendence but also one that risks imprisoning the believer in illusions. This ambivalence is embodied in the contrast between Catherine’s opium dreams and Miss MacIntosh’s grounded common sense, reflecting the complex role religion plays as both a solace and snare in human life.

Chapter 55

  • “Chinatown” U.S. Chinatowns were ethnic enclaves, primarily in the West, established in the mid-19th century by immigrants drawn by the Gold Rush and railroad jobs, but were devastated by the 1882 Chinese Exclusion Act which banned further immigration and caused populations to decline. By the early 20th century, Chinatowns were predominantly male-dominated communities centered on social societies, gambling, and opium dens, but after the 1943 Magnuson Act repealed the exclusion laws, populations began to rise again, leading to changes in the established communities.

Chapter 63

  • King Canute or Cnut was king of England, Norway, and Denmark. Miss MacIntosh invokes King Canute, who commanded the tides to stop, to illustrate the futility of "waste our lives in the vain expectation of that which might never happen". Canute, who despite being king, could not control the natural world, serves as a reminder that one must not dream of "what was not, of what would never be". He represents the limitations of human power against the forces of nature and the folly of arrogance. Catherine also mentions King Canute when she is lost in her opium-induced dreams. This further connects him with the realm of illusion and the rejection of reality that Miss MacIntosh so vehemently opposes. The story notes her mention of the "drowned pearl-diver rising to the surface, the griffin with folded wings, the centaur with its long-lashed eyelids closed," alongside her comment that James, the chauffeur, could be depended upon. Miss MacIntosh is said to be able to "do away with bodiless phantoms and monsters, with imaginary nightingales or turrets of ivory or turrets of gold or turrets of brass". King Canute, as used by Miss MacIntosh, directly contrasts with Catherine's "opium paradise." He embodies the futility of attempting to control the uncontrollable, while Vera's mother indulges in dreams that defy reality. Miss MacIntosh emphasizes that the "opium paradise was never God's way of life". The references to King Canute, particularly by Miss MacIntosh, serve as a critique of those who dwell in idealistic or unrealistic notions. He is a symbol of misplaced faith in one's ability to alter the fundamental realities of the world.

Chapter 66

  • The Tong Wars were a series of violent disputes beginning in the late 19th century among rival Chinese Tong factions centered in the Chinatowns of various American cities, in particular San Francisco. Tong wars could be triggered by a variety of inter-gang grievances, from the public besmirching of another Tong's honor, to failure to make full payment for a "slave girl", to the murder of a rival Tong member. Each Tong had salaried soldiers, known as boo how doy, who fought in Chinatown alleys and streets over the control of opium, prostitution, gambling, and territory. In San Francisco's Chinatown district, the Tong Wars lasted until 1921, with the various criminal Tongs estimated between nineteen and as many as thirty at the peak of the conflict, though the actual number is uncertain, with frequent splintering and mergers between the various Tongs. While a loose alliance, consisting of the Chinatown police, Donaldina Cameron, the courts, and the Chinese community itself tried to stem the tide of the fighting Tongs, it was the 1906 San Francisco earthquake and subsequent fires caused by the earthquake that was the death knell for the Tongs in San Francisco, as it destroyed the brothels, gambling dens, and opium houses that the criminal organizations had used for the majority of their revenue. Recovery was slow for most Tongs, so they abandoned the old Chinatown.

Chapter 71

  • The pearls are repeatedly connected to Vera's mother and her legacy. They represent the "lost estate" of her mother, something precious yet intangible that Vera inherits and wears, symbolizing the remnants of the past and the enduring connection to her mother's life and identity. The pearls are described as "real pearls which... would bring no more than cultivated pearls" in the market, suggesting a contrast between their material and symbolic value. The pearls also symbolize continuity amid loss and the passing of time. Vera’s mother believed that the pearls could not be lost or stolen, and that their number paradoxically increased with loss, implying an infinite or eternal quality beyond physical reality. This suggests a theme of immortality or persistence beyond death, where loss in one sense begets gain in another "the more pearls there were" despite losses in life. The novel often blurs the line between dream and reality, and pearls, like many other images, are part of this interplay. Pearls are associated with the ephemeral and the eternal, with illusions and truths mingling. They appear alongside motifs of opium dreams, ghosts, and phantoms, symbolizing the fragile and shifting nature of existence and memory. The pearls are linked to the theme of identity, as something that persists yet changes, much like Vera’s mother herself, who is simultaneously present and absent, real and dreamlike. The pearls become a metaphor for what remains of a person’s essence when all else has faded or been lost. The imagery of pearls dripping or cascading like tears, linked to the mother’s lifeless but radiant hands, suggests mourning and the cycle of life and death. Pearls are connected to sorrow ("the pity, pity, pity of nothing, of something"), highlighting their role as symbols of grief and the preciousness of what is gone.

Chapter 82
