Movement V. The Many Selves: The Self That Will Not Stay One

Part of Exploring Young's Poetry, a reading of The Collected Poems of Marguerite Young (2022).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Movement IV. Grief That Stays: The Weather That Does Not Lif · Exploring Young's Poetry · Movement VI. The Crossing Point: What One Generation Spends →

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

A person, in Young, is never only one person, and this movement watches the idea travel the whole length of her life, changing temperature as it goes. It begins gently, in the early "Song of Two," where a beloved's words "Dip softly, softly through my days," the other already lodged inside the self. In the early "Transition" a dropped mirror breaks and "one bluebird" becomes "a thousand bluebirds," the single view shattered into multiplicity. Prismatic Ground gives the warm version, "Like Secret Roots," whose speaker yields until "I must be both sky and field," and "Minnows and a Monster," where the fisher becomes his catch. The Middle Years push it cosmic in "Who is limited?", the lovers "absorbed by leaves." And the late "Of Deed, Love, Death" turns it to dread, the soul "dividing like an annelid" into an endless regress of selves. The point is not that these people are broken. It is that a single fixed identity was always the fiction, and the crowd inside a person is the ordinary truth of being alive. The novel runs on this current, a consciousness that pours through every character until they can no longer be told cleanly apart.

"Song of Two"

"Like wings which dip into a cloud ... Dip softly, softly through my days / The words which you have spoken." The beloved's words move through the speaker's days like something living inside them, the other person lodged in the self. It is the small early cousin of "Like Secret Roots," the boundary of the self already porous to what it loves.

"Transition"

A dropped looking glass breaks and "all things multiply": "one bluebird drifts above / A thousand pools of glass" until "I watch / A thousand bluebirds pass." The single view shatters into multiplicity and the speaker is "lost forever" in the "breaking silver light." The one becomes many, Not One Self arriving early through a broken mirror, with the unfixable real of Dubious Ground close behind it.

"Like Secret Roots."

The beloved works "under my brow" like roots beneath a field until the speaker yields and "must be both sky and field." The self stops being one thing and becomes the whole landscape it loves. Not One Self here is not disintegration but expansion, identity opening past its own edge.

"Minnows and a Monster."

The one who casts "delicate meshes" to catch songs ends up merged with the "eerie being" hauled from the water, fisher and catch changing places. The self dissolves into what it went out to seize. To grasp the other is to become it.

"The White Rat."

The "white rat" is both the self and a trapped thing in "a theatre of emptiness," body and soul split and set against each other. The poem makes the divided self its whole stage. Not One Self, here, is a captivity as much as a truth.

"Ventriloquist: The Coffee Hour."

A speaker throws his voice into a wooden boy who is "impassive" and heartless yet carries the speaker's life, self and dummy talking across a single throat. The poem literalizes the crowd inside a person. In the novel the same divided selves live in Mr. Spitzer and Miss MacIntosh, and in Vera's struggle to tell dream from waking.

"Night Watches."

"Meteors falling, broken from dolorous stars" scatter the self into "prisoned valleys," fragments cast down and confined. The poem sets the divided self against itself in cosmic dark. Exile and transformation are two names for the same plural condition.

"Who is limited?"

Two lovers imagine "none between us and the most hypothetical / Stars," until "We are absorbed by leaves," their "wedding ring ... mirrored entire on space." The self expands past every limit into the universe it touches, the boundary erased upward and outward. It is "Like Secret Roots" grown cosmic, Not One Self reaching the stars and the leaves at once.

"Of Deed, Love, Death"

"The soul dividing like an annelid ... one soul all souls ... dividing," "everything twice and all souls repeated," "Infinities of bodies swinging from infinities of trees in regress." Not One Self reaches its darkest, most vertiginous form, the self splitting without end into a terror of repetition. What "Like Secret Roots" felt as expansion the late poem feels as endless division, the same principle turned to dread.
