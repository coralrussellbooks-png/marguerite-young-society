Chapter 61: The Frog in His Throat

← Chapter 60 · Guide Home · Chapter 62 →

Research notes: Chapter 61

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Themes: Duality and Doubles

Symbols: Mirrors and Reflections · Silence and Deafness

Philosophy: Heraclitus and Flux · Kant and Hegel · Marguerite Young on Her Craft · Plato and Platonism

Allusions: American Writers · Deafness and Sign Language · Music and Composers

There is an old idiom, "a frog in his throat", that means the inability to speak, especially from overwhelming emotion. Young knows this. Chapter 61 is built on it, very deliberately, and by the end of the chapter she has made it into something that will stay with you.

What actually happens

Mr. Spitzer encounters an old mute who has trained a frog to speak for him. The frog lives in the mute's mouth. It has learned to croak human words, including, we are told, the Lord's Prayer. The mute is his voice. The frog is the mute's voice. The chain of substitution goes all the way down.

When Mr. Spitzer meets him, the frog has died.

Together, the two men (a near-deaf composer who makes music no one hears, and a mute who has just lost the only voice he ever had) walk to an upland meadow scattered with wildflowers and bury the frog. Then they walk back. The way back, Mr. Spitzer notices, seems shorter than the way there.

That is all that externally happens. Everything else is what Mr. Spitzer thinks while it is happening.

The mute as mirror

By this point in the novel, you know Mr. Spitzer well: a man whose music is inaudible, who exists at the edges of perception, who cannot always “tell the living from the dead.” The old mute is his perfect double. Here is someone for whom silence is not metaphor but reality: a man who has never been able to hear the world's sounds, who exists in a country whose language he cannot access, a stranger in every room he enters.

Mr. Spitzer watches the mute's hands moving through their complex sign language (crosses, circles, directions, shapes that mean many things at once or nothing that can be pinned down) and tries to interpret them. He cannot, entirely. But he doesn't need to. He understands the essential thing: this man is grieving, and the grief has no outlet, and it is immense.

He wonders, as he walks with the mute toward the meadow, whether the mute is his ideal audience. His music, after all, is inaudible. A deaf man who cannot hear it is no different, in practical terms, from anyone else. And yet the mute is present. He is there, walking beside him. Sometimes presence is the thing.

The meadow

Young gives the frog's burial meadow one of the most extraordinary passages in the novel: a long inventory of wildflowers, grasses, and herbs, each one carrying its own symbolism. Milkwort and moonwort, nightshade and heliotrope, frog lilies and star grass and wild rambler roses and moon vines. The meadow is a threshold, and Young populates it accordingly: plants associated with nurturing and plants associated with poison, plants that turn toward the sun and plants that thrive in shadow, plants named for the creatures of water and plants named for the stars.

You don't need to look up every plant, though the "For the Curious" section at the end touches on a few, but do notice what Young is doing: she is making the act of burial into something cosmic. The frog was small. Its grave is surrounded by nature's symbolic vocabulary. This is not disproportionate. Young believes that everything small contains everything large.

Mr. Spitzer drops a red rose into the grave before the earth covers it. The red rose: immortality, resurrection, love that persists. For a frog.

The idiom made literal

The mute's frog was in his throat (literally, the animal living in his mouth) and also in the older, emotional sense: it was the block between silence and speech, between feeling and expression. Now the frog is dead, and the mute is left with nothing but his hands. His frog in his throat is gone. He cannot even croak the Lord's Prayer.

Young is doing something very quiet and very devastating here. The idiom "a frog in my throat" describes a temporary condition: a moment when feeling overwhelms speech. For the mute, this was his permanent condition, his whole life. And the frog that literalized it, the creature that bridged his silence and the world of sound, is in the ground under the wildflowers.

"No being but becoming"

While walking back from the burial, Mr. Spitzer finds himself meditating on a line that cuts to the heart of everything Young has been saying across the novel: there is no being but becoming. The wheel turns, and that is all. Images flow into images, like water into water, without stopping anywhere, without fixing into a stable self.

This is not nihilism. It is the opposite of nihilism. It is the claim that existence is not a state you can arrive at and possess, but a process you are always inside of. There is no moment when you have finally become yourself. There is only the next moment, and the one after that, and the frog lilies growing over the grave.

The airplane

Through most of this novel, time has been soft and uncertain; you might be in the 1900s, or earlier, or somewhere outside of calendar time altogether. In Chapter 61, Mr. Spitzer looks up at what he thinks is a moving star and realizes it is an airplane light seen through clouds.

Young drops this quietly, almost without emphasis. But it is the novel's first clear glimpse of the modern world. The airplane is there, overhead, moving through the same sky where the wild ducks flew in Chapter 60 and the moose's antlers reached toward the Pleiades. The mystical and the contemporary inhabit the same clouds. They always did.

What to hold onto

Two men in a flower-strewn meadow, burying a frog. One cannot hear. The other makes music no one hears. Between them, they hold a red rose and everything that has been lost. The way back will feel shorter.
