Chapter 16: Falada

On Chapter 16 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of A Guide to Reading Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 15 · Guide Home · Chapter 17 →

Research notes: Chapter 16

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Falada · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Symbols: Horses and Carriages · The House

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Angels and Archangels · Deafness and Sign Language · Fairy Tales and Folklore · Gambling and Playing Cards

We have been building toward this chapter since Chapter 13 told us that something happened on the night of Vera’s fourteenth birthday that changed everything. Chapter 14 gave us Mr. Spitzer’s grief. Chapter 15 gave us Vera at the sea, giddy and briefly rebellious. Chapter 16 takes us inside the house and down the long corridor to Miss MacIntosh’s door.

Young does not give us the revelation in this chapter either. But she brings us to the threshold and stops there, the door swinging open, the moonbeams cutting through the dark, Miss MacIntosh standing by the window, unbelievably not herself.

The revelation is in Chapter 17. But Chapter 16 is the approach, and it is one of the most beautiful sustained passages in the novel. Don’t rush it.

What actually happens in Chapter 16

Vera is riding her black pony, Falada, through the grounds of the estate on the night of her fourteenth birthday. She rides through the dark allees and orchards and abandoned sports courts, past the gargoyles and marble statues and the garden of the blind. She is exhilarated, wild, laughing, whispering her secrets into the pony’s drooping ear.

She decides not to visit her mother: Catherine is either deep in her dreams or rising to the surface, and bringing a real horse into that bedroom could kill her with shock. So Vera and Falada climb the stairs of the great house instead, moving through its extraordinary interior: the mosaic emperors, the funeral gondola in the fountain, the phoenix above the fireplace, the tomb of the unnamed musician, the tapestries of angels restoring “sight to the blind and hearing to the deaf.”

They are heading to Miss MacIntosh. She is the one who will know they are real.

The door to Miss MacIntosh’s bedroom, which is always “locked and barred from the inside,” swings open of itself. Falada snorts. In the moonlight, Miss MacIntosh is standing by the window. And the chapter ends on the moment before Vera can fully take in what she sees.

Falada

The pony’s name carries an enormous amount of freight, and Young makes sure you know it.

Mr. Spitzer traces the name to an old Arabic fairy tale with parallels across many countries and ages. Falada is the horse whose head is “cut off and nailed above the city gates and whose” severed head then speaks “oracles and parables to the people passing through.” A head that speaks after death. A horse that carries truth even when it has been silenced.

The fairy tale Young is drawing on is most familiar in its German version, collected by the Brothers Grimm, called The Goose Girl. In that story, a princess is betrayed by her handmaid, who steals her identity and her prince. The princess’s magical horse Falada is killed to prevent it from revealing the truth. Its head is nailed above a gate. Every morning, as the true princess passes under it, Falada’s severed head speaks to her: Alas, alas, if thy mother could see thee now, her heart would break.

Young chose this name with exquisite care. Falada is the horse that speaks truth from beyond death. Vera rides him directly toward Miss MacIntosh’s door. He seems to know the way, she says. He is “my head, my intelligence. I was the headless rider.”

The headless rider going where the speaking head leads her. Toward the truth she has not yet been able to see.

The interior of the house

The chapter’s long central passage, as Vera and Falada move through the house’s interior on their way to Miss MacIntosh, is one of Young’s great architectural inventions. Read it slowly.

The house contains multitudes. A funeral gondola in an interior fountain. A phoenix above a fireplace of cold ashes. The empty tomb of a musician who has “gone to join the world of music”: its lid a gold grand piano held up by cherubim with folded wings. A “marble boy lying on his tomb with a marble book opened in his hand and marble rose petals upon its pages and his marble dog sleeping at his feet,” with no one knowing if the tomb was ever occupied. “Mosaic emperors like the faces of old playing cards.” Sleeping marble lions from a Venetian doge’s palace.

This is Catherine’s house, and it reflects her: it is a world where art and death are indistinguishable, where music leads to a tomb, where tombs may be empty, where angels on tapestries restore the senses the novel has been mourning, sight to the blind, hearing to the deaf. The house is the inner landscape of a woman who has refused the outer world for decades, accumulated into architecture.

And through all of it, Falada’s hooves clatter. He and Vera make no sound. They move like a pair of moths.

Why Vera goes to Miss MacIntosh

The logic of this moment is quiet but precise. Vera cannot go to her mother because Catherine lives in a world where the boundary between real and phantom is completely dissolved. If Catherine saw a girl on a horse, she might think they were phantoms (which would be fine, she is not afraid of phantoms) or she might recognize them as real, which could kill her with shock. Either way, Vera cannot be known there.

So she goes to Miss MacIntosh. Because Miss MacIntosh, “though she might condemn us, would know that we were real.”

This is everything. After four chapters of approaching and circling and withholding, Young gives us the clearest statement of what Miss MacIntosh means to Vera: she is the person who confirms reality. Not who loves Vera most. Not who knows Vera best. The person whose recognition makes Vera real. In a house full of hallucination, with a mother who lives entirely inside her own mind, Miss MacIntosh is the one solid witness. Her knowing you are there means you are there.

And the door, which is always “locked and barred from the inside,” swings open of itself.

The sentence at the end

Young ends the chapter on one of her most carefully constructed sentences.

“She had greatly changed since last I had seen her, and she was unbelievably not herself, and yet—how was it that I recognized her instantly as if she were something I had always dimly known, and the shock of this knowledge was but the affirmation of some older shock which had been forgotten and laid over by the autumn leaves, the snow falling, the first buds of spring?”

Two things are happening at once here. Vera sees something that shocks her. And in the same moment she recognizes it as something she has always, at some level, already known. The shock is not of discovery but of confirmation. The older shock (the one that was forgotten, buried under the seasons) surfaces.

Young is telling you, without yet telling you, that the revelation about Miss MacIntosh is not a betrayal. It is a recognition. The truth was always there, waiting under the ordinary surface of things, and Vera is about to stop not knowing it.

Chapter 17 delivers it. Go there now.
