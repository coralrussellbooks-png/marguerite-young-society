Chapter 60: The Wild Sonata

← Chapter 59 · Guide Home · Chapter 61 →

Research notes: Chapter 60

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Themes: Fate and Chance · Music, Sound, and Silence

Symbols: Birds · Music and Instruments · Silence and Deafness

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Gambling and Playing Cards · Greek Mythology · Music and Composers

After two chapters of Mr. Spitzer walking the city in philosophical anguish (questioning his skeleton, giving coins to musicians he can't quite see, trying to determine whether he is alive or dead) Chapter 60 offers something unexpected: a moment of grace. Not resolution. Not answers. But something that cuts through all the questions like a flock of wild ducks cutting through fog.

Hold on to that image. We'll get there.

What actually happens

Mr. Spitzer is still walking the city, still thinking. He wonders, with genuine uncertainty, whether he is actually a musician at all, or whether he has been a gambler all along, someone who mistook the music in his head for a vocation rather than a symptom. He reads only the sporting pages. He has never attended a concert. He cannot say “the value of a sand dollar or a Boston dollar or an English pound sterling.”

Then, at dusk, he practices moose calls in the middle of the city. A moose answers. Its antlers loom through the purple fog like tree boughs, merging with traffic lights and the Pleiades.

And then the wild ducks come.

Musician or gambler?

This is the chapter's opening question, and it's worth taking seriously. Mr. Spitzer has been described to us, and has described himself, as a composer. But Chapter 60 introduces genuine doubt: what if the music existed only in his head, as germinal, unwritten, unrealized? What if the gambling, the sporting pages, the horse races, the cards falling: what if that was the real Mr. Spitzer, and the composer was a story he told himself?

Young doesn't answer this. She is not interested in debunking Mr. Spitzer. She is interested in what it means to carry a vocation you cannot verify. Many people live their entire lives with music in their heads they never find a way to express. Does that make them musicians? Does it matter?

Mr. Spitzer suspects he might be what he calls a "cubistic dream player": a self fractured into multiple, simultaneous viewpoints that don't resolve into a coherent whole, like a Cubist painting seen from every angle at once. He is the gambler and the composer, and possibly neither, and the two versions of himself don't reconcile.

The moose in the city

When Mr. Spitzer practices moose calls in the urban dusk and a moose actually answers (antlers rising through the fog, indistinguishable from tree boughs, traffic lights, and the scatter of the Pleiades overhead) you are reading one of the novel's purest expressions of what Young means by the dissolution of borders.

The moose should not be there. The city should exclude it. And yet: it answers. Young is not asking you to decide whether this is hallucination or vision or literal event. She is showing you that Mr. Spitzer inhabits a world where these categories have become permeable, where if you call into the wilderness with enough longing, something wild calls back, regardless of where you are standing.

This is also, quietly, a scene about his dead brother. Mr. Spitzer wonders throughout this chapter how he knows what Peron knew, if he is himself and not Peron. The moose call is a kind of answer to that question: some communication travels across distances that should make it impossible.

The wild ducks

This is the moment the chapter has been building toward, and Young gives it everything she has.

The wild ducks appear overhead: exotic, "beautiful Asiatics" with crested heads and gold-veiled eyes, their wings gold-tipped, drifting through great clouds. Their honking fills the air as a "wild sonata," drowning out the traffic, the city, the noise of ordinary existence. Mr. Spitzer, standing in the street, feels himself dissolving into the sound: imagines himself metamorphosed into wings, suspended between earth and sky, a silent observer caught inside the music.

And here is what he realizes: he doesn't need to write his elegy anymore. He has been trying, across many chapters, to compose an elegy for his brother, for himself, for everything that has been lost. But the wild ducks are the elegy. Their honking calls, unrepeatable and wild and entirely indifferent to human grief, are the music he could never have written. They will accompany him, he thinks, "through the long reaches of the silence."

This is one of Young's most tender moments. The elegy you cannot compose will still be given to you. Not in the form you expected (not as a composed piece, not from a harpsichord with all black keys or a viola da gamba) but as something wild and passing overhead, not made for you, not about you, and somehow exactly right.

Sound moving through imagined silence

Young describes sound in this chapter as something that moves "like the human spirit through heavens of imagined silence." This is her persistent argument about music, and by extension about art: it is not the sound itself but its movement through silence that constitutes the real thing. The wild sonata of the ducks matters because it passes through a world that is, underneath everything, silent.

Mr. Spitzer's dead brother could not imagine silence, we're told. It was unimaginable to him. But Mr. Spitzer has made his peace with it. And maybe that is why the ducks come to him, because he is not afraid of the silence they will leave behind.

What to hold onto

Mr. Spitzer standing in a city street, looking up. The fog. The traffic lights. The Pleiades. And through all of it, a flock of wild ducks with gold-tipped wings and crested heads, honking their way across the sky in a wild sonata he could never have composed, which is his elegy, which is enough.

He doesn't need to write the music anymore. It was always going to come to him from outside.
