Chapter 30

On Chapter 30 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Woman in Young's Magnum Opus.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Eustace

Eustace opens the chapter as pure fluidity, invisible and shapeshifting, "mocking bird, old man, old woman, military figures," and this refusal of fixed form is the pillar Young then hands to Cousin Hannah. Catherine's private communication with a presence no one else can see is the isolation of a woman whose nonconforming vision goes unwitnessed, and her wish for card partners like Cousin Hannah, who can be "two partners at once," turns that isolation toward multiplicity as power rather than deficit.

Cousin Hannah enters as the transgressive figure the whole novel has been circling. She refuses the Spitzer brothers and forecasts their ruin; she carries a worldly knowledge of "politics, assassinations, and royal intrigues" the domestic sphere was built to keep from women; she will not settle, will not take long visits or telephone calls, and treats mobility itself as a claim against confinement. Her feats cross every boundary Eustace's shapeshifting anticipated: archer, dueler, marksman, mountain climber, jockey, disguised "as an Arab king," riding "against the courtly Spanish cavalcades, driving them before her like chickens." The shedding of "a skirt," mourned and redeemed, is the shedding of an imposed femininity, and her impatience and decisiveness, her power to cut "great cables of iron" and "silken threads," are the traits patriarchy codes masculine reclaimed for a woman who will not be governed.

This is radical female agency and it is the novel's insistence that "the individual is the one illusion," the two meeting in a single figure. Hannah moves through disguise and self-multiplication the way the book moves through overlapping identities; Catherine calls her an "emancipated extension" of herself, so that the two women are one refusal wearing two bodies. Her survival past hired assassins, her strategic navigation of "one-eyed ogres greater than death," her dedication to "the overthrow of the dream and of the dreamer," set active resistance against Catherine's opium escape without letting either cancel the other. The chapter closes on the image of sailing "two ships" at once, one for the living and one for the dead, the dual reality every woman navigates, and it treats that doubleness as command rather than division.
