Pillar: Pillar 1 Not One Self · Across every book

Act I: The Fractured Interior

You are not a single, stable identity. You are a crowded room of conflicting inner voices.

Most of us were raised on a story about the self. Somewhere under the noise sits one core, one true voice, waiting to be found. Therapy, self-help, and personality quizzes promise the same prize: meet the real you. Marguerite Young spent her whole career politely declining that prize.

The Framework

In Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, almost no one is only one person. Mr. Spitzer, a quiet lawyer, cannot say for certain whether he is himself or his dead twin brother, a gambler. Catherine Cartwheel, the narrator's opium-dreaming mother, lives among visitors who may exist only inside her mind. Even Miss MacIntosh, the plain and sensible nursemaid who seemed the one solid thing in Vera Cartwheel's childhood, turns out to be hiding a bald head under a wig and a body marked by loss. The person everyone relied on for common sense was a composite too.

Young does not treat these doublings as symptoms to be cured. They are simply what a person is. In her work the self is a gathering, not a point.

Walt Whitman said it plainly in Song of Myself (1855): "Do I contradict myself? Very well then I contradict myself, (I am large, I contain multitudes.)" Whitman does not apologize for the contradiction. He widens to hold it. Inner conflict becomes evidence of size rather than proof of failure.

Carl Jung reached a similar place from the clinic. He saw the psyche as a society of partly independent complexes, each with its own mood, memory, and agenda. For Jung, health did not mean silencing the crowd. It meant bringing the crowd into conversation.

Two things change once the self is understood as plural. First, inner conflict stops being a sign that something is broken. The part that wants to leave and the part that wants to stay may both be telling the truth. Second, other people become harder to reduce. If a person holds multitudes, then no single act, post, or worst day tells the whole story. Plurality is not an excuse. It is a demand for patience.

The Art Companion

The Portuguese poet Fernando Pessoa wrote under dozens of heteronyms, among them Alberto Caeiro, Ricardo Reis, and Álvaro de Campos. Each had a biography, a style, and a philosophy that argued with the others. Pessoa did not think of them as pen names. He thought of them as people living in him. Read a Caeiro poem beside a Campos poem and the plural self stops being a theory.

For a visual companion, sit with Pablo Picasso's Portrait of Dora Maar (1937), where a single face looks forward and sideways at once. The painting does not choose which view is true.

Reflection Questions

  • When I face a hard decision, whose voices argue inside me? What is each one trying to protect?

  • Which of my inner voices did I inherit from a parent, a teacher, or an old friend?

  • Is there a part of me I keep offstage? What would it say if it had the floor for one full minute?

  • Where have I reduced another person to a single version of themselves?

The Exercise: Cast of Characters

  1. Write down five to seven inner voices. Give each a name that fits, such as The Accountant, The Runaway, or The Child at the Window.

  2. Under each name, note what it wants, what it fears, when it first showed up, and its favorite sentence.

  3. Choose one real decision currently on the table. Write a short scene in which three of these characters sit at a kitchen table and argue it out. Do not let anyone win.

  4. Finish with one sentence spoken by the host, the part that holds the room open for all of them.
