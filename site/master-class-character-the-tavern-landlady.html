The Tavern Landlady: The Life Spoken For

← The Tavern Landlord · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Mr. Weed →

Spoiler note: This portrait is written for readers who have finished the novel. It discusses the character’s whole story, including how it ends.

Characters: Jacqueline White · Madge Capehorn · Vera Cartwheel

After the landlord, who talks more than anyone in the book, comes the woman he married, and she is his exact reverse. He owns almost nothing and says everything. She owns everything and says nothing. She never appears at the counter, never climbs the stairs, never gets a line of her own. She is the thinnest figure in this whole set, a woman known only as a phrase in her husband's mouth, and the thinness is not an oversight.

Everything known about her arrives secondhand, through the landlord's talk. She had been "a circus fat lady," a body that people once paid to look at, and by the time he found her she was "losing weight," "down to two hundred pounds now in her stocking feet," a spectacle already in the act of shrinking. She owned the Tavern. "It was she who had owned this hotel," he says, "a good enough port for a man in a storm," and that single fact turns out to hold up the entire building the book takes place in.

She is the ground. The Tavern is where the bus stops and where all these people converge, Madge and the football boy and the hangman and Vera, and the Tavern is hers. The landlord married into her house. Every tall tale he tells at the counter, every shipwreck and jungle and knot, is told standing on real estate she holds the deed to. He is the performance and she is the floor it happens on. The book is full of people who float, the sleeping, the dreaming, the summoned, the drowned, and she is the rare one who owns something solid, and she is barely visible for owning it.

She is also the rescue. The landlord had gone down on four ships and brought such bad luck that "none but a mad captain would sign me on," rejected by the sea that kept trying to drown him and by every crew that heard about it. Then, in his own telling, "my wife signed me on." She took the jinxed man the ocean would not keep and the captains would not have, and gave him a berth on dry land. The harbor in the storm is not a metaphor he reaches for. It is the actual woman who owned the actual roof, and who signed the papers that made a drowning man into a landlord.

What she says, she is never allowed to say herself. Every word of hers in the book is relayed, quoted, carried secondhand by the man who talks over her. She is the one who "reminded him" that God said in the Bible no one had ever seen the thing he claimed to have seen. She is the "my wife says" who thinks he should see America before he brags about the rest of the world. She is the "as my wife says" behind the half-price room given to the legless man with no legs to stand on. She is sense, scripture, and charity, the whole domestic counterweight to his tall tales, and she is present only as a clause inside them. The ventriloquist, who made coffee cups and empty rocking chairs speak, is married to the one person at the Tavern who is never given her own voice.

So she stands at the far edge of everything the set has shown. Madge and Jacqueline and James and the coachman were given too much life, whole marriages and murders and consciences built onto strangers and absences. The landlady is given too little. She is real, she owns the house, she saved the man, and the book lets her exist only as something he says. She is not a person invented out of nothing. She is a person talked over until almost nothing is left showing, which is the same power running the other direction.

The connection for a writer is the one the landlord's chapter was too cheerful to mention. Making a person up is only half of what a teller does. The other half is deciding who does not get to speak, whose life gets folded into a clause, whose ownership of the actual house goes unremarked while the talker performs on top of it. Young sets the loudest voice in the book beside the most silenced one and marries them, and she does not soften it. The landlady is what the method costs. Every time a writer gives a sleeping stranger a marriage or a dead girl a whole biography, some real person at the edge of the frame is being handed a smaller share of the telling, owning the floor and saying nothing on it. The honest writer keeps one eye on her, the woman who signed the drowning man on and holds the deed to the house where the story happens, and remembers that she had sense and scripture and mercy of her own, and a voice, and that the only reason it is never heard is that someone else was doing all the talking.
