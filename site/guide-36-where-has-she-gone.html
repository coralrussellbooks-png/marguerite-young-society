Chapter 36: Where Has She Gone

← Chapter 35 · Guide Home · Chapter 37 →

Research notes: Chapter 36

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh

Themes: Duality and Doubles · Memory and the Past · The Search for Meaning

Symbols: Angels, Devils, and Myth · Mirrors and Reflections

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Judaism · Napoleon and Wellington · Revelation and the Apocalypse · The History of Opium

There is a particular kind of grief that doesn't look like grief from the outside. It looks, instead, like a logical problem. Like a question of coordinates. Like someone who has mislaid an object and is working, methodically, through all the places it might be.

This is Catherine in Chapter 36. Cousin Hannah is dead, and Catherine knows she is dead, and Catherine's response to this fact is to treat it as insufficient information. Dead is not a location. Dead does not tell you where someone has gone. And Catherine (who has spent her life in a household where the borders between waking and dreaming, real and imagined, present and departed have never been firm) sees no particular reason why death should be any more conclusive than anything else.

Chapter 36 is one of the novel's great meditations on what we do with the dead when they refuse to arrive.

What actually happens in Chapter 36

Catherine wants to know where Cousin Hannah is. Not in any mystical or vague sense; she wants this information concretely. Cousin Hannah is dead, yes, but Catherine considers herself to be living among both the living and the dead, and she sees no reason why this shouldn't grant her access to Cousin Hannah's whereabouts.

Mr. Spitzer was the last living person to see Cousin Hannah alive. Catherine blames him, with a frustration that has calcified over years into something close to contempt, for not being able to tell her more. He is obtuse about it. He won't say, or can't say, or the knowledge has slipped through the particular kind of consciousness he has: one that receives everything a beat too late.

In one of the chapter's sharpest moments, Mr. Spitzer briefly rouses himself from his usual lassitude to accuse Catherine of never having “loved any living being—not even” his dead twin brother. This lands. Young doesn't linger on it, but you should.

Meanwhile, Catherine turns over the question of what Cousin Hannah would look like now, having undergone her transformation. Would she be a man or a woman on the other side? Queen of the dead, or invader of heaven? Catherine suspects heaven will not contain her for long. A woman like Cousin Hannah, Catherine thinks, would make heaven into chaos inside of a week.

Mr. Spitzer, and the problem of arriving late

Young gives us, in Chapter 36, one of the novel's most precise and quietly devastating portraits of Mr. Spitzer. His eyes and ears are out of sync. He hears sounds a minute after they happen. Every singer he listens to is slightly off-key. He exists at what Young calls a second remove from life, not absent, exactly, but never quite present either, always receiving the world a half-step behind the moment it occurs.

He is also, Young tells us, a counterfeit of himself. His twin brother is dead, and something in Mr. Spitzer died with him, and what remains is a copy, a man who goes through the motions of being a person without quite filling the shape.

Notice how Young has arranged this. Mr. Spitzer, whose identity is fractured by the loss of his twin, is the person Catherine needs to locate Cousin Hannah. The keeper of the last information is the person least able to reliably hold information. The bridge between the living and the dead is a man who is already, in his way, only partially alive.

This is not cruelty on Young's part. It is the novel's particular understanding of how grief compounds. Loss doesn't distribute itself evenly. It lands on the people least equipped to carry it, and then it asks them to be of service anyway.

Catherine, memory, and the lie of the mirror

The chapter gives us an image of Catherine looking at her own face in an inaccurate mirror. The reflection is there, but it does not quite match. This is not played for surrealism; it is, for Catherine, simply accurate. She has never been able to hold a fixed image of herself. She is made of fragments, partial selves, vague thoughts that don't resolve into a continuous person.

Young draws the contrast explicitly: Catherine's approach to memory is radical, imaginative, porous. She cannot distinguish what she remembers from what she has invented, and she is not entirely sure the distinction matters. Mr. Spitzer, by contrast, is conservative, precise, buttoned against the flood of association. He trusts only what he can verify. He trusts very little.

They are, in this sense, a perfect mismatch for the conversation Catherine needs to have. She wants to swim out into the deep water of what might have happened to Cousin Hannah. Mr. Spitzer will not leave the shore.

Cousin Hannah, and the question of what comes next

Catherine's meditation on Cousin Hannah's afterlife is one of the chapter's great set pieces, and it deserves to be read slowly.

She imagines Cousin Hannah having traveled after death through Moscow, St. Petersburg, Siberia, Babylon: the same vast, border-crossing restlessness she showed in life, now loosed from the constraint of a body. Young gives us Cousin Hannah as a great traveler, a figure whose posthumous geography is as improbable and sweeping as her biography.

But then Catherine gets to the real question: what is Cousin Hannah now? Is she still a woman? The chapter floats the possibility that death might dissolve that category: that Cousin Hannah could have become something neither man nor woman on the other side, something that has escaped the classifications entirely. This is not a question Young resolves. It is a question she wants you to sit with.

And then: Catherine's conviction that Cousin Hannah would not last long in heaven. That she would be too disruptive, too restless, too constitutionally opposed to any hierarchy, including celestial ones, to remain a peaceful resident. Heaven, in Catherine's cosmology, is not equipped for a woman like Cousin Hannah. She would turn it into another campaign.

The bridges, and what they cross

Young threads through this chapter a series of bridges: the “Brooklyn Bridge, Waterloo Bridge, the Bridge of Sighs” in Venice, the Bridge of Lies in Sibiu, Romania. Each one a different kind of crossing, a different relationship between what is on either side.

The Bridge of Sighs, named for the sighs of condemned prisoners who crossed it from the courtroom to the cells, and the Bridge of Lies, which legend says will collapse under anyone who tells a lie upon it, are the ones to hold. Chapter 36 is a chapter about what cannot be crossed, about the gap between the living and the dead that Catherine keeps trying to negotiate and keeps finding uncrossable. The bridges are there. The other side is not accessible.

The accusation, and what it costs

Return, for a moment, to Mr. Spitzer's accusation: that Catherine never loved any living being. Not even his dead twin brother.

It is the kind of thing that is said in exhaustion, in the specific tiredness of someone who has watched another person love the absent more devotedly than they have ever loved the present. And it may be true. Catherine's great loves are Cousin Hannah, who is dead. Miss MacIntosh, who is gone. The opium dreams that are more real to her than the room she sits in. She is a woman whose most passionate relationships are with things that cannot require anything of her in return.

Mr. Spitzer, living and corporeal and difficult and obtuse, does not stand a chance against that kind of devotion. He never did. He knows it. The accusation is not an attack. It is a grief of its own.

What to hold onto

If Chapter 36 feels like it is circling without landing, here is what it is circling: Catherine is trying to grieve, and she cannot find the form for it. The dead are supposed to go somewhere. They are supposed to be locatable, even if unreachable. But Cousin Hannah has not gone anywhere Catherine can name, and Mr. Spitzer, the only witness, cannot tell her, and the mirrors show an inaccurate face, and the bridges are there but the other side is fog.

This is the chapter where the novel asks, as quietly and as relentlessly as it can: what do you do with a loss that won't tell you where it went?

You keep looking. You imagine. You write letters that are illegible. You wait for sounds that arrive a minute late. You hold the question open like a door and let the wind come through.
