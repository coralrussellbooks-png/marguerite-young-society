Research notes: Chapter 6 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 6

Characters: Homer Capehorn · Jacqueline White · Madge Capehorn

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The most immediately visible feature of the chapter is its refusal to stabilize identity. Madge is simultaneously:

  • The pregnant wife
  • The dead girl she was before marriage
  • Her dead mother (who died in childbirth at twenty-three)
  • The rival, Jacqueline
  • The child she is carrying
  • The telephone operator
  • The dancing girl
  • The bride and the corpse

These are not roles Madge plays sequentially. They co-exist, interpenetrate, and contradict each other within single sentences. Consider:

"She was no longer that which she had been but only that which she would become"

This is not a statement about change over time. It is a statement about the simultaneous occupation of multiple temporal selves. The grammar itself refuses linear identity; she was, she would become, but crucially, she is both at once.

The chapter's most structurally radical move is the dissolution of the boundary between Madge and Jacqueline. By the chapter's end, Madge cannot tell them apart:

"Perhaps she seemed, even to herself, that person she warned against since it was so hard for her to tell the difference between one person and another now that she was not single."

And:

"Or wouldn't we melt into one like gold in the fire?"

The plural self here is not psychological fragmentation in the clinical sense. It is ontological. The text suggests that the self was never singular to begin with: that singularity was the illusion, and multiplicity is the ground condition. Madge's pregnancy literalizes this: she is, physically, more than one person. But the text insists this was always already true, before the pregnancy, before the marriage, before anything.

Homer, too, is plural: the sleeping husband, the football hero, the boy who loved Jacqueline, the son who lost his father, the man who married Madge. His plurality is less articulated because he is less conscious of it, which is itself a comment on the gendered distribution of self-awareness in the text.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter is saturated with mirror imagery, face powder, painted lips, stuffed birds, glass jewels: the apparatus of constructed appearance. But the text does not treat these as mere vanity. They are epistemological statements.

"She was always, it seemed, taking off, putting on her other face"

The "other face" is not false. There is no face beneath it that is more real. The text makes this explicit:

"She frowned, for she was not pleased with her image in the dark glass."

The dark glass cannot show a true image because there is no true image to show. The face powder, the rouge, the painted eyebrows; these are not concealment of reality but participation in its construction. The chapter's closing image is devastating in this regard:

"What had she done? She had painted two mouths — she had painted two hearts."

Two mouths. Two hearts. Not one real and one fake. Two. The illusion has not failed to conceal; it has succeeded in revealing that there was always more than one.

The bus journey itself operates as a figure for this pillar. The road turns back on itself, passes the same gates twice:

"The road was turning and turning upon itself, and we seemed to be going back the way we had already come."

There is no destination that is more real than the journey. The "same old river town" they are returning to is not a stable reality awaiting their arrival; it is another construction, another version of what was already constructed.

"Everything the same as it had been before or almost the same, the same old faces smiling at her in the whirling, moon-colored fogs"

The "almost" is crucial. Not the same. Almost. Reality is always the almost-same reconstruction of what was already a construction.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The chapter does not present grief as an episode. It presents grief as the atmosphere in which everything occurs. The weather imagery throughout (fog, rain, snow, wind, flooding) is not decorative. It is the externalization of a grief that has no beginning and no end.

"This land was like a dying sea, hills swelling, valleys perceptibly moving."

The landscape grieves. Or rather: the landscape is grief made spatial. Madge's grief for her mother (whom she killed by being born), for her father (lost through the ice), for her own girlhood, for Jacqueline (whom she has never met), for the child she is carrying (who is not hers), for the marriage that was never real; these griefs do not resolve into each other. They accumulate.

"She must shed a ton of tears, all the tears she had ever saved, had never wept before, and rivers would flow from her eyes before she was through weeping."

The image of saved tears is precise: grief was not absent before. It was deferred, stored, accumulating. The chapter's present is the moment when the accumulated weight becomes impossible to carry without acknowledgment, but acknowledgment does not discharge it. The rivers flow, but the source does not dry up.

The most structurally honest statement of this pillar comes near the chapter's end:

"All her life had been this poor parody of life, this sad pretense she could no longer countenance nor endure, for she was dead, and she had died at the hour of her birth."

Grief here is not a response to loss. It is the condition of having been born into a world where loss is structural. She died at birth because birth itself, in this world, is the beginning of dying. The grief is not about any particular event. It is about the nature of existence.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrative voice of this chapter occupies a position of extraordinary ethical complexity. It is not Madge's voice entirely; it is something that watches Madge watching herself, that sees her seeing, that refuses to resolve what she cannot resolve.

The narrator does not explain Madge. Does not diagnose her. Does not offer the reader a stable position from which to judge. Consider how the text handles the question of whether Jacqueline exists:

Sometimes she thought, crying in her sleep, "that there was no other girl but only one she had imagined, that ever since she had found herself with the child of a stranger hanging to her heart, she had been imagining things"

The text does not confirm or deny this. It stays in the room with the uncertainty. Is Jacqueline real? Is she a projection? Is she Madge's mother? Is she Madge herself? The text will not say. This is not evasion. It is the ethical stance of the witness: to see fully without rushing toward the resolution that would make seeing unnecessary.

The stuffed bird on Madge's hat is one of the chapter's most persistent images, and it functions as a figure for the witness:

"the stuffed bird seeming ready to take wing, to leave its precarious perch, its brittle twig"

The bird watches. It cannot fly. It is dead but present. It bobs in agreement with whatever Madge does, "mechanical as consciousness, always in agreement with the dual-minded wearer of the hat", which is to say it witnesses without judgment, without the capacity to intervene, without the ability to leave.

The narrator is that bird. The reader is invited to be that bird. Not to fix. Not to conclude. To stay.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The bus journey is literally recursive: the road turns back on itself, passes the same landmarks multiple times. But the text's deeper recursion is temporal and psychological.

Madge is returning to the town she escaped. She is returning pregnant, as her mother returned (or never returned) pregnant. She is returning with a husband who does not love her, as, we infer, her mother may have done. She is returning to give birth in the house of her husband's mother, as her mother gave birth in whatever house she died in.

"Was this that honeymoon which she had never planned, had never even dreamed of, that she should be returning to the same old river town she long ago had escaped from"

The recursion is not failure. It is the structure of how time works in this text. You return. You return with what you have accumulated. The return is not the same as what you left because you are not the same. But it is recognizably the same, which is what makes it terrible and also, in the deepest sense, generative.

Homer's recursion is different but parallel: he is returning to his mother's house, to his father's absence, to the landscape of his childhood. He is returning as a married man to a place where he was a boy. The marriage is itself a recursive structure; he married Madge, in part, to carry the child of an absent father, as he himself was the child of an absent father.

"He had dreamed that he was his mother's little boy, never that he was the child's old father, never that old hunter."

The recursion here is: the son becomes the absent father. The structure repeats. This is not presented as tragedy in the melodramatic sense. It is presented as how it works.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter's sentences enact this pillar formally. They extend, accumulate, refuse to close. Clauses pile upon clauses. Lists proliferate:

"feed it to the rose-breasted wood ibis, to the little woodcocks, the little woodchucks, the wood lice, the beetles, feed it to the butterflies, the gnats. She would feed it to the wild turkeys gabbling in dark forests, to the sandpipers wading in dark streams, feed it to the purple moose, the Arctic hawk, the crows on the fences."

This list does not end because it cannot end. The incompletion is the point. To end the list would be to suggest that the world of things to which the unwanted child might be fed has limits. It does not. The sentence's refusal to conclude is its honesty.

The marriage itself is structurally incomplete:

"Marriage was not the realization of a young girl's dreams"

"Maybe marriage never put an end to things, never laid the whirling ghost."

Marriage, the institution designed to complete (to join, to resolve, to conclude) does not complete. It opens. It multiplies. It creates new incompletions. The text does not lament this. It observes it with the precision of someone who has stopped expecting completion to arrive.

Madge's wedding ring (destroyed, thrown away, never gold to begin with) is the chapter's central image of the ethics of incompletion:

"I stuck it into the clay mouth. I slipped it into an urn—but after all, it was not gold."

The ring, symbol of completion (no beginning, no end), is revealed as brass. As something that slips off. As something returned to clay. The circle is broken not by accident but by the nature of what it was made of.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Madge is the chapter's primary evidence. She is:

  • A woman, in a text that reveals how women bear the structural weight of reproduction, mortality, and grief that men are permitted to sleep through
  • Pregnant with a child that is not her husband's
  • Returning to a town she escaped
  • Working class (telephone operator, pottery worker) in a world that values the banker's daughter
  • Neither the idealized dead girl (Jacqueline) nor the respectable wife

She exists at every margin simultaneously. And it is precisely this marginal position that gives her the clearest sight in the chapter. She sees what Homer cannot see because he is sleeping. She sees what Jacqueline cannot see because Jacqueline is dying. She sees the structure of the world because she is not protected by any of its centers.

"She had always evaded life, never once had dreamed of life which should follow life when she was dead, never once had looked beyond the superficial face she saw. Never had she known another person, not even herself."

This is the text's indictment of the centered self: the self that does not have to see because it is protected by its position. Madge, by contrast, has been forced to see everything, from the margins, from the bus seat, in the dark.

Jacqueline functions as the idealized center (beautiful, popular, beloved, free) and the text systematically dismantles her. She is dying. Her beauty is fading. She is spreading disease. The center does not hold. The margin was always where the real knowledge lived.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This is perhaps the pillar most structurally embedded in the chapter's texture. The past does not recede in this text. It presses upward through everything.

Madge's mother (dead before the chapter begins, dead before Madge can remember her) is present on every page. She is present in:

  • The pregnancy (Madge is the age her mother was when she died in childbirth)
  • The father's mistake (he confused Madge for her mother)
  • The marriage bed (Madge's bed was her mother's marriage bed, with its "drunkard's walk" quilt)
  • The child Madge is carrying (who will be born as Madge's mother died)

"After twenty-three years of reproaching Madge that she was not her mother, that she had taken her mother's life by being born"

The mother is not behind Madge. She is beneath her, in the sediment, pressing up through every act. Madge cannot escape her because she is not separate from her. She is the continuation of her.

The father, similarly, does not stay in the past:

"The spirit of Madge's old father had returned from the river."

He returns with the spring floods. He was never fully gone. The river holds him and releases him. The past is not a place one leaves. It is a geological layer that surfaces whenever conditions are right.

The town itself operates as sedimentary time:

"these same gate posts twice, three times, these same old orchards flooded to the tree-tops"

The same. Flooded but the same. The flood is new. The orchards are old. The new thing happens to the old thing, but the old thing does not disappear. It is submerged, and then it surfaces again.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter's language is itself the subject of the chapter. Madge is a former telephone operator: someone whose entire professional existence was the mediation of language, the connecting and disconnecting of voices. Her final disconnection is not merely personal. It is linguistic:

"Your party is disconnected."

Language failed her at the moment she most needed it, when trying to reach her father across whatever distance separated them. The failure of language is the failure of connection, which is the failure of the self to reach outside itself.

The chapter's language performs its own constructedness constantly. Words slip. Names blur. "Jack" becomes "Jacqueline" becomes "Jack Robinson" becomes "Jack Frost" becomes "Jack-in-the-pulpit":

"Jack Robinson. Jack Frost who paints the window pane white. Frost flowers on the glass. Little Jack Fleet. Little Jack White. Jack-in-the-pulpit."

This is not wordplay. It is the demonstration that language does not refer to stable realities. "Jack" names a man, a weather phenomenon, a flower, a nursery rhyme figure. The name does not anchor the thing. The thing escapes the name. Language is always already constructing rather than describing.

Madge's speech to Homer (her long, shrill, recursive monologue) is language that knows it is not being heard:

"He had not acted as if he had heard her."

She speaks anyway. The speech is not communication. It is construction; she is building herself, building the marriage, building the rival, building the grief, through the act of speaking, whether or not anyone receives it. Language here is not a vehicle for truth. It is the practice through which truth is provisionally assembled.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter is not elsewhere. It is here, in the grey bus, in the stuffed bird, in the powder and the rouge, in the pregnant belly, in the sleeping husband's red cheeks.

"Fleecy clouds scudded along the sky both dark and luminous, indeterminate as if this haze of fitful twilight were the hour before dawn"

The landscape is not beautiful in any conventional, transcendent sense. It is beautiful in the way that the real is beautiful: flooded, derelict, mist-covered, broken. The windmill with its broken silver arm. The roan horse racing its shadow. The bony mule grey as the flooded mist. These are not symbols pointing to a better world. They are the world, and the world is enough.

The chapter's most explicit statement of immanent transcendence comes in Homer's description of the unborn:

"They say a babe lives centuries in its mother's womb before it is born. Stars shine there, too, and light the dark waters."

The stars are inside the womb. The transcendent is inside the immanent. The sacred is not above the grey bus; it is in the dark water of the womb, in the child that is not Homer's, in the body of the woman who is dying and giving birth simultaneously.

The gold wedding ring that was never gold, that was brass, is also, in its way, a figure for immanent transcendence:

"Her wedding ring would be, if she was dead, this circle of creation, all of one piece having no beginning and no end, gold that would not melt in crucibles of fire."

The ideal ring, the ring of creation, is described in the conditional: would be, if she was dead. The transcendent ring exists only in the imagination of the woman who threw away the brass one. The sacred is not the ideal ring. The sacred is the throwing away of the brass one, the act of refusing the false transcendence while imagining the true one, here, in this bus, in this dark.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is the pillar that gives the chapter its deepest structure. Madge is not a self moving through time. She is where time crosses itself.

She is the crossing point of:

  • Her mother's death and her child's birth
  • Her father's disappearance and her husband's sleeping
  • Jacqueline's dying and her own giving birth
  • The town she escaped and the town she is returning to
  • The dead past and the not-yet-born future

She was "five months without shedding a drop of blood, and she had four months yet to go, and she lived now by the calendar, by time which never before had touched her"

She has become time's body. The calendar, which never touched her before, now is her. She is constituted by what was deposited before she arrived (her mother's death, her father's love and violence, the town's history, the class structures that made Jacqueline the banker's daughter and Madge the telephone operator) and she is constituting the ground the not-yet-born child will stand on.

The child (unnamed, ungendered, not-yet-born) is the future that Madge is making even as she mourns. The child is not hers and not Homer's and yet it will be born into their names, their history, their sediment. The child will stand on ground that Madge's grief made.

"She would give birth, but there would be no old doctor ever there to say to her that it was all a dream, this life, this other life"

No one will tell her it was a dream because it was not a dream. The obligation is structural. She did not choose to be the crossing point. She did not choose to carry the child of the dead father, to return to the town of her own dead father, to be the age of her dead mother. These things were deposited before she arrived. And yet she carries them forward. The carrying continues.

The chapter's final image (the two mouths, the two hearts) is the crossing point made visible:

"What had she done? She had painted two mouths — she had painted two hearts."

Two. Not one. The crossing point is not a single line. It is where multiple lines intersect. Madge is not one person with one mouth and one heart. She is the place where her mother's mouth and her own mouth cross. Where Jacqueline's heart and her own heart cross. Where the dead and the living cross.

This is not a problem. This is what she is. This is what the chapter has been saying, in its long, recursive, incomplete, grief-saturated, immanently sacred way, from the first turning of the road to the last painted heart.
