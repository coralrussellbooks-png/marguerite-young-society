Chapter 8. Firing the Engine

A paint scraper, a fireman's cab, a death on the rails, and a brotherhood

At fourteen, in the spring of 1870, Eugene Debs left school. Young says he did so "with considerable grief" (263). The family grocery was failing, and the boy went to work. The chapters from page 262 to about 282 follow him from the paint shop to the locomotive cab and back, and then into the union that would shape the next twenty years of his life.

Fifty cents a day

His first job came through a friend of his father's, Pierre Solomon, a French painter of boxcars who decorated engines with roosters, eagles, and owls. Solomon got Eugene hired at the railroad paint shop, where "Debs's diurnal pay had been fifty cents" (264). He had to pay a dollar, two days' wages, for his own paint scraper.

He kept it. Years later, as a labor leader, he hung that scraper on his office wall (264), proof to anyone who doubted that his hands had ever been blackened. Young treats the tool almost as a relic. It is The Sacred Is Here in a single object, a scraper that cost two days' pay and stayed with him for life.

The fireman's cab

He was promoted to fireman by accident, when the night fireman showed up too drunk to work (264). The job meant shoveling coal on moving engines in all weather. Young describes the winter runs: the young fireman "frozen by glacial cold on one side and his eye fried like an egg by the burning heat on the other side" (265). "The pay was measured not by time but by mileage" (265). Delays and breakdowns cost the railroad nothing and the worker everything.

Young adds a sly joke about his reading habits. The fireman did not read Plato's Republic on the job. He knew that a fireman caught reading philosophy would give the owners all the excuse they needed. At night, though, he read constantly: French and German poets, Shakespeare, and chapter after chapter of Les Misérables (266).

When the depression of 1873 cost him his place, he went to St. Louis. There he bunked with an engineer in a railroad flophouse, sleeping on a mattress of cornstalks, reading late and taking care to "shield with his red fireman's cap" the lamp so the light would not wake his roommate (272). He also reported home that he had killed thirty-seven bedbugs in a few minutes one night (268).

The man under the wheels

The turning point in these chapters is a death. Young reports a letter in which Debs, answering his mother's anxious question about a young man named Stewart killed near Terre Haute, blamed the dead man for his own fate. Young notes that this was the "first and last time" Debs would blame a worker for his own death (268).

Then she tells what really brought him home. It was not his mother's fear, she says, but the horror of watching a fellow worker who, "while working on a wheel had been knocked down, mangled, and mutilated" under the moving train (272).

Young follows this with one of her most biting passages, a mock version of the kind of notice the railroads issued about their dead. "Answered not to any name." "Was killed by error of his own and not the management" (273). The official language made every death the worker's fault. This is Reality Is Constructed in its coldest form. The accident report, like the "act of God" in Part One, turns a system into a single careless man.

Debs had written that very sentence about Stewart. The book shows him learning never to write it again.

The billing clerk and the brotherhood

Back in Terre Haute, Debs became a billing clerk for Herman Hulman's wholesale grocery, one of the largest businesses in town. Out of his first pay, he bought a five-year subscription to the New York Herald (273).

He still longed for the cab. In 1875, when Joshua Leach, grand master of the Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen, came to Terre Haute, the grocery clerk stepped out of the crowd and asked to join. Leach, an Irish immigrant who had fired engines on the Erie, took him in, prophesying that he had signed up "a young towhead who would do his duty and make his mark" in the brotherhood (277). Debs became secretary of the new Vigo Lodge.

The brotherhood's magazine, which Debs would soon help edit, recorded the dangers of the trade with grim precision. Young quotes its list of the causes of wrecks: "flying switch, runaway engine or train, careless stopping or switching, bad switching, cattle on track, snow or ice, washout, landslide" (280). It reported a worker in Jersey City whose foot caught in the rails and who threw himself under the wheels rather than live crippled (283). Young's summary is simple: "Debs was not and would never be a worshiper of the capitalistic gods of terminus" (283).

What the lens shows

This is the section where the book's philosophy becomes a job. The Margins Reveal the Truth is literal here, the view from the fireman's side of the cab. Grief Doesn't End appears in the dead brakeman and the widow's-benefit rolls. Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act appears in the magazine's patient lists of the dead, which Debs would keep compiling for years. The young man who once blamed a dead worker learns to record the dead and refuse the verdict.

Checking the record

  • The date he left school. The book contradicts itself. The chronology in the front matter says Debs began working in the railroad shops in 1869. The text says spring 1870 (263), which matches the standard accounts.

  • Why he left the cab. Most biographers give weight to his mother's fears and to the layoffs of the 1873 depression. Young acknowledges the mother but insists the decisive cause was witnessing the accident. Both may be true. Her version is an interpretation, not a settled fact.

  • Vigo Lodge. Accurate. The Terre Haute lodge of the Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen was founded in February 1875, with Debs as its secretary.
