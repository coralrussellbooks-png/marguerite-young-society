Definition from research

Chapter 12

  • “perhaps with two Mr. Spitzers, one who had died, one who had lived”: Joachim and Peron Spitzer, the twins.
  • “put his bet on the dark horse”: The phrase comes from Disraeli’s The Young Duke (1831): “a dark horse, which had never been thought of.”

Chapter 13

  • “his brother had not been buried anywhere on this vast, amorphous estate”: Peron Spitzer, Joachim’s twin, lies elsewhere and is not among Catherine’s dead guests.

Chapter 14

  • The deceased twin, described as quick, impetuous, and decisive.
  • Committed suicide by falling through a window; his death is enveloped in mystery and doubt.
  • Contrasts with Mr. Spitzer’s hesitant survival.
  • Represents a life fully lived and a death fully embraced.
  • His legacy haunts Mr. Spitzer, who often thinks in his brother’s voice or perspective.
  • “the case of Spitzer versus Spitzer”: A case in which he “must appear as both the defender and the accuser.”
  • “at shrouded parimutuel windows”: The parimutuel, a betting pool shared among the winners, was invented by Joseph Oller in Paris in 1867.
  • The quick, brilliant twin, a gambler “in the horse circles,” who “had fallen from a tall building.”
  • Catherine calls him the one “loved by her, yet never loving her,” yet at dawn she mourns “the slow one, the sick musician,” confusing which twin she lost.

Chapter 16

  • “had been sent to Mr. Spitzer by someone who had admired Mr. Spitzer’s brother”: Peron, the gambler of the horse circles (Chapter 14).

Chapter 22

  • “the kind of thing which should interest the late departed Mr. Spitzer”: Peron, the gambler of the race-tracks, “sometimes… seen double, standing in two places at once.”

Chapter 25

Listed as: Peron Spitzer (Joachim’s twin brother)

  • The deceased twin, described as quick, impetuous, and decisive.
  • Died in a fall through a fourteenth-floor window, perhaps suicide, perhaps an accident; his death is enveloped in mystery and doubt.
  • Contrasts with Mr. Spitzer’s hesitant survival.
  • Represents, to Mr. Spitzer, a life fully lived and a death perhaps fully embraced.
  • His legacy haunts Mr. Spitzer, who often thinks in his brother’s voice or perspective.
  • A gambler, “quick to live, quick to die,” a man of many superficial loves who would always “take another chance.”
  • “Peron had rushed impetuously ahead, and he had fallen through a glass window on the fourteenth floor”: Peron’s death, left between suicide and accident: “this intention made to seem this irrational accident.”

Chapter 26

Listed as: Peron Spitzer (Joachim’s twin brother)

  • Impulsive, competitive, impetuous, and restless.
  • His and Joachim’s lives and deaths intertwine; Peron’s death haunts Joachim.
  • He distrusted the law and wanted only “a crooked lawyer,” one who “would fix the witness and the judge,” and he often tried to fix his brother.
  • He preferred “the great crowds of Derby Day” to any dream, and “had been afraid of death.”
  • Represents the chaotic, fleeting nature of life and human memory.
  • He would have swept Catherine “into the living sunlight,” even if the sunlight were her death.
  • “the great crowds of Derby Day”: The Derby at Epsom, run since 1780. William Powell Frith painted its crowds in “The Derby Day” (1858).

Chapter 26 · The Dead Brother

  • A symbol of lost potential, memory, and the haunting presence of the past in the present.

Chapter 27

Listed as: Peron Spitzer (Joachim’s twin brother)

  • A man of “blatant tastes” who “had worn loud checks” and preferred “low company, lapsed souls, void characters, dice-rattlers.”
  • He “laughed at Mr. Spitzer’s silence,” yet left a fine, accurate watch, which he seems to have “most thoughtfully removed” before he jumped.
  • The twins “had always suffered, except for this final accident, the same accidents, one not falling unless the other fell.”

Chapter 28

Listed as: Peron Spitzer (Joachim’s twin brother)

  • A loudmouthed, pragmatic man who embraced the cruelty of actual life and seemed indifferent to spiritual or afterlife concepts.
  • His untimely death deeply affects Mr. Spitzer’s psyche and way of life.
  • He loved “the wastrel wheels of chance, the games of loaded dice, the croupiers, the billiard hall artists… the drugged horses.”
  • He “would most certainly have seen no value, no unearthly significance as to butterflies or any other spirits.”
  • “the wastrel wheels of chance, the games of loaded dice, the croupiers”: Roulette (“little wheel”), whose croupier spins it.

Chapter 28 · Contrast Between Mr. Spitzer and His Brother Peron

  • Peron is portrayed as a man focused on the immediate, material world, indifferent to spiritual or afterlife concepts, and unconcerned with the delicate beauty Mr. Spitzer values.
  • Peron’s death, “by accident or suicide,” marks a turning point for Mr. Spitzer, leading to the end of many of his pursuits and a deepening of his introspection.

Chapter 29

Listed as: Peron Spitzer (Joachim’s twin brother, allegedly dead by suicide)

  • A mysterious, volatile figure who lived a fragmented life.
  • His death profoundly impacts Mr. Spitzer, whose mourning colors his worldview.
  • Peron’s character contrasts with Mr. Spitzer’s logical nature; he is seen as a greater mystery.
  • His funeral drew “the merriest mourners who ever were”: dice shooters, vaudeville stars, mountebanks, and “ruined gamblers.”
  • He “airily dismissed Joachim from his mind when a purpose had been served” and would have shown “not a spark of gratitude.”
  • His coffin “had been sealed, and no one had looked upon his dead brother’s shattered face.”
  • “where the turf meets the surf”: The slogan of the Del Mar racetrack, opened in 1937 by Bing Crosby and Pat O’Brien. Crosby sang “Where the Turf Meets the Surf.”

Chapter 31

  • “not one horseshoe of the red and white roses… two dice cubes in a lucky throw, seven and eleven”: Floral horseshoes and dice were set on gamblers’ graves. Seven and eleven are winning throws in craps.

Chapter 33

  • The Boston newspaper reports him “seen recently walking along Benevolent Street, peering into old doorways.”
  • “even her funeral, as he remembered his”: Mr. Spitzer remembers his own funeral because he buried his twin. Young said, “Grief was so great that he thought the wrong brother died” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).

Chapter 34

  • He “apparently committed suicide, taking a coward’s way out.”

Chapter 35

  • Unlike Joachim, he “had always had an ace in the hole.”
  • His “astronomical gambling losses” kept Joachim “in a state of colossal bankruptcy for years.”
  • “Peron would never have lingered here.”
  • “unlike his brother, who had always had an ace in the hole”: In stud poker the hole card is dealt face down. An ace in the hole is a hidden winning card, and by the early 1900s the phrase meant any hidden advantage.

Chapter 36

  • “His brother would never have had such patience.”
  • He is the “superficial brother who had had no powers of memory or whose memory had been only in his own behalf.”
  • “if there had been no dead brother with his identical face”: The chapter portrays a triangular relationship between Peron, Joachim, and Catherine, marked by unfulfilled love, contrasting lives, and the tragic consequences of loss. Catherine’s preference for Peron and her rejection of Joachim create a dynamic of longing and sorrow that defines the emotional core of the narrative. Joachim embodies steadfastness and grief, while Peron represents passion and escape, and Catherine is caught between these polarities, her own existence fragile and dreamlike.

Chapter 37

  • “Peron had been right—and Joachim had been wrong.”
  • Hannah is “gone where poker-faced Peron was.”

Chapter 38

  • He was “this man of great wit but little brain—no legal knowledge in his head, no intellectual pretensions whatever.”
  • Catherine believes “he had expressed his love by his silence, his distance.”
  • “Sometimes she thought that only he lived.”

Chapter 41

  • A figure symbolizing tragic melancholy and finality in death.
  • “Peron had killed himself by jumping from the tallest building in a cloud as a star flashed, as a train whistled.”

Chapter 47

  • A contrast to Mr. Spitzer, more impulsive and superstitious, who had a different view of love and death.
  • His death catalyzed Mr. Spitzer’s deep reflections and mourning: Mr. Spitzer is grateful “for the fortunate error of his suicide,” which “had not been the end of wisdom but the beginning of wisdom.”
  • “His brother had died young, but had he ever married?”
  • “give up his assumption that all things which existed should be tangible”: Peron and Joachim as materialism and immaterialism. In philosophy, materialism says that everything that exists is fundamentally physical, composed of matter and energy. All phenomena, including consciousness, are ultimately reducible to physical processes. They point to the success of science in explaining the physical world and that consciousness can be explained by brain activity. Physicalism is similar in that everything can be explained by physics. Immaterialism believes there is an existence of entities or properties that are not physical, such as minds, consciousness, or spiritual realms. Non-physical entities or properties exist alongside or instead of the material world. Idealists believe that only minds and their perceptions are real, while the material world is a construction of the mind. Peron symbolizes the external, material world with its pleasures, follies, and impermanence, while Joachim represents the internal, immaterial realm of thought, memory, and spiritual seeking. Their contrast embodies the novel’s exploration of human existence as oscillating between these poles, neither fully attainable nor entirely separable, but always entwined in the complex dance of life and death. See Spinoza’s mind and body as one in the Chapter 45 resources.

Chapter 48

  • Contrasts with Mr. Spitzer by his flamboyance and willingness to “burn brightly,” symbolizing a different approach to life and death. His early death and vibrant life are a foil to Mr. Spitzer’s cautious and contemplative nature.
  • “Long before his apparent suicide, he had tempted fate in many ways of which he was unconscious even at his brightest moments.”
  • “Never was he so flamboyant as when his horse had lost… He sorrowed when he won.”

Chapter 49

  • “His brother could never have accepted an ineluctable reality such as Mr. Spitzer feared. His brother had thought that there was something he could do to change things, to make for greater hope or greater hopelessness.”
  • “They were born together.” Mr. Spitzer “did not know which brother he was, and this was the crux of his mystery.”
  • “or perhaps he was the third”: “Who is the third who walks always beside you? / When I count, there are only you and I together” (T. S. Eliot, The Waste Land, 1922).

Chapter 51

  • “the gambler had become the celestial musician”: The gambler is Peron and the musician Joachim; see Peron Spitzer.
  • “Had he given birth to his dead twin”: See Peron Spitzer.
  • “Never should he see his brother’s face”: Mr. Spitzer can see his brother only “through all the mazed images of the dead, through all the differences.” He lives through death no more miraculously than “some crazy old gambler who had thrown his last dice,” and he tries “to go legal,” as biased as “a left-handed portrait painter in a world of right-handed portrait painters.” “His effort to go straight had made him crooked,” and his way is “finally circular.” He practices only “in the courts which were paved with the moonstones of lost cases under a firmament of lost stars,” and he prefers “the seething silence to the sound, preferring mystery to exposure, preferring a cloak to nakedness.”
  • “perhaps had walked off the roof like his poor brother”: Here Peron walked off the roof of the building and not through the window as earlier, which brings to mind the 1929 stock market crash and the stories of investors and brokers jumping from buildings. The wave of such suicides is largely an urban myth, amplified by sensational newspapers and the dark humor of the time; John Kenneth Galbraith called it a legend (The Great Crash, 1929, 1955). There were suicides: a brokerage firm clerk and a member of the New York Mercantile Exchange jumped to their deaths in the financial district in the weeks after the crash, others died by gas, gun, or poison, and the suicide rate rose as the Depression deepened, from 17.0 per 100,000 people in 1929 to 21.3 in 1932. Winston Churchill, in New York at the time, wrote of a man who fell from a building near his hotel (Wikipedia). Although Miss MacIntosh pays a visit to Wall Street and invests “imaginary money,” it is not mentioned if Peron or Joachim invested in Wall Street.

Chapter 52

  • Mr. Spitzer’s dead twin is present in every list he makes, for he is “mindful always that he was two persons, himself and his dead brother, and that what appeared in one column should appear in the other.” His greatest fear is “that there might be no differing counterpart.”

Chapter 53

  • Mr. Spitzer’s brother, a gambler and more flamboyant, volatile personality.
  • Peron’s life is described as colorful, many-sided, and seemingly successful, but also transient and elusive.
  • His death creates a “rift” in Spitzer’s life and intensifies Spitzer’s struggle with his own existence and identity.

Chapter 54

  • Mr. Spitzer’s deceased brother, described as wild, gregarious, and reckless, almost the opposite of Joachim. Peron’s life and death haunt Joachim, and his presence is felt as a contrasting force driving Joachim’s reflections and wanderings.

Chapter 55

Listed as: Peron (Mr. Spitzer’s Brother)

  • A gambler, extrovert, and embodiment of chaos, risk, and unpredictability.
  • Lives a life of abandon, irresponsibility, and indifference to consequence.
  • His death leaves Joachim to bear the burdens and debts he left behind.
  • Represents the impulsive, irrational, and destructive forces in life, contrasting sharply with Joachim’s order.
  • His laughter and ghostly presence continue to haunt Joachim.

Chapter 56

Listed as: Peron

  • Peron is Mr. Spitzer’s deceased brother, described as eccentric with a flamboyant lifestyle, many coats and hats, and a lavish wardrobe. Peron’s death triggers Mr. Spitzer’s existential reflections.

Chapter 57

Listed as: Peron

  • Peron, Mr. Spitzer’s deceased brother, is described as a gambler and a man of dignity in death. Peron’s identity is intertwined with mystery and symbolic imagery (black suit, red rose, bright red necktie), representing a life ended but still profoundly present in Mr. Spitzer’s mind.

Chapter 59

Listed as: Peron Spitzer (Joachim’s twin brother)

  • A contrasting figure who feared life because it was brief.
  • His death deeply affects Mr. Spitzer and acts as a catalyst for many of Spitzer’s reflections.
  • Represents the opposite approach to life compared to Mr. Spitzer’s fear of life’s length.

Chapter 60

  • Peron appears as the gambler Mr. Spitzer measures himself against: a man who read only the sporting page, never attended a concert in his life, and could not have imagined silence.
  • He would have known how to call moose and wild ducks, and would have recognized the ducks’ horns “even if he had been in his grave.”
