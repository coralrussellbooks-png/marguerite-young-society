Chapter 46

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act · The Sacred Is Here

The Questions

Mr. Spitzer has “never yet…been present at a climax,” coming always before or after. What kind of witness never arrives at the resolving moment?

He finds “that things divine were things secular,” valuing “every corruscating pebble or spark.” Where does he locate the sacred?

The lutes of heaven, he knows, were “manufactured by the old lute-makers of earth.” What happens to paradise when its instruments are made by hand?

An alpenhorn belongs to the mountains; Mr. Spitzer keeps it in the level coastlands. What is an instrument out of its place?

Mr. Spitzer never arrives at the resolving moment, and the chapter states it as a law of him: “Never yet had he been present at a climax.” He came before Cousin Hannah died, or he came afterward, but not at the last instant, just as he cannot be present at the butterfly’s exact turn from chrysalis to flight. This is the Witness’s deepest shape. To witness is precisely not to be at the climax, not to seize the resolving moment and close it, but to attend the long before and the long after, the approach and the aftermath, and to let the resolving instant itself remain unwitnessed and unpossessed. His never being present at the climax is not a failure of timing. It is what witnessing is.

And he locates the sacred in the secular, which is The Sacred Is Here stated as plainly as the book ever states it. “Music arose from this chaos…that things divine were things secular, and so he valued…every corruscating pebble or spark shining through the iris-eyed fog.” The divine is not in a beyond; it is in the pebble, the spark, the seagull in the rotted hull, the ordinary shore he walks in the evening. Mr. Spitzer, who elsewhere yearns toward a heaven of silent music, here finds the holy underfoot, in the secular things his walks pass, and values them as “aspects of a dying world” rather than as steps toward a better one.

The instruments of paradise, he knows, are made by hand. The lutes played “by the angels of heaven were manufactured by the old lute-makers of earth,” men “in their graves,” and “none which had been strung in paradise.” This dismantles the beyond from the inside: there is no heavenly music that was not first built by mortal makers in an earthly workshop, no paradise whose furniture did not come from here. The Sacred Is Here presses all the way into heaven and finds it manufactured, which is not a debunking but a relocation, the sacred brought home to the hands that actually made the lute.

The alpenhorn out of place holds the chapter’s ache. Mr. Spitzer, as executor, keeps Cousin Hannah’s alpenhorn, an instrument for calling across mountains, in the flat coastlands “scarcely above the tide,” where there are no mountains for it to call across. “What could an alpenhorn evoke here?” It is the sacred object stranded from the elsewhere it was made for, and Mr. Spitzer keeps it anyway, the way he keeps all the instruments of a heaven that turns out to be earthly, valuing the made thing in the wrong landscape because the made thing, here, in the tide-level ordinary, is what there is.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that treats Mr. Spitzer’s never reaching the climax as a sad incapacity, and the reading that hears in his lutes-of-heaven a real paradise being described. Neither the failed timing nor the genuine beyond is what the chapter offers.

The footing is that the Witness attends the before and after and never seizes the resolving moment, so his absence from every climax is the discipline itself; that the divine is the secular, the sacred found in the pebble and the spark underfoot rather than in a beyond; and that even the instruments of paradise were made by earthly hands, so heaven is relocated to the workshop that built the lute. The sacred is here, and the elsewhere was manufactured here too. Mr. Spitzer keeps the alpenhorn in the landscape it was never meant for, valuing the made thing where he stands, and the chapter asks for no mountain to justify it.

For Reflection

Is there a kind of attention that works precisely by not being present at the decisive moment?

What sacred thing has one located in a distant beyond that might be found, instead, in the pebble underfoot?

If the instruments of every paradise were made by hand, here, what does that change about the beyond?
