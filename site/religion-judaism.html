Definition from research

The novel holds no single view of Jews or Judaism. It gives instead a spread of voices, from raw antisemitism to detached political opinion to genuine, complicated sympathy, and it is careful about whose mouth each view comes from.

The ugliest sentiments belong to characters the book has already marked as broken. Mr. Bonebreaker, the ranting Gospel salesman, slurs Jews and builds a heaven where a saved Christian's star outshines a Jew's, and Miss MacIntosh says plainly that this is unfair, even as the lake wind drowns her out. A widow in the Tavern Hotel spits a tirade that blames Jews for everything, praises Hitler, and turns just as viciously on Black people, a slur-laden outburst that indicts only herself. A rubber salesman is sure his failures are a "Jewish conspiracy." In each case the hatred sits in a mouth the reader cannot trust, which is the book's quiet way of judging it.

The most disturbing figure is the Little Toy Salesman, a grotesque who reads as a shrunken Hitler. Small, pot-bellied, bandy-legged, sucking milk from a baby bottle, he blames a "Jewish conspiracy" for his ruin and mutters about the "fleshly Jews" who bred and built the world he despises. He calls himself a murderer of women and children, the lone survivor of a storm that drowned everyone stronger, and he whispers to Esther of a coming war in which "bad men would blow up the world" for fun. The horror and the absurdity together, world-ending hatred packed into a diapered little body, are the point.

Miss MacIntosh's own view is political rather than personal. She hopes the Jews will "all go back to Palestine," asking simply "where would they go," a blunt, dated answer shaped by her anti-colonial cast of mind more than by any malice. Her scorn for money and the Stock Exchange as a "palace of evil" is general anti-capitalism, not a coded slur. Her wish belongs to its moment: the book's mid-century world was one in which the Holocaust had just made the case for a Jewish homeland impossible to ignore, and the state of Israel had newly and contentiously come into being on contested ground.

Against all this stands Joe Goldberg, the one fully drawn Jewish character, and the book's sympathy runs with him. A featherweight champion, "hard as rock," who "knocked out all his enemies," he is brought down by tuberculosis, which the novel calls "God's hand." Dying in the Colorado mountains, wasted and light as clouds, he imagines himself boxing God on a peak, a professional matched against amateurs in a fight with "no rules." His Jewishness is bound up with the fight itself: he traces his strength to a family of "fighters and rabbis," back to those who "fought the angels." He writes Esther long, aching letters, calls her the "peak of his existence," and begs to be remembered before he is "knocked out, forgotten, wrapped in a sheet." It is the most tender portrait in the section, a man who wants only to leave something of himself behind.

Vera, as ever, mostly watches. She records the hatreds and the tenderness alike without stepping in to judge, and the arrangement does the judging for her: the bigots are the deluded, and the Jewish life the book draws in full is the one it grieves for.
