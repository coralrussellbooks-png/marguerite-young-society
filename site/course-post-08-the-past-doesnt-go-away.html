Pillar: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away · Across every book

Act II: The Relational Edge

The past is not a country behind us. It is a heavy layer pressing upward directly underneath our feet.

The common picture of time is a road. The past is back there, shrinking in the rearview mirror, and the future is ahead. Moving on means driving forward. Yet anyone who has returned to a childhood home, or felt an old argument rise in a new relationship, knows the road picture is wrong. The past is not behind. It is below, and it pushes up.

The Framework

In William Faulkner's Requiem for a Nun (1951), the lawyer Gavin Stevens says, "The past is never dead. It's not even past." Faulkner's whole fictional county, Yoknapatawpha, is built on that principle. Slavery, war, land theft, and family secrets are not history there. They are the soil the characters walk on, and the soil is still shifting.

Marguerite Young builds time the same way, and her pacing makes the reader feel it. Her sentences are long and full of clauses, and each clause can reach back decades. A single paragraph in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling may hold Vera's present bus ride, her childhood in the seaside mansion, and her mother's opium visions all at once. The story barely moves forward. Instead it thickens, layer on layer, like sediment settling in still water. In Angel in the Forest, Young walked the streets of 1940s New Harmony and found its failed utopias of the 1810s and 1820s still pressing up through the town's houses and gardens.

Geology gives Radical Immanence its model. Sedimentary rock forms as layer after layer settles and compresses. Nothing is thrown away. The oldest layers bear the most weight, and when the ground shifts, they are the ones that fold upward into view. Personal and collective histories work the same way. A town's old rail line still sets the path of its poorest neighborhood. A grandmother's fear still shapes how her grandson handles money.

Seeing time this way changes how we act. If the past is behind, the task is to forget it. If the past is underfoot, the task is to read it, carefully, the way a geologist reads a cliff face. That reading is how old harms get repaired rather than repeated.

The Art Companion

The German painter Anselm Kiefer builds huge canvases from paint, straw, ash, lead, and dirt. His works return again and again to the buried weight of Germany's twentieth century. Standing before one feels like looking at a cross-section of earth that has swallowed history and cannot digest it.

Andrei Tarkovsky's film Mirror (1975) weaves a man's childhood, his mother's youth, wartime newsreels, and his present life into a single flowing texture. The scenes are not arranged in order. They are stacked, and each one seeps into the others.

Reflection Questions

  • What part of my family's past presses up in how I speak, eat, argue, or spend?

  • What old event in my town still shapes its streets, its divisions, or its silences?

  • Which of my past selves feels closest to the surface right now?

  • What would change if I treated the past as underfoot rather than behind?

The Exercise: Reading the Ground

  1. Walk a single block slowly, ideally an older one. Bring a notebook.

  2. List every clue of an earlier era: a faded advertisement painted on brick, a bricked-up doorway, a worn stone step, a street named for a vanished family, a tree older than any building nearby.

  3. Sort the clues into layers, oldest at the bottom of the page. Guess the decade for each.

  4. Choose one clue and research it through a library, a historical society, or an older neighbor. Write a short paragraph on how that buried layer still shapes the block today.

  5. At home, repeat the exercise on a single room. Which objects are inherited? Which habits came with them?
