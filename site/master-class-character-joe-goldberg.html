Joe Goldberg: The Champion Who Could Not Be Remembered

← Walter · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · The Chicago Detective →

Spoiler note: This portrait is written for readers who have finished the novel. It discusses the character’s whole story, including how it ends.

Characters: Esther Longtree · Joe Goldberg · Miss MacIntosh

He writes from the top of a frozen mountain to the one person who has forgotten him. Joe Goldberg, "the golden cock, the featherweight champion who had worn a crown of golden feathers," is dying of tuberculosis in the Colorado snow, and he spends his last strength on letters to Esther Longtree, begging her to remember one night in a graveyard years ago when he "took her against her will" and, he believes, fathered her child. She was "the peak of his existence," he tells her, greater than any crowd in Chicago or St. Louis, the one triumph "that God could not take away." And she cannot place him. She reads his letters "with squinting eyes," she cannot finish them because they are "too long and fine to read to the end," and for the life of her she cannot remember the champion, because, the book says with terrible simplicity, "she could remember the weak but not the strong."

Esther holds every sterile, weeping salesman who ever cried in her arms, the puny fathers, the ones who lost. The one man who came to her strong and victorious, who wanted to be remembered for his strength, is the one man she cannot find in her memory at all. He weighs nothing there, "too much like an angel's feather for her to know him." He staked his immortality on being witnessed by her, and she is the witness who keeps only the defeated. His demand to be held for his victory is the very thing that makes him unholdable.

His last fight is with God, in an arena the book strips from everyone else. "There was no umpire," "no audience, no crowd," "the bleachers were empty and white as bones," and he is "boxing with shadows," "fighting with his imagination only." God is "a professional, fighting with weak amateurs," there are no rules in the game, and Joe is losing to soft blows he cannot even feel until he is on his knees. He fought his whole life to see "the surprised look in the other guy's eyes" as they fell and glimpsed the empty heaven, and now God is giving him that look, feinting and slow and absent-minded, knocking out the man light as a feather. This is the wheel of fortune the book reads its own way, the champion who was spread wide at the top now stripped to the vertical line, alone, in contact at last with what he is made of. And Joe cannot accept the bottom. He keeps insisting he "won every battle but the last," keeps reaching for the crown "of dove feathers" and the roaring crowd, keeps framing his death as a bout he might still take. The one thing the philosophy asks, that a person stand honestly at the bottom and witness where he actually is, is the one thing the champion will not do. He wants the horizontal back. He wants the applause.

His deepest hope is the one the book denies him with the most tenderness. He believes he left "the only real mark he had ever made on anyone" in Esther, a child, a piece of himself carried forward. And the child "was never born and were never conceived, so far as she could recall." She remembers the graveyard, the tombs, the angel whose nose he knocked off as they struggled, all "the details that she knew so well," but she cannot attach the details to the man. The mark is there; the maker is gone from it. She was marked and cannot name what marked her, and so his one bid to survive himself dies in the memory of the only woman who could have carried it.

The turn for a writer is the nakedest fear in the whole set, because Joe Goldberg is the artist who works for posterity and is forgotten by the reader he worked for. He sends his self out in letters, "with trembling fingers, with bleeding eyes," a speck of his own blood on the white paper, and the reader cannot finish the pages, cannot tell him from any other man who passed through, "travelling salesman or tramp." He fights the blank and holy opponent in an empty arena where no one watches, which is the writing life. And his one consolation, that he left his mark, that the work touched someone, is answered by a woman who kept the mark and lost the man. Set him beside Miss MacIntosh's fled lover, whose umbrella is kept while he is forgotten, and beside Esther, who mourns the weak and cannot see the strong. The champion learns what the book keeps teaching, that memory holds the ones who suffered and lets the victors fall through, that the surest way to be forgotten is to demand to be remembered for having won. He wanted to be the golden cock crowned in feathers. He is remembered, if at all, as the weight of a feather, drifting, that no one caught.
