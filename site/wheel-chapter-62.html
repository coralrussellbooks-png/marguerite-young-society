Research notes: Chapter 62 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 62

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The chapter opens with Mr. Spitzer uncertain whether he has ever slept, and this uncertainty is not incidental; it is constitutive. He cannot locate himself in time, in consciousness, or in identity. He is simultaneously:

  • Joachim Spitzer the musician, described as "dead musician and author of the music of the spheres"
  • Joachim Spitzer the lawyer, "Harvard graduate magna cum laude and attorney at law, retired"
  • The conductor of a water orchestra he has never assembled
  • The gargoyle "spouting water music from his mouth"
  • The pack rat's counterpart, existing in an exchange economy of stolen and returned objects
  • His brother's echo, wearing "his brother's milky blue stone ring", writing with both left and right hands as two different selves

The most explicit statement of plural selfhood comes near the end: "he was Mr. Spitzer now — there were not two. There was one. There was one who had gone over to the many". This is not resolution into unity but collapse of distinction: the plural self not reconciled but acknowledged as irreducibly multiple. His brother is not a separate person but a layer of himself, a sediment (see Pillar 8) pressing up through his identity.

The will itself, written by his left hand and witnessed by his right hand, is the legal document of a plural self attempting to notarize its own multiplicity. The absurdity is philosophical: you cannot witness yourself because there is no stable self to do the witnessing.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter's entire architecture is built on the impossibility of distinguishing illusion from reality. Mr. Spitzer's dream sequence at the opening does not end; it bleeds into his waking actions. He writes his will "by the pale light of the old oil chimney lamp... still burning in the sunlight", where night's light and day's light fuse. There is no clean boundary between sleeping and waking, between dream river and real river.

The dream itself performs this: "But perhaps all these rivers were one great river of reality burning from light to darkness, darkness to light, water to freezing, ice to star". The word "reality" appears here inside the dream: reality is not what the dream obscures but what the dream is. The larger cave, more carefully lit.

The most devastating instance of this pillar is the violinist of the Philadelphia Symphony, who spent twenty years creating the illusion of music where no music was, never touching the strings, missing them "by an infinitesimal distance." He was praised as the perfect example of musicianship. When he accidentally touched the strings, when he crossed into reality; he was dismissed. The system rewarded the construction of illusion and punished contact with the real. This is not satirical exaggeration. It is the chapter's central parable: the conductor could not tolerate reality breaking through the performance of reality.

Mr. Spitzer's own music participates in this: he describes compositions full of "sounds which never should be heard" and "ultimate mysteries unsolvable", music that is more real for being unplayable, because the playable is always already a reduction of what was intended.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Grief in this chapter is not an event. It is atmospheric. It does not attach to a single loss but permeates everything: the composition of music no one plays, the brother whose ring Mr. Spitzer wears, the cities under the frozen sand, the cancelled stamps of exploded monarchies, the dead clarinetist, the tubercular horn-blower.

The chapter's weather is consistently elegiac. Mr. Spitzer writes "aquatic nocturnes", "flooded pastorales", a "Pavanne for a Dead Lady", a "Pavanne for the Dead Polar Bear and the Entombed Butterfly", an "Elegy for a Frog Eating a Firefly", and, most tellingly, "his elegy for himself who was dead and entombed in this life as in a dark star".

He is writing elegies for things that have not yet finished dying, including himself. This is grief as permanent weather in its purest form: "death did not balance with life. Death was greater than life — as night was greater than day". There is no consolation offered, no stage of acceptance reached. The chapter ends not with resolution but with Mr. Spitzer crossing Louisburg Square, absent-minded, thinking he was in Philadelphia: geographically lost inside his own grief, unable to locate himself in the present because the past is so much heavier.

The pack rat exchange (musical scrap for musical scrap, pearl button for apple core) is the chapter's small, tender acknowledgment that even grief has its economies, its strange reciprocities. But crucially, the exchange never balances: "whoever had stolen away his life, his death had brought nothing, nothing in return".

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Mr. Spitzer is constitutionally incapable of looking away. The chapter is structured as an act of radical witnessing, of the horn-blower destroying his lungs, of the suicidal clarinetist playing from the Gloucester rooftop, of the silent violinist on the Library steps, of his own dying.

His response to the horn-blower is the clearest enactment of this pillar. Reason would suggest: stop blowing, save your life. But Mr. Spitzer, recognizing that reason would be its own violence here, instead says: "Blow on, horn-blower, blow on! Blow moon and sun and stars before thee!". This is not irresponsibility. It is the witness refusing to impose resolution on another's irreducible situation. He stays in the room. He does not flinch from the blood-specked phlegm, the spurting horn, the numbered days.

The scene with the silent violinist is even more extraordinary. Mr. Spitzer watches for an extended duration, his eyes sometimes "rolled back completely in his head", in a state of total receptive attention. He does not intervene. He does not explain. He does not simplify what he sees into a category (beggar, madman, genius). He simply witnesses, and then cries "Bravo, bravo!", which is the only appropriate response to something witnessed fully: acknowledgment without reduction.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Mr. Spitzer has been writing his will for so long it is "already several volumes long". He has been composing music his entire life in "scraps and fragments". He keeps "this task for his leisure years" of arranging his musical phrases into one great symphony: leisure years that will never come, and he knows it.

The will is the perfect emblem of recursive construction: a legal document that attempts to conclude, to settle, to distribute, but which keeps growing, keeps returning to its beginning, keeps adding codicils and cautionary phrases. It is witnessed by the same hand that writes it. It is stamped with a notary's seal "of about the period of the turn of the century", already obsolete at the moment of use.

His music operates the same way: "his scraps of paper flying around him like leaves merely reminded him of something attempted". The building is never finished. He knows this. He builds anyway: writing on shirt cuffs, on black handkerchiefs, in pigeon holes and warped drawers. The recursive return is not defeat. It is the only honest form the work can take.

The pack rat is the chapter's figure for recursive construction in its most elemental form: taking and returning, building a nest from stolen materials, exchanging one thing for another in an endless economy of provisional making.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

This pillar is perhaps the chapter's most explicit philosophical concern. Mr. Spitzer directly addresses it:

"for what great musical composition was ever played as its composer had written it, and had not many great composers written their works with visual patterns none could play"

The music that cannot be played is not failed music. It is the most honest music, because it refuses to pretend that what can be expressed equals what was intended. The gap between intention and execution is not a technical problem; it is the condition of all making.

The clarinetist who committed suicide leaving the note "I am out of tune. I have gone to join a greater orchestra" is the tragic version of this pillar: the musician who could not survive his own incompletion, who needed a perfection this world cannot provide. Mr. Spitzer mourns him but does not judge him. The greater orchestra the clarinetist sought is, in Radical Immanence terms, a fantasy of completion, and yet Mr. Spitzer finds in this fantasy something beautiful, something worth mourning.

The recording angel who has "lost all his feathers but one", keeping his last pen for his last note, is the most poignant image of ethical incompletion in the chapter. He cannot write everything. He must choose his last note carefully. This is not failure of the recording project. It is the integrity of the ongoing sentence refusing false conclusion.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Every musician in this chapter is marginal. The tubercular horn-blower destroying himself. The suicidal clarinetist. The silent violinist dismissed for touching the strings. Mr. Spitzer himself, "never seen in musical circles, never seen at a Pop concert in the Great Shell, never seen along the windy Esplanade", is the most marginal of all: a musician who has never had his music played, a lawyer who witnesses his own will, a man who cannot remember his own address.

But the chapter makes clear that these marginal figures are not exceptions to the musical world; they are its most truthful representatives. The violinist who never touched the strings was praised as the perfect example. When he accidentally produced reality, he was expelled. The margin is where the constructed nature of the center becomes visible.

Mr. Spitzer's sympathy "with all derelicts and particularly wandering musicians whose music could not be recognized or musicians who played upon unrecognizable instruments" is not charity. It is epistemological solidarity: the recognition that the unrecognized musician knows something about music that the recognized musician, safely inside the system, cannot access.

The man kicked out of the orchestra "knew more about music than any playing in an orchestra, for the player was under the powers of the despotic conductor and could play only his own part". Marginality grants a totality of perception that inclusion forecloses.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The chapter's prose style is itself sedimentary; each sentence deposits layer upon layer, never clearing away what came before. The cancelled postage stamps of "exploded monarchies", the "old Polish kings who had been deposed", Queen Victoria in her youth, the great Consort in his age, the Leaning Tower of Pisa, Brooklyn Bridge, the Greek drachma of the period of Theseus; these are not random accumulations. They are the sediment of history pressing up through the drawers of Mr. Spitzer's desk.

The cities are the most explicit expression of this pillar: "Nineveh and Tyre and Ur of the Chaldees and Carthage... cities under the frozen sand of the Great Gobi Desert". These are not metaphors for loss. They are layers beneath the present, still there, still pressing. Mr. Spitzer writes the street music of dead cities, not as elegy for what is gone but as acknowledgment of what is still present beneath.

His own memory performs this sedimentary logic: "he had known since memory began... that all his memories were not his own, that they came from an alien source". The past that accumulates in him is not only his past. It is the past of all those who deposited experience before he arrived: his brother, the musicians he has known, the cities under the sand. The image of the old roll-top desk with its warped "drawers he had not opened for years" is the chapter's domestic figure for sedimentary time: the past is literally there, in the drawers, waiting. Opening them does not retrieve a past that has gone. It surfaces a past that never left.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The prose style of this chapter is itself the argument. The sentences do not end; they accrete, qualify, contradict, revise. This is language performing its own constructedness, refusing the false closure of the period, the false authority of the declarative sentence.

Mr. Spitzer writes his will with "spidery signature so delicate that one should look at it through a magnifying glass or even a telescope": language so fine it requires magnification to be read, so personal it approaches illegibility. This is the opposite of legal language, which pretends to speak from nowhere, to apply universally. Mr. Spitzer's legal language names its own location: written by a dead man, witnessed by himself, sworn to by the Pledge of Allegiance instead of the usual oath.

The music itself functions as language in this chapter, but language that "should be influenced by the waves and the wind blowing the strings", language that cannot be separated from its material conditions. There is "no score, no sheet music but the swelling waves". The score is the world. The world is the score. This is language that has stopped pretending to be a neutral vehicle.

The silent violinist's "beautiful sign language" is the chapter's ideal of honest language: a language that makes no pretense of transparency, that is visibly, physically, located in the body of the one who speaks it.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Mr. Spitzer does not look to heaven. He looks at the bull's-eye lamp, the checkered oilcloth, the warped drawers, the pearl buttons, the pack rat's exchange. The sacred in this chapter is entirely immanent: located in ordinary objects, ordinary exchanges, ordinary failures.

The water music is the chapter's central image of immanent transcendence. It is not music that points toward heaven. It is music that "should be played only upon swelling water, rivers or lakes or oceans": music that points deeper into the material world, into the waves, the cordages, the ear shells, the wings of migratory butterflies.

The dying dolphin, the sea anemones, the firefly, the pack rat; these are the chapter's sacred figures. Not angels, not gods, but creatures at the edges of visibility, creatures whose existence is fragile and brief and therefore most fully present.

The recording angel is the chapter's one explicitly supernatural figure, but he is presented as exhausted, nearly featherless, keeping his last pen for his last note. He is not transcendent. He is immanently depleted, subject to the same economies of loss as everything else. Even the angel is inside the world, not above it.

The most beautiful statement of immanent transcendence in the chapter: "he would live as long as his music lived and breathed". Not in heaven. Not in memory. In the music itself, in the material vibration of the world.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This pillar is the chapter's deepest structure. Mr. Spitzer is explicitly described as constituted by what came before him and constituting what comes after:

"just as one man might be two bodies existing sequentially as in time, perhaps one existing when the other was dead, so also one man might be two bodies existing simultaneously as in space"

He is the crossing point of his brother's life and his own, of the dead musicians and the unborn listeners, of the music written and the music never played. His will (his "mortmain", his "dead hand") is the legal instrument of the crossing point: the dead reaching forward to constitute the conditions of the living.

The music he bequeaths "to the generations", "free as a cloud driven down the wind", is not a gift from a completed self to a future self. It is the structural obligation of the crossing point: the ancestors in the sediment, the carrying continuing.

The final image of the chapter (Mr. Spitzer crossing Louisburg Square, asking where he is, being told "Boston", murmuring "My God! I thought I was in Philadelphia") is the crossing point made comic and heartbreaking simultaneously. He does not know where he is because he is constituted by so many places, times, and selves that no single location can contain him. He is not lost. He is everywhere at once, which from the outside looks exactly like being lost.
