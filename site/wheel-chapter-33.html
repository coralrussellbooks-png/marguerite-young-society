Chapter 33

On Chapter 33 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of The Wheel and the Spokes.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 32 · Wheel and Spokes Home · Chapter 34 →

Research notes: Chapter 33 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 33

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The chapter refuses to stabilize any single identity. My mother is simultaneously alive and reported dead, simultaneously a "famous beauty" and a forgotten recluse, simultaneously a bride and someone who "had never been married". She is married to a camel, a church steeple, a key, a keyhole, the wind, a flame. These are not metaphors for a single hidden truth. They are competing, simultaneous, irresolvable versions of selfhood.

Mr. Spitzer himself is doubled: perpetually shadowed by his brother Peron, seen walking on Benevolent Street, whose debts Mr. Spitzer inherits, whose watch he squints at to "detect a moment's falsehood". He is never quite one person either; he is himself plus the negative space of his brother.

Even the narrator exists only as a grammatical position, "my mother", never named, never located, present only as a relational axis. The self here is not a substance. It is a set of relations that keep shifting.

"She would often say that she was the kind of woman who could love only the man she had never married, perhaps the man she had not known, had not seen."

The self is constituted by its absences, its negations, its uncompleted relations. This is Not One Self not as pathology but as ontological condition.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

This pillar is perhaps the chapter's central philosophical axis. Mr. Spitzer explicitly theorizes it:

"her conscious states were probably like the unconscious states of other people"

He does not say she is more deluded than others. He says the lamp-lighters, the factory workers at their time-clocks and wheels: they too can live only while they dream. They too were never quite awake. The opium is not the cause of unreality. It is merely the visible mechanism of a universal condition.

The Boston newspaper's error, reporting my mother as dead, is not treated as a simple mistake to correct. Mr. Spitzer reflects that "this Boston newspaper, though noted for its errors, would probably be right, having merely mistaken the time". The distinction between true and false collapses into a distinction between premature and eventual. The lie will become true. The truth was always provisional.

The wedding itself, that foundational event, dissolves under scrutiny: "her powers of ratiocination were not good", she cannot reconstruct it, she may have been married to Death, she cannot confirm the bridegroom's identity. There is no sunlit world outside the cave. The wedding, like all originary events, exists only as sedimented impression, partially retrieved, partially invented.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The chapter is saturated with grief, but it never moves. It does not arc toward resolution. It accumulates.

Mr. Spitzer carries "this diffused sense of woe, this sorrow which seemed to have no source in his own life". This is the precise phenomenology of grief as permanent weather: it is not attached to a specific loss, not traceable to an event, not curable by understanding its origin. It is climate. He inherited it, or it preceded him, or it is simply what it is to be a conscious being in this world.

My mother's grief is equally sourceless and total. She grieves the social seasons that never were, the garden parties attended by no one, the yacht meets with kings in canvas shrouds. She grieves not what she had and lost, but what was never recognized while it existed. This is grief without even the consolation of having been real.

The wedding music is described as "a vast overture which was never quite completed, an eternal prelude to nothing". Grief here is not the aftermath of something. It is the condition of living in a world where completion never arrives.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Mr. Spitzer is the chapter's primary ethical figure, and his ethics are precisely those of the Witness. He does not fix. He does not conclude. He stays in the room.

He chooses not to correct the newspaper's error about my mother's death. He chooses not to correct her wedding memories. He chooses not to intervene in her opium-sustained illusions. This is not passivity born of indifference; it is a principled refusal to simplify, a recognition that correction would be its own violence:

"What should she do with accuracy, and why should it confine her? Who was he to correct these surface impressions or differentiate between black and white, green and yellow and red? Who was he to say that a thing was different from what it seemed? How would he know?"

His witnessing extends to the physical: when my mother speaks to a human-sized blackbird and says to open the door, Mr. Spitzer gets up and opens the door. He does not argue. He does not diagnose. He acts within her reality. This is attention as practice, taken to its logical conclusion; he enters the world as she perceives it rather than demanding she enter his.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Mr. Spitzer's harp music is the chapter's clearest emblem of recursive construction:

"he was writing harp music which should be the exact imitation of the wind playing through the strings and cables of a bridge... and his music should be finished only with the dying wind"

The composition can only be completed by something outside itself, the wind's death, which means it is structurally unfinishable. He returns to it, measures it, studies "the velocity of wind over bridges, the tonnage of wind pressure against cables", accumulating data toward a work that recedes as he approaches. He builds anyway. The building is never finished. That is not failure. That is how it works.

Similarly, my mother recursively reconstructs her wedding: returning to it, finding different details each time, arriving at different bridegrooms. The reconstruction is the event. There is no original beneath the reconstructions to recover.

Mr. Spitzer also mails himself a letter he never receives: a perfect image of recursive construction: the self attempting to communicate with itself across time, the message marked with the sender's own return address, containing blank paper. The content is nothing. The act of sending and waiting is everything.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter is formally and thematically committed to incompletion. Sentences extend across pages. The wedding is never reconstructed. The music is never finished. The letter is never delivered. The dead letter office holds the message indefinitely.

Mr. Spitzer explicitly defends incompletion as the honest condition:

"there were many more important errors to rectify"

meaning: the completed correction of any single error would be a false closure that obscures the larger incompleteness of all knowledge. He allows things to take their course "for they ultimately would", not because he is resigned, but because he understands that forcing resolution is its own distortion.

My mother's memory is described as "always quite beautifully inaccurate", and Mr. Spitzer's modifier beautifully is philosophically precise. The inaccuracy is not ugly failure. It is the honest shape of a consciousness that refuses to falsify experience by completing it prematurely.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Every figure in this chapter exists at the margins of recognized social reality. My mother is reported dead while alive. She entertains "ambassadors to vanished courts and kings of vanished realms": people whose social categories no longer exist, who are marginal not by personal failure but by historical obsolescence.

Mr. Spitzer is marginal by disposition: furtive, avoiding strangers, carrying his brother's debts to creditors he cannot identify. He exists in the gap between himself and his brother, between the living and the dead, between the correctable and the uncorrectable.

The figures at my mother's wedding (king penguins, melancholy seals, tinkling duchesses) are at the edge of the human, the edge of the real, the edge of the socially legible. They are the evidence that the categories of the real are constructed, that the center is maintained only by constant suppression of the marginal.

The human-sized blackbird is perhaps the chapter's most concentrated image of this pillar: a figure that is simultaneously person and animal, that participates in conventional social exchange (opening doors), that exists at the categorical boundary where the constructed nature of "the human" becomes most visible.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This pillar is enacted in the chapter's very texture. The past does not recede. It presses upward through every sentence.

My mother's wedding, possibly decades past, is more present than the present moment. The social seasons that never were accumulate beneath her current despair. Mr. Spitzer carries his brother's financial debts and his mother's emotional debts and his own unlived life simultaneously, all pressing upward at once.

The newspaper's error about my mother's death is treated as a temporal rather than factual mistake: "having merely mistaken the time". This is sedimentary time precisely: the past and future are not separate from the present but are layers of the same geological formation. The death is real; it is simply not yet.

Mr. Spitzer's walk down Benevolent Street the evening after reading about his brother retraces his brother's steps; he moves through space that is already layered with another's passage. The street is not neutral. It is sediment. His black cape whirling "like the sobbing of the rain upon the river Styx" marks him as a figure who moves through multiple temporal layers simultaneously: the living world and the underworld occupying the same geography.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter is acutely self-conscious about language's constructive power. The newspaper's error does not merely describe my mother as dead; it constitutes a social reality in which she is dead, one that Mr. Spitzer must decide whether to correct or allow to stand. Language here is not reporting on a pre-existing fact. It is creating a social fact.

My mother's wedding vows, whatever they were, constructed a marriage that she simultaneously insists never happened. The language of the ceremony did or did not create the marriage, but the uncertainty is not resolvable by appeal to what "really" occurred, because what really occurred is itself a construction of memory and narration.

The passage about her wedding music, "many reverberations, many keys and strings and wandering echoes", describes language itself: echoes of echoes, meaning that has lost its origin, signification that continues after the signifying event has dissolved. The text performs what it describes.

Mr. Spitzer's blank letter is the purest image of language as construction: he mails himself a message containing nothing, marked with his own address, and waits for it. The envelope, the address, the postal system, all the apparatus of linguistic communication, surround an absence. The construction is complete. The content is void. This is language naming its own location: I am the structure that surrounds what cannot be said.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter is entirely immanent; it does not point outward to a beyond but inward and downward into the thickness of ordinary experience.

My mother's opium visions are not escapes from reality. They are intensifications of the immanent: the world made more itself, more saturated, more present. Mr. Spitzer recognizes this:

"hers was a greater freedom, a greater aura of improbable possibility than if she had ever closed a door or written finis to a deathless dream"

The sacred here is the deathless dream, not a transcendent realm but the ordinary world experienced without the violence of closure.

The wedding music ("ethereal", vast, never completed) is sacred not because it points to God but because it points deeper into experience, into the wind, into the harp strings, into the cables of bridges. Mr. Spitzer's harp composition is a sacred project precisely because it seeks to imitate this world (the wind on this bridge, the pressure of these cables) with perfect fidelity. The sacred is the ordinary world more fully attended to.

Even grief is sacred here. The "diffused sense of woe" that Mr. Spitzer carries is not a problem to solve but a form of contact with the weight of existence: the immanent transcendence of being a conscious creature in a world where everything is always already passing.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is perhaps the pillar most structurally present in the chapter's deepest architecture. The narrator exists only as "my mother": constituted entirely by the sediment deposited before their arrival, speaking from within a structure they did not choose.

Mr. Spitzer is explicitly a crossing point: he carries his brother's debts, his mother's unlived life, his own unfinished music, the dead letter addressed to himself. He is where time crosses itself: the living man who has attended his own funeral, who walks the streets of Boston as if haunting them, who squints at his dead brother's watch to detect falsehood in the present moment.

My mother's wedding, that founding event, is the crossing point of multiple times: the remembered past, the misremembered past, the imagined past, the present moment of remembering, the future death already reported. All of these cross in the single act of her trying to reconstruct what happened.

The chapter ends with Mr. Spitzer opening a door that is already open: a perfect image of the Crossing Point. He acts in response to a reality he cannot perceive, constituting the ground of someone else's experience, carrying forward an obligation that is not chosen but structural. The ancestors are in the sediment. The carrying continues. He opens the door.
