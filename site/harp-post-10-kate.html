Chapter 10. Kate

← Chapter 9 · The Unfinished Harp: Reading Harp Song for a Radical · Chapter 11 →

A marriage history has judged harshly, and Young's attempt at fairness

Kate Metzel Debs has had bad press. Debs's devoted younger brother Theodore disliked her, and later writers, most memorably Irving Stone in his 1947 novel Adversary in the House, cast her as a cold, social-climbing wife who stood in her husband's way. The chapters from page 316 to about 352 are Young's attempt to see her more fairly. They do not make her a heroine. They make her a person.

The girl at the ironing board

Young gives Kate a hard start. Her father, a German immigrant of mechanical talent, died young. Her mother married a widower with children of his own, John Jacob Bauer, and moved the family to Terre Haute (316). Kate stayed home helping with the younger children.

Debs first saw her through a kitchen window at night, "leaning above an ironing board" (318). Asked years later why he married her, he said there was no mystery beyond love itself: "quite simply, he had fallen head over heels with the girl" (318). Kate said she had been drawn by "his electrifying power and his brilliance of mind" and that he had awakened her interest in woman suffrage, though she had no wish to be in public. "She had been content to stay behind the scenes. She had polished the hearthstones" (318).

Theodore's view and Young's

Young lays out the case against Kate through Theodore, for whom she was "a self-adorning clotheshorse" (319). He believed she had schemed to catch his brother. Young grants that Kate "was never to accompany him on his missions in behalf of brotherhood" and was "not the wild duck" some would have chosen for him but "a very domestic duck" (318).

Then she pushes back. Theodore, she writes, should have stopped to consider what it meant to be married to a man who was never home. Her sympathy lies with the wife left behind, and she pays close attention to the hours. Theodore himself said that short working hours "were as unknown to Debs... as if he had been the citizen of another planet" (348). Tenants in his office building called him "the man who never sleeps" (348).

Kate kept "the porch light burning in expectation of his return" (350). Young's chapter on her nights alone begins with a sharp question about woman's work and whether it was ever done (345). This is Grief Doesn't End in a domestic key. The marriage was loving and also lonely, and Young refuses to resolve it into either.

The child who never came

The Debses had no children. Young states that "The couple had desired a large family" (332), and then offers a cautious speculation, marked as such. Kate may have feared being left a widow with small children if her husband died on the rails or in a strike, as so many railroad widows had been (335–336). Young does not know, and she says so by hedging. It is a guess from inside the fear of a railroad wife, and she offers it as nothing more.

The crane in the watermark

In the middle of the Kate chapters, Young describes something Kate learned about her husband's world: the blacklist. Railroads would dismiss a union man with a glowing letter of reference on company stationery. Held to the light, the paper showed a watermark of a crane. A crane "with broken legs" meant the man's union had been broken and he could be hired. A crane "standing upright on its legs" meant he belonged to an active union and must not be (345).

It is the clearest image in the book of Language Shapes What's Possible. The letter said one thing in ink and another in the paper itself. A worker carried his own sentence of unemployment from town to town without being able to read it.

Money in a grocery sack

Young ends this stretch with a detail about Debs and money that says as much as any speech. He never used a bank. He carried the union's remaining coins in an old grocery sack to his brother-in-law's drugstore and kept them in the safe (339). Kate, Young suggests, worried with good reason about a husband who gave away what little came in.

What the lens shows

Not One Self here belongs to a marriage. Kate was domestic and cultured, supportive and resentful, loved and left alone, all at once. Young refuses Theodore's single verdict and Stone's melodrama alike. This is Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act applied to the woman at the edge of the great man's story, whose view from the porch is as real as his view from the platform.

Checking the record

  • The marriage. Debs married Kate Metzel on June 9, 1885, as the book's chronology says.

  • Kate's reputation. The harsh image of Kate owes much to Theodore Debs's accounts and to Irving Stone's novel. Later historians, including Nick Salvatore, have drawn a more balanced picture, closer to Young's.

  • The watermark blacklist. Young presents the crane watermark as fact. Blacklisting of union railroad men was real and widespread. The specific crane watermark belongs to railroad labor lore and is hard to document. It is best read as a story workers told, which Young, characteristically, did not separate from the record.
