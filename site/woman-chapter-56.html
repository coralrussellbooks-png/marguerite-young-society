Chapter 56

On Chapter 56 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Woman in Young's Magnum Opus.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Clothing is the chapter's whole preoccupation, and the mask made of cloth is its pillar. Mr. Spitzer dresses his dead brother for the funeral, choosing the formal swallow-tail over the loud checkered suits Peron wore alive, dressing the corpse "like himself" and not like the man it was, and the performance is masculinity constructed from the outside, the self as mask, gender kept up by what a body is wrapped in even after the body has stopped. The poker chips and dice and racing forms laid in as "coins" to pay the ferry boatman tie manhood to gambling and risk and sport, and death made "a formal occasion" requiring proper dress is the performative self carried across the boundary between life and death, the mask worn to the far shore.

The funeral crowds with male ritual, musicians and a bugler, the "official track bugler" whose prestigious career marks the patriarchal institutional power that presides over mourning, and against it Mr. Spitzer imagines his own funeral of "lyres and harps" made of flowers, delicate and feminine-coded, the crossing point tucked into a daydream of how he would rather be buried. Peron himself is a "squid changing colors and evading capture," a self that will not be pinned to one shape, and the shape-shifting is the plural selfhood the book carries in the brothers, gender and identity both refusing to hold still.

Mr. Spitzer gives Peron's clothing away, the hunting jackets and jockey suits, the hats and scarves, the "ass's head" hat and Balaklava helmets, the jewel-colored jockey suits and medieval clown costumes distributed to homeless men, and the dispersal of a wardrobe is the dispersal of a self and a legacy, clothing as the layers of a gender that comes apart when its wearer does. The tiny "dwarf's suit" among them, and Spitzer's question of who mourns the small and fragile, is the book's care for the marginalized and the non-normative, and his grief that "mourned for everything," breaking the lines between subject and object, life and death, self and other, is the illusion of the individual dissolving in a widower's blurred sight.

Reality itself goes "unreal" for him here, a "dead man walking," Spitzer and Peron interchangeable, appearances all clouds and shadows and changing colors, and the absence of any true "being" underneath is the constructed, mutable self the book prefers to any essence. Barnacles and seaweed cling to him, "all things, being none," and he asks the tailor who clothed him whether he is a "poor puppet" or a "tailor's dummy," a self with no author, holding a "golden key" to a door that is "no more." The chapter ends on the dead musician who keeps singing, the "demented sea shells," the "silken skies torn in two," the music that goes unheard sounding past the death of the one who made it, and no architect anywhere to have made either the man or the world he mourns in.
