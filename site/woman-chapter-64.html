Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 64

← Chapter 63 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 65 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Lorena · Miss MacIntosh · Solomon the Turtle · Vera Cartwheel

Miss MacIntosh loved once, she tells Vera in this chapter, and it was a woman, and the crossing point moves here from the body to the heart. Lorena was a "beautiful dancing girl," dark and fat, an orphan under Miss MacIntosh's care who became a strip-tease dancer, and the single love of this plain, grounded woman's life reaching toward another woman is the crossing point in its love-object, the seam the book writes from opened not in disguise or in the mirror but in whom a heart is allowed to want. Solomon the turtle opens the chapter and Miss MacIntosh dismisses the loss of him, "you will never miss him," the small crossing creature set down early so the larger one can arrive.

The wig comes on and off through the chapter as it always does, and Vera's love for the bald woman underneath is the book affirming worth beyond hair and beauty and the shape a body is supposed to keep. Miss MacIntosh's groundedness, her reality against the illusions Vera's mother lives in, is the pole the book has leaned on since Chapter 3, and it is more poignant here because the reader now knows the pole loves a woman, keeps a bald head under its helmet of hair, and carries the crossing inside its plainest self.

Her life story is the economic vulnerability of a woman without support, the impoverished origins, the drifting through employments, the lack of any institution to catch her, and the mastectomy and the lifelong baldness are the body as the place the self comes apart carried through a working life. The horse-hair wig she makes herself, and the boys who try to hook it off her head, are the self as mask and the detachable self and the world's policing of a woman who does not fit, the same hook the head-hunter reached for when he wanted her head, the same detachable self the whole book keeps handling.

Her ancestry and her rejection of romantic ideals, her disdain for the idle rich and for the empire's false values, widen her feminism from the body outward into a critique of the structures that starve and sort people, and her insistence on labor and plainness over ornament is the same refusal she wears on her scalp. The chapter leaves her a woman who loved a woman, kept a bald head under a horse-hair wig, and would not be reduced to the roles or the beauty the world offered her, autonomous in the flesh and in the heart at once.
