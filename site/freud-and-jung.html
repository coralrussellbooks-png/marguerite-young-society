Definition from research

Chapter 1

  • Harold Bloom says Don Quixote is the first modern novel, and that the protagonist is at war with Freud's reality principle, which accepts the necessity of dying. Bloom says that the novel has an endless range of meanings, but that a recurring theme is the human need to withstand suffering. Edith Grossman, who wrote and published a highly acclaimed English translation of the novel in 2003, says that the book is mostly meant to move people into emotion using a systematic change of course, on the verge of both tragedy and comedy at the same time. Grossman has stated: The question is that Quixote has multiple interpretations [...] and how do I deal with that in my translation. I'm going to answer your question by avoiding it [...] so when I first started reading the Quixote I thought it was the most tragic book in the world, and I would read it and weep [...] As I grew older [...] my skin grew thicker [...] and so when I was working on the translation I was actually sitting at my computer and laughing out loud. This is done [...] as Cervantes did it [...] by never letting the reader rest. You are never certain that you truly got it. Because as soon as you think you understand something, Cervantes introduces something that contradicts your premise. The novel's structure is episodic in form. The full title is indicative of the tale's object, as ingenioso (Spanish) means "quick with inventiveness", marking the transition of modern literature from dramatic to thematic unity. The novel takes place over a long period of time, including many adventures united by common themes of the nature of reality, reading, and dialogue in general. Although burlesque on the surface, the novel, especially in its second half, has served as an important thematic source not only in literature but also in much of art and music, inspiring works by Pablo Picasso and Richard Strauss. The contrasts between the tall, thin, fancy-struck and idealistic Quixote and the fat diddy, world-weary Panza is a motif echoed ever since the book's publication, and Don Quixote's imaginings are the butt of outrageous and cruel practical jokes in the novel. Even faithful and simple Sancho is forced to deceive him at certain points. The novel is considered a satire of orthodoxy, veracity and even nationalism. In exploring the individualism of his characters, Cervantes helped lead literary practice beyond the narrow convention of the chivalric romance. He spoofs the chivalric romance through a straightforward retelling of a series of acts that redound to the knightly virtues of the hero. The character of Don Quixote became so well known in its time that the word quixotic was quickly adopted by many languages. Characters such as Sancho Panza and Don Quixote's steed, Rocinante, are emblems of Western literary culture. The phrase "tilting at windmills" to describe an act of attacking imaginary enemies (or an act of extreme idealism), derives from an iconic scene in the book. It stands in a unique position between medieval romance and the modern novel. The former consists of disconnected stories featuring the same characters and settings with little exploration of the inner life of even the main character. The latter are usually focused on the psychological evolution of their characters. In Part I, Quixote imposes himself on his environment. By Part II, people know about him through "having read his adventures", and so, he needs to do less to maintain his image. By his deathbed, he has regained his sanity, and is once more "Alonso Quixano the Good".

Chapter 3

  • “be proved, and the truth was better than the lie, even should the lie seem truer than the truth, every lie containing a seed of truth in it, no doubt, but ours was the freedom to make a choice between the truth and the lie and the truth and the truth, to do what was good and right, leaving no uncertainty.” - The passage underscores that while lies may be seductive and intertwined with truth, it is the conscious choice to pursue what is good and true that defines human freedom and moral integrity. Many cultures and thinkers have expressed the belief that, despite the potential pain or discomfort of truth, it ultimately offers a more beneficial and sustainable foundation than deception. A common African proverb states, "The bitter truth is better than a sweet lie". This highlights the idea that even if the truth is difficult to hear, it is ultimately more valuable than a comforting falsehood. Writers and thinkers like Tolstoy have emphasized the importance of truth for moral clarity and authentic relationships. They argue that while lies might offer temporary comfort, they ultimately lead to unsustainable situations and undermine trust. In contrast to the idea of truth being better, the concept of the "big lie," popularized by Adolf Hitler, suggests that people can be persuaded to believe an enormous falsehood if it is presented with sufficient audacity and repeated often enough. This highlights the potential power of lies, even if they are ultimately destructive. Mark Twain famously noted that "a lie can travel halfway around the world while the truth is putting on its shoes". This emphasizes the speed and ease with which falsehoods can spread, compared to the effort often required to establish and disseminate truth. This phrase, attributed to Mark Twain as well, highlights how statistics can be manipulated to create misleading narratives. It underscores the potential for even seemingly factual information to be used to deceive. Jung’s work strongly emphasizes the importance of truth, particularly self-truth, for psychological well-being and individuation. He believed that facing one's shadow and confronting one's inner truths, even if difficult, is crucial for personal growth and wholeness. Lying, especially to oneself, can hinder this process and lead to psychological stagnation.

Chapter 16

  • “the shock of this knowledge was but the affirmation of some older shock which had been forgotten”: Freud’s “The Uncanny” (1919) describes the uncanny as something long familiar and forgotten that returns as strange.

Chapter 18

  • “such a man as is hidden in the woman”: Jung named the woman within the man the anima, and the man within the woman the animus.

Chapter 22

  • “My mother saw everything but mistook everything she saw for something else”: What Vera describes resembles visual agnosia, a term Freud coined in On Aphasia (1891).

Chapter 37

  • Life as a state of experience and potential - Socrates thought death might be a dreamless sleep or passage to another realm where one can converse with the dead. Existentialism believes death is an inevitable aspect of human existence and that it can lead to a heightened sense of self-awareness and responsibility. Epicureanism argues death cannot harm a person, as it’s either not yet experienced (while alive) or not experienced at all (once dead). Death is defined as the loss of the capacity to engage in vital activities and the cessation of consciousness. Wittgenstein said that death is not an experience in life. Schopenhauer believed that understanding death as the ultimate end of life can provide a perspective on the meaning of life itself. Freud thought the life instinct (Eros) and the death instinct (Thanatos) are fundamental forces in human behavior. The chapter depicts life as a state of ongoing experience and potential characterized by uncertainty, illusion, and transformation. It is not a fixed reality but a mutable and often paradoxical condition where life and death, truth and illusion, identity and multiplicity coexist and where human understanding is limited and provisional.

Chapter 37

  • Descartes’ dream argument - He used the experience of dreaming to question the certainty of our perceptions and beliefs. He argued that if we cannot definitively distinguish between waking and dreaming, then our sensory experiences, which are the foundation of our knowledge, might be illusions. There is philosophical skepticism because the possibility of dreams may be genuine or a fabrication. It leads one to question the validity of knowledge of the external world. Freud believed dreams are a symbolic representation of unconscious thoughts and desires. Jung expanded on Freud’s ideas that dreams can also reveal universal, archetypal patterns in the human psyche. Some theories suggest dreams are a natural byproduct of the brain’s activity during sleep, possibly involving memory consolidation, emotional processing, or the clearing out of mental clutter. Neuroscience has provided insights into the physiological processes which in turn influences philosophical discussion about the nature and functions of dreams. Some philosophers think dreams are a way of accessing another realm of existence or experiencing different perspectives on reality. The chapter concludes that dreams are not simply illusions but fundamental to existence and consciousness, blurring distinctions between reality and unreality, life and death, self and other, and that this ambiguity is a central and inescapable part of human experience.

Chapter 43

  • Carl Jung believed that dreams reveal more than they conceal. They are a natural expression of our imagination and use the most straightforward language at our disposal: mythic narratives. The components of our mythic lives all have a similar structure throughout the lifespan. His idea of a collective unconscious refers to the psychological constants in all societies, such as rites-of-passage into womanhood, or the growing fascination with death after middle age.

Chapter 51

  • The last paragraph grapples with the elusive nature of reality. The initial fears of the "naked valet" and the "mad couturier" who clothes him with "the colors of a dream" suggest the influence of external forces shaping our perception of self and the world. This aligns with philosophical discussions about appearance versus reality, as seen in Plato's Allegory of the Cave, where humans perceive only shadows of true forms. The line, "For the dream of life, even like that of death, must ever contain within it an element of the unrealized, the unrealizable —else all would be complete," suggests that imperfection and the unattainable are integral to existence. This resonates with existentialist thought, which emphasizes the inherent incompleteness and absurdity of human life. The description of Mr. Spitzer "dreaming like the heart of God upon a world never created, uncreated, this world dreaming itself even when there was no counterpart" suggests a world that is in a constant state of becoming, and perhaps only exists within the mind of the dreamer. Mr. Spitzer's fear of being evicted from the "citadel" and being born into a world where he misses fundamental sensory experiences (smell of the rose, sound of birds) speaks to the existential anxiety of losing one's sense of self and belonging. The idea of a mother who is a "bearded man armored" challenges traditional notions of gender and identity. This can be linked to philosophical discussions about the social construction of gender and the fluidity of identity. The image of the "old jockey who dreamed that his dead horse lived" and the subsequent shock of realizing the dream's reality highlights the power of the unconscious and the irrational. This aligns with psychoanalytic theories, particularly those of Freud, which emphasize the influence of unconscious desires and repressed memories on human behavior. The phrase "We lived by an imperfect concatenation, even as we died—by echoes, tremblings, reverberations, sourceless thoughts" suggests that life is a series of disjointed and fragmented experiences. This aligns with Buddhist philosophy, which emphasizes the impermanence of all things and the illusory nature of the self.

Chapter 76

  • The belief that “some little boys who were murderers, too, and killed their mothers, but it was only because their mothers had already killed them, in quick ways and slow ways” suggests a psychological interpretation of violence as a symptom or consequence of earlier trauma, specifically maternal harm or neglect. This resonates with psychoanalytic and developmental psychology concepts about how early childhood experiences, especially those involving caregivers, profoundly shape emotional regulation, attachment, and later behavior. Children who experience harm, whether physical, emotional, or symbolic (such as neglect or abandonment), may internalize and later reenact these patterns, sometimes violently. The phrase “mothers had already killed them, in quick ways and slow ways” can be interpreted both literally and metaphorically. Psychologically, “killing” here may represent not only physical harm but also emotional or psychological damage such as: rejection, neglect, or emotional unavailability. This “kills” parts of the child’s spirit, identity, or sense of safety. This damage can manifest later in destructive behaviors, including violence towards others, even those closest to the child. The passage underscores the difficulty in attributing simple blame in situations of cruelty and violence. It suggests a cycle where victims may become perpetrators, and cruelty can be both cause and effect. This aligns with psychological understandings of the “cycle of abuse,” where patterns of trauma perpetuate across generations or within family dynamics, making it challenging to identify clear origins or solutions. The sentence carries an existential weight, reflecting on the ambiguity and complexity of human suffering. It challenges the reader to see cruelty not as a straightforward moral failing but as part of a tangled web of pain and reaction. Psychologically, this can be related to the concept of moral injury or the struggle to reconcile acts of harm within relationships that are also sources of love and care.

Chapter 80

  • There are two famous pseudocyesis cases (phantom pregnancies): Queen Mary I experienced a well-documented false pregnancy, where she showed physical symptoms, and her doctors and court prepared for a birth, even drafting announcement letters with blanks for the date and sex. Anna O was Sigmund Freud’s famous patient who experienced a false pregnancy where she believed she was in labor with her therapist’s child.

Chapter 81

  • “He whispered into her ear that there would be a great war and that the bad men would blow up the world some day of calm just for fun, that all the men and all the women and all the children would be killed, and…” everything would be poisoned and disappear forever.” This may refer to the proliferation of nuclear weapons during the Cold War. The Cold War saw a rapid nuclear arms race that saw the U.S. and Soviet Union build massive arsenals, which fueled the proliferation of nuclear weapons to other nations like the UK, France, and China. This escalation was driven by mutual suspicion and doctrines like Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD), but was tempered by arms control efforts such as the Partial Test Ban Treaty and the Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT). The period is also marked by dangerous standoffs, such as the Cuban Missile Crisis, which underscored the need to prevent nuclear conflict. Little Boy was the codename for the type of atomic bomb dropped on the Japanese city of Hiroshima on 6 August 1945 during World War II. It was the first nuclear weapon used in warfare. Carl Jung had visions and dreams about carnage and thought he was going insane. From his journals it is considered a premonition of World War I.
