Chapter 32: Weddings Are Funerals, and Funerals Are Weddings

← Chapter 31 · Guide Home · Chapter 33 →

Research notes: Chapter 32

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh

Themes: Women, Gender, and Marriage

Symbols: Weddings and Funerals

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Exploration and the Poles · Fairy Tales and Folklore · The History of Opium · Women's Suffrage

Chapter 32 is the completion of the Cousin Hannah portrait and, simultaneously, the fullest account we will receive of Catherine’s marriage, or what passed for one. Young weaves the two together because they are the same story: Hannah’s lifelong battle to rescue Catherine, and Catherine’s marriage to the absent bridegroom who was always already gone.

This is a long, rich chapter and it asks a lot of you. Take your time. Young is doing something philosophically ambitious at the end, her final statement about death and travel and partial knowledge, that rewards the full weight of everything that came before it.

What actually happens in Chapter 32

The chapter deepens the Cousin Hannah portrait through Catherine’s eyes. Hannah was explosive and instantaneous, always hurried, “the greatest courier of every storm.” She flourished upon renewed discouragement, almost with a terrible zest. “She had lived through so many dangers” that her face was ravaged, her eyes burning, “a saber wound upon her cheek, a grizzled chin.”

We learn that Hannah had tried to break up Catherine’s marriage, had beaten at the bridegroom with her white umbrella, screaming like ten thousand demons, attempting to carry Catherine away in the manner of a robber. And we learn, finally, what Catherine’s marriage actually was: something spectral, a ceremony with a faceless bridegroom, a wedding “to all the fading images of this world.” “She had been married to a sail-boat which suddenly lifted its wings and sailed away. She had been married to a porpoise and a star,” an iceberg, a church bell.

The chapter closes with Catherine’s final statement: she “had always wanted to die while travelling—as then she would never completely die.” “Death is that which is caused by our partial knowledge.”

Hannah’s philosophy of woman

Chapter 32 gives us Hannah’s feminist argument in its most developed form. She had been “the high-souled lover of woman,” she said, come to rescue her “from slavery to man worse than enchantment by an Eastern drug.” She “had urged woman to use her parasol as her sword,” to fly her skirts as banners on tallest mountain peaks, “to show her little bird feet” and arise and make war.

But Hannah had also been opposed to what she saw as women’s frivolities: the great garden hats with billowing plumes, the silks, the secretive veils, the narcotic flowers, the parasols used as flirtation weapons. This is where her feminism develops its own tension. She loved woman and was opposed to femininity as she found it. She wanted to rescue women from men and also from the performance of womanhood that men had imposed.

Young presents this honestly and without resolution. Hannah is right about the oppression. She is also something of a tyrant in her prescriptions. She had urged Catherine to arise and take horse and make war, but Catherine’s nature required the opium bed, the dark tower, the dreamed bridegroom. You cannot save someone by demanding they become you.

Catherine’s marriage

The account of Catherine’s marriage is the most important revelation in the chapter, and Young delivers it sideways, through Catherine’s own uncertain and shifting memory.

“She had been married on the tennis court,” in flying veils, passing a “great ball which was the winged moon.” “She had been married on the clouded archery range,” shooting “a golden arrow through a cloud. She had been married to a sail-boat,” a porpoise, a star, an iceberg, a church bell. She had never seen her bridegroom’s face. In the midst of the wedding ceremony, she had felt herself “hypnotized, drawn by a magnetic force which came from no known place or star,” not knowing “what she was doing or who the bridegroom was.”

This is not madness. It is the most honest account available of a marriage to someone who was constitutionally, essentially absent. Catherine’s husband left her, she is vague about when and how and who he was, and went into the mountains and never returned. Young has given us glimpses of him across the novel: the man who walked a frozen cable between snow-capped peaks and fell. What Catherine married was the “great traveller who never would return.” She married, in other words, the void that all the others in her life have been orbiting: the man who was already gone at the moment of the wedding.

Hannah had tried to interrupt this wedding, beating at the phantom bridegroom with her umbrella. She could not break it up. Catherine had married the absent one. That is the only marriage available to a woman whose capacity for love requires absence as its condition.

Weddings are funerals, and funerals are weddings

Near the chapter’s end Young gives Catherine one of the novel’s most compressed philosophical statements: weddings are funerals, and funerals are weddings. “When one marries, a part of one must die. When one dies, one is forever married, married through separation unto eternity. The great lover is always the absent one. He is one whom no one has ever known or loved.”

This is Catherine’s philosophy of love in its final form, and it is not evasion. It is observation. The love that persists is always the love that was never consummated, never fixed in the ordinary daylight of a shared life. Miss MacIntosh loved someone she could not name. Cousin Hannah loved someone she could not reach. Catherine loves someone who was never there. Mr. Spitzer loves Catherine who will never choose him. The novel’s great lovers are all loving across an absence that cannot be closed.

The wedding to the absent bridegroom is not, in Catherine’s understanding, a tragedy. It is the condition of love itself. “The great lover is always the absent one.” The moment you catch him, he becomes something smaller. The marriage to the iceberg, the church bell, the sail-boat that “lifted its wings and sailed away”; these are not failures of love. They are its most accurate forms.

Hannah’s end

The chapter gives us something heartbreaking near its close: Hannah’s deathbed confession. She had told Catherine, in some final visit, “that these great battles had been as nothing to her, had been as mere incidents in a busy life.” She realized now “that the greatest heroes were not those who perished in the loud clash of arms.” “The greatest battles were fought in the domestic wars by those who had never left home. They were fought by man and woman. It was always an unequal battle, for woman reigned supreme. The man who had left home was a coward who should never be forgiven.”

This is Hannah’s final reversal. The woman who had been to Persia and Arabia and the Alps and Hudson Bay and fought against Spanish cavalcades and Turkish lords, who had believed her entire life that action was the answer, at the end acknowledged that she had been fighting the wrong battles in the wrong places. The domestic war was the real one. She had gone far away to avoid it.

Young delivers this through the uncertainty of how the confession could have occurred: Catherine had not seen Hannah, and both were bedridden, and it may have been a dream. But Mr. Spitzer acknowledges it was possible. He had been the go-between. He had seen Hannah in her last days, wrapped “in his dark cape, the snow like ermine” on his collar, and she had known when she saw him that her time was close.

The final sentence

The chapter ends: “my mother had always wanted to die while travelling—as then she would never completely die—for every traveller dies merely by passing through a city, and none will know that landscape which he passes through in the long night. Death is that which is caused by our partial knowledge.”

This is the novel’s most direct statement of its central philosophical proposition. We die because we know only part of the world we pass through. If we could know it completely, if we could see the full landscape of the long night, we might not die, or at least we would not die completely. Partial knowledge is the condition of mortal life. The traveller who passes through a city and does not stay, who does not learn its streets and faces, never fully dies there because he never fully lived.

Catherine wants to die while travelling because she has spent her whole life in one bed, and the one death she fears is the complete one: the death of the person who stayed. The traveller’s death is a partial death. A partial death is a kind of life.

What to watch for as you keep reading

Light spoilers: This section hints at what happens later in the novel. Skip it if you want to meet the story fresh.

The Cousin Hannah chapters are complete. She will continue to surface in the novel’s memory, she is too large a presence to disappear entirely, but Young has now given us everything we need to understand her. The great captain who died in her bed, of her life, having lost the domestic war she fought on every other front.

Catherine’s final sentence, “death is that which is caused by our partial knowledge,” is the novel’s thesis statement in its most compressed form, and everything that follows will be an elaboration of it. Every character in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is dying of partial knowledge: of themselves, of the people they love, of the world they move through. The novel is Young’s attempt to supply the full knowledge, or at least to map the partiality.

The suitor who “had subscribed heavily to the suffrage movement” after Hannah “left him at the church door with a white bouquet in his hands” is one of Young’s best peripheral figures. He appears in one clause and is never mentioned again. He has spent his entire life supporting the cause of the woman who refused him. Young does not decide whether this is noble or absurd. It is both.
