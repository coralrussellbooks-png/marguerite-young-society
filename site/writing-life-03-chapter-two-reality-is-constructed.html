Chapter Two: Reality Is Constructed

Pillar: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed · Across every book

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · The Stone Deaf Man · Vera Cartwheel

The second pillar is the one most likely to be misunderstood, so it is worth unpacking. Young does not argue that illusion is preferable to reality, that we should choose fantasy over fact, or that the interior life is more valuable than the exterior world. She argues something more fundamental and more disturbing: that the distinction itself is false. That illusion is not the opposite of reality but its medium. That human beings do not live in the world and then sometimes retreat into illusion. They live in illusion the way fish live in water, and what we call reality is one particular illusion that has achieved consensus.

Western thought has organized itself around the real and the illusory as a foundational opposition. Plato's cave is the origin story: the shadows on the wall are an illusion, the sunlit world outside is reality, and the philosopher's task is to drag people from the former toward the latter. Young answers this differently. The evil demon is not the nightmare. The dream of escaping the demon is the nightmare, because the escape route leads nowhere. There is no sunlit world outside the cave. There is only a larger cave, more elaborately lit, whose inhabitants have agreed to call their shadows real.

Young accomplishes this by giving illusions the same novelistic density she gives everything else, the same accumulation of specific detail, the same insistence that this place is real because a self is genuinely living in it.

In Chicago in the mid 1930s, Young spent about two years reading aloud, night after night, to Minna Weissenbach as the woman passed into opium dreams. As Dowling tells it, Young came to see herself as providing “the verbal passport” to another world. She dedicated Moderate Fable to Minna’s memory, and she later said that her poem “Bishop Berkeley of Cloyne,” with its brain kept in fluid and furnished with a false world, “contains the future opium lady.” The density Young gives the world is the density of a world she once sat beside and listened to.

The philosophical point is not that this way is right and that way is wrong. It is that the framework that would let us adjudicate between them, the framework that sorts experience into real and illusory, is itself an illusion. The solid world is sustained by the same mechanism as the opium world: a self that has organized its experience around a set of commitments and called those commitments reality. The solid world was always a performance of solidity.

The self does not receive reality passively. It constructs reality actively, from the materials available to it, shaped by desire and grief and history and the particular plurality it contains. What we call consensus reality is the construction that enough selves have agreed on to treat as given. It is not less constructed for being agreed upon. It is just more crowded.

Young had put the claim in one sentence long before the novel. In a graduate essay on Edward Young’s Night Thoughts she wrote that “Sanity may consist only in sharing the hallucinations of our neighbors.” In “Bishop Berkeley of Cloyne” (1944) the claim becomes verse: “there is no substratum of dead fact,” and “We eat and drink ideas, we are clothed with them / As water clothes the waterfall.”

If illusion is primary, if the real is always already a construction, then the question of who gets to construct it is a political question of the first order. Every dominant political system depends on the naturalization of its own construction: the idea that the way things are is the way things are, not the way things have been arranged by people with interests in keeping them arranged that way. Young's insistence that all reality is constructed does not produce the conclusion that all constructions are equal. It produces the opposite conclusion: that the question of which constructions dominate, and at whose expense, is the central political question, and that the first act of political resistance is the refusal to mistake the dominant construction for nature.

Young drew this conclusion herself. Understanding illusion, she said, keeps a person from “blindly following a leader.” A year after the war, in “Complex Life and Complex Letters,” she attacked critics who praised the dull as “good, old American,” and observed that “Goering and Goebbels were sensible.” Common sense as a faith lived by, had shown what it could be made to serve.

Young's formal choices in her writing are themselves an argument about illusion. Young refuses the formal signals that literary fiction conventionally uses to mark the boundary between interior and exterior, between dream and waking, between illusion and reality. This is not a stylistic quirk. It is a philosophical position expressed through craft. The form would be saying what the argument denies if it treated one character’s world as thinner than the another character. Young is too rigorous for that inconsistency.

The result is a reading experience that is genuinely disorienting and genuinely clarifying by the time you understand what Young is doing. The disorientation is not a failure of communication. It is the communication. You are being asked to experience the collapse of the real and the illusory, not to be told about it.

That is the second pillar. Reality is not what we thought it was. It is not less real for that. It is more real, because what we thought it was was always already a simplification, and the world Young is pointing toward is the actual one: plural, constructed, contested, and alive in ways that the simplified version never was.

It begins gently, in early poems where a farmer keeps his world alive on a teacher’s old postcards and the construction is allowed to stand as what keeps him going. It becomes epistemology in the graduate essays and the Berkeley poem, turns social and political in "Angel in the Forest", where Father Rapp is a “manufacturer of whisky and illusion,” and ends as the economic illusion of capitalism in "Harp Song for a Radical". Throughout, Young held that experience, dream included, is real, and she was a stubborn realist about facts: “I don’t make up anything.” The construction she attacks is the common-sense world, not experience itself.

Phase One, Childhood and the Early Poems (1908 to 1934)

Pillar 2: Reality Is Constructed

The pillar is present only lightly, and in a tender form. "The Seeker" (1928) is about a farmer who keeps a whole world alive through a teacher's old postcards and a son in Paris whose letters "have been lost somewhere at sea." The world he lives in is held together by the story he tells, and the poem does not expose him. It lets the construction stand as what keeps him going. "Street Fires" (1927) gives the first image of common sense against beauty. The old women of Hawthorne Lane sweep their lawns until "Not one gleam on their lawns they keep," and "That Beauty may die, they light their fires." The "Sonnet" (1928) honors a woman who bought "Thin painted china when she needed bread" while others "laughed at loving this so old." In both poems a shared sense of what is proper is set against a private sense of what matters. That is the seed of Miss MacIntosh's "Common sense is the finest sense."

Complication: no argument is made yet, and the constructions shown are mostly kindly.

Phase Two, The Chicago Years, Prismatic Ground, and the Graduate Essays (1935 to 1937)

Pillar 2: Reality Is Constructed

The Night Thoughts essay gives the pillar in one sentence: "Sanity may consist only in sharing the hallucinations of our neighbors." The Patmore essay places Patmore between idealism and realism, draws on James's "wild data" and Arthur Lovejoy's question of where the image of a distant star is located, and concludes that "the wild data continue to behave in a wild manner."

The poems keep testing the ground. The sleepwalker of "Dubious Ground" is "always aware / Of the dubious texture of the ground." "Drowning Song" instructs: "Be lucid of that element... For thus you turn from fact to dream." The cow of "Country Scene" meditates "above the illusion of grass, / In implausible meadows." "The Dark Wood" makes the same wood two worlds. At night Ellen is trapped by watching frogs. At dawn, "Limpid with bird song," it is "The innocent wood."

The question of who benefits appears for the first time in "Paradise Is Still Lost." Heaven is a police order run by "God the great G-man," whose stool pigeon is a tuberculous boy: "They will get him yet."

Complication: the Patmore essay's "wild data" already points to Young's later position. Reality is not only agreement. It is wilder and larger than any agreed version.

Phase Three, The Philosophical Poems (1938 to 1945)

Pillar 2: Reality Is Constructed

"Bishop Berkeley of Cloyne" (1944) is the center: "The outer realm of fact was always false," "there is no substratum of dead fact," and "We eat and drink ideas, we are clothed with them / As water clothes the waterfall." The poem also imagines a brain kept in fluid and furnished with a false world. Young later said it "contains the future opium lady" of the novel. "A High Subjectivity" says "The idea of the universe is inconclusive." "The Responsibility of Parentage" takes a Jamesian, practical turn: "the unreality of experience makes no ascertainable / difference / To the strings of the harp." In "Winter Scene" (1942) earth is "celebrant for the failure of a theory." "Proem to Reality" (1945): "There is no reference but to shifting fixity," and "There is no whole."

The political edge sharpens with the war, from the state's execution room in "The Clinic" (1941) to "Didactic Poem Against War," which says "this century has willed / Men like walrus herds shall all be killed," and "our own nature has willed this most sorrowful theory." A draft calls matter "that courtesan," and says "Elevation... is not salvation."

Complication: the argument is never allowed to win. Berkeley asks, "Yet how will away the absent hills?" "Null Class" demands, "Who shall prove the farmer in the field / And that lean horse whose ear twitches." The unpublished "Wild Yam Fruit" is a plain poem of things seen: hulls, persimmons, oak leaves.

Phase Four, "Angel in the Forest" and “Complex Life and Complex Letters” (1945 to 1946)

Pillar 2: Reality Is Constructed

Rapp is a "manufacturer of whisky and illusion." The New Harmony of 1940 is "nothing certain, but illusion in every facet." Owen's creed is that people are "the creatures of circumstance." In Young's account Rapp keeps order by seeming to see everything, "as many eyes as the eyes in the feathers of a peacock's tail," and "As vice-regent of the Deity, Father Rapp left nothing to the Deity." The keeper of the shared fiction is the one who benefits from it.

"Complex Life and Complex Letters" (1946) carries this into culture. It attacks critics who praise the dull as "good, old American," and notes that "Goering and Goebbels were sensible." A year after the war, common sense is named as a political danger.

Complication: illusions have material results: the whisky sold, the graves dug, the Ozark dead "who had perished on the asphalt road." The Owenites, "by omitting all which did not come within the domain of reason," found "they had omitted reason itself." The construction is real in its effects.

Phase Five, "Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" (written from the mid 1940s, published 1965)

Pillar 2: Reality Is Constructed

Petrolle reads the book as a contrast. Catherine knowingly makes her own fictions, while Miss MacIntosh accepts the culture's ready-made ones, down to her belief that her baldness is ugly. Oboler finds culture as the "organization of illusion, of memory," and common sense as the deepest American illusion. Miss MacIntosh is the keeper of that sense: "Common sense is the finest sense." When Vera sees her bald, she cries out the pillar herself: "Nothing that you think is happening is happening! Nothing that you think is true is true. Nothing that you see is real!" Petrolle cites Mr. Spitzer: "Nothing lived except through imagination."

Complication: some shared constructions are kind. The stone-deaf man says they will marry "for legality helped," and the book treats this tenderly. Shaviro's reading holds that "even the unreal things were real," which keeps Young's realism about experience.

Part One sets Catherine’s world against Miss MacIntosh’s (Chapter Two). What the novel adds is a keeper of the shared fiction who is loved. In the graduate essays and the Berkeley poem the pillar was a question of knowledge, and in "Angel in the Forest" the keeper was Father Rapp, a figure of power. Here the keeper of common sense is the person Vera loves most, so the collapse of the solid world arrives as grief as well as discovery.

Phase Six, The Occasional Prose, Interviews, and Self-Interpretation (1940s to 1995)

Pillar 2: Reality Is Constructed

She told Clark there is no reality except, realistically, its unreality, and told Neville that "everything is fiction," even God. She said that understanding illusion keeps a person from "blindly following a leader." "Horse Utopia" reports a rancher's paradise that restores men's "ancient dignities" and ends on a ducking stool for rebellious women.

Complication: she was a stubborn realist about facts: "I don't make up anything. I always know if it didn't happen." She checked her inventions against a psychiatrist, a hairdresser, and medical literature.

Phase Seven, "Harp Song for a Radical" (written in her last decades, published 1999)

Pillar 2: Reality Is Constructed

The pillar becomes economic. Marx sees a society "based upon the appearances of the ghosts of illusion as if they were real," and hopes for "the death of the illusion of capitalism." Capitalism is "an institution as sacred as if it had been handed down by God to man." A reservation is "a land of promise of which the promises were all illusion even as were the promissory notes." The czar's mock execution of Dostoyevsky is a "theatrical pseudo-masterpiece": power writes better fictions than the utopians. Ruas records Young's definition of maturity: "When you have examined all the illusions of life, and know that there isn't any reality, but you nevertheless go on, then you are a mature human being."

This answers the question that runs from "Paradise Is Still Lost" through "Complex Life and Complex Letters": the owners of rails, mines, and land, and the state that stages their fictions, benefit from the shared construction.
