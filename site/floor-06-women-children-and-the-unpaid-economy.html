Care, Autonomy, and the Right to Be Heard

A Companion Document to the Constitution

The Philosophical Foundation

Two categories of human beings have been consistently excluded from the full protections of the legal and economic systems that govern their lives: women, whose labor has been extracted without compensation and whose bodies have been subject to state and private control, and children, whose interior lives and expressed needs have been systematically discounted by the institutions responsible for their care. These exclusions are not accidents of history. They are political choices, maintained across centuries, that this constitution is designed to reverse.

The philosophical foundation is straightforward: every person has a body that belongs to them, labor that deserves compensation, a voice that deserves to be heard, and a right to safety in the places where they live. None of these things have been reliably extended to women and children under the legal and economic systems currently in place. This document describes what extending them fully would require.

Reproductive Autonomy

Reproductive autonomy is the right of every person to make their own decisions about their body, their fertility, and their family. The decision to become pregnant, to continue a pregnancy, to end a pregnancy, to use contraception, to seek fertility treatment, or to decline any of these: these are decisions that belong to the individual. The state has no legitimate interest in compelling any of them.

The history of state intervention in reproductive decisions is not a history of protecting life. It is a history of controlling whose bodies produce children, under what conditions, and for whose benefit. Enslaved women were compelled to bear children to increase the labor force of enslavers. Poor women and women of color were sterilized without consent under eugenic programs that operated in Missouri, Ohio, and across the country well into the twentieth century. Women who sought abortion before it was legal died from unsafe procedures in numbers the medical record documents. The criminalization of abortion that has followed the 2022 overturning of Roe v. Wade has produced documented cases of women denied medically necessary care until they were critically ill, of pregnancies carried to term against medical advice, of physicians refusing to treat miscarriages for fear of prosecution.

This constitution's reproductive autonomy provision covers the full range of reproductive decisions without restriction based on the political composition of any legislative body at any given moment. The coverage includes abortion at any stage when medically indicated, contraception in all forms, fertility treatment, prenatal care, maternal care, and postpartum care. The person who cannot access these services because of cost, geography, or legal restriction is not exercising free choice. They are experiencing compulsion by another name.

Equal Pay and the Unpaid Economy

Women in the United States earn approximately eighty-two cents for every dollar earned by men for comparable work. The gap is larger for Black women, Latinas, and Native women. The gap has narrowed over decades but has not closed, and at current rates of change would not close for generations. This is not a market outcome reflecting differential skill or effort. It is the accumulated result of decisions made at every level of the economy about whose work is worth more.

The equal pay provision of this constitution is enforceable. It requires equal pay for work of equal value, not only for identical jobs but for comparable jobs across occupations that have been sex-segregated by historical hiring patterns. The nursing profession is not less valuable than the engineering profession because nurses are predominantly women. The childcare worker is not less valuable than the facilities manager because childcare workers are predominantly women. The comparable worth standard requires that pay reflect the actual demands of the work rather than the gender composition of the workforce doing it.

The unpaid economy is larger than most economic accounting acknowledges. The care work performed without pay in households, the majority of which is performed by women, includes childcare, elder care, care of disabled family members, household management, and the emotional labor of maintaining family and community relationships. This work is not less real because it is unpaid. It is not less economically significant because it does not appear in GDP calculations. It is the infrastructure on which the paid economy runs.

This constitution's protections for the unpaid economy include the recognition of caregiving as economically productive work for purposes of Social Security calculation, pension eligibility, and economic floor provisions. The person who left paid employment to raise children or care for an aging parent has not been idle. They have been doing work the economy depends on. The economic floor does not penalize them for it.

Paid Caregiving

The people who perform paid caregiving work, the childcare workers, the home health aides, the nursing home staff, the personal care assistants, are among the lowest-paid workers in the economy. The median wage for childcare workers is below the poverty line in many states. The people who care for other people's children and aging parents are often unable to afford care for their own. This is not a coincidence. It is the market's valuation of work that is predominantly performed by women, predominantly by women of color, and that has been culturally constructed as an extension of the unpaid domestic labor women are expected to perform naturally rather than as skilled professional work deserving professional compensation.

The floor provisions of this constitution apply to caregiving work with full force. The minimum wage floor, the benefits floor, the workplace safety provisions, the right to organize: all apply to caregiving workers without the exemptions that have historically excluded domestic workers from labor law protections. The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 explicitly excluded domestic workers, a provision whose primary effect was to exclude the Black women who performed most domestic work in the South from the protections the law provided to white workers. That exclusion and its descendants are repealed.

Universal childcare, funded publicly and available to every family regardless of income, is both a caregiving provision and an economic floor provision. The family that cannot afford childcare cannot afford to work. The cost of childcare in most metropolitan areas in the United States now exceeds the cost of in-state college tuition. This is not a personal financial problem for individual families to solve. It is a collective infrastructure problem that requires collective investment.

Domestic Violence

Domestic violence is not a private matter. It is a public safety issue and a constitutional obligation. The state has an affirmative duty to protect people from violence in their homes with the same force it applies to violence in the street. The historical treatment of domestic violence as a private family matter, beneath the concern of law enforcement and the courts, produced a legal landscape in which a woman was more likely to be killed by her intimate partner than by any stranger, and in which the legal system's primary response was to encourage reconciliation.

The legal framework for domestic violence response has improved substantially since the passage of the Violence Against Women Act in 1994, though that act has required repeated reauthorization fights that have been used to weaken its protections. The constitutional provision removes the possibility of that weakening: the obligation to protect people from intimate partner violence is a constitutional floor, not a statutory one subject to political revision.

The constitutional obligation includes: mandatory training for law enforcement and prosecutors in domestic violence dynamics, including coercive control; civil legal aid for survivors navigating protective orders, custody proceedings, and housing; housing protections that allow survivors to break leases and access emergency housing without penalty; workplace protections that prevent survivors from being fired or penalized for absences related to violence; and immigration protections for survivors whose legal status has been weaponized by abusers to prevent them from seeking help.

The economic connection between domestic violence and poverty is well documented. The survivor who cannot leave because she cannot afford to, because she has no access to independent income, because the abuser has controlled her finances, is not making a free choice to remain. The economic floor provisions and the domestic violence provisions work together: financial independence is safety infrastructure.

No Child Marriage

Child marriage is legal in some form in the majority of American states. The minimum age for marriage with parental or judicial consent is below eighteen in many jurisdictions, and in some states there is no minimum age at all. The overwhelming majority of child marriages involve girls married to adult men. The documented outcomes of child marriage include school dropout, economic dependence, higher rates of domestic violence, and higher rates of maternal mortality. Child marriage is a mechanism for transferring girls from parental control to spousal control without their meaningful consent, at an age when they cannot legally consent to most decisions about their own lives.

This constitution sets eighteen as the minimum age for marriage without exception. Parental consent does not authorize the marriage of a child. Judicial approval does not authorize it. The child who is too young to vote, to sign a contract, or to consent to medical treatment is too young to consent to marriage.

Gender-Neutral Parental Leave

Parental leave policy shapes who is assumed to be the primary caregiver and who is assumed to be the primary earner. When parental leave is available only to mothers, or when the leave available to fathers is token compared to the leave available to mothers, the policy encodes an assumption that caregiving is women's work and paid employment is men's work. That assumption is then enforced by the structure of the leave itself: the mother who takes six months of leave and the father who takes two weeks will, predictably, develop different relationships to caregiving and different career trajectories.

This constitution requires gender-neutral parental leave at the same duration and compensation level for all parents, regardless of gender, and regardless of whether the child entered the family through birth, adoption, or foster placement. The leave is funded through the same progressive tax mechanism as other floor provisions, so that the cost does not fall on individual employers in ways that create incentives to avoid hiring people who might use it. The policy that makes caregiving equally available to all parents makes it equally expected of all parents, which changes the economic and social calculus that currently assigns it primarily to women.

Children's Right to Be Heard

Children are people. Their experiences are real, their expressed needs are valid, and the institutions responsible for their care are obligated to take what they say seriously. This sounds obvious. It is not how the institutions that serve children currently operate.

The child in the child welfare system is the subject of decisions made by adults: the caseworker, the judge, the foster parent, the biological parent, the guardian ad litem. The child's expressed preferences are considered at the discretion of the adults involved, weighted against other factors, and often overridden. The research on outcomes in the child welfare system is consistent: children who are heard in the processes that determine their placement, their education, and their family relationships have better outcomes than children who are not. Hearing them is not only an ethical obligation. It is a practical one.

The child in the school disciplinary system, the child in the psychiatric facility, the child in the juvenile detention facility, the child in any institutional setting: all have a right to be heard in the decisions that affect them. This does not mean that children's expressed preferences are always determinative. It means that the process is not complete without them. The adult who makes a decision affecting a child without asking the child what they think, what they need, and what they are afraid of, is making an incomplete decision.

This constitution's children's right to be heard provision requires that in any legal or administrative proceeding affecting a child, the child's perspective is sought and documented, with appropriate support for children who cannot communicate in the formats the proceeding requires. The provision applies in family court, in school disciplinary proceedings, in child welfare placements, and in any institutional setting where children reside. It does not require any specific outcome. It requires the process be honest about what it is deciding and for whom.

Children's Interior Life in Institutional Settings

The institutions in which children live, the foster care system, the juvenile detention system, the residential psychiatric system, the congregate care system, share a structural problem: the people who run them are accountable primarily to the agencies that fund them, not to the children in their care. The child who is moved between foster placements, who loses connections with siblings and teachers and neighborhoods, who accumulates a file of behavioral interventions rather than a history of relationships: this child's interior life is not the primary concern of the system processing them. Their safety is managed. Their compliance is enforced. Their grief at what they have lost is rarely the subject of anyone's professional obligation.

This constitution's provision for children's interior life in institutional settings requires that any institution in which children reside maintain individualized records of each child's relationships, expressed preferences, cultural connections, and developmental history; that placement decisions account for continuity of these connections rather than treating children as interchangeable across placements; that children in institutional settings have access to at least one consistent adult relationship that is not contingent on their compliance; and that independent oversight of these institutions includes direct interviews with children about their experience.

These requirements are not expensive relative to the costs of the outcomes that institutional care without them produces. The child who ages out of foster care without stable relationships, educational continuity, or economic foundation does not disappear. They appear in the homeless shelter, the jail, the emergency room. The investment in their interior life while they are in the system is the investment that prevents those later costs. More importantly, it is the right thing to do.

The Transition

The provisions in this document do not require new institutions. They require existing institutions to do what they already claim to do: protect people from harm, compensate work fairly, and make decisions in children's best interests. The gap between the claim and the practice is large. Closing it requires enforcement, investment, and the political will to treat the people these provisions protect as the full persons they are.

The economic provisions, equal pay, caregiving compensation, parental leave, and the unpaid economy protections, work together with the economic floor to produce a labor market in which caregiving is valued rather than extracted. The safety provisions, domestic violence response, reproductive autonomy, and no child marriage, work together to produce a legal system in which control of women's and children's bodies is treated as the harm it is. The voice provisions, children's right to be heard and children's interior life in institutional settings, work together to produce institutions that are accountable to the people they serve rather than to the agencies that fund them.

None of this is finished when the provisions are enacted. The building of the infrastructure, the training of the workforce, the cultural change that makes these provisions normal rather than contested: that takes longer.
