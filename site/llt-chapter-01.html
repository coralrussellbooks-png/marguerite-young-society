Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 1

← The Figures and What They Carry · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 2 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Pillars: The Sacred Is Here

The Questions

When Vera flees a “background of illusion,” is she escaping the built world, or only trading one invention for another?

If “the individual is the one illusion,” what happens to the idea of a single, sealed self?

Is Miss MacIntosh’s “clear reality of simple things” the ground Vera is looking for, or the first of the book’s temptations to settle?

What is Vera actually asking for when she asks for a truth that does not fail?

Vera stands at the threshold and starts the book off with her question, the search for life, for love, for a truth that does not fail. She is fleeing what she calls a “background of illusion,” and it is tempting to read the flight as an escape from the false into the real. It is not. Everyone in this book lives inside a built world; that is the ordinary condition. What Vera is leaving is one invention, her mother’s, and the question the book will spend itself asking is not whether to live in an invention but whether the invention is sealed. Catherine’s “opium paradise” is the sealed kind in its first appearance, a world shut against the daylight and mistaken for the only world there is.

Against that sealed dream the chapter sets a line that carries the book’s understanding of the self. “Every heart is the other heart. Every soul is the other soul. Every face is the other face. The individual is the one illusion.” The single, bounded, sealed person is the illusion here, not the world. Selves run into one another, and a person cannot be sorted into a clean box. The claim seeds what the book will later make flesh in Mr. Spitzer, who cannot find the border between himself and his dead brother. At the threshold it is stated at its widest: the one thing that is not real is the sealed individual.

Into this the chapter brings Miss MacIntosh, and it brings her as a temptation. She sees the world “even with the naked eye,” she keeps to “the clear reality of simple things,” she tells Vera to leave the “realm of shades” and “see America first, the broad interior, the spacious Middle West.” This is The Sacred Is Here, and it is genuinely attractive, the daylight after the shuttered house. It is also the first place the book offers a reader to stop. Vera calls her a “moral guide, the unswerving, the true,” and the surface of the chapter invites agreement. She will be complicated steadily as the book goes on, but here she stands as the clean alternative to Catherine’s dream, and the cleanness is the lure.

Vera is the seeker, and this chapter is the first hour of her education. She inherits Catherine’s sealed dream and sets out from it wanting a truth she can reach and hold. She does not yet know that the fixed, final, arrived-at truth is the kind that fails, and that not-yet-knowing is the engine of everything after. She stands where the sealed dream she came from meets the long open search she is beginning, and the entire book is the distance between them.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the clean binary its surface seems to offer, illusion on one side and common sense on the other, Catherine’s dark house and Miss MacIntosh’s daylight. Neither the flight from illusion nor the arrival at plainness is the answer, and to read the chapter as that escape is to miss what Vera is setting out to find.

The footing is the distinction the rest of the book will sharpen. The question is never illusion against reality but sealed against open. Everyone lives in an invention, and the individual who believes himself sealed and single is the one illusion. Vera sets out asking for a truth that does not fail, and the honest shape of that truth is not the fixed thing she imagines but the one kept open all the way down. The search is real and it has an answer. The answer is not the thing she thinks she is looking for.

For Reflection

What “background of illusion” does one flee, and is the place fled to any less built?

If every heart is the other heart, where does one person end?

What would it mean to want a truth that does not fail without wanting one that never changes?
