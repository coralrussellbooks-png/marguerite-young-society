Chapter Nine: Language Shapes What’s Possible

Characters: Esther Longtree · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

The ninth pillar addresses the medium through which all of the other pillars are expressed and contested.

If the self is plural, if reality is constructed, if knowledge is situated, if community is held through difference, if time is sedimentary, then language is not the neutral medium through which these truths are communicated. It is the medium in which they are constructed, contested, and either honored or betrayed. The ninth pillar: language is not the vehicle of truth but the medium of its construction, and must be held accordingly.

Gertrude Stein's investigation of language, conducted across her poetry, her prose experiments, and her theoretical essays, is an investigation of the relationship between grammatical structure and the structure of experience. Stein understood that the grammar of a language is not a neutral tool for expressing pre-existing thoughts. It is a set of commitments about what kinds of things exist, how they relate to each other, and what counts as a complete thought. The noun, in standard English grammar, is the primary unit of existence. Things are nouns. People are nouns. The verb is what happens to or between nouns. This grammatical structure encodes a metaphysical commitment: the world is made of stable things that persist through time, and change and action are secondary to the stable things that undergo them.

Stein's experiments with grammar are experiments in building a language that can hold a different metaphysical commitment: the commitment to a world made of processes rather than things, of becoming rather than being, of the verb rather than the noun. Young inherits this project and extends it. The long sentences of "Miss MacIntosh, My Darling", the sentences that circle back and refuse to finish and accumulate subordinate clauses like geological layers, are sentences that resist the grammatical commitment to stable nouns and clean predications. They are sentences built for a world where the self is a verb and reality is a construction and grief is permanent weather rather than a temporary condition.

Young found the principle early and kept refining it. In her graduate essay on Coventry Patmore she observed that in his world “even the nouns act like verbs.” Her drafts of the 1940s name “the error of language” that “conceives / The whole as whole hypostasizing.” Late in life she said it in three words: “Style is thinking.” The short sentence catches “maybe a tenth of what you think,” and the long sentence is a dragnet for thoughts that, in James’s phrase, we never catch “on the wing.”

The language through which political life is conducted is not a neutral medium. It is a set of commitments about who exists, what counts as a person, what kinds of experience are real and what kinds are marginal. The people whose existence is not captured by the stable nouns of political grammar are not simply underrepresented. They are grammatically erased. The plural self cannot be a noun in the grammar of liberal political theory, which requires the unified rational agent as its primary unit. The person whose identity is multiple, whose interests are not unified, whose experience crosses the categories that the grammar of political discourse has built, is not a person that the grammar can process.

Young's language, with its resistance to the stable noun and its commitment to the ongoing process, is a political act in this sense. It is the construction of a grammar that can hold the plural self, that can give voice to the experience that standard political grammar erases, that can make visible the people who are invisible to the language of the long con.

Adrienne Rich's essay "Notes Toward a Politics of Location" brings the ninth pillar into direct conversation with feminist practice. Rich argues that political speech is always located, always spoken from a particular body in a particular place with a particular history, and that the pretense of speaking from nowhere is not a form of universality but a form of domination. The voice that claims to speak for everyone is always the voice of someone in particular. Rich's politics of location requires that speakers name their location, acknowledge the partiality of their perspective, and build their political speech from the honest acknowledgment of where they are standing rather than from the pretense of standing nowhere.

This is the ninth pillar's political expression. Language that names its own location is language that can be held accountable. Language that pretends to speak from nowhere is language that cannot be questioned, because there is no particular location to question. Young said the same in her own terms. “It was my fate to be born in Indiana,” she said, and she told her students to “start where you are.”

The political half of this pillar arrived late. It first appears in Young’s own pages in "Angel in the Forest", where the opponents of a common good “substitute words for things.” In the novel it becomes Miss MacIntosh’s “jargon of old saws” set against a counter-language of touch, and in "Harp Song for a Radical" it becomes the politics of naming, as when the word “red” calls up “the red man who had been slaughtered.” This also records a tension with this chapter’s reading of Rich: Young sometimes called herself “a mirror” and “neither a woman or a man,” which comes close to the voice from nowhere.

Phase One, Childhood and the Early Poems (1908 to 1934)

Language is the farmer's way out in "The Seeker," since grammar is what the farm took from him and what he tries to win back alone. "Song of Two" lets spoken words "Dip softly, softly through my days." The song is something "welded together." There is no theory yet.

Phase Two, The Chicago Years, Prismatic Ground, and the Graduate Essays (1935 to 1937)

Pillar 9: Language Shapes What’s Possible

The Patmore essay supplies the most striking match in the whole record: in Patmore's world "even the nouns act like verbs." The poems weigh language's power and its limits. "Experience in Song": "But pollen of a syllable / Receives celestial ground." In "Anthology Piece," a girl's "moon-struck syllables" "Draw as the moon the angry waters," though "None will ever speak her name." "Elegy for the Body" asks "What metaphor / Of bird or flower / Can make her heart / To beat again," and answers that "imageless / Is quietness."

Complication: the politics of language is absent.

Phase Three, The Philosophical Poems (1938 to 1945)

Pillar 9: Language Shapes What’s Possible

The drafts state the critique of nouns: "the error of language conceives / The whole as whole hypostasizing." "That Apple Was Mental" names "A noun which has no corresponding entity in space." In "That Cloud," "the personal pronoun attains a status in infinity," and April "has its being only in a name." "Who is limited?" places the limit in definition itself: our hearts denied our contiguity with the universe, "having defined / Limitations." Against nouns stand process: "all things are process," and "Ourselves that process." "Speculative Evening" uses James's own term, "specious present."

Complication: the politics are present only through logic. Logicians omit spinsters, a definition is "but a thistle stalk," and "all their words were adjusted long ago" in "At the Cinema."

Phase Four, "Angel in the Forest" and “Complex Life and Complex Letters” (1945 to 1946)

Pillar 9: Language Shapes What’s Possible

The political side of the pillar appears for the first time in Young's own pages. The 1940 philosopher says the opponents of a common good "substitute words for things. They purchase space in newspapers." The Rappites' "exclusive use of the German language" is "another barrier between themselves and their neighbors." Owen wants "a Bible of his own, in plain, simple language, without mystery, wigs, or parrots," and offers Mrs. Malaprop as proof that minds are written on.

Phase Five, "Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" (written from the mid 1940s, published 1965)

Pillar 9: Language Shapes What’s Possible

Vera's childhood is drilled in "hackneyed proverbs and moral aphorisms," a "jargon of old saws," the inherited grammar of good sense. Young said of Miss MacIntosh, "it's just her verbalism that makes her seem sensible." The ending offers a counter-language. Vera spells her name on her lover's hand, and he answers, "There be words of ours... not in the alphabet of the stars. Flowers spell them out on the earth, and grasses talk." Esther's misspelled sign still says what it needs to.

What the novel adds is inherited language as a character’s whole mind, and a counter-language set against it. "Angel in the Forest" named the politics of words. The novel shows a child raised inside a grammar of good sense and an ending that speaks in touch, flowers, and misspelling.

Phase Six, The Occasional Prose, Interviews, and Self-Interpretation (1940s to 1995)

Pillar 9: Language Shapes What’s Possible

"Style is thinking." The short sentence catches "maybe a tenth of what you think." The long dragnet sentence tries to catch thoughts that, in James's words, we never catch "on the wing." In 1989 she described it as including "without undue censorship." In Woolf she finds "a journey, but there is hardly a journeyer." "The World of Silence" asks how a man with no language can describe "his guilt complex if he has not even one small word," notes that sign language exists "all in the present tense," and suspects that for the deaf "time may be an illusion, a human construct." "It was my fate to be born in Indiana," and students should "start where you are."

Complication: she called herself "neither a woman or a man," wrote in "Feminine Sensibility" that sexual differences "dissolve, bearing no relation to art," and called herself "a mirror," close to a voice from nowhere. Only late did she name "a rage against women" in her reviewers. Also in an interview Young said she wanted to be a detective or a journalist but she realized the opportunities for women were limited at the time.

Phase Seven, "Harp Song for a Radical" (written in her last decades, published 1999)

Pillar 9: Language Shapes What’s Possible

Debs speaks in "the time-worn, often antique coins of speech" to reach "all the orphans in this orphaned world." Marx might have fared better in America "if it had not been called dialectical materialism," or if the "most unread red book in America had not been called red," since the word called up "the red man who had been slaughtered." Weitling's universal language is a "Simple Simon language" that "never did get off its feet." Young told Ruas that these Americans "all spoke in metaphors."
