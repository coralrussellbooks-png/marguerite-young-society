Chapter 29: Butterflies of Resurrection

On Chapter 29 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of A Guide to Reading Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Research notes: Chapter 29

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer

Themes: The Search for Meaning

Symbols: Butterflies and Moths · Weddings and Funerals

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Exploration and the Poles · Gambling and Playing Cards · Homer’s Epics

Chapter 29 is the longest in the Spitzer sequence and one of the most ambitious chapters in the novel. Young gives Mr. Spitzer the floor entirely, and what he does with it is extraordinary: he speaks about butterflies for the length of a short novella, and in doing so, speaks about everything, death, resurrection, the soul, God, his brother, his grief, the question of whether any life is truly one’s own.

This is the chapter where Young earns Mr. Spitzer’s full stature as a character. He arrives here as the novel’s most profound thinker, its secret philosopher, its dark atheist who knows more about the life beyond the grave than anyone, because he has been living in the aftermath of death for so long that the two worlds have merged.

The chapter is long and the prose is dense. Let it wash over you. You will not catch every butterfly name or classical allusion on a first reading. You are not supposed to. Let the accumulation do its work.

What actually happens in Chapter 29

Mr. Spitzer speaks about butterflies. He speaks about the difficulty of distinguishing butterfly from moth, and how this mirrors the difficulty of distinguishing happiness from sorrow, brother from brother. He speaks about the butterfly as psyche, the Greek word for soul, and about butterflies observed far out at sea, wandering far off their course, perhaps blown by hurricanes between stars.

He describes everything he knows: the mourning cloak “hibernating in a hollow tree trunk” through a frozen winter, the chrysalis like a mummy in its shroud, the birth of the butterfly from the caterpillar’s silken cocoon. He catalogs hundreds of species by name: pearl crescents, painted ladies, red admirals, purple emperors, old monarchs, Arctic satyrs, Homeric butterflies, “butterflies with Latin messages written on their wings.”

He describes Peron’s funeral: “the merriest mourners who ever were,” “merry andrews and fat aldermen and” ruined gamblers and dice shooters, a crowd his brother had loved and Joachim had never understood. He describes his own grief afterward, betrayed only to an old lamp-lighter on a windy street corner “whose face was also streaked with tears,” who said of Peron: “he will never light another lamp.”

He meditates on the sock question: a sock so many times darned and patched, are these new socks or old? When the last original thread has gone, what remains? He concludes the chapter asking whether his butterflies, still glowing in his cavernous mind after all these years, are more beautiful than anything Catherine dreams.

The butterfly as the argument

Chapter 29 is structured as a philosophical argument delivered through natural history, and the argument is this: the butterfly is the proof, or at least the best available evidence, that transformation is real.

Mr. Spitzer watched the caterpillar wrap itself in silk and sleep and emerge. He has “seen the preparations of the caterpillar for a future life,” he says, and watched it achieve “immortality within the logic of mortality.” The chrysalis is like a mummy. The butterfly that emerges is the same being and a completely different one. You cannot tell, at the crucial moment of emergence, whether you are witnessing a birth or a death. Both are true simultaneously.

This is why he began collecting butterflies in the first place. Not as a hobby. As an investigation into the single question his life has been organized around: is transformation possible? Is it possible to come out the other side of a catastrophic change (his brother’s death, his own stopped life) as something new and whole, rather than simply as what remained after everything was taken?

He has no answer. But he has the butterflies, which at least demonstrate that the thing happens (somewhere, in some other kingdom) even if he has never managed it himself.

The oceanic butterflies

One of the chapter’s most beautiful passages is about butterflies observed far out at sea, miles from any shore, travelling through day and night, buffeted by winds. Some believe they were blown off course by hurricanes. Others believe they sail by “their own dreams, being these wandering psyches,” “seeking reunion with immortal love.

Mr.” Spitzer holds both explanations without resolving them. Were they blown, or did they choose to go? Is there a difference? He has seen the caterpillar sea, “wings of butterflies like sails of ships gleaming through distant clouds.” He believes these oceanic migrations may be intentional, not merely an accident or monsoon.

This is Mr. Spitzer’s life in miniature: a being far off its course, in conditions that should not support its survival, moving through the dark either because of forces beyond its control or because it is a wandering soul seeking reunion with something it has lost. He cannot say which. Neither can we.

Peron’s funeral

Young gives us the full account of Peron’s funeral here, and it is one of the most affecting passages in the novel. The crowd was “the merriest mourners who ever were”: merry andrews “and broken-down vaudeville stars, waterfront characters and cue-ball champions,” dice shooters and ruined gamblers, old fly-by-nights, mad mountebanks. A crowd that spoke in languages Joachim did not understand: “roulette wheel talk, bird cage talk,” “spirit jockeys and glass bead madames.” He has been slowly learning the translation ever since.

Everyone thought his self-containment was supernatural, his serenity almost marvelous. It was grief. Utterly devastating grief. Almost no one sensed it. Only the old lamp-lighter on the windy street corner afterward, “whose face was also streaked with tears,” who said what needed to be said: “he will never light another lamp.”

Young gives us this moment (the great lawyer and musician, his face streaked with tears in the wind, standing on a street corner with a lamp-lighter) and then moves on. She does not dwell. She trusts it to carry its own weight. And it does.

The sock question

Near the chapter’s end, Mr. Spitzer poses what he calls the philosopher’s question: his socks have been so many times darned and patched that he wonders whether the last original thread has now disappeared, making them technically new socks, albeit very fragile ones easily unravelling.

This is the Ship of Theseus, Young’s version: the famous philosophical puzzle about whether an object that has had all its parts replaced is still the same object. Mr. Spitzer applies it to himself. He has been so patched and darned over the years since his brother’s death (by grief, by impersonation, by wearing Peron’s watch, by attending Peron’s funeral as both chief mourner and the body in the coffin) that he genuinely cannot say whether what remains is Joachim or something else, made of so many replacements that the original is gone. The ghostly conformation of the old sock persists. The leg inside is still his leg. But what are these socks?

Young places this right before the final butterfly catalog because that is exactly where it belongs: the sock question is the human version of the butterfly question. The caterpillar becomes the butterfly. The old sock becomes the new sock. At what point in the transformation are you still you?

What to watch for as you keep reading

Light spoilers: This section hints at what happens later in the novel. Skip it if you want to meet the story fresh.

After Chapter 29, the novel leaves the breakfast table entirely. You have now received the full portrait of Mr. Spitzer: five chapters, from his professional life through his grief through his butterflies. He will continue to appear throughout the novel, always arriving just after the extraordinary thing has happened, always missing the party, always treading softly on the thinnest ice. You now know why.

The butterflies will keep returning. Young uses them the way she uses all her recurring images: lightly, from different angles, always adding a new facet. When you see a butterfly in the chapters ahead, carry what you know from Chapter 29: it is the soul in transit, the being mid-transformation, the creature that cannot be told from the flower it rests on until one of them moves.

The lamp-lighter who said “he will never light another lamp” is one of the novel’s most quietly charged minor figures. He mourns Peron with tears on his face. He knows what Peron was to the city’s darker corners in a way Joachim never did. He offers Joachim the only eulogy that matters, and then he goes on lighting his lamps down the dark street. Young gives us one sentence of his and moves on. That is often her method: the peripheral figure who carries more weight than the central ones.
