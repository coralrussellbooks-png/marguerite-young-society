Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 38

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah

Two modes of refusal share the chapter, and their doubling is the pillar. Cousin Hannah is the "great captain of extremes," the militant who sacrifices everything to suffrage and travels "to the outermost reaches of space"; Catherine is the "beautiful and unawakened corpse," the dreamer whose opium and romantic fantasy refuse the world from the opposite side. Action and dream are two faces of one unwillingness to accept a life the culture hands down, and Young holds them together without turning either into a simple lesson. Catherine's sense that suffrage is "quite unworthy" of Hannah's spirit is the ambivalence a dreamer feels toward the activist, and the gap between them is the gap between two feminist survivals rather than between courage and failure.

The chapter's deepest note is the burden of unexpressed love. Catherine's "dead love," never spoken and "as heavy as all the stars," names the erasure of women's emotional lives under an order that suppresses them, and it makes the unlived feeling weigh more than any lived one, restraint carrying a force the world never let it spend. Hannah's invitations to Egypt and far places, which Catherine cannot accept, are the missed openings for feminist awakening, and Catherine's imagined alternatives, riding "in balloons," picketing Buckingham Palace, wandering under nomadic stars, are the many shapes rebellion might have taken.

Hannah's strength stays elemental, "like a finely tempered blade," a "perpetual motion" that survives by fortitude and battles "one-eyed ogres greater than death," while Catherine's frailty and opium dreams are her own mode of endurance. The chapter closes on the confusion of truth and fantasy, the house as a "loony bin," dreams "more real than life," and it credits the dream as resistance even as it counts the cost of retreat. Action and dream rhyme, and the chapter leaves feminism as both a cause worth dying for and a haunted refuge.
