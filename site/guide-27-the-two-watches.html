Research notes: Chapter 27

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: The Search for Meaning

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Chapter 27 completes the three-chapter portrait of Mr. Spitzer that began in Chapter 25. We are still at the breakfast table, still inside Vera’s meditation, and Young is still deepening our understanding of Mr. Spitzer before the novel moves outward to other characters and other lives.

This chapter is quieter than the previous two. It circles around a single image: the two watches, one ticking and one broken. And it gives us the most precise account yet of what Mr. Spitzer is actually like as a presence in the world: ponderously dignified, perpetually late, always caught in revolving doors and between traffic streams, walking through glass walls and barely noticing, asked by a stranger on a crowded street if he wants to die.

What actually happens in Chapter 27

Vera continues her meditation on Mr. Spitzer. Young builds him outward from the inside: his preference for isolation and neutrality, his knowledge of his own deficiencies, his habit of carrying calling cards and an out-dated passport and scrolls of silent music so that, if found among the drowned women and fallen men of a city morgue, he might be identified as himself and distinguished from his brother.

The chapter’s center is the two watches. Mr. Spitzer wears his dead brother’s wristwatch, which is accurate and fine, used by Peron for timing horses at the races. His own identical watch is broken: its crystal shattered, its wheels rusted, its mechanism clogged with dust, stopped long ago, perhaps “several days after his brother’s death,” perhaps several days before. He has recently found it again behind bottles and scrolls on his dressing table and begun wearing it on his left wrist, under his cuff, as a reminder that his brother’s life was brief.

The chapter ends with Mr. Spitzer being escorted through traffic by a stranger who asks if he wants to die. He replies in the negative while “shaking his head in the affirmative.” The stranger leads him safely across.

The two watches

The image of the two watches is one of the novel’s most precise and most economical symbols, and Young gives it the space it deserves.

Peron’s watch, now on Mr. Spitzer’s right wrist, is intact, accurate, excellent for timing horses. Peron removed it thoughtfully before jumping; this detail, Young notes carefully, destroys “in some people’s minds, the hypothesis of an impulsive, thoughtless suicide.” A man who removes his watch before jumping has planned something. Or at least has cared about the watch. Mr. Spitzer, who has been equivocating about whether Peron’s death was an accident or choice, finds himself forced to acknowledge: the watch was removed. Deliberately. It ticks on now on Joachim’s trembling wrist, marking “the course of the inaccurate planets, the recurring years, the unknown flow of time.”

Mr. Spitzer’s own watch, now on his left wrist under his cuff, is broken. Crystal shattered. Wheels rusted. Mechanism clogged with dust. It stopped around the time of Peron’s death; he cannot remember if it was before or after, having been in a state of shock that negated personal memory. He may have fainted while walking under a light snowfall “at about the time of his brother’s death, perhaps through sympathy, for they had always suffered” “the same accidents, one not falling unless the other fell.”

So: the living brother wears the dead one’s running watch and the dead one’s broken watch simultaneously. The time that moves is Peron’s. The time that stopped is Joachim’s. He tells time by his brother’s clock and carries the evidence of his own stopped life against his skin.

His paraphernalia

Young gives us, in this chapter, the full inventory of what Mr. Spitzer carries on his person at all times. It is a list worth holding:

His flowing cape of darkness. His high silk hat. His black necktie. A rosebud with maidenhair. Numerous calling cards. “An out-dated passport for a journey he” has not taken and has not thought of taking. Scrolls of silent music, “perhaps several abandoned beginnings, mere preludes, perhaps several tentative endings, mere epilogues to the great body of his uncreated work, unfinished epithalamiums and dirges and vast recessionals.” Notes for voiceless choirs, tongueless nightingales, deaf listeners.

He carries this paraphernalia so that if he is ever found “in a city morgue among the drowned women and the fallen men,” he can be identified. He can be distinguished from his brother. The calling cards, the passport, the scrolls of music; these are his proof of existing as himself and not as Peron. They are the documents of a man who is not sure he will be recognizable without them.

The epithalamiums are wedding songs. The dirges are for the dead. The recessionals are for departures. Mr. Spitzer carries, in his pockets, music for occasions he has never attended and will never attend: the wedding that did not happen, the funeral that is always pending, the departure that is always coming. He writes the music for the life he did not live.

The dignity question

Near the chapter’s end, Young gives us one of the most endearing observations about Mr. Spitzer in the novel: there is probably nothing he fears “so much as the loss of his dignity, that he might seem ridiculous in other people’s eyes.”

And then, immediately: he is always caught in revolving doors. He is always caught between traffic streams, always crossing the street just when the red light turns green. He is afraid of icy pavements and polished floors and the crystalline glare of a half-dark sky. He is always being held over from one chord into the next, always experiencing the blurring of effects that produces a terrible discord where he expected a concord.

Young lets these two things stand side by side without resolution: his profound concern for dignity, and his complete inability to move through the world without incident. He is ponderously proud of having walked through a glass wall and survived. He chuckles softly about the desk clerk who told him he was dead. He considers it a joke he played. And then he crosses the street at the wrong moment and a stranger has to ask him if he wants to die and lead him safely through the traffic.

He replies in the negative while “shaking his head in the affirmative.” Young does not comment. She does not need to.

What to watch for as you keep reading

Light spoilers: This section hints at what happens later in the novel. Skip it if you want to meet the story fresh.

After Chapter 27, the novel begins its great expansion. We are about to leave the breakfast table, leave the coastal house, and enter the wider landscape of American life that Young has been preparing us for since Chapter 1. New characters, new geographies, new obsessions. The Miss MacIntosh sequence and the Spitzer portrait are your foundation. Every new character you meet will rhyme, in some way, with what you have learned here.

The “out-dated passport for a journey” not taken is one of the novel’s small perfect images of Mr. Spitzer’s suspended life. He is prepared for a departure that never comes. He carries the credential for a crossing that he has not thought of making. And yet he renews it, presumably, keeps it on his person, because one day the moment might arrive. Mr. Spitzer is always almost ready to leave.

The detail that “one not falling unless the other fell,” the twins sharing their accidents through sympathy, is the chapter’s most concentrated statement of what it means to be a twin who survives. Mr. Spitzer may have fainted at the moment of Peron’s death. His watch may have stopped then. He fell without dying; Peron fell and did not survive the fall. They shared the accident. One got up and one did not.
