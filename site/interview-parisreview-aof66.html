Young's Interviews Analyzed

"The Art of Fiction No. 66," Paris Review, Fall 1977 (interviewer Charles Ruas)

← Previous · Young's Interviews Analyzed · Next →

A reading for the Miss MacIntosh project.

The source at a glance

This is the Paris Review's "The Art of Fiction No. 66," published in the Fall 1977 issue, conducted by Charles Ruas. It is the most widely cited of all Young's interviews and, for the project, its importance is partly a matter of status: it is the edited, author-approved print version of the same body of talk as the WBAI "career" recording that Ruas also made. Same interviewer, same anecdotes, largely the same wording, so the two are best treated as the raw and the finished forms of one interview, with this Paris Review text as the citation-grade version. Most of its content, the opium lady, the from-life method, the muralist and pluralist imagination after William James, the sea-walk, the deaf painter, the "prosaic sprite," Wilder's line about "revealing the unconscious," was analyzed already through the WBAI recording, so this reading concentrates on what the print version adds or fixes. The headnote supplies clean facts: the first poetry book "Prismatic Ground" in 1939, "Moderate Fable" and "Angel in the Forest" together in 1945, eighteen years on the novel to 1965, and a Riley biography abandoned in the late sixties for a "brief" life of Debs that grew into a large study of Utopian and anti-Utopian trends. The setting is the familiar red-walled Village apartment with a merry-go-round horse and a life-sized antique doll.

What the print version adds at the opening

The Paris Review opens on material the WBAI career analysis did not foreground. Young describes her grandmother's death from a series of strokes, when she and her sister were left to absorb the old woman's "wild hallucinatory statements" alone, a scene Young likens to "Lear on the heath," her grandmother "speaking and dancing at the edge of her grave," not mad but "between dream and reality." She recalls a childhood of "Angels in the House" and a game of whirling to catch Satan at the command "Get thee behind me Satan," and her first thought at the grandmother's death, that the old woman could now tell Anatole France in heaven that her granddaughter adored him. Prompted by Ruas that "Anais Nin tells the story of your attempting to commit suicide," Young deflects it, denying any real attempt and treating her adolescent thoughts as a literary pose, the conviction of every young poet that they will be "dead at 21, of old age," dressed up with Dorothy Parker and Cowper. Two craft anecdotes are sharper here than anywhere else: visiting Thornton Wilder's Chicago class, where he hid his cigarettes until the desk caught fire, she kept the one maxim "the patient loves his malady," which she says governed Miss MacIntosh; and she quotes a reviewer's image of her finishing the novel as Sidney flung down his "Arcadia," "There it is, it's finished."

What the print version fixes

Because it is edited text rather than a home recording, the Paris Review corrects details that the transcripts garble, which matters for anyone quoting the archive. The psychiatrist who confirmed the meaning of Miss MacIntosh's black gloves and of the detective who fears anything black is named here "Dr. Hammershlagd," where the WBAI transcript rendered the name as "Harberstock"; the print form is the more reliable one. The Virginia Woolf drowning is given as the River Ouse, which the transcript slurred. And the eighteen-year span is fixed twice, in the headnote and in Young's own remark that had she known the book would take eighteen years she "would have dropped in holy horror," which sits against the looser "seventeen years," "seventeen years and eight months," and "twenty years" that appear across the other sources. In general, where this text and the WBAI recording differ in wording, the Paris Review is the version to cite.

What it consolidates

For the rest, the interview is the authoritative statement of positions established elsewhere in the set. Indiana as legend to be raised into "a universal and cosmic statement" rather than regional local color; her use of birds and beasts "for their symbolic value, their value as icons" out of the bestiaries and her Euphues thesis; her coolness toward Cather and warmth toward Twain and Dreiser as "great thinkers, great dreamers"; the Midwest writer "dipped and dyed in education"; the "muralist imagination" and "the many as opposed to the one," which Ruas names as William James and Young accepts as "also my temperament"; characters taken "from the newspapers, and from biographies and medical histories," on the Browning principle that "the poet is a reporter," with the flat rule "I do not believe in inventing characters." The opium lady, unnamed here but given in full, remains the origin, and Wilder's "revealing the unconscious" the theme. This is the clean, quotable form of all of it.

Set against the other sources

The single most useful point for the compilation is the pairing itself: the WBAI "career" recording and this Paris Review interview are two forms of one interview event by Charles Ruas, one spoken and one edited for print, so they should be cross-referenced rather than counted as separate testimonies, and the Paris Review should be the quotation of record. It confirms the commission of Miss MacIntosh in 1944 on the strength of "Angel in the Forest" and "Moderate Fable," the opium-lady origin, the Toussaint L'Ouverture biography she set aside, and the Adlai Stevenson invisible-elephant letter, all in reliable form. Its corrections, above all "Dr. Hammershlagd" for the transcript's "Harberstock," should be carried into any master list. And its headnote gives the cleanest short account of the shape of her career, including the pivot in the late sixties from the abandoned Riley biography to the Debs project, which the Neville profile and the Dowling chapter document from the other end. Where the interviews disagree on the writing span, this text's "eighteen years," doubled in the headnote and in Young's own words, is the figure with the best authority.
