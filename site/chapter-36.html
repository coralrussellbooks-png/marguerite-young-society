To All My Darlings

Chapter 36

On Chapter 36 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 3, 2026

← Chapter 35 · Home · Chapter 37 →

Summary

Mr. Spitzer, roused for once from his lethargy, accuses Catherine of never having loved any living being. He lives “at this second remove from reality,” hearing every sound a minute late, a counterfeit of himself who fears that any change of habit would bring the skies down. He is conservative where she is radical, and she has never distinguished imagination from memory. Catherine’s mind is on Cousin Hannah, whom she would woo back from death for a winter’s visit, though Hannah never answers her invitations, which Catherine has probably not yet sent. She blames Mr. Spitzer, the last to see Hannah alive, for refusing to say where Hannah has gone, and suspects he is jealous of a returning spirit. She imagines Hannah’s travels from Moscow to Babylon and across Al Sirat into a Moslem paradise without women, and wonders whether Hannah has become a man or a woman. If a woman should be in heaven, she thinks, it would not be heaven long.

Key passages

From the research

  • Mr. Spitzer, despite his usual politeness, briefly animates to accuse Catherine of never having loved any living being, not even him when, as it seemed, he was dead and in his grave. This accusation is significant as it frames the emotional and existential void experienced by the characters. Mr. Spitzer’s condition, his lethargy and feeling of being a counterfeit of himself, symbolizes a life that is a mere imitation, a repetition without meaning, reflecting a profound alienation from reality.
  • The passage describing how Mr. Spitzer’s eyes and ears are out of sync, how he perceives events late, and how every singer is off-key, poignantly illustrates the theme of disconnection from reality. This “second remove from life” emphasizes the difficulty of grasping truth or coherence in a fractured existence.
  • This passage contrasts Catherine’s radical blending of imagination and memory with Mr. Spitzer’s conservative, precise judgments. It highlights the fragile boundary between memory and imagination, suggesting that Catherine lives in a dreamlike state, unable or unwilling to distinguish reality from illusion, thereby sustaining her connection to lost loved ones and past events.
  • Cousin Hannah, the “fearlessly adventuresome cousin,” symbolizes a restless spirit whose life and death continue to haunt Catherine. Her failure to return, Catherine’s own evasiveness (the invitations she has probably not yet sent), and her futile attempts to communicate with Hannah underscore the theme of unresolved grief and the impossibility of reclaiming the past. The descriptions of Hannah’s travels and battles, and her transformation after death, suggest a transcendent yet ambiguous existence beyond mortal constraints.
  • The chapter uses powerful cosmic and natural imagery to evoke the vastness and mystery surrounding death and memory. Catherine’s longing to reach Cousin Hannah, the discussion of burial rites, and the question of where Hannah now resides (heaven, another world, or oblivion) deepen the meditation on mortality and the persistence of the dead in memory.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s role as the last living person to see Cousin Hannah alive, yet his refusal or inability to reveal her whereabouts, symbolizes both the barrier between the living and the dead and the limits of human knowledge about death. His competitive, jealous nature also represents a human obstacle to mourning and remembrance.
  • The richly detailed recounting of Hannah’s exploits across various cities and landscapes (Moscow, St. Petersburg, Siberia, Babylon) paints her as a mythic figure, a “great traveller” and warrior who transcended earthly bounds. This passage emphasizes her larger-than-life character and the enduring impact of her legacy, even as the physical world forgets her.
  • The chapter explores the blurring of identities, the shifting nature of time and place, and the instability of Catherine’s perception. The discussion of whether Hannah is now a man or woman, queen of the dead or invader of heaven, reflects the theme of transformation and the failure of fixed definitions. The metaphor of the house growing larger or smaller symbolizes the mutable nature of memory and existence.
  • Catherine’s belief that a woman should not remain long in heaven, that heaven will soon become a chaotic hell, speaks to a subversive view of cosmic order and gender roles. It suggests a world where traditional hierarchies and realities are unsettled, echoing Catherine’s own unsettled mind and the disruptive legacy of the suffrage captain.
  • The final lines, mentioning that “one should not know one’s mother’s face, one’s father’s face,” and that “the infant should be an old man crying in the storm,” encapsulate the pervasive sense of dislocation, confusion, and the inversion of natural order that permeates the chapter.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • Mr. Spitzer lives “at this second remove from reality.” What would the first remove be?
  • “He had led a single life— knowing he was dead.” What does Catherine’s charge of “a double life” miss?
  • Catherine never opened “those letters she had inscrutably written to herself.” What is she afraid they say?

Characters

  • Is Mr. Spitzer’s silence about Hannah’s whereabouts ignorance, jealousy, or kindness?
  • Mr. Spitzer accuses Catherine of never having “loved any living being.” Is he right?
  • Hannah was “in grey New England a warring old maid” and “in Araby a great desert king.” Which is she?

Connections

  • In Chapter 35 Hannah was to come “with dancing nymphs.” Here she crosses Al Sirat into a heaven without women. What has changed?
  • The golden cock crows over Hannah here, as the crowing cock made her dance in Chapter 34. What does it announce each time?
  • Mr. Spitzer fears that “all the skies might fall upon his head.” At the end heaven itself turns to chaos. Who brings the sky down?

Reflection

  • Can memory and imagination be told apart?
  • “Total ignorance was total knowledge.” Is there wisdom in not knowing?
  • Why might a person grow larger in death, “aggrandized by the pathos of distance”?

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • Portrayed as a man deeply affected by loss and memory, embodying lethargy and a near-death consciousness.
  • He experiences time and reality with delay and distortion, e.g., hearing sounds a minute late.
  • His identity is fractured; he is a “counterfeit” of himself, haunted by the death of his twin brother.
  • He is conservative, cautious, and fearful of change, symbolizing a resistance to the fluidity of memory and identity. “Thus he was conservative where she was radical.”
  • He has worn “the sombre black” of “these funeral clothes” ever since his brother’s death.
  • “He had led a single life— knowing he was dead.”
  • He was the last to see Hannah alive: “He had sat by her bedside and murmured diffident, distant remarks not calculated to disturb her peace.”
  • “He wished to have no rival in life or death.”
  • He walks Boston “noting always that an old landmark had disappeared,” “so that he was really afraid to turn his back upon the world.”
  • “He was envious of one who had escaped the purlieus of mortality long ago.”

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • A figure consumed by memory and imagination, struggling to distinguish between them.
  • She is radical, imaginative, and deeply invested in the past and in the memory of Cousin Hannah.
  • Her mind wanders between reality and dream, reflecting on loss and the elusive nature of death.
  • She is “an incorrigible lover of the dead.”
  • “She had always been afraid of the sad news she might read—the news that she herself was dead.”
  • “Often, she wept over the deaths of persons she had never known.”
  • Sometimes she thinks the house has grown “large enough to include almost the entire population of the dead.”
  • “Perhaps it was only yesterday that she had lain down for a moment on her pale-blue silk divan with its golden lion hooves… And many years, some which had left no trace, had passed since then.”
  • She is “talkative as a magpie in a dark forest,” and “proud of her omnipotence, her ghostly powers.”

Cousin Hannah

  • A “fearlessly adventuresome” suffrage captain, dynamic and rebellious in life.
  • She embodies passion and the struggle against societal constraints. Catherine is now concerned with her “many problems of personal unity.”
  • Post-death, she becomes an ambiguous, almost mythical figure, transcending time and space.
  • Her death and disappearance symbolize the fragmentation and mystery surrounding memory and legacy.
  • She had been “in grey New England a warring old maid urging ladies to arise and rebel” and “in Araby a great desert king.”
  • “Cousin Hannah, unlike himself, was aggrandized by the pathos of distance. Death had not diminished her.”
  • She “had been an apparition even in this life.” “She remained ambiguous as that which never was.”
  • She was “the queen of the strong-minded.” “She had driven a breach in the walls of Moscow.”

Peron Spitzer

  • “His brother would never have had such patience.”
  • He is the “superficial brother who had had no powers of memory or whose memory had been only in his own behalf.”

The Black Coachman

  • He is buried in “the leapings of long-roaring ebony waves,” “though he was bodiless now and though at times she saw his lantern eyes where fireflies glittered.”
  • “There were now no hitching posts—certainly none for an old coachman wandering through this forever fading or faded world, driving imaginary horses.”

Mr. Chandelier

  • “Had not her way been lighted by Mr. Chandelier, the rock crystal branches lighting a dark sky, moving as she moved?”

Research: Themes

Death and the Afterlife

See: Death and Mortality

  • For Catherine, death is not an end but a transformation and extension of existence into unknown realms. “Death had only extended her travels.”
  • The boundary between life and death is blurred; characters experience a “second remove” from life, living in a liminal space.
  • Death brings changes that alter identity and reality, sometimes enlarging horizons (as with Cousin Hannah) or shrinking and isolating (as with Mr. Spitzer).

Memory versus Imagination

See: Dreams and Imagination · Memory and the Past · Madness and Sanity

  • The chapter explores the fragile line between memory and imagination.
  • “It seemed to him that she had never distinguished between imagination and memory, the thinnest line sometimes dividing them.”
  • Catherine struggles to separate what is remembered from what is imagined or dreamed.
  • This blurring reflects the human difficulty in holding onto a fixed identity or past, especially after loss.
  • “Hers was undoubtedly the logic of madness, as he had many times pointed out.”

Identity and Fragmentation

See: Identity and the Self

  • Characters experience fragmented selves, double lives, or counterfeit identities.
  • The death of a twin brother creates a split in Mr. Spitzer’s identity.
  • Cousin Hannah’s identity transcends death and conventional existence, becoming fluid and ambiguous.

Time and Perception

See: Time · Phenomenology

  • Time is experienced non-linearly; sounds arrive late, events are recalled out of sequence.
  • The narrative reflects a consciousness that is “always in danger” of missing or misinterpreting life’s moments.
  • This theme underscores the instability of human experience and the elusive nature of reality.
  • “Each sound reached his dead ear always a minute late.” “His eyes were separated from his ears.”

The Role of Women and Feminine Power

See: Women, Gender, and Marriage

  • Cousin Hannah represents a powerful, rebellious feminine figure who challenges societal and cosmic orders.
  • There is tension between masculine and feminine realms, e.g., Catherine’s picture of a Moslem Paradise where women are absent.
  • Catherine’s contemplation of womanhood and power in death questions traditional gender roles and metaphysical status.
  • “For if a woman should be in heaven, it should not be heaven long, my mother thought.”

Rivalry and Envy

See: Twins and Doubles

  • “His envy had never died, having outlived its subject or its object.”
  • Without a dead brother “with his identical face,” he “would have been a rival to himself and still unsatisfied.”
  • “He was this obstacle, this darkness between my mother and her love, her dead love, it little mattering who that dead love might be.”

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Water and the Sea

See: The Sea and Water

  • Recurring images of water, surf, tides, and waves symbolize fluidity, change, and the unconscious.
  • Water is associated with identity shifts and the impermanence of life.
  • The ground under Mr. Spitzer’s feet is “the nightmare ocean where all must change identities.”
  • Hannah has crossed “the shoreless sea of which there was no end.” “There was no sea.”

Stars and the Sky

See: Moon and Stars

  • Stars symbolize distant memories, lost souls, or unreachable ideals.
  • The motif of stars falling or being connected across time suggests cosmic continuity despite human fragmentation.
  • “Our star’s light did not reach out far, did not illumine the entirety of space, was feeble as one firefly in the heavens of the universe.”

Mirrors and Reflections

See: Mirrors and Reflections

  • Mirrors signify distorted or partial reflections of identity and reality.
  • Hannah’s face “in an inaccurate mirror of consciousness” hints at the elusive self and the failure of memory.
  • If he changed a habit, “all the skies might fall upon his head, break like mirrors into a thousand splintered fragments.”

Animals and Nature

See: Birds · Horses and Carriages

  • Birds (blackbirds, seagulls), horses, and natural phenomena (fog, wind, fireflies) evoke transformation, freedom, and the boundary between worlds.
  • The black coachman lies where “the white manes of the running horses” streak the waves.

Architectural and Geographic References

See: Keys, Doors, and Locks

  • Bridges, doors, and roads symbolize transitions, passages between life and death, memory and oblivion.
  • “That door of death which, though very narrow, is wide enough to admit the skies and the stars.” “Or was it the door of life?”
  • Specific references to Boston, Araby, Moscow, and other places create a sense of vast, multi-locational experience transcending physical geography.

Clocks and Watches

See: Clocks and Watches

  • Mr. Spitzer would “consult his broken watch.”
  • Hannah “could not have been persuaded to stay beyond her hour or half hour or quarter hour, the strikings of distraught, fantastic cuckoo clocks.”

Research: Resources

  • “But who knew the hidden man, that man he might never be, neither himself nor his twin brother”: In her first draft “Mr. Spitzer knew that he was really Peyron the gambler,” which “seemed so tricky to me” that she rewrote it so that he does not know (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983). See Joachim Spitzer and Marguerite Young on Her Craft.
  • “He had thought he was listening to the music of the spheres”: The music of the spheres, or musica universalis, is the metaphysical principle that mathematical relationships express qualities or “tones” of energy. See Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres.
    • “There’s not the smallest orb which thou behold’st / But in his motion like an angel sings… But whilst this muddy vesture of decay / Doth grossly close it in, we cannot hear it” (Shakespeare, The Merchant of Venice 5.1). See Shakespeare.
  • “heard the musician only when the music had ceased”: Edmund Husserl took a melody as his example of time-consciousness: each note, as it passes, is held in “retention” while the next sounds, so that a melody is never heard all at once (Edmund Husserl, The Phenomenology of Internal Time-Consciousness, 1928). See Phenomenology.
  • “So he lived at this second remove from reality”: Plato’s imitator is “thrice removed from the king and from the truth,” an imitation of an imitation (Plato, Republic 10, trans. Benjamin Jowett). See Plato and Platonism.
  • “if he should forget to wear his wrist watch or carry his cane”: Mr. Spitzer has some symptoms of obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD). Wilhelm Griesinger described compulsive brooding, “Grübelsucht,” in 1868.
  • “then all the skies might fall upon his head”: Celtic envoys told Alexander the Great that they feared nothing except that the sky should fall on them (Arrian, Anabasis 1.4). In the folktale of Henny Penny, or Chicken Little, an acorn on the head convinces a hen that the sky is falling. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “It seemed to him that she had never distinguished between imagination and memory”: Julia Shaw is a psychologist and an expert in complex autobiographical false memory (Julia Shaw, The Memory Illusion, Random House, 2016). She asserts that all memories are illusions which are similar to how quantum physicists describe reality, much as Einstein wrote that “the distinction between past, present and future is only a stubbornly persistent illusion” (Albert Einstein, letter to the family of Michele Besso, March 21, 1955). It’s the same way Plato talked about prisoners in a cave confusing shadows on the wall with the reality outside the cave. A 2000 study found a robust false memory effect. However, participants were able to differentiate false memories and actual memories when using specific phenomenological tasks (Jeffrey S. Anastasi, Matthew G. Rhodes, and Marcy C. Burns, “Distinguishing between Memory Illusions and Actual Memories Using Phenomenological Measurements and Explicit Warnings,” American Journal of Psychology 113, no. 1, 2000, 1-26). False memory in the novel is portrayed as a multifaceted phenomenon that shapes and distorts reality for the characters. It is linked to themes of identity, time, and the elusive nature of truth. Memory is not a stable foundation but a shifting, sometimes deceptive, landscape where truth and illusion coexist. Characters struggle to discern reality amid the fluidity and fallibility of memory, and this struggle underscores much of the novel’s psychological depth and philosophical inquiry. See Plato and Platonism and Time, Physics, and Chaos Theory.
  • “as if the last rooster had not already crowed over her”: The cock crowing represents the transition from life to death, the inevitability of change, and the sober acknowledgment of human mortality.
  • “Our star’s light did not reach out far”: In 1823 Heinrich Olbers asked why, if the stars are endless, the night sky is dark. One answer is that the universe has a finite age, and the light of the farthest stars has not yet reached us. See Astronomy.
  • “Hers was undoubtedly the logic of madness”: “Though this be madness, yet there is method in’t” (Shakespeare, Hamlet 2.2). See Shakespeare and Madness and Sanity.
  • “aggrandized by the pathos of distance”: Nietzsche’s phrase, the “pathos of distance,” is the feeling of rank that sets the noble apart from those below (Friedrich Nietzsche, Beyond Good and Evil, 1886, section 257). See Nietzsche.
  • “despotic, one-eyed ogres in darkened rooms”: The one-eyed ogre recalls Polyphemus, the Cyclops who penned Odysseus and his men in his dark cave (Homer, Odyssey 9). See Homer’s Epics and Greek Mythology.
  • “by winged Mercury”: Mercury, the Roman form of the Greek Hermes, is the god of financial gain, commerce, eloquence, messages, communication, etc.
    • He wears winged sandals and a winged hat as messenger of the gods. See Greek Mythology.
  • “those letters she had inscrutably written to herself”: According to the Greek historian Hellanicus, the Persian queen Atossa (about 550-475 BC) was the first to write letters. Writing a letter to yourself is thought of as therapeutic or a type of time capsule of telling your future self how you were doing in the past. Mr. Spitzer writes letters that never arrive. Catherine writes letters to herself that are illegible, and never opens them. Letter writing in the novel symbolizes the human desire to communicate and be remembered, the persistence of memory amid loss, the pain of unfulfilled desires, and the complex, often failed attempts to bridge the gaps between individuals, between life and death, and between reality and illusion. The motif is deeply intertwined with the novel’s broader themes of memory, identity, and the elusive nature of truth and connection.
  • “hailstones as big as cannon balls”: “And there fell upon men a great hail out of heaven, every stone about the weight of a talent” (Revelation 16:21). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “Had not the heavens divided, splitting apart”: “And the heaven departed as a scroll when it is rolled together” (Revelation 6:14) marks the destruction of the old world order, as the heavens are seen as being destroyed and replaced with a new creation. Surah Ar-Rahman (55:37) describes the heavens splitting apart and becoming rose-red like burnt oil. Others read this metaphorically as a profound spiritual or emotional transformation, where the old self is broken down to make way for something new. At Jesus’s baptism by John the Baptist, “he saw the heavens opened, and the Spirit like a dove descending upon him” (Mark 1:10). It serves as an emblematic expression of cataclysmic change, upheaval, and transition from one state to another, highlighting the novel’s exploration of mortality, spiritual crisis, and the thin boundary between life and death. See Revelation and the Apocalypse and The Bible.
  • “Should she send a slow messenger to overtake the swift”: In Aesop’s fable the slow tortoise overtakes the swift hare, who stops to sleep. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “beyond the pearly gates”: “And the twelve gates were twelve pearls” (Revelation 21:21). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “if there had been no dead brother with his identical face”: The chapter portrays a triangular relationship between Peron, Joachim, and Catherine, marked by unfulfilled love, contrasting lives, and the tragic consequences of loss. Catherine’s preference for Peron and her rejection of Joachim create a dynamic of longing and sorrow that defines the emotional core of the narrative. Joachim embodies steadfastness and grief, while Peron represents passion and escape, and Catherine is caught between these polarities, her own existence fragile and dreamlike. See Peron Spitzer and Twins and Doubles.
  • “for total ignorance was total knowledge”: Nicholas of Cusa called it learned ignorance, De docta ignorantia (1440): the wise man knows that he does not know. Socrates said much the same in Plato’s Apology. See Plato and Platonism.
  • “the queen of the strong-minded”: “Strong-minded women” was a common nineteenth-century name, often a sneer, for advocates of women’s rights. See Women’s Suffrage.
  • “she who had objected to the song of even a male cicada”: Only male cicadas sing, by vibrating drumlike tymbals on the abdomen.
  • “Had she not gone over from the cross to the crescent”: To go over from the cross to the crescent is to convert from Christianity to Islam. Isabelle Eberhardt (1877-1904) did so, dressed as a man, rode the Sahara as “Si Mahmoud,” and drowned in a flash flood at Aïn Sefra.
    • Islam’s presence in the territory of modern-day Russia dates back to the 7th century with the Caucasus region coming into contact with the faith through the Muslim conquest of Persia. Volga Bulgaria is the first Muslim state from 922. From here Islam spread through trade and economic relationships and also through the Golden Horde Khanate in 1242. Muslim communities spread throughout the Volga region, the Urals, North Caucasus and Siberia. Many ethnic groups including Tatars, Bashkirs, Chechens, Ingush and Avars have historically practiced Islam in Russia. Sunni Islam is the majority from the Hanafi school and Sufism has a strong presence in the North Caucasus. Before the 10th century, Russians practiced Slavic religion, and Orthodox Christianity became the state religion of Kievan Rus in 987. (Wikipedia)
  • “Had he not seen the dying body in the enclosed air”: The chapter emphasizes the ephemeral nature of the physical body after death, its dissolution into elements like dust and water, and the uncertain fate of the spirit, wrapped in imagery of natural elements such as sea shells, clouds, and stars, which may symbolize the continuation of some form beyond corporeal life.
  • “that door of death which, though very narrow”: “Because strait is the gate, and narrow is the way, which leadeth unto life, and few there be that find it” (Matthew 7:14). See The Bible.
  • “Was it the old Boston Post Road”: Boston Post Road was a system of mail delivery routes between New York City and Boston, Massachusetts which became one of the first major highways in the US.
    • In January 1673 the first post rider set out on it from New York for Boston.
  • “should not Boston have remembered this great horseman by an equestrian statue”: Boston’s equestrian statues were of men: Thomas Ball’s George Washington in the Public Garden (1869), and Augustus Saint-Gaudens’s Shaw Memorial (1897) on the Common, Robert Gould Shaw on horseback leading the Black soldiers of the 54th Massachusetts.
  • “Had she not ridden from Minsk to Pinsk”: “From Minsk to Pinsk” is a common trope in classic Jewish humor, used to evoke a long, arduous, or circular journey within the Russian Empire. It is less a single specific joke between two businessmen than a setting for numerous different stories, often featuring Jewish peddlers or paupers and other travelers. It refers to a journey or passage between two places that symbolize a vast and difficult journey through strange or foreign lands. Minsk and Pinsk are actual cities in Eastern Europe, located in Belarus, and the phrase evokes a sense of traveling through a remote, challenging, or even mysterious landscape. See Judaism.
  • “Had she not wintered in Archangel”: Archangel, or Arkhangelsk, is the Russian port on the White Sea, named for its Monastery of the Archangel Michael. An Archangel is an angel of high rank usually associated with the Abrahamic religions. It was Russia’s chief seaport until St. Petersburg was founded in 1703, and its harbor is icebound for about half the year. See Angels and Archangels.
  • “She had come in through the Grand Central Station”: Grand Central Terminal, often called Grand Central Station, opened in 1913 and is located in Midtown Manhattan, New York City.
  • “She had crossed Brooklyn Bridge, Waterloo Bridge, the Bridge of Sighs, the Bridge of Lies”: The bridges are significant as metaphors for the liminality and ambiguity of existence, marking the passage between opposing states such as life and death, reality and dream, presence and absence. They highlight the instability and fluidity of boundaries in the novel’s world, underscoring the themes of transition, loss, and the elusive nature of truth and identity.
    • Brooklyn Bridge: See the Chapter 2 resources.
    • Waterloo Bridge is a road and foot traffic bridge crossing the River Thames in London. It opened in 1817 and was named for Wellington’s victory at Waterloo. See Napoleon and Wellington.
    • The Bridge of Sighs is a bridge in Venice, Italy. It joins the Doge’s Palace to the prisons. “I stood in Venice, on the Bridge of Sighs; / A palace and a prison on each hand” (Lord Byron, Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage 4, 1818). See Romantic Poets.
    • The Bridge of Lies is a legendary pedestrian bridge located in the center of the Transylvanian city of Sibiu in central Romania. The cast-iron bridge dates from 1859, and legend says it will collapse if anyone tells a lie upon it.
  • “She had visited many hanging gardens”: The Hanging Gardens of Babylon were one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World listed by Hellenic writers.
  • “Had she not climbed the seventh crystal stairway”: The seventh crystal stairway may refer to Jacob’s Ladder. “Behold a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven” (Genesis 28:12). See Genesis.
    • Seven heavens are named in the Talmud (Chagigah 12b), and in Islamic tradition Muhammad climbed through the seven heavens on the Night Journey, the Mi’raj, a word that means ladder. See Judaism.
  • “visited the inner courts of many seraglios”: A seraglio, or serail, is the sequestered living quarters used by wives and concubines in an Ottoman household.
  • “There was no sea”: “And I saw a new heaven and a new earth: for the first heaven and the first earth were passed away; and there was no more sea” (Revelation 21:1). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “The sky was pearl grey streaked by mare’s-tails”: Mares’ tails are high cirrus clouds. See the Chapter 34 resources and Clouds and Storms.
  • “she had lain down for a moment on her pale-blue silk divan”: Like Washington Irving’s Rip Van Winkle (1819), who lay down for a nap in the Catskills and woke twenty years later. See American Writers.
  • “Had she not crossed Al Sirat, delicate as a human hair”: As-Sirat is the bridge over hell to paradise in Islamic tradition, said in a hadith to be “thinner than a hair and sharper than a sword” (Sahih Muslim 183).
  • “the dance of the black-eyed houris”: The houris of the Qur’an are “fair ones with wide, lovely eyes, like unto hidden pearls” (Qur’an 56:22-23, trans. Marmaduke Pickthall, 1930). Catherine’s heaven with “no woman” is not the Qur’an’s, which promises paradise to believers “whether of male or female” (4:124, Pickthall).
  • “the golden cock crowing at dawn”: In the Chinese zodiac, a person born in the Year of the Rooster is said to be determined and brave enough to face all difficulties. The golden rooster in the chapter can be understood as a metaphor for the liminal space between life and death, the natural and supernatural, and the continuity of existence beyond physical demise. It is a powerful emblem of both hope and the eerie, unsettling awareness of mortality that permeates the novel’s atmosphere. See Astrology, Alchemy, and Esoterica.
    • A hadith says that roosters crow because they have seen an angel (Sahih al-Bukhari 3303).
    • Weissenbach’s golden cockerel. See the Chapter 14 resources.
  • “Was she king of the living or queen of the dead”: Persephone, carried off by Hades, became queen of the dead. See Greek Mythology.
  • “Not every lost chord could be resolved”: “Seated one day at the organ, / I was weary and ill at ease… But I struck one chord of music, / Like the sound of a great Amen” (Adelaide Anne Procter, “A Lost Chord,” 1858). Arthur Sullivan set it as “The Lost Chord” in 1877. See Music and Composers.
  • “this anatomy of sorrow”: Robert Burton’s The Anatomy of Melancholy (1621) gathers everything known of sorrow, and includes his own “Digression of Air,” a cosmography of the heavens.
  • “Was she become a man”: In Virginia Woolf’s Orlando (1928), the hero wakes as a woman: “Orlando had become a woman—there is no denying it.” See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “How had she not changed? All definitions failed and failed again”: The chapter portrays the form after death not as a fixed or final state but as a mutable, ambiguous, and complex condition, filled with transformations and lacking clear boundaries, blending aspects of life and death and challenging conventional understandings of identity and existence.
  • “One should not know one’s mother’s face, one’s father’s face”: “Now the brother shall betray the brother to death, and the father the son; and children shall rise up against their parents” (Mark 13:12), among the signs of the end. See The Bible.
  • “The infant should be an old man crying in the storm”: “Blow, winds, and crack your cheeks! rage! blow!” (Shakespeare, King Lear 3.2), the old king crying in the storm. See Shakespeare.
