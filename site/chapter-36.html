To All My Darlings

Chapter 36

On Chapter 36 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 35 · Home · Chapter 37 →

Summary

Catherine wants to know exactly where Hannah is now. Since she lives among both the living and the dead, she sees no reason she should not be told. She blames Mr. Spitzer, the last person to see Hannah alive, for his useless vagueness. Roused for once, he accuses Catherine of never having loved any living being. Catherine wonders whether Hannah is a man or a woman on the other side, and suspects she would turn heaven to chaos within a week.

Characters present

Joachim Spitzer · Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Vera Cartwheel

Key passages

From the research

  • Mr. Spitzer, despite his usual politeness, briefly animates to accuse Catherine of never having loved any living being, not even his dead brother. This accusation is significant as it frames the emotional and existential void experienced by the characters. Mr. Spitzer’s condition, his lethargy and feeling of being a counterfeit of himself, symbolizes a life that is a mere imitation, a repetition without meaning, reflecting a profound alienation from reality.
  • The passage describing how Mr. Spitzer’s eyes and ears are out of sync, how he perceives events late, and how every singer is off-key, poignantly illustrates the theme of disconnection from reality. This "second remove from life" emphasizes the difficulty of grasping truth or coherence in a fractured existence.
  • This passage contrasts Catherine’s radical blending of imagination and memory with Mr. Spitzer’s conservative, precise judgments. It highlights the fragile boundary between memory and imagination, suggesting that Catherine lives in a dreamlike state, unable or unwilling to distinguish reality from illusion, thereby sustaining her connection to lost loved ones and past events.
  • Cousin Hannah, the "fearlessly adventuresome cousin," symbolizes a restless spirit whose life and death continue to haunt Catherine. Her refusal to return, her evasiveness, and Catherine's futile attempts to communicate with her underscore the theme of unresolved grief and the impossibility of reclaiming the past. The descriptions of Hannah's travels and battles, and her transformation after death, suggest a transcendent yet ambiguous existence beyond mortal constraints.
  • The chapter uses powerful cosmic and natural imagery to evoke the vastness and mystery surrounding death and memory. Catherine’s longing to reach Cousin Hannah, the discussion of burial rites, and the question of where Hannah now resides (heaven, another world, or oblivion) deepen the meditation on mortality and the persistence of the dead in memory.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s role as the last living person to see Cousin Hannah alive, yet his refusal or inability to reveal her whereabouts, symbolizes both the barrier between the living and the dead and the limits of human knowledge about death. His competitive, jealous nature also represents a human obstacle to mourning and remembrance.
  • The richly detailed recounting of Hannah’s exploits across various cities and landscapes (Moscow, St. Petersburg, Siberia, Babylon) paints her as a mythic figure, a "great traveller" and warrior who transcended earthly bounds. This passage emphasizes her larger-than-life character and the enduring impact of her legacy, even as the physical world forgets her.
  • The chapter explores the blurring of identities, the shifting nature of time and place, and the instability of Catherine’s perception. The discussion of whether Hannah is now a man or woman, queen of the dead or invader of heaven, reflects the theme of transformation and the failure of fixed definitions. The metaphor of the house growing larger or smaller symbolizes the mutable nature of memory and existence.
  • Catherine’s belief that a woman should not remain long in heaven, that heaven will soon become a chaotic hell, speaks to a subversive view of cosmic order and gender roles. It suggests a world where traditional hierarchies and realities are unsettled, echoing Catherine’s own unsettled mind and the disruptive legacy of the suffrage captain.
  • The final lines, mentioning that "one should not know one’s mother’s face, one’s father’s face," and that "the infant should be an old man crying in the storm," encapsulate the pervasive sense of dislocation, confusion, and the inversion of natural order that permeates the chapter.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • Portrayed as a man deeply affected by loss and memory, embodying lethargy and a near-death consciousness.
  • He experiences time and reality with delay and distortion, e.g., hearing sounds a minute late.
  • His identity is fractured; he is a "counterfeit" of himself, haunted by the death of his twin brother.
  • He is conservative, cautious, and fearful of change, symbolizing a resistance to the fluidity of memory and identity.

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • A figure consumed by memory and imagination, struggling to distinguish between them.
  • She is radical, imaginative, and deeply invested in the past and in the memory of Cousin Hannah.
  • Her mind wanders between reality and dream, reflecting on loss and the elusive nature of death.

Cousin Hannah

  • A "fearlessly adventuresome" suffrage captain, dynamic and rebellious in life.
  • She embodies personal unity, passion, and the struggle against societal constraints.
  • Post-death, she becomes an ambiguous, almost mythical figure, transcending time and space.
  • Her death and disappearance symbolize the fragmentation and mystery surrounding memory and legacy.

Research: Themes

Death and the Afterlife

See: Death and Mortality

  • Death is portrayed not as an end but as a transformation and extension of existence into unknown realms.
  • The boundary between life and death is blurred; characters experience a "second remove" from life, living in a liminal space.
  • Death brings changes that alter identity and reality, sometimes enlarging horizons (as with Cousin Hannah) or shrinking and isolating (as with Mr. Spitzer).

Memory vs. Imagination

See: Dreams and Imagination · Memory and the Past

  • The novel explores the fragile line between memory and imagination.
  • Catherine struggles to separate what is remembered from what is imagined or dreamed.
  • This blurring reflects the human difficulty in holding onto a fixed identity or past, especially after loss.

Identity and Fragmentation

See: Identity and the Self

  • Characters experience fragmented selves, double lives, or counterfeit identities.
  • The death of a twin brother creates a split in Mr. Spitzer’s identity.
  • Cousin Hannah’s identity transcends death and conventional existence, becoming fluid and ambiguous.

Time and Perception

See: Time

  • Time is experienced non-linearly; sounds arrive late, events are recalled out of sequence.
  • The narrative reflects a consciousness that is "always in danger" of missing or misinterpreting life’s moments.
  • This theme underscores the instability of human experience and the elusive nature of reality.

The Role of Women and Feminine Power

See: Women, Gender, and Marriage

  • Cousin Hannah represents a powerful, rebellious feminine figure who challenges societal and cosmic orders.
  • There is tension between masculine and feminine realms, e.g., the description of Moslem Paradise where women are absent.
  • Catherine’s contemplation of womanhood and power in death questions traditional gender roles and metaphysical status.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Water and the Sea

See: The Sea and Water

  • Recurring images of water, surf, tides, and waves symbolize fluidity, change, and the unconscious.
  • Water is associated with identity shifts and the impermanence of life.

Stars and the Sky

See: Moon and Stars

  • Stars symbolize distant memories, lost souls, or unreachable ideals.
  • The motif of stars falling or being connected across time suggests cosmic continuity despite human fragmentation.

Mirrors and Reflections

See: Mirrors and Reflections

  • Mirrors signify distorted or partial reflections of identity and reality.
  • Catherine’s face in an inaccurate mirror hints at the elusive self and the failure of memory.

Animals and Nature

  • Birds (blackbirds, seagulls), horses, and natural phenomena (fog, wind, fireflies) evoke transformation, freedom, and the boundary between worlds.

Architectural and Geographic References

  • Bridges, doors, and roads symbolize transitions, passages between life and death, memory and oblivion.
  • Specific references to Boston, Araby, Moscow, and other places create a sense of vast, multi-locational experience transcending physical geography.

Research: Resources

  • “Had not the heavens divided, splitting apart?” Revelations 6:14 - the destruction of the old world order, as the heavens are seen as being destroyed and replaced with a new creation and Surah Ar-Rahman 37-45 describes the heavens splitting apart and becoming rose-red like burnt oil. Other interpretations interpret this metaphorically as a profound spiritual or emotional transformation, where the old self is broken down to make way for something new. Mark 1:10 describes a moment during Jesus’s baptism by John the Baptist where the heavens were torn open and the Spirit descended upon him like a dove. It serves as an emblematic expression of cataclysmic change, upheaval, and transition from one state to another, highlighting the novel's exploration of mortality, spiritual crisis, and the thin boundary between life and death.
  • Islam’s presence in the territory of modern-day Russia dates back to the 7th century with the Caucasus region coming into contact with the faith through the Muslim conquest of Persia. Volga Bulgaria is the first Muslim state from 922. From here Islam spread through trade and economic relationships and also through the Golden Horde Khanate in 1242. Muslim communities spread throughout the Volga region, the Urals, North Caucasus and Siberia. Many ethnic groups including Tatars, Bashkirs, Chechens, Ingush and Avars have historically practiced Islam in Russia. Sunni Islam is the majority from the Hanafi school and Sufism has a strong presence in the North Caucasus. Before the 10th century, Russians practiced Slavic religion, and Orthodox Christianity became the state religion of Kievan Rus in 987.
  • The chapter emphasizes the ephemeral nature of the physical body after death, its dissolution into elements like dust and water, and the uncertain fate of the spirit, wrapped in imagery of natural elements such as sea shells, clouds, and stars, which may symbolize the continuation of some form beyond corporeal life.
  • The chapter portrays the form after death not as a fixed or final state but as a mutable, ambiguous, and complex condition, filled with transformations and lacking clear boundaries, blending aspects of life and death and challenging conventional understandings of identity and existence.
  • The chapter portrays a triangular relationship between Peron, Joachim, and Catherine, marked by unfulfilled love, contrasting lives, and the tragic consequences of loss. Catherine's preference for Peron and her rejection of Joachim create a dynamic of longing and sorrow that defines the emotional core of the narrative. Joachim embodies steadfastness and grief, while Peron represents passion and escape, and Catherine is caught between these polarities, her own existence fragile and dreamlike.
  • Music of the spheres or Musica universalis is the metaphysical principle that mathematical relationships express qualities or ‘tones’ of energy.
  • Mr. Spitzer has some symptoms of OCD (Obsessive-Compulsive Personality Disorder) which was first identified in 1868 by Wilhelm Griesinger.
  • Julia Shaw is a psychologist and an expert in complex autobiographical false memory. She asserts that all memories are illusions which are similar to how quantum physicists describe reality, what Einstein called a persistent illusion. It’s the same way Plato talked about prisoners in a cave confusing shadows on the wall with the reality outside the cave. Current research found a robust false memory effect. However, participants were able to differentiate false memories and actual memories when using specific phenomenological tasks. False memory in the novel is portrayed as a multifaceted phenomenon that shapes and distorts reality for the characters. It is linked to themes of identity, time, and the elusive nature of truth. Memory is not a stable foundation but a shifting, sometimes deceptive, landscape where truth and illusion coexist. Characters struggle to discern reality amid the fluidity and fallibility of memory, and this struggle underscores much of the novel’s psychological depth and philosophical inquiry.
  • The golden rooster in the Chinese Zodiac is a person determined and brave enough to face all difficulties. The golden rooster in the chapter can be understood as a metaphor for the liminal space between life and death, the natural and supernatural, and the continuity of existence beyond physical demise. It is a powerful emblem of both hope and the eerie, unsettling awareness of mortality that permeates the novel's atmosphere.
  • Mercury in Greek mythology is the god of financial gain, commerce, eloquence, messages, communication, etc.
  • Letter writing has been around since 500 BC. Writing a letter to yourself is thought of as therapeutic or a type of time capsule of telling your future self how you were doing in the past. Mr. Spitzer writes letters that never arrive. Catherine writes letters that are illegible. Letter writing in the novel symbolizes the human desire to communicate and be remembered, the persistence of memory amid loss, the pain of unfulfilled desires, and the complex, often failed attempts to bridge the gaps between individuals, between life and death, and between reality and illusion. The motif is deeply intertwined with the novel's broader themes of memory, identity, and the elusive nature of truth and connection.
  • Boston Post Road was a system of mail delivery routes between New York City and Boston, Massachusetts which became one of the first major highways in the US.
  • “From Minsk to Pinsk” is a common trope in classic Jewish humor, used to evoke a long, arduous, or circular journey within the Russian Empire. It is less a single specific joke between two businessmen than a setting for numerous different stories, often featuring Jewish peddlers or paupers and other travelers. It refers to a journey or passage between two places that symbolize a vast and difficult journey through strange or foreign lands. Minsk and Pinsk are actual cities in Eastern Europe, located in Belarus, and the phrase evokes a sense of traveling through a remote, challenging, or even mysterious landscape.
  • An Archangel is an angel of high rank usually associated with the Abrahamic religions.
  • Grand Central Station or terminal is located in Midtown Manhattan, New York City.
  • The bridges are significant as metaphors for the liminality and ambiguity of existence, marking the passage between opposing states such as life and death, reality and dream, presence and absence. They highlight the instability and fluidity of boundaries in the novel's world, underscoring the themes of transition, loss, and the elusive nature of truth and identity.
  • Brooklyn Bridge: see Chapter 2, Resources, for the full entry.
  • Waterloo Bridge is a road and foot traffic bridge crossing the River Thames in London.
  • The Bridge of Sighs is a bridge in Venice, Italy.
  • The Bridge of Lies is a legendary pedestrian bridge located in the center of the Transylvanian city of Sibiu in central Romania.
  • The Hanging Gardens of Babylon were one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World by Hellenic culture.
  • The seventh crystal stairway may refer to Jacob’s Ladder.
  • Seraglios or serail is the sequestered living quarters used by wives and concubines in an Ottoman household.
  • The cock crowing represents the transition from life to death, the inevitability of change, and the sober acknowledgment of human mortality.
