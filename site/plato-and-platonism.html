Definition from research

Chapter 2

  • Platonism is the philosophy of Plato which affirms the existence of abstract objects, which are asserted to exist in a third realm distinct from both the sensible external world and from the internal world of consciousness. It is the opposite of nominalism.

Chapter 3

  • Plutarch was a Greek Middle Platonist philosopher, biographer, essayist, and priest at the Temple of Apollo. Plutarch functions as a symbol of the mother’s intellectual and cultural anchoring amidst her drifting consciousness, representing a link to enduring human wisdom and the complexities of love, fate, and history that permeate her visionary world.

Chapter 6

  • “You think that beauty is as beauty does”: The proverb says a person’s true beauty is in their character and actions, not their physical appearance. The older proverb is “handsome is as handsome does” (1500s). The sentiment is found in the writings of Geoffrey Chaucer in “The Wife of Bath’s Tale” (c. 1387) from The Canterbury Tales. Chaucer’s lines suggest that the most virtuous person, regardless of physical appearance, is the most beautiful. The Greek philosophers Plato and Aristotle linked beauty to virtue, order, and symmetry. This perspective influenced the development of the proverb, suggesting that inner qualities are just as important as outward appearance in determining beauty.
  • “the angels do not marry, for they are male and female in one body”: In Plato’s Symposium Aristophanes tells of the first humans, double beings later split in two, each half forever seeking the other.

Chapter 8

  • “that dream within the dream”: This may refer to Edgar Allan Poe’s poem “A Dream within a Dream” (1849), which says, “All that we see or seem / Is but a dream within a dream.” This dream within a dream is a recurring theme throughout the book. The idea that life is a dream goes back to Plato, Aristotle, and Descartes. The first stanza ends with that statement, and the last stanza turns it into a question: “Is all that we see or seem / But a dream within a dream?” This symbolizes the recursive, elusive quality of existence in the novel: a reality constantly refracted through multiple dreamlike states that challenge the notion of a fixed, singular truth.

Chapter 10

  • “What is man but a poor, two-legged animal?”: Plato defined man as a featherless biped, and Diogenes answered with a plucked chicken. Lear calls man “such a poor, bare, forked animal” (King Lear 3.4).
  • “good, true, beautiful”: Plato’s three highest ideas, the Good, the True, and the Beautiful.

Chapter 12

  • The theme of shadows runs through the novel. It brings to mind Plato’s work, The Allegory of the Cave, or Plato’s Cave. It is an allegory presented by the Greek philosopher Plato in his work, Republic. The Cave describes a group of people who have lived chained to the wall of a cave all their lives, facing a blank wall. The people watch shadows projected on the wall from objects passing in front of a fire behind them and give names to these shadows. The shadows are the prisoners’ reality, but are not accurate representations of the real world. The shadows represent the fragment of reality that can normally be perceived through the senses, while the objects under the sun represent the true forms of objects that can only be perceived through reason. The philosopher is like a prisoner who is freed from the cave and comes to understand that the shadows on the wall are actually not the direct source of the images seen. A philosopher aims to understand and perceive the higher levels of reality. However, the other inmates of the cave do not even desire to leave their prison, for they know no better life. (Wikipedia) See the Chapter 3 resources.
  • “that we are not as shadows”: “What shadows we are, and what shadows we pursue” (Edmund Burke, 1780), and “our days upon earth are a shadow” (Job 8:9).

Chapter 13

  • “immortal gardens”: In Plato’s Apology, Socrates looks forward to death as a chance to converse with Orpheus, Homer, and Hesiod.

Chapter 14

  • “the music of the spheres, the silent music”: Boethius was a Roman senator, consul, magister officiorum, polymath, historian, and philosopher of the Early Middle Ages. He sought to reconcile the teachings of Plato and Aristotle with Christian theology. He broke logic into three parts: that which defines, that which divides, and that which deduces. There are three types of arguments: those of necessity, of ready believability, and sophistry. In De Musica there are three classifications: Musica mundana, Musica humana, Musica instrumentalis. In Dante’s Divine Comedy, the spirit of Boethius is pointed out by Saint Thomas Aquinas and is mentioned further in the poem. In his book De Institutione Musica (Fundamentals of Music) his drawings appear striking and may be an inspiration for the organization of Young’s novel. Musica universalis or music of the spheres or harmony of the spheres is a Greek philosophical concept founded by Pythagoras. This metaphysical principle stated that mathematical relationships could produce ‘tones’ of energy that could produce, among other things, sounds. Pythagoras proposed that the celestial bodies all have their own unique hum based on their orbital revolution. This idea influenced many scholars until the end of the Renaissance. Johannes Kepler, a German astronomer from the 1600s wrote extensively on the subject. (Wikipedia) Later astronomy set it aside. Boethius wrote The Consolation of Philosophy in prison awaiting execution, with its Wheel of Fortune.
  • “the appearance beyond the appearance”: Allegory of the Cave, Plato describes people who have spent their entire lives chained by their necks and ankles in front of an inner wall with a view of the empty outer wall of the cave. They observe the shadows projected onto the outer wall by objects carried behind the inner wall by people who are invisible to the chained “prisoners” and who walk along the inner wall with a fire behind them, creating the shadows on the inner wall in front of the prisoners. The “sign bearers” pronounce the names of the objects, the sounds of which are reflected near the shadows and are understood by the prisoners as if they were coming from the shadows themselves. Only the shadows and sounds are the prisoners’ reality, which are not accurate representations of the real world. The shadows represent distorted and blurred copies of reality that can be perceived through the senses, while the objects under the Sun represent the true forms of objects that can only be perceived through reason. Three higher levels exist: natural science; deductive mathematics, geometry, and logic; and the theory of forms. Socrates explains how the philosopher is like a prisoner freed from the cave and comes to understand that the shadows on the wall are not the direct source of the images seen. A philosopher aims to understand and perceive the higher levels of reality. However, the other inmates of the cave do not even desire to leave their prison, for they know no better life. (Wikipedia)
  • “knew that the dog was Socrates”: Socrates was a Greek philosopher who wrote no texts of his own but is credited as one of the founders of Western philosophy. In the work Republic, written by Plato, there is a Socratic dialogue. In it Socrates argues that the dog is very much like a philosopher because the dog distinguishes the face of a friend and of an enemy only by the criterion of knowing and not knowing. (Wikipedia) Socrates swore “By the dog, the god of the Egyptians” (Gorgias 482b).
  • “the coachman was her conscience”: The Katha Upanishad: “Know the Self as the lord of the chariot, the body as the chariot, the intellect as the charioteer, the mind as the reins, and the senses as the horses.” In Plato’s Phaedrus, the soul is a charioteer driving two horses.

Chapter 23

  • “Poor mortals all and blind as bats in a cave”: This may echo Plato’s prisoners of the cave, who take shadows for the real (Republic 7).

Chapter 24

  • “which was already occupied by Plato’s boat”: Plato’s boat might refer to the Ship of State by Plato (Republic, Book 6). It is a metaphor comparing governing a city-state to being the captain of a ship and only philosopher kings should rule.

Chapter 28

  • “that spindle of creative flame which is said to sleep in the eternal darkness, weaving our bodies”: Weaving plays a significant role in the mythologies of various cultures, often symbolizing creation, destiny, and even the weaving of life itself. In many stories, weaving is attributed to deities or individuals who control fate or have supernatural abilities. For example, the Greek Moirae (Fates) spin, measure, and cut the thread of human lives, and the Norse goddess Frigg is associated with weaving and seiðr magic, which involves weaving destiny. Greek mythology: Athena, Arachne, Moirae (Fates), Penelope, Philomela. Norse mythology: Frigg, The Norns. Other mythologies: Neith, Laima, Päivätär and Kuutar, Spider Woman, Arianrhod. In Plato’s Myth of Er (Republic 10), the Spindle of Necessity turns the spheres on the knees of Necessity, and her daughters, the Fates, spin each life. In the chapter the “spindle of creative flame” weaves bodies, souls, and dreams, so that identity is woven like a tapestry from many threads of experience and memory, made of many intertwined selves rather than one.

Chapter 29

  • “between the ecstasy of happiness and that of sorrow”: In philosophy, happiness and sorrow are intertwined, with sorrow often seen as a necessary component for experiencing the depth of joy and vice versa rather than as simple opposites. It can be viewed in a number of ways: a dialectical relationship (yin and yang-complementary forces where one cannot exist without the other); the contrast highlights the value of positive experiences; a catalyst for growth, introspection, and a deeper understanding of life’s complexities. Philosophical perspectives are found in Buddhism, Christianity, Hinduism, Stoicism, Utilitarianism, Hedonism, Life Satisfaction Theory. Plato and Aristotle’s eudaimonia where flourishing and well-being are achieved by living a virtuous life, not simply experiencing happiness. Nirvana is a state of liberation from suffering and the cycle of rebirth. Equanimity is the ability to remain calm and balanced in the face of both pleasure and pain. Mr. Spitzer finds it as hard to tell butterfly from moth as “to distinguish between brother and brother or between the ecstasy of happiness and that of sorrow,” for “Seldom did happiness and sorrow occur in pure and disrelated states.”

Chapter 32

  • “that pale cavalier who, with disheveled locks, with foaming mouth, is driven by the mare”: In Plato’s Phaedrus the soul is a charioteer with two horses, one noble and one unruly, who must master the wild one with the bit.

Chapter 36

  • “So he lived at this second remove from reality”: Plato’s imitator is “thrice removed from the king and from the truth,” an imitation of an imitation (Plato, Republic 10, trans. Benjamin Jowett).
  • “It seemed to him that she had never distinguished between imagination and memory”: Julia Shaw is a psychologist and an expert in complex autobiographical false memory (Julia Shaw, The Memory Illusion, Random House, 2016). She asserts that all memories are illusions which are similar to how quantum physicists describe reality, much as Einstein wrote that “the distinction between past, present and future is only a stubbornly persistent illusion” (Albert Einstein, letter to the family of Michele Besso, March 21, 1955). It’s the same way Plato talked about prisoners in a cave confusing shadows on the wall with the reality outside the cave. A 2000 study found a robust false memory effect. However, participants were able to differentiate false memories and actual memories when using specific phenomenological tasks (Jeffrey S. Anastasi, Matthew G. Rhodes, and Marcy C. Burns, “Distinguishing between Memory Illusions and Actual Memories Using Phenomenological Measurements and Explicit Warnings,” American Journal of Psychology 113, no. 1, 2000, 1-26).
  • “for total ignorance was total knowledge”: Nicholas of Cusa called it learned ignorance, De docta ignorantia (1440): the wise man knows that he does not know. Socrates said much the same in Plato’s Apology.

Chapter 37

  • “How should death be different from life, my mother asked”: Life as a state of experience and potential: Socrates thought death might be a dreamless sleep or passage to another realm where one can converse with the dead. Existentialism believes death is an inevitable aspect of human existence and that it can lead to a heightened sense of self-awareness and responsibility. Epicureanism argues death cannot harm a person, as it’s either not yet experienced (while alive) or not experienced at all (once dead). Death is defined as the loss of the capacity to engage in vital activities and the cessation of consciousness. Wittgenstein wrote that death is not an event in life, for we do not live to experience it (Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus 6.4311). Schopenhauer believed that understanding death as the ultimate end of life can provide a perspective on the meaning of life itself. Freud thought the life instinct (Eros) and the death instinct (Thanatos) are fundamental forces in human behavior.
  • “as man became woman with her heaving breasts”: Gender as a social construct refers to the socially created roles, behaviors, expressions, and identities associated with being a woman, man, boy, or girl. It’s not inherently determined by biology, but rather shaped by cultural norms, societal expectations, and historical contexts. This means gender is not fixed and can vary across cultures and time. In short, it is not a biological reality: it is socially created, varies across cultures, is dynamic and fluid, and is distinct from biological sex. Philosophical thought on gender goes back to ancient Greece, where Pythagoras’s table of opposites set male against female (Aristotle, Metaphysics 1.5) and Plato argued that women could be guardians (Republic 5). Later came John Locke, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Judith Sargent Murray (“On the Equality of the Sexes,” 1790), and Mary Wollstonecraft (A Vindication of the Rights of Woman, 1792). The chapter and novel present gender as a fluid and changeable construct rather than a rigid, innate fact, using metaphorical and dreamlike language to question fixed dichotomies between male and female, self and other, life and death.
  • “the floor of the Lost Atlantis”: Atlantis can be a metaphor for something that is no longer attainable. It is a fictional island used as an allegory for the hubris of nations in Plato’s Timaeus and Critias (about 360 BC). It has had a large influence on literature, arts, and culture.

Chapter 38

  • “the beautiful music of the spheres”: Musica Universalis (music of the spheres) concept incorporates the metaphysical principle that mathematical relationships express qualities or “tones” of energy that manifests in numbers, visual angles, shapes and sounds which are all connected within a pattern of proportion. This has been explored by Pythagoras, Plato, Aristotle (he rejected the idea), Boethius developed three categories of music (musica mundana, musica humana, and musica quae in quibusdam constituta est instrumentis), Kepler expounded the idea in Harmonices Mundi (1619). Boethius’s writings were later illustrated, and Young may have taken inspiration from these illustrations for the novel.
  • “Heracleitus is supposed to say that all things are in motion and nothing at rest; he compares them to the stream of a river, and says that you cannot go into the same water twice” (Plato, Cratylus 402a, trans. Benjamin Jowett).

Chapter 39

  • Plato divided the soul into three parts, reason, spirit, and appetite (Republic 4).
  • “she knew she was insane, quite mad”: “Divine madness” or “theia mania” refers to unconventional, sometimes unpredictable behavior attributed to divine inspiration or the influence of a god, often associated with mystical experiences or spiritual practice. Plato explored the idea as a source of creativity and inspiration: “the greatest of blessings come to us through madness, when it is sent as a gift of the gods” (Plato, Phaedrus 244a, trans. Harold N. Fowler, 1925). It can take the form of “religious ecstasy” where a person has a profound connection to the divine, transcending normal perceptions and societal norms.

Chapter 40

  • In Plato’s Symposium (212d) Alcibiades bursts in drunk, crowned with ivy and violets, and praises Socrates.
  • “who should know when the dream ended”: In philosophy there is the question of dreams by Zhuangzi, Plato, and Aristotle. Descartes asked and tried to answer the following question “How can I know that I am not now dreaming?” which seems to be the same question Catherine is preoccupied with. Descartes’ answer was that we cannot trust our senses in waking life without invoking a benevolent God who would not deceive us. Catherine takes this one step further at the end of Chapter 39 and asks, “What if God is dreaming?” In psychology the question “Is Life a Dream?” is answered with the coherence of the waking world does give evidence that it is not just a figment of our imagination. However, the objects that we perceive as permanent are in reality through the science of quantum physics mostly made up of empty space. In a sense people do live in a dream world that is created by the mind. When people are awake, their minds conform their dreaming to a reliable set of patterns, which they assume to be determined by a reality that exists independently of their experience of it, though they have no way of knowing that reality except through the complex ways in which it affects their dream. There is also reality that is experienced and affected by psychedelic drugs and other altered states of consciousness. The chapter weaves together the philosophical inquiries of Zhuangzi, Plato, Aristotle, and Descartes on dreams to underscore the complexity and mystery of human consciousness and the elusive quest for truth amid illusions.
  • “As between the figurehead and the greatest captain of the greatest boat”: Plato’s parable of the ship (Republic 6.488), where a quarrelling crew ignores the true navigator, symbolizes the dangers of a political system not based on expertise. It is often called the Ship of State, where the ship represents a nation or government, with the crew, captain, and passengers symbolizing different social classes or politicians. It encapsulates the novel’s exploration of human vulnerability, the elusiveness of truth, and the interplay between reality and fantasy. It stands as a haunting image of mortality, the passage of time, and the inescapable drift toward oblivion, yet it also hints at the persistence of memory and the faint glimmers of hope amid the surrounding darkness.

Chapter 44

  • “splitting her apart into the lover and the loved”: In Plato’s Symposium, Aristophanes tells how the first humans were split in two, so that each half seeks its other: “The women who are a section of the woman do not care for men, but have female attachments” (Symposium 191e, trans. Benjamin Jowett, 1871).

Chapter 45

  • “The deepest sleep is dreamless.”: Socrates says that if death is a dreamless sleep, “to die is gain; for eternity is then only a single night” (Plato, Apology 40d-e, trans. Benjamin Jowett, 1871). The Mandukya Upanishad names deep sleep, where the sleeper desires nothing and dreams no dream, as the third state of the self.

Chapter 47

  • “Perhaps this world was the Lost Atlantis”: Atlantis is a fictional island mentioned in an allegory on the hubris of nations in Plato’s Timaeus and Critias: “in a single day and night of misfortune… the island of Atlantis in like manner disappeared in the depths of the sea” (Timaeus 25d, trans. Benjamin Jowett, 1871).
  • “he was agonized by attributing consciousness to everything”: This may refer to panpsychism, the view that mind, or something like mind, is a fundamental and widespread feature of reality, present in some form even in its most basic elements. It has roots in ancient Greek philosophy, in Thales, who held that “all things are full of gods” (Aristotle, On the Soul 1.5), and in Plato, and later in Spinoza, Leibniz, and Schopenhauer. Mr. Spitzer suffers because he senses consciousness not only in people but in clouds, stars, waves, palings, and weathervanes, so that the line between the living and the lifeless blurs and his own sense of self grows unstable. It belongs to the novel’s larger struggle with reality, illusion, memory, and identity.

Chapter 48

  • “for this life was a long rehearsal of music for the silence”: Life is a rehearsal, a practice, for the silence of death. The “music” is the experience of a life, passing and temporary; the “silence” is death, where all of it ceases, and which “could surely not answer or reproach.” Socrates says that “the true philosophers, Simmias, are always occupied in the practice of dying” (Plato, Phaedo 67e, trans. Benjamin Jowett, 1871), and Montaigne titled an essay “That to philosophize is to learn to die” (Essays 1.20, 1580). Hamlet’s last words are “the rest is silence” (5.2).
  • “he who knew rhombs, circles, semicircles”: Mr. Spitzer knows the geometry of things, rhombs, circles, boxes within boxes, octahedrons, and the eclipses of light by darkness and darkness by light: the nested, overlapping forms of a reality both ordered and hidden. He calls them “the forms of a divine nature.” In Plato’s Timaeus the elements are built of regular solids, air of the octahedron (55-56), and Galileo wrote that the book of the universe “is written in the language of mathematics, and its characters are triangles, circles, and other geometric figures” (The Assayer, 1623, trans. Stillman Drake, 1957).

Chapter 49

  • “There was nothing he feared so much as the light of the sun”: The prisoner led out of Plato’s cave is dazzled and pained by the sun (Republic 7, 515e-516b), and La Rochefoucauld wrote that “Neither the sun nor death can be looked at steadily” (Maxims 26, 1665). “For life was itself this indirection”: “Tell all the truth but tell it slant — / Success in Circuit lies” (Emily Dickinson, written about 1868, published 1945).
  • Porphyry of Tyre was a Neoplatonic philosopher born in Tyre, Roman Phoenicia during Roman rule. He edited and published the Enneads, the only collection of the work of Plotinus, his teacher. He wrote original works in the Greek language on a wide variety of topics, ranging from music theory to Homer. His Isagoge or Introduction, an introduction to logic and philosophy, was the standard textbook on logic throughout the Middle Ages in its Latin and Arabic translations. Porphyry was, and still is, also well-known for his anti-Christian polemics. Through works such as Philosophy from Oracles and Against the Christians (which was banned by Constantine the Great), he was involved in a controversy with early Christians. Important works and philosophy attributed to him. His Isagoge set the question for the Middle Ages, whether genera and species exist in reality or only in the mind. (Wikipedia)

Chapter 50

  • “threads wrapped around a spindle”: In Plato’s myth of Er the heavens turn on the Spindle of Necessity, and “on the upper surface of each circle is a siren, who goes round with them, hymning a single tone or note. The eight together form one harmony” (Republic 10, 617b, trans. Benjamin Jowett, 1871).

Chapter 51

  • “Seldom did he meet with anyone who did not suffer from some form of complex illusion beyond necessity”: Most people Mr. Spitzer meets suffer “some form of complex illusion beyond necessity”: the old woman who believes she is a bird cage, the piano-tuner declaring foreheads “hollow.” Blindness and deafness become metaphors for the limits of perception: the blind portrait painter, the blind war veteran in the galleries of Europe, the deaf music critics who hear every error without hearing a note, judgment resting on something other than the senses. “One great dream informing all dreamers and all dreams” recalls a collective unconscious or universal mind, or Plato’s Form of the Good, the source all strive toward behind the shadows of sense. Happiness is fleeting as a snowflake; “three minutes of happiness seemed perhaps all there might be,” and “the greatest happiness might come through the sorrow of waiting, of postponement.” The passage holds the absurdity of existence and, through Mr. Spitzer’s work for the dead, the responsibility of making sense of their lives.

Chapter 52

  • “Should doubt move mountains which faith had not moved”: Mr. Spitzer wonders if doubt should move mountains “which faith had not moved.” This is a direct inversion of the common saying that faith can move mountains. This highlights a core philosophical tension between believing in something despite a lack of evidence (faith) and questioning everything (doubt). Jesus told his disciples, “If ye have faith as a grain of mustard seed, ye shall say unto this mountain, Remove hence to yonder place; and it shall remove; and nothing shall be impossible unto you” (Matthew 17:20, KJV). The image of the suffragette searching for a lost skirt, leading to the death of her lover, suggests the absurdity of human pursuits. The pursuit of something seemingly trivial (the skirt) leads to tragic consequences, mirroring the existentialist idea that life is inherently meaningless, and humans create their own meaning through their actions. The description of the skirt as “white lace gleaming through snow and its little flowers which were like the memories of little loves” blurs the lines between reality and memory. It suggests that perceptions of reality are shaped by past experiences and emotions, leading to a subjective and potentially distorted understanding of the world. The mention of the suffragette crying “Long live woman! Vive la femme! Viva la donna!” in many languages, “perhaps even in Sanskrit which was the language written upon a silent snowflake,” touches on the philosophy of language. It suggests that language can be a powerful tool for expressing universal concepts (like the importance of women) but that there are also limits to what language can convey. There are “secret languages never to be translated into words,” implying that some truths are beyond the reach of verbal expression. The reference to, “the suffragette who had lost her apostle” could also be referencing the loss of an ideal form. Platonism is the belief that everything seen is a shadow or “lesser” version of a perfect form. The loss of her apostle, who could be a student or follower, is also the loss of what the suffragette sees as a perfect version of herself. The next paragraph emphasizes the individual’s choice and action in the face of an indifferent or absurd universe. The suffragette’s decision to dress as a man can be seen as an assertion of her freedom and rejection of societal norms. The fantastical images of footmen and golden stairways highlight the human tendency to create meaning and beauty in a world that may ultimately be meaningless. The contrast between Cousin Hannah’s practical clothing and the elaborate attire of other climbers could be interpreted as a reflection of the philosophical debate between idealism and materialism. The suffragette’s focus on functionality suggests a more materialistic worldview, while the other climbers’ emphasis on appearance could be seen as representing idealism. The repeated blurring of the lines between mountains and clouds, and the shifting perceptions of the moon, raise questions about the nature of reality and the limitations of human perception. The statement, “the moon had floated most amazingly distending worlds,” suggests that reality is not fixed but is shaped by individual perspectives. The image of the footman cutting a golden stairway from a frozen lake to the mountains of the moon could be an allusion to Plato’s Allegory of the Cave. The stairway could represent the philosopher’s ascent from the darkness of the cave (the material world) to the light of the sun (the realm of Forms). The mountains of the moon could symbolize a higher realm of knowledge and understanding. The paragraph’s emphasis on nature, individual experience, and the search for spiritual meaning aligns with the themes of Transcendentalism. The suffragette’s mountain climbing could be seen as a metaphor for the individual’s journey to transcend the limitations of the material world and connect with a higher power or truth. The mention of “horizons unknown as the stars floated” could also be interpreted in a nihilistic way, suggesting that there are no ultimate goals or destinations and that all human endeavors are ultimately meaningless. At first it’s a whimsical and imaginative description of mountain climbing, then, beneath the surface, it explores fundamental philosophical questions about freedom, reality, perception, and the meaning of life which invites philosophical reflection.
  • “being that Lost Atlantis”: Plato tells of Atlantis in the Timaeus and Critias (about 360 BC), an island empire beyond the Pillars of Hercules that “in a single day and night of misfortune” disappeared in the depths of the sea (Timaeus, trans. Benjamin Jowett).

Chapter 53

  • “They probably would not have understood Mr. Spitzer’s swan song”: The legend that the swan sings most beautifully just before it dies is old; in Plato’s Phaedo (84e-85b) Socrates says swans sing at their death out of joy that they are going to the god they serve.

Chapter 55

  • “some old watered bond issues of the Lost Atlantis”: “Lost Atlantis” is a mythical island civilization described by Plato in his dialogues as a powerful, technologically advanced society that was destroyed by earthquakes and sank beneath the sea. While the story is a well-known myth and has inspired theories about real locations and even modern technological advancements, most historians and scientists believe Atlantis was a philosophical allegory created by Plato to serve as a cautionary tale against hubris.

Chapter 61

  • “A swan when it is dying has the right to sing”: The swan song myth is an ancient belief that swans, typically silent throughout their lives, sing a beautiful song just before they die. The term is now used metaphorically to describe a final, often brilliant, performance, work, or gesture before retirement or death. This myth has roots in Greek and Roman mythology, appearing in the writings of Aeschylus and Plato, and was later referenced in English literature by Chaucer. The English phrase “swan song,” a translation of the German Schwanengesang, dates from 1831; Thomas Carlyle is credited with popularizing it in his satire Sartor Resartus, written in 1831.

Chapter 71

  • “What’s Wrong with Democracy?”: What’s Wrong with Democracy? Critiques of democracy existed across a wide range of historical periods and came from diverse intellectual and social movements. These arguments addressed everything from the flaws of ancient Greek democracy to the systemic exclusions within American democracy before the Civil Rights era. Philosophers like Plato warned that in a democracy, the masses, driven by ignorance and emotion, could become susceptible to self-serving demagogues. This “rule by the mob” could lead to rash, unjust decisions, such as the execution of Socrates in ancient Athens. Later, thinkers like Thomas Hobbes argued that democracy leads to chaos and civil war, advocating for a strong, singular sovereign to prevent societal collapse. Some classical and 19th-century thinkers, including Plato, Thomas Carlyle, and John Ruskin, argued that society should be led by a knowledgeable elite rather than the common people. This perspective asserted that only a select few possessed the wisdom and virtue necessary to govern effectively, and that the majority of people were ill-equipped for the complexities of statecraft. As democracy expanded during the Industrial Revolution, critics like Carlyle, John Ruskin, and William Lecky argued that it produced anarchy and chaos within modern industrial society. They believed that democratic capitalism prioritized profit and individual self-interest over social stability and the public good. Commentators like Matthew Arnold expressed concern that democracy led to a flattening of culture and a decline in intellectual standards. He feared that the “average” tastes of the masses would displace high culture and that society would suffer from a lack of moral and intellectual leadership. Before the passage of the Civil Rights Act in 1964, critics pointed out the hypocrisy of American democracy, which systematically excluded many of its own citizens. This was particularly evident in the Jim Crow South, where discriminatory laws like poll taxes and literacy tests disenfranchised millions of African Americans and poor whites. The history of American democracy has been marked by periods of deep instability and division. Scholars point to the 1890s and the aftermath of the Compromise of 1877, which ended Reconstruction, as periods of “backsliding” where racial conflict produced significant damage to democratic ideals. This history was built on the foundation of racial exclusion, as some historians note that democratic norms only took hold among white people after racial equality was removed from the agenda. Especially during the Gilded Age (1865-1915), the rise of powerful, and often corrupt, political machines led many Americans to question the health of their democracy. Critiques focused on the manipulation of the voting process, the influence of money in politics, and the resulting feeling that elections were no longer fair or truly representative of the people. The failure of some interwar and post-WWII democratic governments, such as France’s Fourth Republic, was often cited as a failure of democracy itself. Frequent changes in government and a fractured party system led many to conclude that democracy was inherently unstable and inefficient. The Civil Rights Act marks a significant turning point in the realization of American democratic ideals.
