Chapter 49

  • Problem of Universals - The problem of universals is an ancient question from metaphysics that has inspired a range of philosophical topics and disputes. "Should the properties an object has in common with other objects, such as color and shape, be considered to exist beyond those objects? And if a property exists separately from objects, what is the nature of that existence?” The problem of universals relates to various inquiries closely related to metaphysics, logic, and epistemology, as far back as Plato and Aristotle, in efforts to define the mental connections a human makes when they understand a property such as shape or color to be the same in nonidentical objects. Universals are qualities or relations found in two or more entities. As an example, if all cup holders are circular in some way, circularity may be considered a universal property of cup holders. Further, if two daughters can be considered female offspring of Frank, the qualities of being female, offspring, and of Frank, are universal properties of the two daughters. Many properties can be universal: being human, red, male or female, liquid or solid, big or small, etc. Philosophers agree that human beings can talk and think about universals, but disagree on whether universals exist in reality beyond mere thought and speech.

The only value of universal characters is that they help us, by reasoning, to know new truths about individual things.” William James, The Principles of Psychology (1890). There are at least three ways in which a realist might try to answer James' challenge of explaining the reason why universal conceptions are more lofty than those of particulars: the moral–political answer, the mathematical–scientific answer, and the anti-paradoxical answer. Each has contemporary or near-contemporary advocates.

Chapter 51

  • Boethius was a Roman senator, consul, magister officiorum, polymath, historian, and philosopher of the Early Middle Ages. He was a central figure in the translation of the Greek classics into Latin, a precursor to the Scholastic movement, and, along with Cassiodorus, one of the two leading Christian scholars of the 6th century. The local cult of Boethius in the Diocese of Pavia was sanctioned by the Sacred Congregation of Rites in 1883, confirming the diocese's custom of honoring him on the 23rd of October. Boethius was born in Rome a few years after the forced abdication of the last Western Roman emperor, Romulus Augustulus. A member of the Anicii family, he was orphaned following the family's sudden decline and was raised by Quintus Aurelius Memmius Symmachus, a later consul. After mastering both Latin and Greek in his youth, Boethius rose to prominence as a statesman during the Ostrogothic Kingdom, becoming a senator by age 25, a consul by age 33, and later chosen as a personal advisor to Theodoric the Great. In seeking to reconcile the teachings of Plato and Aristotle with Christian theology, Boethius sought to translate the entirety of the Greek classics for Western scholars. He published numerous transcriptions and commentaries of the works of Nicomachus, Porphyry, and Cicero, among others, and wrote extensively on matters concerning music, mathematics, and theology. His Arithmetica, geometria, et musica became the classical quadrivium or the basis of the liberal arts curriculum in the Middle Ages. This grouping emphasizes the interconnectedness of these disciplines, with arithmetic and geometry as foundational, and music was understood as a manifestation of mathematical principles. These disciplines were seen as pathways to understanding the underlying mathematical structure of the universe and were considered essential for a well-rounded education.

The line, "For the dream of life, even like that of death, must ever contain within it an element of the unrealized, the unrealizable —else all would be complete," suggests that imperfection and the unattainable are integral to existence. This resonates with existentialist thought, which emphasizes the inherent incompleteness and absurdity of human life. The description of Mr. Spitzer "dreaming like the heart of God upon a world never created, uncreated, this world dreaming itself even when there was no counterpart" suggests a world that is in a constant state of becoming, and perhaps only exists within the mind of the dreamer. Mr. Spitzer's fear of being evicted from the "citadel" and being born into a world where he misses fundamental sensory experiences (smell of the rose, sound of birds) speaks to the existential anxiety of losing one's sense of self and belonging. The idea of a mother who is a "bearded man armored" challenges traditional notions of gender and identity. This can be linked to philosophical discussions about the social construction of gender and the fluidity of identity. The image of the "old jockey who dreamed that his dead horse lived" and the subsequent shock of realizing the dream's reality highlights the power of the unconscious and the irrational. This aligns with psychoanalytic theories, particularly those of Freud, which emphasize the influence of unconscious desires and repressed memories on human behavior. The phrase "We lived by an imperfect concatenation, even as we died—by echoes, tremblings, reverberations, sourceless thoughts" suggests that life is a series of disjointed and fragmented experiences. This aligns with Buddhist philosophy, which emphasizes the impermanence of all things and the illusory nature of the self.

Chapter 52

  • Mr. Spitzer wonders if doubt should move mountains “which faith had not moved." This is a direct inversion of the common saying that faith can move mountains. This highlights a core philosophical tension between believing in something despite a lack of evidence (faith) and questioning everything (doubt). - Matthew 17:20. The image of the suffragette searching for a lost skirt, leading to the death of her lover, suggests the absurdity of human pursuits. The pursuit of something seemingly trivial (the skirt) leads to tragic consequences, mirroring the existentialist idea that life is inherently meaningless, and humans create their own meaning through their actions. The description of the skirt as "white lace gleaming through snow and its little flowers which were like the memories of little loves" blurs the lines between reality and memory. It suggests that our perceptions of reality are shaped by our past experiences and emotions, leading to a subjective and potentially distorted understanding of the world. The mention of the suffragette crying "Long live woman! Vive la femme! Viva la donna!" in many languages, "perhaps even in Sanskrit which was the language written upon a silent snowflake," touches on the philosophy of language. It suggests that language can be a powerful tool for expressing universal concepts (like the importance of women) but that there are also limits to what language can convey. There are "secret languages never to be translated into words," implying that some truths are beyond the reach of verbal expression. The reference to, "the suffragette who had lost her apostle" could also be referencing the loss of an ideal form. Platonism is the belief that everything we see is a shadow or "lesser" version of a perfect form. The loss of her apostle, who could be a student or follower, is also the loss of what the suffragette sees as a perfect version of herself. The next paragraph emphasizes the individual's choice and action in the face of an indifferent or absurd universe. The suffragette's decision to dress as a man can be seen as an assertion of her freedom and rejection of societal norms. The fantastical images of footmen and golden stairways highlight the human tendency to create meaning and beauty in a world that may ultimately be meaningless. The contrast between Cousin Hannah's practical clothing and the elaborate attire of other climbers could be interpreted as a reflection of the philosophical debate between idealism and materialism. The suffragette's focus on functionality suggests a more materialistic worldview, while the other climbers' emphasis on appearance could be seen as representing idealism. The repeated blurring of the lines between mountains and clouds, and the shifting perceptions of the moon, raise questions about the nature of reality and the limitations of human perception. The statement, "the moon had floated most amazingly distending worlds," suggests that reality is not fixed but is shaped by our individual perspectives. The image of the footman cutting a golden stairway from a frozen lake to the mountains of the moon could be an allusion to Plato's Allegory of the Cave. The stairway could represent the philosopher's ascent from the darkness of the cave (the material world) to the light of the sun (the realm of Forms). The mountains of the moon could symbolize a higher realm of knowledge and understanding. The paragraph's emphasis on nature, individual experience, and the search for spiritual meaning aligns with the themes of Transcendentalism. The suffragette's mountain climbing could be seen as a metaphor for the individual's journey to transcend the limitations of the material world and connect with a higher power or truth. The mention of "horizons unknown as the stars floated" could also be interpreted in a nihilistic way, suggesting that there are no ultimate goals or destinations and that all human endeavors are ultimately meaningless. At first it's a whimsical and imaginative description of mountain climbing, then, beneath the surface, it explores fundamental philosophical questions about freedom, reality, perception, and the meaning of life which invites the reader to think about their own philosophical reflections.

Chapter 52

  • The entire scene is dreamlike, blurring the line between reality and illusion. Mr. Spitzer finds himself entangled in wedding gowns, which transform into seas, sails, and shrouds. This reflects a questioning of what is real and what is merely a construct of the mind. The passage suggests that the material world might be less substantial than the dreams and ideas that inhabit it. This aligns with metaphysical idealism, which posits that reality is fundamentally mental or spiritual. Mr. Spitzer's inability to discern whether there is one moon or many mirrored moons, or even no moon at all, highlights the limitations of human knowledge. He is trapped within his own "hypothesis," which he cannot prove. This underscores the theme of uncertainty and the difficulty of attaining objective truth. The census-taker reference is interesting because census-takers are about collecting data to create an idea of a population, Mr. Spitzer fears the census-taker because he fears the idea of ultimate knowledge. The passage touches on existential themes of meaninglessness and the search for purpose. The old maid's rejection of marriage and her call to "divest themselves of excess luggage" can be interpreted as a rejection of societal norms and a pursuit of individual freedom. However, her obsession with wedding gowns suggests an underlying longing for connection and meaning, even in the face of her conscious rejection of it. Mr. Spitzer's role as a "dead man coming to mourn for her" reinforces the sense of existential angst and the confrontation with mortality. The imagery of carousels of brides where there is no bride, and a spiral staircase finding through clouds where constellations sleep, underscores the absurdity of existence. Mr. Spitzer is caught in a world of contradictions and paradoxes, where meaning is elusive and reason fails to provide answers. The reference to a world of mirrored moons and the difficulty in knowing which is real is reminiscent of Plato's Allegory of the Cave. The world we perceive may be a mere reflection of a higher, more authentic reality. The wedding gowns themselves are rich in symbolism. They represent not only marriage but also societal expectations, lost dreams, and the weight of the past. The fact that Mr. Spitzer is "trapped" by them suggests that he is burdened by these forces, unable to break free from the constraints of tradition and memory. Mr. Spitzer likens himself to Charon's ferry boat. Charon is a figure in Greek mythology who carries souls of the newly deceased across the river Styx that divided the world of the living from the world of the dead.

  • “Lost Atlantis” is a mythical island civilization described by Plato in his dialogues as a powerful, technologically advanced society that was destroyed by earthquakes and sank beneath the sea. While the story is a well-known myth and has inspired theories about real locations and even modern technological advancements, most historians and scientists believe Atlantis was a philosophical allegory created by Plato to serve as a cautionary tale against hubris.

  • “A swan when it is dying has the right to sing” - The swan song myth is an ancient belief that swans, typically silent throughout their lives, sing a beautiful song just before they die. The term is now used metaphorically to describe a final, often brilliant, performance, work, or gesture before retirement or death. Chapter 71

  • What’s Wrong with Democracy? Critiques of democracy existed across a wide range of historical periods and came from diverse intellectual and social movements. These arguments addressed everything from the flaws of ancient Greek democracy to the systemic exclusions within American democracy before the Civil Rights era. Philosophers like Plato warned that in a democracy, the masses, driven by ignorance and emotion, could become susceptible to self-serving demagogues. This "rule by the mob" could lead to rash, unjust decisions, such as the execution of Socrates in ancient Athens. Later, thinkers like Thomas Hobbes argued that democracy leads to chaos and civil war, advocating for a strong, singular sovereign to prevent societal collapse. Some classical and 19th-century thinkers, including Plato, Thomas Carlyle, and John Ruskin, argued that society should be led by a knowledgeable elite rather than the common people. This perspective asserted that only a select few possessed the wisdom and virtue necessary to govern effectively, and that the majority of people were ill-equipped for the complexities of statecraft. As democracy expanded during the Industrial Revolution, critics like Carlyle, John Ruskin, and William Lecky argued that it produced anarchy and chaos within modern industrial society. They believed that democratic capitalism prioritized profit and individual self-interest over social stability and the public good. Commentators like Matthew Arnold expressed concern that democracy led to a flattening of culture and a decline in intellectual standards. He feared that the "average" tastes of the masses would displace high culture and that society would suffer from a lack of moral and intellectual leadership. Before the passage of the Civil Rights Act in 1964, critics pointed out the hypocrisy of American democracy, which systematically excluded many of its own citizens. This was particularly evident in the Jim Crow South, where discriminatory laws like poll taxes and literacy tests disenfranchised millions of African Americans and poor whites. The history of American democracy has been marked by periods of deep instability and division. Scholars point to the 1890s and the aftermath of the Compromise of 1877, which ended Reconstruction, as periods of "backsliding" where racial conflict produced significant damage to democratic ideals. This history was built on the foundation of racial exclusion, as some historians note that democratic norms only took hold among white people after racial equality was removed from the agenda. Especially during the Gilded Age (1865–1915), the rise of powerful, and often corrupt, political machines led many Americans to question the health of their democracy. Critiques focused on the manipulation of the voting process, the influence of money in politics, and the resulting feeling that elections were no longer fair or truly representative of the people. The failure of some interwar and post-WWII democratic governments, such as France's Fourth Republic, was often cited as a failure of democracy itself. Frequent changes in government and a fractured party system led many to conclude that democracy was inherently unstable and inefficient. The Civil Rights Act marks a significant turning point in the realization of American democratic ideals.
