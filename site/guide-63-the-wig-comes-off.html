Chapter 63: The Wig Comes Off

← Chapter 62 · Guide Home · Chapter 64 →

Research notes: Chapter 63

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Decay and Ruin

Symbols: Hair, Wig, and Baldness · Shadows

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Milton · Revelation and the Apocalypse · Shakespeare · The History of Opium

She is back.

After the long passage through Mr. Spitzer's interior world (the street musicians, the silent violinist, the frog's burial, the water music will) we return to Vera, and to Miss MacIntosh. And in this chapter, something happens that the novel has been building toward since its opening pages: Miss MacIntosh takes off her wig.

This is not a small moment. Hold on to it.

What actually happens

Vera confronts Miss MacIntosh. The confrontation is charged with everything that has accumulated between them across Vera's lifetime: the dependence, the gratitude, the resentment, the love, the fear of loss. Vera demands honesty. She calls their relationship a "hollow pretense" and pushes for something real, something true, beneath the surface of their arrangement.

Miss MacIntosh, in response, removes her wig. She is completely bald: alopecia universalis, a condition in which the body grows no hair anywhere. The red hair that Vera has always associated with her, the hair like "the sunset burning with bees," was never hers. It was a wig. And now it is off, and Miss MacIntosh is speaking more openly than she ever has: about her past, her loneliness, her body, her approaching death.

She also, toward the end of the chapter, foreshadows that she will disappear.

The wig and what it means

Miss MacIntosh has been, from the novel's first pages, Vera's figure of absolute reality. She was the anti-hallucination: the plain, practical, broken-nosed, red-haired woman who believed in facts, schedules, and the cold honest surface of things, in a household drowning in her mother's opium dreams.

And now it turns out that the red hair (the most vivid, most distinctive feature of her plainness) was itself a fiction.

Young is not undoing Miss MacIntosh. She is completing her. The woman who stood for reality against illusion, it turns out, has been wearing an illusion on her head every day of Vera's life. This doesn't make her a hypocrite. It makes her human. She needed protection too. She built her own small shelter of pretense against a world that laughed at bald women, that had no place for someone who didn't fit the expected shape of femininity. Her wig was her mackintosh; it kept the rain out.

When she removes it in front of Vera, she is doing what she has always done: insisting on truth, even when the truth is humiliating. The wig comes off because Vera demanded honesty. Miss MacIntosh gives it.

What Miss MacIntosh reveals

She speaks of sleeping in doorways. Of being broke: financially and otherwise. Of saving on electric lights because she couldn't afford them and because she hated what they stood for: Benjamin Franklin, who had "tampered with God’s nest egg and most private creation," flooding the world with artificial brightness that disturbed natural darkness, natural privacy, natural sleep.

She speaks of her missing breast (a mastectomy, a loss she has carried under her clothes through every chapter she has appeared in) and of her baldness, and of the crooked way her wig sat, and of the laughter she endured.

She says: "I could have slipped unnoticed through the crowds" if she had been a man. "No one would have laughed."

She calls love "a system of mutual deception." She says “people are never quite what they seem.” She says nobody knows anybody.

And then, at the end, she tells Vera to be of good cheer. She says they are pilgrims, and there is a long day ahead, and one must “live each day as if it were the last,” and knit body and soul together even as they unravel faster than you can knit.

Her argument with Franklin, Edison, and Constantine

Miss MacIntosh's hatred of electric lights is not mere crankiness; it is a coherent worldview. She objects to Benjamin Franklin and Thomas Edison for the same reason she objects to Emperor Constantine: all three men imposed an artificial order on the natural world, disrupting the rhythms of darkness and light, sleep and waking, that she believes are fundamental to honest living. Constantine changed the calendar. Franklin discovered electricity. Edison commercialized it. The result, in Miss MacIntosh's view, is a world perpetually overlit, overexposed, stripped of the natural darkness that "dreamless sleep" requires.

She is not simply nostalgic. She is making a philosophical argument: that authenticity requires darkness, that privacy requires shadow, that the soul needs to be allowed to go dark sometimes, and that those who fill every hour with artificial light are doing something spiritually as well as practically disruptive.

You may or may not agree. But for Miss MacIntosh, her preference for candles over electric lights is as principled as anything else she believes.

Vera's world within

Against Miss MacIntosh's catalogue of practical knowledge (monsoons and mackerel skies, the Bering Strait, nautical navigation, Australian shipping, household management, the Stock Exchange) Vera offers only her "world within." She cannot care about the Brooklyn Bridge or harbor buoys or cup racing. Her ears roar with distant reverberations. She is trembling at the threshold of life, not yet through the door.

This has been Vera's condition since page one of the novel. Miss MacIntosh has been trying, all her life, to drag her across that threshold. Into the practical. Into the immediate. Into what is "near and present." And Vera keeps falling back into the interior, into the dream, into her mother's opium-lit world where everything is beautiful and nothing is required.

The confrontation in Chapter 63 is, in part, about this: Vera wanting something from Miss MacIntosh that Miss MacIntosh cannot give, a bridge between the world within and the world without, and Miss MacIntosh insisting that the only bridge is the one Vera builds herself, day by day, by paying attention to monsoons and tides and the names of clouds.

"Bare and ruined choirs"

Miss MacIntosh's prophetic speech in this chapter reaches toward apocalypse: the sun and moon in the same sky, the earth still wrapped in darkness, birds gone silent, flowers perishing in the seed, tides that will not return what they take. She is quoting, consciously or not, from Milton's tradition of Christian elegy, evoking desolation and the loss of divine order.

And then she says: "There will be only, my dear, the dream."

In Miss MacIntosh's mouth, this is not consolation. It is a warning. If you retreat entirely into the dream (if you abandon the work of paying attention to the real, immediate, practical world) you will be left with nothing but illusion when everything else is stripped away.

She is warning Vera about her mother's fate. She is warning Vera about herself.

The foreshadowing

She will disappear. She says so. "I shall surely disappear as surely as the sun sets." This is the chapter that places her death within reach, not yet, but coming. You can feel it in the way she speaks: more openly than she ever has, as if she knows she has little time left for pretense, even the pretense of her wig.

The umbrella is still in her hand. She will keep the rain out until she cannot.

What to hold onto

Miss MacIntosh, her wig off, her bald head bare in the fog, telling Vera to be of good cheer. She who never dreamed, surrounded by a world that dreams. She who stood for reality, revealed to have worn a fiction on her head. She who told Vera to knit body and soul together, unraveling faster than she can knit.

She is not diminished by this. She is, finally, entirely herself.
