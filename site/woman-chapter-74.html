Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 74

← Chapter 73 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 75 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Esther Longtree · The Chicago Detective

A trial Esther dreads shapes this chapter, and the failure of the record to hold her is what would convict her. She fears arrest for something "she could not prove," summoned before an imagined jury of unsympathetic women, accused of everything from infanticide to never having conceived at all, and the impossibility of producing her children is the interrupted birth turned into legal jeopardy, a woman unable to show the court the losses her body carried. The rumors that swarm her, the worst of them that she might have no children, are the policing of a womanhood measured only by delivery, and the female jury that would judge her is the internalized order the book keeps setting women inside.

The Chicago detective is the surveillance that hunts her, and he finds her lying "like a corpse," misreading her body as victim or suspect, and then he violates her, the rape carried in imagery of exposure and helplessness, the bare hips and the torn coat. The violation is the intrusion of patriarchal violence into a woman's most private ground, and the detective who "had not had anything to do with her condition" yet disturbs her peace is male authority claiming a body it did not make, the same claim the law makes on every woman's estate and illness in this book.

What Esther fears is not the men but the "little child's hand" laid across her being, the internalized weight of a motherhood forced on her, and her silence during the detective's intrusion is dissociation and survival at once. The detective as a "Christopher Columbus in the world of the dead" is the male investigator exploring a woman's trauma he cannot comprehend, and Esther's sense that conception is a secret to both mother and father, that a bee might have been its agent, is the book reclaiming the mystery of the female body from the men who would file and forecast it.

She wears "wedding rings upon her nipples but none upon her fingers," the disjunction between the marriage the world demands and the body that carries life outside it, and her perpetual pregnancy and vanished cycles are the interrupted birth made a permanent condition. The chapter ends on the question "Who was innocent? Who was guilty?", and the refusal to answer is the diffusion of agency the book keeps insisting on, a violence with no single hand behind it, a woman who will not be made the sole author of a loss the whole world helped commit.
