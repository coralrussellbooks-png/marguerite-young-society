Pillar: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here · Across every book

Act III: The Flat Horizon

Stop looking up at the sky for a savior. The sacred lives entirely within the grit, fog, and mud of the present moment.

For most of Western history, the holy lived upstairs. Heaven sat above the clouds, truth sat behind appearances, and the real life began after this one ended. Ordinary days were a waiting room. Radical Immanence closes the upstairs. It does not abolish the sacred. It relocates it, completely, into the kitchen, the bus station, the hospital corridor, and the cold coffee on the counter.

The Framework

Traditional transcendence works by altitude. The sacred is higher, elsewhere, and later, and the world below borrows its meaning from above. Marguerite Young's immanent transcendence keeps the word transcendence but changes its direction. The sacred still exceeds the ordinary, yet it exceeds it from inside, the way a flood exceeds a riverbank without leaving the valley. In Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, there is no heaven waiting at the end of Vera Cartwheel's journey. There is only more journey, and the journey is where every luminous moment happens.

The philosopher who drew this map most rigorously was Gilles Deleuze. His last published text, the short essay "Immanence: A Life" (1995), appears in English in the collection Pure Immanence (2001). In it he describes a field of absolute immanence that "does not depend on an object or belong to a subject." A life, he writes, "contains only virtuals," and is made of virtualities, events, and singularities.

Tracing the Map: Deleuze's Plane and Young's Sacred

Deleuze, writing with Félix Guattari in What Is Philosophy? (1991), called this field the plane of immanence. It is not a thing inside the world or a heaven above it. It is the flat ground on which all things, thoughts, and events take place. Here, point by point, is how that plane maps onto Young's immanent transcendence and onto the pillars of this course.

The most moving point of contact sits in the middle of Deleuze's essay. He retells a scene from Charles Dickens's Our Mutual Friend. A scoundrel named Riderhood lies near death, and the people who despise him suddenly gather around him with tender care, fighting for the spark of life still flickering in him. As he recovers, he becomes his mean self again, and their tenderness fades. For Deleuze, that deathbed moment shows "a life" in its pure form, impersonal and singular, worthy of attention apart from any judgment of the person. This is the ethic of Young's witness (Pillar 4). Her novel attends to addicts, liars, and the half-mad with exactly that deathbed tenderness, and never lets it fade.

The mapping also has a real seam, and honesty requires naming it. Deleuze distrusted the language of the sacred. He would likely hear "immanent transcendence" as a contradiction, a door left open for heaven to return. Young, by contrast, never gave up the vocabulary of angels, grace, and the holy. Radical Immanence resolves the tension this way: transcendence is measured by intensity, not altitude. The sacred is not higher. It is deeper, denser, and more fully here. Deleuze's Spinozan joy and Young's luminous mud are two names for the same flat, bright ground.

One more difference points forward to the next lesson. Deleuze's plane is impersonal. Young's is spoken by a single, trembling human voice. The final pillar, the crossing point, is where those two meet.

A Short Manifesto for the Ordinary

  1. Nothing better is coming to redeem this moment. This moment is already the site of redemption.

  2. Washing a dish is not a gap between meaningful events. It is a meaningful event.

  3. The holy does not descend. It rises from below, through attention.

  4. No place is too plain, no task too small, no person too broken to carry the sacred.

  5. Enchantment is not a mood. It is a practice, repeated daily, like sweeping.

The Carmelite lay brother known as Brother Lawrence taught this in a seventeenth-century Paris kitchen, finding God among the pots and pans. The Zen tradition teaches it too: before enlightenment, chop wood and carry water; after enlightenment, chop wood and carry water.

The Art Companion

Johannes Vermeer's The Milkmaid (around 1658) shows a kitchen maid pouring milk from a jug into a bowl. Nothing else happens. Yet the light on the bread, the bowl, and her sleeves makes the moment feel as serious as any altarpiece.

Wim Wenders's film Perfect Days (2023) follows Hirayama, a man who cleans public toilets in Tokyo. He tends his plants, reads paperbacks, and photographs trees at lunch. The film finds a quiet holiness in his routine without ever saying so aloud.

Gerard Manley Hopkins opened his 1877 sonnet with the line "The world is charged with the grandeur of God." Read through Radical Immanence, the key word is charged. The grandeur is not beyond the world. It is inside it, like electricity in a wire.

Reflection Questions

  • Where have I been waiting for meaning to arrive from somewhere else, or later?

  • Which daily task have I treated as dead time between the moments that count?

  • When have I felt something holy in something plain?

  • What would change if nothing better were coming, and this life were already enough to love?

The Exercise: The Liturgy of One Chore

  1. Choose one ordinary daily task: washing dishes, folding laundry, the walk to the bus, feeding a pet.

  2. For seven days, do that task with full attention. No phone, no podcast. Notice three physical details each time, such as the temperature of the water, the weight of a towel, the sound of keys.

  3. After each session, write one sentence about anything that appeared: a memory, a feeling of gratitude, a flash of beauty, or simply boredom honestly noticed.

  4. On the eighth day, write a five-line personal manifesto for the ordinary, modeled on the one above.
