Paradox: What the Dialectic Leaves Behind

← Dialectics and the Refusal of Synthesis · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · The Characters →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer

If the dialectic is the system Young invokes and refuses, paradox is the state she leaves it in. A dialectic that is denied its synthesis does not disappear; it settles into paradox, two opposed things held together without resolution, each true, neither cancelling the other. The novel is saturated with paradoxes for exactly this reason. Paradox is not one device among many in the book. It is the residue of every system the novel has put under load and watched fail to resolve, and it becomes the fundamental structural and thematic condition of the work, blurring the lines between opposed concepts and unsettling the ordinary understanding of reality, identity, time, and existence.

A reader might reach for the Zen koan as a comparison, the paradoxical statement that stops rational thought and forces a different kind of seeing, and the comparison is useful as a reader's tool. The novel itself contains no Buddhism; the koan is an analogy we might bring to the book, not a source Young worked from. What the two share is the refusal of the tidy answer. Where they part is that the koan aims at an awakening beyond the paradox, and Young's paradoxes aim at nothing beyond themselves. They are where the reader is asked to stay.

The principal paradoxes are these.

Reality and Illusion. This is the central one. The novel questions what is real and what is not, and repeatedly suggests that illusion is more potent and more meaningful than the objective fact it is supposed to be measured against. Catherine's opium visions are dismissed as unreal and are also the richest, most densely inhabited world in the book. The paradox is not resolved in either direction. The novel does not finally rule the visions false, nor does it endorse them as true. It holds the real and the illusory in a suspension it never lifts.

Life and Death. The boundary between the living and the dead is porous and often, in the novel's own phrasing, not to be drawn at all: "no great distinction should be made between the living and the dead." Death is not an endpoint but a state that coexists with life and extends it. The dead visit, advise, and are loved; Mr. Spitzer lives inside a dead brother's memory. The paradox is that death does not end the relations the living have with the dead, and the novel treats this not as metaphor but as the plain condition of its world.

Identity and Multiplicity. The singular, stable self is undermined at every turn. Characters are perceived as several beings at once, lose track of who they are, or absorb aspects of one another. The twin brothers are the sharpest case: the confession that the surviving brother is Peron and the dead one Joachim does not resolve their identities but dissolves the question, leaving two men who were never cleanly two. Identity here is not a fact to be uncovered but a paradox to be inhabited.

Appearance and Underlying Truth. What looks obvious hides depth, and what looks fantastical carries the deeper reality. Miss MacIntosh is the plain, sensible surface under which the novel's most guarded secret waits; the opium visions, the least credible thing in the book, hold some of its truest perceptions. The novel keeps inverting the expected relation between the apparent and the real without ever settling which is which.

Time and Space. Time in the novel is not linear or fixed but fluid, cyclical, and sometimes simultaneous, the past pressing into the present and events recurring rather than passing. Space is no more stable; rooms shift, distances expand and contract, the house relocates in the night. Neither dimension offers the reader a fixed coordinate to stand on, and the novel never restores one.

Order and Chaos. Characters seek and impose order and are perpetually overwhelmed by the chaos it was meant to contain. Miss MacIntosh's facts and schedules, Mr. Spitzer's measured paces, are efforts at control that the novel's disordered world dissolves. The order is real and the failure is certain, and the novel presents both at once.

Tangible and Intangible. The material world is repeatedly made to seem elusive, less solid than the non-material world of thought, dream, and spirit. Objects lose their fixity; the felt weight of a memory or a phantom exceeds the weight of the physical thing. The novel does not choose the immaterial over the material so much as refuse the assumption that the tangible is the more real of the two.

Love and Absence. Love in the novel is sought most powerfully where it cannot be had, and directed toward the dead or the unreal. The novel states the paradox in its own voice: a love "which never was, yet was more powerful than any love which should be realized in this mortal" world. Catherine loves a dead man precisely because the dead cannot disappoint; Mr. Spitzer's devotion is never declared and never returned. Love is realized here as absence, and the absence is not a lack of love but its intensest form.

These paradoxes are not stylistic ornament. They are what the novel is left holding once it has invoked the systems that promise to resolve contradictions and shown that they cannot. They create a disorienting but richly textured world in which the interior life is constantly projected onto and tangled with the exterior one, so that the reading experience is itself a passage through ambiguity. The novel's language, spiraling and repetitive, enacts the same refusal at the level of the sentence: a prose that will not resolve into simple definition or linear progression because the reality it describes does not. Literary Devices, a later section, will provide examples of the same suspension performed inside single sentences.
