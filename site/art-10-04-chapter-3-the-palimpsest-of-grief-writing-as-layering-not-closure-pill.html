I. The Philosophy: The Myth of the Clean Resolution

Writing culture loves closure. Resolve the arc, land the ending, revise it clean, publish, done. Workshop culture teaches the satisfying payoff, and wellness culture sells the same story about grief: write it out, process it, get closure, come back cheerful and productive. Writing gets sold as the purest engine for that cure, the page as a place to work your pain into a shape and be free of it. Radical Immanence calls this a comforting lie. Grief does not clear out. It accumulates. Each loss does not replace the last one. It lies down on top of it, layer over layer, until you are writing on a page thick with everything you have lost. You never finish grieving. You learn to carry it.

Writers have their own word for this, and it is honest. A palimpsest is a page that was written on, scraped, and written on again, where the old text bleeds back up through the new. The earlier words do not leave. They wait under the surface and surface again. That is not a defect in the page. That is how writing actually holds time. The strike-through you can still read, the draft that shows under the final line, the sentence you cut that keeps shaping the ones around it.

This changes what an honest manuscript can be. Traditional publishing prizes the clean copy, where every false start, every cut character, every drafting struggle is ground away under a smooth final surface. But hiding your struggle is a kind of dishonesty. The world does not resolve. People do not arrive at a settled end. Grief does not finish. So an unfinished, scratched, openly reworked text is not a failed piece of work. The page that lets its ghost lines show, its strike-throughs, its abandoned drafts, its sentences that trail off, is more honest than any sealed manuscript that pretends nothing ever broke.

II. Deep Journal Prompts

The Archive of the Unfinished. Go through your old files, notebooks, or the folder of things you abandoned. Pull out a piece you gave up because you could not figure out how to end it or resolve the arc. Read it for ten minutes. Write a love letter to its incompleteness. Why is this stalled, raw, unresolved thing more true to your actual life than it would be if you forced a neat ending onto it just to be done?

The Core Sample. On the left side of a page, list your real losses: not only deaths, but lost years, abandoned versions of yourself, broken ties. On the right side, trace how each loss shows up in your writing. Which one pulls you toward certain words, certain rhythms, certain subjects you circle without meaning to, even when you are not writing about it?

III. Studio Exercises

The Draft That Is Never Done

Start a serious, layered piece under one unbreakable rule: it will never be finished.

Phase 1. The build. Spend three sessions drafting something dense and careful. Layer real skill into it. Make specific passages genuinely beautiful.

Phase 2. The weekly cut. Every week, no matter how good it reads, take a black marker, scissors, or the delete key and cut or black out at least a fifth of what is there. Cut the line you loved. Break the paragraph that flowed. Let the cutting be as honest as the writing.

Phase 3. The layered return. Write new lines directly into the gaps and the blacked-out places. Let the surviving fragments and the broken syntax of the old drafts show through the new text. Let the wound stay legible under the repair.

The studio rule. Print or save a dated copy each week so you keep the record, but never let the file settle into a final, published state. It is a process, not a monument.

IV. Cross-Medium Interference

The Cut-Word Object

Keep a printed page of the piece beside your desk. Every week, right after you cut into the draft, take scissors and physically cut a fifth of the words out of the printed page. Do not rearrange them to make sense. Paste the survivors down with the gaps left raw and open. Over weeks the page becomes an object, a map of every cut you made, the words refusing to stay whole. Pin it where you write, as proof that the cutting and the making are the same practice.
