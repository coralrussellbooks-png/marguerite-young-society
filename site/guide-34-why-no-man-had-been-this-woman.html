Chapter 34: Why No Man Had Been This Woman

← Chapter 33 · Guide Home · Chapter 35 →

Research notes: Chapter 34

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Homer Capehorn · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Chandelier · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: The Search for Meaning

Symbols: Birds

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Suicide in History and Literature · The History of Opium · Women's Suffrage

Chapter 34 is the last of the Cousin Hannah chapters and, in some ways, the most intimate. After the grand sweep of her adventures and the epitaphs and the battle to rescue Catherine, Young brings us inside Catherine’s private experience of Hannah: what it was like to be in the room with her, what Catherine saw in her that she could not name, what Hannah’s presence revealed about both of them.

This is also the chapter where Young, writing in 1965, brings the novel as close as it will come to naming directly what has been circling since Cousin Hannah first appeared. She does not name it. She moves around it with extraordinary precision. Read with that precision in mind.

What actually happens in Chapter 34

The chapter continues the meditation on Catherine’s marriage: the wedding in white that was also in black, the dog who performed the ceremony in church Latin, the St. Bernard who gave her the Viaticum. Then it opens into a long, sustained portrait of what Cousin Hannah was like as a physical presence in Catherine’s bedroom: the iron keys jangling at her belt, the metallic sparks she gave off “at the sound of a cock crowing,” the silver scales she seemed to wear, her quick wit, her macabre retorts, her contempt for mortal men, her boundless indifference to Mr. Spitzer.

Young gives us Catherine’s most revealing admission about Hannah: Catherine “had felt both very flirtatious and very guilty, trying to resist this man who was no man.” She had imagined, when Hannah came, that she was in the presence of the love she had always wanted, not Peron’s reckless passion, not Joachim’s faithful devotion, but something that surpassed both, that seemed to dilate the heavens by its grandeur.

The chapter ends with Cousin Hannah’s final verdict on Mr. Spitzer: that he would cruelly disappoint Catherine “at the last, crucial moment, abandoning her to the furies,” even as Peron had done when he “apparently committed suicide, taking a coward’s way out—leaving Joachim to accept that vicarious responsibility which had always been the slow brother’s sense of peculiar guilt and” inestimable loss.

Why no man had been this woman

Near the chapter’s center, Catherine asks the question that gives this chapter its title: “why no man had been this woman, why no woman had been this man, why poor old Cousin Hannah, an unmarried spinster,” “was always like a traveller between two worlds.”

Young does not answer this question directly, but she surrounds it with everything needed to understand it. Hannah had seemed “to contain two people, herself and another, a secret man, even should he be only a puppet, a mere pygmy,” “or a butterfly sailing at midnight.” She had seemed voluminous enough to contain this double presence. She “had always acted as if she were about to meet” “her enemy, a timorous man, though perhaps he should be that man whom no one else should see.”

Catherine’s experience of Hannah was of someone who was perpetually between: between man and woman, between presence and absence, between the physical world of mountains and swords and the imaginary world of the bedroom. She had seemed as strange as Mr. Chandelier, “as strange as the golden cock crowing in the twilight of” Catherine’s opium dreams. She had been a threat to weakness and a dazzlement. And Catherine, “having no object for her love, had viewed her as the object of her lost romance, wondering why” this was not a man.

Young is writing about something the novel cannot name directly, and she does it by surrounding the unnamed thing with precise observation. The iron keys at Hannah’s belt, giving off dissonant music. The metallic sparks “at the sound of a cock crowing.” The silver scales she seemed to wear, shining in her own light. The quick wit, the macabre retort, the boundless indifference to Joachim. The deathbed confession that the greatest battles were domestic ones. These are the details of a particular interior life rendered from the outside, by a woman who loved Hannah and did not know how to say it.

Catherine’s most private knowledge

Young gives Catherine a moment of self-awareness that is one of the novel’s most quietly devastating: “my mother had felt both very flirtatious and very guilty, trying to resist this man who was no man.” And then: as “my mother had not wished, naturally, to show her secret agreement with her.”

Catherine knew. She had always known, at some level, that her agreement with Cousin Hannah (with her argument against men, with her contempt for mortal love, with her proposals to escape) went deeper than she ever showed. She performed flirtatiousness and dreamy inattention when Hannah came, precisely because Hannah’s presence threatened the carefully maintained architecture of Catherine’s illusions. Hannah was real in a way that the phantom bridegroom was not. And that reality was more dangerous than any dream.

Catherine’s response was to become more romantic, more nostalgic, more flirtatious exactly when Hannah appeared. As a defense. Young names this with characteristic precision: “my mother, in spite of her inclinations,” had not wished to show her secret agreement. The inclinations were there. The secret agreement was there. The performance of disagreement was the only protection available.

The cock crowing

Young places a strange, vivid image near the chapter’s center: “at the sound of a cock crowing, she had strutted, dancing a weird dance, giving off showers of metallic sparks, stretching her long neck as her cheeks twitched.” Because in certain Moroccan courtyards “she had visited, she had seen that the cock is” a “great golden sultan, lord of the morning, lord of all he surveys, that even the hens wear black veils like the secret women in purdah.”

The cock crowing is the sound of male dominion announcing itself. It is the sound that enrages Hannah more than anything else in the world. And her response is to become the cock: to dance its weird dance, to give off its metallic sparks, to stretch her neck and twitch her cheeks. She does not counter the male assertion. She embodies it, transforms it, claims it. This is what Hannah does with every force she encounters: she becomes it rather than opposing it, and in becoming it, changes what it means.

Hannah’s final verdict on the brothers

The chapter ends with Cousin Hannah’s assessment of both Spitzer brothers, delivered through Catherine’s memory: Joachim would cruelly disappoint her “at the last, crucial moment, abandoning her to the furies,” even as Peron had done when he “committed suicide, taking a coward’s way out—leaving Joachim to accept” the vicarious responsibility that “had always been the slow brother’s sense of peculiar guilt and” inestimable loss.

This is Hannah’s harshest verdict, and Young makes it her last word on the brothers. Peron was a coward who left. Joachim accepted the coward’s guilt as his own, because that is what slow brothers do. Neither chose Catherine. Neither gave her what she needed. And Joachim, Hannah predicted, would not be there at the end.

Whether Hannah is right is the question the novel will keep open. But she is right that the responsibility passed from one brother to the other, that the guilt for Peron’s death was taken on by the one who survived, and that this transference has defined Joachim’s life ever since.

What to watch for as you keep reading

The Cousin Hannah sequence, chapters 30 through 34, is now complete. Young has given us the full portrait: the great adventurer, the suffragette, the person who contained two selves, the one who failed to rescue Catherine, who died in her bed of her life, who left Mr. Spitzer to compose her unfinished epitaph. She will surface again in the novel’s memory, but her portrait is complete.

Catherine’s desire for “a teacher of arithmetic who would teach her to add” and subtract and divide, and then plane and solid geometry, so she might know who the geometer was) is one of the novel’s most moving passages of longing. She wants to understand the structure of reality. She wants to know whether the great illusionist who designed these “concentric circles and eccentric circles and” wandering orbs is sane or insane. She will never know. The geometer does not show his face. But the yearning for that knowledge, the desire for “one real person in the world,” is the same desire that drives Vera’s search for Miss MacIntosh. The novel turns on it.

The Irish housemaid who “polished a mirror all night long” after the golden cock crowed and thought it was morning, and eventually went stark staring mad: is a brief, perfect figure for what happens to ordinary people in Catherine’s household when the extraordinary impinges too long on the ordinary. Not the servants who adapted, who went along. The one who could not tell night from day and tried to make the mirror clear and lost her mind doing it.
