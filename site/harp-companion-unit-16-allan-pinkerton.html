Unit 16, pp. 393-432: Allan Pinkerton

← Unit 15 · Harp Song for a Radical: A History Companion · Unit 17 →

Background for this unit: The History in Harp Song for a Radical

Young gives a full portrait of Allan Pinkerton, the Glasgow Chartist who became America's most famous detective and the scourge of organized labor. His career runs from counterfeiters on a Fox River island to Lincoln's secret passage through Baltimore, the Reno gang, and the botched raid on Jesse James's mother. By the end, Debs's magazine is calling his agents carrion crows.

Major entries

Allan Pinkerton's Chartist youth (pp. 399-406)

  • Who he was: Born in the Gorbals of Glasgow on August 25, 1819, the son of a police sergeant. He became a cooper, a barrel maker, and joined the Chartists, the workers' movement demanding the vote for all men, a secret ballot, and pay for members of Parliament.

  • The Newport Rising: On November 4, 1839, several thousand Welsh Chartists led by John Frost marched on Newport to free the imprisoned Chartist Henry Vincent. Soldiers fired from the windows of the Westgate Hotel, killing about twenty-two (p. 404). Frost was transported to Australia.

  • Where the record differs: Young places Pinkerton in Frost's march with "his cooper's hammer on his back" (p. 404). There is no good evidence he was in Wales. He belonged to the militant "physical force" Chartists of Glasgow, and his arrest warrant there in 1842 is why he fled.

  • George Julian Harney (pp. 404-405): A fiery Chartist orator and friend of Marx and Engels, who first published the Communist Manifesto in English, in his paper, in 1850.

  • Further reading: James Mackay, Allan Pinkerton: The First Private Eye (1996).

From Dundee to Chicago (pp. 407-413)

  • What happened: Pinkerton married Joan Carfrae and sailed for America in 1842. They were shipwrecked off Nova Scotia and robbed before reaching Chicago. He set up a cooperage in Dundee, Illinois, on the Fox River. In 1847 he tracked a gang of counterfeiters to an island in the river later called Bogus Island (p. 410), which launched his career.

  • The agency: He became a deputy sheriff and Chicago's first police detective in 1849. In 1850 he founded what became the Pinkerton National Detective Agency, working mainly for railroads, among them the Illinois Central, the Michigan Southern, and the Rock Island (p. 411). Its logo was an open eye with the motto "We Never Sleep," which Young turns into "The Eye That Never Sleeps."

  • The abolitionist: His Chicago house was a station on the Underground Railroad. In March 1859 he helped John Brown move eleven freed slaves toward Canada (pp. 411-412), and he was expelled from his Baptist church at Dundee for his views and his drinking (p. 412).

  • The Baltimore Plot: In February 1861 Pinkerton reported a plot to kill Lincoln in Baltimore, centered on a barber named Cipriano Ferrandini (Young's "Fernandina," p. 412). Lincoln passed through the city secretly at night. During the war Pinkerton ran intelligence for General George McClellan and famously overestimated Confederate strength.

  • Further reading: Frank Morn, "The Eye That Never Sleeps": A History of the Pinkerton National Detective Agency (1982).

The Reno gang (pp. 414-419)

  • What happened: On October 6, 1866, John and Simeon Reno and Franklin Sparks robbed an Ohio and Mississippi train leaving Seymour, Indiana (p. 415). It is usually counted as the first peacetime train robbery in the United States. The gang's later robberies ranged as far as Council Bluffs, Iowa (p. 416).

  • The lynchings: Pinkerton's men captured most of the gang, but vigilantes did the rest. In July 1868 a mob hanged three members near Seymour. On the night of December 11, 1868, masked men stormed the jail at New Albany, Indiana, and hanged Frank, William, and Simeon Reno and Charlie Anderson (p. 417).

  • Why it matters: The Renos made train robbery a national fear and made the Pinkertons the railroads' private police, the role they later played against strikers.

  • Further reading: Frank Morn's history of the agency, cited above, covers the Reno pursuit.

The raid on the Samuel farm (pp. 420-427)

  • What happened: After Jesse and Frank James and the Younger brothers killed several Pinkerton operatives in 1874, agents surrounded the Missouri farm of the Jameses' mother, Zerelda Samuel, in Clay County, on the night of January 25-26, 1875. They threw a device into the house that exploded. Her right arm was mangled and had to be amputated, and her eight-year-old son Archie Samuel was killed (pp. 425-426).

  • Why it matters: The raid turned public sympathy toward the James brothers and nearly won them an amnesty from the Missouri legislature (p. 427). It fixed the Pinkertons' reputation for violence.

  • Further reading: T. J. Stiles, Jesse James: Last Rebel of the Civil War (2002).

Pinkerton's decline and legacy (pp. 419-432)

  • What happened: A stroke in 1869 partly paralyzed him. Young describes his cure at a mineral spring on the Pine River at St. Louis, Michigan (p. 419), a town later known for manufacturing DDT. The Chicago fire of October 1871 destroyed his office and records (p. 420). His sons William and Robert took over the business, and he retired to his estate, the Larches, at Onarga, Illinois (p. 428). He died in 1884.

  • Against labor: Under his sons the agency supplied armed guards and spies to break strikes. At Homestead, Pennsylvania, on July 6, 1892, about three hundred Pinkerton guards arriving by barge fought striking steelworkers, and several men on each side were killed (pp. 396, 404). The public outcry led Congress in 1893 to bar the federal government from hiring the agency.

  • Debs's view: The Locomotive Firemen's Magazine wrote that Pinkertons "come as carrion crows come to a carcass" (p. 431).

  • Further reading: Paul Krause, The Battle for Homestead, 1880-1892 (1992).

Shorter entries

  • Terence Powderly (p. 394): A machinist from Pennsylvania who led the Knights of Labor as grand master workman from 1879 to 1893 and was mayor of Scranton. The Knights kept their name secret until 1881, reached perhaps 700,000 members in 1886, and then declined quickly.

  • Charles O. Morton (p. 393): The grave robber who stole the body of Benjamin Harrison's father in 1878, helped by his wife, dressed as a man.

  • The 1892 election (p. 396): Harrison lost to Cleveland. Many blamed the Homestead violence for turning labor votes against the Republicans.

  • John Whistler (pp. 407-408): An Irish soldier captured with General John Burgoyne's army at Saratoga in 1777. He switched to the American side and built the first Fort Dearborn at Chicago in 1803. He was the grandfather of the painter James McNeill Whistler (Unit 20).

  • Mrs. O'Leary's cow (p. 420): The legend that her cow kicked over a lantern and started the Chicago fire was invented by a reporter, Michael Ahern, who admitted it in 1893.

  • The American Miners' Association (p. 430): Founded near St. Louis in January 1861, the first national union of coal miners.

  • Pinkerton's books (pp. 426-432): Ghostwritten volumes under his name included The Molly Maguires and the Detectives (1877) and Strikers, Communists, Tramps and Detectives (1878).

  • Morgan's Raiders (p. 419): John Hunt Morgan's Confederate cavalry, which raided Indiana and Ohio in July 1863. Young says the Renos rode with them. They are better known as bounty-jumpers who enlisted and deserted repeatedly.

Allusions

  • King Log (p. 397): Aesop's fable of the frogs who asked for a king and got a log, for a do-nothing ruler.

  • Romeo and Juliet, Macbeth (p. 400): Shakespeare, for Chicago's feuds.

  • Robert Burns, "Address to the Unco Guid" (p. 409): Burns's poem against the self-righteous, a favorite of both Scots and Debs.

  • Robin Hood (p. 421): The outlaw hero the James brothers were made into.

  • Salus populi (pp. 416-417): "The welfare of the people," the vigilantes' justification.
