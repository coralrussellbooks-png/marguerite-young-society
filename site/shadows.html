Chapter 49 · Shadows and Reflections

  • Shadows emphasize duality, absence, and the elusive nature of identity.
  • Reflections question reality versus illusion, with Mr. Spitzer sometimes chasing shadows or reflections rather than reality.

Chapter 64 · Fog and Shadows

  • Reflect confusion, concealment, the unknown, and the liminal space between reality and dreams.

Chapter 68 · Shadows and Darkness

  • Represent the unknown, death, and the persistent presence of the past.

Chapter 72 · The “little shadow boxer”

  • Represents the unborn child, hope, and the tension of life not fully realized.

Chapter 75 · The Little Shadow Boxer

  • A mysterious child figure that embodies Esther’s ambiguous feelings about her unborn child and her fractured conscience.
  • Esther Longtree is a complex, symbolic character whose life and experiences can be analyzed in the context of the abortion rights movement in the US.
  • Esther Longtree is portrayed as a woman burdened by an "everlasting pregnancy," haunted by multiple stillborn children, miscarriages, and the symbolic weight of unborn or lost children. She is at once maternal and mysteriously barren, pregnant yet never fully delivering living offspring that the world can recognize or accept. Her pregnancies are often described as "clouded," "mysterious," or "stillborn," and she experiences isolation, societal judgment, and legal threats because of her reproductive history.
  • Esther faces accusations, lawsuits, and societal condemnation for the loss or absence of children, implicitly tied to notions of infanticide or abortion. She dreads being brought to court, exposed to public scrutiny, and misjudged by a jury that cannot understand her unique suffering and mystery.
  • Esther’s narrative is filled with ambiguity since she does not always know the fathers of her children, nor can she fully explain the fate of her pregnancies. This ambiguity echoes the clandestine and complex nature of abortion and reproductive control for women of her era, especially those marginalized by society.
  • Despite her "mulish" stubbornness and physical strength, Esther is powerless against the forces governing her body and fate. She is caught between societal expectations, personal despair, and the biological realities of her reproductive health. Her identity as a mother is fraught with loss, secrecy, and yearning.
  • The stillborn children and the "little shadow boxer" under her heart symbolize both the tangible and intangible losses women endure: lost potential lives, societal invisibility, and the emotional and spiritual costs of reproductive control or failure.
  • Esther’s fear of legal entanglement, the threat of arrest, and the impossibility of proving her innocence reflect the harsh realities women faced when dealing with unwanted pregnancies or abortions before legal reform. Her case is described as unprecedented, with no legal precedent to protect her.

Chapter 77 · Shadows and Darkness

  • Recur throughout as metaphors for hidden truths, guilt, and fear.
  • Esther’s shadow and the detective’s shadow symbolize identity and the unknown.

Chapter 78 · The Shadow

  • The twin Rosemary often appears as a shadow figure, representing the unseen, the lost, and the internalized guilt and doubt within Esther.

Chapter 79 · The Shadow

  • Rosemary’s presence as a shadow symbolizes loss and absence.
  • Esther’s own shadow represents the duality of her existence.

Chapter 81 · Little Shadow Boxer

  • Represents the unborn child, hope, and the shadow of past losses.
