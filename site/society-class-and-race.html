Society, Class, and Race

Definition from research

Chapter 2

  • “upon which boxer would triumph, the black or the white”: In 1908 Jack Johnson became the first Black heavyweight champion of the world, and white promoters spent years searching for a “Great White Hope” to beat him. When Johnson defeated the former champion James J. Jeffries in 1910, riots broke out in cities across the country. Mr. Spitzer lays “his bet on the black man.”

Chapter 3 · Social Order and Class

  • Miss MacIntosh rejects aristocratic airs and illusions of grandeur.
  • She advocates for the dignity of simple people and honest labor.

Chapter 5 · Political and Social Decay

  • The bus-driver’s tirades against Democrats reflect frustration with political corruption and societal decline.
  • The country is depicted as a “dead country,” with broken roads, empty towns, and lost hopes.

Chapter 8 · Skepticism Toward Institutions

  • The bus-driver’s distrust of “old Doc,” politicians, and even the concept of God highlights disillusionment with traditional authorities and systems.
  • Medical science is portrayed as outmoded and helpless in the face of mortality.

Chapter 8 · Social Stigma and Reputation

  • Beatrice Fitzgerald’s character embodies themes of social judgment, stigma (being called a prostitute), and resilience.
  • The conflicting opinions of Madge and Homer reveal the complexity of social roles and the harshness of gossip

Chapter 10 · Social Roles and Expectations

  • Miss MacIntosh’s rejection of marriage and Vera’s reflections reveal societal pressures on women.
  • The notion that “none would have her” contrasts with Vera’s hope for unexpected companionship.

Chapter 11

  • “Admiral Peary”: Robert E. Peary claimed the North Pole in 1909 and was made a rear admiral in 1911. Matthew Henson, the Black explorer beside him, by his own account reached the spot first and got little credit for decades. In 1897 Peary brought six Inuit from Greenland to New York for the American Museum of Natural History, and four died within months. The museum staged a fake burial for the boy Minik while it kept his father Qisuk’s skeleton.
  • “colder than Boston society”: John Collins Bossidy’s toast (1910): “And this is good old Boston, / The home of the bean and the cod, / Where the Lowells talk only to Cabots, / And the Cabots talk only to God.”

Chapter 14

  • “The black coachman had had no wages for years”: The history of domestic work in the United States is deeply intertwined with the exploitation of Black women, men, and immigrant laborers through systemic racism, limited economic opportunities, and discriminatory labor practices. Enslaved Black women performed essential, unpaid domestic labor before emancipation, with conditions often leading to sexual abuse and the work continuing in a similar vein after abolition. For both Black and immigrant workers, this path was one of few available options, resulting in low pay, long hours, and a lack of protections, while modern policies continue to perpetuate a cycle of vulnerability.
  • “the black coachman with the white, rolling eyes”: The “white, rolling eyes” are the stock caricature of minstrel shows and early films, which made Black servants figures of fear and comedy.
  • “imaginary street Arabs”: “Street Arab” was the Victorian name for homeless children, a slur drawn from the image of Arabs as rootless wanderers.
  • “Mrs. Astor could not go, and Mrs. Vanderbilt would not be seen”: Mrs. Astor is Caroline Schermerhorn Astor, a famous American socialite who led the Four Hundred, the list of New York society in the Gilded Age. Mrs. Vanderbilt is likely Grace Graham Wilson Vanderbilt, another American socialite of the Vanderbilt family. In 1883 Alva Vanderbilt’s costume ball forced Mrs. Astor to call on the Vanderbilts, a famous defeat for old society.
  • “what had become of the color line?”: W. E. B. Du Bois: “The problem of the twentieth century is the problem of the color-line” (The Souls of Black Folk, 1903). Catherine has just turned the coachman white and the horses black.
  • “a long weekend in Newport”: Newport, Rhode Island, where Gilded Age families summered in marble “cottages.”

Chapter 14 · Race and Servitude

  • The black coachman is unpaid, nameless, and ordered to serve in death.
  • He is described by the minstrel-show stereotype of “white, rolling eyes.”
  • Catherine’s slip, “the carriage and the white coachman and the four black horses, what had become of the color line?” turns the whole arrangement inside out.

Chapter 17

  • “some Irish hooligan or drunken sailor”: “Hooligan” came into use in 1890s London, said to be from a rowdy Irish family named Houlihan. The word carries the era’s contempt for the Irish poor.

Chapter 19

  • “the cap of a sleeping car porter”: After the Civil War, the Pullman Company hired Black men, many of them formerly enslaved, as sleeping car porters. They worked long hours for low pay and tips, and passengers called every one of them “George,” after George Pullman. A. Philip Randolph organized the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters in 1925, and in 1937 it became the first Black union to win a contract with a major American company.

Chapter 22

  • “a needle-workers’ strike or a new garment union”: In the “Uprising of the 20,000” (1909), shirtwaist makers in New York, most of them young immigrant women, struck for months. The International Ladies’ Garment Workers’ Union was founded in 1900.
  • “a fall in sea island cotton”: Sea Island cotton, a long-staple variety cultivated on coastal islands of South Carolina and Georgia, was intimately linked to the institution of slavery. Its high quality and demand made it a lucrative crop, but its production relied entirely on the forced labor of enslaved Africans. The Sea Island cotton industry thrived from the late 1700s to the late 19th century, fueled by the brutal exploitation of enslaved people. The cultivation, harvesting, and processing of Sea Island cotton were entirely dependent on the labor of enslaved Africans. The Civil War ended slavery, but not the crop. Sea Island cotton was grown by freedpeople and others until the boll weevil destroyed it around 1920.
  • “How does the other half live?”: Jacob Riis, How the Other Half Lives (1890). The conservative opinion in the Wikipedia article, that immigrants chose to live in slums as a stepping stone to living in America, is ludicrous. It is very convenient that slum lords would argue we need slums. Any conservative minority has been paid to have that conservative opinion in the US, from history up to the present. Riis’s autobiography, The Making of an American (1901), shows the nuance: he may have had prejudices against certain ethnicities, as racism has always been a thing, but that doesn’t explain that slums are necessary for the working class.
  • “The mill workers were striking”: In the Lawrence textile strike of 1912, the “Bread and Roses” strike, women and children of more than twenty nationalities walked out of the mills.
  • “a baseball autographed by a dead St. Louis Cardinal”: Young mentioned the St. Louis Cardinals and the Cardinals/Dodgers rivalry and this mention seems to be through the lens of racism. During this period, after the 1942 season, Branch Rickey, who had built up the Cardinals farm system as their general manager, moved to become the Dodgers’ general manager. In 1947, after Rickey broke the color line by signing Jackie Robinson to the Dodgers, there were rumors that southerners playing for the Cardinals were planning to boycott games against the Dodgers, although the players later denied it. In general, the Cardinals were latecomers to integration. Front-office executive Bing Devine said the owner from 1947 to 1953, Fred Saigh, refused to sign black players. There was a widespread belief that St. Louis was, in many ways, a Southern city. In the mid-1950s many of its stores and restaurants refused to serve black customers. The Cardinals, with baseball’s largest radio network blanketing the Midwest and South, had cultivated white Southern fans. Their ballpark was also the last in the majors to abolish segregated seating. Because of their lack of black players, the Cardinals’ play suffered on the field tremendously in the 1950s. Meanwhile, with the success of Robinson, the Dodgers doubled down on the opportunity to sign players of color from the Negro leagues. In the subsequent years after their pennant-winning season in 1947, they would sign Don Newcombe, Roy Campanella, and Jim Gilliam from the Negro leagues, adding to an already tremendous team. The Dodgers made the World Series in 1949, 1952, 1953, 1955, 1956, and 1959 (winning championships in 1955 and 1959) and were a historic pennant race away from making it in 1951, in part because they were the first to accept African American players. The 1951 season included a 14-game winning streak for the Dodgers against the Cardinals, the longest such streak in the rivalry.
  • “shirts going in job lots, slightly damaged by fire and flood”: May refer to the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire in 1911 in New York City which killed 146 workers because a door to one stairway was locked, and the fire escape collapsed. Firefighters’ ladders couldn’t reach the upper floors. Workers, mostly all women, jumped to their deaths. The tragedy led to New York’s Factory Investigating Commission and its safety laws (1911-15).
  • “an old Jew’s pawn shop under the sign of the three golden balls”: The three golden balls were the sign of a pawnbroker. They come from the arms of the Medici and the legend of St. Nicholas’s three bags of gold. Young’s “old Jew’s pawn shop” draws on an old antisemitic stereotype. Medieval Christian law barred Jews from most trades and left them moneylending, and then despised them for it.

Chapter 23 · Historical and Cultural Reflections

  • References to historical figures such as Emperor Constantine, Benjamin Franklin, and President Garfield serve as symbols of human folly or misguided progress.
  • The chapter also evokes images from ancient times and myths (e.g., Helen of Troy, Ulysses) to illustrate timeless human experiences affected by modern changes.

Chapter 24

  • “she should send her gold-turbaned blackamoor”: “Blackamoor” figures, carved and gilded Black servants in turbans, were a fashion of European decorative art. They turned enslaved people into ornaments.

Chapter 26

  • “sleeping car porters with golden roosters on their heads”: Pullman sleeping-car porters were Black men, many of them formerly enslaved, hired by the Pullman Company from 1867 for low wages and long hours, and passengers called every one of them “George.” The Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, led by A. Philip Randolph, was founded in 1925.

Chapter 45 · Public vs. Private Life

  • Cousin Hannah was a public hero, a suffragette with noble deeds and a fiery spirit, yet her private life was marked by confusion, betrayal, and unfulfilled love.
  • Mr. Spitzer reflects on how public memory tends to forget the complexity of individuals, often reducing them to symbols or monuments.
  • Cousin Hannah had a "double life," perhaps even a "triple life," that no one truly understood.

Chapter 71 · Social and Cultural Tensions

  • The hotel reflects a microcosm of social prejudice and conflict.
  • The landlady’s rant reveals anti-Semitism, racism, and political resentment, referencing Hitler, Roosevelt, and local attitudes.

Chapter 74 · Societal Judgment and Isolation

  • Esther fears the court trial, anticipating gossip, blame, and misunderstanding from the community.
  • She is alienated and misunderstood, with the jury and townspeople serving as a metaphor for harsh societal judgment.

Chapter 77 · Racism and Social Injustice

  • The white Chicago detective is interested only in the deaths of white children, ignoring black children’s deaths, revealing racial prejudice and societal neglect.

Chapter 80 · Race and Identity

  • Walter's father was a black man, described metaphorically as "the prince of Africa" and "as black as the ace of spades."
  • Esther’s shock and societal stigma over this interracial relationship and child profoundly impact her life.
  • Walter’s blackness is a symbolic and literal source of conflict, shame, love, and fear for Esther, reflecting broader themes of racial tension and identity.
