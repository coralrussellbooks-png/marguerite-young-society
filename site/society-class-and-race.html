Chapter 2

  • “upon which boxer would triumph, the black or the white”: In 1908 Jack Johnson became the first Black heavyweight champion of the world, and white promoters spent years searching for a “Great White Hope” to beat him. When Johnson defeated the former champion James J. Jeffries in 1910, riots broke out in cities across the country. Mr. Spitzer lays “his bet on the black man.”

Chapter 3 · Social Order and Class

  • Miss MacIntosh rejects aristocratic airs and illusions of grandeur.
  • She advocates for the dignity of simple people and honest labor.

Chapter 5 · Political and Social Decay

  • The bus-driver’s tirades against Democrats reflect frustration with political corruption and societal decline.
  • The country is depicted as a “dead country,” with broken roads, empty towns, and lost hopes.

Chapter 8 · Skepticism Toward Institutions

  • The bus-driver’s distrust of “old Doc,” politicians, and even the concept of God highlights disillusionment with traditional authorities and systems.
  • Medical science is portrayed as outmoded and helpless in the face of mortality.

Chapter 8 · Social Stigma and Reputation

  • Beatrice Fitzgerald’s character embodies themes of social judgment, stigma (being called a prostitute), and resilience.
  • The conflicting opinions of Madge and Homer reveal the complexity of social roles and the harshness of gossip

Chapter 10 · Social Roles and Expectations

  • Miss MacIntosh’s rejection of marriage and Vera’s reflections reveal societal pressures on women.
  • The notion that “none would have her” contrasts with Vera’s hope for unexpected companionship.

Chapter 11

  • “Admiral Peary”: Robert E. Peary claimed the North Pole in 1909 and was made a rear admiral in 1911. Matthew Henson, the Black explorer beside him, by his own account reached the spot first and got little credit for decades. In 1897 Peary brought six Inuit from Greenland to New York for the American Museum of Natural History, and four died within months. The museum staged a fake burial for the boy Minik while it kept his father Qisuk’s skeleton.
  • “colder than Boston society”: John Collins Bossidy’s toast (1910): “And this is good old Boston, / The home of the bean and the cod, / Where the Lowells talk only to Cabots, / And the Cabots talk only to God.”

Chapter 14

  • “The black coachman had had no wages for years”: The history of domestic work in the United States is deeply intertwined with the exploitation of Black women, men, and immigrant laborers through systemic racism, limited economic opportunities, and discriminatory labor practices. Enslaved Black women performed essential, unpaid domestic labor before emancipation, with conditions often leading to sexual abuse and the work continuing in a similar vein after abolition. For both Black and immigrant workers, this path was one of few available options, resulting in low pay, long hours, and a lack of protections, while modern policies continue to perpetuate a cycle of vulnerability.
  • “the black coachman with the white, rolling eyes”: The “white, rolling eyes” are the stock caricature of minstrel shows and early films, which made Black servants figures of fear and comedy.
  • “imaginary street Arabs”: “Street Arab” was the Victorian name for homeless children, a slur drawn from the image of Arabs as rootless wanderers.
  • “Mrs. Astor could not go, and Mrs. Vanderbilt would not be seen”: Mrs. Astor is Caroline Schermerhorn Astor, a famous American socialite who led the Four Hundred, the list of New York society in the Gilded Age. Mrs. Vanderbilt is likely Grace Graham Wilson Vanderbilt, another American socialite of the Vanderbilt family. In 1883 Alva Vanderbilt’s costume ball forced Mrs. Astor to call on the Vanderbilts, a famous defeat for old society.
  • “what had become of the color line?”: W. E. B. Du Bois: “The problem of the twentieth century is the problem of the color-line” (The Souls of Black Folk, 1903). Catherine has just turned the coachman white and the horses black.
  • “a long weekend in Newport”: Newport, Rhode Island, where Gilded Age families summered in marble “cottages.”

Chapter 14 · Race and Servitude

  • The black coachman is unpaid, nameless, and ordered to serve in death.
  • He is described by the minstrel-show stereotype of “white, rolling eyes.”
  • Catherine’s slip, “the carriage and the white coachman and the four black horses, what had become of the color line?” turns the whole arrangement inside out.

Chapter 17

  • “some Irish hooligan or drunken sailor”: “Hooligan” came into use in 1890s London, said to be from a rowdy Irish family named Houlihan. The word carries the era’s contempt for the Irish poor.

Chapter 19

  • “the cap of a sleeping car porter”: After the Civil War, the Pullman Company hired Black men, many of them formerly enslaved, as sleeping car porters. They worked long hours for low pay and tips, and passengers called every one of them “George,” after George Pullman. A. Philip Randolph organized the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters in 1925, and in 1937 it became the first Black union to win a contract with a major American company.

Chapter 22

  • “a needle-workers’ strike or a new garment union”: In the “Uprising of the 20,000” (1909), shirtwaist makers in New York, most of them young immigrant women, struck for months. The International Ladies’ Garment Workers’ Union was founded in 1900.
  • “a fall in sea island cotton”: Sea Island cotton, a long-staple variety cultivated on coastal islands of South Carolina and Georgia, was intimately linked to the institution of slavery. Its high quality and demand made it a lucrative crop, but its production relied entirely on the forced labor of enslaved Africans. The Sea Island cotton industry thrived from the late 1700s to the late 19th century, fueled by the brutal exploitation of enslaved people. The cultivation, harvesting, and processing of Sea Island cotton were entirely dependent on the labor of enslaved Africans. The Civil War ended slavery, but not the crop. Sea Island cotton was grown by freedpeople and others until the boll weevil destroyed it around 1920.
  • “How does the other half live?”: Jacob Riis, How the Other Half Lives (1890). The conservative opinion in the Wikipedia article, that immigrants chose to live in slums as a stepping stone to living in America, is ludicrous. It is very convenient that slum lords would argue we need slums. Any conservative minority has been paid to have that conservative opinion in the US, from history up to the present. Riis’s autobiography, The Making of an American (1901), shows the nuance: he may have had prejudices against certain ethnicities, as racism has always been a thing, but that doesn’t explain that slums are necessary for the working class.
  • “The mill workers were striking”: In the Lawrence textile strike of 1912, the “Bread and Roses” strike, women and children of more than twenty nationalities walked out of the mills.
  • “a baseball autographed by a dead St. Louis Cardinal”: Young mentioned the St. Louis Cardinals and the Cardinals/Dodgers rivalry and this mention seems to be through the lens of racism. During this period, after the 1942 season, Branch Rickey, who had built up the Cardinals farm system as their general manager, moved to become the Dodgers’ general manager. In 1947, after Rickey broke the color line by signing Jackie Robinson to the Dodgers, there were rumors that southerners playing for the Cardinals were planning to boycott games against the Dodgers, although the players later denied it. In general, the Cardinals were latecomers to integration. Front-office executive Bing Devine said the owner from 1947 to 1953, Fred Saigh, refused to sign black players. There was a widespread belief that St. Louis was, in many ways, a Southern city. In the mid-1950s many of its stores and restaurants refused to serve black customers. The Cardinals, with baseball’s largest radio network blanketing the Midwest and South, had cultivated white Southern fans. Their ballpark was also the last in the majors to abolish segregated seating. Because of their lack of black players, the Cardinals’ play suffered on the field tremendously in the 1950s. Meanwhile, with the success of Robinson, the Dodgers doubled down on the opportunity to sign players of color from the Negro leagues. In the subsequent years after their pennant-winning season in 1947, they would sign Don Newcombe, Roy Campanella, and Jim Gilliam from the Negro leagues, adding to an already tremendous team. The Dodgers made the World Series in 1949, 1952, 1953, 1955, 1956, and 1959 (winning championships in 1955 and 1959) and were a historic pennant race away from making it in 1951, in part because they were the first to accept African American players. The 1951 season included a 14-game winning streak for the Dodgers against the Cardinals, the longest such streak in the rivalry.
  • “shirts going in job lots, slightly damaged by fire and flood”: May refer to the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire in 1911 in New York City which killed 146 workers because a door to one stairway was locked, and the fire escape collapsed. Firefighters’ ladders couldn’t reach the upper floors. Workers, mostly all women, jumped to their deaths. The tragedy led to New York’s Factory Investigating Commission and its safety laws (1911-15).
  • “an old Jew’s pawn shop under the sign of the three golden balls”: The three golden balls were the sign of a pawnbroker. They come from the arms of the Medici and the legend of St. Nicholas’s three bags of gold. Young’s “old Jew’s pawn shop” draws on an old antisemitic stereotype. Medieval Christian law barred Jews from most trades and left them moneylending, and then despised them for it.

Chapter 23 · Historical and Cultural Reflections

  • References to historical figures such as Emperor Constantine, Benjamin Franklin, and President Garfield serve as symbols of human folly or misguided progress.
  • The chapter also evokes images from ancient times and myths (e.g., Helen of Troy, Ulysses) to illustrate timeless human experiences affected by modern changes.

Chapter 24

  • “she should send her gold-turbaned blackamoor”: “Blackamoor” figures, carved and gilded Black servants in turbans, were a fashion of European decorative art. They turned enslaved people into ornaments.

Chapter 26

  • “he had hesitantly called to leave his engraved calling card, to pay, in fact, his respects to the new owners”: Leaving an engraved calling card was the ritual of a nineteenth-century social call.
  • “sleeping car porters with golden roosters on their heads”: Pullman sleeping-car porters were Black men, many of them formerly enslaved, hired by the Pullman Company from 1867 for low wages and long hours, and passengers called every one of them “George.” The Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, led by A. Philip Randolph, was founded in 1925.

Chapter 30

  • “Moroccan pirates returning from raids upon the sleeping coast of Spain with beautiful captive girls”: The Barbary corsairs of Salé and Algiers raided the coasts of Europe and sold captives into slavery.

Chapter 37

  • “not even, as Mr. Spitzer sometimes remarked, a Chinaman’s chance”: A “Chinaman’s chance,” no chance at all, is a racist American phrase of the late nineteenth century. California’s Supreme Court had ruled in People v. Hall (1854) that Chinese people could not testify against whites, and the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 barred them from the country.

Chapter 45 · Public Versus Private Life

  • Cousin Hannah was a public hero, a suffragette with noble deeds and a fiery spirit, yet her private life was marked by confusion, betrayal, and unfulfilled love.
  • Mr. Spitzer reflects on how public memory tends to forget the complexity of individuals, often reducing them to symbols or monuments.
  • Cousin Hannah had a “double life,” perhaps even a “triple life,” that no one truly understood.

Chapter 49

  • “only an old night porter riding with her”: Pullman sleeping-car porters were almost all Black men, many of them formerly enslaved or the sons of the enslaved; passengers called every porter “George,” after George Pullman, and they worked long hours for low pay and tips until they organized the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters under A. Philip Randolph in 1925. “Black as the ace of spades” was a common racist simile; see the ace of spades in the Chapter 42 resources.
  • “had done to Indians in their lonely tepees”: The United States has never been held legally to account for genocidal acts against its Indigenous peoples, though many historians and academics describe the Mystic massacre (1637), the Trail of Tears (1838-1839), the Sand Creek Massacre (1864), and the Mendocino War (1859) as genocidal. Roxanne Dunbar-Ortiz states that U.S. history, as well as inherited Indigenous trauma, cannot be understood without dealing with the genocide the United States committed against Indigenous peoples (An Indigenous Peoples’ History of the United States, 2014). From the colonial period through the founding of the United States and into the twentieth century, it entailed torture, terror, sexual abuse, massacres, systematic military occupations, removals of Indigenous peoples from their ancestral territories, the forced removal of Native American children to military-like boarding schools, allotment, and a policy of termination. During Pontiac’s War, General Jeffery Amherst wrote to Colonel Henry Bouquet, “You will Do well to try to Innoculate the Indians by means of Blanketts, as well as to try Every other method that can serve to Extirpate this Execrable Race” (July 16, 1763), part of a broader genocidal attitude displayed toward Native Americans during the colonization of the Americas. Under the Indian Vaccination Act of 1832, Secretary of War Lewis Cass ordered that no surgeon be sent higher up the Missouri than the Mandans, leaving the upper Missouri nations unvaccinated, and when smallpox swept the northern plains in 1837 it killed thousands of Mandans, Hidatsas, Arikaras, Assiniboines, Crees, and Blackfeet (J. Diane Pearson, “Lewis Cass and the Politics of Disease: The Indian Vaccination Act of 1832,” Wicazo Sa Review 18, no. 2, 2003). The United States has to date undertaken no truth commission and built no memorial for the genocide of Indigenous people, and it does not acknowledge or compensate for the historical violence of westward expansion. American museums such as the Smithsonian Institution do not dedicate a section to the genocide; in 2013 the National Congress of American Indians passed a resolution to create a space for a National American Indian Holocaust Museum inside the Smithsonian, but it was ignored (Wikipedia). After the Family Planning Services and Population Research Act of 1970, the Indian Health Service sterilized thousands of Native American women, many without informed consent: a 1976 General Accounting Office report found 3,406 sterilizations in just four of its twelve areas from 1973 to 1976, and by some estimates a quarter or more of Native American women of childbearing age were sterilized (Jane Lawrence, “The Indian Health Service and the Sterilization of Native American Women,” American Indian Quarterly 24, no. 3, 2000). In 1977 Marie Sanchez, chief tribal judge on the Northern Cheyenne Indian Reservation, told a United Nations conference on the Indigenous peoples of the Americas in Geneva that Native American women suffered involuntary sterilization, which she equated with modern genocide.
  • “the black eunuch and the white”: In the Ottoman palace the harem was guarded by Black eunuchs under the Kızlar Ağası, their chief, and the outer palace by white eunuchs.

Chapter 50

  • “Come in, Blackamoor”: Blackamoor is an old, racist English word for a Black African. Figures of Black servants in livery and turbans, called blackamoors, were fashionable decorations in European and American houses; the opium lady greets the human-sized blackbird with it.
  • There is an actual case. Henrietta Lacks, a Black woman treated for cervical cancer at Johns Hopkins in 1951, had cells taken without her knowledge or consent; they became HeLa, the first immortal human cell line and an important source of medical data. Neither she nor her family was compensated, and the family did not learn of the cell line until the 1970s (Rebecca Skloot, The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks, 2010). Its use for medical research and for commercial purposes continues to raise concerns about privacy and patients’ rights; in 2023 her estate settled a lawsuit against a company that had sold the cells.

Chapter 51

  • “a mandarin with a long cue”: The queue was forced on Han Chinese men by the Manchu Qing rulers in 1645 on pain of death, and Western caricature later made it a racist emblem of the Chinese.

Chapter 54

  • “an Indian stockade”: During the forced removal of the Cherokee people, also known as the Trail of Tears, stockades were built to hold them captive before their westward journey to Indian Territory. These stockades were often overcrowded, lacked adequate sanitation and food, and were plagued by disease. A stockade camp at Rattlesnake Springs in Tennessee held many Cherokees awaiting relocation, and it was the site of the final Cherokee council before their departure. The Treaty of Greenville, which concluded a period of conflict between the United States and Native American tribes in the Ohio region, was signed at a large stockade in Greenville, Ohio.
  • “Indian massacre!”: “Indian massacre” The phrase eventually became commonly used to also describe mass killings of American Indians. Killings described as “massacres” often had an element of indiscriminate targeting, barbarism, or genocidal intent. According to historian Jeffrey Ostler, “Any discussion of genocide must, of course, eventually consider the so-called Indian Wars, the term commonly used for U.S. Army campaigns to subjugate Indian nations of the American West beginning in the 1860s. In an older historiography, key events in this history were narrated as battles. It is now more common for scholars to refer to these events as massacres. This is especially so of a Colorado territorial militia’s slaughter of Cheyennes at Sand Creek (1864) and the army’s slaughter of Shoshones at Bear River (1863), Blackfeet on the Marias River (1870), and Lakotas at Wounded Knee (1890). Some scholars have begun referring to these events as “genocidal massacres,” defined as the annihilation of a portion of a larger group, sometimes to provide a lesson to the larger group.” In The Wild Frontier: Atrocities during the American-Indian War from Jamestown Colony to Wounded Knee, lawyer William M. Osborn compiled a list of alleged and actual atrocities in what would eventually become the continental United States, from first contact in 1511 until 1890. His parameters for inclusion included the intentional and indiscriminate murder, torture, or mutilation of civilians, the wounded, and prisoners. His list included 7,193 people who died from atrocities perpetrated by those of European descent, and 9,156 people who died from atrocities perpetrated by Native Americans. In An American Genocide, The United States and the California Catastrophe, 1846-1873, historian Benjamin Madley recorded the numbers of killings of California Indians between 1846 and 1873. He found evidence that during this period, at least 9,400 to 16,000 California Indians were killed by non-Indians. Most of these killings occurred in what he said were more than 370 massacres (defined by him as the “intentional killing of five or more disarmed combatants or largely unarmed noncombatants, including women, children, and prisoners, whether in the context of a battle or otherwise”).
  • “Gold Dust Scouring Twins”: were fictional, racially stereotypical black children who served as mascots for Fairbanks’ Gold Dust washing powder from the 1890s until 1960. Created by the N.K. Fairbanks Company, the twins, “Goldie” and “Dusty,” were depicted doing household chores and promoting the slogan “Let the Gold Dust Twins do your Work.” They were a highly successful, albeit controversial, marketing tool, appearing on packaging, in print, and eventually on a popular radio show, becoming as famous as other racially insensitive brands.

Chapter 55

  • Shut out of most work by law and prejudice, many Chinese immigrants ran hand laundries, whose painted signs in Chinese characters Young calls mystical.
  • “the hitching posts which were little black boys with golden turbans”: “little black boys with golden turbans” The earliest versions of lawn jockeys, used as hitching posts, were created in the 1850s or early 1860s. There are several legends about the black lawn jockeys. The Jim Crow Museum of Racist Memorabilia writes that “there is very little, if any, primary source material for the claim that lawn jockeys were used as signaling devices for escaping slaves on the Underground Railroad.” It is featured in two stories: Raymond Chandler’s 1942 Philip Marlowe novel, The High Window, and a short story by Flannery O’Connor in 1955, The Artificial Nigger. The story suggests that people are not intrinsically racist: rather, racist views are handed down from generation to generation because some people stand to profit from racism. O’Connor said that it was her favorite story she had written.

Chapter 57

  • “would always be a Boston Abolitionist”: Boston abolitionists, active from the 1830s to the Civil War, included prominent figures like William Lloyd Garrison, Wendell Phillips, and Lewis Hayden, who formed societies like the New England Anti-Slavery Society and published the anti-slavery newspaper The Liberator. These activists, both Black and white, organized and rallied against slavery, often facing violent opposition from anti-abolitionist mobs in the city. They also supported the Underground Railroad, assisted fugitive slaves, and pushed for social justice within their communities, eventually contributing to the eventual end of slavery.

Chapter 65

  • “Why should the black men, the railroad porters, the shoe-shiners”: The history of returning enslaved people to Africa, known as the Back-to-Africa Movement, involved efforts by groups like the American Colonization Society (ACS) in the 19th century to send freed slaves to colonies like Liberia and Sierra Leone, aiming to create homelands for Black people in Africa. While supported by some, including President Lincoln at times, it faced strong opposition from many African Americans who saw it as racist and a way to strengthen slavery, with thousands settling in Liberia before the Civil War despite harsh conditions and high mortality. Founded in 1816 by white elites, the ACS established Liberia (settled 1822) as a colony for freed U.S. slaves, modeled partly on British efforts in Sierra Leone. Paul Cuffee was an early proponent who, in 1815, transported free Black people to Sierra Leone, envisioning trade and immigration. The British settled formerly enslaved people in Sierra Leone starting in the late 1780s, a model the ACS followed.
  • In the Bible, Ham, one of Noah’s three sons, had four sons: Cush, Mizraim, Phut, and Canaan, who became ancestors of peoples in Africa and the Middle East, particularly Ethiopia, Egypt, Libya, and Canaan (modern Israel/Palestine). The story in Genesis details how Ham saw his father Noah naked while drunk, leading Noah to curse Ham’s son, Canaan, rather than Ham himself, a narrative known as the Curse of Ham. After the Flood, Noah became drunk and lay uncovered in his tent. Ham saw his father’s nakedness and told his brothers, Shem and Japheth. Shem and Japheth, however, respectfully covered Noah without looking. Noah, upon waking, cursed Canaan (Ham’s son) to be a “servant of servants” to his brothers, a passage later misused to justify slavery. In the American South the Curse of Ham was preached as God’s own warrant for enslaving Africans, and Mr. Bonebreaker still calls Black men “the sons of Ham.”

Chapter 66

  • In 1897 Peary brought six Inuit from Greenland to New York, where four of them died, among them Qisuk; the American Museum of Natural History staged a false burial for his son, Minik, and kept Qisuk’s skeleton. Its remains were returned to Greenland in 1993.
  • “followed by this Pinkerton agent”: The Pinkerton National Detective Agency, founded in Chicago by Allan Pinkerton in 1850, hired Kate Warne in 1856, the first woman detective in America. Pinkertons were also hired by mill and mine owners to spy on and break strikes, as at Homestead in 1892.
  • “At the head of Wall Street”: From 1711 to 1762 New York’s municipal slave market stood on Wall Street. See the Wall Street broker in the Chapter 54 resources.
  • Peter Stuyvesant, the Dutch director-general of New Amsterdam, is buried there (1672). In 1654 he tried to turn away twenty-three Jewish refugees from Brazil, and the Dutch West India Company overruled him; he also held enslaved Africans.
  • “who have no social security”: The Social Security Act of 1935 created old-age pensions in the United States. Miss MacIntosh, always saving for her old age, gives the poor old fish none.

Chapter 66 · Stock Exchange

  • A place of illusionary wealth and human folly; also a site where she confronts capitalism and socialism.

Chapter 67

  • “These heathen Chinee”: “The Heathen Chinee” was the popular name of Bret Harte’s poem “Plain Language from Truthful James” (1870). Harte meant to mock the white cardsharps who cheat a Chinese player, but the phrase became an anti-Chinese slur, sung and shouted during the agitation that led to the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882.
  • “A Chinaman’s chance” meant no chance at all. Chinese workers were given the most dangerous work on the railroads and in the mines, and in People v. Hall (1854) the California Supreme Court ruled that a Chinese witness could not testify against a white man.

Chapter 69

  • “a very little black man who had no tongue but had swallowed a diamond”: At the Kimberley diamond mines in South Africa, from 1885 Black miners were locked in closed compounds, searched naked, and held and dosed with purgatives before they left, to recover any diamonds they had swallowed.
  • “Hagenbeck’s out of Berlin”: Hagenbeck’s out of Berlin may refer to The Tierpark Hagenbeck zoo in Stellingen, Hamburg, Germany. The collection began in 1863 with animals that belonged to Carl Hagenbeck Sr., a fishmonger who became an amateur animal collector. The park itself was founded by Carl Hagenbeck Jr. in 1907. It is known for being the first zoo to use open enclosures surrounded by moats, rather than barred cages, to better approximate animals’ natural environments. Among his collections, however, were also human beings whom he exhibited in “human zoos.” The private zoo is still run by the Hagenbeck family. Hagenbeck’s was based in Hamburg, not Berlin. From 1874 it exhibited Sámi, Nubians, Inuit, and other peoples in its “ethnological shows.”

Chapter 70

  • “She was descended from three signers of the Declaration of Independence”: Lineage societies such as the Daughters of the American Revolution (1890) made descent from the founders a mark of status. In 1939 the DAR barred the Black contralto Marian Anderson from singing at its Constitution Hall, and Eleanor Roosevelt resigned from it in protest.
  • “It would admit both Jews and Negroes”: Catherine’s hotel “would admit both Jews and Negroes.” Jews and African Americans could not freely stay in hotels in the US until the passage of federal and state laws that banned discrimination in public accommodations, which was enforced with the Civil Rights Act of 1964. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 was a landmark piece of legislation that explicitly prohibited racial discrimination in public places, such as hotels, and this was the first time that Jewish people were also protected from discrimination in public places. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibited discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin in public accommodations like hotels, restaurants, and theaters. In the pre-1964 era, many hotels openly discriminated against both African Americans and Jews, often barring them from the premises entirely or requiring them to stay in separate, inferior accommodations. This was particularly true in the South, where discriminatory practices were widespread, even after the Civil War. In 1877 the banker Joseph Seligman was turned away from the Grand Union Hotel at Saratoga Springs because he was a Jew, and resorts advertised themselves as “restricted.” From 1936 Black travelers carried Victor Hugo Green’s Negro Motorist Green Book to find where they could sleep.

Chapter 71

  • Prejudice against Italian immigrants ran deep: in 1891 a New Orleans mob lynched eleven Italian Americans, the largest mass lynching in American history, and in 1927 Massachusetts executed the anarchists Sacco and Vanzetti after a trial many believed unjust.
  • “Tom Swift in the Land of Giants”: Tom Swift in the Land of Giants may refer to a book in the various Tom Swift book series. All books are credited to the pseudonym Victor Appleton while the character was created by Edward Stratemeyer for his book packaging house, the Stratemeyer Syndicate. Tom Swift is the main character of six series of American juvenile science fiction and adventure novels that emphasize science, invention, and technology. The books contain heavy racism, depicting Africans as brutish, uncivilized animals, and the white protagonist as their paternal savior. The Tom Swift books have been credited with assisting the success of American science fiction and with establishing stories focusing on brilliant scientists and inventors as a basic cultural myth. The series uses many adverbs and the phrase Tom Swiftly or Tom Swifty was coined to describe a book that uses many adverbs. Tom Swift’s fictional inventions have apparently inspired several actual inventions. A number of scientists, inventors, and science fiction writers have also credited Tom Swift with inspiring them.
  • “The President’s Daughter”: There are two books by this name. One published by Nan Britton in 1927 is a memoir, detailing her affair with President Warren G. Harding. The second is Clotel; or, The President’s Daughter (1853) by William Wells Brown, about the enslaved daughters of Thomas Jefferson, the first novel published by an African American.
  • “They want an American Pope”: Vatican conspiracy theories are conspiracy theories that concern the Pope or the Roman Catholic Church. A majority of the theories allege that the Church and its representatives are secretly controlling secular society with a Satanic agenda for global domination. In the 1920s the Ku Klux Klan in Indiana enrolled perhaps a quarter of a million members, preaching against Catholics, Jews, and Black Americans and holding the state government until its leader, D. C. Stephenson, was convicted in 1925 of the rape and murder of Madge Oberholtzer.
  • “There are no Negroes in this county, for the sun will not set on a Negro’s head here”: Such places were called sundown towns: Black people had to be gone by nightfall or risk violence. Indiana had scores of them. See the Kentucky lynching in the Chapter 68 resources.
  • “We don’t want mulattoes”: Indiana forbade marriage between white and Black people from its first years until 1965; the Supreme Court struck down the last such laws in Loving v. Virginia (1967).
  • “There’s a nigger in the woodpile somewhere”: In the English language, negro, is a term historically used to denote persons considered to be of Black African heritage. The word negro means the color black in both Spanish and in Portuguese, where English took it from. The term can be construed as offensive, inoffensive, or completely neutral, largely depending on the region or country where it is used. It has various equivalents in other languages of Europe. In the English language, the word nigger typically is an ethnic slur used against black people, especially African Americans. Because it is considered extremely offensive, even if only mentioned and not used as a slur, it is often referred to by the euphemism, the N-word. It became a racist insult by the 20th century. Accordingly, it began to disappear from general popular culture. Its inclusion in classic works of literature has sparked controversy and ongoing debate. The phrase, meaning a hidden catch, dates from the 1840s and is said to come from fugitive slaves hidden in woodpiles on their way north.

Chapter 71 · Social and Cultural Tensions

  • The hotel reflects a microcosm of social prejudice and conflict.
  • The widow’s rant reveals anti-Semitism, racism, and political resentment, referencing Hitler, Roosevelt, and local attitudes.

Chapter 72

  • “thank you, George Washington”: George Washington was an aggressive surveyor and land speculator who acquired over 50,000 acres, often on indigenous lands, earning him the name “Town Destroyer” (Conotocarious) from the Iroquois. He was a lifelong, large-scale slaveholder, inheriting ten enslaved people at age eleven and later controlling over 300, while navigating brutal, disease-ridden early frontier life.

Chapter 73

  • In 1904 the St. Louis World’s Fair put Ota Benga, a Mbuti man from the Congo, on display in its anthropology exhibit, and in 1906 the Bronx Zoo caged him in its monkey house until Black ministers forced his release. He died by suicide in Lynchburg, Virginia, in 1916.
  • “there had been thirty rounds”: Glove fights once ran long: on July 4, 1910, in Reno, Jack Johnson, the first Black heavyweight champion, knocked out Jim Jeffries, the “Great White Hope,” in the fifteenth round of a fight scheduled for forty-five. Riots broke out in some two dozen places, most of them white mobs attacking Black Americans; between 11 and 26 people were killed, among them a Black man lynched in Wheeling, West Virginia, for driving an expensive car (Wikipedia). New York’s Walker Law of 1920 capped bouts at fifteen rounds.

Chapter 74 · Societal Judgment and Isolation

  • Esther fears the court trial, anticipating gossip, blame, and misunderstanding from the community.
  • She is alienated and misunderstood, with the jury and townspeople serving as a metaphor for harsh societal judgment.

Chapter 77 · Racism and Social Injustice

  • The white Chicago detective is interested only in the deaths of white children, ignoring black children’s deaths, revealing racial prejudice and societal neglect.

Chapter 80 · Race and Identity

  • Walter's father was a black man, described metaphorically as "the prince of Africa" and "as black as the ace of spades."
  • Esther’s shock and societal stigma over this interracial relationship and child profoundly impact her life.
  • Walter’s blackness is a symbolic and literal source of conflict, shame, love, and fear for Esther, reflecting broader themes of racial tension and identity.
