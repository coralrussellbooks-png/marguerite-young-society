Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 69

← Chapter 68 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 70 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Justice O’Leary

The "Fire Escape" room holds the start of this chapter, and invisibility and erasure and the body coming apart run through it. A slop jar of blonde hair combings and a lipstick-stained tobacco flake, a silver church steeple labeled "Forget me not," are the traces of women's presence discarded and half-seen, the erased kept at the threshold the way the book always keeps them. The tattooed landlord fills the space with his own loud story, his happy marriage to a former circus fat lady, his battleship tattoo and his boasts, and his loquaciousness eclipsing the silent women near him is male narrative crowding out female presence, the wife a background figure to the tale a man tells about himself.

His stories run to the grotesque, a mad butcher selling human beings as meat, women preserved as stew, and the commodified and consumed female body is the book's violence carried in a landlord's yarn. The artificial eye he finds in the hangman's pocket, set beside the black poker chip, is the body as the place the self comes apart and the deception under the surface, a false eye and a hidden token where a whole man is supposed to be. The chained buzzards he calls canaries, and Tillie, "a very young girl," knitting a baby hood, hold women's confinement and the early nurturing role side by side, the caged bird and the child already learning to mother.

Dr. Justice O'Leary argues with Sarah, a woman who crawls on the floor and haunts him and will not answer, and his insistence that she is "dead at the age of one," a "dirty baby" with "no tooth in her head," is the interrupted birth returned in the doctor who tended imaginary births, a dead child still present and still refused. Sarah's silence, her refusal to speak or be recognized, is invisibility and erasure worked into a haunting, the woman present and denied, and the boundary between life and death stands open around her exactly as it stands open around Catherine and the coachman, a person the man cannot make either alive or gone.

O'Leary's litany of diseases, his sneer at "Lady Gonorrhea," his fixation on Sarah's beauty and lineage and the "stink of the dead" on her, are the stigmatizing of the female body the book keeps exposing, a woman reduced to shame and contagion and denied the standing to answer. His plan to have "house management" evict her is the institutional machinery for making a woman disappear, and the chapter leaves Sarah crawling and silent and unremoved, the dead child who will not stay dead, kept at the threshold where the book keeps everyone it cannot let the world erase.
