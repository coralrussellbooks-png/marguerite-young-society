Content note: The Esther Longtree chapters (72 to 81) deal with stillbirth and pregnancy loss, rape, infanticide, racist violence including lynching, and cruelty to children.

Summary

Esther's parents never consummated their marriage. For seven years a large, dark woman in men's clothes came to their house at night with a tiny silver spoon, until Esther and Rosemary were conceived. An uncle, a boxer, told her the story, laughing. On her seventh birthday, Esther's mother took her to visit the dying, blind go-between, and Esther saw in her the father she never had. Ever since, every day has been her birthday.

Key passages

From the research

  • Esther is described as "illusive," a natural woman born of barren parents who never consummated their marriage. Her conception was not ordinary but happened "across distance," involving a third person, an "old concubine" or "other woman," who carried a silver spoon used to impregnate her mother. This section emphasizes the unnaturalness and mystery surrounding her birth, portraying Esther as a being born out of doubt and separateness rather than love or union of flesh and soul. Her parents' marriage was arid, with no conjugal love, and Esther’s existence itself is framed as a "terrible joke" on nature.
  • The "other woman," a large, dark, and somewhat menacing figure, acted as a go-between for Esther's parents who could not physically unite. She carried the silver spoon, symbolizing the method of conception, and her presence is shadowy and powerful. This woman’s cold, almost androgynous characteristics and her role in the conception add a mysterious and somewhat sinister tone to Esther’s origins. The chapter notes that this took place over many years, with the "other woman" visiting regularly, setting an alarm clock, and performing ritualistic acts to ensure the conception would succeed.
  • Esther’s mother’s feelings of fear, pain, and hopelessness during the prolonged and unnatural conception are described in detail. The husband's passive role contrasts sharply with the other woman’s active involvement. The mother’s suffering is emphasized, including her physical pain and emotional torment. Esther is born kicking and afraid, a product of a "long honeymoon" that never became a true marriage night. The birth is marked by difficulty and instruments, symbolizing the unnaturalness and struggle of Esther’s arrival into the world.
  • Esther’s mother repeatedly reminds her of the stillborn child (Rosemary), whom Esther is supposed to have "killed" in the womb with her "big feet." Esther’s innocence is corrupted early by the family’s dark secrets and the community’s scorn. The chapter highlights the emotional neglect and psychological burden Esther endures, including being called "Little Jackass" by her father and teased by her uncle. The tragedy of Rosemary's stillbirth and Esther’s survival is a source of lifelong guilt and alienation.
  • On Esther’s seventh birthday, she and her mother make a difficult journey to visit the other woman, who had helped bring Esther into the world. This visit is symbolic, portraying the harsh reality behind the fantasy of a "golden palace." The other woman is found dying, blind, and monstrous, yet still the "father" figure in Esther’s story. This episode underscores the unnatural triangle and the burden of Esther's existence, as well as her mother’s complex feelings of gratitude, regret, and unresolved pain.
  • The chapter closes with a poignant meditation on Esther’s gradual death, a metaphor for her lifelong suffering and alienation. Despite her physical presence and vitality, Esther is portrayed as a "happy dead," burdened by the weight of her origins and the absence of natural love. Her connection to nature, to other selves, and to dead creatures highlights her liminal state between life and death, presence and absence.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Esther Longtree

  • The protagonist, described as a large, muscular, and natural woman, yet burdened by the unnaturalness of her conception and birth. She embodies both physical strength and emotional vulnerability.
  • She is “illusive,” caught between life and eternity, haunted by the shadow of her stillborn twin Rosemary.
  • Known as “Little Jackass,” a nickname signifying her struggle with identity and acceptance.
  • She experiences loneliness and rejection but has a deep maternal heart.
  • Esther’s internal conflict is central, revealing layers of shame, defiance, and longing.
  • Her mother’s contradictory feelings of desire, regret, and hope illustrate the emotional complexity of motherhood under duress.

Esther’s Mother

  • A small, delicate, and fragile woman who yearned for a child but was unable to conceive naturally.
  • She endured the long, torturous process of conception mediated by the “other woman.”
  • Often blames Esther for the barren landscape of their lives and the shadow of Rosemary.

Esther’s Father (The Policeman)

  • A large, distant man who never consummated the marriage and remained emotionally aloof.
  • He is passive, absorbed in his own interests, such as reading old baseball scores.
  • His relationship with Esther’s mother is marked by distance and lack of physical union.

The Other Woman (The Go-Between / The Concubine)

  • The "other woman" who assists with Esther Longtree's conception is a complex and symbolically rich character, embodying themes of alienation, unnatural intervention, and the blurring of gender and human roles.
  • The other woman is portrayed as a large, dark, and somewhat grotesque figure, physically contrasting with Esther's small, delicate mother. She is described as immense and misshapen, with a dimpled chin like Esther's, yet her body smells of manure and old tulip bulbs rotted by rain, evoking an earthy, almost animalistic presence. She wears men's shoes, a man's hat, and coat, casting a shadow "like a man's shadow" on the wall, blurring gender lines and emphasizing her liminal status between man and woman.
  • She functions as a go-between or intermediary in the conception process: carrying a tiny silver spoon, the instrument used to impregnate Esther's mother. This spoon symbolizes the unnatural and clinical intervention in what would typically be a natural act of conception. The process is slow, torturous, and prolonged, spanning seven years of visits, during which the other woman comes at night, sometimes knocking or whistling to announce her arrival. She is methodical, patient, and calm, yet possesses a "slow anger." Her presence is both cruel and necessary, a facilitator of life born out of barrenness and absence of conjugal love.
  • Unlike the natural union of man and woman, the conception of Esther occurs "across a great, arid distance," facilitated by the other woman’s spoon. This reflects the barrenness and emotional coldness of Esther's parents' marriage, emphasizing estrangement and the failure of traditional love and procreation.
  • Her wearing of men's clothes and casting a male shadow suggests a hybrid or ambiguous gender identity, symbolizing the breakdown of conventional roles. She holds power over the creation of life, yet is an outsider, "a foreigner, someone who spoke with a strange accent," possibly exiled or carrying a "crime," which adds a sense of otherness and ostracism.
  • She is associated with both life-giving and ominous death imagery, such as carrying a funeral wreath and walking among frozen boughs and songless birds. Her hands are described as large, flat, and dark, capable of warming Esther’s mother but also marking the child with the shadow of her intervention. This duality underscores the paradox of creation: birth emerging from pain, alienation, and sacrifice.
  • While she is described as cruel "as a child," she is also "more wonderful, more beautiful than either of her parents," suggesting a mysterious, inscrutable depth beneath her harsh exterior. She slaps and pinches Esther's mother to arouse life, indicating a brutal but necessary form of care.
  • The other woman operates in secrecy, moving "back and forth" between Esther’s parents, whose marriage is unconsummated. She is the physical manifestation of the "shadow" that lies over Esther’s family history and conception, highlighting themes of hidden trauma, secrets, and the unnatural origins of Esther’s being.
  • The other woman’s intervention results in Esther’s conception, an event described as unnatural and painful, but also miraculous in producing a child with a "big heart" and intense life force despite the barrenness of her parents' union. Esther's identity is deeply shaped by this conception, as she is "the child of the unnatural mating," simultaneously mulish and vulnerable, burdened by loneliness and the weight of her origins.
  • Her presence and role also deepen the novel’s exploration of family dysfunction, the failures of traditional marriage, and the ways in which life asserts itself against bleakness and despair. The other woman, though harsh and alien, becomes a symbol of the persistence of life and the complexities of identity born from fractured beginnings.
  • The other woman is a potent symbol of the unnatural, intermediary force enabling Esther’s conception amid a loveless and barren marriage. She embodies ambiguity, cruelty, and hidden power, bringing to life themes of alienation, gender fluidity, and the paradoxical relationship between life and death. Her character is crucial to understanding Esther’s origins and the larger thematic fabric of the novel.

Rosemary

  • Esther’s stillborn twin, whose spirit and memory haunt Esther.
  • Symbolizes what might have been and the loss that shadows Esther’s life.

Esther’s Uncle

  • The uncle is portrayed as a complex and somewhat enigmatic figure within the narrative of Esther Longtree's conception and family history. He is described as a pugilist who died of cancer and left his estate to an old spiritualist medium, a tall, thin-cheeked man in contact with astral forces, star bodies, and influences. This detail suggests that the uncle was connected to both the physical and metaphysical realms, bridging the earthly and the astral, which resonates with the novel's themes of spiritual and existential ambiguity.
  • His role in Esther’s life is marked by a peculiar mix of cruelty and candor. He mockingly informs Esther that her conception was "unnatural" and that she was called "Little Jackass" by her father because of this unnaturalness. His laughter until his face turned purple while telling her this indicates a harsh and mocking attitude, perhaps reflecting the family's broader discomfort or denial regarding the circumstances of Esther's birth. The nickname "Little Jackass" itself is symbolic: implying stubbornness, mule-like qualities, and an outsider status that Esther internalizes as part of her identity.
  • The uncle’s death and bequest to a spiritualist medium also suggest his fascination or involvement with the occult or unseen forces, emphasizing the novel's recurring motifs of mystery, otherworldliness, and the blurred lines between life and death. His character embodies a link to the past and the mystical, adding layers to Esther's tangled family legacy and the shadowy "third person" involved in her conception.
  • The uncle functions as a symbolic figure who exposes the unnatural and painful origins of Esther's life through his teasing and his association with spiritualism, reflecting the novel’s themes of alienation, the supernatural, and the complex intersections of family, identity, and fate.

  • Esther Longtree symbolizes a deeply troubled and complex history of barrenness, failed unions, and unnatural origins that can be read as a metaphor for certain aspects of American history, especially related to its cultural and social foundations. Her life story is marked by the unnatural conception and barren marriage of her parents, who never consummated their union in the usual way. Instead, Esther was conceived through an intermediary, a dark, fierce go-between woman who carried a silver spoon between her parents over many years of painful, slow attempts at conception. This act was carried out without love, intimacy, or unity between the parents, symbolizing a broken or forced foundation rather than a natural, loving creation. Esther herself is born kicking, burdened by the shadow of a stillborn twin, Rosemary, representing loss, failure, and the cost of this unnatural origin. Symbolically, Esther’s story reflects a history of disconnection, loneliness, and suffering born from a society or culture that lacks genuine union and affection at its core. The barren marriage, the long and cruel struggle to bring life into being without real love, and the presence of the mysterious third figure (the go-between woman) who acts almost like a dark force intervening between the natural order, all suggest themes of alienation and forced creation. Esther’s immense physical presence, her repeated pregnancies of stillborn children, and her ultimate loneliness and marginalization reflect the burdens of a history that is both prolific and deeply wounded. Esther Longtree can be seen as symbolizing the nation’s own troubled birth and development: a land born of separateness and conflict, of broken promises and harsh realities underneath the surface of prosperity and growth. Her story recalls the suffering of those who have been marginalized, neglected, or burdened by the consequences of historical injustices and the failures of foundational relationships, whether racial, cultural, or familial. Esther Longtree embodies the tragic history of a people born out of a fractured union, carrying within them the scars of that origin and the weight of unfulfilled hopes and lost futures. Her life’s anguish and resilience mirror the broader American narrative of creation through suffering, alienation, and the struggle for identity and belonging.
  • The intermediary woman who facilitated Esther Longtree’s conception is a complex and symbolically potent figure whose role resonates deeply when analyzed alongside the historical context of Black Americans’ civil rights struggles in the United States. She is a foreigner, someone with a strange accent, and carries an aura of mystery and hardship, possibly linked to an old crime or exile. This woman embodies a liminal, almost mythic figure: part man, part woman, part shadow crossing boundaries between genders, generations, and roles. She is the "go-between" in a domestic and biological triangle that is itself fractured and barren, symbolizing the failure of natural union and the intrusion of a painful, unnatural process. The intermediary woman’s outsider status (foreign, dark, and stigmatized) can be read as emblematic of the marginalized Black American figure during the civil rights era. Like the go-between, Black Americans were often seen as "other," standing apart from the mainstream, burdened by historical injustices and systemic exclusion. Yet, this figure is also crucial to the creation of life, a bearer of possibility and transformation despite the cruelty and hardship she embodies. Similarly, Black Americans’ struggle was against a backdrop of oppression but also one of profound resilience and the potential to create new social realities. The intermediary woman’s presence between Esther’s parents and her shadow-like figure evoke the heavy legacy of slavery, segregation, and historical trauma that Black Americans carried. Esther herself, born of this unnatural and painful process, embodies the complex identity born from a fractured past.   • Modern Spiritualism is widely considered to have begun in March 1848 in Hydesville, New York, following the claims of the Fox sisters, Margaret and Kate, who reported hearing mysterious rappings that they believed were spirit messages. The movement originated in an area of upstate New York known as the "Burned-over District," which was already a center for religious fervor and movements like Millerism and Mormonism during the Second Great Awakening. The movement quickly spread, with early adherents seeking direct communication with the spirits of the deceased. By the 1850s, it had spread across the country and internationally. In the 1850s, the movement attracted the attention of scientists who began investigating the reported supernatural occurrences. The credibility of the movement was challenged, especially in 1888 when the Fox sisters confessed to faking the original rappings. However, many believers continued their faith, and the movement adapted. Influential figures like Emma Hardinge Britten, a medium and lecturer, used the platform of Spiritualism to advocate for social causes like women's rights and temperance. Spiritualism experienced a major revival after World War I, as many people sought to connect with loved ones who had died in the war. The movement continued to develop organizations and gather followers, with communities like Cassadaga, Florida, becoming known as centers for spiritualist activities. Throughout the mid-20th century, Spiritualism persisted, adapting to new generations and social changes, and leaving a lasting impact on American religious and cultural history.
  • Spiritualism is the metaphysical school of thought opposing physicalism and also is the category of all spiritual beliefs from ancient to modern. They believe that spirit mediums are gifted to carry on such communication, but that anyone may become a medium through study and practice. They believe that spirits are capable of growth and perfection, progressing through higher spheres or planes, and that the afterlife is not a static state, but one in which spirits evolve. The two beliefs, that contact with spirits is possible, and that spirits may dwell on a higher plane, lead to a third belief, that spirits can provide knowledge about moral and ethical issues, as well as about God and the afterlife. Many believers speak of spirit guides or specific spirits, often contacted, and relied upon for worldly and spiritual guidance. According to Spiritualists, anyone may receive spirit messages, but formal communication sessions are held by mediums, who claim thereby to receive information about the afterlife. Spiritualism developed and reached its peak growth in membership from the 1840s to the 1920s, especially in English-speaking countries. By 1897, spiritualism was said to have more than eight million followers in the United States and Europe, mostly drawn from the middle and upper classes.

She is conscious of her pain and her role as a bearer of suffering, and this claim may be a manifestation of her grappling with her identity and the meaning of her life amidst her hardships.
  • According to Christian theology, Mary was not impregnated by a literal bird like a dove, but rather conceived Jesus through the power of the Holy Spirit. While artistic depictions sometimes show a dove (a symbol of the Holy Spirit) during the Annunciation, this is a symbolic representation.
  • “an old coat made of old fruit bags” likely refers to garments created from repurposed cotton mesh citrus bags (orange, grapefruit, or lemon sacks), or more commonly, patterned cotton flour/feed sacks used for clothing during the Great Depression and World War II, which often featured bright, fruit-themed, or floral prints.
  • Women historically pinched their cheeks and bit their lips to induce a natural, rosy flush of color, particularly during the Victorian era (19th century) and the Elizabethan era (16th century). This practice was used to enhance beauty without the stigma of wearing obvious makeup, which was often considered improper, associated with actresses, or linked to low morals.
  • “woman carrying candle, book, bell, funeral wreath” The phrase "bell, book, and candle" refers to a Latin Christian method of excommunication by anathema, imposed on a person who had committed an exceptionally grievous sin. Evidently introduced by Pope Zachary around the middle of the 8th century, the rite was once used by the Latin Church. This form of excommunication was inflicted on Robert II of France by Pope Gregory V for his marriage to Bertha of Burgundy in the year 996, because Bertha was his second cousin. He was later reconciled with the Church after negotiations with Gregory's successor Pope Silvester II.
  • Sexton beetles, commonly known as burying beetles, are large, orange-and-black insects that act as nature’s undertakers by burying small, dead animals (like mice or birds) to feed their larvae. They are among the few insects that display active parental care, with both parents preparing the carcass, removing hair, and feeding their young.
  • The idiom, to be born with a silver spoon in their mouth, means someone born into a wealthy family or someone who has a prosperous background or is of a well-to-do family environment, often with the connotation that the person does not fully realize or appreciate the value of his or her advantage, its having been inherited rather than earned. It first appeared in print in English as early as 1719, in the translation of the novel Don Quixote.
  • In vitro fertilization was not successful in humans until 1977. Prior to the development of IVF in humans, a physician and professor was doing research on reproduction in animals in 1891. In 1934, IVF was tried on rabbits. In 1948, research was expanded on IVF. In 1958, mouse embryos were grown in vitro and transferred into female mice.   • A treacling mill may refer to treacle mining which is a joke about mining of black treacle or molasses in a raw form similar to coal. The subject purports to be serious but is an attempt to test credulity. Thick black treacle makes the deception plausible. The topic has been a joke in British humor since the mid-19th century.
  • A "dark woman" who assisted with births ,may refer to the following: 1) the Biblical midwife Shiphrah, who was a Nubian woman mentioned in the Book of Exodus. Shiphrah, along with her colleague Puah, defied the Pharaoh's order to kill newborn Hebrew males. 2) Biddy Mason, who was born into slavery, was a midwife and advocate for other enslaved people in 19th-century California. After gaining her freedom, she became a successful entrepreneur and philanthropist. 3) Margaret Charles Smith was an African American midwife in Alabama who delivered over 3,000 babies during a career spanning over 30 years, often in rural areas with limited healthcare access. 4) Mary Francis Hill Coley was a midwife from Georgia who delivered over 3,000 babies during her career. Her work was featured in the 1952 documentary, All My Babies. 5) Susie Carey was a third-generation midwife featured in a photograph at the National Museum of African American History and Culture. 6) Sarah Hudson Grant was a Black Southern midwife whose work is remembered today.
  • Artificial insemination is the deliberate introduction of sperm into a female's cervix or uterine cavity for the purpose of achieving a pregnancy through in vivo fertilization by means other than sexual intercourse or in vitro fertilisation. It is a fertility treatment for humans, and is common practice in animal breeding. The first recorded case of artificial insemination was John Hunter in 1790, who helped impregnate a linen draper's wife. The first reported case of artificial insemination by a donor occurred in 1884 when William H. Pancoast, a professor in Philadelphia, took sperm from a handsome student in his class to inseminate an anesthetized woman without her knowledge. The incident was not published until twenty-five years later in a medical journal. The sperm bank was developed in Iowa starting in the 1950s in research conducted by University of Iowa medical school researchers Jerome Sherman and Raymond Bunge. In the United Kingdom, the British obstetrician Mary Barton founded one of the first fertility clinics to offer donor insemination in the 1930s. Mary Barton and her first husband were medical missionaries in pre-partition India, then still ruled by Britain. She witnessed the way in which women would be punished or even killed for being childless. At the time, it was taboo to suggest that it might be the husband, and not the wife, who was infertile - not only on the Indian subcontinent but also in the United Kingdom. Barton understood that both men and women could be infertile and in 1943 was the lead author on an article for the British Medical Journal. It was signed by many of the researchers active in the field in Great Britain at that time. With her second husband, Bertold Wiesner, Mary Barton successfully inseminated an estimated 1500 women, the majority with sperm provided from Wiesner, some 100-200 from neuroscientist Derek Richter as well as an unknown number from as yet unidentified donors. It is estimated that Wiesner is the biological father of around 600 children born following these procedures, although some believe this figure could be as high as 1000. When Barton and Wiesner published their paper on the procedure it was publicly condemned by the Pope who called it a sin, and the Archbishop of Canterbury who called for the British parliament to make human artificial insemination illegal. Although it was not criminalized it was not legalized either and therefore the status of artificial insemination was ambiguous. Consequently, the activities of Barton and Wiesner at the fertility clinic were conducted in secrecy and all inseminated women were instructed to tell nobody about it.
  • Instruments to assist with childbirth have evolved from rudimentary tools for destructive purposes in antiquity to sophisticated instruments like forceps and vacuum extractors today. Early interventions included destructive tools for delivering stillborn fetuses and instruments for procedures like symphysiotomy, a painful procedure to widen the pelvis. Modern forceps, introduced by the Chamberlen family in the 16th century, were a breakthrough for delivering living infants, and their use, along with vacuum extractors, remains a vital part of modern obstetrics.
  • Vera’s birthday and Esther Longtree’s birthday are portrayed with contrasting atmospheres and underlying thematic significance, reflecting their differing life circumstances and symbolic roles.
  • Vera’s birthday, her fourteenth, is described with a sense of ritual and expectation, though tinged with loneliness and unrealized celebration. Vera receives a formal party dress, a filmy white creation, somewhat too mature for her, chosen by Mr. Spitzer in a moment of abstraction linked to the memory of Vera’s mother. Despite the preparation, there are no guests, no grand party, and only Miss MacIntosh, her nursemaid, and Vera herself are present. The absence of guests and the muted atmosphere emphasize Vera’s isolation and the unrealized joys of childhood. Miss MacIntosh insists on simplicity and adherence to routine, asking Vera to take care of her duties, underscoring the austere and disciplined environment Vera inhabits. Vera is caught between the innocence and hope of youth and the harsh, confining realities imposed by her surroundings and caretakers. The birthday marks a threshold of maturity but also a loss of innocence, as Vera is trained for domesticity and marriage, and the celebration itself becomes a subdued, private event rather than a joyous public occasion. The birthday is also symbolic of Vera’s gradual awakening to the complexities and burdens of life. It is described as a moment on the edge of life’s "beginning of the end," a turning point where the dreams and illusions of childhood begin to confront reality. The party dress and the preparations seem almost a costume for a role Vera must play rather than an expression of genuine celebration. The atmosphere is one of quiet resignation and subdued hope, with Vera realizing she has "but one" life and must face the responsibilities and limitations of her social role.
  • Esther Longtree’s birthday, by contrast, is enveloped in sorrow, mystery, and the burdens of an "everlasting pregnancy," symbolic of a life marked by loss, unfulfilled hopes, and complex emotional struggles. Esther is the "passive policeman’s only daughter," a big, awkward woman whose life is overshadowed by repeated stillbirths and the mysterious fate of her children. Her birthday is less a celebration and more a reflection on her difficult existence, her isolation, and her maternal suffering. She is surrounded by the imagery of loss and death: stillborn children, shadows, ghosts, and spiritual burdens. Her pregnancy is portrayed as both a physical and metaphysical state, representing ongoing grief and hope intertwined. Esther’s birthday is not a moment of public recognition or joy but a private, almost secretive experience filled with anxiety and anticipation of an uncertain future. She is burdened by the expectations and mysteries of motherhood, the absence of fathers, and the threat of legal and social accusations. The narrative emphasizes her loneliness and the complexity of her emotional and physical state, weaving natural imagery (flowers, birds, snowflakes) with the harsh realities of her life.
  • Vera’s birthday is marked by a subdued, almost melancholic celebration within a structured, disciplined environment, while Esther’s birthday is enveloped in sorrow, mystery, and the physical and emotional weight of repeated loss. Vera’s day is a threshold into maturity, Esther’s a reflection of enduring struggle.
  • Vera’s birthday symbolizes the fragile transition from innocence to experience, the imposition of societal expectations, and the tension between dreams and reality. Esther’s birthday symbolizes endurance, maternal sacrifice, and the haunting presence of loss and unfulfilled potential.
  • Vera’s birthday occurs within the protective but confining care of Miss MacIntosh, reflecting themes of control, preparation for societal roles, and the looming responsibilities of adulthood. Esther’s birthday reflects marginalization, survival amid hardship, and the complexities of maternal identity in a world shaped by absence and loss.
  • Vera’s birthday carries a quiet hope and the bittersweet nature of growing up alone. Esther’s birthday carries a profound sense of isolation, struggle, and the interplay of hope and despair.
