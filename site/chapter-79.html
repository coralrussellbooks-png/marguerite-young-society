To All My Darlings

Chapter 79

On Chapter 79 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 10, 2026

← Chapter 78 · Home · Chapter 80 →

Content note: The Esther Longtree chapters (72 to 81) deal with stillbirth and pregnancy loss, rape, infanticide, racist violence including lynching, and cruelty to children. This chapter describes Esther’s conception with a silver spoon carried by a go-between, the go-between’s rape of Esther’s mother, the mother’s attempt to get rid of the pregnancy, and her cruelty to her child.

Summary

Esther’s parents, the huge passive policeman and his tiny child-bride, never consummated their marriage. For seven years a large, dark, foreign woman in a man’s hat, coat, and shoes came at night to their kitchen, carrying a tiny silver spoon between husband and wife while the policeman read old baseball scores behind the stove, until Esther and Rosemary were conceived. When the mother went alone to the go-between’s mule farm to show her the pregnancy, the go-between took her into bed and held her down, and in that minute the mother saw that it was a man; afterward she tried and failed to get rid of the child. Esther’s uncle, a pugilist, told her the story, laughing. On her seventh birthday her mother, dressed in her old bridal clothes, led her all day through briers and marshes to the old mule farm, where the go-between lay dying and blind on a bed of chicken feathers, with Esther’s own crooked nose and dimpled chin: her father. Ever since, every day has been her birthday, and she has died with every moth.

Key passages

From the research

  • Esther is described as “illusive,” a natural woman born of barren parents who never consummated their marriage. Her conception was not ordinary but happened “across distance,” involving a third person, an “old concubine” or “other woman,” who carried a silver spoon used to impregnate her mother. This section emphasizes the unnaturalness and mystery surrounding her birth, portraying Esther as a being born out of doubt and separateness rather than love or union of flesh and soul. Her parents’ marriage was arid, with no conjugal love, and Esther’s existence itself is framed as a “terrible joke” on nature.
  • The “other woman,” a large, dark, and somewhat menacing figure, acted as a go-between for Esther’s parents who could not physically unite. She carried the silver spoon, symbolizing the method of conception, and her presence is shadowy and powerful. This woman’s cold, almost androgynous characteristics and her role in the conception add a mysterious and somewhat sinister tone to Esther’s origins. The chapter notes that this took place over many years, with the “other woman” visiting regularly, setting an alarm clock, and performing ritualistic acts to ensure the conception would succeed.
  • Esther’s mother’s feelings of fear, pain, and hopelessness during the prolonged and unnatural conception are described in detail. The husband’s passive role contrasts sharply with the other woman’s active involvement. The mother’s suffering is emphasized, including her physical pain and emotional torment. Esther is born kicking and afraid, a product of a “long honeymoon” that never became a true marriage night. The birth is marked by difficulty and instruments, symbolizing the unnaturalness and struggle of Esther’s arrival into the world. When the pregnant mother went to the mule farm, the go-between took her into bed and held her “pinioned,” “stilled her screaming,” and in that minute the mother saw “that it was a man.”
  • Esther’s mother repeatedly reminds her of the stillborn child (Rosemary), whom Esther is supposed to have “killed” in the womb with her “big feet.” Esther’s innocence is corrupted early by the family’s dark secrets and the community’s scorn. The chapter highlights the emotional neglect and psychological burden Esther endures, including being called “Little Jackass” by her father and teased by her uncle. The tragedy of Rosemary’s stillbirth and Esther’s survival is a source of lifelong guilt and alienation.
  • On Esther’s seventh birthday, she and her mother make a difficult journey to visit the other woman, who had helped bring Esther into the world. This visit is symbolic, portraying the harsh reality behind the fantasy of a “golden palace.” The other woman is found dying, blind, and monstrous, yet still the “father” figure in Esther’s story. This episode underscores the unnatural triangle and the burden of Esther’s existence, as well as her mother’s complex feelings of gratitude, regret, and unresolved pain.
  • The chapter closes with a poignant meditation on Esther’s gradual death, a metaphor for her lifelong suffering and alienation. Despite her physical presence and vitality, Esther is portrayed as a “happy dead,” burdened by the weight of her origins and the absence of natural love. Her connection to nature, to other selves, and to dead creatures highlights her liminal state between life and death, presence and absence.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “The times were out of joint and she the dead.” What does Esther borrow from Hamlet?
  • “Live, oh, happy dead!” What does Esther envy in the dead?
  • “She had been brought into this arid, barren world without any love but her father’s kiss upon her mother’s brow.” What does such a beginning do to a child?

Characters

  • The go-between was “more wonderful, more beautiful than either of her parents.” Why would Esther think so?
  • The policeman sits behind the stove reading old baseball scores. “At best, what was the man’s role in life?”
  • At the mule farm the mother saw “that it was a man who had taken her away from her husband.” Who, then, is Esther’s father?

Connections

  • Esther claims “her own was the virgin birth.” How is her conception a dark mirror of the Annunciation?
  • The go-between had “the great chin, dimpled like Esther Longtree’s.” What else does Esther inherit?
  • Esther expected “a golden palace in a garden of talking birds and silver fountains” and found the old mule farm. Where else in the novel does a promised palace turn out to be a ruin?

Reflection

  • “Perhaps every parent was a stranger, Esther sometimes thought.” Is that true?
  • “From that day, her seventh birthday, she had trusted no one.” What was broken that day?
  • “Her innocency had been spoiled by men, women, children.” Can a child’s innocence be spoiled before she understands anything?

Research: Characters

Esther Longtree

  • The protagonist, described as a large, muscular, and natural woman, yet burdened by the unnaturalness of her conception and birth. She embodies both physical strength and emotional vulnerability.
  • She is “illusive,” caught between life and eternity, haunted by the shadow of her stillborn twin Rosemary.
  • Known as “Little Jackass,” a nickname signifying her struggle with identity and acceptance.
  • She experiences loneliness and rejection but has a deep maternal heart.
  • Esther’s internal conflict is central, revealing layers of shame, defiance, and longing.

Esther’s Mother

  • A small, delicate, and fragile woman who yearned for a child but was unable to conceive naturally.
  • She endured the long, torturous process of conception mediated by the “other woman.”
  • Often blames Esther for the barren landscape of their lives and the shadow of Rosemary.
  • Her contradictory feelings of desire, regret, and hope illustrate the emotional complexity of motherhood under duress.
  • At the go-between’s mule farm she was held “pinioned” and saw “that it was a man”; afterward she tried, and failed, to get rid of the child. She tied Esther by a rope (“She still bore the marks of the rope”), left her “out-of-doors when it rained,” and on Esther’s birthdays, which were also Rosemary’s, baked two cakes or none.

Esther’s Father (The Policeman)

  • A large, distant man who never consummated the marriage.
  • He “had been good and kind and had forgiven her everything imaginable, even her birth,” calling Esther his Little Jackass and saying she smelled of wild flowers, though he also pinched her black and blue.
  • He is passive, absorbed in his own interests, such as reading old baseball scores.
  • His relationship with Esther’s mother is marked by distance and lack of physical union.

The Other Woman (The Go-Between / The Concubine)

  • The “other woman” who assists with Esther Longtree’s conception is a complex and symbolically rich character, embodying themes of alienation, unnatural intervention, and the blurring of gender and human roles.
  • The other woman is portrayed as a large, dark, and somewhat grotesque figure, physically contrasting with Esther’s small, delicate mother. She is described as immense and misshapen, with a dimpled chin like Esther’s, yet her body smells of manure and old tulip bulbs rotted by rain, evoking an earthy, almost animalistic presence. She wears men’s shoes, a man’s hat, and coat, casting a shadow “like a man’s shadow” on the wall, blurring gender lines and emphasizing her liminal status between man and woman.
  • She functions as a go-between or intermediary in the conception process: carrying a tiny silver spoon, the instrument used to impregnate Esther’s mother. This spoon symbolizes the unnatural and clinical intervention in what would typically be a natural act of conception. The process is slow, torturous, and prolonged, spanning seven years of visits, during which the other woman comes at night, sometimes knocking or whistling to announce her arrival. She is methodical, patient, and calm, yet possesses a “slow anger.” Her presence is both cruel and necessary, a facilitator of life born out of barrenness and absence of conjugal love.
  • Unlike the natural union of man and woman, the conception of Esther occurs “across a great, arid distance,” facilitated by the other woman’s spoon. This reflects the barrenness and emotional coldness of Esther’s parents’ marriage, emphasizing estrangement and the failure of traditional love and procreation.
  • Her wearing of men’s clothes and casting a male shadow suggests a hybrid or ambiguous gender identity, symbolizing the breakdown of conventional roles. She holds power over the creation of life, yet is an outsider, “a foreigner, someone who spoke with a strange accent,” possibly exiled or carrying a “crime,” which adds a sense of otherness and ostracism.
  • She is associated with both life-giving and ominous death imagery, such as carrying a funeral wreath and walking among frozen boughs and songless birds. Her hands are described as large, flat, and dark, capable of warming Esther’s mother but also marking the child with the shadow of her intervention. This duality underscores the paradox of creation: birth emerging from pain, alienation, and sacrifice.
  • While she is described as cruel “as a child,” she is also “more wonderful, more beautiful than either of her parents,” suggesting a mysterious, inscrutable depth beneath her harsh exterior. She slaps and pinches Esther’s mother, and at the mule farm she holds her “pinioned” in bed and stills her screaming; in that minute the mother sees that the go-between is a man.
  • The other woman operates in secrecy, moving “back and forth” between Esther’s parents, whose marriage is unconsummated. She is the physical manifestation of the “shadow” that lies over Esther’s family history and conception, highlighting themes of hidden trauma, secrets, and the unnatural origins of Esther’s being.
  • The other woman’s intervention results in Esther’s conception, an event described as unnatural and painful, but also miraculous in producing a child with a “big heart” and intense life force despite the barrenness of her parents’ union. Esther’s identity is deeply shaped by this conception, as she is “the child of the unnatural mating,” simultaneously mulish and vulnerable, burdened by loneliness and the weight of her origins.
  • Her presence and role also deepen the novel’s exploration of family dysfunction, the failures of traditional marriage, and the ways in which life asserts itself against bleakness and despair. The other woman, though harsh and alien, becomes a symbol of the persistence of life and the complexities of identity born from fractured beginnings.
  • The other woman is a potent symbol of the unnatural, intermediary force enabling Esther’s conception amid a loveless and barren marriage. She embodies ambiguity, cruelty, and hidden power, bringing to life themes of alienation, gender fluidity, and the paradoxical relationship between life and death. Her character is crucial to understanding Esther’s origins and the larger thematic fabric of the novel.

Rosemary

  • Esther’s stillborn twin, whose spirit and memory haunt Esther.
  • Symbolizes what might have been and the loss that shadows Esther’s life.

Esther’s Uncle

  • The uncle is portrayed as a complex and somewhat enigmatic figure within the narrative of Esther Longtree’s conception and family history. He is described as a pugilist who died of cancer and left his estate to an old spiritualist medium, a tall, thin-cheeked man in contact with astral forces, star bodies, and influences. This detail suggests that the uncle was connected to both the physical and metaphysical realms, bridging the earthly and the astral, which resonates with the novel’s themes of spiritual and existential ambiguity.
  • His role in Esther’s life is marked by a peculiar mix of cruelty and candor. He mockingly informs Esther that her conception was “unnatural” and that she was called “Little Jackass” by her father because of this unnaturalness. His laughter until his face turned purple while telling her this indicates a harsh and mocking attitude, perhaps reflecting the family’s broader discomfort or denial regarding the circumstances of Esther’s birth. The nickname “Little Jackass” itself is symbolic: implying stubbornness, mule-like qualities, and an outsider status that Esther internalizes as part of her identity.
  • The uncle’s death and bequest to a spiritualist medium also suggest his fascination or involvement with the occult or unseen forces, emphasizing the novel’s recurring motifs of mystery, otherworldliness, and the blurred lines between life and death. His character embodies a link to the past and the mystical, adding layers to Esther’s tangled family legacy and the shadowy “third person” involved in her conception.
  • The uncle functions as a symbolic figure who exposes the unnatural and painful origins of Esther’s life through his teasing and his association with spiritualism, reflecting the novel’s themes of alienation, the supernatural, and the complex intersections of family, identity, and fate.

Research: Resources

  • “Esther Longtree was illusive”: Esther Longtree is a deeply tragic and complex figure, embodying themes of unnatural conception, loneliness, maternal suffering, and existential isolation. Her characterization is rich with symbolism and psychological depth, revealing a woman who is both physically imposing and emotionally vulnerable. Esther is described as a large, muscular, wind-burned woman with dark skin and crossed eyes that reflect a dazzling inner light where there is no actual light. Her body is often swollen due to pregnancy, yet she carries a profound sadness and a sense of being out of place in the world. She is mulish, stubborn, and somewhat vulgar, traits that stem from her difficult origins and emotional turmoil. Despite her toughness, her heart is described as “big and leaking,” full of regrets, expectations, and memories, highlighting her emotional complexity and capacity for deep feeling. Esther’s origins are central to her character. She was conceived in a way that defies the natural order, her parents never consummated their marriage physically, and instead, a third person, an “old concubine,” using a silver spoon, facilitated her conception across a great emotional and physical distance between her parents. This unusual conception symbolizes the emotional barrenness and alienation in her family. Her parents’ marriage is described as arid and loveless, lacking the fundamental union of flesh and soul. Esther’s conception is painful, slow, and unnatural, taking seven years and involving this mysterious third woman who serves as a go-between. The figure of the “other woman,” the old concubine who carries the silver spoon and physically intervenes in the conception, is a haunting presence in Esther’s story. This woman is physically and symbolically powerful and described as large, dark, and foreign, with masculine traits. She embodies the harsh reality and cruelty of Esther’s conception and birth. She is portrayed as both cruel and strangely beautiful in an inner, invisible way, representing an essential but unsettling force in Esther’s life and origin. Esther was born kicking and afraid, a difficult birth requiring instruments. She is the only one to survive; her twin, Rosemary, was stillborn. This loss haunts Esther, who is burdened by her mother’s guilt and neglect, and by a sense of having caused harm. She carries the memory and presence of Rosemary as a kind of shadow or ghost, which deepens her loneliness and feeling of being an unnatural offspring. Esther’s relationship with her parents is fraught with pain and misunderstanding. Her mother is distant, delicate, and childlike herself (never truly matured or fulfilled) and she often blames Esther for the darkness and barrenness of their world. Her father is large, passive, and somewhat detached, calling Esther “Little Jackass,” a nickname that reflects both affection and cruelty. Both parents are emotionally unavailable, and Esther is left to navigate the world as an awkward, oversized child of a loveless union. Esther stands as a symbol of isolation born of a union that never was, a child who bridges eternity and life but belongs fully to neither. She is “cloud-like,” “illusive,” and driven to the edge of existence, a creature shaped by absence and distance. Her physical largeness contrasts with the emotional barrenness around her, making her a living paradox and a figure of existential struggle. Despite her tragic origins and the neglect she suffers, Esther has a “maternal heart” and a deep desire for love and motherhood. Yet she repeatedly experiences loss (stillbirths, shadows, unborn children) and carries the burden of these losses within her. She is a woman perpetually pregnant with doubtfulness and sorrow, embodying both fertility and barrenness simultaneously. On her seventh birthday, Esther and her mother undertake a difficult journey to visit the “other woman,” the go-between who had helped conceive Esther. This journey is symbolic, marked by hardship, loss of clothing, and the harshness of the natural and social world. The visit reveals the death and decay underlying Esther’s origin: the go-between is dying, blind, and stained by darkness, and Esther perceives her as a kind of paternal figure despite her appearance and gender. Esther is caught between life and death, reality and shadow, natural and unnatural. Her death is “gradual,” tied to her continuous experience of loss and alienation. She tries to be good despite the hatred and misunderstanding she faces, and she is marked by a profound loneliness that aligns her with the natural world’s shadows and ghosts. Her character embodies the tragic consequences of emotional and physical disconnection within a family and society. Esther Longtree in this chapter is a richly drawn figure representing the painful intersection of unnatural conception, family dysfunction, loneliness, maternal longing, and existential grief. She is a living paradox: large yet vulnerable, natural yet unnatural, maternal yet burdened by loss. Her story is woven with symbolism and mythic resonance. See Esther Longtree.
    • Esther Longtree symbolizes a deeply troubled and complex history of barrenness, failed unions, and unnatural origins that can be read as a metaphor for certain aspects of American history, especially related to its cultural and social foundations. Her life story is marked by the unnatural conception and barren marriage of her parents, who never consummated their union in the usual way. Instead, Esther was conceived through an intermediary, a dark, fierce go-between woman who carried a silver spoon between her parents over many years of painful, slow attempts at conception. This act was carried out without love, intimacy, or unity between the parents, symbolizing a broken or forced foundation rather than a natural, loving creation. Esther herself is born kicking, burdened by the shadow of a stillborn twin, Rosemary, representing loss, failure, and the cost of this unnatural origin. Symbolically, Esther’s story reflects a history of disconnection, loneliness, and suffering born from a society or culture that lacks genuine union and affection at its core. The barren marriage, the long and cruel struggle to bring life into being without real love, and the presence of the mysterious third figure (the go-between woman) who acts almost like a dark force intervening between the natural order, all suggest themes of alienation and forced creation. Esther’s immense physical presence, her repeated pregnancies of stillborn children, and her ultimate loneliness and marginalization reflect the burdens of a history that is both prolific and deeply wounded. Esther Longtree can be seen as symbolizing the nation’s own troubled birth and development: a land born of separateness and conflict, of broken promises and harsh realities underneath the surface of prosperity and growth. Her story recalls the suffering of those who have been marginalized, neglected, or burdened by the consequences of historical injustices and the failures of foundational relationships, whether racial, cultural, or familial. Esther Longtree embodies the tragic history of a people born out of a fractured union, carrying within them the scars of that origin and the weight of unfulfilled hopes and lost futures. Her life’s anguish and resilience mirror the broader American narrative of creation through suffering, alienation, and the struggle for identity and belonging.

  • “a spoon having been used as the instrument to impregnate her mother”: Artificial insemination is the deliberate introduction of sperm into a female’s cervix or uterine cavity for the purpose of achieving a pregnancy through in vivo fertilization by means other than sexual intercourse or in vitro fertilisation. It is a fertility treatment for humans, and is common practice in animal breeding. The first recorded case of artificial insemination was John Hunter in 1790, who helped impregnate a linen draper’s wife. The first reported case of artificial insemination by a donor occurred in 1884 when William H. Pancoast, a professor in Philadelphia, took sperm from a handsome student in his class to inseminate an anesthetized woman without her knowledge. The incident was not published until twenty-five years later in a medical journal. The sperm bank was developed in Iowa starting in the 1950s in research conducted by University of Iowa medical school researchers Jerome Sherman and Raymond Bunge. In the United Kingdom, the British obstetrician Mary Barton founded one of the first fertility clinics to offer donor insemination in the 1930s. Mary Barton and her first husband were medical missionaries in pre-partition India, then still ruled by Britain. She witnessed the way in which women would be punished or even killed for being childless. At the time, it was taboo to suggest that it might be the husband, and not the wife, who was infertile, not only on the Indian subcontinent but also in the United Kingdom. Barton understood that both men and women could be infertile and in 1943 was the lead author on an article for the British Medical Journal. It was signed by many of the researchers active in the field in Great Britain at that time. With her second husband, Bertold Wiesner, Mary Barton successfully inseminated an estimated 1500 women, the majority with sperm provided from Wiesner, some 100-200 from neuroscientist Derek Richter as well as an unknown number from as yet unidentified donors. It is estimated that Wiesner is the biological father of around 600 children born following these procedures, although some believe this figure could be as high as 1000. When Barton and Wiesner published their paper on the procedure it was publicly condemned by the Pope who called it a sin, and the Archbishop of Canterbury who called for the British parliament to make human artificial insemination illegal. Although it was not criminalized it was not legalized either and therefore the status of artificial insemination was ambiguous. Consequently, the activities of Barton and Wiesner at the fertility clinic were conducted in secrecy and all inseminated women were instructed to tell nobody about it. (Wikipedia) See Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery and Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest.

  • “in the dim light of a gas jet”: Gas jets, or gasoliers, provided prominent indoor and outdoor lighting in the US from the early 19th century until being largely replaced by electric light, with remnants existing in specialized, theatrical, or rural settings. These systems utilized coal gas, manufactured gas, or, by the late 19th century, acetylene and natural gas to provide bright, steady illumination via burners, often employing mantles for increased efficiency.
  • “an old spiritualist medium”: Modern Spiritualism is widely considered to have begun in March 1848 in Hydesville, New York, following the claims of the Fox sisters, Margaret and Kate, who reported hearing mysterious rappings that they believed were spirit messages. The movement originated in an area of upstate New York known as the “Burned-over District,” which was already a center for religious fervor and movements like Millerism and Mormonism during the Second Great Awakening. The movement quickly spread, with early adherents seeking direct communication with the spirits of the deceased. By the 1850s, it had spread across the country and internationally. In the 1850s, the movement attracted the attention of scientists who began investigating the reported supernatural occurrences. The credibility of the movement was challenged, especially in 1888 when the Fox sisters confessed to faking the original rappings. However, many believers continued their faith, and the movement adapted. Influential figures like Emma Hardinge Britten, a medium and lecturer, used the platform of Spiritualism to advocate for social causes like women’s rights and temperance. Spiritualism experienced a major revival after World War I, as many people sought to connect with loved ones who had died in the war. The movement continued to develop organizations and gather followers, with communities like Cassadaga, Florida, becoming known as centers for spiritualist activities. Throughout the mid-20th century, Spiritualism persisted, adapting to new generations and social changes, and leaving a lasting impact on American religious and cultural history. See Spiritualism and the Occult and American Religious Movements.
    • Spiritualism is the metaphysical school of thought opposing physicalism and also is the category of all spiritual beliefs from ancient to modern. They believe that spirit mediums are gifted to carry on such communication, but that anyone may become a medium through study and practice. They believe that spirits are capable of growth and perfection, progressing through higher spheres or planes, and that the afterlife is not a static state, but one in which spirits evolve. The two beliefs, that contact with spirits is possible, and that spirits may dwell on a higher plane, lead to a third belief, that spirits can provide knowledge about moral and ethical issues, as well as about God and the afterlife. Many believers speak of spirit guides or specific spirits, often contacted, and relied upon for worldly and spiritual guidance. According to Spiritualists, anyone may receive spirit messages, but formal communication sessions are held by mediums, who claim thereby to receive information about the afterlife. Spiritualism developed and reached its peak growth in membership from the 1840s to the 1920s, especially in English-speaking countries. By 1897, spiritualism was said to have more than eight million followers in the United States and Europe, mostly drawn from the middle and upper classes.
  • “had promised her a pair of golden gloves”: The Golden Gloves, amateur boxing tournaments for young fighters, began in New York in 1927 and in Chicago in 1928, sponsored by the city newspapers.
  • “this old concubine walking between her parents”: Concubinage in the U.S. was primarily defined by the sexual exploitation of enslaved Black women by white owners, alongside localized systems like plaçage in Louisiana. These coerced, non-marital relationships were driven by power imbalances, racial prohibitions on marriage (anti-miscegenation laws), and the economic value of offspring. See Society, Class, and Race and Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “this large-fisted, ugly woman who had brought the life and the shadow”: The intermediary woman who facilitated Esther Longtree’s conception is a complex and symbolically potent figure whose role resonates deeply when analyzed alongside the historical context of Black Americans’ civil rights struggles in the United States. This woman is described vividly and somewhat grotesquely as a large, dark, misshapen figure with broad, earth-stained hands, wearing men’s shoes, a man’s hat, and coat. She is a foreigner, someone with a strange accent, and carries an aura of mystery and hardship, possibly linked to an old crime or exile. She is neither kind nor gentle but is methodical, fierce, and patient, walking back and forth between Esther’s parents with a silver spoon, the instrument used to impregnate Esther’s mother over the course of seven years. This woman embodies a liminal, almost mythic figure: part man, part woman, part shadow crossing boundaries between genders, generations, and roles. She is the “go-between” in a domestic and biological triangle that is itself fractured and barren, symbolizing the failure of natural union and the intrusion of a painful, unnatural process. The intermediary woman’s outsider status (foreign, dark, and stigmatized) can be read as emblematic of the marginalized Black American figure during the civil rights era. Like the go-between, Black Americans were often seen as “other,” standing apart from the mainstream, burdened by historical injustices and systemic exclusion. Yet, this figure is also crucial to the creation of life, a bearer of possibility and transformation despite the cruelty and hardship she embodies. Similarly, Black Americans’ struggle was against a backdrop of oppression but also one of profound resilience and the potential to create new social realities. Esther’s conception through an intermediary and a silver spoon, rather than through a natural marital union, parallels the forced and unnatural social conditions imposed upon Black Americans (segregation, disenfranchisement, and systemic violence) that distorted natural human relations and family structures. The intermediary’s role in bridging a distance or barrier between two barren parents mirrors the social barriers (legal, racial, and cultural) that Black Americans sought to overcome in their fight for civil rights. The intermediary woman’s presence between Esther’s parents and her shadow-like figure evoke the heavy legacy of slavery, segregation, and historical trauma that Black Americans carried. Esther herself, born of this unnatural and painful process, embodies the complex identity born from a fractured past. The intermediary’s shadow, her “darkness,” and the violence implicit in the conception process reflect the historical violence and the pain of inheritance that civil rights activism sought to confront and transcend. The intermediary woman subverts traditional gender and familial roles by acting as the agent of conception in an unconventional way. This subversion can be seen as analogous to the civil rights movement’s challenge to established power structures and norms. The movement demanded recognition and redefinition of identity, agency, and rights, much as the intermediary woman’s presence disrupts the conventional narrative of family and birth. The intermediary is simultaneously a figure of cruelty and care, oppression and agency. She is paid for her role, yet wields a strange power over the life created. This duality reflects the complex dynamics in civil rights struggles, where oppressed communities both suffered under systemic injustice and exercised profound agency in shaping their destinies. The intermediary woman in Esther Longtree’s conception is a richly layered symbol and explores themes of alienation, unnaturalness, and painful mediation that resonate powerfully with the historical and social conditions of Black Americans fighting for civil rights. She embodies the barriers to natural unity imposed by history and society, yet also the endurance and complexity of life born from hardship and estrangement. Her figure invites reflection on the intersections of race, gender, power, and identity in a fractured world. See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “a gramophone was playing”: The gramophone, invented by Emil Berliner in 1887, is an early, mechanical, analog sound reproduction device that plays flat, spirally-grooved discs (shellac records). It uses a stylus and horn to amplify sound mechanically, without electricity. As a precursor to the modern turntable, it revolutionized music by allowing mass production of recordings, distinguishing itself from cylinder-based phonographs. See Music and Composers.
  • “she was not a virgin eating green cherries”: Green, unripe cherries were a byword for a child’s bellyache; Esther means a girl too young to know better. “green cherries” have been a part of candied fruit recipes for generations.
  • “After all, her own was the virgin birth”: The Immaculate Conception is the belief that the Virgin Mary was free of original sin from the moment of her conception. Original sin is the Christian doctrine that holds that humans, through the fact of birth, inherit a tainted nature in need of regeneration and a proclivity to sinful conduct. According to Christian apocryphal and Islamic tradition, Saint Anne was the mother of Mary and the maternal grandmother of Jesus. Saint Anne appears as the mother of Mary in the late 2nd-century Gospel of James. Saint Anne and her husband, Joachim, are infertile, but God hears their prayers and Mary is conceived. The conception is claimed to occur without sexual intercourse between Saint Anne and Joachim, which fits well with the Gospel of James’ persistent emphasis on Mary’s sacred purity, but the story does not advance the idea of an immaculate conception. The Orthodox Church claims that Mary was conceived by her parents in the same way everyone is conceived. Anne (Hannah in Arabic) is recognized as a highly spiritual woman and as the mother of Mary. In the Quran she is described as childless until her old age. One day, Hannah saw a bird feeding its young while sitting in the shade of a tree, which awakened her desire to have children of her own. She prayed for a child and eventually conceived. Joachim (Imran in Arabic) died before the child was born. Expecting the child to be male, Hannah vowed to dedicate him to isolation and service in the Second Temple. However, Hannah bore a daughter instead, and named her Mary. Her words upon delivering Mary reflect her status as a great mystic, realizing that while she had wanted a son, this daughter was God’s gift to her. She said: “My Lord! Truly, I brought her forth, a female. And God is greater in knowledge of what she brought forth. And the male is not like the female” (Quran 3:36). So her Lord received her with the very best acceptance. And her bringing forth caused the very best to develop in her. Joachim was a rich and pious man, who regularly gave to the poor. At the temple, Joachim’s sacrifice was rejected, so the couple’s childlessness was interpreted as a sign of divine displeasure. Joachim consequently withdrew to the desert, where he fasted and did penance for 40 days. Angels then appeared to both Joachim and Anne to promise them a child. Joachim later returned to Jerusalem and embraced Anne at the city gate, located in the Walls of Jerusalem. An ancient belief held that a child born of an elderly mother who had given up hope of having offspring was destined for great things. Joachim is named as the patron saint of fathers, grandfathers, grandparents, married couples, cabinet makers, and linen traders. “The Holy Ghost shall come upon thee, and the power of the Highest shall overshadow thee” (Luke 1:35, KJV). See The Bible and Augustine and Christian Theology.
  • “she was Jesus Christ or anybody holy”: Esther Longtree claims she is Jesus Christ as part of her complex self-perception, intertwined with her tragic and sorrowful life marked by endless pregnancies and stillbirths. She is a character burdened by a profound sense of loss and unfulfilled motherhood, feeling perpetually pregnant with children who never fully came to life. This identification with Jesus Christ seems to symbolize her own suffering, sacrifice, and bearing of pain, much like the figure of Christ who endured suffering for humanity. Esther’s claim is connected to her experience of being “always pregnant,” carrying the weight of loss, sorrow, and the burden of unrealized hopes. Her life is marked by a sense of being different, isolated, and carrying an immense internal burden: a “pregnancy which was more than the memory provided for.” She is aware of her uniqueness and the vastness of her suffering, and by likening herself to Jesus Christ, she expresses the depth of her endurance and the sacrifice implicit in her condition. Esther’s self-identification with Christ also reflects her feeling of being misunderstood and unrecognized, much like the biblical Christ who was often rejected and not understood by those around him. She is conscious of her pain and her role as a bearer of suffering, and this claim may be a manifestation of her grappling with her identity and the meaning of her life amidst her hardships.
  • “A dove of God, she had read somewhere, impregnated Mary”: According to Christian theology, Mary was not impregnated by a literal bird like a dove, but rather conceived Jesus through the power of the Holy Spirit. While artistic depictions sometimes show a dove (a symbol of the Holy Spirit) during the Annunciation, this is a symbolic representation. At Christ’s baptism the Spirit descended “like a dove” (Matthew 3:16, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “that Christ was the youngest brother”: The Gospels make Jesus the eldest: “Is not this the carpenter, the son of Mary, the brother of James, and Joses, and of Juda, and Simon? and are not his sisters here with us?” (Mark 6:3, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “that would be a treacling mill”: A mule walking in an endless circle turned the rollers of a cane mill, pressing sorghum or sugar cane for molasses, or treacle; a merry-go-round of mules would be such a mill. See Horses and Carriages.
    • A treacling mill may refer to treacle mining which is a joke about mining of black treacle or molasses in a raw form similar to coal. The subject purports to be serious but is an attempt to test credulity. Thick black treacle makes the deception plausible. The topic has been a joke in British humor since the mid-19th century.
  • “This third person, this dark woman”: A “dark woman” who assisted with births may refer to the following: 1) the Biblical midwife Shiphrah, one of the “Hebrew midwives” of Exodus 1:15, though some readers have taken her and Puah for Egyptians. Shiphrah, along with her colleague Puah, defied the Pharaoh’s order to kill newborn Hebrew males. 2) Biddy Mason, who was born into slavery, was a midwife and advocate for other enslaved people in 19th-century California. After gaining her freedom, she became a successful entrepreneur and philanthropist. 3) Margaret Charles Smith was an African American midwife in Alabama who delivered over 3,000 babies during a career spanning over 30 years, often in rural areas with limited healthcare access. 4) Mary Francis Hill Coley was a midwife from Georgia who delivered over 3,000 babies during her career. Her work was featured in the 1952 documentary, All My Babies. 5) Susie Carey was a third-generation midwife featured in a photograph at the National Museum of African American History and Culture. 6) Sarah Hudson Grant was a Black Southern midwife whose work is remembered today. See Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery, Society, Class, and Race, Judaism, and Ancient Egypt.
  • “an old coat made of old fruit bags”: “an old coat made of old fruit bags” likely refers to garments created from repurposed cotton mesh citrus bags (orange, grapefruit, or lemon sacks), or more commonly, patterned cotton flour/feed sacks used for clothing during the Great Depression and World War II, which often featured bright, fruit-themed, or floral prints.
  • “pinching her cheeks until they were pink as rosebuds”: Women historically pinched their cheeks and bit their lips to induce a natural, rosy flush of color, particularly during the Victorian era (19th century) and the Elizabethan era (16th century). This practice was used to enhance beauty without the stigma of wearing obvious makeup, which was often considered improper, associated with actresses, or linked to low morals.
  • “in the twinkling of an eye”: See the Chapter 72 resources. “In a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trump” (1 Corinthians 15:52, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “the other woman carrying candle, book, bell, funeral wreath”: “woman carrying candle, book, bell, funeral wreath” The phrase “bell, book, and candle” refers to a Latin Christian method of excommunication by anathema, imposed on a person who had committed an exceptionally grievous sin. Evidently introduced by Pope Zachary around the middle of the 8th century, the rite was once used by the Latin Church. This form of excommunication was inflicted on Robert II of France by Pope Gregory V for his marriage to Bertha of Burgundy in the year 996, because Bertha was his second cousin. He was later reconciled with the Church after negotiations with Gregory’s successor Pope Silvester II. (Wikipedia) See Augustine and Christian Theology.
  • “of ladders to heaven”: “And he dreamed, and behold a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven” (Genesis 28:12, KJV). See Genesis.
  • “the birth of Esther was hard, requiring instruments”: Instruments to assist with childbirth have evolved from rudimentary tools for destructive purposes in antiquity to sophisticated instruments like forceps and vacuum extractors today. Early interventions included destructive tools for delivering stillborn fetuses and instruments for procedures like symphysiotomy, a painful procedure to widen the pelvis. Modern forceps, introduced by the Chamberlen family in the 16th century, were a breakthrough for delivering living infants, and their use, along with vacuum extractors, remains a vital part of modern obstetrics. See Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery.
  • “the grey hen crowing on the bed-post”: A crowing hen was an omen of ill luck: “A whistling woman and a crowing hen / Are neither fit for God nor men.” See Fairy Tales and Folklore and Birds.
  • “like the sexton beetle digging with its little foot”: Sexton beetles, commonly known as burying beetles, are large, orange-and-black insects that act as nature’s undertakers by burying small, dead animals (like mice or birds) to feed their larvae. They are among the few insects that display active parental care, with both parents preparing the carcass, removing hair, and feeding their young.
  • “Her innocency had been spoiled by men, women, children”: “I will wash mine hands in innocency” (Psalm 26:6, KJV); innocency is the King James word. See The Bible.
  • “The bleeding hand of a dead child, lifting, might testify against her”: By the old ordeal of the bier, a murdered body was believed to bleed afresh when its murderer came near. In Richard III, Henry VI’s corpse bleeds before Gloucester: “Dead Henry’s wounds / Open their congeal’d mouths and bleed afresh!” (1.2). See Shakespeare and Guilt, Innocence, and Justice.
  • “something old, something new, something blue”: “Something old, something new, something borrowed, something blue,” the old wedding rhyme; the mother leaves out the borrowed. See Women, Gender, and Marriage and Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “a golden palace in a garden of talking birds and silver fountains”: In the Arabian Nights tale “The Talking Bird, the Singing Tree, and the Golden Water,” three children seek a bird that speaks, a tree that sings, and a fountain of golden water. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “So every day was thus her birthday”: Vera’s birthday and Esther Longtree’s birthday are portrayed with contrasting atmospheres and underlying thematic significance, reflecting their differing life circumstances and symbolic roles.
    • Vera’s birthday, her fourteenth, is described with a sense of ritual and expectation, though tinged with loneliness and unrealized celebration. Vera receives a formal party dress, a filmy white creation, somewhat too mature for her, chosen by Mr. Spitzer in a moment of abstraction linked to the memory of Vera’s mother. Despite the preparation, there are no guests, no grand party, and only Miss MacIntosh, her nursemaid, and Vera herself are present. The absence of guests and the muted atmosphere emphasize Vera’s isolation and the unrealized joys of childhood. Miss MacIntosh insists on simplicity and adherence to routine, asking Vera to take care of her duties, underscoring the austere and disciplined environment Vera inhabits. Vera is caught between the innocence and hope of youth and the harsh, confining realities imposed by her surroundings and caretakers. The birthday marks a threshold of maturity but also a loss of innocence, as Vera is trained for domesticity and marriage, and the celebration itself becomes a subdued, private event rather than a joyous public occasion. The birthday is also symbolic of Vera’s gradual awakening to the complexities and burdens of life. It is described as a moment on the edge of life’s “beginning of the end,” a turning point where the dreams and illusions of childhood begin to confront reality. The party dress and the preparations seem almost a costume for a role Vera must play rather than an expression of genuine celebration. The atmosphere is one of quiet resignation and subdued hope, with Vera realizing she has “but one” life and must face the responsibilities and limitations of her social role.
    • Esther’s seventh birthday is her mother’s anniversary: dressed in her old bridal clothes, the mother leads her all day through briers and marshes to the old mule farm to see the dying go-between, who is Esther’s father. On other birthdays, which were also Rosemary’s, her mother baked two cakes or none. See Rosemary.
    • Vera’s birthday is marked by a subdued, almost melancholic celebration within a structured, disciplined environment, while Esther’s birthday is enveloped in sorrow, mystery, and the physical and emotional weight of repeated loss. Vera’s day is a threshold into maturity, Esther’s a reflection of enduring struggle.
    • Vera’s birthday symbolizes the fragile transition from innocence to experience, the imposition of societal expectations, and the tension between dreams and reality. Esther’s birthday symbolizes endurance, maternal sacrifice, and the haunting presence of loss and unfulfilled potential.
    • Vera’s birthday occurs within the protective but confining care of Miss MacIntosh, reflecting themes of control, preparation for societal roles, and the looming responsibilities of adulthood. Esther’s birthday reflects marginalization, survival amid hardship, and the complexities of maternal identity in a world shaped by absence and loss.
    • Vera’s birthday carries a quiet hope and the bittersweet nature of growing up alone. Esther’s birthday carries a profound sense of isolation, struggle, and the interplay of hope and despair.
