Dante's Divine Comedy as Cosmological Map for Miss MacIntosh, My Darling

I. Mr. Spitzer's Belief

Mr. Spitzer believes that people need to travel through the lower world to reach the upper world. This is the summary of a passage in the novel that has not received the attention it deserves. It is a cosmological position stated explicitly by one of the novel's two primary consciousnesses. And the cosmological system it draws on is Dante's Divine Comedy: nine circles of Hell, seven levels of Purgatory, nine levels of Heaven, with God in the Empyrean beyond space and time.

Young cited Dante throughout Miss MacIntosh. Dante Alighieri is named directly in the novel. The Divine Comedy is referenced as a journey from sin to heaven, like Pilgrim's Progress. Roland appears in the Heaven of Mars. Boethius, whose De Musica the previous chapter addresses, is pointed out by Saint Thomas Aquinas in Dante's Paradiso. The Beatrice of Dante's Comedy, Beatrice Portinari who guides Dante through Paradise and symbolizes divine grace and theology, names a character in the novel's bus sections: Beatrice Fitzgerald, marginal, nearly blind, judged by her community, who may be royal by the bar sinister of her name. Dante's Cleopatra, whom he places in the circle of Lust, is echoed by the old woman in the novel who believes herself to be the reincarnation of Cleopatra.

These are not decorative allusions. Young embedded Dante's structure in the novel's movement. The question is where.

II. The Three Canticles and the Novel's Three Movements

The Divine Comedy has three books: Inferno, Purgatorio, Paradiso. Dante descends through Hell, ascends through Purgatory, and rises through Heaven. The journey is downward before it is upward, and the downward journey is necessary: without the full knowledge of Hell, the ascent through Purgatory and Paradise cannot be earned or understood.

Miss MacIntosh moves in a structure that rhymes with this even if it does not replicate it exactly. The novel opens with loss: Miss MacIntosh is gone. Catherine is in her opium bed. Vera is on a bus fleeing the house where she grew up. This is the beginning of the descent. The first half of the novel, roughly Chapters 1 through 43, moves through the lower world: the house by the sea, Catherine's dreams, Miss MacIntosh's secret revealed, Vera's loss of innocence in Chapter 13, Mr. Spitzer's early meditations on his dead twin brother and his unplayed music. These are the nine circles traversed slowly, the full accounting of what has been lost and what was never real.

The second half, roughly Chapters 44 through 82, moves through something that is not exactly Purgatorio but has its quality: the long process of each character arriving at what they actually are rather than what they have been pretending to be. Cousin Hannah's death and the revelation of her secret love. Mr. Spitzer's increasingly explicit meditations on the blind Eros and the love that exceeds the limitations of the individual. Esther Longtree's appearance as the mother of us all. These are not ascending toward Paradise. They are arriving at a kind of honest accounting. Dante's Purgatorio is the place where sin is acknowledged and purged. Young's second half is where illusion is finally named.

The Paradiso equivalent in Miss MacIntosh is not a location but a moment: the final chapter, Vera's declaration of love for the stone deaf man, her recognition of Esther Longtree as the mother of us all, the acceptance of her place in the wheel's rotation. It is not transcendence. It is acceptance. But acceptance, in this novel, is the highest rung available. The Empyrean is wherever Vera stops running and names what she loves.

III. The Seven Sins and the Seven Virtues

Dante's Purgatorio is organized around the seven deadly sins: Pride, Envy, Wrath, Sloth, Avarice, Gluttony, Lust. Each terrace of Purgatory is devoted to the purging of one sin. The corresponding virtues are taught on each terrace as the alternative to the sin being cleansed.

Every major character in Miss MacIntosh carries a dominant Dantean sin as their primary wound. Catherine's sin is closest to Sloth in its Dantean form: the failure to love adequately, the withdrawal from the demands of active engagement with the world, the opium bed as the physical expression of a soul that has abdicated its obligations. Miss MacIntosh's sin is Pride: the performance of a self she is not, the refusal to acknowledge the private reality that contradicts her public presentation. Mr. Spitzer's sin is Avarice in its emotional form: the hoarding of love never declared, the refusal to spend the self in relationship, the accumulation of grief and unexpressed devotion that never becomes actual giving. Vera's journey through the novel is the process of encountering these sins in the people who were supposed to teach her how to live, and arriving at something that is not virtue in the Dantean sense but is the honest acknowledgment of what each sin costs.

The seven levels of Purgatory with seven vices and seven virtues: this is the structural skeleton beneath Miss MacIntosh's second half. Young did not map the sins onto characters with academic schematicism. She absorbed the structure and built her own version of it, populated with characters whose wounds match the Dantean categories without reducing to them.

IV. Beatrice Fitzgerald and Beatrice Portinari

The novel's Beatrice is introduced on the bus, near the end of the bus journey section, as a figure of social marginality and judgment. She is nearly blind and losing the other eye. She is called a common prostitute by Madge, who resents her. She is called royal by Homer, who respects her name: Fitzgerald, with its prefix Fitz, meaning born of royalty on the wrong side of the blanket. She hides in darkness by putting out the lights so others will not see her. She has a son in the Navy who still remembers her.

Dante's Beatrice is the guide through Paradise, the figure who symbolizes divine grace and theology, the woman whose death in 1290 prompted La Vita Nuova and whose presence in the Comedy transforms the journey from a descent into Hell into a complete cosmological circuit. She appears at the top of Purgatory to relieve Virgil of his guiding function, which is reason's limit, and guide Dante through the levels of Heaven that reason alone cannot reach.

Young's Beatrice is not a guide. She is barely seen. She is almost blind. She hides. But the name carries its weight deliberately: royal by bar sinister, nearly sightless, marginal to the community that judges her, remembered by a son who has traveled the world. In a novel where Young said there are no coincidences, the name Beatrice attached to a woman who is going blind at the moment Vera's bus approaches the end of its journey is a structural marker. The guide through Paradise is losing her sight as the novel approaches its final reckoning. The Dantean figure who should be most visible is the one who puts out the lights so no one can see her. This is Young's revision of Dante: the divine mediator is not transcendent. She is exhausted and nearly blind and living in a tin house in a flooded yard.

V. What This Requires from the Novel

The Dante chapter is the architectural argument that most requires close verification against the novel's actual chapter structure. The three-canticle structure, the seven sins, the specific placement of Beatrice Fitzgerald as a Dantean revision: all of these need to be traced against the chapters as they exist, counting the specific positions and noting where the Dantean parallels hold and where Young departs from them.

What is established here is the framework and the evidence that makes the framework plausible: Young cited Dante directly and repeatedly; Mr. Spitzer explicitly articulates the lower world/upper world structure that is the Divine Comedy's organizing journey; the character named Beatrice carries the weight of her name in ways that are structurally coherent with the Dantean guide function; and the novel's movement from loss through reckoning to a final acceptance has the shape of the three canticles even if it does not replicate their theological content.

Dante's journey ends in the vision of God, a light that the soul finally apprehends after the full descent and ascent. Young's journey ends in Vera's love for the stone deaf man and her recognition of Esther Longtree as the mother of us all. The light that Vera finally apprehends is not divine but human: the love that persists through the impossibility of shared sound, the mother who carries all the stillborn children, the wheel that keeps turning after the hub is gone.

Mr. Spitzer was right. You have to go through the lower world first.
