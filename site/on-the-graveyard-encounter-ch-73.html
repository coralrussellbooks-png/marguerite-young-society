On the Graveyard Encounter

Joe Goldberg’s letter describes their encounter in a graveyard on a foggy, windy night: he knocked the nose off a stone angel, he took her against her will. Young situates this assault in a graveyard deliberately, in the place of the dead, among the stones of the already-lost, in fog and wind, the most indeterminate of atmospheres. The classical tradition she draws on is long: Greek mythology is full of women assaulted in liminal spaces, at the border of the human and the divine, and producing from those encounters children who are marked by the violence of their conception. Persephone, Callisto, Daphne, Europa, Io: the escape from sexual pursuit is one of mythology’s oldest motifs, and the woman who cannot escape is almost always transformed: into a tree, into a star, into a state of perpetual pregnancy. Esther does not transform. She continues. She gets up. She goes back to work. This refusal of mythological resolution is one of Young’s most pointed choices.
