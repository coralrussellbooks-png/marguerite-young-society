The Abolition of Private Schooling

Why Separate Is Never Equal and What Comes After

A Companion Document to the Constitution

The Philosophical Foundation

Private schooling allows families with money to choose who their children go to school with. When parents choose a private school, they are often choosing a school where the student body is economically and racially similar. Children in those schools grow up without sustained contact with people whose lives look very different from their own.

This matters educationally. One of the core purposes of education, in the framework this constitution is built on, is developing the capacity to be genuinely present to other people: to understand their experiences, to take their lives seriously, to think and reason alongside them. That capacity does not develop in a school from which difference has been screened out by tuition. A child who has never been required to share a classroom with someone from a radically different background has missed something that no amount of academic preparation can substitute for.

The other side of the sorting causes its own harm. The child in the underfunded public school receives a clear message from the structure of the system: the families who could afford to leave have left, and their departure has taken resources, political attention, and social investment with them. That is not a neutral fact. It tells children something about whose education the society considers worth funding.

Private schooling is not incidentally connected to class reproduction. It is one of its primary mechanisms. The networks, the expectations, the ease in professional and social settings where power is exercised: these are transmitted through the experience of being educated alongside other children whose families have resources. The abolition of private schooling is an argument about what education is for and who it is for.

The History

Private Schools and Segregation

The modern American private school system cannot be understood outside its relationship to school desegregation. After the Supreme Court's 1954 decision in Brown v. Board of Education required the integration of public schools, many communities, particularly in the South, responded by creating private schools that could maintain racial separation after the law required public schools to integrate. The White Citizens' Councils that organized resistance to desegregation explicitly promoted private alternatives to integrated public schools.

Many of these private academies were explicitly racially exclusive. When the IRS moved in the 1970s to revoke the tax-exempt status of racially discriminatory private schools, the political reaction became one of the organizing forces behind the religious right as a political movement. The public argument shifted from explicit racial exclusion to religious liberty: the freedom of religious schools to operate according to their beliefs. That argument has served as the primary public justification for private schooling ever since, while the racial and economic stratification the system produces has continued largely unchanged.

The connection between private schooling and resistance to integration is not only historical. The private school system as it currently operates, regardless of the founding intentions of any individual school, produces racial and economic stratification that closely mirrors what the explicitly segregationist academies of the post-Brown era produced. The legal framework is different. The outcome is largely the same.

Religious Schools

The most politically complex category of private schools is the religious school. The argument for religious schools rests on two claims: the right of parents to raise their children in their religious tradition, and the right of religious communities to operate educational institutions consistent with their beliefs. Both claims are real. Neither is a sufficient argument for maintaining a separate educational system that produces the segregation and inequality the public school system is constitutionally obligated to address.

The right to raise children in a religious tradition is not the same as the right to educate them in a separate school system supported by public tax exemptions and, increasingly, by public voucher programs. Religious education is available outside school hours, on weekends, and through the existing infrastructure of religious community life. This constitution does not prohibit religious education programs or the formation of religious identity in the home and community. It requires that the publicly supported educational system be a common institution in which all children participate.

A religious school that teaches that certain groups of people are sinful or lesser, that excludes students on grounds of sexual orientation or gender identity, or that provides a curriculum inconsistent with this constitution's educational obligations, is not simply exercising religious liberty. It is using religious liberty as grounds for reproducing exclusions the constitution prohibits, while benefiting from the tax treatment that makes it financially viable. The resolution is clear: public subsidy is what this constitution controls. Religious institutions may operate educational programs for their communities. They are not entitled to public funding for doing so in ways inconsistent with the constitutional education obligation.

What the Research Says

The argument that private schools produce better educational outcomes than public schools requires careful examination. Studies that appear to show a private school advantage generally do not adequately account for selection effects: the private school student comes from a family with the economic and social resources to choose a private school, to be involved in their child's education, and to provide supplementary support that improves academic outcomes regardless of which school the child attends. When researchers attempt to control for these factors, the private school advantage largely disappears.

The research on school integration is more consistent and more compelling. Students in economically and racially integrated schools show better outcomes, across a wide range of measures, than students in segregated schools. The benefits are not concentrated among lower-income students who gain access to more-resourced schools. They are distributed across the student population. Students from affluent families educated alongside students from less affluent families do not experience a decline in academic outcomes. They gain in the civic capacities that integrated education develops.

The research on civic outcomes is particularly striking. Students educated in socioeconomically diverse settings show greater civic engagement as adults, greater political participation, greater willingness to support public goods, and greater capacity for deliberation across difference. These are capacities that a democracy requires. A student who has never shared a classroom with someone from a different background does not develop them automatically. The private school is not a neutral choice. It has costs for the student who attends it, and for the society that depends on shared civic life.

The Transition

The Timeline

The abolition of private schooling is not instantaneous. The transition requires a timeline long enough to allow families, schools, and communities to adjust, and short enough to constitute a genuine commitment rather than an indefinite deferral. A fifteen-year transition window is appropriate: long enough for the public school system to build the capacity to receive every student, short enough to be a real change rather than a symbolic gesture.

During the transition period, existing private schools continue to operate but receive no new public subsidy: no tax exemptions for new contributions, no voucher funding, no public contracts of any kind. This creates a financial pressure that accelerates the voluntary movement of families and teachers toward the public system as the public system improves. It does not require the immediate closure of any institution.

The Endowments

The endowments of private schools, accumulated over generations through tax-exempt donations, represent a large concentration of educational wealth built with public subsidy. Upon the transition of a private school to public status, or the closure of a private school that does not transition, its endowment transfers to the public school foundation of the jurisdiction in which it operates, to be used for the improvement of public education in that jurisdiction, with priority given to schools serving the lowest-income communities.

Phillips Exeter Academy's endowment is more than one billion dollars for a school of about eleven hundred students. The redirection of that endowment to the public schools of New Hampshire would substantially transform those schools' capacity. The counterargument is that the endowment was donated specifically for private education at Exeter. That is true. It was donated with tax exemptions that represented a public subsidy. The redirection of wealth accumulated through that subsidy toward the public purpose of education is not confiscation. It is the return of what the public made possible to the public that made it possible.

The Teachers

Teachers currently employed in private schools are not responsible for the system they work in and should not bear the cost of the transition. The transition includes full portability of credentials, seniority, and pension rights into the public system for private school teachers who choose to transition. The public system absorbs these teachers with full recognition of their experience.

The transition creates a significant expansion of the public teaching workforce at exactly the moment the public system is being asked to serve a larger and more diverse student population. Private school teachers who transition bring skills, experience, and in many cases specialization in arts, languages, and other subjects the current public system has underinvested in.

Making the Public School Worth Choosing

The abolition of private schooling is only sustainable if the public school system that replaces it is genuinely worth choosing. Families who currently send their children to private schools are not always doing so from a desire to exclude. They are often doing so because the public school available to them does not provide what their child needs: class sizes are too large, course offerings too limited, buildings too deteriorated, safety too uncertain. The transition requires simultaneous investment in the public school system that makes these concerns addressable.

The equalized funding provisions, investment in early childhood education, universal design provisions, curriculum rebuilding, and teacher pay and autonomy provisions described in the education companion document are prerequisites for a public school system that can actually replace what the private system currently provides. The abolition of private schooling without the investment in the public alternative is not a policy. It is a demand. The two must happen together, funded adequately and implemented honestly, for the transition to produce the outcome the philosophy requires.
