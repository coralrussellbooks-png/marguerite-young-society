Parallels from Debs to the Present

Part of a reading of Harp Song for a Radical (1999), Marguerite Young’s life of Eugene Victor Debs.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Harp Song for a Radical · The Marguerite Young Society

Passages from the book, with page numbers, sorted by the complaint they share with the present.

Money and corporations run the government

  • p. 261: The government "conspired with the regnant financial powers of Wall Street."

  • p. 364: Cleveland's government was "in thralldom to iron and steel and gold and silver and copper and lead."

  • p. 482: Railroad presidents "had the government in their back pockets."

  • p. 509: The "industrial lords to whom the government had prostituted itself."

  • p. 527: "The railroads owned the government."

  • p. 535: Companies "whose ambassadors were not only in Washington but in the Congress and the Senate, no matter what administration was in power." This is the lobbyist complaint.

  • p. 578: In Debs's essay "Socialism," capitalists spend their time "choosing judges, buying editors, hiring preachers, corrupting politics."

Lawmakers who are slow, self-serving, or bought

  • p. 305: Lawmakers "all too slow for him in the righting of wrongs and were very good in the passage of half-baked bills."

  • p. 314: Debs left the legislature because of "pettifogging political hucksters who delayed progressive decisions for years and acted in behalf of the vested interests."

  • p. 389: Senators tried to kill the Sherman Antitrust Act by loading it with amendments. Senator Ingalls: "The purification of politics is an iridescent dream."

  • p. 414: In the Crédit Mobilier scandal, stock was paid off "to high officials in the government."

  • p. 513: President Hayes left income and assets off his tax reports.

The two parties are really the same

  • p. 170: Weitling thought "both major parties were iridescent bugbears very much like each other."

  • p. 231: Debs's father came to see that Republicans and Democrats were "governed by the same powers."

  • p. 352: The "differences between the two major parties were mainly delusional."

  • p. 517: A warning of "corruption at no distant day if the people continue to ignore the duties of citizenship in the blind adherence to party leaders."

Voting doesn't change anything, and elections get stolen

  • p. 195: "Their ballots had been chewed up by water rats."

  • p. 363: "They had the ballots, but the laws were so framed that their interests and rights were ignored."

  • p. 397: Henry Adams: voters "could not be sure where King Banker lurked behind."

  • pp. 468, 486, 525: The disputed 1876 election, when Tilden won but Hayes took office.

One justice system for the rich, another for the poor

  • p. 27: "Those who stole a country's treasures from the poor were not punished by corrupt governments," and "if they were fined, the penalties were less than the rewards."

  • p. 51: The rich "raised up a barricade of laws by which to protect themselves from the starving poor."

  • p. 198: The Supreme Court was "following elections as the hearse follows the horse."

  • p. 328: The courts "tilted the scales of justice in favor of the rich."

  • p. 366: The Supreme Court ruled that a railroad owed nothing to the widow of a brakeman killed on the job.

  • p. 449: "The law did not pertain for the rich man who stole a continent" but came down hard on a poor man who stole an ear of corn.

  • pp. 197, 506: Court injunctions used as a corporate weapon against strikers.

  • p. 236: Debs was jailed without a jury trial.

Government force used for corporations, against workers

  • p. 197: Federal troops were sent in against the 1877 strikers.

  • p. 469: "The state and federal governments protected corporation heads."

  • p. 487: Putting down the strike cost more than meeting "the far from immoderate demands of the workers" would have.

  • p. 489: Troops were sent to protect "the poor, starving, abused, beaten, battered imperial corporations."

  • p. 491: The city police force was cut in half to save money, while Pinkerton security men were brought in against strikers.

  • p. 536: Harrison sent federal troops to Coeur d'Alene. Strikers were held in barbed-wire bullpens.

Profiteering and public money going to the well connected

  • p. 234: The federal government "ignored as many malfeasances as it could" by corporate land grabbers "while poor war widows and their children living in hog huts in that same North starved."

  • p. 237: "The profiteering war vultures" of Wall Street.

  • p. 413: Money for rebuilding Chicago after the fire went to the merchants and railroad men, who were "channeling the golden sluices to themselves."

  • p. 448: The Civil War "enriched the war profiteers by not taxing them but by passing the burden on to the poor."

  • p. 498: The rich could buy their way out of the war.

Inequality, and democracy for the few

  • p. 75: A "hypocritical democracy which gave protection to the few rich but not to the many poor."

  • p. 191: The nation's real split was between rich and poor, not North and South.

  • p. 258: A "dollars and cents democracy" with "dollars for some and cents for others."

  • p. 490: The Constitution seemed "to favor business interests and property owners and those of privilege."

  • p. 491: The middle class was "not rising but were sinking."

  • p. 511: "The true threat to this democracy came from the rich corrupter and not from the masses of the poor."

  • p. 525: A democracy "where everything was measured by the cost, the cost to the rich and not to the poor."

  • p. 531: Workers wanted a government "more concerned with life and liberty and less with property and property rights."

Monopolies

  • p. 197: Industrial empires whose heads had "more power than was given to the czar of Russia."

  • p. 448: A "monarchical monolith such as had no business in a democracy."

  • p. 526: The Farmers' Declaration of Independence called the railway monopoly "a history of repeated injuries and oppressions."

Contempt for the poor and unemployed, and no safety net

  • p. 138: The "powers in Washington" left the poor to drift.

  • p. 197: The Chicago Tribune "advocated a little strychnine or arsenic as the cure" for the unemployed tramps.

  • p. 263: Higher education was open only to those who could afford it.

  • p. 472: Banks foreclosed on farms, and machines put people out of work.

  • p. 479: President Arthur refused to enforce the eight-hour law. Petitioners asked for public works for the starving.

  • p. 483: Homeless people took turns sleeping on police station floors.

  • p. 504: Tom Scott's view: "the duty of the Government merely to protect the people while they are taking care of themselves."

Worker safety and corporate responsibility

  • p. 185: After a train wreck, the railroad blamed God, and the president-elect had the railroad defended in court.

  • p. 307: Debs's bill to make railroads compensate injured workers.

  • p. 434: "Let the laws be so framed that soul-less corporations cannot starve it to death."

  • p. 450: Company negligence killed 566 miners and maimed 1,665 in seven years, all "called those accidents which were inevitable."

The press

  • p. 349: "The subsidized press employed to dwarf the importance of labor."

  • p. 502: Newspapers called striking workers "alien" communists.

Civil liberties

  • pp. 3–5: Wilson and the Espionage Act, which jailed conscientious objectors.

  • pp. 215, 230, 448: The suspension of habeas corpus.

Partisan fighting and bad management

  • p. 234: The Johnson impeachment as a partisan show trial.

  • p. 248: Congress's mud-slinging over Mary Lincoln's pension.

  • p. 339: Kate Debs "could have given a lesson to the United States government in how to make both ends meet on a limited budget."
