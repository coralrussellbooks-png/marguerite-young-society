Chapter 46

On Chapter 46 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Woman in Young's Magnum Opus.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

Cousin Hannah dies in the mountains in this chapter, and her death is the pillar at full stretch: the dissolving boundary between life and death, a woman who grows larger in dying than she was allowed to be alive. She is the "great liberator of woman," the Troubadour with her "flaming message," the suffrage captain whose mountain-climbing is the breaking of every limit set on women, and the frost that takes her, the mirrors "rheumed with frost like her dead eyes," the iceberg moving into the room, is the cold the book keeps at the edge of all its women. Mr. Spitzer is not present when she goes. "Never yet had he been present at a climax," and his absence is the male witness late again to the woman's decisive hour, the erasure that keeps overtaking her even in death.

The alpenhorn is left behind, "twice her height," with no other claimant, and Mr. Spitzer keeps it so it will not go to strangers. It is the preservation of a woman's legacy against oblivion and it is the music that goes unheard, an instrument that cannot raise the mountains "in level coastlands," a sound that will not carry the thing it was built to carry, the same failed transmission the disconnected telephone kept staging. When he blows it by the sea, the "wandering notes like some old ghost calling to his lost love" draw "lost men" after him, "lost sheep" and "grey loons," and the calling is the book's practice with the erased: the one who sounds for the vanished gathers the vanished around him.

Ghosts, the chapter says, never appear naked but are "clothed by the clothing of a dream," and behind them stands "the great mask of life and love, even of death." This is the self as mask raised into law, the dead dressed by the dream exactly as the living were, real and dream as one country holding both. Hannah herself is forgotten now by mountain-climbers, uncelebrated, taken by the public for a "charlatan" or a "mountebank," and only Mr. Spitzer knows her "love" and her "secret cowardice," her "granite" that was "but a façade" crumbling into "waters and clouds." The misreading is the erasure of a feminist life and the crumbling is the body as the place the self comes apart, granite going to water on a woman the record could not hold.

Her lightness is the chapter's last figure. She was "so light" she should have eaten "sand grains and pebbles" like migrating cranes for ballast, her "jagged forehead" set against the winter that follows "like a courtier in her wake," and the snow covers her "dead heart" while the book refuses to say whether she was "the greatest coward" or the greatest hero. "Love went on when there was neither subject nor object," which is the illusion of the individual stated over a deathbed, love outlasting both the one who loved and the one who was loved, persisting where no separate self remains to hold it. Her final cry, "Find your way to the lightless star," and the promise that "mountains resurrect themselves before the last great cataclysm" while "my love goes down," leave feminism as a light that guides toward the unknown even as it sinks, enduring past its own setting.
