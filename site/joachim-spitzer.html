Definition from research

Chapter 2

Listed as: Mr. Spitzer (Joachim)

  • Catherine’s faithful but often ignored visitor, a semi-retired lawyer.
  • He is a figure of reason, order, and routine, marked by absent-mindedness and a broken career after the death of his twin brother (Peron).
  • Despite his marriage proposal being rejected by Catherine, he visits her regularly.
  • He represents the tether to reality and the outside world.
  • His relationship with Catherine is ambiguous; he is both her guardian and unrequited lover.

Chapter 13

Listed as: Mr. Spitzer

  • Mr. Spitzer is described as "pompous" and "beclouded," suggesting a man who carries a certain self-importance or authority, yet his thoughts and arguments are somewhat confused or unclear. He is "many-valved," implying layers or complexities in his personality or reasoning, which are not easily penetrable.
  • He is a lawyer who argues cases and makes decisions, which indicates a rational, structured mind accustomed to logic and human argument. Yet this external rationality contrasts sharply with the surreal, silent garden environment where "the ear is dead".
  • The repeated motif of deafness is central to his character. While Mr. Spitzer physically experiences a kind of internal "roaring" in his right ear, a noise which "did not come from this world," he is metaphorically deaf to true communication or sound in the broader existential sense described by Catherine.
  • The conversations with Catherine underline Mr. Spitzer’s role as a symbol of human limitation and isolation. Despite his attempts to speak and reason, he is trapped in a world where "there was no sound" and "he could not hear another’s voice, not even his own voice speaking". This frames him as a figure caught between the rational world of law and argument and a deeper, more profound silence that challenges or negates those faculties.
  • Their interaction reveals a tension between denial and acceptance of this silence and deafness. Mr. Spitzer tries to argue against the idea that his ear is dead, pointing out that his dead brother’s ear was not buried on the estate, yet Catherine remains insistent that "the ear was dead," emphasizing a metaphysical or symbolic deafness beyond just physical hearing.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s character embodies the tragic human predicament of isolation and the failure of communication. The novel suggests that even in his legal profession, where voice and argument are paramount, his voice is unheard in the cosmic or existential sense. This heightens the pathos of his character as someone who functions in society but is fundamentally cut off from true connection or understanding.
  • “a subdued roaring, a noise which came from the head only”: Tinnitus, a ringing or roaring in the ears with no outside source. Beethoven described it in the Heiligenstadt Testament (1802).
  • “his music was the silence”: John Cage’s 4′33″ (1952) is a piece of silence, in which the audience hears only the sounds of the room. Mr. Spitzer has written “only silent music” (Chapter 2).

Chapter 14

  • Joachim Spitzer is depicted as one of two Mr. Spitzers (twins or doubles) each reflecting and haunting the other. The chapter explores the idea that there were “four Mr. Spitzers,” due to reflections and mirrored images, and that the boundaries between them blur, creating a complicated identity crisis.
  • This duality extends to the notion that one Mr. Spitzer is “vacant and slow and smiling,” while the other had died by suicide, attempting to “kill the Mr. Spitzer who was the pale reflection in his own heart.”
  • The surviving Spitzer is burdened by his brother’s shadow, the “dark counterpart running through all his nights and days,” which disrupts his peace and confounds his existence.
  • Joachim is portrayed as a man who has not achieved conventional success.
  • He is described as a “failure,” living “the most retired, the most unassuming life” in the shadow of his brother’s more colorful and gregarious personality.
  • Despite his literal-mindedness and conservative habits, he is haunted by self-doubt and confusion, often unsure of his own actions and memories.
  • Instances such as finding his brother’s belongings in his coat pockets, or the mysterious “second cup of coffee” he supposedly drank but cannot explain, illustrate his fragmented sense of self and reality.
  • Joachim’s relationship with Vera’s mother is complex. He is her lawyer, executor, and companion in her illness, yet she doubts his trustworthiness and resents his evasiveness and silence.
  • Catherine’s obsession with the black coachman (a symbolic figure representing continuity, memory, and conscience) serves as a foil to Joachim’s own failures and ambiguities.
  • She accuses him of neglecting or betraying the black coachman and the family’s legacy, suggesting that he may have “cheated the black coachman out of his employment and inheritance” and failed to honor her dying wishes.
  • Joachim’s inability or unwillingness to answer her questions about the black coachman and other matters deepens the tension and highlights his evasive nature.
  • Joachim is a man of silence and restraint, in contrast to his brother’s vibrant life.
  • He has ceased composing “oral music” and instead writes “silent music, a dirge for a dead clam,” symbolizing his withdrawal from active life and communication.
  • This self-imposed silence and his “utter self-forgetfulness and selflessness” suggest a man resigned to obscurity and loss, attempting to protect himself and others from the chaos that surrounds him.
  • His life is overshadowed by the death of his brother, an event that has left him “hopelessly benumbed” and struggling to keep up with time and reality.
  • He is pursued by people who mistake him for his deceased brother, and he attempts to shield his brother’s memory, sometimes maintaining the illusion that the brother still lives.
  • This haunting presence causes him distress but also a certain pride in having “preferred the last footstep, the last doorway,” indicating a preference for quiet endurance over dramatic exit.
  • Joachim is enveloped in mystery and ambiguity. His actions and whereabouts are uncertain; he is sometimes imagined wearing the black coachman’s livery, sometimes unable to explain trivial facts like train journeys.
  • His existence blurs the line between the living and the dead, the real and the illusory.
  • He is “a man who did not know himself” and whose life is marked by “minor but palpable discrepancies” and “things which did not add up.”
  • Joachim’s character is embedded in a cosmic framework where ordinary human concerns intersect with metaphysical questions.
  • He and Catherine speak of “the music of the spheres,” “the silent music,” and the “lawless image of the dream,” suggesting a man grappling with the ultimate nature of reality and existence.
  • His legal work, particularly the “case of Spitzer versus Spitzer,” symbolizes his internal conflict and the unresolved tensions of his identity.
  • Joachim shows a degree of faithfulness and compassion, toward Catherine.
  • He visits Catherine regularly, tries to please her, and protects her interests “to the best of his present legal ability.” However, his “slumberous inertia” and evasiveness frustrate her, revealing a gap between his intentions and her expectations.
  • Joachim Spitzer’s life is inextricably linked to the death of his brother Peron. The brother’s suicide or accidental death seems to have precipitated a crisis in Joachim’s identity and existence.
  • He is described as having taken up his brother’s mantle in some respects: wearing his brother’s cufflinks, carrying his cane, and even being mistaken for him by acquaintances. Yet he is also deeply burdened by the consequences, trying to protect his brother’s reputation while simultaneously being haunted by his brother’s unresolved, chaotic life.
  • This complicated relationship fuels Joachim’s feelings of inadequacy and deepens his tendency toward silence and withdrawal.
  • “the case of Spitzer versus Spitzer”: A case in which he “must appear as both the defender and the accuser.”

Chapter 23

  • “Am I my brother’s keeper?”: Genesis 4:9. Miss MacIntosh asks what Mr. Spitzer might. It resonates with his struggle with his sense of duty, guilt, and responsibility for his brother’s legacy and the lives entangled in their shared history. The question also connects to broader existential concerns about individual responsibility in a chaotic and often indifferent world. The novel explores themes of illusion, memory, and the blurred boundaries between life and death, self and other. It asks whether one is truly responsible for the fate, suffering, or salvation of another, particularly a close other like a brother, and by extension, for others in one’s life or society. It highlights the novel’s exploration of moral responsibility, the limits of that responsibility, and the intimate yet often painful ties that bind individuals to one another in a world marked by loss, deception, and uncertain reality.

Chapter 24

Listed as: Mr. Spitzer

  • A mysterious and somewhat unsatisfactory companion to Catherine, embodying confusion and lost memories.
  • He represents uncertainty about earthly matters while espousing nebulous spiritual certainties about an ultimate Heaven.
  • His characterization as uncertain, evasive, and disconnected from his own memories contrasts with Catherine’s intense visionary life, highlighting themes of memory and reality.
  • “Mr. Spitzer was a roundhouse of the trains which run no more”: In Dickens’s “The Signal-Man” (1866), a railway signalman is haunted by a ghost and a red danger light before a fatal crash, which fits Young’s “the red where there should be the green.”

Chapter 25

Listed as: Joachim Spitzer (Peron’s twin brother)

  • Described as “useless as regret,” a man trapped in liminality, between being alive and dead, reality and illusion, a “soul divided.”
  • Portrayed with physical and psychological frailty: fat, slow, hesitant.
  • He embodies indecision and ambiguity, unable to commit or fully engage with life.
  • Haunted by his brother Peron’s death and his own survival, which he views as a “living death.”
  • Experiences illusions and confusion; his senses and memory are unreliable.
  • His life is marked by routine, melancholy, and a lack of clear purpose, yet he retains a faint spark of life and pride.
  • He comes to Catherine each evening, “when the moon arose,” and there his ambiguous existence is both challenged and sustained.
  • He remembers that he loved Catherine “even with his dying breath,” and imagines what his life might have been “if ever she had accepted his passive hand in marriage.”
  • He reflects endlessly on Peron’s mysterious death.
  • He walks through a glass wall without noticing or being hurt, a sign of his tenuous grasp on reality.
  • The glass wall brings bills and threatening letters, and finally a claim of his own.
  • He is caught between a life unresolved and a death he keeps rehearsing.

Chapter 26

Listed as: Joachim Spitzer (Peron’s twin brother)

  • Family lawyer, guardian of the dead’s legacy.
  • Scrupulous, conservative, somber, introspective.
  • Struggles with the paradox of his profession and personal grief over his twin brother. He is caught between duty and emotional turmoil.
  • Believes in a final peace beyond life and law, emphasizing oblivion rather than memory after death.
  • His work involves dealing with the hidden past, dusty archives, and eccentric clients, many of whom create baffling legal puzzles through multiple wills or lost heirs.
  • This career, entered reluctantly, “only because of his father’s wishes,” keeps him continually close to death and the mysteries of inheritance.
  • A notable case in Mr. Spitzer’s youth involved an elderly Boston lady who believed she was Cleopatra reincarnated. She left her house and garden to her innumerable Siamese cats, with the ancient caretaker to live there and draw an income as long as the cats lived, and Mr. Spitzer to supervise. The cats are described almost as immortal beings, with a mysterious presence that haunts the house, symbolizing the blurred lines between life and death, reality and illusion.
  • He also considers Catherine’s narcotic-induced oblivion as a form of escape from reality, pondering the value of memory and oblivion in death.
  • Mr. Spitzer philosophizes on the necessity of traveling through the “lower world” (a metaphorical space of moral and social darkness) before reaching the “upper world” or enlightenment. This journey includes encountering thieves, street women, and the underbelly of life, suggesting his awareness of the complex layers of existence beyond his legal profession.
  • He identified his brother at “the morgue of a great city… among homeless men and fallen women and faceless creatures,” and his life has not been entirely his own since.
  • He proposed to Catherine, and when she refused “for the last fickle and unconstant time” his “passive hand,” he “turned so silently away.”
  • He hires the servants, pays her “astronomical bills,” and pays Peron’s debts “with every new day of his life.”

Chapter 27

Listed as: Mr. Spitzer

  • A man burdened by his awareness of his difficulties and haunted by memories and illusions.
  • He is isolated, secretive, and struggles to reconcile his inner world with external reality.
  • His interactions with Catherine and his own sense of time and self are central.
  • Mr. Spitzer rarely leaves his home except for business and is burdened by his fragmented memory and difficulties.
  • He wears his deceased brother’s watch, and under his cuff on his left wrist his own broken one, as a memento and a symbol of time and loss.
  • Mr. Spitzer experiences dislocation in time and space: arriving early or late, encountering sudden weather changes, and experiencing shifts in scenery.
  • Mr. Spitzer struggles with the notion that life, time, and events are illusions, yet he continues to strive for dignity and order in his existence.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s clothes are always described and contain items from other people (Peron and Jock).
  • He carries calling cards, an out-dated passport, and his silent music, so that if he is found in a city morgue, “all men should know that he had not been his brother.”
  • He fears “the loss of his dignity” above all, yet is proud of walking through glass and of the clerk who “insisted that he was dead,” “thinking of the joke he had played.”

Chapter 32

Listed as: Mr. Spitzer

  • Mr. Spitzer serves as the "only go-between" between Cousin Hannah and Catherine, facilitating communication during times when direct contact was difficult or impossible. This intermediary role is underscored by his musical whispering ("musically whispering as he had thought what to say, what not to say") which suggests a careful, delicate handling of sensitive matters and emotions. His interactions hint at an awareness of the emotional and existential weight carried by these women, especially Cousin Hannah nearing the end of her life.
  • Mr. Spitzer appears as a figure who exists somewhat on the threshold between life and death, reality and dream. The chapter notes that Catherine had visited Cousin Hannah only in a dream, riding a carriage driven by a "dead black coachman," evoking an otherworldly atmosphere. Mr. Spitzer's presence in this liminal space, with his dark cape and snow "like ermine upon his collar," symbolizes a mediator between the living and the fading, between the tangible and the ephemeral. This imagery suggests that Mr. Spitzer is linked to the inevitable passage of time and mortality that frames the chapter.
  • There is a tone of respect and perhaps even reverence in Mr. Spitzer’s attitude toward Cousin Hannah. He "certainly hoped that my mother’s visit had not occurred after she was dead," indicating his sensitivity to timing and propriety in moments of transition between life and death. His role is not intrusive but rather one of discreet support and careful facilitation.
  • While Cousin Hannah and Catherine are enmeshed in their struggles (whether political, personal, or metaphysical) Mr. Spitzer’s character represents a connection to the external, practical world. His visits to the mansion, his knocking and waiting in the vestibule, contrast with the dreamlike, shifting realities inhabited by the women. This contrast shows him as a bridge between the ordinary and the extraordinary, the real and the imagined.

Chapter 40

Listed as: Mr. Spitzer

  • Embodies a skeptical and analytical perspective amid the swirling dreamlike and often chaotic narrative centered on Catherine and Cousin Hannah.
  • Portrayed as a patient observer who frequently comments on Catherine's behavior and mental state.
  • He is described as believing himself to be "non-competitive," a detail that suggests a self-aware, possibly detached attitude toward social or intellectual contests.
  • His habitual gestures like pulling the jowl of his cheek, tapping his teeth with a toothpick which is noted as a "tiny diamond-headed gold walking stick", add a layer of characterization that conveys both a certain meticulousness and an almost aristocratic fastidiousness. This imagery paints Mr. Spitzer as a man deeply engaged in subtle, perhaps cynical reflection.
  • His observations reveal an inclination toward rational skepticism. For example, he frequently notices Catherine’s "serene unconcern for facts," and how she "would introduce the living among the dead," blurring the boundaries between reality and fantasy, life and death. Mr. Spitzer seems to see Catherine’s tendencies as both a kind of madness and a poignant human frailty, noting how she assumes that some living people have already "passed over to the other shore," thus reflecting her confusion or perhaps her unique perception of existence.
  • He is described as "waiting all his life here to prove that he no longer lived, that he had died long ago," which imbues his character with a certain existential irony or fatalism. This suggests that he is preoccupied with themes of mortality and the nature of existence, paralleling the chapter’s broader meditations on life, death, time, and memory.
  • In the context of the chapter’s dreamlike atmosphere, where time is non-linear, identities are fluid, and reality merges with imagination, Mr. Spitzer functions as a grounding figure, representing a rational, if somewhat resigned, viewpoint. His presence highlights the tension between Catherine’s mystical, fragmented experience of reality and the desire for logical coherence and explanation.

  • Haunted by his brother’s absence and the loss of a shared past, struggling with identity and reality.
  • Symbolizes the rational, structured side of human nature, trying to impose order on chaos.

Chapter 56

Listed as: Mr. Spitzer

  • Mr. Spitzer is the grieving brother, depicted as a man shattered by loss, struggling with his identity and the nature of reality. He clings to his old, threadbare clothes as symbols of himself and his grief.

Chapter 57

Listed as: Mr. Spitzer

  • Mr. Spitzer is grappling with grief, guilt, and confusion after his brother’s death. He is introspective, haunted by recurrent dreams, and obsessed with settling an ambiguous tailor’s bill related to his brother’s funeral suit. He represents the struggle between reality and illusion, life and death.

Chapter 58

Listed as: Mr. Spitzer

  • Mr. Spitzer is a large, reflective man, draped in a black cape, who is a silent composer and connoisseur of music. He is caught between life and death, reality and imagination. His deafness contrasts with his profound musical understanding, highlighting a spiritual or metaphysical connection to music beyond sound. He embodies melancholy, wisdom, and a deep sense of loss, especially connected to his dead brother and the passing of time.

Chapter 59

Listed as: Mr. Spitzer

  • A melancholic, introspective figure who is both a lawyer and a musician.
  • Haunted by memories of his brother Peron and his own existential plight.
  • Portrayed as a man caught between life and death, reality and illusion.
  • Exhibits deep sympathy for poor musicians, often giving coins indiscriminately.
  • Suffers from sensory impairments and a profound sense of alienation from his own body and identity.
  • Experiences a metaphysical substitution of his skeleton and body, questioning his own existence.
  • His thoughts reflect a complex understanding of mortality and immortality, and he embraces darkness as a form of illumination.

Chapter 60

Listed as: Mr. Spitzer

  • Torn between his identity as a musician and as a gambler, Mr. Spitzer experiences profound self-doubt and alienation.
  • His focus on gambling and his distracted thoughts reveal a fragmented psyche, obsessed with trivial and profound matters simultaneously.
  • Despite his urban surroundings, he has a deep affinity for natural sounds, which evoke powerful emotional and spiritual responses.
  • Memories of his dead twin brother and lost musical compositions haunt him, reflecting a sense of unfinished business and loss.
  • Mr. Spitzer embodies the human struggle to find meaning through art, memory, and connection, even when confronted with mortality and oblivion.

Chapter 61

Listed as: Mr. Spitzer

  • An old, reflective, and solitary musician, possibly a lawyer turned silent observer of life.
  • He is contemplative, mourning not only the frog and the mute but also larger existential losses.
  • Characterized by his flowing black mourning cloak, and his slow, deliberate movements.
  • His music is described as unheard, symbolic of his isolation and the futility of his art in a silent world.

Chapter 62

Listed as: Mr. Joachim Spitzer

  • A retired lawyer and musician, described as a composer and conductor of a unique “water music.” He is deeply reflective, often dreaming and contemplating his life, music, and the cosmos. He appears to be a symbol of the artist’s struggle and existential quest.

Chapter 82

Listed as: Mr. Spitzer

  • A complex figure who reveals his true identity as Peron, the brother of Joachim. He has waited years to confess his love and to close a chapter on the past. His grief is profound, encompassing the loss of Catherine, the old clam-digger, and his own illusions.
