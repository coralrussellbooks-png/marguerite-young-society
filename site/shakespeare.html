Definition from research

Chapter 2

  • “great frogs with jewels in their foreheads”: An old belief held that the toad carried a precious stone, the toadstone, in its head, a cure for poison. Shakespeare used it in As You Like It: “Sweet are the uses of adversity, / Which, like the toad, ugly and venomous, / Wears yet a precious jewel in his head.”
  • “such as a dusty sparrow falling”: Jesus says of the sparrows, “one of them shall not fall on the ground without your Father” (Matthew 10:29), and Hamlet answers his friend’s fears with “There is special providence in the fall of a sparrow.” Catherine later counts “falling sparrows, falling leaves, drops of water, grains of sand.”
  • “If ears could see, if eyes could hear”: Bottom wakes from his enchanted night in A Midsummer Night’s Dream with the senses mixed up: “The eye of man hath not heard, the ear of man hath not seen, man’s hand is not able to taste, his tongue to conceive, nor his heart to report, what my dream was.” Bottom is parodying 1 Corinthians 2:9.

Chapter 3

  • “She dismissed the insubstantial fabric of the dream”: The phrase echoes Prospero in Shakespeare’s The Tempest: “And, like the baseless fabric of this vision, . . . this insubstantial pageant faded, / Leave not a rack behind. We are such stuff / As dreams are made on.”
  • “those who died but once, those who could not afford to die twice”: Julius Caesar says in Shakespeare’s play, “Cowards die many times before their deaths; / The valiant never taste of death but once.”
  • “our good works should not live after us”: Miss MacIntosh reverses Mark Antony in Shakespeare’s Julius Caesar: “The evil that men do lives after them; / The good is oft interred with their bones.”

Chapter 5

  • “seeing the breath on the mirror where there was no breath, the feather move where there was no feather”: Holding a mirror or a feather to the lips was the old test for death. King Lear, holding the dead Cordelia, calls, “Lend me a looking-glass; / If that her breath will mist or stain the stone, / Why, then she lives,” and then, “This feather stirs; she lives!”
  • “this womb of time”: Iago says, “There are many events in the womb of time which will be delivered” (Othello 1.3).
  • “in his second childhood”: In Shakespeare’s As You Like It, Jaques’s seven ages of man end in “second childishness and mere oblivion.”

Chapter 6

  • “To thine own self be true, and it will follow as the day the night”: Polonius gives this advice to his son Laertes in Hamlet (1.3): “This above all: to thine own self be true, / And it must follow, as the night the day, / Thou canst not then be false to any man.” Homer gets it backwards, “as the day the night.”
  • “come to the wedding feast and had stayed for the funeral”: Hamlet reversed: “the funeral baked meats / Did coldly furnish forth the marriage tables” (Hamlet 1.2).
  • “it had not been a marriage of true souls”: Shakespeare’s Sonnet 116: “Let me not to the marriage of true minds / Admit impediments. Love is not love / Which alters when it alteration finds.” Later Madge asks whether love can “alter.”
  • “Oh, Romeo, oh, Romeo!”: Juliet’s “O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?” (Romeo and Juliet 2.2).
  • “love is blind”: “But love is blind, and lovers cannot see” (The Merchant of Venice 2.6).
  • “Ding, dong, bell”: Ariel’s song for a drowned father in The Tempest: “Full fathom five thy father lies . . . Ding-dong, bell.” Madge’s father drowned. The nursery rhyme “Ding, dong, bell, / Pussy’s in the well” also ends in drowning.
  • “She was Juliet, and I was Romeo”: In the play Romeo kisses Juliet, believing her dead, and drinks poison before she wakes from the sleeping potion.

Chapter 7

  • “A fool, Lear in the storm”: In Shakespeare’s King Lear, the “storm” is both a physical and metaphorical force. Lear is exposed to a violent natural storm on the heath, symbolizing his internal turmoil and the chaos of his kingdom after he disowns his loyal supporters and his loving daughter Cordelia. This exposure forces him to confront his own mortality and the suffering of others, leading to a shift in his perspective and, ultimately, a descent into madness. It is a metaphorical description that situates old Doc in the tradition of tragic figures overwhelmed by chaos, emphasizing themes of madness, vulnerability, and existential struggle that run through the novel.
  • “I know old Gertrude from away back”: Gertrude may be named after the character in Hamlet by Shakespeare. Both Gertrudes embody the tragic consequences of familial and marital complexities: Miss MacIntosh’s Gertrude through literal murder and madness, Shakespeare’s Gertrude through moral ambiguity and political betrayal. They both represent the fragility of women’s positions within patriarchal and violent family systems, caught between roles as wives, mothers, and individuals subjected to forces beyond their control. Their characters explore themes of guilt, innocence, and the ambiguity of human motives, complicating simplistic moral judgments. Both have a presence associated with death and decay, whether physical or psychological, underscoring the novel’s and play’s meditation on mortality.
  • “no lock which could not be opened at last by the locksmith”: The proverb “Love laughs at locksmiths” comes from Shakespeare’s Venus and Adonis.
  • “the drunken sexton who used to dance a merry jig when anybody died”: Hamlet’s gravedigger sings at his work: “Has this fellow no feeling of his business, that he sings at grave-making?” (Hamlet 5.1).
  • “It was that skull the children played with”: “Alas, poor Yorick!” Hamlet holds the jester’s skull (Hamlet 5.1).
  • “She was Mrs. Hathaway, and she became Mrs. Hathaway”: Shakespeare’s wife was Anne Hathaway, a fitting married name for a Gertrude straight out of Hamlet.

Chapter 7

  • “What did he expect, marrying his brother’s wife”: Levirate marriage is the custom where the brother of a deceased man is obliged to marry his brother’s widow. (Wikipedia) Deuteronomy 25:5 commands it, and Leviticus 20:21 forbids it. Henry VIII used Leviticus to end his marriage to Catherine of Aragon, his brother’s widow. In Shakespeare’s Hamlet, Gertrude, Hamlet’s mother, marries her husband’s brother Claudius after the king’s death. Since women were completely dependent on their father, brother, or husband, they did not have much choice. In order to not be destitute they had to be attached. Even now women are poorer at retirement with less savings mainly because of missed years of income raising children. Social Security was partly founded to take care of these widows who wrote letters to FDR.

Chapter 8

  • “What happened to Julius Caesar?”: Julius Caesar was a Roman general and statesman. He was stabbed in the Senate in 44 B.C. by conspirators including Brutus, his friend, and Shakespeare gave him the words “Et tu, Brute?”
  • “Cyclops, men whose heads do grow beneath their shoulders”: Othello tells of “the Anthropophagi, and men whose heads / Do grow beneath their shoulders” (Othello 1.3). The Cyclops is the one-eyed giant of the Odyssey, like one-eyed Beatrice.

Chapter 9

  • “the canker eats the rose”: “And loathsome canker lives in sweetest bud” (Shakespeare, Sonnet 35).
  • “all local habitations and all names”: The poet “gives to airy nothing / A local habitation and a name” (A Midsummer Night’s Dream 5.1).

Chapter 10

  • “his brother worm who was under the ground”: Job says “to the worm, Thou art my mother, and my sister” (Job 17:14), and Hamlet says, “Your worm is your only emperor for diet” (Hamlet 4.3).
  • “What is man but a poor, two-legged animal?”: Plato defined man as a featherless biped, and Diogenes answered with a plucked chicken. Lear calls man “such a poor, bare, forked animal” (King Lear 3.4).
  • “Porpoises shall eat the drowned sailor. The waters of this world shall close over his head”: The preacher gives the bride and groom a sailor’s burial at sea. Milton’s “Lycidas” (1638) mourns a friend drowned in the Irish Sea, and Ariel sings of a drowned father in The Tempest: “Full fathom five thy father lies.” The line foreshadows Miss MacIntosh lost in the waves.

Chapter 11

  • “Pericles”: Pericles (c. 495-429 BC) led Athens in its golden age and had the Parthenon built.
  • “a society of night devoted exclusively to atheistic propositions”: “The school of night” comes from Love’s Labour’s Lost (4.3): “Black is the badge of hell, / The hue of dungeons and the school of night.” The phrase became a name for Raleigh’s circle with Thomas Harriot and Christopher Marlowe, men accused of doubting Genesis and God. In 1594 an inquiry at Cerne Abbas examined charges of atheism against Raleigh’s circle.

Chapter 12

  • “Juliet, 1899”: Maude Adams played Juliet at the Empire Theatre in New York in May 1899, opposite William Faversham. A dream in Chapter 6 has “She was Juliet, and I was Romeo.”
  • “we were the masterpieces of nature”: “What a piece of work is a man! … the beauty of the world, the paragon of animals!” (Hamlet 2.2).
  • “baying like a dog at the moon”: “I had rather be a dog, and bay the moon, / Than such a Roman” (Julius Caesar 4.3).
  • “should stir no feather in the air”: Lear holds a feather to Cordelia’s lips: “This feather stirs; she lives!” (King Lear 5.3).
  • “She could not soar upward on wings of words to heaven”: Claudius: “My words fly up, my thoughts remain below: / Words without thoughts never to heaven go” (Hamlet 3.3).

Chapter 13

  • “Ben Jonson”: Ben Jonson was a significant early modern English poet and playwright, known for his satirical comedies, masques, and poetry. He was a contemporary of Shakespeare, received a royal pension in 1616, which has led some to call him England’s first Poet Laureate, and is celebrated for plays like Volpone (a dark comedy about greed and corruption) and The Alchemist (a satire about fraud and human folly). “To Celia,” also known as “Drink to me only with thine eyes,” is a famous love song by Ben Jonson, first published in his 1616 collection The Forest. (Wikipedia)
  • “fixed like the North star”: “But I am constant as the northern star, / Of whose true-fix’d and resting quality / There is no fellow in the firmament” (Julius Caesar 3.1).
  • “my mother feigned death”: Juliet drinks the Friar’s potion to lie as if dead (Romeo and Juliet 4.1).
  • “not even the creaking of starlight”: Aristotle reports the Pythagorean answer: the spheres do make music, and people do not hear it because they have heard it since birth (On the Heavens 2.9). Lorenzo tells Jessica, “Such harmony is in immortal souls; / But whilst this muddy vesture of decay / Doth grossly close it in, we cannot hear it” (The Merchant of Venice 5.1).

Chapter 14

  • “himself the food of worms”: “Your worm is your only emperor for diet” (Hamlet 4.3).
  • “but what was a name?”: “What’s in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet” (Romeo and Juliet 2.2). Catherine says it of a servant whose name no one kept.
  • “their cosmic union in the womb of time”: “There are many events in the womb of time which will be delivered” (Othello 1.3).

Chapter 15

  • “that we know not who or what we are, not even when the hour is late”: Ophelia says, “Lord, we know what we are, but know not what we may be” (Hamlet 4.5). Miss MacIntosh’s version denies even the first half.
  • “Man was but a poor, forked animal”: Lear: “unaccommodated man is no more but such a poor, bare, forked animal as thou art” (King Lear 3.4).
  • “the fairest flower is plucked the earliest”: The Passionate Pilgrim (1599), published under Shakespeare’s name, has “Sweet rose, fair flower, untimely pluck’d, soon vaded.”
  • “still water runs deep”: “Still waters run deep” is a proverb of Latin origin now commonly taken to mean that a placid exterior hides a passionate or subtle nature. Formerly it also carried the warning that silent people are dangerous, as in Suffolk’s comment on a fellow lord in Shakespeare’s Henry VI, Part 2: “Smooth runs the water where the brook is deep” (3.1). (Wikipedia)

Chapter 16

  • “a rose by any other name would smell as sweet”: Romeo and Juliet 2.2. Catherine forgot the coachman’s name in Chapter 14, while Mr. Spitzer believes “all things were in their names.”
  • “there would be a ding and a dong and a bell through all the rooms”: The nursery rhyme “Ding, dong, bell, / Pussy’s in the well,” and Ariel’s song in The Tempest.

Chapter 17

  • “her corset stays sticking out like the quills of an angry porcupine”: The Ghost tells Hamlet his tale would make “each particular hair to stand on end, / Like quills upon the fretful porpentine” (Hamlet 1.5). A few lines later, “My hair crawled,” and soon Vera’s hair stands “on end.”
  • “that zero hour, the hour of midnight when strangest things do often happen”: “Zero hour” was the soldiers’ name for the hour of attack in the First World War. “’Tis now the very witching time of night, / When churchyards yawn” (Hamlet 3.2).
  • “who knows not the grinning skull?”: Yorick’s skull: “Where be your gibes now?… Not one now, to mock your own grinning?” (Hamlet 5.1).
  • “one rose which canker shall not eat”: “And loathsome canker lives in sweetest bud” (Shakespeare, Sonnet 35).

Chapter 18

  • “Never married, never buried. An old man’s love is a young maid’s sorrow”: Both lines are old proverbs, often found in folk traditions and songs, particularly in the context of warnings against marrying old men, as in the ballad “Maids When You’re Young Never Wed an Old Man” (Ballad Index). John Heywood’s Proverbs (1546) has the other side: “Better to be an old man’s darling than a young man’s warling.” This phrase encapsulates the sorrowful and ambiguous nature of love explored in the book, especially the idea that some loves are doomed to remain incomplete, bringing pain and sorrow rather than joy. It also hints at the novel’s recurring motif of lost or ghostly loves that continue to affect the living long after they have faded from ordinary life.
  • “a fool’s paradise”: The phrase is Middle English, and the earliest known use is in the Paston Letters of 1462. In Romeo and Juliet, the Nurse warns Romeo not to “lead her into a fool’s paradise” (2.4). Miss MacIntosh tells Vera she was “living in a fool’s paradise, and now you know there is no answer.”
  • Prospero’s “We are such stuff / As dreams are made on, and our little life / Is rounded with a sleep” (The Tempest 4.1) compares life to a dream.
  • “the true love is that which does not alter when it alteration finds”: Shakespeare wrote, “Love is not love / Which alters when it alteration finds… O no! it is an ever-fixed mark / That looks on tempests and is never shaken” (Sonnet 116). Young follows with “The true love wears an old shirt and is familiar.”
  • “where was Shakespeare?”: William Shakespeare was an English playwright, poet and actor.
  • “Watchman, what of the night? Who goes there?”: “Watchman, what of the night?” (Isaiah 21:11), and the opening of Hamlet, “Who’s there?”
  • “Out, out, brief candle!”: “Out, out, brief candle! / Life’s but a walking shadow” (Macbeth 5.5).
  • “we are sinned against more than we are sinning”: “I am a man / More sinned against than sinning” (King Lear 3.2).
  • “to stand with one foot in the sea and one foot on the shore”: “Men were deceivers ever, / One foot in sea and one on shore, / To one thing constant never” (Much Ado About Nothing 2.3).
  • “the king of rags and patches”: Hamlet calls his uncle “A king of shreds and patches” (Hamlet 3.4).
  • “most men are conceived in the night and die with the ebbing of the tide”: In Dickens’s David Copperfield (1850), Mr. Peggotty says of the dying Barkis, “People can’t die, along the coast, except when the tide’s pretty nigh out.” Falstaff died “ev’n at the turning o’ th’ tide” (Henry V 2.3).

Chapter 18 · The Brief Candle

  • Classic metaphor for the fragility and brevity of life.

Chapter 19

  • “my strange bedfellow”: “Misery acquaints a man with strange bedfellows” (The Tempest 2.2).
  • “it’s an ill wind that blows nobody good”: John Heywood’s proverb (1546). Falstaff’s man Pistol says, “Not the ill wind which blows no man to good” (Henry IV, Part 2 5.3).

Chapter 20

  • “sent shivering like ghosts at dawn”: The ghost in Hamlet “faded on the crowing of the cock” (Hamlet 1.1).

Chapter 21

  • “that love is love which is love forevermore and unchanging, which does not alter with the changing circumstance”: “Love is not love / Which alters when it alteration finds” (Sonnet 116), as in Chapter 18.
  • “The Devil could quote his Scriptures, too, chapter and verse for his purpose”: “The devil can cite Scripture for his purpose” (The Merchant of Venice 1.3). In the temptation, Satan quotes Psalm 91 to Christ (Matthew 4:6).
  • “The greatest sorrows were those not described”: Seneca’s Phaedra has “Curae leves loquuntur, ingentes stupent” (“Light griefs speak, great ones are struck dumb”). Malcolm tells Macduff, “Give sorrow words: the grief that does not speak / Whispers the o’er-fraught heart and bids it break” (Macbeth 4.3).

Chapter 21 · Love as Eternal and Unchanging

  • “That love is love which… does not alter with the changing circumstance” echoes Sonnet 116, as in Chapter 18.
  • Vera emphasizes that true love remains constant despite changing circumstances. Love “does not ebb, does not wane,” symbolizing an ideal of permanence in an impermanent world.
  • This eternal love contrasts with the physical and emotional struggles depicted, underscoring a spiritual or emotional steadfastness.

Chapter 22

  • “it was a wise child who knew his own father”: “It is a wise father that knows his own child” (The Merchant of Venice 2.2). Telemachus says, “My mother says I am his son; but I know not, for no man knows his own father” (Odyssey 1).
  • “I know no Greek, less Latin, less Hebrew”: Ben Jonson said Shakespeare had “small Latine, and lesse Greeke” (First Folio, 1623).
  • “Here’s a horse of a different color”: “My purpose is, indeed, a horse of that colour” (Twelfth Night 2.3).
  • “those who fall in love are always blind”: “But love is blind, and lovers cannot see” (The Merchant of Venice 2.6).
  • “What dead king lies on the balcony at Elsinore?”: Hamlet’s father, whose ghost walks the platform at Elsinore (Hamlet 1.1). At the end Fortinbras orders the bodies “high on a stage be placed to the view” (5.2).
  • “Who ever saw the ghost of his father?”: Hamlet’s father’s ghost (Hamlet 1.1-1.5).
  • “It takes more than clothes to make the man”: It means that a person’s character, personality, and true worth cannot be judged solely by their appearance or what they wear. A person’s inner qualities and actions are what truly define them, not just their outward appearance. This saying directly contradicts the proverb “clothes make the man.” Polonius: “the apparel oft proclaims the man” (Hamlet 1.3).

Chapter 24

  • “Spinoza and Shakespeare and Mrs. Sydington were already in the green”: Spinoza was a Dutch philosopher, author of the Ethics (1677). Shakespeare was a famous British playwright.
  • “a sea change, a transformation”: “Nothing of him that doth fade, / But doth suffer a sea-change / Into something rich and strange” (The Tempest 1.2).

Chapter 25

  • “dead sparrows falling”: “One of them shall not fall on the ground without your Father” (Matthew 10:29), and Hamlet’s “There’s a special providence in the fall of a sparrow” (5.2).
  • “having been the jester with bells”: The court fool’s cap and bells. In Yeats’s “The Cap and Bells” (1894), a jester wins the queen only by sending her his cap and bells.

Chapter 26

  • “when we should have put off this mortal clay”: “Thou hast made me as the clay; and wilt thou bring me into dust again?” (Job 10:9), and Hamlet’s “When we have shuffled off this mortal coil” (3.1).
  • “a bouquet of winter violets”: Ophelia’s “I would give you some violets, but they withered all when my father died” (Hamlet 4.5). Violets were a flower of faithfulness.

Chapter 27

  • “had died a thousand deaths”: “Cowards die many times before their deaths; / The valiant never taste of death but once” (Julius Caesar 2.2).

Chapter 29

  • “to shuffle off these mortal coils”: “When we have shuffled off this mortal coil” (Hamlet 3.1).
  • “wild goose chases and pretty chicks… pigs in the poke”: Mercutio’s “Nay, if our wits run the wild-goose chase” (Romeo and Juliet 2.4). A “pig in a poke” is a bargain bought unseen in its sack.
  • “the similitude of death, its twin brother”: Sleep and Death are twins in Hesiod (Chapter 25), and sleep is “death’s counterfeit” in Macbeth 2.3.

Chapter 30

  • “where Childe Roland had blown his horn before a dark tower”: Childe Roland is a character in a poem by Robert Browning. The character Roland in the poem makes a journey to the Dark Tower much like the journey of Pilgrim’s Progress and the Divine Comedy. The name Child Roland comes from the Shakespeare play King Lear Act 3 Scene 4 Lines 195-197. Browning’s poem is “Childe Roland to the Dark Tower Came” (1855). The horn also recalls Roland of The Song of Roland, who blew his horn Oliphant at Roncevaux (778).

Chapter 31

  • “in love with no man on earth but with a fleeting shadow, the mere shadow of a man”: Shadows in scripture and Shakespeare.

Chapter 32

  • “worse than enchantment by an Eastern drug or spiritous liquor or witches’ brew”: The Eastern drug is opium, Catherine’s own enchantment. The witches’ brew is the cauldron of Macbeth: “Double, double toil and trouble; / Fire burn and cauldron bubble” (4.1).
  • “these hollow men, these advocates of lost causes, these men of straw, these things of naught”: “But hollow men, like horses hot at hand, / Make gallant show and promise of their mettle” (Shakespeare, Julius Caesar 4.2). Matthew Arnold called Oxford the “home of lost causes” (Essays in Criticism, 1865), and “a thing of naught” is Flute’s phrase in A Midsummer Night’s Dream (4.2).

Chapter 33

  • “The sere stars shook like autumn leaves”: “My way of life / Is fall’n into the sear, the yellow leaf” (Macbeth 5.3).
  • “never to join in the chorus of this dumb-show”: A dumb-show, also known as a pantomime or mime, is a dramatic performance using gestures and body movements without words, often used to summarize, supplement, or comment on the main action of a play. Hamlet’s players perform a dumb-show before The Murder of Gonzago (3.2).

Chapter 34

  • “Men die of many reasons before they die of love”: “Men have died from time to time, and worms have eaten them, but not for love” (Rosalind, As You Like It 4.1).
  • “beetling cliffs”: “The dreadful summit of the cliff / That beetles o’er his base into the sea” (Hamlet 1.4).
  • “as ineffectual as the poorest gallows birds with their chains gibbering”: “The sheeted dead / Did squeak and gibber in the Roman streets” (Hamlet 1.1).
  • “this mortal stage, my mother’s bedroom”: “All the world’s a stage” (As You Like It 2.7).

Chapter 35

  • “pitched her tents in strange lands from which no living man returns”: “The undiscover’d country from whose bourn / No traveller returns” (Shakespeare, Hamlet 3.1).
  • “a dream retreating at cockcrow”: Hamlet’s father’s ghost “faded on the crowing of the cock” (Shakespeare, Hamlet 1.1).
  • “She had caused many a flutter in many a dovecote”: “Like an eagle in a dove-cote, I / Flutter’d your Volscians in Corioli” (Shakespeare, Coriolanus 5.6).
  • “toppling cloud-topped towers”: “The cloud-capp’d towers, the gorgeous palaces, / The solemn temples, the great globe itself, / Yea, all which it inherit, shall dissolve” (Shakespeare, The Tempest 4.1).

Chapter 36

  • “There’s not the smallest orb which thou behold’st / But in his motion like an angel sings… But whilst this muddy vesture of decay / Doth grossly close it in, we cannot hear it” (Shakespeare, The Merchant of Venice 5.1).
  • “Hers was undoubtedly the logic of madness”: “Though this be madness, yet there is method in’t” (Shakespeare, Hamlet 2.2).
  • “The infant should be an old man crying in the storm”: “Blow, winds, and crack your cheeks! rage! blow!” (Shakespeare, King Lear 3.2), the old king crying in the storm.

Chapter 39

  • “coming with her petard”: A petard was a bomb for blowing in a gate. “For ’tis the sport to have the engineer / Hoist with his own petar” (Shakespeare, Hamlet 3.4).
  • Shakespeare’s Coriolanus (1608) is the proud general who scorns the people.
  • “take arms against a sea of troubles”: “Take arms against a sea of troubles” is from Hamlet, Act III, Scene I, the famous soliloquy “To be, or not to be.” The metaphor resonates with the novel’s exploration of the ambiguous and often turbulent nature of existence, where the “sea of troubles” can be seen as the emotional and existential storms that engulf the characters. Taking up arms is a way of asserting agency and maintaining one’s integrity amid the chaos.

Chapter 40

  • “Cousin Hannah had also spoken the King’s English”: “Here will be an old abusing of God’s patience and the King’s English” (Shakespeare, The Merry Wives of Windsor 1.4).
  • “the roaring, silent sea of dreams where all should change”: “Nothing of him that doth fade, / But doth suffer a sea-change / Into something rich and strange” (Shakespeare, The Tempest 1.2).

Chapter 41

  • “mad Antony’s love”: Mad Antony’s love may refer to Mark Antony’s obsession with women, above all Cleopatra, for whom he lost Rome. In Shakespeare’s Antony and Cleopatra his dotage is “the bellows and the fan / To cool a gipsy’s lust” (1.1). He had five wives and fathered many children.
  • “nature was unknitting her”: Macbeth’s “Sleep that knits up the ravell’d sleave of care” (2.2).
  • “less than the fabric of her dreams”: Prospero’s “the baseless fabric of this vision” and “We are such stuff / As dreams are made on” (The Tempest 4.1).
  • “more vulnerable to the arrows of chance”: “The slings and arrows of outrageous fortune” (Hamlet 3.1).
  • “that from which no traveller returns”: “The undiscover’d country from whose bourn / No traveller returns” (Hamlet 3.1).

Chapter 42

  • “this whirligig of leaves and stars”: “And thus the whirligig of time brings in his revenges” (Twelfth Night 5.1).
  • “Perhaps the secret casket contained a vapor and not a jewel”: Morocco opens the golden casket and finds a skull: “All that glisters is not gold” (The Merchant of Venice 2.7).

Chapter 43

  • “Then put out the light”: This echoes Othello, Act 5, Scene 2, where Othello stands over the sleeping Desdemona and says “Put out the light, and then put out the light,” before smothering her in her bed. Several things in Cousin Hannah’s deathbed cries point to the play: she calls to a “Black Moor, beloved of woman,” she asks whether the turban-winder has come “to strangle me so that I cannot cry out,” and she lies in a bed that has become a deathbed. The suffrage captain, who spent her life refusing the role of wife, casts herself at the end as Shakespeare’s most famous murdered wife.
  • “Ah, Black Moor, beloved of woman”: Moors was a term first used by European Christians to designate the Muslim inhabitants of Maghreb, Iberian Peninsula, Malta, and Sicily during the Middle Ages (Wikipedia). Othello is “the Moor of Venice.” The Black Moor becomes a black rooster.

Chapter 43 · Black Moor / Blackamoor

  • A mysterious figure, possibly a lover or phantom, representing the suffragette’s complex relationships and desires.

Chapter 44

  • “the shining toils of memory”: Toils are nets for catching game. Octavius says of the dead Cleopatra that “she looks like sleep, / As she would catch another Antony / In her strong toil of grace” (Antony and Cleopatra 5.2).
  • “withered Cleopatra”: Young reverses Enobarbus: “Age cannot wither her, nor custom stale / Her infinite variety” (Antony and Cleopatra 2.2). See mad Antony in the Chapter 41 resources.

Chapter 45

  • “Put out the light, she said.”: See Othello’s “Put out the light” in the Chapter 43 resources.
  • “Here was the noblest Roman of them all.”: Antony over the body of Brutus: “This was the noblest Roman of them all” (Julius Caesar 5.5).
  • “a toad with eyes like lamps”: See the toad that “Wears yet a precious jewel in his head” (As You Like It 2.1) in the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “Ah, traitor moon!”: “O, swear not by the moon, th’ inconstant moon, / That monthly changes in her circled orb” (Romeo and Juliet 2.2).

Chapter 46

  • “ghosts have seldom or never appeared naked in men’s dreams”: Hamlet’s father appears “in complete steel” on the battlements and later “in his habit as he lived” (Hamlet 1.4, 3.4).
  • “The cat will mew, and dog will have his day” (Hamlet 5.1). The dog days of late summer are named for Sirius, the Dog Star, which appears earlier in this chapter.
  • “Some were killed by a rain drop”: The steeplejack’s litany recalls Richard II: “let us sit upon the ground / And tell sad stories of the death of kings: / How some have been depos’d; some slain in war” (3.2).

Chapter 47

  • “good-night, sweet prince”: “Good night, sweet prince, / And flights of angels sing thee to thy rest!” (Hamlet 5.2). It signifies a final, loving farewell to a cherished figure who has endured great trials and is now moving beyond mortal existence. It reflects themes of mortality, transition, and the enduring nature of love even beyond death. This farewell is both personal and universal, blending sorrow with a quiet hope for peace in the beyond.
  • “I am the Ethiope”: “Ethiope” is Shakespeare’s word: Romeo sees Juliet hang “upon the cheek of night / Like a rich jewel in an Ethiope’s ear” (Romeo and Juliet 1.5), and in A Midsummer Night’s Dream Lysander insults the dark Hermia, “Away, you Ethiope!” (3.2). “Can the Ethiopian change his skin, or the leopard his spots?” (Jeremiah 13:23). The phrase is embedded in a passage describing various mystical and spectral figures, blending historical, mythical, and symbolic references. It reflects the theme of multiplicity and transformation of self, the blending of identities across time and space, and the difficulty of pinpointing a single, fixed identity or reality. The declaration “I am the Ethiope” symbolizes one aspect of this fluid, shifting self: an identity that is part of a broader cosmic and spiritual transformation, often with connotations of being an outsider or other.
  • “Should one moment of inestimable madness dethrone that king”: “O, let me not be mad, not mad, sweet heaven! / Keep me in temper; I would not be mad!” (King Lear 1.5).

Chapter 48

  • “out, out of this life”: “Out, out, brief candle! / Life’s but a walking shadow” (Macbeth 5.5). Mr. Spitzer speaks it with a match burning in his hand.
  • “good-night, sweet prince and lady”: See “good-night, sweet prince” and “good-night, my lady” in the Chapter 47 resources.
  • “for this life was a long rehearsal of music for the silence”: Life is a rehearsal, a practice, for the silence of death. The “music” is the experience of a life, passing and temporary; the “silence” is death, where all of it ceases, and which “could surely not answer or reproach.” Socrates says that “the true philosophers, Simmias, are always occupied in the practice of dying” (Plato, Phaedo 67e, trans. Benjamin Jowett, 1871), and Montaigne titled an essay “That to philosophize is to learn to die” (Essays 1.20, 1580). Hamlet’s last words are “the rest is silence” (5.2).
  • “laws of undiscovered countries”: “The undiscover’d country from whose bourn / No traveller returns” (Hamlet 3.1) is death.
  • “this dread henbane of human life”: The ghost of Hamlet’s father was poisoned “With juice of cursed hebenon in a vial, / And in the porches of mine ears did pour / The leperous distilment” (Hamlet 1.5); hebenon is often read as henbane.
  • “Perhaps he was translated”: “By faith Enoch was translated that he should not see death” (Hebrews 11:5); to be translated is to be carried to heaven without dying. “Bless thee, Bottom, bless thee! thou art translated” (A Midsummer Night’s Dream 3.1).

Chapter 49

  • “the griffin with its wings of gold”: Griffins were said to guard the gold of the far north (Herodotus 3.116). “The smallest worm will turn being trodden on” (Henry VI, Part 3, 2.2), but this worm never turned.

Chapter 51

  • Eros is a concept in ancient Greek philosophy referring to sensual or passionate love. It has been used in philosophy and psychology as an equivalent to life energy. “Love looks not with the eyes, but with the mind; / And therefore is wing’d Cupid painted blind” (A Midsummer Night’s Dream 1.1).

Chapter 52

  • “that old jack of all trades who was the master of none”: The saying “Jack of all trades, master of none” grew from older forms; in 1592 Robert Greene sneered at the young Shakespeare as a Johannes factotum, a Jack of all trades.
  • “she could not have worn her heart upon her sleeve”: Iago says in Shakespeare’s Othello, “But I will wear my heart upon my sleeve / For daws to peck at” (1.1).
  • “Love knew no age”: Jessica says in Shakespeare’s The Merchant of Venice, “But love is blind, and lovers cannot see / The pretty follies that themselves commit” (2.6). Cupid was sometimes depicted blindfolded; Erwin Panofsky traced the image in “Blind Cupid,” in Studies in Iconology (1939).

Chapter 53

  • “old Dan Cupid, the wizened cherub, the blind god of love”: Dan was an old title of respect, from the Latin dominus; Berowne calls him “Dan Cupid” in Shakespeare’s Love’s Labour’s Lost (3.1). See blind Cupid in the Chapter 52 resources.
  • “a queen of the desert sands had played billiards”: The “queen of the desert” who played pool may refer to Cleopatra in Shakespeare’s play Antony and Cleopatra where she is bored and asks Charmian to play billiards. “Let it alone; let’s to billiards: come, Charmian” (2.5), an anachronism, since billiards is a much later game.
  • “a former featherweight who had thought, every time he entered the ring, he was battling his father’s ghost”: The fighter retired from the ring and lived in isolation believing he had killed his father’s ghost when he killed that opponent. This is similar to the plot line in the Shakespeare play Hamlet where the ghost is Hamlet’s dead father.

Chapter 54

  • “his wife had cuckolded him”: A cuckold is the husband of an adulterous wife (or partner for unmarried companions); the wife of an adulterous husband is a cuckquean. In biology, a cuckold is a male who unwittingly invests parental effort in juveniles who are not genetically his offspring. A husband who is aware of and tolerates his wife’s infidelity is sometimes called a wittol or wittold. The slang term bull refers to the dominant man who has relations with the cuckold’s partner.
  • Lord Banquo is a character in Shakespeare’s play MacBeth. He is at first an ally of Macbeth and they meet the Three Witches together. After prophesying that Macbeth will become king, the witches tell Banquo that he will not be king himself, but that his descendants will be. Later, Macbeth in his lust for power sees Banquo as a threat and has him murdered by three hired assassins.
  • “My kingdom for a horse”: This line is from the play Richard III by Shakespeare. It reflects the desperate yearning for a way forward amid confusion, loss, and the labyrinthine journey through memory, identity, and the dream-like world. It conveys the idea of sacrificing everything for the ability to move, to find something lost (such as the bride’s skirt or love), or to regain agency in a disorienting and dreamlike existence.

Chapter 55

  • “Should the alteration recognize that it was altered?”: Young turns Shakespeare’s “Love is not love / Which alters when it alteration finds” (William Shakespeare, Sonnet 116, 1609).
  • “cast off, as he would say, these mortal coils”: “When we have shuffled off this mortal coil, / Must give us pause” (William Shakespeare, Hamlet, 3.1).

Chapter 56

  • “all kinds of head coverings including the ass’s head”: In Shakespeare’s A Midsummer Night’s Dream, Puck gives the weaver Bottom an ass’s head, and Titania, under a love spell, falls in love with him (3.1).
  • “the glass of fashion and the powdered fop”: Ophelia remembers Hamlet as “The glass of fashion and the mould of form” (William Shakespeare, Hamlet, 3.1).
  • “How often had he died”: “Cowards die many times before their deaths; / The valiant never taste of death but once” (William Shakespeare, Julius Caesar, 2.2).
  • “The mandrakes screamed”: The mandrake root, forked like a human body, was said to shriek when pulled from the ground and to kill or madden whoever heard it: “shrieks like mandrakes torn out of the earth, / That living mortals, hearing them, run mad” (William Shakespeare, Romeo and Juliet, 4.3).
  • “Those were pearls that were his eyes”: Ariel sings of the drowned king: “Full fathom five thy father lies; / Of his bones are coral made; / Those are pearls that were his eyes” (William Shakespeare, The Tempest, 1.2).

Chapter 57

  • “alert interest and baited breath”: Young’s spelling puns on the usual phrase, from Shylock: “With bated breath and whispering humbleness” (William Shakespeare, The Merchant of Venice, 1.3). Bated means held back, abated.
  • “knowing, also, that dark is fair, that fair is dark”: “Fair is foul, and foul is fair” (William Shakespeare, Macbeth, 1.1).
  • “He had seen a cloud like a great skull”: Compare Hamlet teasing Polonius: “Do you see yonder cloud that’s almost in shape of a camel?” (William Shakespeare, Hamlet, 3.2).

Chapter 58

  • “killing the song sparrow in his fall”: “There is special providence in the fall of a sparrow” (William Shakespeare, Hamlet, 5.2), after “one of them shall not fall on the ground without your Father” (Matthew 10:29, KJV).
  • “a tree with its bare branches filled with singing birds”: Young reverses Shakespeare’s winter tree: “Bare ruin’d choirs, where late the sweet birds sang” (William Shakespeare, Sonnet 73, 1609).
  • “they were those who wore their hearts upon their sleeves”: “I will wear my heart upon my sleeve / For daws to peck at” (William Shakespeare, Othello, 1.1).
  • “the star lilies swimming through dark waters”: Young’s star lilies are crinoids, sea lilies and feather stars, animals that look like flowers. A feather star begins life on a stalk like a lily and then breaks free and swims. Their “sea-change” is Ariel’s: “Nothing of him that doth fade / But doth suffer a sea-change / Into something rich and strange” (William Shakespeare, The Tempest, 1.2).

Chapter 59

  • “the red rose of the sun or the white rose of the moon”: Compare the Temple Garden scene of Henry VI, Part 1, where the lords of York pluck white roses and the lords of Lancaster red, the start of the Wars of the Roses (William Shakespeare, 2.4).
  • “this undiscovered shore”: Compare Hamlet’s “undiscover’d country from whose bourn / No traveller returns” (William Shakespeare, Hamlet, 3.1).
  • “that which has a local habitation and a name”: The poet “gives to airy nothing / A local habitation and a name” (William Shakespeare, A Midsummer Night’s Dream, 5.1).

Chapter 61

  • “a silver helmet with beaver down to hide his face”: The beaver is the face guard of a helmet; of the ghost of Hamlet’s father, “he wore his beaver up” (William Shakespeare, Hamlet, 1.2).
  • “Abusing of God’s patience and the king’s English” (William Shakespeare, The Merry Wives of Windsor, 1.4).

Chapter 62

  • “add to his music of aubade and serenade his nocturne”: Aubade is a song or poem that greets the dawn, traditionally a morning love song about lovers parting at daybreak. The term, from the Old Occitan word auba meaning “dawn,” can also refer to morning music or a song sung outdoors at dawn as a compliment. The concept has been used in literature for centuries, notably in medieval France, and also appears as a subject for visual art, like the Picasso painting The Aubade. “It was the nightingale, and not the lark” (William Shakespeare, Romeo and Juliet, 3.5).

Chapter 63

  • “a kind of fool’s paradise”: “If ye should lead her into a fool’s paradise” (William Shakespeare, Romeo and Juliet, 2.4).
  • “There shall be bare and ruined choirs”: “Bare ruined choirs, where late the sweet birds sang” (William Shakespeare, Sonnet 73).
  • “it’s a wise child who knows his own father”: Shakespeare turns the proverb around: “It is a wise father that knows his own child” (The Merchant of Venice, 2.2).
  • “Here’s the livid skull that might be any fool’s or king’s”: “Alas, poor Yorick! I knew him, Horatio” (William Shakespeare, Hamlet, 5.1).
  • “a plague, a plague, a light, a light!”: Compare “A plague o’ both your houses!” (Romeo and Juliet, 3.1) and the king’s cry “Give me some light. Away!” (Hamlet, 3.2).

Chapter 65

  • “Romeo and Juliet, Paul and Virginia, Eloise and Abelard, Tristram and the white Yseult”: Romeo and Juliet is a tragedy written by William Shakespeare early in his career about two young Italian star-crossed lovers whose deaths ultimately reconcile their feuding families. (Wikipedia)

Chapter 66

  • “born in Denmark hard by the castle of Elsinore”: The description situates his origin geographically and culturally: he is from Denmark, specifically near Elsinore Castle, which is famously known as the setting of Shakespeare’s play Hamlet. Elsinore Castle is a real fortress located in Helsingør, Denmark. Like Hamlet, he is a Dane marked by tragedy: he “had left home because of a great tragedy.”

Chapter 68

  • “Dark Lady was winning”: The racehorse’s name recalls the “Dark Lady” of Shakespeare’s Sonnets 127 to 152, the mysterious woman of the poet’s desire.
  • “the mandrakes screaming when they are pulled up by the roots”: This folklore is based on the mandrake plant which is a hallucinogenic and narcotic. It is said that it grows from the blood of a hanged man and the tuber can resemble a human form. Forensic medicine does record erection and the discharge of semen in some deaths by hanging, and the folklore of the gallows mandrake grew from it. Juliet fears “shrieks like mandrakes’ torn out of the earth” (Romeo and Juliet 4.3), and in Genesis Rachel trades for Leah’s mandrakes to bear a child (Genesis 30:14-16).

Chapter 69

  • “a canker in the rose immortal”: “And loathsome canker lives in sweetest bud” (Shakespeare, Sonnet 35). Compare Blake’s “The Sick Rose” (1794), destroyed by “The invisible worm.”
  • “a wormwood”: Culturally wormwood has been used in the Christian Bible to connote something incredibly bitter. In the Book of Revelation it is used as the name of the star. In Hamlet, he uses the word wormwood to describe the situation he believes is going on with his family. “And the name of the star is called Wormwood” (Revelation 8:11, KJV).

Chapter 70

  • “A rose by any other name would smell as sweet”: “What’s in a name? that which we call a rose / By any other name would smell as sweet” (Romeo and Juliet 2.2). Mr. Spitzer answers the same way at the end of the chapter: “What’s in a name?”

Chapter 72

  • “Johnny-jump-ups springing in her path”: The Johnny-jump-up, the wild pansy, is also called heartsease; in A Midsummer Night’s Dream it is love-in-idleness, whose juice makes the sleeper love the first creature seen on waking (2.1).

Chapter 73

  • “too, too solid for his tastes”: “O, that this too too solid flesh would melt, / Thaw and resolve itself into a dew!” (Hamlet, 1.2). Dew falls all through this chapter.

Chapter 76

  • “thin as a comma sleeping in the womb of time”: “There are many events in the womb of time which will be delivered” (Iago, Othello, 1.3).
  • “by Cupid, the blind god of love”: In classical mythology, Cupid is the god of desire, erotic love, attraction and affection. “And therefore is wing’d Cupid painted blind” (A Midsummer Night’s Dream, 1.1).

Chapter 78

  • “Poor, poor little thing, poor little other one who would have loved her mother so much, Rosemary”: “There’s rosemary, that’s for remembrance; pray, love, remember” (Ophelia, Hamlet, 4.5). Esther’s mother remembers Rosemary all her life.

Chapter 79

  • “the times were out of joint and she the dead”: “The time is out of joint: O cursed spite, / That ever I was born to set it right!” (Hamlet, 1.5).
  • “The bleeding hand of a dead child, lifting, might testify against her”: By the old ordeal of the bier, a murdered body was believed to bleed afresh when its murderer came near. In Richard III, Henry VI’s corpse bleeds before Gloucester: “Dead Henry’s wounds / Open their congeal’d mouths and bleed afresh!” (1.2).

Chapter 81

  • “gently rocking in the vasty deep”: “I can call spirits from the vasty deep. / Why, so can I, or so can any man; / But will they come when you do call for them?” (Henry IV, Part 1, 3.1).
