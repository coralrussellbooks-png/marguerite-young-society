Definition from research

Chapter 2

  • “great frogs with jewels in their foreheads”: An old belief held that the toad carried a precious stone, the toadstone, in its head, a cure for poison. Shakespeare used it in As You Like It: “Sweet are the uses of adversity, / Which, like the toad, ugly and venomous, / Wears yet a precious jewel in his head.”
  • “such as a dusty sparrow falling”: Jesus says of the sparrows, “one of them shall not fall on the ground without your Father” (Matthew 10:29), and Hamlet answers his friend’s fears with “There is special providence in the fall of a sparrow.” Catherine later counts “falling sparrows, falling leaves, drops of water, grains of sand.”
  • “If ears could see, if eyes could hear”: Bottom wakes from his enchanted night in A Midsummer Night’s Dream with the senses mixed up: “The eye of man hath not heard, the ear of man hath not seen, man’s hand is not able to taste, his tongue to conceive, nor his heart to report, what my dream was.” Bottom is parodying 1 Corinthians 2:9.

Chapter 3

  • “She dismissed the insubstantial fabric of the dream”: The phrase echoes Prospero in Shakespeare’s The Tempest: “And, like the baseless fabric of this vision, . . . this insubstantial pageant faded, / Leave not a rack behind. We are such stuff / As dreams are made on.”
  • “those who died but once, those who could not afford to die twice”: Julius Caesar says in Shakespeare’s play, “Cowards die many times before their deaths; / The valiant never taste of death but once.”
  • “our good works should not live after us”: Miss MacIntosh reverses Mark Antony in Shakespeare’s Julius Caesar: “The evil that men do lives after them; / The good is oft interred with their bones.”

Chapter 5

  • “seeing the breath on the mirror where there was no breath, the feather move where there was no feather”: Holding a mirror or a feather to the lips was the old test for death. King Lear, holding the dead Cordelia, calls, “Lend me a looking-glass; / If that her breath will mist or stain the stone, / Why, then she lives,” and then, “This feather stirs; she lives!”
  • “this womb of time”: Iago says, “There are many events in the womb of time which will be delivered” (Othello 1.3).
  • “in his second childhood”: In Shakespeare’s As You Like It, Jaques’s seven ages of man end in “second childishness and mere oblivion.”

Chapter 6

  • “To thine own self be true, and it will follow as the day the night”: Polonius gives this advice to his son Laertes in Hamlet (1.3): “This above all: to thine own self be true, / And it must follow, as the night the day, / Thou canst not then be false to any man.” Homer gets it backwards, “as the day the night.”
  • “come to the wedding feast and had stayed for the funeral”: Hamlet reversed: “the funeral baked meats / Did coldly furnish forth the marriage tables” (Hamlet 1.2).
  • “it had not been a marriage of true souls”: Shakespeare’s Sonnet 116: “Let me not to the marriage of true minds / Admit impediments. Love is not love / Which alters when it alteration finds.” Later Madge asks whether love can “alter.”
  • “Oh, Romeo, oh, Romeo!”: Juliet’s “O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?” (Romeo and Juliet 2.2).
  • “love is blind”: “But love is blind, and lovers cannot see” (The Merchant of Venice 2.6).
  • “Ding, dong, bell”: Ariel’s song for a drowned father in The Tempest: “Full fathom five thy father lies . . . Ding-dong, bell.” Madge’s father drowned. The nursery rhyme “Ding, dong, bell, / Pussy’s in the well” also ends in drowning.
  • “She was Juliet, and I was Romeo”: In the play Romeo kisses Juliet, believing her dead, and drinks poison before she wakes from the sleeping potion.

Chapter 7

  • “A fool, Lear in the storm”: In Shakespeare’s King Lear, the “storm” is both a physical and metaphorical force. Lear is exposed to a violent natural storm on the heath, symbolizing his internal turmoil and the chaos of his kingdom after he disowns his loyal supporters and his loving daughter Cordelia. This exposure forces him to confront his own mortality and the suffering of others, leading to a shift in his perspective and, ultimately, a descent into madness. It is a metaphorical description that situates old Doc in the tradition of tragic figures overwhelmed by chaos, emphasizing themes of madness, vulnerability, and existential struggle that run through the novel.
  • “I know old Gertrude from away back”: Gertrude may be named after the character in Hamlet by Shakespeare. Both Gertrudes embody the tragic consequences of familial and marital complexities: Miss MacIntosh’s Gertrude through literal murder and madness, Shakespeare’s Gertrude through moral ambiguity and political betrayal. They both represent the fragility of women’s positions within patriarchal and violent family systems, caught between roles as wives, mothers, and individuals subjected to forces beyond their control. Their characters explore themes of guilt, innocence, and the ambiguity of human motives, complicating simplistic moral judgments. Both have a presence associated with death and decay, whether physical or psychological, underscoring the novel’s and play’s meditation on mortality.
  • “no lock which could not be opened at last by the locksmith”: The proverb “Love laughs at locksmiths” comes from Shakespeare’s Venus and Adonis.
  • “the drunken sexton who used to dance a merry jig when anybody died”: Hamlet’s gravedigger sings at his work: “Has this fellow no feeling of his business, that he sings at grave-making?” (Hamlet 5.1).
  • “It was that skull the children played with”: “Alas, poor Yorick!” Hamlet holds the jester’s skull (Hamlet 5.1).
  • “She was Mrs. Hathaway, and she became Mrs. Hathaway”: Shakespeare’s wife was Anne Hathaway, a fitting married name for a Gertrude straight out of Hamlet.

Chapter 7

  • “What did he expect, marrying his brother’s wife”: Levirate marriage is the custom where the brother of a deceased man is obliged to marry his brother’s widow. Deuteronomy 25:5 commands it, and Leviticus 20:21 forbids it. Henry VIII used Leviticus to end his marriage to Catherine of Aragon, his brother’s widow. In Shakespeare’s Hamlet, Gertrude, Hamlet’s mother, marries her husband’s brother Claudius after the king’s death. Since women were completely dependent on their father, brother, or husband, they did not have much choice. In order to not be destitute they had to be attached. Even now women are poorer at retirement with less savings mainly because of missed years of income raising children. Social Security was partly founded to take care of these widows who wrote letters to FDR.

Chapter 8

  • “What happened to Julius Caesar?”: Julius Caesar was a Roman general and statesman. He was stabbed in the Senate in 44 B.C. by conspirators including Brutus, his friend, and Shakespeare gave him the words “Et tu, Brute?”
  • “Cyclops, men whose heads do grow beneath their shoulders”: Othello tells of “the Anthropophagi, and men whose heads / Do grow beneath their shoulders” (Othello 1.3). The Cyclops is the one-eyed giant of the Odyssey, like one-eyed Beatrice.

Chapter 9

Chapter 10

  • “his brother worm who was under the ground”: Job says “to the worm, Thou art my mother, and my sister” (Job 17:14), and Hamlet says, “Your worm is your only emperor for diet” (Hamlet 4.3).
  • “What is man but a poor, two-legged animal?”: Plato defined man as a featherless biped, and Diogenes answered with a plucked chicken. Lear calls man “such a poor, bare, forked animal” (King Lear 3.4).
  • “Porpoises shall eat the drowned sailor. The waters of this world shall close over his head”: The preacher gives the bride and groom a sailor’s burial at sea. Milton’s “Lycidas” (1638) mourns a friend drowned in the Irish Sea, and Ariel sings of a drowned father in The Tempest: “Full fathom five thy father lies.” The line foreshadows Miss MacIntosh lost in the waves.

Chapter 11

  • “Pericles”: Pericles (c. 495-429 BC) led Athens in its golden age and had the Parthenon built.
  • “a society of night devoted exclusively to atheistic propositions”: “The school of night” comes from Love’s Labour’s Lost (4.3): “Black is the badge of hell, / The hue of dungeons and the school of night.” The phrase became a name for Raleigh’s circle with Thomas Harriot and Christopher Marlowe, men accused of doubting Genesis and God. In 1594 an inquiry at Cerne Abbas examined charges of atheism against Raleigh’s circle.

Chapter 13

  • “Ben Jonson”: Ben Jonson was a significant early modern English poet and playwright, known for his satirical comedies, masques, and poetry. He was a contemporary of Shakespeare, received a royal pension in 1616 and is often counted as England’s first Poet Laureate, and is celebrated for plays like Volpone (a dark comedy about greed and corruption) and The Alchemist (a satire about fraud and human folly). “To Celia,” also known as “Drink to me only with thine eyes,” is a famous love song by Ben Jonson, first published in his 1616 collection The Forest.
  • “fixed like the North star”: “But I am constant as the northern star, / Of whose true-fix’d and resting quality / There is no fellow in the firmament” (Julius Caesar 3.1).
  • “my mother feigned death”: Juliet drinks the Friar’s potion to lie as if dead (Romeo and Juliet 4.1).
  • “not even the creaking of starlight”: Aristotle reports the Pythagorean answer: the spheres do make music, and people do not hear it because they have heard it since birth (On the Heavens 2.9). Lorenzo tells Jessica, “Such harmony is in immortal souls; / But whilst this muddy vesture of decay / Doth grossly close it in, we cannot hear it” (The Merchant of Venice 5.1).

Chapter 18

  • “Never married, never buried. An old man’s love is a young maid’s sorrow”: Both lines are old proverbs, often found in folk traditions and songs, particularly in the context of warnings against marrying old men. This phrase encapsulates the sorrowful and ambiguous nature of love explored in the book, especially the idea that some loves are doomed to remain incomplete, bringing pain and sorrow rather than joy. It also hints at the novel’s recurring motif of lost or ghostly loves that continue to affect the living long after they have faded from ordinary life.
  • “a fool’s paradise”: originated in Middle English between 1150 and 1500. The earliest known use of the phrase was in 1462 by William Paston, a justice. It was also used in Romeo and Juliet by Shakespeare. This encapsulates Vera’s temporary surrender to hopelessness, caught between the desire for meaning and the recognition of life’s inherent ambiguities and disappointments.
  • Shakespeare’s “We are such stuff as dreams are made on” is a line from The Tempest suggests the transience and ephemeral nature of life, comparing it to a dream.
  • “the true love is that which does not alter when it alteration finds”: Shakespeare Sonnet 116 . This sonnet presents the extreme ideal of romantic love: it never changes, it never fades, it outlasts death and admits no flaw. What is more, it insists that this ideal is the only love that can be called “true” (if love is mortal, changing, or impermanent) the speaker writes, then no man ever loved. The first quatrain says what love is not (changeable), the second quatrain says what it is (a fixed guiding star unshaken by tempests), the third quatrain says more specifically what it is not (“time’s fool”, subject to change in the passage of time), and the couplet announces the speaker’s certainty. The speaker makes an almost legalistic argument for the eternal passion of love, and the result is that the passion seems stronger and more urgent for the restraint in the speaker’s tone. expresses a profound idea about the nature of genuine love. It means that true love remains constant and unchanging even when it encounters changes, challenges, or hardships. Real love is steadfast and endures despite the inevitable transformations that life brings to people and circumstances. Within the context of the novel, this idea reflects the complex and often painful experiences of love, loss, and identity that the characters endure. Love is portrayed not as something fleeting or dependent on circumstances, but as a deeper, more enduring force that persists beyond physical changes, disappointments, or even death itself. This concept resonates with the novel’s broader themes about illusion and reality, permanence and change, and the search for an unalterable truth amid the flux of life. True love, then, is like a constant heart, the “other shore” that remains steady even as everything else shifts around it.

Chapter 47

Chapter 47

  • “God rest ye merry gentlemen” - The song dates back to the 15th century and its historic meaning is “may God grant you peace and happiness” or “God keep you mighty, gentlemen” It appears in two Shakespeare plays and Charles Dickens’s, A Christmas Carol. It symbolizes a blending of mourning and celebration, highlighting the tension between mortality and the desire for comfort or joy beyond death. It underscores the novel’s theme that life and death are inseparable and intertwined, and that even in the face of death, there may be a call to continue the “dance” of existence, however enigmatic or elusive that may be.

Chapter 47

  • “I am the Ethiope” may refer to A Midsummer Night’s Dream by Shakespeare. The phrase is embedded in a passage describing various mystical and spectral figures, blending historical, mythical, and symbolic references. It reflects the theme of multiplicity and transformation of self, the blending of identities across time and space, and the difficulty of pinpointing a single, fixed identity or reality. The declaration “I am the Ethiope” symbolizes one aspect of this fluid, shifting self: an identity that is part of a broader cosmic and spiritual transformation, often with connotations of being an outsider or other.

Chapter 59

  • Mr. Spitzer presents a profound psychological and existential complexity that invites comparison to Shakespeare's Hamlet, especially in terms of themes of identity, death, and the nature of existence. Mr. Spitzer is deeply haunted by death and the boundaries between life and death, much like Hamlet. He reflects on the nature of his own existence, questioning whether he is truly himself or a substituted being inhabiting another's body and soul. This existential confusion mirrors Hamlet's famous soliloquies where he ponders mortality, the self, and what lies beyond death. Mr. Spitzer’s fears of losing identity, becoming “an echo lost under a domeless sky” or “a mirror,” echo Hamlet’s anxieties about the persistence of self after death and the fear of oblivion. Both characters exhibit profound doubt and skepticism. Mr. Spitzer doubts the reality of his perceptions and even the integrity of his own body, soul, and spirit, suggesting a “substitution” or a “temporary tenant” inhabiting his flesh and bones. Hamlet similarly struggles with uncertainty about the ghost’s authenticity, his own purpose, and the moral implications of action versus inaction. This pervasive ambiguity drives both men into introspective states laden with melancholy and philosophical questioning. Music, silence, and echoes play a crucial role in Mr. Spitzer’s experience, symbolizing the ephemeral nature of life and the haunting persistence of memory and death. He is “tormented by that echo which required the eternal silence and emptiness,” and fears becoming “an echo lost under a domeless sky”. Hamlet’s world is also fraught with symbolic resonance: ghostly voices, the “sound and fury” of life, and the silence of death. Mr. Spitzer’s silent music and elusive notes function as a metaphor for Hamlet’s struggle to articulate his inner turmoil and to find harmony amid chaos. Mr. Spitzer is burdened by the memory of his dead brother and by “beautiful memories of memories” that “thronged through his mind, as many as the dead”. This recalls Hamlet’s fixation on the memory of his father and the ghostly injunction to remember and avenge. Both characters are tied to the past in ways that complicate their present existence and paralyze their actions. Mr. Spitzer’s isolation is profound since he feels “porous as a cloud,” “disembodied,” and estranged from his own body and its skeleton. He is a “psychological beggar wearing a coat of patchwork,” a figure of alienation and displacement. Hamlet’s alienation is similarly acute, as he feels estranged from the corrupt world around him and from his own sense of self after his father’s death and mother’s remarriage. Both figures engage in philosophical meditations on the nature of existence, the self, and the cosmos. Mr. Spitzer’s reflections on the tri-stranded being (body, soul, and spirit) and the fluidity between life and death, suggest a mystical and metaphysical depth akin to Hamlet’s contemplations on fate, providence, and the “undiscovered country”. Fear is central to both characters. Mr. Spitzer fears losing his identity, the silence, the unknown, and the transformation into something unrecognizable. Hamlet is famously paralyzed by his fears and doubts: fear of action, fear of the moral consequences, and fear of death itself. This chapter reveals Mr. Spitzer as a modern, fragmented, and philosophically tormented figure who embodies many of Hamlet’s existential concerns. Both characters wrestle with the instability of identity, the shadow of death, the burden of memory, and the search for meaning amid uncertainty. Mr. Spitzer could even be read as a modern counterpart to Hamlet: an introspective, melancholic, and metaphysically troubled man who inhabits a liminal space between life and death, self and other. His ongoing struggle with identity, death, and the meaning of existence echoes Hamlet’s own profound dilemmas. Both figures exemplify the “tragic sense” of life, caught in a flux of doubt, fear, and the search for self-understanding.
