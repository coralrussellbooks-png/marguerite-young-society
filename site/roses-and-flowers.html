Chapter 4 · Nature’s Cruelty and Funeral Wreaths

  • The natural world is depicted not as nurturing but cold and indifferent.
  • Funeral wreaths and deaf old people symbolize death, silence, and abandonment.

Chapter 10 · Red Geranium

  • Might symbolize life, vitality, and domesticity.

Chapter 12 · Flowers

  • Sensual, tactile flowers in the garden illustrate the blending of senses and the threshold between perception and imagination.

Chapter 12

  • “the garden of the blind”: Fragrance gardens for the blind were planted to be touched and smelled. The Brooklyn Botanic Garden opened one in 1955. Vera and Miss MacIntosh walk through this garden in Chapter 3.
  • “sweet william, cosmos, rose geranium, phlox, nasturtium, nicotiana”: Scented garden flowers. Rose geranium gives off its scent at a touch, and nicotiana, flowering tobacco, opens and perfumes the air at night.
  • “flowers of the old Jerusalem, flowers of the new Jerusalem”: “I am the rose of Sharon, and the lily of the valleys” (Song of Solomon 2:1), and the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down “as a bride adorned for her husband” (Revelation 21:2).

Chapter 13

  • “The mist flowers drooped their purple, langorous heads”: Mistflower, a real wildflower of the American South, blooms in soft purple heads in late summer.

Chapter 14

  • “a horseshoe of white roses”: Floral horseshoes and saddles of roses honored racing men. Since 1932 the Kentucky Derby winner has worn a garland of roses.
  • “the Battle of the Roses”: The Wars of the Roses (1455-1487) were a series of civil wars in England between the houses of York, with its white rose, and Lancaster, with its red.

Chapter 15

  • “bayberry and beach rose and meadows of billowing Queen Anne’s lace”: Bayberry wax made the colonists’ Christmas candles. Legend says Queen Anne pricked her finger making lace, and the one dark floret at the center of the flower is her drop of blood.
  • “the fairest flower is plucked the earliest”: The Passionate Pilgrim (1599), published under Shakespeare’s name, has “Sweet rose, fair flower, untimely pluck’d, soon vaded.”

Chapter 15 · Nature’s Contrasts

  • The “humped, naked dunes” with “seaweed,” “Queen Anne’s lace,” and “wild grass” represent the wildness and beauty of life.
  • The “low, stunted, blackened spruce and hemlock” suggest decay and resilience simultaneously.

  • “the last rose of the last summer on earth”: “The Last Rose of Summer” is a poem by the Irish poet Thomas Moore, published in 1813 and written while staying at Jenkinstown Castle in County Kilkenny, Ireland, where he was said to have been inspired by the China rose ‘Old Blush.’ It poignantly represents the final vestiges of youth, vitality, and love, standing on the brink of disappearance, much like the characters grappling with mortality and the fading of dreams.

Chapter 19 · Broken Cherubim and Fallen Alabaster Rose

  • Fragments of lost innocence or beauty.

Chapter 22

  • “cabbage roses such as grew, if not in What Cheer, Iowa”: Miss MacIntosh’s hometown.

Chapter 24

  • “the bronze-colored chrysanthemums which are the flowers for the dead”: In France and Italy, chrysanthemums are laid on graves on All Saints’ Day.

Chapter 25 · The Rose in the Buttonhole

  • Initially a symbol of life and vitality but is doubted by Catherine, considered an ear or something else. The white tuberose and the white carnation are things Jock wore.
  • Highlights the instability of perception.

Chapter 37 · The Cross and Rose

  • Symbolize transformation and the intertwining of suffering and beauty, life and death.

Chapter 42 · The Bridegroom and Withered Flowers

  • Represent unfulfilled love and abandonment, contrasting with Cousin Hannah’s militant independence and loneliness.

Chapter 44 · Snowdrops and Snow

  • Represent purity, fragility, and the coldness of death. The melting snowdrops in her hand symbolize fading life and lost love.

Chapter 47 · White Rose

  • Purity, love, and mourning.

Chapter 56 · White and Red Roses

  • The white rose worn by Mr. Spitzer symbolizes passing beyond life (death or transcendence), while Peron’s red rose is a signifier of identity and remembrance.

Chapter 57 · The Rose (Red and White)

  • Represents memory, fidelity, and duality. Mr. Spitzer imagines a rose named after himself and his brother, red and white together, symbolizing their intertwined fates and memories. The symbolism of red and white flowers together generally represents unity, harmony, and the balance of opposing concepts like passion and purity. This meaning is most common in Western traditions and varies significantly depending on the cultural context and the specific flowers chosen.

Chapter 61 · The Rose

  • The red rose dropped into the frog’s grave symbolizes immortality, resurrection, and eternal life.

Chapter 74 · The Old Rosebud

  • Placed on Esther’s body, symbolizing innocence, death, or a token of remorse.

Chapter 75 · The Funeral Wreath

  • Symbolizes death and mourning; it appears on the dead baby and connects to Esther’s fears about paternity and legacy.

Chapter 79 · Nature (Clouds, Stars, Flowers)

  • Contrasts with the harshness of Esther’s birth, representing purity, innocence, and the natural cycle of life.
  • The “clouds filled with holes” evoke fragility and vulnerability.

Chapter 81 · Black Mourning Band and Old Rosebud

  • Symbols of death, mourning, and lost beauty.
