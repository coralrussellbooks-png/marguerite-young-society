To All My Darlings

Chapter 47

On Chapter 47 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 5, 2026

← Chapter 46 · Home · Chapter 48 →

Summary

Mr. Spitzer remembers Cousin Hannah’s last words, that the mountains moved, that her love lay white under the snow, and that if they could have had a child, “red as her blood, white as the snow,” this great battle never would have taken place. Walking the foggy shore, he thinks of what he owes his dead twin, who robbed death of its terror and will be a “Welcoming Committee of One” on the other side, and he wonders which brother he is. He keeps a count of all things and finds that something is always missing: “Among all things possible, there was the rejected possibility.” He dreams of music never to be played, fears marriage as he fears change, and is agonized by attributing consciousness to everything. He wonders whether Hannah was the bride or the bridegroom and whether her love was her skirt, for which her love had died in the mountains. Afraid to look on her dying face lest beauty return to it, he lays a skirt over her body, and as her attorney, closing her house before it is torn down, he decides it is better that lost things sink whole than drift scattered.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, Mr. Spitzer reflects that Cousin Hannah, like himself, “had deferred her life to the next life” and that her death blurs the boundary between life and afterlife. He questions whether their love and existence end or begin anew, pondering the symbolism of reveille or taps in bugle calls. This passage underscores the theme of death as a transition rather than an end, and the ambiguity surrounding the afterlife and memory.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s relationship with his twin brother is a recurring motif. He muses on owing much to his brother, both financially and spiritually, and imagines his brother waiting across the divide of death as a “Welcoming Committee of One” on the other side. This reflects the intertwining of identity, memory, and fate, and how death and life are shared continuums rather than isolated events.
  • Throughout the chapter, there is a pervasive sense of death as a profound mystery, “this shrouded mystery, it seemed to him, this veil of gossamer which none should lift,” emphasizing the inscrutability of death and the impossibility of fully comprehending it. Mr. Spitzer’s uncertainty about whether the suffrage captain was the bride or bridegroom, or had passed beyond recognition, highlights death’s ambiguity and impenetrability.
  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates the interdependence of love and death, asserting “Death was ever this dying love,” and that transience is the essence of beauty and love. The mourner, like Mr. Spitzer himself, exists only to mourn, suggesting that love outlives life but is always shadowed by mortality. This passage poignantly links grief, memory, and the human condition.
  • Mr. Spitzer is troubled by the limits of memory and identity. He questions whether he can truly know himself or others, acknowledging his own forgetfulness and the “factor missing” in all attempts to capture reality or the self. This theme emphasizes the fragility and partiality of human understanding.
  • Music recurs as a metaphor for existence. Mr. Spitzer imagines “music written never to be played,” encompassing all sounds, silences, and lost notes, symbolizing life’s incompleteness and the ineffable nature of experience. Music embodies the complexity, beauty, and loss inherent in life and death.
  • A striking symbolic image is the suffrage captain’s skirt, which Mr. Spitzer lays over her body. He speculates that “perhaps her love was her skirt hiding her great wound, her disappointment, her bleeding heart.” The skirt or veil represents both concealment and protection, underscoring the theme of hidden truths and the mysteries of love and death.
  • Mr. Spitzer meditates on marriage as a metaphor for union, often equated with death or the end of possibility. He contrasts his own fear of commitment with his brother’s more reckless embrace of chance, reflecting on the paradox that marriage might herald death or vice versa, and the loneliness of the single and the unmarried in life and beyond.
  • Near the close of the chapter, Cousin Hannah is imagined as a wanderer, “crying at many gates... forever crying for the image of her love who had gone before her into the darkness.” This suggests that death does not end longing or quest; the dead carry “unfinished business” and infinite frustration, wandering through liminal spaces between life and death.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s fear of change is a subtle but important passage. He fears the transformation that death brings, of beauty fading or returning briefly before complete dissolution, reflecting human anxiety about impermanence and loss.
  • Lastly, there is the image of Mr. Spitzer as a keeper of old papers, lawsuits, and memories symbolizing human attempts to impose order and meaning on life and death, even as all eventually sink beneath the waves. This suggests the futility yet dignity of human effort in the face of mortality and oblivion.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “Among all things possible, there was the rejected possibility. And perhaps the rejected possibility was all there was.” What is the rejected possibility in Mr. Spitzer’s life?
  • “Grief could never be expressed at the wrong time.” Why does Mr. Spitzer write his elegies too soon?
  • “Better that they should exist submerged than that they should exist at the surface where all were lost.” Why would sinking be better than drifting?

Characters

  • Mr. Spitzer owes his brother “far more than the promissory notes.” What does he owe him?
  • “Was she the bridegroom? Was she the bride?” Why can Mr. Spitzer not decide?
  • Mr. Spitzer lays a skirt over Hannah because “surely a lady should be skirted when she went to her death.” Is this a kindness or a judgment?

Connections

  • In Chapter 44 Hannah’s love died going back for her skirt. Here Mr. Spitzer wonders if “her love was her skirt.” How has the skirt changed meaning?
  • Mr. Spitzer fears beauty that grows old, and his brother loved the old, thinking they would grow young in death. Where else do the twins mirror each other?
  • The great census-taker of Chapter 42 returns, and “there was always one soul missing.” Who is missing now?

Reflection

  • “For more than life or death he feared change.” Is change more frightening than death?
  • “The single were the lonely.” Is Mr. Spitzer’s loneliness a choice?
  • “Should one be held accountable for one’s entire life or for one moment?” How would you judge a life?

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • A meditative figure, a twin and a double, who embodies contemplation, loss, and the attempt to reconcile opposites (life/death, love/loss).
  • His identity is fluid, reflecting confusion and multiplicity; he is both mourner and wanderer, seeker and lost soul.

Peron Spitzer

  • A contrast to Mr. Spitzer, more impulsive and superstitious, who had a different view of love and death.
  • His death catalyzed Mr. Spitzer’s deep reflections and mourning: Mr. Spitzer is grateful “for the fortunate error of his suicide,” which “had not been the end of wisdom but the beginning of wisdom.”
  • “His brother had died young, but had he ever married?”

The Old Suffrage Captain (Cousin Hannah)

  • A symbolic figure representing a lost love, death, and unresolved mystery.
  • Her death and legacy inspire Mr. Spitzer’s reflections on love, mortality, and memory.
  • Her “skirt” symbolizes her love, her wound, her identity veiled and hidden.

Research: Resources

  • “red as her blood, white as the snow”: See Grimm’s “Little Snow-White” in the Chapter 45 resources. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “Do I hear the bugle crying reveille or taps?”: Taps, the bugle call played at military funerals, was arranged in 1862 by the Union general Daniel Butterfield at Harrison’s Landing, Virginia.
  • “Good-night and good-morning”: There is an Arabic phrase for the “Good-night and good-morning”; see the Chapter 42 resources. “The lover and the loved” may refer to Rumi or Sufism that also have similar sayings. The phrase emphasizes an understanding of existence in which love creates a unity that transcends time and mortality. It suggests that the perceived separation (between life and death, or between selves in relationship) is ultimately illusory within this “night of love.” The continuity of love persists beyond physical life, blurring the lines that normally divide existence. This interpretation aligns with the novel’s larger thematic exploration of blurred realities, the coexistence of dream and wakefulness, and the merging of opposites like life and death, as seen in the characters’ experiences and reflections throughout the novel.
    • seems to mirror mystical interpretations from Sufism and Christian Theology and signifies the ultimate unity and interconnectedness of love, the person experiencing love, and the object of that love. In Sufism, the phrase highlights the journey of the soul towards union with the divine. The lover seeks to merge with the Beloved and in that ultimate union the distinction between the two dissolves. In Christian Theology the Father can be seen as the Lover, the Son as the Beloved, and the Holy Spirit as the love that binds them: “Behold, then, there are three things: he that loves, and that which is loved, and love” (Augustine, On the Trinity 8.10.14, trans. Arthur West Haddan, 1873). In philosophy the phrase emphasizes the interconnectedness of all things and the illusion of separation. This reflects the novel’s broader meditation on the illusory nature of individuality and the interconnectedness of all beings, suggesting that what appear to be separate states or identities are part of a single, fluid existence. See Augustine and Christian Theology.
  • “good-night, sweet prince”: “Good night, sweet prince, / And flights of angels sing thee to thy rest!” (Hamlet 5.2). It signifies a final, loving farewell to a cherished figure who has endured great trials and is now moving beyond mortal existence. It reflects themes of mortality, transition, and the enduring nature of love even beyond death. This farewell is both personal and universal, blending sorrow with a quiet hope for peace in the beyond. See Shakespeare.
  • “good-night, my lady”: may refer to “Goodnight, Ladies,” first printed as “Farewell Ladies” in 1847 and credited to Edwin Pearce Christy of the Christy Minstrels; the full text appeared in 1867. It is a reverent and intimate goodbye marking the end of an earthly presence and symbolizing the transition to whatever lies beyond, embodying both sorrow and hope.
  • “God rest you merry, gentlemen”: The carol survives in a manuscript of the early 1650s and was printed on a broadside about 1760. “Rest” meant keep, so its historic meaning is “may God keep you merry, gentlemen,” or grant you peace and happiness. The phrase “rest you merry” appears in two Shakespeare plays, As You Like It and Romeo and Juliet, and a caroler sings the carol at Scrooge’s keyhole in Charles Dickens’s A Christmas Carol (1843). It symbolizes a blending of mourning and celebration, highlighting the tension between mortality and the desire for comfort or joy beyond death. It underscores the novel’s theme that life and death are inseparable and intertwined, and that even in the face of death, there may be a call to continue the “dance” of existence, however enigmatic or elusive that may be.
  • “some small Moses in his mollusk boat”: “And when she could not longer hide him, she took for him an ark of bulrushes… and she laid it in the flags by the river’s brink” (Exodus 2:3). Moses is an important prophet in Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Druze faith, Baha’i faith and other Abrahamic religions. He is significant as a liminal figure embodying the journey through mortality and the blurred boundaries between life and death, guiding both the living and the reader through the novel’s complex interplay of reality, memory, and dream. See Judaism and The Bible.
  • “This tendency of an image to repeat an image”: It refers to the idea that images, memories, or experiences tend to recur in life or in the mind, not always exactly the same but often with subtle differences or variations. This repetition suggests that what is perceived or remembered is not a one-time event but part of a continuing pattern or cycle that reappears over time. The variations in repeated images imply that while the core essence may be the same, the perception of it or the circumstances around it might change slightly, affecting how the image or memory manifests. The character’s reflection here invites the reader to consider that life, memory, and identity are not static but fluid and evolving, shaped by recurring motifs and experiences that are never identical yet connected. This idea ties into the novel’s broader themes of illusion, memory, and the elusive nature of reality, where nothing is fixed and the past and present intermingle.
  • “through which he had lived, moved, gathered weight”: “For in him we live, and move, and have our being” (Acts 17:28). It signifies the ongoing process of existence that is simultaneously rich and fragile, substantive and elusive, embodying the novel’s themes of transformation, mortality, and the elusive nature of reality. See The Bible.
  • “Among all things possible, there was the rejected possibility”: It suggests a profound philosophical reflection on existence and choice. It means that within the vast range of possible outcomes or realities, there exists at least one possibility that is consciously or unconsciously excluded or denied. This “rejected possibility” embodies the idea that not everything that could happen or be is accepted or realized; some potentialities remain unchosen or unacknowledged. If one were to eliminate all mistakes or errors from life, one might also lose the essential “seed of truth” those mistakes contain. Thus, the rejected possibility is not merely an absence but a meaningful part of the whole: the unactualized potential that shapes reality and experience. It points to the complexity and ambiguity of life, where certainty is elusive, and truth includes its negations and contradictions.
  • “where the old spouters spouted like the whales”: A spouter is a whaleman. Ishmael spends his first night in New Bedford at the Spouter-Inn (Herman Melville, Moby-Dick, 1851, chapter 3). See American Writers.
  • “old Tritons blowing their ivory horns”: Triton is a Greek god of the sea. See Triton’s conch in the Chapter 42 resources and the Chapter 43 resources. See Greek Mythology.

  • “a pregnant woman swollen with many waters”: This is an allusion to the last character introduced in the last part of the book, Esther Longtree. This “dead self” that “heard no heartbeat” suggests a profound emptiness or loss inside her, where life has failed to take hold or continue. It reflects Esther’s tragic experience of repeatedly carrying pregnancies that do not result in living children, embodying both physical and emotional sorrow. The phrase underscores the theme of loss, barrenness, and the haunting presence of death that permeates her existence, even as her body shows signs of life and fertility.

  • “simultaneities”: Simultaneity, events happening at the same time, is not absolute, particularly for events at different locations. Einstein showed in 1905 that whether two distant events are simultaneous depends on the observer (“On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies”). It encapsulates a key thematic concern in the novel: the dissolution of clear boundaries between states of being and the coexistence of multiple temporal and existential planes, contributing to the novel’s exploration of illusion, memory, and the nature of reality.   • “there was no Savior upon these waters”: When Peter sinks on the waters he cries, “Lord, save me. And immediately Jesus stretched forth his hand, and caught him” (Matthew 14:30-31). This phrase carries thematic weight across the novel, which often explores the fragility of life, the absence or ambiguity of divine aid, and the painful solitude of human suffering amid forces beyond control. It underscores the stark reality that sometimes there is no rescue, no reprieve, no saving grace, only the merciless, indifferent “waters” that engulf all. See The Bible.
  • “Death was that dark lover who drew her down”: See Death and the Maiden in the Chapter 33 resources. See Music and Composers.
  • “music of things unutterable”: Another of philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein’s contributions was silence: “Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent” (Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus 7, trans. C. K. Ogden, 1922). Language has logical limits, and of things beyond them, such as metaphysical or ethical truths, one should be silent. Silence for him was a space for potential meaning and understanding, a recognition of the limits of language, and a way of acknowledging what lies beyond the grasp of words. Mr. Spitzer’s “music of things unutterable” echoes Wittgenstein and is a thematic anchor underscoring the novel’s exploration of the ineffable aspects of human existence. It implies a humility before the mysteries that lie beyond speech and reason, advocating silence as a form of respect or acknowledgment when words fail. This emphasis on silence and the limits of language permeates the novel’s meditations on death, reality, and the spiritual unknown. See Wittgenstein.

  • “I am the Ethiope”: “Ethiope” is Shakespeare’s word: Romeo sees Juliet hang “upon the cheek of night / Like a rich jewel in an Ethiope’s ear” (Romeo and Juliet 1.5), and in A Midsummer Night’s Dream Lysander insults the dark Hermia, “Away, you Ethiope!” (3.2). “Can the Ethiopian change his skin, or the leopard his spots?” (Jeremiah 13:23). The phrase is embedded in a passage describing various mystical and spectral figures, blending historical, mythical, and symbolic references. It reflects the theme of multiplicity and transformation of self, the blending of identities across time and space, and the difficulty of pinpointing a single, fixed identity or reality. The declaration “I am the Ethiope” symbolizes one aspect of this fluid, shifting self: an identity that is part of a broader cosmic and spiritual transformation, often with connotations of being an outsider or other.   • “the king with many crowns”: “His eyes were as a flame of fire, and on his head were many crowns” (Revelation 19:12). See The Bible.
  • “Should it include the road not taken or the road taken?”: “The Road Not Taken” (Robert Frost, 1916). This line invites reflection on how a life and a legacy are understood: are only the choices made to be honored, or also the possibilities forgone? And what bearing does this have on memory, identity, and the way a person makes sense of existence? It exemplifies the novel’s philosophical and often ambiguous approach to life, death, and meaning.
  • “wedded before one is bedded”: Historical wedding traditions included a bedding ceremony in which newlyweds were publicly accompanied to their marital bed by wedding guests. It refers to the traditional or ideal sequence in marriage where the formal union (wedding) takes place before the couple begins their sexual life together (being bedded). It highlights a customary expectation that marriage is a prerequisite to sexual relations. However, this phrase is used in a reflective and somewhat ironic context in the novel, suggesting that for some characters or situations, this order might be reversed or disrupted. It points to complexities and ambiguities in relationships and life, where social or formal conventions do not always align with lived realities.
  • “marriage was that great lottery”: “In our monogamous part of the world, to marry means to halve one’s rights and double one’s duties” (Arthur Schopenhauer, “On Women,” Parerga and Paralipomena, 1851). See Schopenhauer.
  • “some old horse who had been ringed by the great horse-ringer”: A ringer is a horse raced under another horse’s name: “A horse that is taken through the country and trotted under a false name and pedigree is called a ‘ringer’” (Manitoba Free Press, 1882). Ringers were disguised with dye. In the 1920s and early 1930s the con man Peter Christian Barrie bleached and hennaed horses to pass them off as others, and at Hialeah in 1932 one horse’s dye began to fade. Hence the old horse “showing its true colors in a rain storm.”
  • “when all which had been lost should be found”: “For this thy brother was dead, and is alive again; and was lost, and is found” (Luke 15:32), the parable of the Prodigal Son. This phrase encapsulates the novel’s exploration of the elusive nature of truth and existence, where certainties dissolve and are replaced by a perpetual interplay of gaining and losing, remembering and forgetting. See The Bible.
  • “give up his assumption that all things which existed should be tangible”: Peron and Joachim as materialism and immaterialism. In philosophy, materialism says that everything that exists is fundamentally physical, composed of matter and energy. All phenomena, including consciousness, are ultimately reducible to physical processes. They point to the success of science in explaining the physical world and that consciousness can be explained by brain activity. Physicalism is similar in that everything can be explained by physics. Immaterialism believes there is an existence of entities or properties that are not physical, such as minds, consciousness, or spiritual realms. Non-physical entities or properties exist alongside or instead of the material world. Idealists believe that only minds and their perceptions are real, while the material world is a construction of the mind. Peron symbolizes the external, material world with its pleasures, follies, and impermanence, while Joachim represents the internal, immaterial realm of thought, memory, and spiritual seeking. Their contrast embodies the novel’s exploration of human existence as oscillating between these poles, neither fully attainable nor entirely separable, but always entwined in the complex dance of life and death. See Spinoza’s mind and body as one in the Chapter 45 resources. See Spinoza and Peron Spitzer.
  • “he was agonized by attributing consciousness to everything”: This may refer to panpsychism, the view that mind, or something like mind, is a fundamental and widespread feature of reality, present in some form even in its most basic elements. It has roots in ancient Greek philosophy, in Thales, who held that “all things are full of gods” (Aristotle, On the Soul 1.5), and in Plato, and later in Spinoza, Leibniz, and Schopenhauer. Mr. Spitzer suffers because he senses consciousness not only in people but in clouds, stars, waves, palings, and weathervanes, so that the line between the living and the lifeless blurs and his own sense of self grows unstable. It belongs to the novel’s larger struggle with reality, illusion, memory, and identity. See Spinoza, Schopenhauer, and Plato and Platonism.
  • “the lights of corpo santo”: (holy body) is an archaic term referring to St. Elmo’s fire, a weather phenomenon where luminous plasma is created by a corona discharge from pointed objects during electrical storms. St. Erasmus (St. Elmo) is the patron saint of sailors. Sailors sometimes viewed it as a good omen. It carries the connotation of a body that is holy or consecrated, embodying the intersection of human mortality and divine mystery, a key motif in the novel’s meditation on the nature of being and transcendence.
    • Melville calls them corpusants: in “The Candles” the three masts of the Pequod burn “like three gigantic wax tapers before an altar” (Moby-Dick, 1851, chapter 119). See American Writers.
  • “this bella donna”: Bella donna, “beautiful lady,” is also deadly nightshade, whose drops Italian women used to widen their pupils. See the Chapter 38 resources.
  • “The lapidarian was become the jewel”: The lapidarian, the cutter of gems, becomes his jewel, and the mosaicist becomes his mosaic: the maker is lost in what he makes.
  • “chasing her skirt as if it were a Holy Grail”: The Holy Grail and Excalibur serve as potent metaphors for the suffrage captain’s inner journey, her struggles, and the poignant tension between idealism and the limitations imposed by life and fate.
    • The Holy Grail is a treasure in Arthurian literature that has miraculous powers.
    • Excalibur is the legendary sword of King Arthur.
    • The golden fleece, in Greek mythology, is the fleece of the winged ram, Chrysomallos.
    • Jason’s argosy is the ship Argo, in Greek mythology, that was built with the help of the gods so that Jason and the Argonauts could sail and retrieve the golden fleece. See Jason in the Chapter 42 resources.
  • “the Stygian darkness unlighted by a star”: Stygian means of the Styx, the river of the dead in the Greek underworld. See Charon in the Chapter 46 resources. See Greek Mythology.
  • “void and without shape upon the waters of the darkness”: “And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters” (Genesis 1:2). See Genesis.
  • “for more than life or death he feared change”: Mr. Spitzer fears change more than life or death. Change threatens not only the outer world but his identity, perception, and memory, already unsettled by his brother’s death and by living “between two worlds,” never anchored in either. What he dreads most is a loss of self, a transformation that might erase who he is, which is deeper than the finality of death. Here it is the fear of seeing the dying face change, “that beauty returning but for one moment before beauty crumbled into dust.”
  • “should one be held accountable for one’s entire life”: It is a meditation on responsibility and identity: should a person be judged for a whole life or for one transforming moment beyond conscious control, memory, or desire? Such a moment may redefine a person in ways no one foresees, which makes judgment uncertain. Mr. Spitzer asks it of Hannah, who “perhaps at the last moment… suddenly believed in all she had denied.”
  • “Should one moment of inestimable madness dethrone that king”: “O, let me not be mad, not mad, sweet heaven! / Keep me in temper; I would not be mad!” (King Lear 1.5). See Shakespeare.
  • “Should the reprobate be pure as snow”: “Though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow” (Isaiah 1:18), and “If I wash myself with snow water, and make my hands never so clean; yet shalt thou plunge me in the ditch” (Job 9:30-31). See the ace of spades in the Chapter 42 resources. See The Bible, The Book of Job, and Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “crumbled into dust and ashes”: “Behold now, I have taken upon me to speak unto the Lord, which am but dust and ashes” (Genesis 18:27). See Genesis.
  • “Night begot perhaps only the night”: In Hesiod, Night gives birth to her opposite: “From Chaos came forth Erebus and black Night; but of Night were born Aether and Day” (Theogony 123-124, trans. Hugh G. Evelyn-White, 1914). See Greek Mythology.
  • “writing almost as automatically as the sandpipers”: Automatic writing, words set down without conscious direction, was practiced by spiritualists and the Surrealists. Yeats built A Vision (1925) on the automatic writing of his wife, George, begun in 1917.
  • “In the East where men wore skirts”: In much of Asia and the Middle East men wear wrapped or flowing garments: the sarong, the lungi, the dhoti, the thobe. See Clothing, Hats, and Skirts.
  • “Death was the Orient whether it was heaven or earth”: Orient comes from the Latin oriens, rising, for the east where the sun rises. Christian churches and graves were long laid out facing east, toward the rising sun and the hoped-for resurrection. For Mr. Spitzer death is that east, heaven and earth at once, where Hannah goes, “where men wore skirts.”
  • “none had shrived this body”: To shrive is to hear a confession and grant absolution. Hannah dies unshriven.
  • “the kingfisher’s eerie cry”: When Ceyx drowned, Alcyone and he were turned into kingfishers, and the calm days when they nest on the sea are the halcyon days (Ovid, Metamorphoses 11). See the halcyon in the Chapter 43 resources. See Greek Mythology.
  • “But time could not tell him the time”: Clocks and calendars mark time, but they cannot tell Mr. Spitzer where he stands in it. He consults “his broken watch… a watch with no numbers to the hours,” the sundial, the starfish, the kingfisher’s cry. His memory is fractured, and time has become ambiguous, perhaps cyclical, perhaps illusory: “To ask of time the time was like asking of the thief—Where is the jewel you stole?” See Clocks and Watches.
  • “a great rajah’s jewel”: This is foreshadowing for a subplot later on in the book with Mr. Spitzer. See Chapter 54.
  • “the Crusader’s sleeping wife bound by a girdle of iron”: See the chastity belt, largely a legend of later centuries, in the Chapter 31 resources.
  • “There were many keys”: Keys in this chapter embody the human quest for understanding and the limits of that quest in the face of mortality, the unknown, and the mysteries of existence. See Keys, Doors, and Locks.
  • “Better that her cruel lord should never have plowed this stony land”: “Some fell upon stony places, where they had not much earth” (Matthew 13:5). See The Bible.
  • “He rescued all ladies but one he had imprisoned”: Given the novel’s themes of complex and often painful relationships between men and women, and the recurring motif of loss, abandonment, and unfulfilled love, this line points to the paradoxical and tragic nature of such relationships. The man may have been a rescuer to many, but in this one critical case, he failed utterly, leaving the woman isolated and doomed.
  • “oh, how he hated these urban changes”: Mr. Spitzer in general is a conservative character wanting to preserve tradition. Closing Hannah’s estate before the old house is torn down for new traffic, he laments streets that are no more and signposts vanished in the fog. The changes stand for a loss of the familiar, ordered world, the fading of old communities and landmarks, and his sense of disorientation. It is the same fear of change, and of the dislocation change brings.
