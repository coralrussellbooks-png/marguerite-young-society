Summary

Mr. Spitzer remembers Hannah's last words about the mountains moving, and lays her skirt over her body. He wonders whether her love was that skirt, covering her wound. He reflects on what he owes his twin, on materialism and spirit, on everything in the world seeming conscious, and on the rejected possibility, the life that was never lived. He decides it is better that lost things sink whole than drift scattered.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, Mr. Spitzer reflects that Cousin Hannah, like himself, "had deferred her life to the next life" and that her death blurs the boundary between life and afterlife. He questions whether their love and existence end or begin anew, pondering the symbolism of reveille or taps in bugle calls. This passage underscores the theme of death as a transition rather than an end, and the ambiguity surrounding the afterlife and memory.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s relationship with his twin brother is a recurring motif. He muses on owing much to his brother, both financially and spiritually, and imagines their mutual presence across the divide of death, forming a “Welcoming Committee of One” on the other side. This reflects the intertwining of identity, memory, and fate, and how death and life are shared continuums rather than isolated events.
  • Throughout the chapter, there is a pervasive sense of death as a profound mystery, “this shrouded mystery, it seemed to him, this veil of gossamer which none should lift,” emphasizing the inscrutability of death and the impossibility of fully comprehending it. Mr. Spitzer’s uncertainty about whether the suffrage captain was the bride or bridegroom, or had passed beyond recognition, highlights death’s ambiguity and impenetrability.
  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates the interdependence of love and death, asserting “Death was ever this dying love,” and that transience is the essence of beauty and love. The mourner, like Mr. Spitzer himself, exists only to mourn, suggesting that love outlives life but is always shadowed by mortality. This passage poignantly links grief, memory, and the human condition.
  • Mr. Spitzer is troubled by the limits of memory and identity. He questions whether he can truly know himself or others, acknowledging his own forgetfulness and the “factor missing” in all attempts to capture reality or the self. This theme emphasizes the fragility and partiality of human understanding.
  • Music recurs as a metaphor for existence. Mr. Spitzer imagines “music written never to be played,” encompassing all sounds, silences, and lost notes, symbolizing life’s incompleteness and the ineffable nature of experience. Music embodies the complexity, beauty, and loss inherent in life and death.
  • A striking symbolic image is the suffrage captain’s skirt, which Mr. Spitzer lays over her body. He speculates that "perhaps her love was her skirt hiding her great wound, her disappointment, her bleeding heart." The skirt or veil represents both concealment and protection, underscoring the theme of hidden truths and the mysteries of love and death.
  • Mr. Spitzer meditates on marriage as a metaphor for union, often equated with death or the end of possibility. He contrasts his own fear of commitment with his brother’s more reckless embrace of chance, reflecting on the paradox that marriage might herald death or vice versa, and the loneliness of the single and the unmarried in life and beyond.
  • The chapter closes on the image of Cousin Hannah as a wanderer, “crying at many gates... forever crying for the image of her love who had gone before her into the darkness.” This suggests that death does not end longing or quest; the dead carry “unfinished business” and infinite frustration, wandering through liminal spaces between life and death.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s fear of change is a subtle but important passage. He fears the transformation that death brings, of beauty fading or returning briefly before complete dissolution, reflecting human anxiety about impermanence and loss.
  • Lastly, there is the image of Mr. Spitzer as a keeper of old papers, lawsuits, and memories symbolizing human attempts to impose order and meaning on life and death, even as all eventually sink beneath the waves. This suggests the futility yet dignity of human effort in the face of mortality and oblivion.

Research: Resources

  • Another of philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein contributions was silence. “Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent.” Language has logical limits, and when we try to use it to express things that are beyond these limits (like metaphysical or ethical truths), we should be silent. Silence for him was a space for potential meaning and understanding, a recognition of the limits of language, and a way of acknowledging what lies beyond the grasp of words. Wittgenstein's statement in this chapter is a thematic anchor underscoring the novel's exploration of the ineffable aspects of human existence. It implies a humility before the mysteries that lie beyond speech and reason, advocating silence as a form of respect or acknowledgment when words fail. This emphasis on silence and the limits of language permeates the novel’s meditations on death, reality, and the spiritual unknown.
  • “no distinction between the living and the dead, the past and the present, the lover and the loved.” seems to mirror mystical interpretations from Sufism and Christian Theology and signifies the ultimate unity and interconnectedness of love, the person experiencing love, and the object of that love. In Sufism, the phrase highlights the journey of the soul towards union with the divine. The lover seeks to merge with the Beloved and in that ultimate union the distinction between the two dissolves. In Christian Theology the Father can be seen as the Lover, the Son as the Beloved, and the Holy Spirit as the love that binds them. In philosophy the phrase emphasizes the interconnectedness of all things and the illusion of separation. This reflects the novel’s broader meditation on the illusory nature of individuality and the interconnectedness of all beings, suggesting that what we perceive as separate states or identities are part of a single, fluid existence.
  • “good-night, sweet prince” - Hamlet by Shakespeare. It signifies a final, loving farewell to a cherished figure who has endured great trials and is now moving beyond mortal existence. It reflects themes of mortality, transition, and the enduring nature of love even beyond death. This farewell is both personal and universal, blending sorrow with a quiet hope for peace in the beyond.
  • “Good-night, my lady” may refer to a folk song from 1867 by Edwin Pearce Christy. It is a reverent and intimate goodbye marking the end of an earthly presence and symbolizing the transition to whatever lies beyond, embodying both sorrow and hope.
  • “God rest ye merry gentlemen” - The song dates back to the 15th century and its historic meaning is “may God grant you peace and happiness” or “God keep you mighty, gentlemen” It appears in two Shakespeare plays and Charles Dickens’s, A Christmas Carol. It symbolizes a blending of mourning and celebration, highlighting the tension between mortality and the desire for comfort or joy beyond death. It underscores the novel’s theme that life and death are inseparable and intertwined, and that even in the face of death, there may be a call to continue the “dance” of existence, however enigmatic or elusive that may be.
  • Peron and Joachim as materialism and immaterialism. In philosophy, materialism says that everything that exists is fundamentally physical, composed of matter and energy. All phenomena, including consciousness, are ultimately reducible to physical processes. They point to the success of science in explaining the physical world and that consciousness can be explained by brain activity. Physicalism is similar in that everything can be explained by physics. Immaterialism believes there is an existence of entities or properties that are not physical, such as minds, consciousness, or spiritual realms. Non-physical entities or properties exist alongside or instead of the material world. Idealists believe that only minds and their perceptions are real, while the material world is a construction of the mind. Peron symbolizes the external, material world with its pleasures, follies, and impermanence, while Joachim represents the internal, immaterial realm of thought, memory, and spiritual seeking. Their contrast embodies the novel’s exploration of human existence as oscillating between these poles, neither fully attainable nor entirely separable, but always entwined in the complex dance of life and death.
  • “This tendency of an image to repeat an image, perhaps with variations, was something to be considered, weighed, taken into account.” It refers to the idea that images, memories, or experiences tend to recur in life or in the mind, not always exactly the same but often with subtle differences or variations. This repetition suggests that what we perceive or remember is not a one-time event but part of a continuing pattern or cycle that reappears over time. The variations in repeated images imply that while the core essence may be the same, our perception or the circumstances around it might change slightly, affecting how the image or memory manifests. The character’s reflection here invites the reader to consider that life, memory, and identity are not static but fluid and evolving, shaped by recurring motifs and experiences that are never identical yet connected. This idea ties into the novel’s broader themes of illusion, memory, and the elusive nature of reality, where nothing is fixed and the past and present intermingle.
  • “Among all things possible, there was the rejected possibility.” It suggests a profound philosophical reflection on existence and choice. It means that within the vast range of possible outcomes or realities, there exists at least one possibility that is consciously or unconsciously excluded or denied. This "rejected possibility" embodies the idea that not everything that could happen or be is accepted or realized; some potentialities remain unchosen or unacknowledged. If one were to eliminate all mistakes or errors from life, one might also lose the essential "seed of truth" those mistakes contain. Thus, the rejected possibility is not merely an absence but a meaningful part of the whole: the unactualized potential that shapes reality and experience. It points to the complexity and ambiguity of life, where certainty is elusive, and truth includes its negations and contradictions.
  • extraordinary apostrophe is a figure of speech where a speaker or writer directly addresses someone or something that is not present or cannot respond.
  • “Simultaneities” is the concept of simultaneity, or events happening at the same time, it is not absolute, particularly when considering events at different locations. It encapsulates a key thematic concern in the novel: the dissolution of clear boundaries between states of being and the coexistence of multiple temporal and existential planes, contributing to the novel's exploration of illusion, memory, and the nature of reality.
  • “there was no Savior upon these waters” - Matthew 14:22-33 This phrase carries thematic weight across the novel, which often explores the fragility of life, the absence or ambiguity of divine aid, and the painful solitude of human suffering amid forces beyond control. It underscores the stark reality that sometimes there is no rescue, no reprieve, no saving grace, only the merciless, indifferent "waters" that engulf all.
  • “I am the Ethiope” may refer to A Midsummer Night’s Dream by Shakespeare. The phrase is embedded in a passage describing various mystical and spectral figures, blending historical, mythical, and symbolic references. It reflects the theme of multiplicity and transformation of self, the blending of identities across time and space, and the difficulty of pinpointing a single, fixed identity or reality. The declaration “I am the Ethiope” symbolizes one aspect of this fluid, shifting self: an identity that is part of a broader cosmic and spiritual transformation, often with connotations of being an outsider or other.
  • “Should it include the road not taken or the road taken?” The Road Not Taken by Robert Frost. This line invites reflection on how we understand our lives and legacies: Do we honor only the choices we make, or do we also acknowledge the possibilities we forgo? And what bearing does this have on memory, identity, and the way we make sense of our existence? It exemplifies the novel's philosophical and often ambiguous approach to life, death, and meaning.
  • “wedded before one is bedded” historical wedding traditions had a bedding ceremony that was practiced where newlyweds were publicly accompanied to their marital bed by wedding guests. It refers to the traditional or ideal sequence in marriage where the formal union (wedding) takes place before the couple begins their sexual life together (being bedded). It highlights a customary expectation that marriage is a prerequisite to sexual relations. However, this phrase is used in a reflective and somewhat ironic context in the novel, suggesting that for some characters or situations, this order might be reversed or disrupted. It points to complexities and ambiguities in relationships and life, where social or formal conventions do not always align with lived realities.
  • “he was agonized by attributing consciousness to everything” - may refer to panpsychism which suggests that a mind, or mind-like aspect, is a fundamental and widespread characteristic of reality. It says that the subjective experience associated with human consciousness exists, in some form, throughout the universe, even in its most basic elements. It has its roots in ancient Greek philosophy with Thales and Plato and later with Spinoza, Leibniz, and Schopenhauer. It reflects Mr. Spitzer's profound and troubling awareness of the pervasive presence of consciousness or life-like qualities in all things around him. This means that Mr. Spitzer experiences a kind of existential distress or mental anguish because he perceives consciousness, intention, or awareness not just in people but extending to inanimate objects, natural phenomena, and abstract concepts. He senses life, emotion, or meaning in things that are traditionally considered lifeless, such as clouds, stars, waves, or even shadows and this blurs the line between what is truly alive and what is not. This overwhelming and almost mystical perception causes him suffering because it challenges ordinary reality and makes his own sense of self and understanding of the world unstable and confusing. This agonizing attribution is part of Mr. Spitzer's broader struggle with reality, illusion, memory, and identity. It underlines his sensitivity to the ambiguities and mysteries of existence, where the boundaries between life and death, the living and the non-living, and reality and dream are not distinct. His mind is burdened by this expansive and unsettling sense of interconnectedness and animation of all things, which he cannot easily reconcile or control. The phrase captures Mr. Spitzer's deep philosophical and emotional turmoil as he contemplates a universe filled with consciousness beyond human life, contributing to the novel’s themes of illusion, perception, and the elusive nature of reality.
  • “corpo santo” (holy body) is an archaic term referring to St. Elmo’s fire, a weather phenomenon where luminous plasma is created by a corona discharge from pointed objects during electrical storms. St. Erasmus (St. Elmo) is the patron saint of sailors. Sailors sometimes viewed it as a good omen. It carries the connotation of a body that is holy or consecrated, embodying the intersection of human mortality and divine mystery, a key motif in the novel’s meditation on the nature of being and transcendence.
  • “for more than life or death he feared change, change of any kind.” This refers to the character Mr. Spitzer’s deep and profound fear of change itself, beyond even his fear of life or death. This indicates that Mr. Spitzer is so unsettled and troubled by the instability and unpredictability that change brings, that it causes him greater anxiety than the ultimate certainties of life and death. Change here represents not just physical or external alterations but also internal transformations: of identity, perception, and existence. Mr. Spitzer is caught in a state of flux and ambiguity, struggling with his shifting self, memory, and reality. His life is marked by uncertainty, fragmentation, and a blurred distinction between past, present, and future, as well as between life and death. Because of this, change threatens his fragile sense of self and his attempts to find order and meaning. This fear of change is tied to his larger existential condition: he is a man already profoundly altered by the death of his brother and by his own uncertain identity, living "between two worlds," never fully anchored in either. For him, change threatens to dissolve what little stability or continuity he has, making it more terrifying than even death itself. The phrase captures Mr. Spitzer's core existential crisis: he fears the loss of self and the unknown transformations that might erase or radically alter his identity, which is a deeper fear than the finality of death.
  • “should one be held accountable for one’s entire life or for one moment of unforeseen and unforeseeable transmutation, perhaps a change taking place beyond all consciousness or memory or desire?” It reflects a profound philosophical meditation on personal responsibility and identity. This question raises the issue of whether a person should be judged or held responsible for all their actions and choices throughout life, or whether there might be some singular, transformative moment that escapes conscious control or awareness. This moment could represent a radical change in one's nature or being that occurs beyond the reach of conscious thought, memory, or even desire. Through this reflection, it suggests that human identity and moral accountability are complex and possibly unstable. There may be pivotal experiences or changes that redefine a person in ways they neither foresee nor fully comprehend themselves. Hence, it questions the fairness or possibility of holding someone accountable for such a moment, as it may be beyond their conscious control. This theme ties into the novel’s broader exploration of illusion, memory, identity, and the blurred boundaries between life and death, consciousness and unconsciousness. The characters grapple with the elusive nature of reality and selfhood, where life is seen as a series of fragmented, shifting images and experiences, and where the individual self may itself be an "illusion" or subject to sudden, profound change. This passage invites readers to consider the mystery of human change and the limits of judgment, emphasizing the ambiguity and complexity of personal transformation within the novel’s dreamlike, introspective narrative.
  • “Death was the Orient whether it was heaven or earth” may refer to Murder on the Orient Express where Agatha Christie by putting an act of vigilantism at the heart of the mystery story reverses the roles of criminal and victim. This phrase suggests that death is like the Orient in the sense that it can be perceived as both a kind of heaven and a kind of earth or a place beyond ordinary understanding, where familiar dualities merge or dissolve. Death is portrayed as a realm that transcends the usual boundaries between life and afterlife, material and spiritual existence. It is a space that is both real and unreal, tangible and intangible, familiar and utterly unknown. The passage reflects on death as a kind of journey or transition, much like the mystical and often enigmatic idea of the Orient in Western imagination. It is a place where conventional distinctions blur: there is no heaven separate from earth, just as there is no clear division between life and death. The imagery evokes a liminal space where opposites converge, and the soul or spirit moves beyond the limitations of mortal life. It encapsulates the novel's theme of death as a profound, ambiguous, and ineffable state: neither fully this world nor the next, but a complex passage that holds mystery, transformation, and the blurring of boundaries
  • “But time could not tell him the time.” It reflects the profound confusion and disorientation experienced by Mr. Spitzer. Although time as a concept exists, and clocks or calendars mark its passage, to Mr. Spitzer, these markers are unreliable or meaningless. He cannot grasp or "tell" the exact moment or hour in his life because his perception of time is fractured: his memory and consciousness are impaired, and the usual order and progression of time seem disrupted or illusory. This line symbolizes how Mr. Spitzer lives in a state where time has lost its usual significance; it is an abstraction that fails to provide him with certainty or clarity about his existence or experiences. Time is no longer a steady, measurable flow but something ambiguous, elusive, and perhaps even cyclical or fragmented in his reality. It means that although time passes, Mr. Spitzer cannot understand or locate himself within it. The phrase captures his existential uncertainty and the novel’s broader themes of disintegration, memory loss, and the elusive nature of reality and identity.
  • “a great rajah’s jewel” is foreshadowing for a subplot later on in the book with Mr. Spitzer.
  • “He rescued all ladies but one he had imprisoned, leaving her to rot.” Given the novel's themes of complex and often painful relationships between men and women, and the recurring motif of loss, abandonment, and unfulfilled love, this line points to the paradoxical and tragic nature of such relationships. The man may have been a rescuer to many, but in this one critical case, he failed utterly, leaving the woman isolated and doomed.
  • “oh, how he hated these urban changes caused by the population growth” Mr. Spitzer in general is a conservative character wanting to preserve tradition. It reflects Mr. Spitzer’s lament over the transformations in the city and society brought about by increasing population and modernization. This sentiment expresses a nostalgic resistance to the loss of the familiar, orderly world he once knew. The urban changes symbolize not only physical alterations to the cityscape, such as new buildings replacing old ones, changes in neighborhoods, or the disappearance of landmarks but also deeper social and cultural shifts that disrupt established ways of life, traditions, and personal identities. Mr. Spitzer, who is described as a man deeply attached to order, precision, and the continuity of the past, finds these rapid and sometimes chaotic changes troubling. They represent a break from the stability and clarity he had valued, contributing to his sense of disorientation and loss. His dislike is tied to both the physical environment and the social fabric, as population growth often brings about overcrowding, new social dynamics, and a fading of old communities and familiar faces. This feeling is part of the larger themes in the novel dealing with change, memory, loss, and the struggle to hold on to identity amidst a shifting world. Mr. Spitzer’s lament is not just about city planning but about the inevitability of change and the personal and collective dislocation it causes. The phrase captures Mr. Spitzer’s sorrow and frustration at a world in flux, a world moving away from the ordered past he had known toward an uncertain and often alien present.
  • “Good-night and good-morning—simply to imply that she would live beyond the night enfolding her as if it were the night of love which makes no distinction between the living and the dead, the past and the present, the lover and the loved.” There is an Arabic phrase for the “Good-night and good-morning.” “The lover and the loved” may refer to Rumi or Sufism that also have similar sayings. The phrase emphasizes an understanding of existence in which love creates a unity that transcends time and mortality. It suggests that the separation we perceive (between life and death, or between selves in relationship) is ultimately illusory within this “night of love.” The continuity of love persists beyond physical life, blurring the lines that normally divide existence. This interpretation aligns with the novel’s larger thematic exploration of blurred realities, the coexistence of dream and wakefulness, and the merging of opposites like life and death, as seen in the characters’ experiences and reflections throughout the novel.
