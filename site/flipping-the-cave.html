Movement Two: The Pillars in the Traditions

Symbols: Hair, Wig, and Baldness

Philosophy: Plato and Platonism

Pillars: Pillar 2 (Reality Is Constructed) · Pillar 4 (Witnessing Is the Core Ethical Act) · Pillar 7 (The Margins Reveal the Truth)

Chapters discussed: 1, 3, 7, 17, 18, 19, 65

She was bald as the egg or as the rock where nothing grows. She had no hair on her head, no hair at all, not one hair to cast its shadow.

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, Chapter 17

The House Is the Cave

In the seventh book of Plato's Republic, prisoners sit chained in a cave, facing a wall. A fire behind them throws shadows of passing things onto the wall, and since the prisoners have never seen anything else, they take the shadows for the world. For Plato, philosophy is the work of turning a prisoner around, leading him up out of the cave, and bringing him at last into the sunlight. The cave is the mistake. The sun is the cure. Much of Western thought has kept that shape: there is one true light, the same for everyone who looks correctly, and a good life climbs toward it.

Young builds a cave of her own. The Cartwheel house sits on the New England coast with its drawing rooms shut for years. Catherine lies in bed in a room crowded with visitors no one else can see. The fog comes in under the doors. For Vera, growing up there, the line between the living and the dead, the real and the imagined, was never drawn in the first place.

Into this house walks the guide out of the cave. Miss MacIntosh comes from What Cheer, Iowa, and Young presents her exactly as the old story would want: “Her religion was truth to nature, nothing else, as she would always say with a severity of good humor inviting no argument, no sad or meandering response” (Chapter 1). She is “stoutly girded by her whale-boned corset, plainly clothed, visible to all, one who had kept her head above the waters in Chicago and elsewhere.” Even the corset arrives here as a virtue, the outward sign of an upright mind.

Young does not mock any of this, and neither does Vera. In a house where her mother cannot tell the living from the dead, the child needs something that holds still. Miss MacIntosh holds still. The comfort is real. What the novel will take apart is not the comfort but the claim folded inside it: that there is one plain reality, and this one woman has clean access to it.

The Guide Undressed

Young lets that claim stand for sixteen chapters. Then, in Chapter 17, Vera wakes in the night and sees her: “Miss MacIntosh stood by the blowing curtains, the tall windows, a figure dimly seen, her corset stays sticking out like the quills of an angry porcupine, or were those the rays of the moonlight? Where her head should be, there was another moon, cold and dented and shining.” The corset that stood for natural uprightness turns out to be a structure, held in place by force. And then Vera sees “that there was something missing from the mortal woman, that all was not right, for she was bald.”

The creed survives the night, which is stranger than if it had broken. In Chapter 18, “Stuff, fiddle, and balderdash—she suddenly exclaimed, the light beam dancing against her chalk-colored head in the dove-colored dawn—what was she saying? What were these angelic rivers to her, these fountains of unholy light, these immortal roses, these golden shores?” With her own head bare, she still waves away the whole furniture of Catherine's dreams in three nursery words.

In Chapter 19 Vera sees the rest. “Her red wig, almost purple as the light shone through its ragged edges, was perched like a rare tropical bird on the hat rack on the marble night stand next to her plain bed.” And beneath the corset: “I observed what I must surely have always known, the fact that one breast had been amputated. What was left was no bigger than a baby’s fist.”

The woman who taught that the soul must never turn from the bare face of reality has been hiding the bare face of her own. This is not a story about a liar. Miss MacIntosh keeps up her certainty in private, in the dark, with no one watching, against the evidence of her own body. That is what makes it sad rather than false. The champion of plain fact turns out to be the most carefully built person in the house. Plain realism, it turns out, is a shelter too, as much as Catherine's opium dreams.

The Figure Without a Shadow

Hold on to one phrase from Chapter 17: “not one hair to cast its shadow.” In Plato's story, shadows are the error. In Young's, they are something closer to proof that a person is a person. The word shadow, in its different forms, appears more than eight hundred times in the novel. The one figure who claimed to stand in the pure light, pointing everyone else out of the cave, is shown at the moment of exposure as the one who casts no shadow of her own. Shadows are what a self throws when it stands somewhere particular, partial, among others. To lose them is not to reach the sun. It is to lose the very medium that grief and love and knowledge pass through.

The Cave as Home

This is not a claim that nothing is real, or that every view is as good as every other. Young is saying something harder. The claim to stand outside all uncertainty, to see reality with no costume on, is itself a costume. The person who sees this about her own certainty is not made weaker by seeing it. She is freed. The stays can relax. The wig can come off. The scarred body can stand in the dawn for what it is, a human body, carrying its history.

So Young does not lead her people out of the cave. She asks us to learn to live in it honestly: to know an illusion as an illusion without demanding that it vanish, and to move through the Cartwheel house with the patient attention it asks of anyone who wants to understand it. The Garden, the Stream, and the Blind God follows the other half of this turn: what happens to Vera after she has seen.

The Philosopher King in a Nursemaid's Coat

Plato's Republic goes on past the cave. Once the philosopher has seen the sun, what is to be done with those who have not? Plato's answer is the philosopher king, who goes back down into the cave to govern the prisoners, guiding their lives by a vision of reality they cannot check for themselves. He does not ask for their consent. His right to rule comes from the claim that he has seen what they have not.

Miss MacIntosh rules the Cartwheel nursery in that spirit, though with love. In Chapter 3 she allows “no nonsense, no luxury of an endless moment, not even a game of chess as chess was the game of kings, an aristocratic pastime employing kings, queens, pawns upon the board checkered by shadows, all being not equal as they should be in her eyes if the game were to be democratic.” She forbids a game of kings while running a small kingdom of her own. The difference is that her rules are not called rules. They are called the way things are.

Her lessons show how that works. “Man was a poor fish, indeed, in Miss MacIntosh’s opinion. Everything had been put into its place, where everything must stay, not wandering out of its place, nor should the fishes fly above the dark spruce trees tipped by silver lights, nor should the white birds fly under the moving water, nor should we sail in a stone boat with stone sails.” It begins with plain truths. Stone sinks. Apples fall. But the same voice moves, without a break, from stones and apples to people, who must also know their place and stay in it. Vera's own training follows: her fingers must make “a pin-cushion or a pot-holder, a handkerchief with which to dry my foolish tears,” “for life was hard, and it was intended to be hard.” The tears are called foolish before anyone has asked why she is crying.

The deepest part of this rule is that Vera learns to speak it herself. In Chapter 1 she repeats the lesson in her own voice: “Common sense is the finest sense, she had always said, that the soul should not dream of those things far distant and not to be realized, for the way was very plain, quite direct. It was a granite road and not the sea road taken by the ships falling beyond a far horizon.” The guide has taught the child to see her own dreams as the guide sees them.

Hired by a Man of Two Opinions

Miss MacIntosh did not appoint herself. Mr. Spitzer, the estate's lawyer, hired her, and Young gives his reasons at the end of Chapter 3. He asked her no questions, “for none were necessary in such an obvious case, and there had not been a moment’s rapturous doubt in his mind which was usually of two opinions.” Looking back, he says: “He had thought, quite simply, that she would make a good nursemaid, providing a much needed contrast to this environment of phantoms and of shades, of suicidal persons, some only dreaming that they were dead, some only dreaming that they lived, that she would stand out as a stout-hearted practical woman who could never be fooled by the vagaries of others, including his own vagaries which had certainly fooled him.”

The most uncertain man in the novel hired its most certain woman, to supply the certainty he could not. That is often how such authority comes to be. It is called in by people who cannot bear what they cannot sort. And he never looked closely at what he had hired: “Frankly, he had never noticed precisely what Miss MacIntosh wore.”

The Salesman of the Absolute

Young gives the philosopher king one more face. In Chapter 65, as Vera tells it, Miss MacIntosh remembers a street preacher in Chicago, Mr. Bonebreaker, who walked with her in the rain. He had been “a clerk in a St. Louis shoe store where, when he was kneeling one day, God had shown him the waters of the first creation.” He has seen the source, and so he means to lead those who have not.

But his credentials are borrowed from the dead. “From the body of a dead waiter in a Chicago saloon, he had plucked his long-tailed coat. His broken shoes—they were a dead man’s shoes.” And his mind will not stay with the person beside him: “Mr. Bonebreaker’s mind jumped from the beginning to the end of creation, hardly considering the middle, the present scene.” Young's sentences live in exactly the middle he skips. When he turns Miss MacIntosh's wound into a verse, “Fore we have a little sister,” he recited, sonorously, “and she hath no breasts,” the two of them are not even weeping in the same story: “Miss MacIntosh wept furiously, thinking of Hagar, who had had a child pulling at her breast, but Mr. Bonebreaker was thinking of one who had no breasts, neither the left nor the right.” The Religious Architecture looks at the warmth he also offers her.

Logic Takes the Bus

The old schools taught three basic arts, called the Trivium: grammar, logic, and rhetoric. Logic sat at the top, the art closest to truth, the way a trained mind climbed out of mere words into proof. In Chapter 7, logic buys a ticket on Vera's bus.

Madge Capehorn knows the whole story of a woman who may have turned the key on her husband and left him to burn. Her husband, Homer, asks how she can know so much when she was never there. She answers: “Use your head, Homer Capehorn. Have you no imagination? I didn’t have to be there. If I had been there, I might not have known so well. There are some things you know better through never having been there. You do not live to tell of them.” Homer argues the way the textbooks teach: “She was sleeping, and she never walked in her sleep,” he argued. “You can’t prove she did it. You ought not to say anything you cannot prove. If it is not true, it must be false. It stands to reason, doesn’t it? It cannot be both true and false.”

Madge insists that “Keys never turn by themselves. Somebody always turns them.” And the reply comes back, without a name on it but with Homer's logic all over it: “She could have been asleep. Keys do turn by themselves.” Logic, denied its witness, does not climb into clear light. It talks itself into a house where keys turn with no hand on them. The town's long talk, held between many people and owned by none of them, keeps the world more answerable than proof could. Young even lets the questions float loose from their speakers, so that the sentences themselves refuse the clean, separate voices logic needs.

The Radical Immanence Reading

In the language of Radical Immanence, the wig on the hat rack is Pillar 2 (Reality Is Constructed): even plain fact is built and kept up by daily effort. Vera's night watch in Chapters 17 to 19, seeing what she must surely have always known, is Pillar 4 (Witnessing Is the Core Ethical Act). And Madge on the bus, with no proof and a whole town's knowledge, is Pillar 7 (The Margins Reveal the Truth). The cave, flipped, is not a prison to escape. It is the only home the soul has, and the work is to live in it with open eyes.
