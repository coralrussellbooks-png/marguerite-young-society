Chapter 29

On Chapter 29 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Christianity and Religion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Moses Hunnecker

Allusions: Ancient Egypt · Genesis

“when the trumpets of Jericho were blowing in the windy gardens”: Trumpets of Jericho refers to Joshua 6:1-27 in the Bible. The walls of Jericho fell after the Israelites marched around the city walls once a day for six days and seven times on the seventh day and blew their trumpets. Here the “trumpets” are also flowers blowing in the garden. See The Bible.

An old caterpillar sleeps "like the infant Moses in his cradle among the bulrushes," the child hidden on the water to save his life.

Their brilliance is likened to "the jewels of Aaron's breastplate," the twelve stones of Exodus that stood for the twelve tribes.

Butterflies whose colors rival "Joseph's coat of many colors" recall Genesis's beloved, betrayed son, sold into Egypt and raised to power.

“when he was divested of desire”: To Buddhists desire is the craving for worldly pleasure, material objects and immortality. Since all these things can never be attained they only lead to suffering. See Buddhism.

“the Happy Isles, the Fortunate Isles, the Isles of the Blessed”: The list runs from the Happy Isles to Aldebaran, Venus, the Garden of the Hesperides, the third and seventh heavens, Valhalla, Nirvana, and the happy hunting grounds, and ends: “God was not God in Himself, Mr. Spitzer believed, but in these pitiful images of God.” See Judaism, Buddhism, Hinduism, and Astronomy. The Happy Isles are the Isles of the Blessed, as in Tennyson’s “Ulysses” (1842): “It may be we shall touch the Happy Isles.” Fortunate Isles or Isles of the Blessed are semi-legendary islands in the Atlantic Ocean in Greek mythology. Garden of the Hesperides is Hera’s orchard where the golden apples grow. The third heaven in Judaism, Islam, Church of the Latter Day Saints and early Christianity is a division of heaven in religious cosmology as either the abode of God or a lower level of paradise (one of seven). (Wikipedia) Paul was “caught up to the third heaven” (2 Corinthians 12:2). The seventh heaven is one of the seven heavens found in various religious and mythological cosmologies. Valhalla in Norse mythology is the majestic hall located in Asgard and ruled by Odin. Nirvana is a concept in Indian religions that is a state of perfect quietude, freedom, highest happiness, liberation from worldly attachment, suffering and an end to existence. The happy hunting grounds is a concept of the after-life attributed to Native Americans in the US and was most likely made up by US settlers. The stars and planets are rich metaphors for the complexity and mystery of life, death, and the soul’s journey. They embody the fluid boundaries between states of being, the transient nature of beauty and reality, and the cosmic scale of human existence. Aldebaran is a giant star in the constellation Taurus. Venus is the second planet from the sun.

“God, it had been said, was the True Vine”: The list mixes titles of Christ from the Bible and Christian devotion (the True Vine, the Bread of Life, the Door, the Good Shepherd, the Light of the World, the Man of Sorrows, the Prince of Peace) with the Great Spirit of many Native American traditions, and Young sums it up: “God was many things to many people.” See The Bible, Augustine and Christian Theology, Revelation and the Apocalypse, and Judaism. True Vine: an allegory or parable in John 15:1-5 where Jesus’s disciples are described as branches of himself, who is described as the “true vine” and God the Father as the “husbandman.” Bread of Life: John 6:35, a statement by Jesus signifying that he is the source of spiritual nourishment and eternal life, not just physical sustenance. Gate and Door (John 10:9): words often used metaphorically to represent opportunities and access to God’s presence and salvation, with Jesus himself being referred to as both. Wine symbolizes joy, God’s blessings and provisions, and even the blood of Jesus Christ, but it also warns against the dangers of excessive drinking and intoxication. Can represent the fullness of God’s Kingdom (Isaiah’s prophecy). Mediator: a go-between or intercessor who reconciles two parties, particularly God and humanity, with Jesus Christ being the one and only mediator, bridging the gap between the divine and human. Moses was also a mediator between God and Israel. Kings, priests, and prophets also acted as mediators in the Old Testament, representing God to the people. 1 Timothy 2:5, Hebrews 8:6, Hebrews 9:15. Young’s “Methator” is likely a misprint for Mediator. Only-Begotten: emphasizes the unique relationship and divine nature of Jesus Christ as God’s Son. John 1:14 and John 3:16. The Nicene Creed is a foundational Christian statement of belief that affirms Jesus as the only-begotten Son of God. Anointed: signifies being consecrated or set apart for a special purpose or role, often through the ritual of anointing with oil which symbolizes God’s favor, power, and divine authority. 1 Samuel 10:1 and 16:13, Exodus 28:41 and Leviticus 8:12, 1 Kings 19:16. The Hebrew word for anointed is the root term of Messiah meaning The Anointed One. Psalm 23:5. Word: the Word refers to the concept that Jesus Christ, the divine Son of God, is the Word who existed with God from the beginning and through whom all things were made. John 1:1. Restorer: signifies God’s ability and desire to bring back to their former state, or even to a better state, what has been lost, damaged or broken, whether spiritually, physically, or relationally. The story of Job, Israel, and Jesus are examples. Resurrection: the belief that Jesus rose from the dead on the third day after his crucifixion. In Islam, the resurrection is also a key belief. Great Spirit: in many American Indigenous Nations represents a divine force that created and sustains all life with interpretations varying across different Nations. The Great Spirit emphasizes harmony with nature, communication, and guidance. Light of the World: signifies God’s role as a source of truth, guidance, and salvation, illuminating humanity from spiritual darkness. 1 John 1:5, John 8:12, Matthew 5:14-16, Psalm 119:105. Man of Sorrows or Man of Pains is in Isaiah 53:3-5 which describes Jesus Christ (God’s suffering servant) and his suffering and identification with humanity’s pain, as he bore our griefs and carried our sorrows. Old Man is an image used to describe God (Michelangelo’s Sistine Chapel ceiling) with a long white beard and robes. It is not universal but common in Western religious art and literature. Daniel 7:9, Revelation 1:14. King of Glory Psalm 24 It emphasizes his majesty, power, and sovereign rules over all creation. Jesus is also referred to as the Lord of glory 1 Corinthians 2:8. King of Kings is a title used in Judaism and Christianity to describe God’s supreme authority and power over all earthly rulers and powers. 1 Timothy 6:15, Revelation 17:14, 19:11-16. Prince of Peace Isaiah 9:6 is used to describe Jesus and his role as a bringer of peace and reconciliation between humanity and God, as well as among people. Philippians 4:7. Good Shepherd: Jesus Christ appears as the guide, the protector, the healer and shepherd of his sheep. Narrow Way: Matthew 7:13-14 (narrow gate and broad way), suggesting the path to salvation is difficult and requires effort. Psalm 24:7, John 10:2. Rose or Rose of Sharon refers to Jesus Christ as stated in the Song of Solomon 2:1. The rose in general can symbolize love, beauty, and hope. In Islamic mysticism the rose represents divine beauty, as in Saadi’s Gulistan (The Rose Garden, 1258). The rose of Tawhid means to make one, to integrate, to bring unity to that which is fragmented. Thorn: in 2 Corinthians 12:7 Paul refers to a “thorn in the flesh” given to him by God to keep him humble and dependent on Christ’s power, rather than becoming conceited from his revelations. When Paul prayed to God to remove the thorn, God’s response was to strengthen him in his weakness rather than remove it. Risen: the belief that Jesus Christ through his resurrection demonstrated victory over death and offers hope of eternal life. Immanuel: meaning “God with us,” a title for Jesus Christ highlighting God’s presence and intimate relationship with humanity. Isaiah 7:14 and Matthew 1:23. Word made Flesh: God, who through Jesus Christ is believed to have taken on human form, becoming both fully God and fully human. John 1:14, John 1:1. Advocate: Jesus Christ, who is seen as mediator and intercessor between humanity and God, pleading for forgiveness and reconciliation. 1 John 2:1. The Holy Spirit is also referred to as an advocate for God. Intercessor: Jesus Christ as the ultimate intercessor between God and humanity, always living to intercede for those who come to God through him. Hebrews 7:25, 1 John 2:1, John 1:9, Romans 8:26-28. In Islam Shafa’a is the act of pleading to God by an intimate friend of God for forgiveness. In Judaism Moses is the model of intercessory prayer. In Hinduism the Grand Intercessor symbolizes the Divine Mother’s pivotal role in connecting humanity with God, notably embodied by Shri Lakshmi, called purushakara, the one who intercedes with Vishnu for the devotee, in the Sri Vaishnava tradition. Dove: a prominent symbol of the Holy Spirit. Matthew 3:16, Mark 1:10, Luke 3:22, John 1:32, Genesis 8:8-12. In Judaism the dove is a symbol of the soul. In Greek mythology doves are associated with Aphrodite, the goddess of love. King of Light often describes God symbolizing purity, truth, and divine presence, contrasting with darkness and evil. 1 John 1:5, John 8:12, James 1:17. God is also referred to as The Lord of Light and The Red God. King of Darkness or prince of darkness refers to Satan, the leader of spiritual forces of evil, who opposes God and His kingdom. Ephesians 6:12, John 8:44, Colossians 2:15, 2 Corinthians 11:14. Earlier in Chapters 2, 3, 7, 17, 20 and 21.

"the Rose, the Thorn" gathers the crown of thorns, Christ's beauty and his suffering held in a single image.

Mr. Spitzer's musing on "the Word made Flesh" is John 1:14, the incarnation, God become human.

“many other gods, the god of the sun, the goddess of the moon, the judge of the dead, the busy clerk of the underworld”: The gods are Egyptian. Osiris judged the dead, and Thoth, the scribe of the gods, recorded the weighing of the heart (Book of the Dead, spell 125). Young’s list goes on to “the goddess with the head of a cat” (Bastet) and “the jackal god who was the conductor of the dead” (Anubis). See Ancient Egypt. The god of the sun may refer to a solar deity which is a sky deity who represents the Sun, or an aspect of it. Such deities are usually associated with power and strength. Solar deities and Sun worship can be found throughout most of recorded history in various forms. In Egypt, the sun god was Ra. (Wikipedia) The goddess of the moon may refer to a lunar deity and sometimes as a personification. These deities can have a variety of functions and traditions depending upon the culture, but they are often related. Some form of moon worship can be found in most ancient religions. (Wikipedia) The goddess with the head of a cat may refer to Bastet who is a goddess of ancient Egyptian religion, worshiped as early as the Second Dynasty. In ancient Greek religion, she was known as Ailuros. The goddess of wisdom may refer to Ma’at or Isis of Egypt, or to Athena from Greek mythology. The jackal god may refer to Anubis who is the god of death, mummification, embalming, the afterlife, cemeteries, tombs, and the Underworld, in ancient Egyptian religion. The judge of the dead is Osiris, who presided over the weighing of the heart in ancient Egyptian religion. In Christianity, God is the judge of the dead. The busy clerk of the underworld is Thoth, the ibis-headed scribe of the gods, who records the weighing of the heart.

“before the separation of the waters from the land, before the division of light from darkness”: “God divided the light from the darkness… and let the dry land appear” (Genesis 1:4, 9). See Genesis.

“even in a dark night of the human soul”: Dark Night of the Soul is a poem written by a Spanish Christian mystic St. John of the Cross (c. 1578). Dark night in Roman Catholic spirituality describes a spiritual crisis in the journey toward union with God. See Augustine and Christian Theology.

“the last infernal circle of this Divine Comedy”: Divine Comedy (c. 1308-21) is a long Italian narrative poem by Dante Alighieri. It depicts the afterlife according to the Western Church medieval world view of the time. (Wikipedia) Like The Pilgrim’s Progress (1678) it is a journey from sin to heaven. See Dante.

“that a new tenant had not taken over an old, empty house”: Spirit possession is an altered state of consciousness and associated behaviors which are purportedly caused by the control of a human body and its functions by spirits, ghosts, demons, angels, or gods. The concept of spirit possession exists in many cultures and religions, including Buddhism, Hinduism, Islam, Christianity, Judaism, Wicca, Haitian Vodou, Dominican Vudú, and Southeast Asian, African, and Native American traditions. Depending on the cultural context in which it is found, possession may be thought of as voluntary or involuntary and may be considered to have beneficial or detrimental effects on the host. (Wikipedia)

“the seal of hermetic privacy”: Hermeticism is a philosophical and religious system based on the writings attributed to Hermes Trismegistus, a legendary figure in ancient Greece and Egypt. The core principles are: the belief in a single, transcendent God or cosmic principle, there is a correspondence between the macrocosm (universe) and the microcosm (humans and the world around them) as used in the term “As above, so below,” and, in the modern Hermetic book The Kybalion (1908), seven principles (mentalism, correspondence, vibration, polarity, rhythm, cause and effect, gender). Hermeticism has influenced various religious and philosophical traditions. A “hermetic seal” is airtight, named for Hermes Trismegistus, supposed inventor of the alchemist’s sealed glass. See Astrology, Alchemy, and Esoterica.

“out of the whirlwind”: “Then the LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind” (Job 38:1). See The Book of Job. Earlier in Chapters 14 and 21.

“as the plume of Raphael”: The plume (feather) of Raphael may refer to the archangel. See Angels and Archangels.

“like Jacob with his ladder of dreams”: Jacob’s Ladder is a ladder leading to heaven that was featured in a dream the biblical Patriarch Jacob had during his flight from his brother Esau in Genesis 28:12. The significance of the dream has been debated, but most interpretations agree that it identified Jacob with the obligations and inheritance of the people chosen by God, as understood in Abrahamic religions. (Wikipedia) The King James text reads: “And he dreamed, and behold a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven: and behold the angels of God ascending and descending on it.” See Genesis. Earlier in Chapter 14.

“He swore that their chant would be almost Gregorian”: Gregorian chant is a form of monophonic, unaccompanied sacred song in Latin of the Roman Catholic Church. (Wikipedia) See Music and Composers.
