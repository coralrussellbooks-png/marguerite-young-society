Exploring Young's Poetry

The Same Ideas Across The Four Books

← The Vision At Its Most Concentrated · Exploring Young's Poetry · Conclusion →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Not One Self

The poems open the single self in "Like Secret Roots" and "Ventriloquist: The Coffee Hour." Its longest home is Miss MacIntosh, where one consciousness pours through Vera, her opium-dreaming mother Catherine, the bald nursemaid, and Mr. Spitzer until they can no longer be told cleanly apart. In Harp Song the plural self turns public: Eugene Debs is at once the private man from Terre Haute and the symbol of a whole movement made of him.

Reality Is Constructed

The poems capture the built world in "Dubious Ground" and "Bishop Berkeley of Cloyne." Its longest home is Angel in the Forest, whose subject is two utopias invented from nothing on one patch of Indiana ground: Father George Rapp's "Scriptural communism" and the rationalist colony of Robert Owen, "an English cotton lord, who believed all men to be machines." Each rebuilt money, work, marriage, and worship by decree, and each came apart. Harp Song carries the same recognition into economics, showing the wage system Debs fought as an arrangement people made and could unmake, not a law of nature.

Grief Doesn't End

Stated most nakedly in the poems of Movement IV. In Miss MacIntosh it is Mr. Spitzer's endless mourning and Esther Longtree's stillbirths, grief that never converts itself into recovery. In Angel it is the sorrow left in the grass after paradise fails. In Harp Song it is Debs outliving the defeat of nearly everything he built.

Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Not a subject but a stance, and the same stance in all four books. It is how Young writes Angel, standing in the ruins of New Harmony without tidying them into a lesson. It is Vera's whole task as narrator of the novel. It is the refusal, in Harp Song, to shrink Debs into either a saint or a cautionary tale. The readings in this book have tried to keep the same discipline.

Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The poems hold the unfinished open in "Daniel" and "A Crystal Principle." Its fullest work is in Angel in the Forest, whose two failed utopias are not mocked but honored as experiments, since "in imperfection itself there might be many values," and "nothing so beautiful as trial and error, this side of paradise."

Unfinished Is Honest

The moral edge of the same idea, and again the method of all four books more than a topic in any one. Angel refuses to call either utopia simply a failure. The novel refuses a final account of what was dream and what was real. Harp Song refuses to close Debs's case with a verdict. This volume refuses to be the last word on the poems.

The Margins Reveal the Truth

The poems give it the misfit and the condemned in "Silken Hair," "Lot's Wife," and "Milton's Wife." Its longest home is split three ways. Angel recovers the forgotten communitarians and celibates at the margin of the official record. The novel is built almost entirely of figures the world cannot file. And Harp Song is the life of a man who saw the country most clearly from a prison cell, and who counted himself unfree as long as a single soul remained in one.

The Past Doesn't Go Away

The poems press the past up under the present in "Legend in Grass" and "Currin Valley." Angel is the extended demonstration: two utopias layered in one ground, the whole book an argument that no arrangement was inevitable and every one had builders. Harp Song keeps the same watch over labor's buried history, refusing the story in which the present order simply arrived on its own.

Language Shapes What's Possible

Distributed across all four, because it is finally a fact about Young's own sentences. The poems name it in "Experience in Song." But the baroque, self-aware prose of the three longer books, and Angel's decision to call a documented history a fairy tale, keep the maker's hand permanently in view. No book in the set pretends to speak from nowhere.

The Sacred Is Here

The center of the vision, held at its purest in the poems by "Sky of Grasses." Its longest home is Angel in the Forest, which measures a promised heaven against "a city as measured by the jimson weed" and watches paradise come to rest in common soil. The novel finds the same holiness in the smallest domestic and natural detail, and Harp Song locates it in ordinary working people rather than in any promised future state.

The Crossing Point

The poems state it in the war poems of Movement VI, and it is the moral hinge of Harp Song for a Radical, whose whole subject is what a cause may ask one generation to spend for the next. Debs's answer, refusing to trade the future for a wartime advantage, going to prison rather than sign his name to that spending, and then running for president from a cell, is the Crossing Point lived out to the end of a single life.

Eleven ideas, one vision, four books. The poems have shown it whole and small. The history, the novel, and the life are where it opens out. A reader holding these terms can walk into any of the three and find the ground already familiar, which is the only kind of arrival Young ever trusted: not a destination reached at last, but the same ground, underfoot, opening further.
