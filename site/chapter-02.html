To All My Darlings

Chapter 2

On Chapter 2 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 30, 2026

← Chapter 1 · Home · Chapter 3 →

Summary

Vera describes her mother, Catherine. She is an opium addict who has lain in bed for years among jewels and phantoms and treats her hallucinations as more real than the world. The chapter traces how she came to this. There was a brief marriage to Jock Cartwheel, and a love triangle with twin brothers: Catherine loved Peron Spitzer, who did not love her, while his brother Joachim loved Catherine without return. Joachim, Mr. Spitzer, is the family lawyer and visits her bedside every evening. An Egyptian sailor arrives looking for the dead Peron and mistakes Joachim for him, and Catherine gives the sailor elaborate directions through her labyrinthine house. There is also the vanished chauffeur, James, who long ago made off with her automobile and who, in her visions, drives her endlessly toward Alaska. Whenever her canary dies, Mr. Spitzer secretly replaces it. The chapter ends when a canary escapes into a thunderstorm. Catherine calmly accepts that she must now live forever and dismisses its distant twittering as another dream.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a vivid description of Catherine’s detachment from reality, her retreat into a world of dreams, phantoms, and visions. She sleeps in a stately bed, surrounded by jewels and illusions, her body horizontal and still but her mind endlessly active in a twilight realm where days and nights blur into one. This passage establishes her condition as one suspended between life and death, reality and fantasy, and introduces her as a luminous, ethereal figure whose identity is intertwined with opium dreams and spectral visitors like dead kings, angels, and movie stars. The birthmark she conceals and the shadowed cheek symbolize her hidden imperfections and her withdrawal from the world.
  • This chapter explains the complex, tragic backstory of Catherine’s life, including her failed marriage to Jock Cartwheel, a reckless sportsman who disappeared in the Alps, the social humiliations she endured, and the inheritance that burdened her. It also reveals her denial of her father’s strict domination and orthodox faith, and her agnostic stance despite the angels in her opium paradise. It deepens the understanding of her motivations for retreating into this dreamlike existence and the loss and abandonment she suffered.
  • The relationship between Catherine and Mr. Spitzer, a semi-retired lawyer who visits her faithfully every evening, is a crucial element. He is portrayed as a somewhat hapless, absent-minded man broken by his brother’s death, yet devoted to Catherine. Their interactions mix reality and fantasy, with Catherine often tormenting him with her elusive remarks and suggestions of invisible presences, while he tries to maintain order and reason. This dynamic illustrates the tension between her dream world and his grasp on reality, as well as her complex feelings of resentment and fondness toward him.
  • This chapter emphasizes Catherine’s uncanny knowledge of Mr. Spitzer’s movements and minor details of his life which he himself forgets. It raises the question of whether she is truly alive or already dead, and whether Mr. Spitzer even exists as she perceives him. Her world is one of illusions and ghostly realities, where distinctions between life and death, real and unreal, blur. The servants’ superstitions about her nocturnal wanderings and mysterious occurrences in the house add to the enigmatic aura.
  • A significant episode is the arrival of a gold-turbaned Egyptian sailor who claims to have come looking for Mr. Spitzer’s dead twin brother. Catherine insists on the Egyptian sailor’s reality, giving him detailed instructions to navigate the labyrinthine house, while Mr. Spitzer tries to convince the Egyptian sailor of his own death (his twin brother, Peron) and the mistake in identity. This episode symbolizes the permeable boundary between the worlds of the living and the dead, and Catherine’s role as a mediator or gatekeeper of these overlapping realities.
  • Here Catherine expresses a worldview in which the dream is the only reality worth accepting, because the real world is inevitably disappointing and unrealized. She sees life as a vast deception, and the dreams as protective and expansive, allowing a multiplicity of experiences and visions that transcend strict physical reality. This philosophical stance encapsulates the chapter’s major theme which is the tension between illusion and reality and the necessity of dreams to sustain life.
  • Catherine’s only faithful companion is a little yellow canary, kept in a gilded cage and drugged to preserve its fragile existence. The bird symbolizes the tenuous thread of life sustaining her, and the careful subterfuge to replace the canary when it dies illustrates the fragile illusions that maintain her dream world and her existence. Mr. Spitzer’s role in maintaining this secret further highlights the delicate balance between reality and fantasy that defines her life.
  • Catherine imagines herself endlessly riding with James, her ex-chauffeur, toward Alaska, a place she never reaches despite years of travel. This endless, repetitive journey through an endless city symbolizes her trapped state between worlds, the impossibility of escape or arrival, and the persistence of memory and illusion. James’s dark intentions and her helplessness add a note of impending danger to this surreal journey.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • Catherine “presumed always that things were not what they seemed, that all forms must change their shapes.” How does the chapter itself keep changing shape as it moves from her bed to Mr. Spitzer’s walks, the Egyptian, James, and the canary?
  • The dreams “had begotten her, and she was their creation. She had not created the dreams, for they had created her.” What does it mean for a person to be made by her own dreams?
  • Catherine gives the Egyptian directions through a house where “there were rooms between the rooms, and now the doors had changed their places.” How is the house a map of her mind?
  • The servants reason that Catherine must walk at night, “how else could she know so much?” How does the chapter keep both explanations open, the secret detective and the omniscient dreamer?

Characters

  • Mr. Spitzer “did not believe in Fate but in self-determination,” yet “Fate had assigned this peculiar role to him.” Is he faithful by choice, by habit, or by fate?
  • Mr. Spitzer’s “stupidity was now become, in fact, his only unusual gift, almost sublime.” What can a slow, literal-minded man protect that a brilliant one could not?
  • Catherine refused the Mr. Spitzer who loved her for the brother who did not, because of “bodily resemblances beclouding dissimilars.” How do the twins divide what she wanted between them?
  • “That James was absent made him always present.” Why does Catherine’s mind keep the one servant who robbed her and who, in her visions, plots to kill her?
  • Catherine’s blind mother “had fooled many of those who were closest to her,” and her father’s Seeing Eye dog was blind too. What does this family history of hidden blindness suggest about Catherine’s visions?

Connections

  • The canaries are replaced so that “the illusions were sustained, even by precarious subterfuge,” and Mr. Spitzer arranges it. How does this kindly deception compare with the deception Catherine believes the whole world plays on her?
  • Catherine says there are “two worlds,” “this and its counterpart,” and that “no great distinction should be made between the living and the dead.” How does the visit of the Egyptian, looking for a dead man and finding his living twin, act out this belief? See Twins and Doubles.
  • In Chapter 1 Vera rides a bus into the Midwest, searching. Here Catherine drives forever toward Alaska and never arrives. How do the mother’s journey and the daughter’s journey differ?

Reflection

  • Catherine “pretended that the real was the dream, that the dream was the real,” and calls this “a universal failure, that on which the whole of life depended.” In what ways does ordinary life depend on dreams that will never be checked against reality?
  • When Marco Polo flies into the storm, Catherine is calm, “resigned hopelessly to the fact that now she must certainly live, of course, forever.” Why might losing the thing a life depends on bring calm instead of despair?

Research: Characters

Catherine Helena Cartwheel (Snowden)

  • A central figure who has withdrawn from life into a dreamlike, opium-fueled existence.
  • She suffers from an invisible birthmark (a blot on her left cheek), which symbolizes hidden flaws and may represent her motivation for retreating from reality.
  • She is portrayed as a “beautiful corpse,” eternally young yet fixed in a horizontal, motionless state.
  • Surrounds herself with phantoms, dreams, and visions including historical figures, spirits, and imaginary visitors.
  • She is complexly caught between sanity and madness, reality and fantasy, embodying a state of suspended animation.
  • Her retreat to bed and isolation is linked to personal trauma: an unhappy marriage (Jock Cartwheel), abandonment, social humiliation, and a controlling father.

Mr. Spitzer (Joachim)

  • Catherine’s faithful but often ignored visitor, a semi-retired lawyer.
  • He is a figure of reason, order, and routine, marked by absent-mindedness and a broken career after the death of his twin brother (Peron).
  • Despite his marriage proposal being rejected by Catherine, he visits her regularly.
  • He represents the tether to reality and the outside world.
  • His relationship with Catherine is ambiguous; he is both her guardian and unrequited lover.

James, the Chauffeur

  • An ex-convict and former servant who has abandoned the household but remains a figure in Catherine’s dreams.
  • Symbolizes loyalty, escape, and impending threat.
  • Imagined as driving them endlessly to Alaska, plotting harm but never quite reaching the destination.

The Little Yellow Canary

  • Catherine’s only faithful companion, kept in a gilded cage.
  • Symbolizes life, hope, and fragile continuity amidst decay and madness.
  • Catherine believes her life is bound to the canary’s survival.
  • Mr. Spitzer secretly arranges for the canaries to be replaced to maintain the illusion of continuity.

An Egyptian Sailor

  • Depicted vividly as “gold-turbaned, his face glistening like clouded water,” immediately evoking an exotic and otherworldly presence.
  • His arrival is marked by a sense of ambiguity and displacement.
  • He is described as a lost traveler, both literally and metaphorically, crossing boundaries between worlds and times.
  • The sailor’s purpose is to find Mr. Spitzer.
  • Despite the confusion and mistaken identity that ensues, the Egyptian’s quest is sincere and urgent. He carries a piece of papyrus, which is later revealed to be a race dope sheet, connecting him to earthly concerns such as horse racing and victory, yet his demeanor and context suggest he is more than just a mundane visitor.
  • His presence introduces a collision between the “real world” and the mysterious, spectral realm inhabited by Catherine and her guests.
  • The Egyptian Sailor symbolizes the crossing of boundaries: life and death, reality and dream, the past and the present.
  • His lost voyage, caused by “ambiguities” such as misread stars and winds, mirrors Catherine’s own disorientation in time and space, encapsulating themes of fate, chance, and the unknowable.
  • His dance of ecstatic excitement upon seeing Mr. Spitzer, and the subsequent confusion over identity, emphasize the fluidity of existence in this narrative.
  • His interaction with Mr. Spitzer underscores the tension between the tangible and the ethereal, as Mr. Spitzer tries to convince him of the death of his brother, while Catherine embraces the coexistence of these two realms.
  • The Egyptian’s presence unsettles Mr. Spitzer, reflecting a resistance to the intrusion of the supernatural or the past into his orderly, if somewhat diminished, life.
  • Catherine’s support of the Egyptian Sailor, defending his reality and expressing fury at the servants’ neglect of him, reveals her own deep immersion in the dream world and her rejection of mundane reality.
  • This dynamic portrays the Egyptian as a catalyst for revealing Catherine’s fractured state and the complex interplay between sanity and madness.
  • He may also be a reference to “The Shipwrecked Sailor.” It is an ancient Egyptian tale, likely composed during the Middle Kingdom (c. 2040-1782 BCE), found on a papyrus scroll. It tells the story of a sailor who is shipwrecked and marooned on a mythical island, where he encounters a giant serpent and eventually returns home with tales and treasures.

Mr. Res Tacamah

  • Mr. Res Tacamah’s characterization by Catherine as a “drug bottle with ears” implies a dual role: both as a source of the narcotic influence that enables her prolonged sleep-like state and as a silent witness or confidant to her fragmented consciousness and delirium.
  • The figure’s “ears to hear her ravings” indicates that this presence is attuned to her incoherent, abstracted speech, thereby symbolizing the inanimate yet intimate object through which her mind communicates and seeks solace.
  • It also underscores the isolation and detachment from reality experienced by Catherine, for her closest “companions” are non-human, immaterial entities or hallucinations rather than living persons.
  • Mr. Res Tacamah, as a drug bottle, is part of the apparatus that maintains her “slumberous life,” preventing her madness from overtaking her by regulating her doses of opium and sedatives.
  • His presence reflects the complex interplay between dependency, control, and the fragile boundary between wakefulness and sleep that defines her existence.
  • Mr. Res Tacamah is part of Catherine’s dreamscape, a personification of the substances and hallucinations that populate her consciousness.
  • He is described with a proper name, which humanizes the drug bottle and elevates its significance in her interior world, emphasizing how deeply embedded the opium experience is in the fabric of her identity and daily life.

Mr. Chandelier

  • Mr. Chandelier can be seen as an anthropomorphized object, a personification of the chandelier itself.
  • Catherine imagines him as her companion, “not always faithful,” suggesting a capricious, elusive presence that mirrors the instability and unreliability of her dream world.
  • This personification transforms an inanimate object into a character with agency and presence in her inner life, blurring the boundaries between reality and imagination.
  • Mr. Chandelier is described with almost ghostly qualities: he has “seven lighted candles in his seven crystal hands,” an image evoking mysticism and ritual, linking him to the theme of ritualistic control and orthodoxy that pervades Catherine’s background, especially considering her father’s “fantastic rituals each hour of day or night”.
  • The seven candles may also evoke the idea of illumination, yet in her world, this illumination is shadowed, uncertain, part of the “eternal twilight” that envelops her perception.
  • Mr. Chandelier’s presence also serves as a counterpoint to Catherine’s physical paralysis and mental wandering. While she lies “horizontal,” immobile, he is often depicted as moving between stillness and motion, reality and dream. This duality reflects Catherine’s own suspended state between life and death, consciousness and oblivion.
  • Mr. Chandelier is an emblematic figure embodying the interplay of light and shadow, life and death, presence and absence in her opium-induced limbo.
  • Mr. Chandelier’s “seven crystal hands” holding candles may symbolize the multiplicity of facets in Catherine’s fractured consciousness and fragmented reality. Each candle can be interpreted as a flickering point of awareness, a fragile beacon in the “glassy surface” of her uncertain existence. Yet these lights are also delicate and impermanent, just as her world is “this play of illusion” where “all forms must change their shapes”.

Catherine’s parents: mother, father and seeing eye dog

  • Catherine’s mother suffers from a progressive hereditary blindness, which is emphasized repeatedly in the novel.
  • This condition is not merely a physical ailment but also a metaphorical element that shapes her entire existence.
  • Despite losing her sight gradually, she maintains an extraordinary awareness of her surroundings. The narrative highlights her ability to navigate the world with remarkable precision.
  • This portrayal elevates her beyond being a passive victim of blindness; instead, she becomes a figure of resilience and heightened sensory perception, compensating for the loss of sight with other faculties.
  • Her blindness also affects those around her deeply, especially her husband.
  • The husband’s reaction to her blindness is poignant, he begins to wear a dark eyeshade, symbolically aligning himself with her experience of darkness, as if to share her loss or express his mourning for the light she has lost.
  • The husband’s transformation is profound: his body is described metaphorically as “turning into starlight,” and when he eventually becomes blind himself, he relies on a Seeing Eye dog.
  • The Seeing Eye dog, interestingly, is blind as well, yet it leads its master flawlessly, a symbolic reinforcement of the theme of navigating darkness through trust, adaptation, and interdependence.
  • This shared blindness and the mutual care between the husband and wife deepen the portrayal of their relationship and the familial experience of blindness.
  • Catherine’s father, too, is depicted as a figure who lived his last years in darkness, physically blind but mentally and emotionally attuned to his environment.
  • He walks in the garden he planted for his blind wife, following carefully designed paths free of obstacles, suggesting both his love and his protective role.
  • His actions, closing his eyes to sense flowers by texture and odor, reflect a profound adaptation to blindness and an intimate connection with the sensory world beyond vision.
  • His death, alongside that of the old Seeing Eye dog, symbolizes the closing of a chapter marked by darkness but also by dignity and acceptance.

Research: Themes

Reality vs. Illusion

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Catherine inhabits a world where dreams and hallucinations are often more “real” than the tangible world.
  • The novel blurs boundaries between waking life and the dream state, questioning the nature of existence.
  • Catherine’s perception that the real is the dream and the dream is the real reflects a universal human tendency to escape disappointing reality.

Life and Death / Suspension between States

See: Death and Mortality

  • Catherine is described as a living corpse, suspended between life and death, awake and asleep.
  • Her physical body is motionless, yet her mind roams freely through time, space, and memory.
  • Death is never final. There are multiple selves, many dead canaries, and visitors from the spirit world.

Memory and Forgetting

See: Memory and the Past

  • Catherine’s memories are fragmented, clouded, and often replaced by fantasy.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s forgetfulness contrasts with her photographic recall of his movements and lost incidents.
  • The past is both a refuge and a source of pain since she relives moments that never fully happened.

Madness and Sanity

See: Madness and Sanity

  • Catherine’s mental state is ambiguous; she is at times lucid, at times delusional.
  • Opium and drugs regulate her condition, creating a “scheduled madness” that preserves her life.
  • Her madness is a form of self-preservation and rebellion against a brutal world.

Isolation and Dependency

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Catherine is socially and physically isolated, confined to her bed.
  • She depends on a select few (Mr. Spitzer, the canary, James in her imagination) for companionship.
  • The household staff is largely dismissed or feared, emphasizing her detachment from ordinary life.

The Clock and Time

Two Sister Ravens

See: Birds · Twins and Doubles

  • The ravens appear symbolically and thematically as mysterious, almost otherworldly figures that contribute to the surreal, dreamlike atmosphere surrounding Catherine and her suspended existence between life and death.
  • The ravens are described as “two sister ravens who had created the universe.” This description situates the ravens not merely as birds but as powerful, almost divine entities involved in cosmic creation and the shaping of reality itself.
  • Their role emphasizes Catherine’s immersion in a realm where boundaries between reality and illusion, life and death, are blurred.
  • The ravens, linked with creation, suggest a world that is constantly in flux, shaped by mysterious forces beyond ordinary human perception.
  • The ravens belong to the same inverted world in which Catherine asks whether, if servants were known as she was known, their lives would not appear “more monstrous than hers and terribly extra-human.”
  • Their presence as mythic creators contrasts with Catherine’s physical stasis and withdrawal, underlining the tension between dynamic cosmic forces and her horizontal, dream-bound existence.
  • Ravens traditionally symbolize mystery, death, and prophecy in literature and myth, fitting well with Catherine’s liminal state caught between life and death, and her visions of phantoms, spirits, and the dead.
  • Their “sister” relationship may also suggest duality and companionship in a lonely, spectral world where Catherine resides, further personifying the enigmatic and haunting nature of her dreams.

Research: Resources

  • Catherine comes from the Greek Aikaterine, a name of uncertain origin. From early times it was linked with the Greek katharos, “pure,” which is why it is often glossed as “pure” or “innocent.” See On the names (Ch 2).
  • Helena, Catherine’s middle name, is the Latin form of the Greek Helen, often glossed as “torch” or “the bright one.” It is the name of Helen of Troy.
  • Snowden, Catherine’s maiden name, comes from the Old English for “snow hill.”
  • Cartwheel, the wheel of a cart, is an English word from the late fourteenth century. The acrobat’s sideways handspring is recorded from 1861, and cartwheel as a verb from 1907.
  • “the prosaic, fusty, busty old nursemaid”: In the opium lady’s house Young was the prosaic one. Offered opium every evening, she always refused, and Weissenbach called her “the prosaic sprite… because I didn’t need drugs to dream” (Marguerite Young, interview with Charles Ruas, WBAI radio, New York). See Catherine Cartwheel.
  • “her red, marcelled hair”: Marcelled hair is a hair styling technique using hot curling tongs to make deep, regular waves. The Paris hairdresser François Marcel Grateau introduced the method in 1872. It was a popular style for women’s hair in the 1920s often used with a bob cut.
    • Miss MacIntosh “is made up of three or four spinsters in Indianapolis” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). “One of them wore a wig. When I was a little girl, she fascinated us all with her wig and her spare wig. She had two wigs,” and “Ethel Barrymore sort of influenced my idea of the way Miss McIntosh walked” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983). Susan Neville records a woman in Young’s Indianapolis house who “wore a spare wig to keep her head warm, like Miss MacIntosh” (Susan Neville, “Where the Landscape Moved Like Waves,” in Sailing the Inland Sea, Indiana University Press, 2007).
  • “by anything protean”: Proteus is the shape-shifting old man of the sea in Homer’s Odyssey, who turns into a lion, a serpent, a leopard, a boar, running water, and a tree to escape Menelaus. From him comes the word protean, “ever-changing.” Catherine expects “that all forms must change their shapes.” See Homer’s Epics.
  • “for the opium dreams surrounding her had provided no pillars of strength”: Catherine Cartwheel was drawn from a woman Young knew, Minna K. Weissenbach, “the opium lady of Hyde Park” (David O. Dowling, “The Guru,” in A Delicate Aggression: Savagery and Survival in the Iowa Writers’ Workshop, Yale University Press, 2019). As a Depression student at the University of Chicago, Young was sent to Billings Hospital to read Shakespeare aloud to her, and she read to her for two years, “often during her opium dreams.” Weissenbach had been a patron of Hull House and of Poetry magazine and a friend of Harriet Monroe and Thornton Wilder, and Young dedicated Moderate Fable to her (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983). She was addicted “not through choice, but through medication” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). When Young told Wilder she was writing about her, he answered, “as a way of revealing the unconscious.” Only near the end of the book did Young see that the opium lady was also, “in a way,” her own mother, “a kind of Titania-like dreamer” (Marguerite Young, interview with Charles Ruas, WBAI radio, New York). See Catherine Cartwheel and The History of Opium.
  • “Heavily laden with jewels as a Greek corpse”: The ancient Greeks often buried their dead with jewelry, and the richest graves are famous for it. At Mycenae in 1876 Heinrich Schliemann opened royal shaft graves where the bodies lay covered in gold diadems, cups, and death masks, among them the so-called Mask of Agamemnon.
  • “as filled with visitors as the Grand Central Station”: Grand Central Terminal in New York City opened in 1913, replacing the earlier Grand Central Depot and Grand Central Station, and the old name stuck. It is often said that some 750,000 people pass through it each day, and “like Grand Central Station” became the stock phrase for a crowded place.
  • “some from the shores of Hades, voices of the dead”: In Greek mythology the underworld was ruled by Hades, god of the dead and a son of Cronus and Rhea. The dead had to cross the river Styx or Acheron to reach his realm. See Greek Mythology.
  • “faded movie stars of the silent flicker films”: Silent films, made from the 1890s until sound arrived in the late 1920s, had no synchronized recorded sound or dialogue. Early projection flickered on the screen, so the movies were called “flickers,” later “flicks.” They were accompanied by live music.
  • “imaginary telephone operators plugging in at imaginary switchboards”: A telephone switchboard was a vertical panel of jacks. Operators, most of them young women, connected each call by hand, plugging a cord into the right jack.
  • “drowned pearl-divers”: Pearl-divers recover pearls from wild mollusks (oysters or mussels) in the sea or fresh water, holding their breath on long dives. Pearls and pearl-divers return throughout the chapter in Catherine’s “numberless pearls.”
  • Oedipus was a mythical Greek king of Thebes. He unknowingly fulfilled the prophecy that he would kill his father and marry his mother, bringing disaster to his city and family. His story is best known from Sophocles’ Oedipus the King, and Freud named the “Oedipus complex” after him.
  • Troy was the city in Asia Minor that the Greeks, led by King Agamemnon, besieged for ten years. Homer’s Iliad tells of part of the tenth year of the war, and the city’s fall is told in the Odyssey and in Virgil’s Aeneid. See Homer’s Epics.
  • “New England spinsters with faces checkered like chessboards”: New England spinsters like Susan B. Anthony and Louisa May Alcott were famous. Spinster first named a woman who spun wool or flax for a living, and from the seventeenth century it became the legal term for an unmarried woman. In nineteenth-century New England, many unmarried women were regarded as “highly moral and fully womanly creatures,” and many wrote in their journals that they “remained unmarried not because of individual shortcomings but because they didn’t find the one ‘who could be all things to the heart.’” See Women’s Suffrage.
  • “a blemish, a stain, a birthmark”: Birthmarks have been called everything from an Angel Kiss to a Mark of the Devil. They have been feared and also seen as lucky omens with special meaning. Nathaniel Hawthorne’s story “The Birth-Mark” (1843) tells of a scientist who cannot bear the small mark on his beautiful wife’s cheek and kills her trying to remove it. See American Writers.
    • The birthmark was Weissenbach’s: “One was that she had an almost invisible birthmark, and this Chicago lady did, and she always said that she turned her cheek toward the shadow. She would never allow that part of her face to be seen. Although I never saw the birthmark” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).
  • “fine white rice powder”: Face powder made from finely ground rice was used for centuries, from the white makeup of Japanese geisha to the drawing rooms of Europe, to give the skin a pale, even finish.
  • “numberless pearls running through her ivory-colored fingers”: The pearl is the only gem made by a living creature, layered around a wound or irritant, and Catherine, who hides her own blemish, runs “numberless pearls” through her fingers all through the chapter. Pearls symbolize wisdom acquired through experience, purity, generosity, integrity, and loyalty. They are believed to attract wealth and luck as well as offer protection. See Pearls and Jewels.
  • “her lunar hysteria”: The phrase joins two old beliefs about women. Hysteria comes from the Greek hystera, “womb,” because doctors long blamed women’s nervous disorders on the womb, and the moon was thought to rule the womb’s monthly cycle. No reliable lunar effects on human behavior are known, though the moon’s cycle does govern the spawning of many sea animals, such as corals. Some studies suggest that around 30% of women may start their period near the new moon, but this is likely due to random chance, as statistically, a certain percentage of cycles would be expected to coincide with any given phase of the moon. The idea of a connection between the moon and menstruation is an old one, potentially stemming from the fact that the terms “menstruation” and “menses” are derived from words meaning month and moon, respectively. While a direct link between lunar phases and menstruation is not supported by current research, some studies suggest the moon may have an indirect influence on sleep and mood, which could indirectly affect the menstrual cycle for some individuals.
  • The idea of the moon influencing human behavior has been around for centuries and is deeply ingrained in many cultures. There is no scientific evidence to support the idea of lunar hysteria, also known as lunacy or the belief that the full moon causes erratic behavior or mental instability. While the term “lunacy” is derived from the Latin word “luna” for moon, and the belief in a lunar effect on human behavior is ancient, numerous studies have debunked this connection.
  • “the needle pits in her arm, the opium dosages”: The hypodermic syringe was developed in the 1850s by Alexander Wood in Scotland and Charles Pravaz in France. Injected morphine, the chief alkaloid of opium, soon became a common treatment for pain and “nerves,” and the marks of the needle a sign of addiction.
  • Opium and its derivatives, such as laudanum and morphine, were sold freely in America throughout the nineteenth century in patent medicines and on prescription. By the late 1800s women made up about sixty percent of American opiate addicts, many of them introduced to the drug by doctors treating “female complaints.” The Harrison Narcotics Act of 1914 brought the sale of opiates under federal control. See On opium (Ch 2), Opium, and The History of Opium.
  • “the life-sized chessmen moving in the garden”: Lewis Carroll’s Through the Looking-Glass (1871) turns a whole countryside into a chessboard, where Alice becomes a pawn and the chess pieces walk and talk. Catherine’s house has “magnified chessmen,” and Mr. Spitzer finds himself moved about like a piece in her game. See Alice in Wonderland.
  • “two sister ravens who had created the universe”: Ravens are creators in several mythologies. In the stories of the Haida, Tlingit, and other peoples of the Pacific Northwest, Raven brings light to the world by stealing the sun, moon, and stars. In Norse myth, Odin’s two ravens, Huginn and Muninn, “Thought” and “Memory,” fly over the world each day and return to tell him everything they have seen. See Birds.
  • Edgar Allan Poe wrote the poem, The Raven, as a narrative, without intentional allegory or didacticism. The main theme of the poem is one of undying devotion. The scholar experiences a perverse conflict between desire to forget and desire to remember. He seems to get some pleasure from focusing on loss. He assumes that the word “Nevermore” is the raven’s “only stock and store,” and yet he continues to ask it questions, knowing what the answer will be. His questions, then, are purposely self-deprecating and further incite his feelings of loss. Poe leaves it unclear whether the raven actually knows what it is saying or whether it really intends to cause a reaction in the poem’s narrator. The scholar begins as “weak and weary,” becomes regretful and grief-stricken, before passing into a frenzy and, finally, madness. Christopher F. S. Maligec suggests the poem is a type of elegiac paraclausithyron, an ancient Greek and Roman poetic form consisting of the lament of an excluded, locked-out lover at the sealed door of his beloved. See Edgar Allan Poe.
  • In traditional women’s roles the daughter passed from the protection of her father’s family to the husband. Jock, “a professional ne’er-do-well,” did not honor this tradition, and Catherine paid him a fortune to disappear.
  • “Scaling the Jungfrau or Matterhorn”: The Jungfrau, “the Maiden,” is a peak in the Bernese Alps of Switzerland, first climbed in 1811. The saddle below it, the Jungfraujoch, has been reached by railway since 1912.
  • Matterhorn is on the border between Switzerland and Italy. It was the last great Alpine peak to be climbed. On the first ascent in 1865, four of Edward Whymper’s party fell to their deaths on the way down, and the body of one was never found.
  • “some fairy grotto of rock crystal chandeliers and musical stalactites”: Stalactites ring like bells when struck. In the 1950s, at Luray Caverns in Virginia, an inventor fitted the cave with the Great Stalacpipe Organ, which plays tunes by tapping the stalactites with rubber-tipped mallets.
  • “wearing a white rose on his coat lapel”: The rose has significant meaning in various folk cultures and traditions, and each color of the rose can have different meanings. White: Purity, innocence, new beginnings, reverence, and remembrance. Jock vanishes into the snow wearing the flower of remembrance, “so should be easily recognized.” See Roses and Flowers.
  • The Dolomites are a mountain range in northeastern Italy, part of the Southern Alps, known for their pale limestone peaks.
  • “all the furnitures of earth and choirs of obsessed angels being also only an idea in the mind of God, and God was dead or sleeping”: Young borrows the phrase from George Berkeley, who wrote in his Principles of Human Knowledge (1710) that “all the choir of heaven and furniture of the earth” have no existence apart from being perceived, and that things go on existing when no one looks because they are held in the mind of God. Nietzsche declared in The Gay Science (1882) that “God is dead.” On Mount Carmel, Elijah mocked the prophets of Baal that their god must be busy or away, “or peradventure he sleepeth” (1 Kings 18:27). If God is “dead or sleeping,” as Catherine is, nothing holds the world in being but her own dreaming mind. See Empiricism (Locke, Berkeley, Hume) and Nietzsche.
  • “his factories having been conducted like clockwork, wheels within wheels”: The phrase comes from the prophet Ezekiel’s vision of the heavenly chariot: “their appearance and their work was as it were a wheel in the middle of a wheel” (Ezekiel 1:16). It came to mean any intricate system of hidden motives and machinery. See The Bible.
  • “a place set at his table for the wanderer angel”: Hebrews 13:2 reads, “Be not forgetful to entertain strangers: for thereby some have entertained angels unawares.” The verse recalls Abraham, who fed three strangers who proved to be messengers of God (Genesis 18). At the Passover seder a place and a cup are set for the prophet Elijah. Catherine’s father “would turn no beggar away from his door for fear it might be an angel in disguise.” See Angels and Archangels.
  • “an angel who had come from Tarsus”: Tarsus was the capital of the Roman province of Cilicia. It is the birthplace of Paul the Apostle, the great traveler of the early church, whose journeys are told in the Acts of the Apostles.
  • “had divided the waters from the earth and had created great whales”: Genesis 1:9-10 tells how God gathered the waters together and let the dry land appear, and Genesis 1:21 reads, “And God created great whales, and every living creature that moveth.” See Genesis.
  • “the twelve goblets for the twelve hours in their places”: The number twelve has been significant in religions and nature. In nature there are about twelve lunar months in a solar year, and Jupiter takes about twelve years to circle the sun. The Western and Chinese zodiacs both have twelve signs. In Greek myth there are twelve Olympians and twelve Titans, and Hercules carries out twelve labors. In Aeschylus’s Eumenides, Athena calls a jury of Athenian citizens, often counted as twelve, the number still used for American trial juries. In the Bible Ishmael has twelve sons, and Jacob has twelve sons who are the ancestors of the twelve tribes of Israel. Jesus chooses twelve Apostles, the Book of Revelation returns to the number again and again, and Christmas has its twelve days. The house also holds “twelve grand pianos,” and no one ever plays them.
  • “She was the agnostic”: An agnostic is someone who believes that the existence or non-existence of a god or ultimate reality is unknown or unknowable. They neither affirm nor deny the existence of a higher power, holding that the truth about such matters is inaccessible to human understanding. Agnosticism is primarily concerned with the possibility of knowledge rather than the belief in a deity. It focuses on whether or not humans can have knowledge about the existence or nature of a god, not whether they believe in one. Agnostics typically acknowledge that there is no definitive proof either for or against the existence of God or other supernatural beings. Agnosticism doesn’t necessarily mean a lack of spirituality or belief in something greater than oneself. Some agnostics may hold spiritual beliefs or practices while still maintaining that the ultimate reality is unknowable. The word was coined by the biologist Thomas Henry Huxley in 1869.
  • “the harpers harped, and the seas were turned to glass”: Both images come from the Book of Revelation, “I heard the voice of harpers harping with their harps” (Revelation 14:2), and “I saw as it were a sea of glass mingled with fire” (Revelation 15:2). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “Mr. Res Tacamah, the drug bottle”: Res is Latin for “thing,” and the Rx on a prescription stands for the Latin recipe, “take.” Tacamah is a form of tacamahac, a strong-smelling resin from the balsam poplar and certain tropical trees, used in making ointments and incense. See Mr. Res Tacamah.
  • “proud Bostonians, Platonists of the period of the intellectual flowering of Athens”: Boston called itself “the Athens of America,” and Van Wyck Brooks’s The Flowering of New England (1936) told the story of its literary golden age. Ralph Waldo Emerson, who wrote that “Plato is philosophy, and philosophy, Plato,” and Bronson Alcott, a devoted Platonist who led public “Conversations,” were among its leaders.
  • Platonism is the philosophy of Plato which affirms the existence of abstract objects, the Forms, which are asserted to exist in a third realm distinct from both the sensible external world and from the internal world of consciousness. It is opposed by nominalism, which holds that only individual things exist. See Plato and Platonism.
  • “centaurs with lilies in their mouths”: The centaur of Greek mythology has the upper body of a human and the lower body and legs of a horse. Most centaurs were wild, but Chiron was wise and taught Achilles and Asclepius.
  • “great frogs with jewels in their foreheads”: An old belief held that the toad carried a precious stone, the toadstone, in its head, a cure for poison. Shakespeare used it in As You Like It: “Sweet are the uses of adversity, / Which, like the toad, ugly and venomous, / Wears yet a precious jewel in his head.” See Shakespeare.
  • “He did not believe in Fate but in self-determination”: Fate is the development of events beyond a person’s control, regarded as determined by a supernatural power. Among early Christians fate was approached in different ways. One approach is “divine omnicausality” where the assumption would be that God causes every creaturely event that occurs and in this way exercises comprehensive control over the minutest aspects of his creatures’ activities. See Fate and Chance.
  • Self-determination, as Mr. Spitzer means it, is the power of a person to decide his own course, the free will that philosophers set against fate and determinism. In psychology, self-determination is concerned with the motivation behind choices people make and the degree to which an individual’s behavior is self-motivated and self-determined.
  • Joachim, Mr. Spitzer’s first name, comes from the Hebrew Yehoyakim, “raised up by God” or “God will establish.” In Christian tradition Joachim is the father of the Virgin Mary.
  • Peron, the name of Mr. Spitzer’s dead twin, is a form of Peter, from the Greek petros, “rock.” See Peron Spitzer and Twins and Doubles.
  • “a bed she imagined as a swan boat”: The Swan Boats in Boston are pedal-powered pleasure boats, each with a great swan at the stern, that carry passengers around the lagoon in the Boston Public Garden. They have been run by the same family since 1877, inspired by Wagner’s opera Lohengrin, in which a knight arrives in a boat drawn by a swan.
  • “he had written only silent music”: In the philosophy of music, idealists hold that musical works are mental entities, imaginary objects that exist in the mind of the composer or listener. John Keats wrote in “Ode on a Grecian Urn” (1819), “Heard melodies are sweet, but those unheard / Are sweeter.” See Music, Sound, and Silence.
  • “a gambler, a spender of borrowed money, a quick suicide, a four-flusher”: Peron is the reckless, worldly twin, “very different from cautious Mr. Spitzer.” The good and bad twin, or the twins who are mistaken for each other, run through literature from Jacob and Esau to Shakespeare’s Comedy of Errors.
  • Alexander Pope was an English poet of the eighteenth century, best known for The Rape of the Lock, An Essay on Man, and his translations of Homer’s Iliad and Odyssey. A childhood illness, probably tuberculosis of the spine, left him hunchbacked and about four and a half feet tall, and in pain for the rest of his life.
  • Lady Mary Wortley Montagu was a writer who challenged the social attitudes of her time toward women and their intellectual growth. Her letters from Turkey, where her husband was ambassador, are among the classics of travel writing, and she brought the practice of smallpox inoculation to England. Pope was smitten with her and wrote her passionate letters, but she did not return his feelings, and after their quarrel he attacked her in his verse.
  • Lord Byron was an English poet and leading figure in the Romantic movement of his era. He was born with a deformity of his foot which caused him to limp. In the summer of 1816, Byron, Percy Bysshe Shelley, and Mary Shelley, who wrote Frankenstein that summer, were a close circle at Lake Geneva. See Romantic Poets.
  • “Shelley’s bright hair like a comet in the wind”: Percy Bysshe Shelley, the Romantic poet of “Ode to the West Wind” and “Ozymandias,” drowned when his boat went down in a storm off the Italian coast in 1822. His body was burned on the beach, and his friend Edward Trelawny said he snatched the heart from the flames.
  • “blind Milton dictating Paradise Lost to those indifferent daughters”: John Milton was completely blind by 1652 and composed Paradise Lost (1667) in his head, dictating it to whoever was at hand, including his daughters. Early biographers reported that the daughters resented the work, and that he had them read aloud to him in languages they did not understand. See Milton and Paradise Lost.
  • Intertextuality is the shaping of a text’s meaning by another text, either through deliberate compositional strategies such as quotation, allusion, calque, plagiarism, translation, pastiche or parody, or by interconnections between similar or related works perceived by an audience or reader of the text. These references are sometimes made deliberately and depend on a reader’s prior knowledge and understanding of the referent, but the effect of intertextuality is not always intentional and is sometimes inadvertent. Often associated with strategies employed by writers working in imaginative registers (fiction, poetry, and drama and even non-written texts like performance art and digital media), intertextuality may now be understood as intrinsic to any text. Catherine’s bedroom, crowded with Pope, Byron, Shelley, and Milton, is a small instance of Young’s method. See Catherine Cartwheel in Intertextuality in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
  • “the Boston South Station”: South Station, opened in 1899, was the busiest railroad station in the world in the early twentieth century, the terminal for trains to New York and the south.
  • “ski trains of the vanished skiers”: In 1931 the Boston and Maine Railroad began running “snow trains” that carried skiers out of Boston to the hills of New Hampshire, a fashion that lasted into the 1950s.
  • “her in the seventh crystal drawing room”: Seven has many cultural and religious meanings: it is a significant number in almost every major religion (Christianity, God rested on the seventh day; seven symbolizes the unity of the four corners of the Earth with the Holy Trinity; it is repeated in the Book of Revelation); it is associated with luck and magical properties. See On the number seven (Ch 2). Catherine’s seventh drawing room joins the chapter’s other sevens, Mr. Chandelier’s “seven lighted candles in his seven crystal hands” and the octopus “wearing seven ruby crowns.”
  • “is there or is there not an invisible elephant in the corridor”: The elephant is famous for its memory, as in the proverb “an elephant never forgets,” and Catherine’s elephant can “remember everything.” In Ivan Krylov’s fable “The Inquisitive Man” (1814), a visitor to a museum describes every tiny insect but never notices the elephant, a story often named as a forerunner of “the elephant in the room.”
    • The elephant was Weissenbach’s too. “I was with her when she entertained an imaginary elephant,” Young said, an elephant “in the hallway,” and when Adlai Stevenson spoke in a campaign of an invisible elephant, she wrote to tell him about it (Marguerite Young, interview with Charles Ruas, WBAI radio, New York).
  • “But the burden of proof, Mr. Spitzer had always hesitantly maintained”: In law, the burden of proof lies with the party who makes a claim, and the old maxim says that the one who asserts must prove. Mr. Spitzer, the lawyer, places it on Catherine, “who had seen the invisible.” The phrase echoes Hebrews 11:27, where Moses “endured, as seeing him who is invisible.”
  • “the decapitated, bleeding head of John the Baptist”: John the Baptist was an itinerant preacher and revered as a major religious figure. The gospels portray John as the precursor of Jesus in Christianity. Herod had him beheaded at the request of Salome, the daughter of Herodias, and his head was brought “in a charger” (Matthew 14:8). See The Bible.
    • “I was literally with her when she had a long conversation with the head of John the Baptist” (Marguerite Young, interview with Charles Ruas, WBAI radio, New York).
  • “Her foot was but the monument in a desert”: The image recalls Shelley’s “Ozymandias” (1818), where a traveler finds “Two vast and trunkless legs of stone / Stand in the desert,” all that is left of a king’s statue. See Romantic Poets.
  • “treeless campagna”: The Campagna is a low-lying area surrounding Rome in the Lazio region of central Italy, once a famously empty, malarial plain dotted with the ruins of Roman tombs and aqueducts.
  • “snakes and headless horsemen”: The headless horseman has appeared in folklore around the world since the Middle Ages. The best-known American version is Washington Irving’s “The Legend of Sleepy Hollow” (1820), where the horseman chases the schoolmaster Ichabod Crane. See American Writers.
  • “the burning of unbaptized babes and of green leaves in Purgatory”: The Irish maid mixes two teachings. In Catholic belief, Purgatory is the temporary state where the souls of the saved are purified before heaven. Unbaptized infants were not sent there. Augustine held that they shared the mildest punishment of the damned, and later theologians placed them in Limbo, a place of natural happiness without the sight of God. See Augustine and Christian Theology.
  • “a game of chance, a number’s racket, a baseball game of which the score was already known”: In 1919 eight players of the Chicago White Sox took money from gamblers to lose the World Series, the “Black Sox” scandal, a baseball game whose score was known before it was played.
  • Mr. Spitzer wears “his brother’s blue-stoned ring” on his finger, and elsewhere in the novel he wears other articles of clothing and accessories that belonged to his dead brother Peron and to Jock Cartwheel.
  • “the Boston Public Garden”: The Boston Public Garden, established in 1837, is a park in the heart of Boston, next to Boston Common, and the first public botanical garden in America. Together with the Common it is the first link in the Emerald Necklace, the chain of Boston parks completed by Frederick Law Olmsted.
  • “a woman all in white, her face shrouded by a white umbrella”: In legend, a woman in white often foretells a tragedy or impending death, unrequited love, or betrayal by a husband or fiancé. The white dress symbolizes purity and also loss.
  • The Woman in White is Wilkie Collins’s fifth published novel, published in 1860 and set from 1849 to 1850. It is a mystery novel and falls under the genre of “sensation novels.” A theme of the story is the unequal position of married women in law at the time. Laura Glyde’s interests have been neglected by her uncle, and her fortune of £20,000 (then an enormous sum of money) by default falls to her husband on her death. Collins dedicated this novel to Bryan Procter, poet and Commissioner for Lunacy, and was inspired by the case of Louisa Nottidge, who was abducted and imprisoned for the monetary convenience of her family. Women could be imprisoned in “lunatic asylums” if they became embarrassing or inconvenient to their husbands or fathers. In addition, before the passage of the Married Women’s Property Act 1882, all of a wife’s assets passed automatically to her husband. William Wilkie Collins criticized the institution of marriage. He had relationships with two women: the widow Caroline Graves, with whom he lived for most of his life, treating her daughter as his own, and the younger Martha Rudd, with whom he had three children. See On the Woman in White (Ch 2).
  • “Perhaps the woman was the swan”: In Greek myth, Zeus came to Leda in the form of a swan, and in folk tales across Europe swan maidens shed their feathered skins to become women, the story behind Tchaikovsky’s ballet Swan Lake (1877). The woman in white on the swan boat may be the swan herself, another of the chapter’s shifting forms.
  • “stopped at the top of a rusted ferris wheel”: The engineer George Ferris built the first Ferris wheel for the World’s Columbian Exposition in Chicago in 1893. It stood 264 feet high, and its cars each held sixty people.
  • “Oblivion was his brother”: In Hesiod’s Theogony, Sleep (Hypnos) and Death (Thanatos) are twin brothers, the sons of Night. The river Lethe, “forgetfulness,” flows through the underworld, and the dead drink from it to forget their lives. Mr. Spitzer, whose twin is dead, has oblivion for a brother. See Greek Mythology.
  • “a secret detective to trace him, a shadow, a private eye, a gumshoe”: The Pinkerton National Detective Agency, founded in Chicago in 1850, took as its emblem an open eye with the motto “We Never Sleep,” and the phrase “private eye” is often traced to it. Gumshoe comes from the soft rubber-soled shoes that let a detective follow without a sound. See The Chicago Detective.
  • “upon which boxer would triumph, the black or the white”: In 1908 Jack Johnson became the first Black heavyweight champion of the world, and white promoters spent years searching for a “Great White Hope” to beat him. When Johnson defeated the former champion James J. Jeffries in 1910, riots broke out in cities across the country. Mr. Spitzer lays “his bet on the black man.” See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “on the black rooster in the black night”: The rooster is symbolic in many different religions. In Christianity it is admired for courage and perseverance. It was used for ceremony and sacrifice. The cock’s crow announces the dawn, and here “the golden feathers of light had streamed through the window” and the bet was lost. In the Gospels Peter denies Jesus three times before the cock crows. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “a lead nickel into an old-time player piano”: A lead nickel was a counterfeit or slug, a worthless coin. Coin-operated player pianos and orchestrions, which played piano, drums, and pipes all at once, filled saloons and amusement halls from the 1890s through the 1920s, “one no doubt playing an entire orchestra.”
  • “like some old Pan among the silver reeds”: In Ovid’s Metamorphoses, the nymph Syrinx flees the goat-god Pan and is turned into reeds, and Pan makes his pipes from them. Elizabeth Barrett Browning retold the story in “A Musical Instrument”: “What was he doing, the great god Pan, / Down in the reeds by the river?” See Greek Mythology.
  • “such as a dusty sparrow falling”: Jesus says of the sparrows, “one of them shall not fall on the ground without your Father” (Matthew 10:29), and Hamlet answers his friend’s fears with “There is special providence in the fall of a sparrow.” Catherine later counts “falling sparrows, falling leaves, drops of water, grains of sand.” See Shakespeare.
  • “his trust in law of which he knew no jot”: Jesus says in Matthew 5:18, “Till heaven and earth pass, one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law.” A jot is the Greek iota, the smallest letter, so Mr. Spitzer knows not even the smallest letter of the law he trusts.
  • Solipsism is a philosophical theory that posits the only thing a person can be sure exists is their own mind and consciousness. It suggests that the external world, other people, and even one’s own body might be mere illusions or figments of the imagination. Solipsism is an extreme form of subjective idealism, meaning it focuses primarily on the individual’s own subjective experience. Solipsism argues that knowledge of anything outside one’s own mind is uncertain or impossible. A central question in solipsism is the problem of other minds, which asks how anyone can know for sure that other people have minds and experiences like their own. Solipsism contrasts the certainty of one’s own mental state (thoughts, feelings, memories) with the uncertainty of anything outside of it, like the physical world or other conscious beings. There can be different interpretations of solipsism, including metaphysical solipsism (where only one’s own mind is real), epistemological solipsism (where nothing outside one’s own mind can be proved to exist), and methodological solipsism (where the self is the starting point for understanding reality). While the term solipsism can sometimes be used in a psychological context, it should be distinguished from the formal philosophical idea. “Solipsism syndrome” is a term used to describe a feeling of isolation and detachment from the outside world, which may be related to solipsistic thinking but is not a diagnosed condition.

Catherine’s world is “this deception played upon her.” See Descartes and Dualism.

In quantum mechanics, the act of observation affects what is observed at the most fundamental levels. In 1935 Erwin Schrödinger imagined a cat sealed in a box with a poison triggered by chance, which by the rules of the theory is both alive and dead until someone opens the box. See Time, Physics, and Chaos Theory.

See Catherine Cartwheel.
  • “these drowned Medusas”: In Greek mythology Medusa, whose name means “guardian” or “protectress,” is the most famous of the monster figures known as Gorgons, whose look turned people to stone. Because a jellyfish trails its tentacles like snaky hair, naturalists call its bell-shaped swimming form a medusa, which suits Catherine’s undersea vision of “balloon fishes” and “giant crabs.”

The clock was first installed in 1410 and is the oldest astronomical clock still operating. Its moving figures of the Apostles, added in 1787 to 1791, file past two small windows every hour, the “Walk of the Apostles.”

It is named for Inverness, the chief town of the Scottish Highlands.

In Catherine’s universe no one steers at all. See the Chapter 1 resources.
  • “the rich who dreamed they were poor, the poor yearning for unjust wealth”: Catherine’s list sets the rich who play at poverty, as Marie Antoinette played milkmaid in her toy village at Versailles, beside the poor who dream of riches. People dream or fantasize and strive for something more than themselves or their present circumstances. Even if people seem to have it all there is still something else out there that they imagine would make it better. For the poor this may be the only way to break free of their poverty and lack of access.

  • “the right wing of the dove”: Young turns the political right wing back into a bird’s wing. The dove is the bird of peace that returned to Noah’s ark with an olive leaf (Genesis 8:11) and the form in which the Holy Spirit descended at Jesus’s baptism (Matthew 3:16), so the phrase sets the politics of left and right beside a wing of peace.

It highlights the interconnectedness of events, suggesting that seemingly insignificant actions can trigger large, unpredictable consequences. The meteorologist Edward Lorenz described the effect in 1963 and named it in a 1972 talk, “Does the Flap of a Butterfly’s Wings in Brazil Set Off a Tornado in Texas?” Ray Bradbury had already told such a story in “A Sound of Thunder” (1952), where a time traveler steps on a prehistoric butterfly and changes the future.

Hart Crane made it the central symbol of his long poem The Bridge (1930). Catherine and James are “always crossing Brooklyn Bridge” later in the chapter. See American Writers.

  • “the rabbits dreaming of the rabbit god”: In Chinese folk religion, Tu’er Shen, the Rabbit God, is the patron of love between men, first described in an eighteenth-century tale by Yuan Mei. Chinese legend also places a Jade Rabbit on the moon, pounding the elixir of immortality.

  • “Why had her father not allowed to any woman in his house a higher education”: Colleges for women were still new in the nineteenth century. Mount Holyoke opened as a seminary in 1837, Vassar in 1861, and Wellesley and Smith in 1875, and Radcliffe began as the Harvard Annex in 1879. Instead Catherine was given “walking, dancing, surf-riding, horsemanship, harp-playing, tennis.” See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “an octopus wearing seven ruby crowns or only old, moving Mr. Chandelier with seven lighted candles in his seven crystal hands”: The Book of Revelation shows “a great red dragon, having seven heads and ten horns, and seven crowns upon his heads” (Revelation 12:3), and a vision of the Son of Man standing among “seven golden candlesticks,” holding “in his right hand seven stars” (Revelation 1:12-16). See Mr. Chandelier and Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “charted as on a mariner’s chart which shows the course of a voyage through waters which remain unknown”: Old sea charts filled their unknown waters with monsters and whirlpools. The Carta Marina of Olaus Magnus (1539) shows sea serpents and whales attacking ships off Scandinavia, and the Hunt-Lenox Globe of about 1510 carries the Latin warning “HC SVNT DRACONES,” “here are dragons.”
  • “the sum of all the parts exceeded the sum, though there had been no mistake in addition”: The saying that “the whole is greater than the sum of its parts” is a paraphrase of Aristotle, who wrote in the Metaphysics that a whole is something beside its parts. Catherine, who can only “multiply, divide,” keeps her own arithmetic. See Aristotle.
  • “this and its counterpart, a dark star, the other”: The Pythagorean philosopher Philolaus, in the fifth century B.C., taught that the earth circles a central fire along with a hidden Counter-Earth, the Antichthon, which no one can see because it always lies on the far side. See Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres.
  • “a drowned Egyptian sailor, gold-turbaned”: The Egyptian may recall the “Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor,” a Middle Kingdom story preserved on a single papyrus now in the Hermitage Museum in St. Petersburg. In it a shipwrecked traveler, cast up on an enchanted island, meets a primordial god in the form of a great serpent, who gives him a gift of moral vision to carry home to Egypt. Further, Richard Mathews writes that this “oldest fantasy text contains archetypal narrative of the genre: an uninitiated hero on a sea journey is thrown off course by a storm, encounters an enchanted island, confronts a monster, and survives, wiser for the experience. . . .” The tale itself begins with a framing device in which an attendant (“follower” or “the sailor”) tries to comfort his master, who is returning from an apparently failed expedition and is anxious about how the king will receive him. (The serpent calls himself the lord of Punt. The Land of Punt was an ancient kingdom, likely located in the Horn of Africa, that traded extensively with ancient Egypt. It was a source of valuable goods like gold, ebony, ivory, and aromatic resins, which were highly sought after by the Egyptians. While the exact location remains debated, current evidence points towards a coastal region encompassing parts of present-day Somalia, Djibouti, Eritrea, and northeast Sudan. The kingdom’s existence is primarily known through Egyptian records and depictions, particularly from the Middle and New Kingdom periods.) An attendant reassures him, advising him on how to behave before the king, and repeating the proverb, “The mouth of a man saves him.” To encourage his master, he tells a tale of a previous voyage of his in which he overcame disaster, including meeting with a god and the king. The tale ends with the master saying, “Do not make the excellent (that is, do not act arrogantly) my friend; why give water to a goose (literally, bird) at dawn before its slaughtering in the morning?” Young’s sailor is lost too, “not knowing his path,” his captain having misread “the rotations of the antique stars.” See On the Egyptian sailor (Ch 2), The Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor, and Ancient Egypt.
    • Young told Studs Terkel where the sailor began: “there was a boat which reached New York Harbor, and the captain of the crew thought that he should be in Boston Harbor. He had misread some of the signs, and I simply used that incident to bring in my own imagination about a drowned Egyptian sailor” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).
  • “a table of the second empire, a bishop’s stone chair”: The Second Empire was the reign of Napoleon III in France (1852 to 1870), whose heavy, gilded, richly upholstered style filled fashionable houses. A bishop’s chair is a cathedra, and the church that holds it is therefore a cathedral.
  • “the auguries of soothsayers and of birds and of horses’ heads”: The Roman augurs read the will of the gods in the flight and feeding of birds. The historian Tacitus wrote in his Germania that the Germanic tribes kept sacred white horses and read omens in their neighing and snorting.
  • “colder than Lapland in the celestial furies of winter”: Lapland is the northernmost region of Finland and also a province of Sweden. Both lie within Sápmi, the homeland of the Sámi people, which stretches across northern Norway, Sweden, Finland, and Russia’s Kola Peninsula, much of it above the Arctic Circle.
  • “the schools of faceless dolphins which should follow in his wake, the great birds with teeth”: Birds with teeth once existed. In the 1870s the paleontologist Othniel Charles Marsh described Hesperornis and Ichthyornis, toothed seabirds of the Cretaceous, from fossils found in Kansas.
  • “He should not be afraid of the minotaur”: The Minotaur is a mythical creature with the head and tail of a bull and body of a man. King Minos kept him in the Labyrinth built by Daedalus, and Theseus killed him and found his way out by following the thread Ariadne gave him. Catherine’s directions through her house, “turn to the right, turn to the left, circle back,” are a labyrinth without a thread. See Greek Mythology.
  • “a gramophone with Edison’s first record”: Thomas Edison invented the phonograph in 1877, and the first words he recorded, on a cylinder wrapped in tinfoil, were “Mary had a little lamb.” Emile Berliner’s gramophone of 1887 played flat discs instead of cylinders, so, strictly speaking, Edison’s first record could not have played on a gramophone, one of the impossibilities of Catherine’s house.
  • “L’Illustration of a decade ago”: L’Illustration was a weekly French newspaper published in Paris from 1843 to 1944. Catherine’s copy lies open at the seventh page, covered with dust.
  • “an ancient, mummified pharaoh wrapped in cloth of gold”: Howard Carter’s opening of the tomb of Tutankhamun in 1922 made the mummified pharaoh in his gold coffins a worldwide sensation and set off a fashion for all things Egyptian.
  • “her bicycle built for two”: The phrase comes from the song “Daisy Bell” (1892) by Harry Dacre: “But you’ll look sweet upon the seat / Of a bicycle built for two.”
  • “the chauffeur whose uniform might well be that of a Hussar”: Hussars were light cavalry that began in fifteenth-century Hungary and spread across Europe, famous for their braided jackets and fur-trimmed pelisses. Their dress became shorthand for military splendor, which is why a chauffeur’s livery could be mistaken for one.
  • “her beautiful White Steam Castle or her Packard or her Ford”: The White Sewing Machine Company of Cleveland built steam-powered White automobiles from 1900 to 1911, among the finest cars of their day. President Taft used a White steamer at the White House. The company then made gasoline cars and, as the White Motor Company, trucks until 1980.
  • Packard was an American luxury automobile made from 1899 to 1958. Its slogan was “Ask the Man Who Owns One.” Henry Ford’s Model T, made from 1908 to 1927, was its opposite, the car for everyone.
  • “some old creature of the Sphinx or of the Roman Empire”: The sphinx is a mythical creature with the head of a human and the body of a lion. The Great Sphinx of Giza guards the pyramids, and the Greek Sphinx of Thebes asked travelers a riddle and devoured those who failed it, until Oedipus answered.
  • Mr. Spitzer guesses that the Egyptian is “some old creature of the Sphinx or of the Roman Empire,” and the two belong together. Egypt became a Roman province in 30 B.C., after the deaths of Antony and Cleopatra. The Roman Empire was the period of ancient Rome after the Republic, from Augustus in 27 B.C. until the fall of the western empire in 476 A.D.
  • “nothing but a race dope sheet listing those Arabian horses no longer running”: Every Thoroughbred racehorse descends in the male line from three stallions brought to England in the late 1600s and early 1700s, the Byerley Turk, the Darley Arabian, and the Godolphin Arabian. The Egyptian studied “the horses’ heads on tombs,” and ancient Greek funeral reliefs often show a horse’s head looking in at the banquet of the dead. See Horses and Carriages.
  • “painting the air with many mysterious hieroglyphs”: Hieroglyphs were the picture characters of the ancient Egyptian writing system. They could not be read for some fourteen hundred years until Jean-François Champollion deciphered them in 1822, with the help of the Rosetta Stone. See Ancient Egypt.
  • “he was his living brother”: The evil twin, or twins who are mistaken for each other, has been featured in many fictional genres. Here the mistake runs the other way: the Egyptian takes the cautious, living twin for the dead gambler, and Mr. Spitzer must argue that he is not his brother. See Twins and Doubles.
  • “party of the second part”: Legal contracts named the two sides “the party of the first part” and “the party of the second part.” The Marx Brothers made the phrases a joke in A Night at the Opera (1935), tearing a contract apart clause by clause.
  • “My mother pretended that the real was the dream, that the dream was the real”: The Chinese philosopher Zhuangzi dreamed he was a butterfly and, on waking, could not tell whether he was Zhuangzi who had dreamed of being a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming he was Zhuangzi. In Pedro Calderón de la Barca’s play Life Is a Dream (1635), Prince Segismundo concludes that “all life is a dream, and dreams are dreams.” See Taoism and Zhuangzi and Life Is a Dream.
  • “that contingent world one might have experienced by taking the other train through the other landscape”: The thought is close to Robert Frost’s “The Road Not Taken” (1916): “Two roads diverged in a wood, and I, / I took the one less traveled by.” Trains also run through Chapter 1, and Young explained that she sent Vera by bus rather than by train because a bus “does not go on a rail.” See American Writers.
  • “If ears could see, if eyes could hear”: Bottom wakes from his enchanted night in A Midsummer Night’s Dream with the senses mixed up: “The eye of man hath not heard, the ear of man hath not seen, man’s hand is not able to taste, his tongue to conceive, nor his heart to report, what my dream was.” Bottom is parodying 1 Corinthians 2:9. See Shakespeare.
  • “the bloody head of Alcibiades staring through an upper window”: Alcibiades was an Athenian statesman, orator, and general. According to Plutarch, assassins set fire to his house in Phrygia in 404 B.C., and when he rushed out, sword in hand, they killed him with javelins and arrows. Plutarch adds that his body was dressed and buried by his mistress.
  • “hospital nuns like great, snowy birds”: The Daughters of Charity of Saint Vincent de Paul, who ran many hospitals, wore the cornette, a starched white headdress with two great wings, until 1964. Its likeness to a bird or a sailboat was the subject of many jokes and stories.
  • “scattered hearts like motes dancing in a sunbeam”: The Roman poet Lucretius, in On the Nature of Things, pointed to the specks of dust that dance in a sunbeam in a darkened room as a picture of the atoms, always moving, colliding, and scattering in the void. See Epicureanism.
  • “Who were all these demidryads?”: Dryads are the tree nymphs of Greek mythology, first the nymphs of the oak, from drys, “oak.” A hamadryad lived and died with her tree. Young’s demidryads are half-nymphs.
  • “Medusas with white owls perched upon their livid locks”: The owl was the bird of Athena, goddess of wisdom, and after Perseus killed Medusa, Athena set the Gorgon’s head upon her aegis, her goatskin shield. Young perches Athena’s owl on Medusa’s snakes.
  • Ben Jonson was an English playwright and poet. He is best known for his satirical plays Volpone and The Alchemist and for the song “Drink to me only with thine eyes.” His tribute to Shakespeare calls him “not of an age, but for all time.” Jonson is buried upright in Westminster Abbey.
  • “a great frog prince sitting at the foot of the bed”: “The Frog King,” the first tale in the Grimm brothers’ collection of 1812, tells of a princess who must let a frog eat from her plate and sleep in her bed. In the original she throws him against the wall, and he turns into a prince. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “the lonely great auk who had no mate”: The great auk was a species of flightless bird that became extinct in the mid-nineteenth century. The last known pair was killed on the island of Eldey, off Iceland, in 1844. See Birds.
  • Molyneux’s problem, posed by the Irish scientist William Molyneux in a letter to John Locke in 1688, asked whether a man who had been born blind and who learned to distinguish and name a globe and a cube by touch, would be able to distinguish and name these objects simply by sight, once he had been enabled to see. Catherine’s blind mother keeps the world of sight in her mind and speaks “of the appearances, the visions, the beautiful world of light.” See Empiricism (Locke, Berkeley, Hume) and Eyes and Blindness.
  • “he had gotten a Seeing Eye dog”: The Seeing Eye, the first guide dog school in the United States, was founded in Nashville in 1929 by Dorothy Harrison Eustis and Morris Frank, a blind young man whose German shepherd, Buddy, became the first guide dog in America. See Dogs.
  • “the mirages, the maelstroms, the whirlwinds”: The Maelstrom, Moskstraumen in Norwegian, is a system of powerful tidal whirlpools off the Lofoten Islands of Norway. Poe’s story “A Descent into the Maelström” (1841) tells of a fisherman who survives being drawn into it. See Edgar Allan Poe.
  • “She had stayed under earth for six months at a time”: Persephone, the daughter of the harvest goddess Demeter, was carried off by Hades to the underworld. Because she had eaten pomegranate seeds there, she must return to him for part of every year, often told as six months, and the earth lies barren while she is gone. See Greek Mythology.
  • “the golden apples of Hesperides, those which had turned to dust in her mouth”: The Hesperides are the nymphs of the evening and the golden light of sunset. They tended a garden at the western edge of the world where golden apples grew, and stealing the apples was one of the labors of Heracles. Apples that turn to dust and ashes in the mouth are the apples of Sodom, which the historian Josephus described as fair to look at but full of ash.
  • “had climbed Jacob’s ladder to heaven”: Jacob’s ladder is a ladder leading to heaven that was featured in a dream the biblical Patriarch Jacob had during his flight from his brother Esau in the Book of Genesis. See Genesis.
  • The philosophy of self is the study of the many conditions of identity that make one subject of experience distinct from other experiences. The self is sometimes understood as a unified being essentially connected to consciousness, awareness, and agency. The philosopher John Mbiti summed up an African understanding of the self: “I am because we are, and since we are, therefore I am.” Mikhail Bakhtin believed that people need others in order to evaluate their own existence and construct a coherent self-image. Charles Horton Cooley called this the “looking-glass self” in Human Nature and the Social Order (1902): a person imagines how they appear to others and takes their own self-image from that imagined judgment. The saying “I am not what I think I am, and I am not what you think I am. I am what I think you think I am” is often attributed to Cooley as a summary of the idea. René Descartes began instead from the solitary thinker: “I think, therefore I am” (Discourse on Method, 1637). Catherine has “been herself only as she had been projected upon the consciousness of others.” See Identity and the Self.
  • “those old, vague archery tournaments”: Archery was one of the first sports thought proper for women, and in the nineteenth century archery clubs in England and America held tournaments where women competed in long skirts and veiled hats. Women’s archery was among the first women’s events at the Olympic Games, in 1904 and 1908.
  • “her preference was always his brother’s wild, worldly, mercurial spirit”: Mercurial comes from Mercury, the quick Roman messenger god of travelers, merchants, thieves, and gamblers, a fitting god for Peron the gambler. One study concluded that women may be drawn to “bad boys” who demonstrate confidence, stubbornness, and risk-taking tendencies or a man’s ability to “sell themselves.”
  • “they were going to Alaska, that cold place”: The United States bought Alaska from Russia in 1867 for $7.2 million, a purchase mocked as “Seward’s Folly,” and it became a state in 1959. In its far north the summer sun barely sets, “the sun dipping below the horizon and immediately arising again,” and in winter it barely rises. See Snow, Ice, and Winter.
  • “Home, James”: The order to a coachman, and later a chauffeur, comes from the old catchphrase “Home, James, and don’t spare the horses,” which became the title of a popular song in the 1930s. In Catherine’s mouth it becomes a question: “for where is home?”
  • “this same barber pole”: The red and white stripes of the barber pole go back to the barber-surgeons of the Middle Ages, who let blood as well as cut hair. The pole stood for the staff a patient gripped, and the stripes for the bandages.
  • “served an unjust sentence breaking rocks for a state highway”: Into the twentieth century, convicts in many American states, especially in the South, were chained together in gangs to quarry stone and build and repair roads.
  • “only a yellow canary with a glassy eye and ruffled wing”: The canary is a finch from the Canary Islands, the Azores, and Madeira, kept as a caged songbird since the 1600s. Coal miners carried canaries underground, since the birds would sicken from poison gas before the men did, and British mines used them until 1986. Catherine’s canary, “all that had ever stood between her and tides of death,” is a miner’s canary in reverse. See Birds.
  • Juniper, Tulip, Cellini, Solomon, Methuselah, Marco Polo, Admiral Peary, Shackleton, Dante: Catherine names her canary “depending on her moods and her position in space,” as when she is “voyaging between two stars,” and the names run from plants to kings, long life, artists, poets, and the great explorers of the earth’s far ends.
    • Benvenuto Cellini was an Italian goldsmith and sculptor of the sixteenth century, known for his bronze Perseus with the Head of Medusa in Florence, his golden saltcellar for King Francis I, and his boastful autobiography.
    • Solomon was the wise king of Israel who succeeded his father, King David, and built the first Temple in Jerusalem.
    • Methuselah was a biblical patriarch and said to have lived 969 years. His name is synonymous with longevity (Genesis 5:27).
    • Marco Polo was an Italian explorer who traveled through Asia along the Silk Road between 1271 and 1295, and spent years at the court of Kublai Khan.
    • Admiral Robert Peary was an American explorer and US Navy officer who made several expeditions to the Arctic. He claimed to have reached the North Pole in April 1909, a claim still disputed. See Exploration and the Poles.
    • Sir Ernest Shackleton was an Anglo-Irish Antarctic explorer, born in County Kildare in 1874. When his ship Endurance was crushed by the ice in 1915, he led all twenty-eight of his men to safety.
    • Dante Alighieri was an Italian poet who wrote the Divine Comedy. See Dante.
  • “a hurricane blowing off the Canary Islands”: The Canary Islands, off the northwest coast of Africa, were not named for the birds. The Roman writer Pliny the Elder said the name came from the Latin canis, “dog,” for the large dogs found there, and the birds were later named for the islands.

Hard to understand; obscure.

In music, a slow, leisurely tempo, or a piece played that way.

Happening by chance; not essential or built in.
Ambivalently
With mixed or contradictory feelings or meanings.

A ghost, or a ghostlike image of a person.
Arabesque
An ornament of intertwined flowing lines, leaves, and tendrils.
Athwart
Across, from side to side; also, against.

A slim, hard-sided briefcase.
Augury
A sign or omen of what is to come, or the art of reading such signs.
Auk
A diving seabird of northern seas. The great auk, a large flightless species, became extinct in 1844.
Balloon fish
A pufferfish, which swallows water to swell its body into a spiny ball when threatened.
Bang-tail
Slang for a racehorse, from the custom of cutting a horse’s tail straight across.
Barometer
An instrument that measures air pressure, used to forecast the weather.

A rope pulled to ring a bell, such as one that summons a servant.
Burro
A small donkey.
Camphor
A strong-smelling white substance from the camphor tree, used in medicines and to keep moths from clothes.
Carrozza
Italian for a carriage.
Centaur
In Greek mythology, a creature with the head and upper body of a man and the body and legs of a horse.
Chagrin
Distress or annoyance at having failed or been humiliated.
Chameleon
A lizard that can change the color of its skin.
Cherubim
Winged angelic beings. In art they are often shown as chubby winged children. The singular is cherub.
Clapper
The hanging metal tongue inside a bell that strikes it to make it ring.
Colloquy
A conversation or formal discussion. Young writes “colloquies of angels.”

The shape and proportions of an animal’s body, especially a horse’s.
Contiguous
Touching or next to one another.
Corpuscle
A blood cell.
Counterpane
A bedspread.
Couturier
A designer and maker of fine clothes for women.
Coverlet
A bedspread, usually a light one.
Crepuscular
Of twilight; dim.
Cubit
An ancient measure of length, from the elbow to the tip of the middle finger, about eighteen inches.
Defunct
No longer living or in existence.
Delusive
Misleading; giving a false impression.
Denizen
An inhabitant of a place.
Desultory
Done without purpose or plan; halfhearted.
Discrown
To take the crown from; to depose a king.
Dope sheet
A racing sheet giving information and tips on the horses running.
Effaced
Erased; wiped out.
Effigy
A sculpture or model of a person, especially one lying on a tomb.
Egregiously
Outstandingly; shockingly.
Elegiac
Mournful, like an elegy, a poem of lament for the dead.
Emanation
Something that flows out from a source.
Ephemeral
Lasting a very short time.
Equipoise
A state of balance.
Ermine
The white winter fur of the stoat, used to trim the robes of kings and nobles.
Ethereal
Light, airy, and delicate, as if not of this world; heavenly.
Euchred
Outwitted or cheated, from the card game euchre.
Evanescent
Quickly fading or vanishing.
Excrescence
An abnormal outgrowth, such as a wart; anything ugly that sticks out.
Eyeshade
A visor worn to shield the eyes from light.
Fatuous
Silly and pointless; foolishly self-satisfied.
Fichu
A triangular scarf of lace or muslin worn over the shoulders and knotted at the breast.
Firmament
The sky or the heavens, imagined as a solid dome overhead, as in Genesis 1.
Four-flusher
A bluffer or cheat. In poker, a four flush is a worthless hand of four cards of one suit passed off as five.
Fusty
Old-fashioned and musty.
Gargoyle
A carved grotesque figure on a building, often serving as a waterspout.
Gate-leg table
A table with hinged leaves held up by legs that swing out like gates.
Gauze
A thin, transparent fabric.
Glow-worm
A beetle whose wingless female and larvae give off a greenish light.
Griffin
A mythical beast with the head and wings of an eagle and the body of a lion.
Gumshoe
Slang for a detective, from the soft rubber-soled shoes that let one follow quietly.
Hanging gardens
The Hanging Gardens of Babylon, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World: terraced gardens said to have been built by King Nebuchadnezzar II. No trace of them has ever been found.
Heraldic
Of heraldry, the coats of arms and emblems of noble families.
Imploring

Ineffable

Inscrutable

Insouciant

Ketch
A two-masted sailboat with the smaller mast set behind the main mast.
Labyrinthine
Like a labyrinth; intricate and confusing.
Laundress
A woman who washes and irons clothes for a living.
Listlessly
Without energy or interest.
Livid
Bluish-gray, the color of a bruise. Also furiously angry.
Lorgnette
A pair of eyeglasses held up to the eyes by a long handle.
Lute
A plucked string instrument with a rounded back shaped like half a pear, popular in Europe from the Middle Ages through the 1700s.
Maelstrom
A powerful whirlpool; any violent, confused situation.
Malingering
Pretending to be ill to avoid work.
Medusa
A jellyfish in its free-swimming, bell-shaped stage, named for the Gorgon of Greek myth whose hair was snakes.
Mercantile
Of merchants and trade; commercial.
Mercurial
Quick and changeable in mood. From the swift Roman messenger god Mercury and the liquid metal named for him.
Mesa
A flat-topped hill with steep sides. Young writes of a “mesa of snow.”
Metropolitan
In the Orthodox churches, a bishop who presides over the other bishops of a province.
Milliner
A maker or seller of women’s hats.
