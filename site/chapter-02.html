Summary

Vera describes her mother, Catherine. She is an opium addict who has lain in bed for years among jewels and phantoms and treats her hallucinations as more real than the world. The chapter traces how she came to this. There was a brief marriage to Jock Cartwheel, and a love triangle with twin brothers: Catherine loved Peron Spitzer, who did not love her, while his brother Joachim loved Catherine without return. Joachim, Mr. Spitzer, is the family lawyer and visits her bedside every evening. An Egyptian sailor arrives looking for the dead Peron and mistakes Joachim for him, and Catherine gives the sailor elaborate directions through her labyrinthine house. There is also the chauffeur, James, with whom she once tried to run away. Whenever her canary dies, Mr. Spitzer secretly replaces it. The chapter ends when a canary escapes into a thunderstorm. Catherine calmly accepts that she must now live forever and dismisses its distant twittering as another dream.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a vivid description of Catherine’s detachment from reality, her retreat into a world of dreams, phantoms, and visions. She sleeps in a stately bed, surrounded by jewels and illusions, her body horizontal and still but her mind endlessly active in a twilight realm where days and nights blur into one. This passage establishes her condition as one suspended between life and death, reality and fantasy, and introduces her as a luminous, ethereal figure whose identity is intertwined with opium dreams and spectral visitors like dead kings, angels, and movie stars. The birthmark she conceals and the shadowed cheek symbolize her hidden imperfections and her withdrawal from the world.
  • This chapter explains the complex, tragic backstory of Catherine’s life, including her failed marriage to a reckless sportsman who disappeared in the Alps, Jock Cartwheel, the social humiliations she endured, and the inheritance that burdened her. It also reveals her denial of her father’s strict domination and orthodox faith, and her agnostic stance despite the angels in her opium paradise. It deepens the understanding of her motivations for retreating into this dreamlike existence and the loss and abandonment she suffered.
  • The relationship between Catherine and Mr. Spitzer, a semi-retired lawyer who visits her faithfully every evening, is a crucial element. He is portrayed as a somewhat hapless, absent-minded man broken by his brother’s death, yet devoted to Catherine. Their interactions mix reality and fantasy, with Catherine often tormenting him with her elusive remarks and suggestions of invisible presences, while he tries to maintain order and reason. This dynamic illustrates the tension between her dream world and his grasp on reality, as well as her complex feelings of resentment and fondness toward him.
  • This chapter emphasizes Catherine’s uncanny knowledge of Mr. Spitzer’s movements and minor details of his life which he himself forgets. It raises the question of whether she is truly alive or already dead, and whether Mr. Spitzer even exists as she perceives him. Her world is one of illusions and ghostly realities, where distinctions between life and death, real and unreal, blur. The servants’ superstitions about her nocturnal wanderings and mysterious occurrences in the house add to the enigmatic aura.
  • A significant episode is the arrival of a gold-turbaned Egyptian sailor who claims to have come looking for Mr. Spitzer’s dead twin brother. Catherine insists on the Egyptian sailor’s reality, giving him detailed instructions to navigate the labyrinthine house, while Mr. Spitzer tries to convince the Egyptian sailor of his own death (his twin brother, Peron) and the mistake in identity. This episode symbolizes the permeable boundary between the worlds of the living and the dead, and Catherine’s role as a mediator or gatekeeper of these overlapping realities.
  • Here Catherine expresses a worldview in which the dream is the only reality worth accepting, because the real world is inevitably disappointing and unrealized. She sees life as a vast deception, and the dreams as protective and expansive, allowing a multiplicity of experiences and visions that transcend strict physical reality. This philosophical stance encapsulates the chapter’s major theme which is the tension between illusion and reality and the necessity of dreams to sustain life.
  • Catherine’s only faithful companion is a little yellow canary, kept in a gilded cage and drugged to preserve its fragile existence. The bird symbolizes the tenuous thread of life sustaining her, and the careful subterfuge to replace the canary when it dies illustrates the fragile illusions that maintain her dream world and her existence. Mr. Spitzer’s role in maintaining this secret further highlights the delicate balance between reality and fantasy that defines her life.
  • Catherine imagines herself endlessly riding with James, her ex-chauffeur, toward Alaska, a place she never reaches despite years of travel. This endless, repetitive journey through an endless city symbolizes her trapped state between worlds, the impossibility of escape or arrival, and the persistence of memory and illusion. James’s dark intentions and her helplessness add a note of impending danger to this surreal journey.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Catherine Helena Cartwheel (Snowden)

  • A central figure who has withdrawn from life into a dreamlike, opium-fueled existence.
  • She suffers from an invisible birthmark (a blot on her left cheek), which symbolizes hidden flaws and may represent her motivation for retreating from reality.
  • She is portrayed as a "beautiful corpse," eternally young yet fixed in a horizontal, motionless state.
  • Surrounds herself with phantoms, dreams, and visions including historical figures, spirits, and imaginary visitors.
  • She is complexly caught between sanity and madness, reality and fantasy, embodying a state of suspended animation.
  • Her retreat to bed and isolation is linked to personal trauma: an unhappy marriage (Jock Cartwheel), abandonment, social humiliation, and a controlling father.

Mr. Spitzer (Joachim)

  • Catherine’s faithful but often ignored visitor, a semi-retired lawyer.
  • He is a figure of reason, order, and routine, marked by absent-mindedness and a broken career after the death of his twin brother (Peron).
  • Despite his marriage proposal being rejected by Catherine, he visits her regularly.
  • He represents the tether to reality and the outside world.
  • His relationship with Catherine is ambiguous; he is both her guardian and unrequited lover.

James, the Chauffeur

  • An ex-convict and former servant who has abandoned the household but remains a figure in Catherine's dreams.
  • Symbolizes loyalty, escape, and impending threat.
  • Imagined as driving them endlessly to Alaska, plotting harm but never quite reaching the destination.

The Little Yellow Canary

  • Catherine’s only faithful companion, kept in a gilded cage.
  • Symbolizes life, hope, and fragile continuity amidst decay and madness.
  • Catherine believes her life is bound to the canary’s survival.
  • Mr. Spitzer secretly arranges for the canaries to be replaced to maintain the illusion of continuity.

An Egyptian Sailor

  • Depicted vividly as "gold-turbaned, his face glistening like clouded water," immediately evoking an exotic and otherworldly presence.
  • His arrival is marked by a sense of ambiguity and displacement.
  • He is described as a lost traveler, both literally and metaphorically, crossing boundaries between worlds and times.
  • The sailor’s purpose is to find Mr. Spitzer.
  • Despite the confusion and mistaken identity that ensues, the Egyptian's quest is sincere and urgent. He carries a piece of papyrus, which is later revealed to be a race dope sheet, connecting him to earthly concerns such as horse racing and victory, yet his demeanor and context suggest he is more than just a mundane visitor.
  • His presence introduces a collision between the "real world" and the mysterious, spectral realm inhabited by Catherine and her guests.
  • The Egyptian Sailor symbolizes the crossing of boundaries: life and death, reality and dream, the past and the present.
  • His lost voyage, caused by "ambiguities" such as misread stars and winds, mirrors Catherine’s own disorientation in time and space, encapsulating themes of fate, chance, and the unknowable.
  • His dance of ecstatic excitement upon seeing Mr. Spitzer, and the subsequent confusion over identity, emphasize the fluidity of existence in this narrative.
  • His interaction with Mr. Spitzer underscores the tension between the tangible and the ethereal, as Mr. Spitzer tries to convince him of the death of his brother, while Catherine embraces the coexistence of these two realms.
  • The Egyptian’s presence unsettles Mr. Spitzer, reflecting a resistance to the intrusion of the supernatural or the past into his orderly, if somewhat diminished, life.
  • Catherine’s support of the Egyptian Sailor, defending his reality and expressing fury at the servants’ neglect of him, reveals her own deep immersion in the dream world and her rejection of mundane reality.
  • This dynamic portrays the Egyptian as a catalyst for revealing Catherine’s fractured state and the complex interplay between sanity and madness.
  • He may also be a reference to "The Shipwrecked Sailor". It is an ancient Egyptian tale, likely composed during the Middle Kingdom (c. 2040-1782 BCE), found on a papyrus scroll. It tells the story of a sailor who is shipwrecked and marooned on a mythical island, where he encounters a giant serpent and eventually returns home with tales and treasures.

Mr. Res Tacamah

  • Mr. Res Tacamah’s characterization by Catherine as a "drug bottle with ears" implies a dual role: both as a source of the narcotic influence that enables her prolonged sleep-like state and as a silent witness or confidant to her fragmented consciousness and delirium.
  • The figure's "ears to hear her ravings" indicates that this presence is attuned to her incoherent, abstracted speech, thereby symbolizing the inanimate yet intimate object through which her mind communicates and seeks solace.
  • It also underscores the isolation and detachment from reality experienced by Catherine, for her closest "companions" are non-human, immaterial entities or hallucinations rather than living persons.
  • Mr. Res Tacamah, as a drug bottle, is part of the apparatus that maintains her "slumberous life," preventing her madness from overtaking her by regulating her doses of opium and sedatives.
  • His presence reflects the complex interplay between dependency, control, and the fragile boundary between wakefulness and sleep that defines her existence.
  • Mr. Res Tacamah is part of Catherine’s dreamscape, a personification of the substances and hallucinations that populate her consciousness.
  • He is described with a proper name, which humanizes the drug bottle and elevates its significance in her interior world, emphasizing how deeply embedded the opium experience is in the fabric of her identity and daily life.

Mr. Chandelier

  • Mr. Chandelier can be seen as an anthropomorphized object, a personification of the chandelier itself.
  • Catherine imagines him as her companion, “not always faithful,” suggesting a capricious, elusive presence that mirrors the instability and unreliability of her dream world.
  • This personification transforms an inanimate object into a character with agency and presence in her inner life, blurring the boundaries between reality and imagination.
  • Mr. Chandelier is described with almost ghostly qualities: he has “seven lighted candles in his seven crystal hands,” an image evoking mysticism and ritual, linking him to the theme of ritualistic control and orthodoxy that pervades Catherine’s background, especially considering her father’s “fantastic rituals each hour of day or night”.
  • The seven candles may also evoke the idea of illumination, yet in her world, this illumination is shadowed, uncertain, part of the “eternal twilight” that envelops her perception.
  • Mr. Chandelier’s presence also serves as a counterpoint to Catherine’s physical paralysis and mental wandering. While she lies “horizontal,” immobile, he is often depicted as moving between stillness and motion, reality and dream. This duality reflects Catherine’s own suspended state between life and death, consciousness and oblivion.
  • Mr. Chandelier is an emblematic figure embodying the interplay of light and shadow, life and death, presence and absence in her opium-induced limbo.
  • Mr. Chandelier’s “seven crystal hands” holding candles may symbolize the multiplicity of facets in Catherine’s fractured consciousness and fragmented reality. Each candle can be interpreted as a flickering point of awareness, a fragile beacon in the “glass surface” of her uncertain existence. Yet these lights are also delicate and impermanent, just as her world is “a play of illusion” where “all forms must change their shapes”.

Catherine’s parents: mother, father and seeing eye dog

  • Catherine’s mother suffers from a progressive hereditary blindness, which is emphasized repeatedly in the novel.
  • This condition is not merely a physical ailment but also a metaphorical element that shapes her entire existence.
  • Despite losing her sight gradually, she maintains an extraordinary awareness of her surroundings. The narrative highlights her ability to navigate the world with remarkable precision.
  • This portrayal elevates her beyond being a passive victim of blindness; instead, she becomes a figure of resilience and heightened sensory perception, compensating for the loss of sight with other faculties.
  • Her blindness also affects those around her deeply, especially her husband.
  • The husband's reaction to her blindness is poignant, he begins to wear a dark eyeshade, symbolically aligning himself with her experience of darkness, as if to share her loss or express his mourning for the light she has lost.
  • The husband's transformation is profound: his body is described metaphorically as "turning into starlight," and when he eventually becomes blind himself, he relies on a Seeing Eye dog.
  • The Seeing Eye dog, interestingly, is blind as well, yet it leads its master flawlessly, a symbolic reinforcement of the theme of navigating darkness through trust, adaptation, and interdependence.
  • This shared blindness and the mutual care between the husband and wife deepen the portrayal of their relationship and the familial experience of blindness.
  • Catherine's father, too, is depicted as a figure who lived his last years in darkness, physically blind but mentally and emotionally attuned to his environment.
  • He walks in the garden he planted for his blind wife, following carefully designed paths free of obstacles, suggesting both his love and his protective role.
  • His actions, closing his eyes to sense flowers by texture and odor, reflect a profound adaptation to blindness and an intimate connection with the sensory world beyond vision.
  • His death, alongside that of the old Seeing Eye dog, symbolizes the closing of a chapter marked by darkness but also by dignity and acceptance.

Research: Themes

Reality vs. Illusion

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Catherine inhabits a world where dreams and hallucinations are often more "real" than the tangible world.
  • The novel blurs boundaries between waking life and the dream state, questioning the nature of existence.
  • Catherine’s perception that the real is the dream and the dream is the real reflects a universal human tendency to escape disappointing reality.

Life and Death / Suspension between States

See: Death and Mortality

  • Catherine is described as a living corpse, suspended between life and death, awake and asleep.
  • Her physical body is motionless, yet her mind roams freely through time, space, and memory.
  • Death is never final. There are multiple selves, many dead canaries, and visitors from the spirit world.

Memory and Forgetting

See: Memory and the Past

  • Catherine’s memories are fragmented, clouded, and often replaced by fantasy.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s forgetfulness contrasts with her photographic recall of his movements and lost incidents.
  • The past is both a refuge and a source of pain since she relives moments that never fully happened.

Madness and Sanity

See: Madness and Sanity

  • Catherine’s mental state is ambiguous; she is at times lucid, at times delusional.
  • Opium and drugs regulate her condition, creating a "scheduled madness" that preserves her life.
  • Her madness is a form of self-preservation and rebellion against a brutal world.

Isolation and Dependency

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Catherine is socially and physically isolated, confined to her bed.
  • She depends on a select few (Mr. Spitzer, the canary, James in her imagination) for companionship.
  • The household staff is largely dismissed or feared, emphasizing her detachment from ordinary life.

  • Solipsism is a philosophical theory that posits the only thing you can be sure exists is your own mind and consciousness. It suggests that the external world, other people, and even your own body might be mere illusions or figments of your imagination. Solipsism is an extreme form of subjective idealism, meaning it focuses primarily on the individual's own subjective experience. Solipsism argues that knowledge of anything outside one's own mind is uncertain or impossible. A central question in solipsism is the problem of other minds, which asks how we can know for sure that other people have minds and experiences like our own. Solipsism contrasts the certainty of one's own mental state (thoughts, feelings, memories) with the uncertainty of anything outside of it, like the physical world or other conscious beings. There can be different interpretations of solipsism, including metaphysical solipsism (where only your mind is real), epistemological solipsism (where you can't prove the existence of anything outside your mind), and methodological solipsism (where the self is the starting point for understanding reality). While the term solipsism can sometimes be used in a psychological context, it's important to distinguish it from the formal philosophical idea. "Solipsism syndrome" is a term used to describe a feeling of isolation and detachment from the outside world, which may be related to solipsistic thinking but is not a diagnosed condition.
