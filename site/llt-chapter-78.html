Chapter 78

On Chapter 78 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 77 · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 79 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Esther Longtree

Pillars: The Margins Reveal the Truth

The Questions

Esther was called “Little Jackass” from the start, “born kicking, stubborn, awkward.” What is it to be marked as the outsider from birth?

She had “fought against being born… reluctant to be born, slow.” What does the book mean by a person who resisted her own beginning?

As a child she “cried in the dark… and no one had come to stay her cries.” What forms in a person no one comes to?

She “made friends of her natural enemies, the woodpeckers, the bluejays.” Why does the outcast turn to what should be against her?

The chapter roots The Margins Reveal the Truth in Esther’s whole life, from birth. She was called “Little Jackass” because “she had been born kicking, stubborn, awkward,” marked as the mulish outsider before she could speak, and she has carried the name and the position ever since. The book has given its clearest sight to those who do not fit the categories, and here it shows that Esther did not become marginal through her strange condition; she was marginal from the first breath, the awkward, unwanted, ill-fitting one, positioned at the edge from birth. Her clear sight from the margin is not a late acquisition but the shape of her whole existence, outsider from the start.

She resisted even her own beginning. “She had fought against being born,” her mother said, “reluctant to be born, slow, slow to move,” and the detail is strange and telling. Esther is the figure who did not want to be delivered, who resisted her own completion into a born child, and this rhymes deeply with her everlasting pregnancy: she who fought against being born now carries children who are never born, as though the reluctance to complete ran through her from both sides, unwilling to be finished herself and unable to finish the lives she carries. The edge she lives at is not only social but existential, a resistance to delivery, to arrival, to the sealed fact of a completed birth.

What formed in her is the loneliness of the child no one came to. “She had often cried in the dark, being afraid of the darkness—had clenched her fists and cried, and no one had come to stay her cries,” and “so she had grown up lonely.” The chapter is exact about the origin of her clear, marginal sight: it grew in the dark, uncomforted, in a child who called and was not answered. The Margins Reveal the Truth is not a gift bestowed but a thing forged in abandonment, the sharp sight of one who learned early that no one comes, that the center will not answer the cries of the child at the margin. Esther sees clearly because she was left alone long enough to look.

And she made friends of her natural enemies. “She had made friends of her natural enemies, the woodpeckers, the bluejays,” turning to the creatures that should have been against her, finding company among the wrong species, the hostile birds. This is the outcast’s characteristic move, and the book honors it: shut out of the human center, Esther crosses even the line between friend and enemy, kind and kind, making companions of the birds that ordinary sense says are not hers to befriend. The categories that sort ally from enemy, like every other category, do not bind her, and from her place at the edge she builds a strange kinship across the lines the center keeps, friends of the woodpeckers, sister to the bluejays, at home among her own natural enemies.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Esther’s lifelong marginality for mere sad backstory, and the reading that treats her friendship with her natural enemies as simple whimsy. Neither the pathos nor the whimsy is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that Esther was marked as the outsider from birth, the awkward, unwanted, ill-fitting one, so her clear sight from the margin is the shape of her life rather than a late acquisition; that she resisted even her own being born, a reluctance to be finished that rhymes with her never-delivered children; that her marginal sight was forged in the loneliness of a child no one came to, sharp because she was left alone to look; and that she makes friends of her natural enemies, crossing even the line between ally and enemy that binds the center. The edge is where she has always lived, and the sight it gives was forged in abandonment. Esther keeps company with her natural enemies, and the chapter honors the kinship she builds across the lines the center keeps.

For Reflection

What clear sight might be forged in a person who was marked as an outsider from the very start?

Is there a resistance to being finished, to arriving, to being sealed as a completed thing, that runs through a whole life?

What kinship becomes possible at the margin, across the lines between friend and enemy that the center keeps?
