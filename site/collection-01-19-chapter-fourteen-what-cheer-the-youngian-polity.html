Chapter Fourteen: What Cheer: The Youngian Polity

← Chapter Thirteen: The Ten Pillars and Daily Life · Radical Immanence Collection · Chapter Fifteen: The Geological We →

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

The town in Young's novel is called What Cheer. It is a real town in Iowa, and it is also, in the novel, the destination that Vera is traveling toward across 1,198 pages without arriving. What Cheer is where Miss MacIntosh came from. It is the solid world's origin point, the place where the pragmatist was formed, the town that produced the woman who insisted on reality before the wig came off.

Vera never gets there in any simple sense. Or rather: what she gets to is not what she was traveling toward, because the traveling changed what the destination means. What Cheer at the end of the journey is not What Cheer at the beginning. The place is the same. The self arriving at it is not.

What does a polity built on Young's ten pillars actually look like? Not as a blueprint, because Young was right that blueprints become the thing they were meant to prevent. But as a set of structural commitments that follow honestly from the philosophical ground.

The first commitment is to the plural self as the unit of political life. Not the individual of liberal theory, the rational self-interested agent who enters the social contract from a position of prior wholeness. The plural self: the one who contains multitudes, whose interests are not unified, whose identity is not singular, who cannot be fully represented by any single preference or position. A politics that takes the plural self seriously builds structures for genuine complexity: ranked choice voting that can capture the plurality of political preference, proportional representation that can hold multiple communities without forcing them to collapse into binary choices, deliberative processes that create space for the self to think across its own plurality rather than forcing it to a single position before the thinking is done.

The second commitment is to the constructed real as the ground of political epistemology. No policy is neutral. No framework is objective. Every structure for collective decision-making embeds assumptions about what is real and what matters and whose experience counts as evidence. A Youngian polity names those assumptions explicitly rather than naturalizing them, and treats the epistemological question, who gets to construct the shared reality, as a political question of the first order.

The third commitment is to honest grief as a public practice. The losses built into the foundation of collective life are not resolved by acknowledgment, but they are made more honestly inhabitable by it. Truth and reconciliation processes that do not demand resolution. Public memorialization that does not convert grief into triumph. The permanent carrying of historical loss as a structural feature of collective memory rather than a stage to be moved through toward a national narrative of recovery and progress.

The fourth commitment is to the witness as a political role. Not only the activist, the organizer, the legislator, the executive. The witness: the one whose function is to maintain the quality of attention the system requires to remain honest about what it is and what it is doing. Journalism understood as witnessing rather than information delivery. The arts funded as a civic necessity because the arts are where collective witnessing happens at the scale required to keep a complex plural society honest about its own complexity.

The fifth commitment is to recursive construction as the explicit design principle of every institution. Nothing is finished. Everything is under ongoing revision. The structures acknowledge their own provisionality and build the mechanisms of revision into their foundations. Not as a concession to uncertainty but as the honest expression of what institutions actually are: provisional constructions made by plural selves in a world that keeps becoming something other than what it was.

The sixth commitment, extending the original five to the full ten-pillar architecture, is to the ethics of incompletion as a governing principle. The political body that takes Young seriously does not only revise its policies. It builds the acknowledgment of its own incompleteness into its founding documents, its deliberative processes, its relationship with the people it governs. The constitution is not a sacred text. It is an ongoing argument.

The seventh commitment is to the ontology of the marginal as evidence rather than exception. The people at the edges of the categories the system uses are not problems to be managed. They are the places where the system's constructions show their seams, and attending to them honestly is the most rigorous form of political self-knowledge available.

The eighth commitment is to sedimentary time as the ground of political memory. The past is not behind us. It is beneath us. Building justly requires attending to what is in the substrate.

The ninth commitment is to honest language as a political obligation. The political body that takes Young seriously does not only revise its policies. It revises its language, because the language is where the erasure begins.

The tenth commitment is to immanent transcendence as the quality of public life. The ordinary world, the bus routes and the town halls and the community gardens and the school buildings, is charged with a significance that requires no transcendent guarantee. It is sacred because plural selves are living in it and attending to it and building in it and grieving in it and witnessing each other in it. That is enough. That is more than enough.

This is What Cheer. Not a utopia. A destination that keeps becoming something other than what you imagined when you set out, because the journey keeps teaching you things you didn't know when you left, and the honest arrival incorporates what the journey taught rather than insisting on the destination it imagined.
