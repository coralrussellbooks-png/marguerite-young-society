Chapter 20

← Chapter 19 · Home · Chapter 21 →

Summary

In the full light of dawn, Vera studies the sleeping woman. It is “the light which had already killed the heart of Miss MacIntosh,” stripping her “bare as roses of their petals”: no “crowning glory of woman’s hair,” no single hair “to suggest a possibility of future growth,” and the “narrow and intolerable vacancy” where the other breast should be. Vera wonders who robbed her of her hair, and when, whether hair by hair or all at once, whether a broken heart caused it, whether she was “deserted at the altar by her bridegroom or her bride.” Then she makes her resolve. She will go on “as if I had never seen her,” iron the shirts with new alacrity, learn the Hebrew measures, coins, months, and the stations of the Exodus, and “protect her from the knowledge of herself.” The old routine is already “the elegiac past,” and she will “hold to every moment as if I lived with a dying man.” The chapter ends as Vera asks, “Was God bald, too,” in a litany of the Bible’s images for God.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a vivid depiction of dawn which paradoxically serves as both illumination and a force that has “already killed the heart of Miss MacIntosh” before her actual death. The light reveals wounds and deprivations that “would not heal with time,” suggesting an exposure of deep, irrevocable suffering and loss.
  • A striking passage focuses on the physical absence of hair on Miss MacIntosh’s head which symbolically represents a loss of feminine identity, vitality, and perhaps a history of mourning or trauma. Vera reflects on the significance of hair as beauty or terror and wonders about the circumstances of her hair loss, whether gradual or sudden, linking it to emotional or existential devastation.
  • Vera experiences a powerful tension between the recognition of Miss MacIntosh’s vulnerable, altered state and a desire to revert to ignorance for the sake of “common sense” and routine. There is a determined resolve to “go on as if I had never seen her,” to protect her from the knowledge of herself, and to maintain the illusion of normalcy despite the stark reality of her condition.
  • The passage detailing Vera’s imagined daily routines contrasts sharply with the internal, almost mystical knowledge of Miss MacIntosh’s profound deprivation. Vera contemplates learning ancient measures, weights, and biblical geography, suggesting a search for meaning or order amid chaos and personal loss.
  • Toward the end, Vera muses deeply about mortality, identity, and divinity. The baldness and missing breast become metaphors for the exposed realities of life and death, while Vera questions the nature of God with rich biblical imagery, reflecting on God’s multifaceted definitions as friend, refuge, crown, and shadow. This passage underscores the theme of confronting harsh truths and the limits of human understanding.
  • The final lines evoke a solemn recognition of the “image of life failing before his eyes,” highlighting Vera’s acute awareness of mortality and the profound emotional weight of witnessing the decline of a once vibrant individual.
  • “I should hold to every moment as if I lived with a dying man, and I should protect her from the knowledge of herself.” Vera’s resolve turns the daily routine into an elegy.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “It was surely the light which had already killed her.” What does the dawn take from Miss MacIntosh?
  • “I should have been less surprised to have found her with a red beard than with no hair at all.” Why would a beard surprise Vera less?
  • Vera promises to learn the digit, the cubit, the gerah, and Nisan and Elul. Why does she turn to measuring now?

Characters

  • Miss MacIntosh was “not endowed… with intuitive imagination.” How does Vera use that to keep the secret?
  • “It would be easy to deceive her.” What has Vera learned about deception overnight?
  • “Was she her only lover?” What does Vera suspect about her past?

Connections

  • Chapter 10’s dream made Miss MacIntosh the bridegroom. Here Vera asks if she was “deserted at the altar by her bridegroom or her bride.” How do the two scenes speak to each other?
  • The Israelites wander from Egypt to Canaan, and “some die… on their way.” How does that journey match Vera’s?
  • In Chapter 15, Miss MacIntosh said “none should ever see it and live.” How does Vera come “close to something which none should see and live to relate”?

Reflection

  • Can keeping someone’s secret be a way of loving them?
  • “Grey hair does not signify a necessary wisdom.” What does?
  • Is pretending that we are simple ever simple?

Research: Characters

Miss MacIntosh

  • A figure of strength and common sense, yet marked by profound physical and existential deprivation.
  • Described as “hale and hearty, a red-headed nursemaid” in the past, yet now revealed in a state of vulnerability and loss.
  • Her condition is a mystery to Vera, who struggles to reconcile her external robustness with the internal and physical ravages revealed by the light.
  • She embodies the tension between resilience and suffering, denial and revelation.
  • Vera resolves to “protect her from the knowledge of herself.”

Vera Cartwheel

  • Exhibits complex emotions: pity, love, denial, and a protective instinct toward Miss MacIntosh.
  • Is philosophical and reflective, wrestling with large existential questions prompted by Miss MacIntosh’s condition.
  • Exhibits a desire to maintain normalcy and routine to shield both herself and Miss MacIntosh from harsh truths.

Research: Themes

Light as a Revealing and Destructive Force

See: Light and Darkness

  • Dawn and light symbolize both revelation and destruction. Vera suggests that light has “already killed the heart of Miss MacIntosh” before her physical death, exposing wounds that cannot heal and deprivations that are eternal.
  • The light strips away illusions, revealing the “fearful forehead” and the “bald and naked, cloudless summit” of Miss MacIntosh’s head, symbolizing a harsh unveiling of truth and vulnerability.
  • There’s also a paradoxical beauty in the light, described as “diamond dawn” and “celestial aureole,” which offers hope for redemption and healing, yet simultaneously exposes harsh realities.

Loss and Absence

See: Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • Miss MacIntosh’s baldness is a potent symbol of loss: loss of hair as a “crowning glory,” loss of feminine nature, and the missing breast as a “door knob,” a stark reminder of what is absent.
  • Vera questions the cause and timing of this loss: was it gradual or sudden? Was it caused by heartbreak, mourning, or some unknowable trauma?
  • This absence is not just physical but metaphysical, representing the stripping away of identity and vitality.

Mortality and the Inevitability of Decay

See: Death and Mortality · Decay and Ruin

  • Vera reflects on mortality, decay, and the “wounds in our sides” that light will bind up to prevent death like “Saul or Solomon or Sennacherib.”
  • There is a tension between the desire to protect Miss MacIntosh from the knowledge of her own condition and the inevitability of facing harsh truths.
  • The novel grapples with the idea that despite external appearances or the passage of time, some wounds and losses are timeless and unhealable.

Identity and Self-Perception

See: Identity and the Self

  • Miss MacIntosh’s lack of “intuitive imagination” and her common sense shield her from the deeper, visionary truths Vera perceives; she is “content with things just as they were” and would deny or not suspect the “empty vision” beyond the commonplace.
  • Vera’s internal conflict involves a desire to pretend normalcy to protect both Miss MacIntosh and herself from confronting the full reality of her condition.
  • There is also a complex emotional dynamic, where Vera simultaneously sees her as an “obstacle” to her happiness and as someone deeply significant, a secret lover or cause of her “vain pursuits.”

Spiritual and Existential Questions

See: Faith, God, and the Supernatural · The Search for Meaning

  • The narrative moves toward existential and spiritual reflection, pondering God’s nature, the problem of creation, and the place of suffering and imperfection in the divine order.
  • Vera likens the “baldness” and loss to a profound experience, possibly greater than mere mortal wisdom or aging, invoking biblical and mythological imagery.
  • The chapter closes with a meditation on life failing before one’s eyes and the idea of seeing something no one “should see and live to relate.”

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Hair and Baldness

See: Hair, Wig, and Baldness

  • Hair symbolizes beauty, identity, and vitality; baldness represents loss, vulnerability, and exposure.
  • Absence of hair is described as a “barren, impoverished landscape without flower or bush or hedge.”

Light and Dawn

See: Lamps, Candles, and Light

  • Dawn is both “diamond” and “many-prismed,” a metaphor for clarity, revelation, but also a harsh revealer of truth.
  • The “Aurora Borealis” in her sparsely furnished bedroom suggests a surreal, otherworldly illumination, the “first experience of light” upon uncreated things.

Missing Breast

See: Women, Gender, and Marriage

  • The missing breast is a stark physical symbol of loss and incompleteness, described as “like a door knob,” emphasizing its unnatural absence.

Biblical and Mythological References

See: The Bible · Angels, Devils, and Myth

  • References to biblical figures (Saul, Solomon, Sennacherib) and ancient measurements, calendars, and places (Nisan, Jyar, Wady Feiran, Egypt to Canaan) ground the narrative in a spiritual and temporal history.
  • Quotes and allusions to Ovid and Greek perspectives on beauty and hair enhance the classical depth of the novel.

Research: Resources

  • “shall bind up the wounds in our sides”: “He healeth the broken in heart, and bindeth up their wounds” (Psalm 147:3). The wounds “in our sides” recall the wound in Christ’s side (John 19:34), which Thomas was told to touch (John 20:27). See The Bible.
  • “that we shall not die like Saul or Solomon or Sennacherib in the midst of our corruption”: Saul fell on his own sword (1 Samuel 31:4). Solomon “slept with his fathers” (1 Kings 11:43) after his wives “turned away his heart after other gods” (1 Kings 11:4). Sennacherib was killed by his sons while he worshipped in the house of his god Nisroch (2 Kings 19:37). See The Bible and Suicide in History and Literature.
  • “celestial aureole”: Aureole is a radiant light around the head or body of a representation of a sacred personage.
  • “when we shall put on our shining armor”: “Let us put on the armour of light” (Romans 13:12), and “Put on the whole armour of God” (Ephesians 6:11). See The Bible.
  • “those who dwell in hidden caves beneath the overhanging, moss-grown rocks”: The kings and great men “hid themselves in the dens and in the rocks of the mountains; And said to the mountains and rocks, Fall on us” (Revelation 6:15-16). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “dark as the ace of spades”: The ace of spades has long been called the death card. The deuce of clubs was “the omen of death” in Chapter 19. See Gambling and Playing Cards and the Chapter 19 resources.
  • “haunted rays of Aurora Borealis”: The northern lights. The Latin name, “northern dawn,” joins Aurora, the Roman goddess of the dawn, and Boreas, the north wind, and is usually credited to Galileo (1619). See Astronomy and Exploration and the Poles.
  • “the experimental light of the first day of crazed creation”: “And God said, Let there be light: and there was light” (Genesis 1:3). See Genesis.
  • “celebrating his triumphs over the Devil and the flesh”: The Litany in the Book of Common Prayer (1549): “From all the deceits of the world, the flesh, and the devil, Good Lord, deliver us.”
  • “less surprised to have found her with a red beard”: The red mustache Vera gave her in Chapter 9. See the Chapter 9 resources and the Chapter 17 resources.
  • “There was no crowning glory of woman’s hair”: “If a woman have long hair, it is a glory to her: for her hair is given her for a covering” (1 Corinthians 11:15). See The Bible, Hair, Wig, and Baldness, and Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “Ugly is a field without grass, a plant without leaves, a head without hair, according to Ovid”: Ovid, The Art of Love (3.249-250): “Turpe pecus mutilum, turpe est sine gramine campus, / et sine fronde frutex, et sine crine caput” (“Ugly is a hornless bull, ugly a field without grass, a bush without leaves, and a head without hair”). The lines come in his advice to women on hiding the loss of hair, which includes buying false hair. See Hair, Wig, and Baldness.
  • “Hair adds beauty to a good face and terror to an ugly one, according to the Greek”: Plutarch’s Life of Lycurgus (22) explains that the Spartans wore their hair long, remembering a saying of their lawgiver Lycurgus “that a fine head of hair made the handsome more comely still, and the ugly more terrible.” See Hair, Wig, and Baldness.
  • “the wheels within wheels”: Ezekiel’s vision: “their work was as it were a wheel in the middle of a wheel” (Ezekiel 1:16). See The Bible.
  • “Calvinistic rigors and denials and aggressions”: John Calvin (1509-1564) taught predestination, that God has chosen from eternity who will be saved. Calvinism shaped the Puritans and the Presbyterian and Reformed churches. Young sets its rigor against a woman’s “vague evasions and whimsicalities.” See Augustine and Christian Theology.
  • “Had some old crow plucked each hair away?”: In Aesop’s fable, a middle-aged man had two sweethearts. The young one plucked out his grey hairs, the old one his dark ones, until he was bald. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “Was she deserted at the altar by her bridegroom or her bride?”: Compare Miss Havisham in Dickens’s Great Expectations (1861), jilted on her wedding day, who stopped every clock and wore her wedding dress ever after. See Weddings and Funerals.
  • “this which was like sackcloth and ashes”: Mordecai “put on sackcloth with ashes” (Esther 4:1), and Christ says the cities “would have repented long ago in sackcloth and ashes” (Matthew 11:21). See The Bible.
  • “the existence which is the phenomenal beyond which we do not inquire”: Kant’s Critique of Pure Reason (1781) separates the phenomenal world, things as they appear to us, from the noumenal, things as they are in themselves, which we cannot know. See Kant and Hegel.
  • “recite the genealogies and names of those who fled into a desert”: The genealogies of Chapter 9. See the Chapter 9 resources.
  • “the digit or fingerbreadth, the tophach or handbreadth, the cubit, the fathom, the reed, the foot”: Miss MacIntosh’s lessons follow the tables of measures, weights, months, and Exodus stations printed in the back of many old Bibles. See Judaism.
    • The digit, or fingerbreadth, was about three-quarters of an inch.
    • The tophach, or handbreadth, was four fingers, about three inches. The table of the tabernacle has “a border of an hand breadth” (Exodus 25:25).
    • The cubit ran from the elbow to the fingertip, about eighteen inches. The long, or royal, cubit was about twenty inches.
    • The fathom is six feet, the span of outstretched arms. Paul’s sailors “sounded, and found it twenty fathoms” (Acts 27:28).
    • The measuring reed of Ezekiel’s temple vision was “six cubits long” (Ezekiel 40:5), about nine to ten feet. John is given “a reed like unto a rod” to measure the temple (Revelation 11:1).
    • A foot is a unit of measurement for length that is equal to 12 inches.
  • “the value of the Attic drachma, the gerah, the beka, the maneh, the talent”: The coins and weights of the Greeks and Hebrews, part of humanity’s effort to impose order and meaning on life. See Judaism.
    • The Attic Drachma was a silver coin and monetary unit of ancient Athens. It was one of the world’s earliest coins and was used from the 6th century BC until the Roman period. It had the head of Athena on one side and an owl on the other.
    • A gerah was an ancient Hebrew unit of weight and currency, the smallest the Hebrews used: “a shekel is twenty gerahs” (Exodus 30:13). The gerah also appears in Leviticus 27:25, Numbers 3:47 and 18:16, and Ezekiel 45:12.
    • The beka was a unit of weight equal to half a shekel: “A bekah for every man, that is, half a shekel” (Exodus 38:26). It was used as a counterweight for the silver half shekel brought to the temple each year. Abraham’s servant gave Rebekah “a golden earring of half a shekel weight” (Genesis 24:22).
    • In the Bible, maneh is a unit of weight that is sometimes translated as “pound”. It appears in Ezekiel 45:12, 1 Kings 10:17, Ezra 2:69, and Nehemiah 7:71,72. A maneh is estimated to weigh around 2.5 or 3 pounds.
    • In the Bible, a talent was a unit of weight and money and it also appears in the parable of the talents. A talent was a unit of weight that was approximately 80 pounds. The Israelites used 29 talents of gold to build the tabernacle. A talent was worth 20 years of labor. Parable of the talents Matthew 25:14. It is about a master who entrusts his wealth to three servants while he goes away on a journey. The parable is often interpreted as a lesson on the responsible use of one’s abilities, resources, and gifts.
  • “I would learn the Jewish calendar”: The Jewish calendar is a lunisolar calendar, which means it’s based on both the moon and the sun. It has 12 months, with each month containing either 29 or 30 days. To keep it in step with the solar year, a leap month is added seven times in every 19 years, the Metonic cycle. The months are Nisan, Iyar, Sivan, Tammuz, Av, Elul, Tishrei, Heshvan, Kislev, Tevet, Shevat, and Adar. In leap years, Adar becomes Adar II and another month, Adar I, is added before it. The civil year begins on Rosh Hashanah, but Nisan is the first month of the religious year: “This month shall be unto you the beginning of months” (Exodus 12:2). “Jyar” is an old spelling of Iyar. The calendar’s structure ensures that Jewish holidays take place in the correct season. It is significant as a motif of disciplined learning, historical consciousness, and the search for order in a world otherwise marked by instability and dreamlike disorientation. See Judaism.
  • “the names of the antediluvian patriarchs”: The antediluvian patriarchs are the individuals listed in Genesis 5 who lived before the Noachian flood: Adam, Seth, Enosh, Kenan, Mahalalel, Jared, Enoch, Methuselah, Lamech, and Noah. They represent the lineage of humanity before the Great Flood. Methuselah lived “nine hundred sixty and nine years” (Genesis 5:27). See Genesis.
  • “the itinerary of the children of Israel from Egypt to Canaan”: The Israelites’ journey from Egypt to Canaan involved a circuitous route through the wilderness, lasting approximately 40 years. The Israelites’ long journey is often attributed to their disobedience, doubt, and lack of faith in God’s leadership. God led them by a longer route to protect them from enemies and allow them time to develop their faith and trust. See Ancient Egypt and The Bible.
    • In the Bible, Rameses refers to a city in Egypt and a pharaoh’s name. The city was a treasury city built by the Hebrews in Egypt. It was the Israelites’ departure point from Egypt. (Exodus 12:37; Numbers 33:3)
    • Succoth was the Israelites’ first camp after leaving Rameses (Exodus 12:37; Numbers 33:5). Another Succoth, in Canaan, is where Jacob built booths (Genesis 33:17), and the feast of Sukkot celebrates the Israelites’ time in the desert.
    • In the Bible, Etham was a place where the Israelites camped after leaving Egypt and before turning back to Pi Hahiroth. It was a significant stop on their journey to the Promised Land. Exodus 13:20, Numbers 33:6-8
    • Pi-HaHiroth is the fourth station of the Exodus mentioned in Exodus 14:2. Marah and Elim come after the crossing, in the wilderness of Shur. The biblical books Exodus and Numbers refer to Pi-HaHiroth as the place where the Israelites encamped between Migdol and the sea, opposite Baal Zephon, while awaiting an attack by the Pharaoh, prior to crossing the Red Sea.
    • Marah is a place where the Israelites found bitter water after crossing the Red Sea. The word “Marah” is Hebrew for “bitterness”. Exodus 15:22-27
    • In the Bible, Elim was a place where the Israelites camped after leaving Egypt. It was the second stop after crossing the Red Sea, where there were “twelve wells of water, and threescore and ten palm trees” (Exodus 15:27). Exodus 15:27, Numbers 33:9, Exodus 16:1
  • “Where was the mouth of Wady Feiran?”: Wadi Feiran or Wadi Faran is Sinai’s largest and widest wadi (an Arabic term referring to a river valley or wet riverbed). It is an intermittent stream and rises from the mountains around Saint Catherine’s Monastery, at 2500 m above sea level. It is one of the alleged sites of Rephidim, a station of the Exodus where Moses struck a rock causing it to spring water, allowing his people the Hebrews to drink. “Wady” is the old spelling of wadi.
  • “Where was the desert of El-Tih?”: El-Tih, Arabic for “the wandering,” is the high limestone desert of central Sinai, named for the Israelites’ forty years of wandering.
    • The poem El Tih was written by Frederick Stewart. It serves as a geographical and symbolic touchstone that highlights the novel’s themes of journey, spiritual searching, education, and the confrontation with inner and outer wildernesses. It situates Vera’s personal experiences within a broader framework of historical, spiritual, and existential questioning.
  • “Should some die… on their way from Egypt to Canaan”: “Your carcases shall fall in this wilderness” (Numbers 14:29). A whole generation died on the way, and Moses saw the land from Mount Nebo but did not enter it (Deuteronomy 34:4-5). See The Bible.
  • “a face which should break all mirrors”: The old saying is of a face that would crack a mirror. See Mirrors and Reflections.
  • “a busy Etruscan seaport. A place where the dead came and went”: The Etruscans of ancient Italy built great cities of the dead, the necropoleis of Cerveteri and Tarquinia, and Populonia was one of their seaports. See Catherine Cartwheel.
  • “Better were two heads than one”: “Two heads are better than one,” in John Heywood’s proverbs (1546), and “Two are better than one” (Ecclesiastes 4:9). See Ecclesiastes.
  • “grey hair does not signify a necessary wisdom”: “The hoary head is a crown of glory, if it be found in the way of righteousness” (Proverbs 16:31), but “great men are not always wise: neither do the aged understand judgment” (Job 32:9). See The Book of Job.
  • “a babe cast into purgatory”: Catholic tradition placed unbaptized infants not in purgatory but in limbo, at the edge of Hell, where Dante puts the virtuous unbaptized (Inferno 4). See Dante.
  • “the dead tree of life”: “The tree of life also in the midst of the garden” (Genesis 2:9). See Genesis.
  • “the missing breast like a door knob”: The “knob where the breast was missing” of Chapter 18. See Miss MacIntosh and the Chapter 18 resources.
  • “Was God bald, too”: Children mocked Elisha, “Go up, thou bald head” (2 Kings 2:23), and the Ancient of Days has “the hair of his head like the pure wool” (Daniel 7:9). See The Bible and Hair, Wig, and Baldness.
  • “What was God, defined in so many terms”: Young’s litany gathers images of God and of Christ from across the Bible. The descriptions emphasize the profound mystery of divinity, the coexistence of opposites within the divine, and the limited capacity of human beings to grasp the full nature of God. See The Bible.
    • “as a fuller’s soap, as a refining fire”: “He is like a refiner’s fire, and like fullers’ soap” (Malachi 3:2). A fuller cleaned and whitened cloth.
    • “as the light of the morning when the sun arises, even as a dawn without clouds”: “As the light of the morning, when the sun riseth, even a morning without clouds” (2 Samuel 23:4).
    • “as rivers of water in a dry place, as a hiding place from the wind”: “A man shall be as an hiding place from the wind… as rivers of water in a dry place, as the shadow of a great rock in a weary land” (Isaiah 32:2).
    • “as a stone of grace”: Proverbs 17:8 reads “a precious stone” in the King James Version and “A stone of grace” in Young’s Literal Translation (1862).
    • “a friend that sticketh closer than a brother” (Proverbs 18:24).
    • “a brother born for adversity” and “a friend that loveth at all times” (Proverbs 17:17).
    • “a root out of a dry ground” (Isaiah 53:2).
    • “a nail fastened to a sure place”: “I will fasten him as a nail in a sure place” (Isaiah 22:23).
    • “as the tender grass by clear shining after the rain” (2 Samuel 23:4).
    • “as a casket”: In older English a casket is a small box for jewels, not a coffin. Compare “in that day when I make up my jewels” (Malachi 3:17).
    • “as an ark”: In the Bible, the Ark of the Covenant was a symbol of God’s presence and a holy place where God dwelt with his people. Noah’s ark carried life through the Flood (Genesis 6-8).
    • “as a crown of glory, as a diadem of beauty”: “In that day shall the Lord of hosts be for a crown of glory, and for a diadem of beauty” (Isaiah 28:5).
    • “as a belt of stars”: Orion’s Belt: “Canst thou bind the sweet influences of Pleiades, or loose the bands of Orion?” (Job 38:31). See Astronomy and The Book of Job.
    • “as the shadow of a great rock in a weary land” (Isaiah 32:2).
    • “Was there the plant not planted by God?”: “Every plant, which my heavenly Father hath not planted, shall be rooted up” (Matthew 15:13).
    • “Were there the strayed sheep?”: the parable of the lost sheep (Matthew 18:12; Luke 15:4).
    • “Was there the relapsing demoniac?”: the unclean spirit that returns with “seven other spirits more wicked than himself” (Matthew 12:43-45).
  • “something which none should see and live to relate”: “There shall no man see me, and live” (Exodus 33:20), as in Chapter 15. See The Bible and the Chapter 15 resources.
