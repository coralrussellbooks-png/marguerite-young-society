Summary

In full dawn Vera studies the sleeping woman, thinking about God, about hair, and about what the night revealed. She makes her decision: she will act as though she never saw. She will iron the shirts more eagerly, learn the genealogies and ancient measures, and keep the secret. She will protect Miss MacIntosh from the knowledge of herself. The chapter ends in a litany of biblical images for God.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a vivid depiction of dawn which paradoxically serves as both illumination and a force that has "already killed the heart of Miss MacIntosh" before her actual death. The light reveals wounds and deprivations that "would not heal with time," suggesting an exposure of deep, irrevocable suffering and loss.
  • A striking passage focuses on the physical absence of hair on Miss MacIntosh's head which symbolically represents a loss of feminine identity, vitality, and perhaps a history of mourning or trauma. Vera reflects on the significance of hair as beauty or terror and wonders about the circumstances of her hair loss, whether gradual or sudden, linking it to emotional or existential devastation.
  • Vera experiences a powerful tension between the recognition of Miss MacIntosh’s vulnerable, altered state and a desire to revert to ignorance for the sake of "common sense" and routine. There is a determined resolve to "go on as if I had never seen her," to protect her from the knowledge of herself, and to maintain the illusion of normalcy despite the stark reality of her condition.
  • The passage detailing Vera’s imagined daily routines contrasts sharply with the internal, almost mystical knowledge of Miss MacIntosh’s profound deprivation. Vera contemplates learning ancient measures, weights, and biblical geography, suggesting a search for meaning or order amid chaos and personal loss.
  • Toward the end, Vera muses deeply about mortality, identity, and divinity. The baldness and missing breast become metaphors for the exposed realities of life and death, while Vera questions the nature of God with rich biblical imagery and reflecting on God's multifaceted definitions as friend, refuge, crown, and shadow. This passage underscores the theme of confronting harsh truths and the limits of human understanding.
  • The final lines evoke a solemn recognition of the "image of life failing before his eyes," highlighting Vera’s acute awareness of mortality and the profound emotional weight of witnessing the decline of a once vibrant individual.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Miss MacIntosh

  • A figure of strength and common sense, yet marked by profound physical and existential deprivation.
  • Described as “hale and hearty, a red-headed nursemaid” in the past, yet now revealed in a state of vulnerability and loss.
  • Her condition is a mystery to Vera, who struggles to reconcile her external robustness with the internal and physical ravages revealed by the light.
  • She embodies the tension between resilience and suffering, denial and revelation.

Vera Cartwheel

  • Exhibits complex emotions: pity, love, denial, and a protective instinct toward Miss MacIntosh.
  • Is philosophical and reflective, wrestling with large existential questions prompted by Miss MacIntosh’s condition.
  • Exhibits a desire to maintain normalcy and routine to shield both himself and Miss MacIntosh from harsh truths.

Research: Themes

Light as a Revealing and Destructive Force

See: Light and Darkness

  • Dawn and light symbolize both revelation and destruction. Vera suggests that light has “already killed the heart of Miss MacIntosh” before her physical death, exposing wounds that cannot heal and deprivations that are eternal.
  • The light strips away illusions, revealing the “fearful forehead” and the “bald and naked, cloudless summit” of Miss MacIntosh’s head, symbolizing a harsh unveiling of truth and vulnerability.
  • There’s also a paradoxical beauty in the light, described as “diamond dawn” and “celestial aureole,” which offers hope for redemption and healing, yet simultaneously exposes harsh realities.

Loss and Absence

See: Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • Miss MacIntosh’s baldness is a potent symbol of loss: loss of hair as a “crowning glory,” loss of feminine nature, and the missing breast as a “door knob,” a stark reminder of what is absent.
  • Vera questions the cause and timing of this loss: was it gradual or sudden? Was it caused by heartbreak, mourning, or some unknowable trauma?.
  • This absence is not just physical but metaphysical, representing the stripping away of identity and vitality.

Mortality and the Inevitability of Decay

See: Death and Mortality · Decay and Ruin

  • Vera reflects on mortality, decay, and the “wounds in our sides” that light will bind up to prevent death like “Saul or Solomon or Sennacherib”.
  • There is a tension between the desire to protect Miss MacIntosh from the knowledge of her own condition and the inevitability of facing harsh truths.
  • The novel grapples with the idea that despite external appearances or the passage of time, some wounds and losses are timeless and unhealable.

Identity and Self-Perception

See: Identity and the Self

  • Miss MacIntosh’s self-awareness and common sense shield her from the deeper, visionary truths Vera perceives; she is “content with things just as they were” and would deny or not suspect the “empty vision” beyond the commonplace.
  • Vera’s internal conflict involves a desire to pretend normalcy to protect both Miss MacIntosh and herself from confronting the full reality of her condition.
  • There is also a complex emotional dynamic, where Vera simultaneously sees her as an “obstacle” to her happiness and as someone deeply significant, a secret lover or cause of his “vain pursuits”.

Spiritual and Existential Questions

See: Faith, God, and the Supernatural · The Search for Meaning

  • The narrative moves toward existential and spiritual reflection, pondering God’s nature, the problem of creation, and the place of suffering and imperfection in the divine order.
  • Vera likens the “baldness” and loss to a profound experience, possibly greater than mere mortal wisdom or aging, invoking biblical and mythological imagery.
  • The chapter closes with a meditation on life failing before one’s eyes and the idea of seeing something no one “should see and live to relate”.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Hair and Baldness

See: Hair, Wig, and Baldness

  • Hair symbolizes beauty, identity, and vitality; baldness represents loss, vulnerability, and exposure.
  • Absence of hair is described as a “barren, impoverished landscape without flower or bush or hedge”.

Light and Dawn

See: Lamps, Candles, and Light

  • Dawn is both “diamond” and “many-prismed,” a metaphor for clarity, revelation, but also a harsh revealer of truth.
  • The “Aurora Borealis” in her sparsely furnished bedroom suggests a surreal, otherworldly illumination, the “first experience of light” upon uncreated things.

Missing Breast

  • The missing breast is a stark physical symbol of loss and incompleteness, described as “like a door knob,” emphasizing its unnatural absence.

Biblical and Mythological References

See: Angels, Devils, and Myth

  • References to biblical figures (Saul, Solomon, Sennacherib) and ancient measurements, calendars, and places (Nisan, Jyar, Wady Feiran, Egypt to Canaan) ground the narrative in a spiritual and temporal history.
  • Quotes and allusions to Ovid and Greek perspectives on beauty and hair enhance the classical depth of the novel.

  • The measurement of a reed can refer to a measuring reed in the Bible. It was six cubits long. The length of a cubit depended on whether it was a common or long cubit.   • The Attic Drachma was a silver coin and monetary unit of ancient Athens. It was one of the world’s earliest coins and was used from the 6th century BC until the Roman period. It had the head of Athena on one side and an owl on the other.
  • A gerah was an ancient Hebrew unit of weight and currency that was equal to 1/20 of a shekel. It was the smallest unit of weight and money used by the Hebrews. Exodus 30:13, Leviticus 27:25, Numbers 3:47, 18:16, Ezekiel 45:12 and Leviticus 27:25
  • In the Bible, beka appears as both a unit of weight and a shortened form of the name Rebecca. It appears in the Torah twice. It was a unit of weight equal to half a shekel. It was used by Israelite men for use of the Sanctuary. It was used as a counterweight for the silver half shekel coin that was brought to the temple each year. Genesis, where a beka of gold was given to Rebecca in a nose ring. Exodus 38:26

  • In the Bible, a talent was a unit of weight and money and it also appears in the parable of the talents. A talent was a unit of weight that was approximately 80 pounds.   • The Jewish calendar is a lunisolar calendar, which means it’s based on both the moon and the sun. It has 12 months, with each month containing either 29 or 30 days. To keep the Jewish calendar in sync with the solar year, a leap month is added every 19 years. This is called the Metonic cycle. The months are Nisan, Iyar, Sivan, Tammuz, Av, Elul, Tishrei, Heshvan, Kislev, Tevet, Shevat, and Adar. In leap years, Adar becomes Adar II and another month, Adar I, is added before it. It begins on Rosh Hashanah. The calendar’s structure ensures that Jewish holidays take place in the correct season. It is significant as a motif of disciplined learning, historical consciousness, and the search for order in a world otherwise marked by instability and dreamlike disorientation.
