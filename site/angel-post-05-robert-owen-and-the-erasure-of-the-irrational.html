Chapter 5. Robert Owen and the Erasure of the Irrational

← Chapter 4 · Angel in the Forest: A Reading in Fourteen Chapters · Chapter 6 →

Marguerite Young / Angel in the Forest Chapter 5

Rapp demanded that his people submit to God. Owen demanded that his people submit to Reason. These look like opposite projects, the mystic and the scientist, the celibate order and the free-thinking commune, and Young grants Owen a sympathy she never quite extends to Rapp. But the deepest thing her book shows is that the two utopias failed in the same shape. A total vocabulary was imposed on a plural population, and the people who did not fit the vocabulary were not argued with. They were made invisible. Where Rapp could not see anyone who was not a future angel, Owen could not see anyone who was not a machine.

The right diagnosis and the wrong cure

Owen believed that man is entirely the creature of his circumstances. Change the circumstances, he held, and you change the man completely, because there is nothing to a person except the conditions that formed him. This was not a cruel idea in its origin. Young can see exactly what Owen saw, and so can we. He had run the cotton mills at New Lanark, and he had looked hard at what industrial capitalism did to the people caught in it, the mill children, the skeletal workers, the whole machinery of poverty grinding human beings down. His diagnosis of that suffering was not wrong. The conditions really were making the people. That much he saw clearly, and it is to Young's credit that she lets him be right about it.

The trouble is the cure. If a person is nothing but the product of circumstances, then to fix the person you rebuild the circumstances and treat the human being inside them as a mechanism to be adjusted. The diagnosis that begins in compassion for the mill children ends in a community where the members are handled like parts. Owen's science of society could relieve suffering only by first deciding that a person is a machine, and a machine has no inner life to consult, no grief that counts, no longing that means anything. The reduction that made the reform possible is the same reduction that made the reform unlivable.

This is the point the philosophy makes when it says reality is something we build, not something we simply find. Owen was certain he had discovered the objective truth of human nature, the science of society, as solid as physics. He did not notice that "man is a machine formed by circumstance" is a constructed version of reality exactly as much as "man is a future angel" is. Both men mistook the version they had built for the world itself, and both then tried to force the world to match. Owen's rationalism felt to him like the truth and looked to Young like one more cathedral of belief, this time in the vocabulary of mechanism.

The Pears family, writing home

The clearest view of what this felt like from the inside comes from the letters of Thomas and Sarah Pears, who arrived at New Harmony as believers and wrote home to Pittsburgh in a mounting confusion that Young lets us read almost in real time.

Sarah Pears could not make the new life mean anything. Her eyes had gone bad from endless sewing, so bad she could barely lift her hand before her face, and she wrote that at times she felt like an animated mummy. She cast, in her own phrase, many a long and lingering look back toward Pittsburgh, toward old Sabbath bells and conversation and the things she had loved, and she said she felt like a bird shut in a cage forever, out of humanity's reach. Her husband fared no better. Thomas Pears was made the community's bookkeeper, and when, in the general confusion, he debited and credited the same sum twice, he was dismissed. Young sees the real reason plainly: the Committee, that great God on wheels, had objected to his soul, to his idea of the pursuit of happiness. Having lost the books, he was put to sorting and picking wool, work he compared to being a slave in the South, and he wrote that he had almost taken to singing shanty songs, as if that could carry the humiliation.

The letters are documentary testimony, and Young uses them that way, as the ground-level record of a rational utopia. But they are also something the science of society had no place for. Sarah's homesickness, Thomas's shame, the small grief of a woman who misses her city and a man who has been demoted for having a soul: none of this exists in Owen's vocabulary. Circumstances do not have feelings. A machine does not long for Pittsburgh. The dancers who walked to the community balls through the mud and arrived black from head to foot are a perfect small image of the whole thing, people trying to reach the promised happiness and showing up filthy, and no line in the science of society can hold what that was like for them. Their experience was real and their utopia had no words for it, which meant that inside the utopia their experience did not officially exist.

The declaration that came too late

The high point of Owen's abstraction is the Declaration of Mental Independence, which he delivered on the Fourth of July, 1826, from Father Rapp's own old pulpit, the very spot from which Rapp had preached submission. Owen announced that mankind had always been enslaved by a trinity of monstrous evils: private property, absurd and irrational systems of religion, and marriage founded on property and religion together. He thought the document more important than the one the founding fathers had signed fifty years before. He started a new calendar from the date. He predicted that within twelve months the principles would begin to overshadow the whole earth.

Young sets this against what was actually happening in the town at that moment, and the contrast is quiet and merciless. While Owen declared the liberation of all mankind, the community around him was coming apart. The members, one observer reported, passed and repassed each other daily and remained perfect strangers, ignorant of one another's feelings, situations, and very often names. A large patch of cabbages rotted from neglect while people argued. Pigs and cows wandered in through the widening holes in the fences. The potatoes rotted in the ground. Owen was addressing all mankind from a pulpit while the people fifteen feet in front of him were hungry, homesick, and unknown to each other. The word "ought," as Young puts it, is a different thing from the word "is." People of fifty summers could not reduce themselves to bald sucklings and be born again as rational creatures on command. The declaration was pitched at a level of abstraction so high that it could not reach a single person in the room.

Old Greenwood in the lightning

The book's sharpest image of what Owen's vocabulary could not hold is old Greenwood, and it deserves to be examined closely. During a summer of terrible storms in New Harmony's second year, Robert Dale Owen, the founder's son, looked out a window into the green light of a thunderstorm and saw old Greenwood walking slowly up and down the flooded street, drenched, going nowhere, carrying upright over his shoulder a slender iron rod ten or twelve feet long, held the way a soldier carries a musket, while lightning tore across the sky and trees came down around him. Robert Dale could make no sense of it. He wondered whether the old man had simply lost his reason and lacked the sense to come in out of the rain.

Later Greenwood explained himself, and his explanation is entirely lucid. He was very old, he said, and unwell, and he suffered much, and he had thought the storm might be a chance to slip off and be laid quietly in the corner of the orchard. He had not wanted to take the responsibility of ending his own life, so he had gone out under the lightning and left the decision to God, who had already struck the house and the colt and the orchard trees and might have spared one more blow for him. There is nothing insane in this. It is the plain speech of an old man in pain who wants his suffering to end and will not raise his own hand to end it. But Owen's rational community has no noun for it. In the vocabulary of mechanism and circumstance, a suffering old man who wants to die is a malfunction, an eccentricity, a Fool of Nature. The science of society can register that Greenwood is behaving irrationally. It cannot hear what he is actually saying, because his whole condition, the weariness of a long life and the wish to be released from it, is written in a language the utopia does not speak. This is the violence the philosophy means when it says that when the official vocabulary has no word for your experience, you are not just misunderstood, you are made not to exist. Greenwood stands in the lightning fully present and fully articulate, and the rational world can only see a man too foolish to come inside.

Dublin, and the reply Owen could not hear

The clearest proof that Owen's problem was a problem of language, and not merely of tact, is what happened when he took his science to Ireland. Speaking before an audience of Catholic divines in Dublin, he told them, more or less, that the potato was worthier of study than the garments priests wore and miraculous food from heaven, that the humble potato would not multiply because priests wore its flowers in their hats, and that if more potatoes were raised there would be fewer souls migrating to another sphere. Grow the food, fix the circumstance, and the misery dissolves. It was his whole gospel in miniature.

The Catholic divines gave their answer, and the answer is not stupid. They spoke of the ravens who neither sow nor reap, whom God feeds anyway, and asked how much better than the birds a man might be. They said that no system could strip human nature of its frailties on this side of paradise, that suffering was not an error to be engineered out of the world but a condition through which vision came, and that the greater the suffering the greater the good that might be seen in it. The lowliest housemaid, they said, might have received the most exalted vision of all. And they asked the question that goes straight to the root of Owen's project: what man, merely by the employment of reason, could add one cubit to his stature, or take away an inch?

That is not superstition dressed up. It is a rival theory of the person, one that holds the human being to be more than the sum of his circumstances, and holds suffering to carry a meaning that no rearrangement of conditions could replace. Owen could not engage it, and the reason he could not is the crux of that chapter. He did not lose the argument with the divines. He never entered it, because his framework had already decided that what they were describing did not exist. To Owen there was reality, which was mechanism and circumstance, and there was error, which was everything else, and the divines had simply been filed under error before they opened their mouths. A vocabulary that begins by ruling the opponent out of existence cannot be argued with, only escaped, and no one in that room could escape it.

The same erasure, wearing reason's clothes

Honestly, it took me a while to understand why Young didn’t make Owen’s utopia the answer to Rapp’s. I wanted to believe that there was a reasonable answer to life. Young is fair to Owen. He was kinder than Rapp, his intentions were better, his diagnosis of industrial misery was largely correct, and he never castrated anyone or promised anyone an angel. But the structure of his failure is the same structure, and Young built the two utopias side by side so that we would feel the sameness underneath the difference. Rapp imposed a single vocabulary, the future angel, and could not see the plural people underneath it. Owen imposed a single vocabulary, the machine formed by circumstance, and could not see Sarah Pears missing Pittsburgh, or Thomas Pears demoted for having a soul, or old Greenwood asking the lightning for rest, or the Catholic divines describing a person that reason could not reduce.

Submission to Reason turns out to erase the individual as thoroughly as submission to God, and it does it by the same method, which is a method of language. Both utopias handed everyone a complete account of what a human being is, and then stopped being able to perceive the humans who did not match the account. What the official vocabulary cannot name, it does not correct or convert. It simply makes them disappear, and calls the disappearance reason.
