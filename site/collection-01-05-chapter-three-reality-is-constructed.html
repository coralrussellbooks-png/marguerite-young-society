Chapter Three: Reality Is Constructed

Pillar: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed · Across every book

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

The second pillar is the one most likely to be misunderstood, so it is worth stating it precisely before unpacking it. Young does not argue that illusion is preferable to reality, that we should choose fantasy over fact, or that the interior life is more valuable than the exterior world. She argues something more fundamental and more disturbing: that the distinction itself is false. That illusion is not the opposite of reality but its medium. That human beings do not live in the world and then sometimes retreat into illusion. They live in illusion the way fish live in water, and what we call reality is one particular illusion that has achieved consensus.

Western thought has organized itself around the real and the illusory as a foundational opposition. Plato's cave is the origin story: the shadows on the wall are an illusion, the sunlit world outside is reality, and the philosopher's task is to drag people from the former toward the latter. Young answers this differently. The evil demon is not the nightmare. The dream of escaping the demon is the nightmare, because the escape route leads nowhere. There is no sunlit world outside the cave. There is only a larger cave, more elaborately lit, whose inhabitants have agreed to call their shadows real.

Catherine's world is the philosophical argument made flesh. Catherine is Vera's mother, the woman who retreats into opium dreams in her mansion on the eastern seaboard while Vera is raised by the practical Miss MacIntosh. The critical tradition has generally read Catherine as the negative pole of the novel: the deluded one, the absent mother, the cautionary example of what happens when you abandon consensus reality for interior fantasy. Young refuses that reading at every turn. Catherine's interior world has geography, population, history, and internal logic. It has weight. Young gives it the same novelistic density she gives the Indiana plains, the same accumulation of specific detail, the same insistence that this place is real because a self is genuinely living in it.

The philosophical point is not that Catherine is right and Miss MacIntosh is wrong. It is that the framework that would let us adjudicate between them, the framework that sorts experience into real and illusory, is itself an illusion. Miss MacIntosh's solid world is sustained by the same mechanism as Catherine's opium world: a self that has organized its experience around a set of commitments and called those commitments reality. When Miss MacIntosh's wig comes off, her reality comes off with it. The solid world was always a performance of solidity. The Indiana plains were always as constructed as Catherine's dreams.

The self does not receive reality passively. It constructs reality actively, from the materials available to it, shaped by desire and grief and history and the particular plurality it contains. What we call consensus reality is the construction that enough selves have agreed on to treat as given. It is not less constructed for being agreed upon. It is just more crowded.

If illusion is primary, if the real is always already a construction, then the question of who gets to construct it is a political question of the first order. Every dominant political system depends on the naturalization of its own construction: the idea that the way things are is the way things are, not the way things have been arranged by people with interests in keeping them arranged that way. Young's insistence that all reality is constructed does not produce the conclusion that all constructions are equal. It produces the opposite conclusion: that the question of which constructions dominate, and at whose expense, is the central political question, and that the first act of political resistance is the refusal to mistake the dominant construction for nature.

Young's formal choices in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling are themselves an argument about illusion. The novel does not distinguish between Catherine's interior world and the exterior world in its prose. The sentences that move through Catherine's opium visions have the same texture, the same density, the same commitment to specificity as the sentences that describe the Grey Goose bus or the Indiana landscape. Young refuses the formal signals that literary fiction conventionally uses to mark the boundary between interior and exterior, between dream and waking, between illusion and reality. This is not a stylistic quirk. It is a philosophical position expressed through craft. The form would be saying what the argument denies if it treated Catherine's world as thinner. Young is too rigorous for that inconsistency.

The result is a reading experience that is genuinely disorienting in the early chapters and genuinely clarifying by the time you understand what Young is doing. The disorientation is not a failure of communication. It is the communication. You are being asked to experience the collapse of the real and the illusory, not to be told about it.

That is the second pillar. Reality is not what we thought it was. It is not less real for that. It is more real, because what we thought it was was always already a simplification, and the world Young is pointing toward is the actual one: plural, constructed, contested, and alive in ways that the simplified version never was.
