Research notes: Chapter 73 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 73

Characters: Esther Longtree · Joe Goldberg

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

Esther is constitutively multiple. She is simultaneously:

  • A waitress serving customers
  • An eternal mother who has never successfully given birth
  • A beloved of men she cannot remember
  • The keeper of secrets she never chose to keep
  • A defendant in a trial that has not yet happened
  • A woman nursing her own thumb, her own fist, a bullfrog

"She had always been so maternal ever since she could remember, she really had, mothering everything, all the torments, and even when she was a little girl had nursed her thumb, wrapping her thumb in a silken hood with draw-strings, imagining the fingernail her other face"

The fingernail imagined as "her other face" is not metaphor; it is a structural confession. Esther does not have one face. Her self proliferates outward into objects, animals, absent children, dying men's memories of her. She is the woman Joe Goldberg remembers, who is not the woman she is. She is the mother of children she cannot locate. She is the peak of a man's existence and simultaneously cannot place him in her mind at all.

Not One Self pillar insists this is not a problem to fix. The chapter agrees completely. There is no moment where Esther strains toward unity or coherence. Her multiplicity is her mode of being. She moves slowly, she forgets, she smiles and is sad, she is "brilliant but dumb"; these are not contradictions awaiting resolution. They are the simultaneous truth of what she is.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The entire chapter is structured around the question of whether anything happened. Did Joe Goldberg meet Esther in the graveyard? Did she conceive his child? Was there a child? The text never resolves this. More radically, it suggests the question itself may be malformed.

"It might have been some other woman he had met among the angels, faceless, void beings, some other woman with immense, swinging hips, with crossed eyes, puzzled smile"

"He could have been right, and she could have been wrong, and there was no answer but time"

The dying fathers build entire architectures of meaning (apple orchards in North Dakota willed to children, requests for photographs, memories of graveyards and marble slabs) on events that may or may not have occurred. But here is the philosophical precision of the chapter: the constructed reality of the dying fathers is not more or less real than Esther's constructed reality of forgetting. Both are fabrications. Both are lived inside with full sincerity.

Old Doctor articulates the epistemological position directly:

"Supreme knowledge was always the last cruelty"

This is not cynicism. It is the honest acknowledgment that the "sunlit world outside the cave" (the true account of what happened, the real child, the verified father) does not exist. There is only the cave of Esther's clouded memory and the cave of Joe Goldberg's dying wish. Neither is more primary. The line between illusion and reality is, as the pillar states, itself a construction.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

This is perhaps the pillar most viscerally embodied in the chapter. Esther does not grieve and recover. She is grief, continuously, structurally, climatically.

"She was nearly always silent, waxing, waxing, never waning, her flesh swollen with tides of memory and of forgetfulness"

The stillborn do not recede. They accumulate:

"The stillborn clapped their hands when she came by, shouted in her ear like torments"

The grief is not located at a single loss. It is the atmosphere of her existence. Every pregnancy adds to the total. Every dying father's letter adds to the total. Every silence she keeps adds to the total. The chapter's extraordinary length (its refusal to conclude, its circling and recircling of the same events from slightly different angles) formally enacts this accumulation. We are not moving through the chapter toward resolution. We are deepening into it, layer by layer.

The most devastating expression of grief-as-weather comes in the description of the birth of nothing:

"The birth of nothing was the hardest birth to bear"

This is not a single event. It is a category of experience she has lived through repeatedly. The hardest grief is not the grief of losing something. It is the grief of the thing that never arrived, the child that moved all night and was not there at dawn. This grief has no object to mourn, no grave to visit, no name to speak. It is pure weather.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Esther is, paradoxically, both the subject of witnessing and its most complex practitioner. She witnesses the dying fathers with a completeness that refuses the easy comfort of truth-telling:

"Let the dying fathers believe the utmost, that she had conceived their children, that their children were born, that their children lived! What could she have said?"

This is not deception in the morally simple sense. It is a form of radical witnessing: seeing the dying man so fully, seeing the fragility of his constructed meaning so completely, that she refuses to destroy it with a fact. Old Doctor names the ethics:

"There was no harm in deceiving the dying who would never live to find out what the matter was"

But Esther also witnesses herself, and this is where the pillar becomes most demanding. She does not flinch from her own forgetting. She does not excuse it or explain it away. She holds it:

"It did disappoint her to be so grossly forgetful"

"Her lack of any feeling was her deepest feeling now"

That last line is one of the most precise formulations of Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act in the entire chapter. She does not pretend to feel what she does not feel. She witnesses even her own numbness without rushing to replace it with performed emotion. The pillar demands refusal to simplify. Esther's silence (to the dying fathers, to the census-taker, to the detective) is the enacted form of that refusal. Every silence says: this cannot be simplified into an answer.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Esther knits:

"She was knitting a little hood in the empty hours here in the restaurant, forgetful that she had no thread, for it did not matter, she having no baby as yet to wear the little hood"

This image is the purest possible icon of Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary. She knits without thread, for a baby not yet born, in a restaurant where fewer and fewer customers come. The building is entirely provisional. It may produce nothing. She builds anyway.

The chapter itself is recursively structured; it returns again and again to Joe Goldberg, to the graveyard, to the stillborn, to the question of the child, each time from a slightly different angle, each time having accumulated slightly more. This is not poor construction. It is the formal enactment of the pillar: the building is never finished, and the return to the beginning is not failure but method.

Esther's perpetual pregnancy is itself recursive construction. She conceives, carries, loses, and conceives again. The cycle does not produce accumulating product. It produces accumulating experience: the sediment of all those pregnancies, all those silences, all those fathers. She is built by her own building.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The dying fathers demand completion. They demand to know: was the child born? Is the child alive? What is the child's age, complexion, weight? They write letters seeking the concluded sentence of their lives.

"Years, years after the child was conceived, the father would write, asking, Was the child ever born? The father might even send something, a little shoe."

The little shoe is heartbreaking precisely because it presumes completion: a child of determinable size, a foot that can be measured. Esther cannot provide this. Not because she is withholding, but because the completed version does not exist. The child is the ongoing sentence. The child is still coming, still moving under her heart, still the little shadow boxer.

The chapter's most explicit statement of this pillar:

"If not, then always."

This extraordinary sentence, placed almost alone, is Unfinished Is Honest in its most compressed form. The child that was not conceived is not simply absent. It is always: permanently in the state of not-yet, permanently in the ongoing sentence. The demand for completion (was it conceived or not?) is answered with a category that refuses the binary: always.

The trial that may come, the detective seeking proof of murder, represents the violence of the demand for completion. The law wants a concluded sentence: guilty or innocent, child born or not born, alive or dead. Esther's body refuses to provide one.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Esther is marginal in every possible register:

  • Physically: deformed foot, crossed eyes, bald spot, "too, too solid flesh"
  • Socially: unmarried, multiply impregnated, working-class waitress
  • Legally: potentially facing murder charges for children who may never have existed
  • Epistemically: unable to remember, unable to verify, unable to provide the accounts that social reality requires

She was "a big, dumb-headed waitress, brilliant but dumb"

The paradox, brilliant but dumb, is not ironic. It names her exact position at the margin of the categories. She exceeds the category of "dumb waitress" (she understands more than the dying fathers, more than old Doctor, more than the detective) while simultaneously being excluded from the category of "brilliant" by her body, her class, her forgetting.

But the pillar's deepest claim is that the marginal person reveals the constructed nature of the categories themselves. Esther does this constantly. Her body refuses to behave as a body should: pregnancies that produce snowflakes, labor that produces nothing, children hidden under persimmon trees and in hollow stumps. She makes visible that "child," "birth," "mother," "father" are categories with edges, and that at those edges, the construction becomes visible.

The old mule is her shadow-double in this register:

"For the mother mule had only rudimentary breasts, small stalks, and when she came into heat, as would sometimes happen because of life's vagaries and life's persistence, yet the heat was meaningless, for nothing could be born"

The mule is the pure marginal figure: biologically positioned at the edge of reproduction, structurally excluded from the category of "mother" while experiencing all its drives. The mule follows Esther everywhere. It is not accidental. It is the text's own ontological argument: Esther and the mule are the evidence that the category of "mother" is a construction, and that the construction has edges where creatures live who cannot be contained by it.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The chapter's temporal structure is explicitly sedimentary. Events do not recede into the past. They press upward:

"The stillborn clapped their hands when she came by, shouted in her ear like torments"

The stillborn are not memories. They are presences, accumulated beneath and around her, still active, still demanding. The chapter circles back through time not as nostalgia but as geology; each pass through the graveyard scene, the birth-of-nothing scene, the dying-fathers scene adds another layer of deposit.

The birth of nothing is the most powerful sedimentary event in the chapter. It happened in summer, but when morning came it was winter. Time had collapsed. The past and present had exchanged places. The landscape had moved. Only Esther remained, and she was thin: emptied of the deposit she had been carrying.

"How had time passed? It had been summer when she was taken by her labor pains, and only one night had passed, but it was winter when the morning came so clear."

This is not magical realism for its own sake. It is the phenomenology of sedimentary time made literal. The night of labor was not one night. It was all the nights. The season that ended was not just summer. It was the accumulated summers of all her pregnancies. Time does not move linearly in Esther's world because sedimentary time does not move linearly. It compresses.

Joe Goldberg, dying in the Colorado mountains, experiences the same compression:

"The stars roaring around him, he could not strike"

His entire career (all the fights, all the victories, all the women) is simultaneously present to him as he dies. The past is not behind him. It is beneath him, and it is rising.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter performs a sustained meditation on the failure of language to contain Esther's experience. The words available to describe her, "mother," "pregnant," "stillborn," "father," "child", are all technically applicable and all fundamentally inadequate.

Consider the word "stillborn." It is used throughout, but the stillborn here are:

"the snowflakes, the marsh butterflies, the cobwebs, the ears without eyes, the eyes without ears, the little foot"

These are not stillborn children in any medical or legal sense. But the word "stillborn" is the only word Esther has for what she produces. The language shapes what can be seen: by using "stillborn," the text makes visible the violence of the word's inadequacy; it names her losses as deaths, as children who almost were, when they may have been something the language has no word for at all.

The dying fathers' language is explicitly constructed and explicitly located:

"He had fought when the blood streamed down his face, had never given in, had won all his battles with men"

Joe Goldberg describes himself entirely in the language of boxing: feinting, sparring, knockouts, the surprised look in the other guy's eyes. This is not neutral description. It is language speaking from a particular location: the masculine, competitive, victory-organized worldview of a professional fighter. When he applies this language to God, to death, to Esther herself ("he had taken her against her will", "he had knocked her out"), the violence of that language's location becomes fully visible.

Esther's language, by contrast, is the language of weather, seasons, accumulation:

"a powder of baby stars strewn upon the dark, glassy sky, an old sound of crying, of falling leaves as thin as air, of wind scraping"

This is language that names its own location; it speaks from inside the body, inside the season, inside the accumulation. It does not pretend to speak from nowhere.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The chapter is saturated with the language and imagery of the sacred (angels, graveyards, God, worms, resurrection, inscriptions) but every sacred reference is pulled back into the immanent. God appears only as Joe Goldberg's opponent in a boxing match. Angels appear only as crumbling stone figures in a hillside graveyard, their noses knocked off during struggles in the grass. The graveyard itself, the most sacred space in the chapter, is littered with:

"the birth control devices littering the bare ground, the paper sacks, the beer bottles, the crumbling angels, the baby angels, the crosses"

This is not desecration. This is The Sacred Is Here in its most precise form. The sacred is not elsewhere. It is here, in the graveyard where lovers meet and are separated by the night policeman, where Joe Goldberg stumbles upon Esther Longtree, where the living and the dead share the same ground.

Old Doctor is the chapter's most explicit theologian of immanent transcendence:

"Death was kindred to love. It was his love of death and not of life which had caused him to enter the medical profession in the first place"

This is not nihilism. It is the recognition that the sacred, the deepest intensity of being, is found not by transcending the mortal world but by going deeper into it, past the surface of the living body into the womb, the grave, the space where love and death are indistinguishable.

Esther herself is the sacred in the ordinary. She is described with consistent imagery of luminosity:

"illuminated from within, increasing"

"her dark eyes bright with light"

She does not transcend her ordinariness: the deformed foot, the restaurant, the slow movement, the forgetting. She is transcendent through it, as it. The sacred lives in her crossed eyes and her bald spot and her everlasting pregnancy. This is exactly where the pillar says it lives.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is the pillar that most completely illuminates the structural meaning of Esther's perpetual pregnancy. She is not a woman waiting to give birth. She is the crossing point: the place where all the dying fathers' time crosses the not-yet-born children's time, where the accumulated past presses up through her body toward a future that has not arrived.

"She was all alone here, waiting, a big, dumb-headed waitress, brilliant but dumb, waiting on customers, waiting for the visitant, whole and complete, the child to come"

The "visitant, whole and complete" is not just a baby. It is the conclusion that time has been building toward: the crossing point made visible. But the chapter's radical insight is that the crossing point cannot be concluded. The little shadow boxer remains under her heart because the crossing point is not an event. It is a position. Esther is constitutively that position.

The pillar states: you are constituted by what was deposited before you arrived, and you are constituting the ground the not-yet-born will stand on. Esther's body is the literal enactment of this. She is constituted by:

  • All the fathers who deposited their seed and their dying wishes in her
  • Old Doctor who deposited his epistemology of silence
  • The graveyard with its inscriptions and its accumulated dead
  • The old mule with its impossible heat

And she is constituting the ground for:

  • The little shadow boxer, not yet born
  • The children of the stillborn, who clap their hands at her passing
  • The jury that will judge her
  • The reader, who stands on the ground she is making

The obligation is structural. She did not choose to be the crossing point. She was made into it by the accumulation of fathers, losses, silences, pregnancies. The carrying continues because it cannot stop. The little shadow boxer is still moving:

"A fury inhabited her womb, a shadow boxing, and might be his—so old, it might creep forth as a white-bearded child leaning on a crutch."

White-bearded, leaning on a crutch, ancient before birth; this is the child of sedimentary time, the child of the crossing point. It has been in gestation so long it has aged inside her. The ancestors are in the sediment. The carrying continues.
