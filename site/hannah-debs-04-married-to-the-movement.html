Part Four: Married to the Movement

Part of a reading of Harp Song for a Radical (1999), Marguerite Young’s life of Eugene Victor Debs.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 25, 2026

← Part Three · The Suffragette and the Socialist: Cousin Hannah and Eugene Debs · Part Five →

What each crusade cost at home

In Chapter 35 of Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, Cousin Hannah is said to have been “married, it had seemed, to the suffrage movement.” The movement was her “husband, her lord and master.” She never married a man.

The society’s essay “Married to the Movement” reads this closely. Hannah, it argues, “has not escaped the marriage structure” but has “transferred it” from a man to a cause that demands total loyalty. She is both the knight who breaks other women’s chains and a bride swept into a devotion as total as any wedding vow. When she dies, Mr. Spitzer opens her trunks and finds “so many wedding gowns, sheets of silk and veils of lace” (Chapter 52), the gowns of the bride she never became. He opens them gently. The novel never pries the lid off her secret. It lets the gowns speak.

There is another buried thing in Hannah’s past. On a mountain, long ago, she let go of a young woman’s hand in a snowstorm, and the girl was lost (Chapter 45). The society’s philosophy essay, “Cousin Hannah and the Life of Action,” suggests her crusade grew out of that moment. Her life of action begins in a private failure to hold on.

Debs married. His wife, Kate, appears in Harp Song, and Young lets her shine in a single sharp line: Kate’s household management “could have given a lesson to the United States government.” It is funny and it is pointed. In Young’s hands the home is not the opposite of politics. It is a smaller government, sometimes better run than the large one.

Young’s sympathy for Kate is a choice. Later writers, most memorably Irving Stone in his 1947 novel Adversary in the House, cast Kate Debs as cold to her husband’s causes. Young does not. She writes about the letters the couple exchanged under railroad pen names, “Signal” and “Headlight,” and she treats the marriage with warmth. The couple had no children. In 1890 they built the house on North Eighth Street in Terre Haute that is now the Debs Home museum.

The contrast is worth sitting with. Hannah’s culture gave a woman two choices, a husband or a cause, and she took the cause and lived inside it as a marriage. Debs, as a man of the same century, could have both a wife and a movement, and a household that kept running while he rode the rails organizing. The cost of a crusade fell differently depending on who carried it.

Young was not sentimental about either arrangement. Hannah’s trunks of gowns are sad, but Hannah is not pitiable. She lived the life she chose and changed laws doing it. And Kate’s competence is praised, but it is the competence of the person who stayed home. Read together, Hannah and the Debs household show the private underside of public courage. Every crusade has a house behind it, empty or full.
