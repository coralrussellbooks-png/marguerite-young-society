Harp Song for a Radical

Chapter 15. What the Song Never Reached

← Chapter 14 · The Unfinished Harp: Reading Harp Song for a Radical

The Pullman strike, the missing volumes, and what Young's philosophy makes of an unfinished life

Harp Song for a Radical is the life of a man who is not yet the man it is about. The Debs of history led the Pullman strike of 1894, went to jail, read Marx in his cell, and came out a socialist. He helped found the Socialist Party and ran for president five times, the last time in 1920 from the Atlanta Penitentiary, jailed for a speech against the First World War. The Debs of Young's book is a union secretary and magazine editor in his early thirties. Everything else lives in the front matter, the opening chapter, the Editorial Note, and one small phrase at the end: "As will be seen."

This final chapter asks what that gap means.

The strike in a single paragraph

The Pullman strike was meant to close the book. Ruas says so in his introduction. Young was writing that chapter when she first fell ill. What survives is one paragraph in the Editorial Note on page 581. It records that Debs was sentenced to six months "for contempt of the federal injunction stopping the strike against the Pullman Palace Car Company" and that in jail "Debs first read Marx and reread old favorites, notably Victor Hugo's Les Misérables." That paragraph is Ruas's, not Young's. It gets even the union's name wrong.

Young's book does not contain the strike, but it contains everything the strike was built from. The court injunction had its first rehearsal in 1877. Robert Todd Lincoln, Pullman's lawyer, grew up in the Lincoln chapters. The Gatling gun that the injunction improved on appeared in the graveyard in Indianapolis. The dream of one big railroad union was Debs's "Ultima Thule of labor federation." Every layer is in place. Only the event is missing.

Where the missing books would have gone

The book's last sentence before the Afterword promises that Debs's elegiac sense would continue "for as long as he lived and perhaps long afterward. As will be seen." Ruas describes the scope of the published book as "the period of American history that formed the historical Debs." Together, those lines point to more books: at least one covering the strike, the prison, and the birth of the Socialist Party, and very likely another for the long campaigns, the war, and Atlanta.

What exists is the foundation. Young spent twenty-five years on the ground floor of a house she did not live to build.

The lens, one last time

This study has read the book through eleven principles of a philosophy that I believe runs through her work. Young never named or alluded to this. Here is what I believe each has found.

Not One Self. Debs is never one thing in these pages. He is his father's reader and his mother's revolutionary, Riley's drinking partner and Kate's absent husband. He is the man who once blamed a dead fireman and never did again, and the cautious union man who would become the most jailed radical in the country.

Reality Is Constructed. The book keeps exposing built things that pass for natural ones: the "act of God," the railroad accident report, the watermarked reference letter, the court that declared Mary Lincoln insane, and the law that hanged the Mollies.

Grief Doesn't End. Dead sisters, dead brakemen, dead sons of Marx and of Pierce, Heine on his mattress grave. None of them are redeemed. Young lets the grief stand.

Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act. Young stays in the room with Mary Lincoln, Lucy Harris, and the Mollies, and with Debs's own lapses, including his silence on Haymarket.

Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary. Every blueprint in the book fails: Icaria, Communia, Harmony, Zion. The dream passes on anyway.

Unfinished Is Honest. The book itself is the proof. It is unfinished, and it is honest.

The Margins Reveal the Truth. Convicts voting, a skeptical wife, a scorned suffragist walking through a mob, a Polish barber paying dues for a colony that no longer exists. The margins see the seams.

The Past Doesn't Go Away. Nothing starts fresh. The Icarians move into the Mormons' ruins, New Harmony is written over Harmony, and Debs is born on top of Colmar, Paris, the Shakespeare Hotel, and a Rappite mortgage.

Language Shapes What's Possible. McGuffey's readers leave out the living struggle. Twain wears a mask. Young's own sentences, long and circling, refuse the flat voice of official history.

The Sacred Is Here. A grocery scale, a paint scraper, a tailors' burial fund, and the eight-hour day that Young said might be all that comes out of a dream. That is enough.

The Crossing Point. Weitling and a Debs baby buried in the same Brooklyn cemetery. Robert Todd Lincoln between the Emancipator and Pullman. McParlan between the Mollies and Haywood. A young man in Terre Haute standing where all of it meets.

A tension to leave open

It would be too neat to say that this philosophy I call Radical Immanence simply confirms Debs's. Debs believed in a coming transformation. In the Afterword, Young quotes his faith that "Out of the misery of the past would surely rise the civilization of the future" (579). That is a promise of a distant arrival, and the principle this study calls The Sacred Is Here distrusts exactly such promises.

Young does not resolve this, and she may not have wanted to. Her own statement of faith, quoted in the first chapter of this study, was different from Debs's. She believed in walking "towards utopia, which may not exist, on a bridge which might end before you reach the other side." Debs believed the bridge reached the other shore. Young believed in walking it anyway. The book holds both.

The record, in sum

Across these chapters, the "Checking the record" sections have turned up a consistent pattern. Young's large outlines are usually right, and her small details are often wrong. There are misdated revolutions and court decisions, a Chinese reformer's name and a poet's name confused, William Jennings Bryan put in the place of William Cullen Bryant, two Harmonies merged into one, a union's name garbled in the editor's own note. At least once, in 1877, she chose her subject's comfort over the harder truth. Some errors are clearly hers. Others show how thinly the manuscript was checked after her death.

None of this makes the book less valuable. It makes it what it is: a great, flawed, unfinished work by a writer who believed that flawed and unfinished were honest conditions, not failures to hide.

The last image

Young ends with Norman Thomas's picture of Debs after death, a tall man in a gray suit, "walking the star belt," with "this world always half in darkness" (580). It is an image of someone still walking and still keeping watch, with no arrival in sight.

That is also a fair picture of the book. It never reaches Pullman, Atlanta, or the end of Debs's road. It walks as far as it can and stops mid-stride, with the next step already promised: "As will be seen."
