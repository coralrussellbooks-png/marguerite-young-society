Summary

Vera wakes at dawn in Miss MacIntosh's bed, half hoping the night was a dream. Before opening her eyes she reconsiders details she never questioned: the baseball cap, the evening ribbons, the veils over the whaler's hat, the umbrella indoors. Then she looks. The bald head lies on the pillow. The red wig hangs on the hat rack. Around the bare room are false teeth in a glass, a Bible chained to the table, a deuce of clubs, cards printed with Dutch windmills, and seaweed hanging on the barred door. Then she sees that one breast has been amputated.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a meticulous depiction of Miss MacIntosh’s largely bare and austere bedroom. The room contains very few ornaments: a bust of Martin Luther, a large seashell used as a doorstop, and crumbling cherubim heads around a blackened fireplace, covered by cheese-cloth drapery. The room is described as "plain," "barely furnished," and showing signs of decay which metaphorically reflects Miss MacIntosh’s own physical and perhaps spiritual condition. This setting evokes a sense of austerity, fragility, and impermanence, mirroring the character’s struggle with her own vulnerabilities.
  • Vera observes Miss MacIntosh’s physical traits with a mix of curiosity and tenderness. Notable is the absence of her eyebrows, the rapid changes in the color of her skin, her habitual wearing of old caps or a baseball cap, and the ever-present whistle around her neck. The description of her headgear and her old black umbrella carried like a shield even indoors, suggests a defensive posture against the world or perhaps her own frailty. These details hint at a woman who is both physically fragile and psychologically guarded, living with an awareness of her own vulnerability.
  • A striking image is that of Miss MacIntosh’s bald head lying next to Vera's pillow, "cold as marble" and "seldom touched by sunlight." This evokes a sense of mortality and the harsh reality of illness or aging. Her red wig, compared to a "rare tropical bird," is carefully placed within reach, symbolizing a constructed identity or a protective mask she wears in public. The presence of her worn clothing, her corset stained and draped over a chair, and other personal belongings scattered around the room reinforce the theme of physical deterioration and survival. Vera’s detailed observation of these items underscores a deep intimacy and respect for Miss MacIntosh’s resilience despite her hardships.
  • Among the personal items is a set of translucent playing cards with Dutch windmills, with the upturned card being the deuce of clubs, described as "the test of tears" and "the omen of death." This is particularly poignant given Miss MacIntosh’s critical view of gambling as "the crafty Devil’s handiwork." The card’s presence symbolizes fate, mortality, and perhaps the inevitability of death. It also highlights the duality in Miss MacIntosh’s beliefs between her Christian faith, her honesty, and her rejection of "double motions" or duplicity in the heart. This passage deepens the psychological complexity of her character and sets a somber, reflective tone.
  • The description of the room’s decay continues, with cracked walls, stained ceilings, barred doors with seaweed and barnacles, mud tracks, grains of sand, and salt gleamings. These vivid natural and physical details evoke a sense of isolation, imprisonment, and the passage of time, suggesting that Miss MacIntosh’s life is enclosed, weathered by external elements and internal struggles. The imagery of the umbrella, still opened on the floor as if for protection during the night, reinforces the motif of defense and vulnerability. Vera’s discovery that one of Miss MacIntosh’s breasts has been amputated is a stark and intimate revelation, symbolizing loss and suffering, yet also the survival and endurance of the woman.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Miss MacIntosh

  • Bald head cold as marble, veined limbs, a missing breast (amputated), thin and ascetic face without any facial hair, red wig resembling a tropical bird, gnarled hands.
  • Wears a baseball cap or old caps in the morning and a green ribbon in the evenings.
  • Always carries a whistle around her neck.
  • Wears a veil-tied hat outdoors, sometimes layered with heavy veiling.
  • Carries an old black umbrella indoors and outdoors, like a shield.
  • Sleeps lightly, alert to the slightest noise.
  • Has a strong Christian faith, critical of gambling and superstition.
  • Exhibits physical and emotional contradictions: shifting skin color, blushing for no apparent reason, and trying to resist external forces (“buck[ing] against the broncho wind”).
  • The playing cards, especially the “deuce of clubs,” symbolize fate, death, or a trial by tears, despite her rejection of gambling.
  • Her patched clothing and worn items reflect hardship and resilience.

Vera Cartwheel

  • She describes their mind as "lucid as day" but simultaneously reveals a hesitation or reluctance to fully open their eyes and confront reality directly. This tension between clarity and avoidance suggests an internal conflict or a fragile psychological state. She seems caught between the realms of dream and waking, reality and imagination, as shown by their uncertainty about whether they are truly awake despite the vividness of the dawn light and surroundings.
  • She meticulously catalogues the objects and conditions in Miss MacIntosh’s bedroom, from the "crumbling heads of cherubim" to the "red wig, almost purple," and the "jeweled water glass" holding false teeth. This detailed observation indicates a careful, almost forensic attention to detail, suggesting a mind that seeks to understand and piece together the significance of the environment and the person within it.
  • There is a tone of gentle curiosity in Vera’s internal questioning about Miss MacIntosh’s peculiarities: her missing eyebrows, the changing colors of her skin, her habitual wearing of hats and a whistle, and the careful way she avoids showing vulnerability ("she was no deep sleeper wrapped in the sloth of sensuality"). These questions are posed with "studied detachment," as if Vera is trying to maintain emotional distance while simultaneously probing deeply into the character of Miss MacIntosh.
  • Despite this detachment, Vera’s sensitivity is apparent in their awareness of the "quiver of wind upon the high cheek bones," the "thin, ascetic mouth," and the "wet and stained" pillow. They notice intimate, tender details such as the "one breast had been amputated," describing what remains as "no bigger than a baby’s fist," which evokes a sense of vulnerability and loss. This reveals an underlying empathy and a recognition of Miss MacIntosh’s suffering and resilience.
  • Vera reflects on themes of fate, morality, and human frailty. This moral reflection suggests that she is not only attentive to physical details but also to symbolic meanings and ethical considerations, which adds a philosophical dimension to their character.
  • Vera’s detailed description of Miss MacIntosh’s room (with its broken, bare, and patched furnishings, barred door, and scattered personal effects) reflects a broader sense of isolation, hardship, and complexity. She seems attuned to this atmosphere of loneliness and endurance, which may mirror their own inner state or the broader themes of the narrative.

Research: Resources

  • The Mental Hygiene Movement in the US was the organized treatment of mental illness which began in the early 19th century, a period when many states opened asylums to house individuals who at the time were understood to be suffering from insanity. Early advocates believed that asylums offered a controlled environment for the development of “cures” for various forms of mental illness, which at that time was thought to be a type of disease requiring expert medical treatment. Led by Dorothea Dix, proponents of asylums “confidently and aggressively asserted that properly organized institutions could cure almost every instance of the disease.” By the 1870s, however, the reformers’ vision had collapsed. Their notion that order and discipline could cure the “recently insane” proved inaccurate. Even individuals with less serious forms of mental illness did not respond as reformers had hoped, and for the more seriously ill, being sent to an asylum often ended up being a lifetime sentence. By the early 20th century, even as many state asylums changed their names to “hospitals” to underscore their ostensible commitment to “curing” mental illness, asylums had devolved into largely custodial institutions. A similar historical path was followed in Texas, which like many other Southern states began opening asylums just before or during the Civil War. Opened in 1861, the Texas State Lunatic Asylum was physically and programmatically modeled closely on the principles of the national movement for asylums. Renamed the Austin State Hospital in 1925, the institution suffered from overcrowding and underfunding through most of its existence.
  • Martin Luther was a German professor of theology, priest, author, composer, Augustinian monk, and a seminal figure in the Reformation. The presence of Martin Luther's bust symbolizes a form of religious austerity and reformist rigor that aligns with Miss MacIntosh’s personality and worldview. Luther, as a historic figure, represented a break from elaborate religious ornamentation and a return to a more straightforward, scripture-based faith. This parallels Miss MacIntosh's disdain for illusion and pretense and her emphasis on truth, fact, and common sense. Martin Luther's presence in the chapter highlights Miss MacIntosh's commitment to a severe, disciplined form of living and believing: rejecting fanciful visions and focusing on practical reality. It also contrasts with the more dreamlike and illusory elements surrounding other characters in the novel, reinforcing Miss MacIntosh as a grounded, clear-eyed moral guide.

  • There is a medical autoimmune disorder called Alopecia universalis where the individual is unable to grow hair anywhere on their body.
