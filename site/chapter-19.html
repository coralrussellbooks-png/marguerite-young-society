Chapter 19

Summary

“That was the false dawn, surely.” Vera wakes in Miss MacIntosh’s bed, sure that the night was a dream for which no one was responsible. Before she opens her eyes, she goes over things she never thought to question: the missing eyebrows, the face that changes color, the baseball cap in the mornings, the green ribbon in the evenings, the veils over the whaler’s hat, the black umbrella carried even indoors. Her mother calls herself “the horizontal person.” Miss MacIntosh is “perpendicular.” Then Vera looks. The bald head lies on the pillow next to hers, “cold as marble,” and the red wig is perched on the hat rack “like a rare tropical bird.” The room is bare and broken: a bust of Martin Luther, cherubim with no wings, a Bible chained to a table, false teeth in a glass, the deuce of clubs turned up, seaweed hanging on the barred door, the umbrella open on the floor. Then Vera observes “what I must surely have always known.” One breast has been amputated, and “what was left was no bigger than a baby’s.”

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a meticulous depiction of Miss MacIntosh’s largely bare and austere bedroom. The room contains very few ornaments: a bust of Martin Luther, a large seashell used as a doorstop, and crumbling cherubim heads around a blackened fireplace, covered by cheese-cloth drapery. The room is described as “plain,” “barely furnished,” and showing signs of decay which metaphorically reflects Miss MacIntosh’s own physical and perhaps spiritual condition. This setting evokes a sense of austerity, fragility, and impermanence, mirroring the character’s struggle with her own vulnerabilities.
  • Vera observes Miss MacIntosh’s physical traits with a mix of curiosity and tenderness. Notable is the absence of her eyebrows, the rapid changes in the color of her skin, her habitual wearing of old caps or a baseball cap, and the ever-present whistle around her neck. The description of her headgear and her old black umbrella carried like a shield even indoors suggests a defensive posture against the world or perhaps her own frailty. These details hint at a woman who is both physically fragile and psychologically guarded, living with an awareness of her own vulnerability.
  • A striking image is that of Miss MacIntosh’s bald head lying next to Vera’s pillow, “cold as marble” and “seldom touched by sunlight.” This evokes a sense of mortality and the harsh reality of illness or aging. Her red wig, compared to a “rare tropical bird,” is carefully placed within reach, symbolizing a constructed identity or a protective mask she wears in public. The presence of her worn clothing, her corset stained and draped over a chair, and other personal belongings scattered around the room reinforce the theme of physical deterioration and survival. Vera’s detailed observation of these items underscores a deep intimacy and respect for Miss MacIntosh’s resilience despite her hardships.
  • Among the personal items is a set of translucent playing cards with Dutch windmills, with the upturned card being the deuce of clubs, described as “the test of tears” and “the omen of death.” This is particularly poignant given Miss MacIntosh’s critical view of gambling as “the crafty Devil’s handiwork.” The card’s presence symbolizes fate, mortality, and perhaps the inevitability of death. It also highlights the duality in Miss MacIntosh’s beliefs between her Christian faith, her honesty, and her rejection of “double motions” or duplicity in the heart. This passage deepens the psychological complexity of her character and sets a somber, reflective tone.
  • The description of the room’s decay continues, with cracked walls, stained ceilings, barred doors with seaweed and barnacles, mud tracks, grains of sand, and salt gleamings. These vivid natural and physical details evoke a sense of isolation, imprisonment, and the passage of time, suggesting that Miss MacIntosh’s life is enclosed, weathered by external elements and internal struggles. The imagery of the umbrella, still opened on the floor as if for protection during the night, reinforces the motif of defense and vulnerability. Vera’s discovery that one of Miss MacIntosh’s breasts has been amputated is a stark and intimate revelation, symbolizing loss and suffering, yet also the survival and endurance of the woman.
  • “I observed what I must surely have always known, the fact that one breast had been amputated. What was left was no bigger than a baby’s.” The revelation is something Vera half knew all along.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “That was the false dawn, surely.” What is false about this morning?
  • Miss MacIntosh’s bedroom has “no dust-catchers,” yet Vera finds it crowded with saved things. Which details give the woman away?
  • The upturned card is “the deuce of clubs, that card which signifies the test of tears.” Why does Young place it within reach of her hand?

Characters

  • Catherine is “the horizontal person,” and Miss MacIntosh is “perpendicular.” What does each posture protect?
  • Vera wants to ask her questions “with studied detachment as if one were considering a land one had not visited and would never visit.” Why that distance?
  • “She was no turncoat,” and “she knew not… of these dualities in one heart.” What does the room say about that claim?

Connections

  • The red wig on the chair post in Chapter 18 is “perched like a rare tropical bird” here. How has Vera’s view of it changed overnight?
  • Chapter 17 heard her talk of the breast “already gone.” What changes when Vera sees it?
  • The seaweed on the door and the open umbrella look ahead to the sea. Where has the novel already told how Miss MacIntosh will die?

Reflection

  • “What I must surely have always known.” Can a person know something and not know it?
  • What do the things a person saves “for a rainy day” say about her?
  • Is there tenderness in looking closely at someone who is asleep, or is it a trespass?

Research: Characters

Miss MacIntosh

  • Bald head cold as marble, veined limbs, a missing breast (amputated), thin, ascetic mouth with “not one soft fuzz of hair upon the upper lip,” red wig resembling a tropical bird, gnarled hands.
  • Wears a baseball cap or old caps in the morning and a green ribbon in the evenings.
  • Always carries a whistle around her neck.
  • Wears a veil-tied hat outdoors, sometimes layered with heavy veiling.
  • Carries an old black umbrella indoors and outdoors, like a shield.
  • Sleeps lightly, alert to the slightest noise.
  • Has a strong Christian faith, critical of gambling, “the crafty Devil’s handiwork.”
  • Exhibits physical and emotional contradictions: shifting skin color, blushing for no apparent reason, and trying to resist external forces (“bucking against the broncho wind”).
  • The playing cards, especially the “deuce of clubs,” symbolize fate, death, or a trial by tears, despite her rejection of gambling.
  • Her patched clothing and worn items reflect hardship and resilience.

Vera Cartwheel

  • She describes her mind as “lucid as day” but simultaneously reveals a hesitation or reluctance to fully open her eyes and confront reality directly. This tension between clarity and avoidance suggests an internal conflict or a fragile psychological state. She seems caught between the realms of dream and waking, reality and imagination, as shown by her uncertainty about whether she is truly awake despite the vividness of the dawn light and surroundings.
  • She meticulously catalogues the objects and conditions in Miss MacIntosh’s bedroom, from the “crumbling heads of cherubim” to the “red wig, almost purple,” and the “jeweled water glass” holding false teeth. This detailed observation indicates a careful, almost forensic attention to detail, suggesting a mind that seeks to understand and piece together the significance of the environment and the person within it.
  • There is a tone of gentle curiosity in Vera’s internal questioning about Miss MacIntosh’s peculiarities: her missing eyebrows, the changing colors of her skin, her habitual wearing of hats and a whistle, and the careful way she avoids showing vulnerability (“she was no deep sleeper wrapped in the sloth of sensuality”). These questions are posed with “studied detachment,” as if Vera is trying to maintain emotional distance while simultaneously probing deeply into the character of Miss MacIntosh.
  • Despite this detachment, Vera’s sensitivity is apparent in her awareness of the “quiver of wind upon the high cheek bones,” the “thin, ascetic mouth,” and the “wet and stained” pillow. She notices intimate, tender details such as the “one breast had been amputated,” describing what remains as “no bigger than a baby’s,” which evokes a sense of vulnerability and loss. This reveals an underlying empathy and a recognition of Miss MacIntosh’s suffering and resilience.
  • Vera reflects on themes of fate, morality, and human frailty. This moral reflection suggests that she is not only attentive to physical details but also to symbolic meanings and ethical considerations, which adds a philosophical dimension to her character.
  • Vera’s detailed description of Miss MacIntosh’s room (with its broken, bare, and patched furnishings, barred door, and scattered personal effects) reflects a broader sense of isolation, hardship, and complexity. She seems attuned to this atmosphere of loneliness and endurance, which may mirror her own inner state or the broader themes of the narrative.

Research: Resources

  • “That was the false dawn, surely”: FitzGerald’s note to the Rubáiyát of Omar Khayyám (1859): “The False Dawn; Subhi Kázib, a transient light on the Horizon about an hour before the Subhi sádik, or True Dawn.” In Chapter 18, “the first light of the first dawn… seemed a falsehood.” See the Chapter 18 resources.
  • “the bust of Martin Luther”: Martin Luther (1483-1546) was a German professor of theology, priest, Augustinian monk, and hymn writer, and the central figure of the Reformation. His “A Mighty Fortress Is Our God” (c. 1529) is still sung in Protestant churches. Luther himself opposed the image-breakers of 1522, but the Reformation he began stripped many churches bare, as Miss MacIntosh strips her room. Miss MacIntosh quoted a line attributed to him at dawn in Chapter 18. See Augustine and Christian Theology, Music and Composers, and the Chapter 18 resources.
  • “the crumbling heads of cherubim”: In the Bible, cherubim are fierce guardians, set “at the east of the garden of Eden… to keep the way of the tree of life” (Genesis 3:24). The winged child heads of decoration are the putti of Renaissance art. These have “already lost their wings.” See Angels and Archangels and Angels, Devils, and Myth.
  • “the acanthus leaves in the molding”: Acanthus is one of the most common plant forms for foliage ornaments and decoration. The Roman architect Vitruvius tells that the sculptor Callimachus saw acanthus growing around a basket left on a young girl’s grave and carved it into the first Corinthian capital (On Architecture 4.1).
  • “my sleeping mother who called herself the horizontal person”: Catherine, who lives lying down, “never walked except in her imagination.” Miss MacIntosh is “perpendicular.” See Catherine Cartwheel.
  • “the cap of a sleeping car porter”: After the Civil War, the Pullman Company hired Black men, many of them formerly enslaved, as sleeping car porters. They worked long hours for low pay and tips, and passengers called every one of them “George,” after George Pullman. A. Philip Randolph organized the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters in 1925, and in 1937 it became the first Black union to win a contract with a major American company. See Society, Class, and Race.
    • “There was a Pullman towel”: Pullman towels were stamped with the company name, and travelers carried them home.
  • “jigsaw puzzles which always turned out to be the faces of presidents or the maps of foreign countries”: The first jigsaw puzzles were “dissected maps,” cut by the London mapmaker John Spilsbury in the 1760s to teach geography.
  • “playing tiddlywinks”: The game was patented in England by Joseph Fincher in 1888 as “Tiddledy-Winks” and became a craze in the 1890s.
  • “my strange bedfellow”: “Misery acquaints a man with strange bedfellows” (The Tempest 2.2). See Shakespeare.
  • “Her red wig, almost purple”: The wig Vera saw “upon the carven chair post” in Chapter 18. See Hair, Wig, and Baldness and the Chapter 18 resources.
  • “Her whale-boned, salmon-colored corset”: The “salmon-colored monstrosity” of Chapter 18, stiffened with whalebone. See Clothing, Hats, and Skirts and the Chapter 17 resources.
  • “Her silver-dollar watch”: This may be a dollar watch, like the Ingersoll pocket watch of 1896, which sold for one dollar and was the first watch most Americans could afford.
  • “a grey fedora”: The fedora takes its name from Victorien Sardou’s play Fédora (1882), in which Sarah Bernhardt wore a soft, creased, brimmed hat. It was a woman’s hat first, and then a man’s. See Clothing, Hats, and Skirts.
  • “the convict’s garb”: Striped prison uniforms began at New York’s Auburn Prison in the 1820s, so a prisoner could be seen anywhere. Most prisons gave them up in the early twentieth century.
  • “a pair of white fur ear muffs”: Chester Greenwood of Farmington, Maine, invented earmuffs at fifteen, in 1873, and patented them in 1877.
  • “her dim mackintosh”: The Scottish chemist Charles Macintosh patented his rubberized waterproof cloth in 1823, and the raincoat took his name. It is her name, too. See Miss MacIntosh.
  • “a world’s almanac”: The World Almanac of Chapter 18. See the Chapter 18 resources.
  • “a broken ship’s compass”: The compass rose of Chapter 18, now broken. See the Chapter 18 resources.
  • “a Moody’s hotel Bible. I noted that she had chained it to a table”: The Gideons placed their first hotel Bible in 1908, in Iron Mountain, Montana. In 1538, Thomas Cromwell ordered every church in England to set up “one book of the Bible of the largest volume in English” where parishioners could read it. It came to be called the Chained Bible, because it was fastened so it could not be taken away. Miss MacIntosh chains hers. See The Bible.
  • “an Atlantic City paperweight”: Atlantic City, New Jersey, built its Boardwalk in 1870 and became the great seaside souvenir town of the East.
  • “the lashless eyelids”: Alopecia universalis is the loss of all hair on the body, including the brows and lashes. See Hair, Wig, and Baldness and the Chapter 17 resources.
  • “the deuce of clubs, that card which signifies the test of tears”: Cartomancy reads fortunes in ordinary playing cards. Young gives the deuce of clubs its meaning herself. Clubs were trumps the night before. See Gambling and Playing Cards and the Chapter 18 resources.
  • “the Devil’s cards are marked”: The Puritans called playing cards “the Devil’s books.” The American historian of cards Mrs. John King Van Rensselaer titled her book The Devil’s Picture-Books (1890). See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “beauty is as beauty does, and beauty is more than skin deep”: Miss MacIntosh bends two proverbs. “Handsome is as handsome does” is in Goldsmith’s The Vicar of Wakefield (1766). “Beauty is but skin deep” goes back to Sir Thomas Overbury’s poem “A Wife” (1613): “All the carnall beautie of my wife, / Is but skinne-deepe.” See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “it’s an ill wind that blows nobody good”: John Heywood’s proverb (1546). Falstaff’s man Pistol says, “Not the ill wind which blows no man to good” (Henry IV, Part 2 5.3). See Shakespeare.
  • “a clump of blackened seaweed hanging on a nail on the door”: “much like some other wig.” Miss MacIntosh will be lost to the sea. See The Sea and Water and the Chapter 6 resources.
  • “one breast had been amputated”: The Halsted operation of Chapters 17 and 18. See Miss MacIntosh, Women, Gender, and Marriage, and the Chapter 17 resources.

Vocabulary

Acanthus
A Mediterranean plant whose deeply cut leaves are carved as ornament, especially on Corinthian columns.
Alabaster
A fine, smooth, white stone used for carving.
Ascetic
Severe and self-denying; giving up comfort and pleasure.
Barnacled
Crusted with barnacles, small shelled sea creatures that cling to rocks, pilings, and the hulls of ships.
Bloomers
Loose trousers gathered at the knee, worn by women.
Broncho
An old spelling of bronco, a wild or half-tamed horse of the American West.
Burnished
Polished until shining.
Chamber pot
A bowl kept under the bed for use as a toilet at night.
Cherubim
Winged angelic beings. In art they are often shown as chubby winged children. The singular is cherub.
Cornice
A decorative molding that runs along the top of a wall or building.
Corset
A close-fitting undergarment, stiffened with whalebone or steel and laced tight, worn to shape the waist.
Deuce
A playing card or die with two spots.
Dominoes
A game played with small oblong tiles marked with spots.
Fedora
A soft felt hat with a brim and a crease along the crown.
Gnarled
Knobbly and twisted, as old trees or hands.
Jersey
A soft knitted cloth.
Lucid
Clear; clear-headed.
Mackintosh
A raincoat of rubberized cloth, named for its inventor, Charles Macintosh.
Nocturnal
Of or happening in the night.
Oilskin
Cloth made waterproof with oil; a raincoat of it.
Opalescent
Shimmering with changing colors, like an opal.
Pedestal
The base on which a statue or bust stands.
Perpendicular
Upright; at a right angle to the horizon.
Plumage
A bird’s feathers.
Pristine
Fresh and pure, as if new.
Salt-and-pepper
Of cloth or hair: flecked with black and white.
Scrutiny
Close, searching examination.
Sealing wax
A wax that is melted to seal letters.
Serge
A sturdy twilled woolen cloth.
Shirtwaist
A woman’s blouse tailored like a man’s shirt.
Sloth
Laziness; one of the seven deadly sins.
Soapstone
A soft, smooth stone that feels like soap, carved into dishes and ornaments.
Tapestry
A heavy cloth woven with pictures, hung on walls.
Tarpaulin
A heavy waterproof cloth for covering things.
Threadbare
Worn so thin the threads show.
Ticking
A strong striped cotton cloth used to cover pillows and mattresses.
Tiddlywinks
A game of flicking small discs into a cup by pressing their edges with a larger disc.
Translucence
The quality of letting light pass through without being clear.
Tremulous
Trembling; quivering.
Turncoat
One who switches sides.
Tweed
A rough woolen cloth, often of mixed colors, first woven in Scotland.
Weathercock
A weathervane shaped like a rooster; also, a person who changes with every wind.
Whalebone
Baleen, the flexible plates from the mouths of certain whales, once used to stiffen corsets.
Whaler
A whaling ship or a man who hunts whales.
