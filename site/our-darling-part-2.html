Marguerite Young, Our Darling, Part 2: What It Reveals About Young and Her Work

← Part 1 · Marguerite Young, Our Darling: What It Reveals, Part by Part · Part 3 →

Source: Marguerite Young, Our Darling: Tributes and Essays, edited by Miriam Fuchs (Dalkey Archive Press, 1994). Part 2 runs from the close of John J. Stephan's recollection through the tributes by Peter Marchant, Rob Wynne, Michael and Betty Higgins, Caroline Sandoz Pifer, Gail Cohen, LaVerne Harrell Clark, and Lee Harrod, and into Jean Petrolle's critical essay. Only the material that illuminates Young and her work is kept below; fond praise without substance is left out. Quoted phrases from the book are marked with quotation marks.

Her teaching and her aesthetic

Peter Marchant's account of her Iowa workshop is the clearest window in this section onto how Young thought about writing. When he pruned his story to its bare action, she made him restore everything he had cut, telling him he had crossed out the best parts and left only surface. Her editorial instinct ran directly against realist tidiness: she told him the “logistical realities” did not matter and that a detail's factual impossibility was beside the point if it belonged to the character's inner life. Her standing advice was to stop hiding behind easy, realistic action, to “explore the phantasmagoric below the surface,” and to let the unconscious take over. Asked by his class why people write, students offered money, fame, and being unheard at home; Young's answer was that they all wrote “because you want to be loved.” Marchant notes that she gave her students more time and energy than she gave her own work, even while she was deep in Miss MacIntosh.

The method and philosophy of the novel

Several contributors try to name what Young was actually doing in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling. LaVerne Harrell Clark quotes a letter Young wrote for Clark's anthology Focus 101 in which Young described her own governing thesis: that there is no reality except, realistically, its unreality, a thesis carried by musicalizations, rhapsodies, and symbolizations that double back on themselves in an oceanic tumult. Clark points back to the early poem “That Chance” in Moderate Fable (1944), whose lines about “deviation from the law / In lonely items of the real” she reads as the same principle underlying the novel's prose. She cites the novelist William Goyen, who called the book's method “classification through amassment” and described it as a mammoth epic and a massive dream, and she reports that Kurt Vonnegut championed the book as the work of a genius.

Clark also records a telling gesture about the book's form. At the end of her Tucson visit Young handed Clark ten or more hotel keys she had carried off during her travels and asked her to mail them back. Clark reads this as Young demonstrating what she believed about prose: that there is no real end, no closure adequate to any ending. Young's own resistance to finishing bears this out. Clark notes the novel was “eighteen years in the making primarily because of its epic dimensions,” and Stephan's memory of the Rome manuscript, a “paperscape” the author would not surrender to an eager editor, shows the same reluctance to close.

Jean Petrolle's essay is the most sustained reading here. She frames the novel as a “magical metaphysic,” a world in which imagination, in the forms of dream, language, and fiction, does not merely describe reality but creates it. She draws on the novel's own voices: Mr. Spitzer's conviction that “Nothing lived except through imagination,” and his sense of standing at a hedge between overlapping worlds. Petrolle argues that the book's dense intertextuality, its kinship with Nabokov, Woolf, Proust, and Joyce, mirrors that multiplicity of worlds, and that Vera Cartwheel's dreaming and shifting speech enact the same idea, that words and dreams construct reality. She places the novel's excess, what the critic Steven Shaviro called its “exorbitance,” in the company of Proust's Recherche.

Poetry and prose as one continuum

Clark's tribute makes plain that Young did not treat the border between poetry and prose as fixed. Young was the first prose writer Clark scheduled for the University of Arizona Poetry Center, over a board that wanted only poets, and Clark's own argument for her was that Miss MacIntosh is in effect a long poem. At the reading itself Young insisted on reading from her poetry collections, Prismatic Ground and Moderate Fable, rather than from the novel, and tried to persuade Clark that the two were of a piece, that the line between poetry and prose in her work was not real.

Influences and kinship

Lee Harrod's recollection of the 1988 Trenton conference turns on Robert Browning. After her reading, what Young wanted to discuss was not her own characters but Browning, whose well-thumbed book, she said, had nurtured her and served as a source of many voices. The connection is pointed: Browning's dramatic monologues, many-voiced and often neglected by critics, stand behind the chorus of voices in her own work. Melville is another touchstone; Stephan recalls reading Moby-Dick aloud with her, and the whale surfaces again in Harrod's parting scene. Clark and Petrolle between them name a wider company for the novel, Joyce and Faulkner for its style, Proust and Nabokov and Woolf for its world.

The world of the novel: voices and music

Rob Wynne's account of producing Miss MacIntosh for WBAI radio catalogs the novel's cast and texture. Among its characters are Miss MacIntosh, Vera Cartwheel, Mr. Spitzer, Mr. Chandelier, the Opium Lady, Old Doc, Cousin Hannah, the Frog Musician, the Bible Salesman, the Arctic Explorer, the Hangman, and Esther Longtree, the carrier of life and death. For the broadcast Wynne assigned each major figure a sound or musical leitmotif, using Mozart's “Adagio for Glass Harmonica” for Miss MacIntosh herself, so that the ethereal, dreamy instrument announced her arrival. His premise was that the book's language is so poetic that concrete sounds could underline it, an outside reader's confirmation of how musical Young's prose is.

Reception, milieu, and later work

Fragments across these tributes sketch the novel's standing and the life around it. Contemporaries called it the distinctive novel of its decade and, in the pianist Wayne McEvilly's phrase, the Great American Novel, with excerpts appearing in well-known journals before the book was complete. Gail Cohen describes the salon Young kept at Pennyfeather's restaurant in Greenwich Village and mentions plans, never realized, to stage Miss MacIntosh as a play. Her teaching spanned the New School for Social Research, Fordham, and Seton Hall. And through the later chapters she is at work on the multivolume biography of Eugene V. Debs, having set aside her study of the Indiana poet of her childhood, James Whitcomb Riley.
