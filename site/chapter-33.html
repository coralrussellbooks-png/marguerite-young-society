To All My Darlings

Chapter 33

On Chapter 33 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 3, 2026

← Chapter 32 · Home · Chapter 34 →

Summary

A Boston newspaper once called Catherine “the late Mrs. Catherine Cartwheel,” widow of Jock, and Mr. Spitzer never told her. The same paper reported Peron seen on Benevolent Street, and Mr. Spitzer walked there the next evening. Catherine cannot reconstruct her wedding. Her bridegroom was a white camel, a church steeple, a key, the wind, perhaps Death while she slept. Mr. Spitzer cannot remember the wedding at all. He writes harp music to imitate the wind in a bridge’s cables, and day after day he asks the post office to trace a blank letter he mailed to himself years ago. He decides her dreaming is no farther from reality than the lives of those who punch time clocks: “They, too, could live only while they dreamed.” When she tells someone to open the door and a human-sized blackbird answers that it is already open, Mr. Spitzer gets up and opens it.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, it is revealed that a Boston newspaper mistakenly referred to Vera’s mother as deceased, calling her “the late Mrs. Catherine Cartwheel” despite her still being alive. Mr. Spitzer chooses not to correct this error, believing it “unimportant and possibly trivial,” as Catherine lives in a realm of “starry illusions” due to her opium addiction. This passage introduces the theme of blurred boundaries between life and death, truth and illusion, and the idea of allowing one’s illusions to persist rather than disturbing them.
  • Catherine sometimes thinks she was never married, or was married to strangers “as often as an erroneous clock struck,” and she “would swear, of course, that she had never married Mr. Spitzer.” Her preference for imagining multiple, shifting, and fantastical bridegrooms underscores her dissociation from reality and her embrace of poetic, surreal identities. This passage highlights her mercurial memory and the fluidity of her self-identity, as well as her rejection of conventional social realities in favor of dreamlike existences.
  • Catherine’s fragmented and vague recollection of her wedding, surrounded by “spirits in the air,” “glassy pebbles rolling on the shore,” “swords of fire, swords of ice,” and “wild, ethereal wedding music,” creates a surreal, almost otherworldly atmosphere. This passage amplifies the motif of memory as unreliable and the blending of reality with fantasy. It suggests that her perception of life events is filtered through a dreamlike, fractured consciousness.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s role as the observer and caretaker is critical. He reflects on Catherine’s addiction, her detachment from time, and her illusory world, concluding that she is “probably no more enslaved than others were by their predisposition toward illusion.” His philosophical musings on consciousness, unconsciousness, and the nature of life and death add depth to the narrative, portraying a compassionate understanding of human frailty and the necessity of illusions. His own struggle with reality, his brother’s ruin, and his meticulous attempts to grasp the elusive, mirror Catherine’s own fragmented existence.
  • A striking passage describes Catherine talking to invisible or imagined persons showing her living in a world where the usual distinctions between presence and absence, reality and fantasy, are dissolved. Mr. Spitzer’s rising to open the door, which the blackbird says is already open, demonstrates his gentle indulgence of her illusions and the poignancy of her condition.
  • References to the “dead lute-player in the garden,” “formal king penguins,” “melancholy seals,” and “tinkling duchesses” contribute to the surreal and symbolic texture of the chapter. These images reinforce the theme of a world where music, memory, and myth intermingle, and reality is perceived through a kaleidoscope of unusual and poetic visions.
  • Mr. Spitzer writes harp music as “the exact imitation of the wind playing through the strings and cables of a bridge,” and day after day he asks the post office to trace a dead letter he mailed to himself, “an envelope containing a piece of blank paper.” His music and his letter are both waiting for something that does not arrive.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • Mr. Spitzer leaves the error alone because “some day this Boston newspaper… would probably be right, having merely mistaken the time.” Is an early obituary a lie?
  • “She would often say that she was the kind of woman who could love only the man she had never married.” Why?
  • Mr. Spitzer mails himself “a piece of blank paper” and asks the post office to trace it. What is he waiting to receive?

Characters

  • Mr. Spitzer “could never remember this wedding,” expressing “his seething belligerence by his blank passivity.” Where else does his politeness hide anger?
  • He fears his unconsciousness “as he feared death.” Why does Catherine’s unconsciousness seem to him “a greater freedom”?
  • The paper reports Peron “walking along Benevolent Street.” Which brother is the newspaper wrong about?

Connections

  • In Chapter 32 Catherine married “a phantom.” Here “the bride had been a door. The bridegroom had been a key.” How do the two weddings differ?
  • Mr. Spitzer’s music imitates “the wind playing through the strings and cables of a bridge.” How does it echo the harp at Catherine’s wedding?
  • In Chapter 14 Catherine said “the coachman was her conscience.” Who drives her carriage here?

Reflection

  • Is anyone ever “quite awake”?
  • Would you correct a friend’s comforting mistake?
  • What would you mail to yourself?

Research: Characters

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • A once-famous beauty and socialite who has long been out of public view.
  • A complex figure, described as living in illusions and opium-induced dreams.
  • Her memories, especially of her wedding(s), are fragmented, poetic, and unreliable.
  • She is not told that a newspaper reported her death, and she sometimes thinks she was never married at all, though her husband was Jock, who “had disappeared when the mountains moved like waves.”
  • She interacts with imaginary figures and experiences reality as a blend of dream and fact.

Mr. Spitzer

  • A close, faithful friend of Catherine.
  • He understands her illusions and indulges them, choosing not to correct her perceptions.
  • He is practical but also philosophical, contrasting his own conscious precision with Catherine’s dreamlike state.
  • He is involved in artistic creation like writing harp music inspired by the wind and bridges.
  • Carries a burden of familial and financial complications (his brother’s debts).
  • He represents a grounding figure, yet he is also caught in Catherine’s world of illusion.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s thoughts include existential questions about reality, illusion, death, and time.
  • He goes to the post office day after day to trace a letter he mailed to himself, “an envelope containing a piece of blank paper.”
  • He believes her conscious states “were probably like the unconscious states of other people.”

Peron Spitzer

  • The Boston newspaper reports him “seen recently walking along Benevolent Street, peering into old doorways.”

Jock Cartwheel

  • “The fashionable dog fancier, the fabulous yachtsman, the great mountain-climber who had disappeared when the mountains moved like waves.”

The Black Coachman

  • At the wedding Catherine saw “his silver buttons, his white gloves,” and sometimes heard “the coachman’s cries to his horses” without seeing him.

Research: Themes

Memory and Illusion

See: Illusion and Reality · Memory and the Past

  • Catherine’s memories are described as “beautifully inaccurate,” blending fact with fantasy.
  • She often confuses time, place, and people, exemplifying the fragility of human memory.
  • Memories of her wedding are surreal involving images like bats, owls, icy landscapes, and ambiguous bridegrooms.
  • The narrative suggests memory as a form of dream or illusion that can persist beyond life.

Death and Existence

See: Death and Mortality

  • Catherine is believed dead by society but is alive in her own dream world.
  • Death is portrayed ambiguously, sometimes as a wedding to Death itself or as slipping into an eternal unconsciousness.
  • The boundary between life and death is blurred, raising questions about what it means truly to live or die.

Time and Reality

See: Illusion and Reality · Time

  • Time is fluid and unreliable in Catherine’s experience; she confuses noon and midnight, beginnings and endings.
  • Mr. Spitzer attempts to keep time (symbolized by his brother’s watch) as a measure of reality, but even he is caught between illusion and fact.
  • The novel questions the nature of reality itself and how much is defined by perception.

Music and Sound

See: Music, Sound, and Silence

  • Mr. Spitzer’s harp music inspired by wind and bridge cables symbolizes the blending of natural forces and artistic creation.
  • Music serves as a metaphor for the ephemeral, invisible forces shaping human experience and memory.

Isolation and Companionship

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Despite Catherine’s isolation in her illusions, Mr. Spitzer remains a constant companion, albeit one often rejected by her.
  • Their relationship is marked by a tension between reality and fantasy, presence and absence.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

The Wedding and Bridegroom(s)

See: Weddings and Funerals · The House

  • Catherine’s ambiguous wedding(s) symbolize transitions, possibly to death or between states of consciousness.
  • The multiple, shifting bridegrooms (white camel, church steeple, wind) reflect the fluidity of identity and reality.

The Black Coachman and Carriage

See: Horses and Carriages

  • Symbolize passage, possibly death or transition.
  • The carriage on the streets of Boston, after years of absence, signals a disruption of normal order.
  • In Chapter 14 Catherine said her body was the carriage and “the coachman was her conscience.”

The Blackbird and the Door

See: Birds · Keys, Doors, and Locks

  • She tells someone to open the door, and the voice answers that it is already open, though the door is closed. She is talking to a human-sized blackbird.
  • These images portray the overlap of the real and the imaginary.

Stars and Celestial Imagery

See: Moon and Stars

  • Stars and heavens often represent unreachable or eternal realms.
  • Catherine refuses to marry Mr. Spitzer “not while the stars shone in the glassy heavens,” linking love and fate to cosmic forces.

The Newspaper Mistake

  • The misreporting of Catherine’s death and Mr. Spitzer’s decision not to correct it sets the tone for the chapter’s exploration of truth versus illusion.

The Wedding Memories

See: Weddings and Funerals

  • Catherine’s hazy, fantastical memories of her wedding illustrate the blurred line between reality and fantasy.

Mr. Spitzer’s Harp Music

See: Music and Instruments

  • His artistic endeavor is inspired by natural sounds and symbolizes the attempt to impose order or meaning on chaos.

Catherine’s Conversations with Invisible Figures

  • Demonstrates her detachment from consensus reality.

Research: Resources

  • “she was still entertaining Chinese mandarins”: Weissenbach, the real opium lady, “dreamed about… mandarins and… a human-sized black bird standing in the hallway” (Marguerite Young, interview with Charles Ruas, WBAI radio, New York). See the blackbird at the end of the chapter. See Catherine Cartwheel.
  • “great balloon ascensionists who long ago had descended to earth, dragging their shrouds and cords”: Salomon Andrée set out by balloon for the North Pole in 1897 and vanished. The bodies and diaries of his party were found on White Island in 1930. See Exploration and the Poles.
  • “the mistake of a printer’s devil”: A printer’s devil was a young apprentice in a printing establishment who performed a number of tasks, such as mixing tubs of ink and fetching type. Notable writers including Ambrose Bierce, Benjamin Franklin, Walt Whitman, and Mark Twain served as printer’s devils in their youth.
  • “like the sobbing of the rain upon the river Styx”: The Styx is the river of the underworld, and the gods swore their oaths by it. See Greek Mythology.
  • “even her funeral, as he remembered his”: Mr. Spitzer remembers his own funeral because he buried his twin. Young said, “Grief was so great that he thought the wrong brother died” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See Twins and Doubles and Peron Spitzer.
  • “The bride had been a door. The bridegroom had been a key”: Christ says “I am the door” (John 10:9) and holds “the key of David” (Revelation 3:7), and the Church is “the bride, the Lamb’s wife” (Revelation 21:9). It means in the chapter that marriage and intimacy are like a doorway and key (symbols of potential connection and union) but this connection is fraught with uncertainty, mystery, and often disconnection or loss in the world of the novel. See The Bible.
  • “though she seemed to remember that he had been at her wedding”: “He that hath the bride is the bridegroom: but the friend of the bridegroom, which standeth and heareth him, rejoiceth greatly because of the bridegroom’s voice: this my joy therefore is fulfilled” (John 3:29). Mr. Spitzer is the friend at the wedding. The quotation encapsulates the emotional and spiritual tension experienced by characters who stand at the threshold of love and loss, celebration and mourning, presence and absence or joy mingled with a sense of something missing or awaited. See The Bible.
  • “swords of fire, swords of ice”: At the gate of Eden God placed “a flaming sword which turned every way” (Genesis 3:24). See Genesis.
  • “that she had been married to Death while she was sleeping”: Death and the Maiden, a theme of Renaissance painting and of Schubert’s song “Der Tod und das Mädchen” (1817), in which Death comes to a girl as a gentle lover: “Thou shalt sleep softly in my arms.” See Death and Mortality.
  • “Near-sighted bats flitting by on leathern wings”: “Save where the weak-ey’d bat, / With short shrill shriek flits by on leathern wing” (William Collins, “Ode to Evening,” 1747). Bats are not near-sighted. They see well and also hunt by echolocation. See Romantic Poets.
  • “Owls, great horned owls hooting above the lonely church steeples”: “From yonder ivy-mantled tow’r / The moping owl does to the moon complain” (Thomas Gray, “Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard,” 1751). Young named Gray’s Elegy as a model for Angel in the Forest (Marguerite Young, interview with Charles Ruas and Joan Schwartz, WBAI radio, New York). See Birds.
  • “the withered harp strings played upon by nothing but the fingers of the invisible wind”: “the withered harp strings played upon by nothing but the fingers of the invisible wind”: At Yaddo, Truman Capote occupied Katrina Trask’s tower, where a harp played when the door opened, and Young said, “I used that harp in my description of the Opium Lady” (Charles Ruas, Conversations with American Writers, Knopf, 1985). See Catherine Cartwheel and Music and Instruments.
    • A harp played by the wind is an Aeolian harp, named for Aeolus, keeper of the winds. In Coleridge’s “The Eolian Harp” (1795) it is “that simplest Lute, / Placed length-ways in the clasping casement.” See Romantic Poets.
  • “The sere stars shook like autumn leaves”: “My way of life / Is fall’n into the sear, the yellow leaf” (Macbeth 5.3). See Shakespeare.
  • “the dead lute-player in the garden, his pear-shaped body, his long, twisted neck”: The lute has a pear-shaped body and a pegbox bent back from its neck. “He was this man who was this musical instrument.” See Music and Instruments.
  • “a great many formal king penguins, a great many melancholy seals”: King penguins live only in the far south, and seals in both polar seas, so “the bipolar illusion” means of both poles. See Exploration and the Poles.
  • “these dream fugues, these polyphonic compositions”: A fugue is a composition in which voices enter one after another on the same theme, as in Bach’s The Art of Fugue. In psychiatry a fugue is also a flight from one’s own identity. See Music and Composers.
  • “differentiate between black and white, green and yellow and red”: Colors are the brain’s interpretation of different wavelengths of light. Human eyes have three kinds of color receptors, most sensitive to red, green, and blue, as Thomas Young proposed in 1802.
  • “Suppose there was no escort from here to there”: The damsel in distress romance trope since ancient times. It is a recurring narrative device in which one or more men must rescue a woman who has either been kidnapped or placed in general peril. Kinship, love, lust or a combination of those gives the male protagonist the motivation or compulsion to initiate the narrative. The female character herself may be competent, but still finds herself in this type of situation. The helplessness of these fictional females, according to some critics, is linked to views outside of fiction that women as a group need to be taken care of by men. The evolution of the trope throughout history has been described as: what changes through the decades isn’t the woman since she is always the weak victim in need of the male savior, it’s the attacker. The faces of the attacker in popular media are legion, whichever group best meets the collective fears of a culture is used (Wikipedia). Whether it’s Joachim Spitzer whom Catherine rejected or Cousin Hannah to remove men altogether as a savior figure, Catherine is not going to be saved by anyone.
  • “the tonnage of wind pressure against cables”: The Tacoma Narrows Bridge, “Galloping Gertie,” twisted in the wind and fell into Puget Sound on November 7, 1940.
  • “to inquire for a dead letter”: The Dead Letter Office received mail that could not be delivered. In Melville’s “Bartleby, the Scrivener” (1853), Bartleby had been a clerk there: “Dead letters! does it not sound like dead men?” See American Writers.
  • “Perhaps it was in the lunar Apennines”: The Apennines are a mountain chain in Italy. The lunar Apennines, the Montes Apenninus, were named by Johannes Hevelius in 1647 after the Italian range. See Astronomy.
  • “those who must punch time-clocks in great factories, those who must turn great wheels”: Willard Bundy patented the time clock in 1888. Chaplin’s Modern Times (1936) opens on workers at the time clock and swallows Charlie in the great wheels. Young said, “Charlie Chaplin has influenced every book I’ve ever written” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983). See Marguerite Young on Her Craft.
  • “never to join in the chorus of this dumb-show”: Dumb-show also known as a pantomime or mime, is a dramatic performance using gestures and body movements without words, often used to summarize, supplement, or comment on the main action of a play. Hamlet’s players perform a dumb-show before The Murder of Gonzago (3.2). See Shakespeare.
  • “none for this web of causality”: Causality is influenced by which one event, process, state, or object (a cause) contributes to the production of another event, process, state, or object (an effect) where the cause is partly responsible for the effect, and the effect is partly dependent on the cause. In general, a process has many causes, which are also said to be causal factors for it, and all lie in its past. An effect can in turn be a cause of, or causal factor for, many other effects, which all lie in its future. Some writers have held that causality is metaphysically prior to notions of time and space. Causality is an abstraction that indicates how the world progresses, so basic a concept that it is more apt as an explanation of other concepts of progression than as something to be explained by others more basic. The concept is like those of agency and efficacy. For this reason, a leap of intuition may be needed to grasp it. Accordingly, causality is implicit in the logic and structure of ordinary language. The topic of causality remains a staple in contemporary philosophy (Wikipedia). The chapter presents causality as unstable and enigmatic, entwined with the themes of illusion, memory, and subjective reality. The traditional, linear cause-effect relationship is replaced by a vision of interwoven, reversible, and often contradictory events, reflecting the novel’s broader exploration of reality as mutable and uncertain.
  • “none for the place, none for the person”: Doctors test whether a patient is “oriented to person, place, and time.” See Madness and Sanity.
  • “except that the door was closed”: Matthew 25 1-13 The Parable of the Ten Virgins. It symbolizes spiritual readiness, and those that are able to enter the wedding feast. This imagery is rooted in Jewish wedding customs, where the bridegroom’s arrival was a central event, and the bride and her attendants would go out to meet him. This parable underscores the tension between those who are prepared to meet reality and those who remain caught in illusions or delay facing their fate. It reflects the urgency of spiritual and moral preparedness amid the uncertainty of life and the inevitability of death. The waiting virgins symbolize individuals poised between hope and despair, life and death, dream and awakening. It highlights the novel’s exploration of human mortality, the fleeting nature of life, and the necessity of readiness for the “bridegroom” or the final reckoning, a theme echoed throughout Vera’s journey and the experiences of the other characters. The bridegroom came, “and the door was shut” (Matthew 25:10). See The Bible and Judaism.
  • “she was talking to a human-sized blackbird”: This was Weissenbach’s: “a human-sized black bird standing in the hallway” (Marguerite Young, interview with Charles Ruas, WBAI radio, New York). See the Chinese mandarins above. See Catherine Cartwheel.
