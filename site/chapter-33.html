Summary

A Boston newspaper once referred to Catherine as "the late Mrs. Catherine Cartwheel," and Mr. Spitzer never told her. The same paper reported Peron seen walking on Benevolent Street, and Mr. Spitzer walked there the next evening. Catherine cannot remember her wedding or her groom, and sometimes thinks she married Death in her sleep. Mr. Spitzer decides that her dreaming is no farther from reality than the routines of people who punch clocks. The chapter ends with him rising to open a door she has asked a human-sized blackbird to open.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, it is revealed that a Boston newspaper mistakenly referred to Vera’s mother as deceased, calling her “the late Mrs. Catherine Cartwheel” despite her still being alive. Mr. Spitzer chooses not to correct this error, believing it trivial and possibly benevolent, as Catherine lives in a realm of “starry illusions” due to her opium addiction. This passage introduces the theme of blurred boundaries between life and death, truth and illusion, and the idea of allowing one’s illusions to persist rather than disturbing them.
  • Catherine’s refusal to acknowledge her marriage (Jock Cartwheel) to Mr. Spitzer and her preference for imagining multiple, shifting, and fantastical bridegrooms underscores her dissociation from reality and her embrace of poetic, surreal identities. This passage highlights her mercurial memory and the fluidity of her self-identity, as well as her rejection of conventional social realities in favor of dreamlike existences.
  • Catherine’s fragmented and vague recollection of her wedding, surrounded by “spirits in the air,” “glassy pebbles rolling on the shore,” “swords of fire, swords of ice,” and “wild, ethereal wedding music,” creates a surreal, almost otherworldly atmosphere. This passage amplifies the motif of memory as unreliable and the blending of reality with fantasy. It suggests that her perception of life events is filtered through a dreamlike, fractured consciousness.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s role as the observer and caretaker is critical. He reflects on Catherine’s addiction, her detachment from time, and her illusory world, concluding that she is “probably no more enslaved than others were by their predisposition toward illusion.” His philosophical musings on consciousness, unconsciousness, and the nature of life and death add depth to the narrative, portraying a compassionate understanding of human frailty and the necessity of illusions. His own struggle with reality, his brother’s ruin, and his meticulous attempts to grasp the elusive mirror Catherine’s own fragmented existence.
  • A striking passage describes Catherine talking to invisible or imagined persons showing her living in a world where the usual distinctions between presence and absence, reality and fantasy, are dissolved. Mr. Spitzer’s action of opening the door when she asks, despite the door being closed and her talking to a blackbird, demonstrates his gentle indulgence of her illusions and the poignancy of her condition.
  • References to the “dead lute-player in the garden,” “formal king penguins,” “melancholy seals,” and “tinkling duchesses” contribute to the surreal and symbolic texture of the chapter. These images reinforce the theme of a world where music, memory, and myth intermingle, and reality is perceived through a kaleidoscope of unusual and poetic visions.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • A once-famous beauty and socialite who has long been out of public view.
  • A complex figure, described as living in illusions and opium-induced dreams.
  • Her memories, especially of her wedding(s), are fragmented, poetic, and unreliable.
  • She refuses to accept certain realities, such as her supposed death or marriage to Jock (Catherine’s late husband).
  • She interacts with imaginary figures and experiences reality as a blend of dream and fact.

Mr. Spitzer

  • A close, faithful friend of Catherine.
  • He understands her illusions and indulges them, choosing not to correct her perceptions.
  • He is practical but also philosophical, contrasting his own conscious precision with Catherine’s dreamlike state.
  • He is involved in artistic creation like writing harp music inspired by the wind and bridges.
  • Carries a burden of familial and financial complications (his brother’s debts).
  • He represents a grounding figure, yet he is also caught in Catherine’s world of illusion.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s thoughts include existential questions about reality, illusion, death, and time.

Research: Themes

Memory and Illusion

See: Illusion and Reality · Memory and the Past

  • Catherine’s memories are described as “beautifully inaccurate,” blending fact with fantasy.
  • She often confuses time, place, and people, exemplifying the fragility of human memory.
  • Memories of her wedding are surreal involving images like bats, owls, icy landscapes, and ambiguous bridegrooms.
  • The narrative suggests memory as a form of dream or illusion that can persist beyond life.

Death and Existence

See: Death and Mortality

  • Catherine is believed dead by society but is alive in her own dream world.
  • Death is portrayed ambiguously, sometimes as a wedding to Death itself or as slipping into an eternal unconsciousness.
  • The boundary between life and death is blurred, raising questions about what it means truly to live or die.

Time and Reality

See: Illusion and Reality · Time

  • Time is fluid and unreliable in Catherine’s experience; she confuses noon and midnight, beginnings and endings.
  • Mr. Spitzer attempts to keep time (symbolized by his watch) as a measure of reality, but even he is caught between illusion and fact.
  • The novel questions the nature of reality itself and how much is defined by perception.

Music and Sound

See: Music, Sound, and Silence

  • Mr. Spitzer’s harp music inspired by wind and bridge cables symbolizes the blending of natural forces and artistic creation.
  • Music serves as a metaphor for the ephemeral, invisible forces shaping human experience and memory.

Isolation and Companionship

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Despite Catherine’s isolation in her illusions, Mr. Spitzer remains a constant companion, albeit one often rejected by her.
  • Their relationship is marked by a tension between reality and fantasy, presence and absence.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

The Wedding and Bridegroom(s)

See: Weddings and Funerals · The House

  • Catherine’s ambiguous wedding(s) symbolize transitions, possibly to death or between states of consciousness.
  • The multiple, shifting bridegrooms (white camel, church steeple, wind) reflect the fluidity of identity and reality.

The Black Coachman and Carriage

See: Horses and Carriages

  • Symbolize passage, possibly death or transition.
  • The carriage on the streets of Boston, after years of absence, signals a disruption of normal order.
  • Catherine also said the Black Coachman was her conscious and the carriage was her body.

The Blackbird and the Door

See: Birds · Keys, Doors, and Locks

  • Catherine talks to a human-sized blackbird and instructs to open a door that is actually closed.
  • These images portray the overlap of the real and the imaginary.

Stars and Celestial Imagery

See: Moon and Stars

  • Stars and heavens often represent unreachable or eternal realms.
  • Catherine refuses to marry Mr. Spitzer “not while the stars shone in the glassy heavens,” linking love and fate to cosmic forces.

The Newspaper Mistake

  • The misreporting of Catherine’s death and Mr. Spitzer’s decision not to correct it sets the tone for the chapter’s exploration of truth vs. illusion.

The Wedding Memories

See: Weddings and Funerals

  • Catherine’s hazy, fantastical memories of her wedding illustrates the blurred line between reality and fantasy.

Mr. Spitzer’s Harp Music

See: Music and Instruments

  • His artistic endeavor is inspired by natural sounds and symbolizes the attempt to impose order or meaning on chaos.

Catherine’s Conversations with Invisible Figures

  • Demonstrates her detachment from consensus reality.

Research: Resources

  • The statement “the bride is the door, the bridegroom is the key” is a metaphor, often used in religious context, where the “bride” represents the Church or believers and the “bridegroom” represents Jesus Christ, with the “key” symbolizing his return or the ability to enter the kingdom of heaven. It means in the chapter that marriage and intimacy are like a doorway and key (symbols of potential connection and union) but this connection is fraught with uncertainty, mystery, and often disconnection or loss in the world of the novel.
  • Matthew 25 1-13 The Parable of the Ten Virgins. It symbolizes spiritual readiness, and those that are able to enter the wedding feast. This imagery is rooted in Jewish wedding customs, where the bridegroom’s arrival was a central event, and the bride and her attendants would go out to meet him. This parable underscores the tension between those who are prepared to meet reality and those who remain caught in illusions or delay facing their fate. It reflects the urgency of spiritual and moral preparedness amid the uncertainty of life and the inevitability of death. The waiting virgins symbolize individuals poised between hope and despair, life and death, dream and awakening. It highlights the novel’s exploration of human mortality, the fleeting nature of life, and the necessity of readiness for the “bridegroom” or the final reckoning, a theme echoed throughout Vera’s journey and the experiences of the other characters
  • John 3:29 “The bride belongs to the bridegroom. The friend who attends the bridegroom waits and listens for him, and is full of joy when he hears the bridegroom’s voice. That joy is mine, and it is not complete.” The quotation encapsulates the emotional and spiritual tension experienced by characters who stand at the threshold of love and loss, celebration and mourning, presence and absence or joy mingled with a sense of something missing or awaited.

  • A printer's devil was a young apprentice in a printing establishment who performed a number of tasks, such as mixing tubs of ink and fetching type. Notable writers including Ambrose Bierce, Benjamin Franklin, Walt Whitman, and Mark Twain served as printer's devils in their youth.
  • The damsel in distress romance trope since ancient times. It is a recurring narrative device in which one or more men must rescue a woman who has either been kidnapped or placed in general peril. Kinship, love, lust or a combination of those gives the male protagonist the motivation or compulsion to initiate the narrative. The female character herself may be competent, but still finds herself in this type of situation. The helplessness of these fictional females, according to some critics, is linked to views outside of fiction that women as a group need to be taken care of by men. The evolution of the trope throughout history has been described as: what changes through the decades isn’t the woman since she is always the weak victim in need of the male savior, it’s the attacker. The faces of the attacker in popular media are legion, whichever group best meets the collective fears of a culture is used. Whether it’s Joachim Spitzer whom Catherine rejected or Cousin Hannah to remove men altogether as a savior figure, Catherine is not going to be saved by anyone.

  • Causality is influenced by which one event, process, state, or object (a cause) contributes to the production of another event, process, state, or object (an effect) where the cause is partly responsible for the effect, and the effect is partly dependent on the cause. In general, a process has many causes, which are also said to be causal factors for it, and all lie in its past. An effect can in turn be a cause of, or causal factor for, many other effects, which all lie in its future. Some writers have held that causality is metaphysically prior to notions of time and space. Causality is an abstraction that indicates how the world progresses, so basic a concept that it is more apt as an explanation of other concepts of progression than as something to be explained by others more basic. The concept is like those of agency and efficacy. For this reason, a leap of intuition may be needed to grasp it. Accordingly, causality is implicit in the logic and structure of ordinary language. The topic of causality remains a staple in contemporary philosophy. The chapter presents causality as unstable and enigmatic, entwined with the themes of illusion, memory, and subjective reality. The traditional, linear cause-effect relationship is replaced by a vision of interwoven, reversible, and often contradictory events, reflecting the novel's broader exploration of reality as mutable and uncertain.
