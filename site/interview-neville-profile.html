Young's Interviews Analyzed

"Where the Landscape Moved Like Waves," a late-life profile by Susan Neville

← Previous · Young's Interviews Analyzed · Next →

A reading for the Miss MacIntosh project.

The source at a glance

This is not a primary interview but a memoir-profile from the Indiana writer Susan Neville's collection "Sailing the Inland Sea." Neville, teaching at Butler, tracked Young down late in her life through her publisher, Dalkey Archive, and visited her over several months after Young returned to Indianapolis to recover from an illness, first in her niece's house and then in a nursing home, up to her death in 1995. The piece braids three kinds of material: Young's talk in her eighties, quotations from her published essays and from a Miriam Fuchs interview, and Neville's own narrative and criticism. For the project it should be weighed differently from the transcripts, since Young's late words come filtered through a devoted biographer and Young herself is frail, "going in and out of dream," as Neville puts it. What it uniquely provides is threefold: the record of the book's reception, a body of late-life biography the interviews never reach, and an account of her final years and death.

The reception it records

Neville documents the arrival of Miss MacIntosh in 1965 as an event, a Life magazine spread and a Fifth Avenue parade with rented buses and, she writes, angels handing out champagne. The praise she gathers is a useful compilation of the record: William Goyen's New York Times review calling the book a masterwork built by amassment like Joyce or Melville; Jerzy Kosinski's "novel of massive achievement"; Mark Van Doren's judgment that her eloquence "has no parallel among the novelists of our time"; Anna Balakian ranking her among the most innovative novelists of the age; Vonnegut calling her "unquestionably a genius"; and Truman Capote's "she was the one who taught us all," which Neville explains through a teaching line, Young taught Paul Griffith, who taught Flannery O'Connor, who called Young her "dearest grand mere." Anne Tyler's tribute frames the essay: she read Miss MacIntosh to recover her nerve while writing "The Accidental Tourist" and gave her hero a copy of it as a companion. Anais Nin's championship, calling the novel the greatest modern novel and Young "as valuable as an explorer of unknown planets," is the thread that first led Neville to Young. Against all this Neville sets the counter-fact the interviews only hint at, that Young was largely written out of her own state's literary memory, and notes the Dalkey reissues, the festschrift "Marguerite, Our Darling," and, importantly, that the Debs biography was published posthumously only in a condensed and heavily edited form, with her intended manuscript surviving in one or two copies.

New late-life biography

Several biographical facts appear here for the first time. The wig that becomes Miss MacIntosh's is tied to a real Indianapolis mentor, a Miss Alexander, who wore a spare wig to keep her head warm, told Young she had "the true gift," and taught her to crash the Art Museum's poetry evenings by acting as if she belonged, "and it worked." Young wrote her first book, the poetry collection "Prismatic Ground," while teaching at Shortridge High School and living at 16th and Penn. She recalls Booth Tarkington's sister, nicknamed "Hautey" for her talk of Terre Haute, who believed she was a resurrection of Cleopatra. She remembers Vonnegut in her Shortridge homeroom sitting with "a bottle of fireflies" for an experiment, later calling to ask whether he could tell Iowa he had studied with her, and she predicts he will be remembered as "a cult figure," a man who "wanted to be popular," which she did not. On marriage she is sharp: had she married she "would have married the worst man," since she sensed she had a large genius "that a man would always be jealous" of, and she says she should have written poems all along because they tell "with lightning swiftness" what she means. She reports lovers and, once, two proposals a day, and names as a crucial mentor a man she calls Freud's physician, who told her she was "a writer of the outside way" and to avoid "the middle way." On happiness she is flat: she "wouldn't give a nickel" for it, since life comes from "absorption in one's work." Her doctor, she says, sent her home to Indianapolis because staying in New York would kill her.

The sharpest statements of her creed

Because Neville draws on Young's essays and the Fuchs interview, the piece holds some of the most compact statements of Young's thought in the whole set. Genre dissolves: there is no real difference between fiction and nonfiction because "everything is fiction," even God, and in thirty or forty thousand years it will all be gone, not even a memory of Shakespeare. The central question of Miss MacIntosh, she says, is not whether you can love someone at first sight but "at second sight," once you know they are "other than you seem," which she calls a matter of illusion and "the depth of the psyche." Maturity is going on without certainty; all her writing concerns the recognition that there is no single reality, and yet one keeps "walking towards utopia," on "a bridge which might end before you reach the other side." She ties this directly to politics: knowing when a dream is real and when it is only a dream is the line "between being sane and being crazy," and whoever understands illusion "doesn't blindly follow any leader." Her Midwest is not provincial but cosmic, "a superficial, mediocre view of it" being the only shallow thing, and in her essay "The Midwest of Everywhere" she declares herself in love "with whatever is eccentric, devious, strange." Neville reads her as the recording angel of a Heraclitean flux nearer to modern physics than to most fiction, and Young grounds it in William James, whose stream of consciousness is "a stream and not a track," so that reason's straight pathways are themselves an illusion. On her method she is terse: "I'm a mirror," and in the realm of the surreal "the illogical is logic." She is cool, too, toward Nin and Henry Miller, whom she judges "ultimately cold" sensualists whose sensuality masked an innocence she avoided through a recognition of evil.

Late work and the end

Even bedridden in her eighties, Young was writing poetry at "a poem a day," a collection of dramatic monologues "modeled on Browning's monologues," beginning lines like "I, Elizabeth Hardwicke," which shows the Browning she names as her first master still governing her at the end. Neville records the astonishing Eartha Kitt poem, built on a story Young insists is true, that Kitt was born, tied off with silk and buried under leaves, and dug alive from the earth by a grandmother who heard her voice, an image that rhymes with the buried and dream children throughout Young's fiction. The old New Harmony material returns too: told that Jane Owen had the angel's stone footprint removed as unreal, Young objects that "the angel is real," since "angels are the dreams of men," and "as long as you're still dreaming, you're alive." Neville's closing scene is a July visit to the nursing home during a freak summer storm, Young talking on through the flooding and failing lights about Chicago in the thirties, about Jung and William James and Kay Boyle, about lovers male and female and a lover she gave to another poet on the condition he visit her on Tuesdays, while hail piled in the gutters until the July landscape looked like ice. She died soon after and was cremated, and Neville reports a funeral of eleven people, with two life-sized dolls seated in the front row, the size of the woman who had loved them.

Set against the interviews

As a secondary, retrospective source, this profile mostly confirms and frames what the transcripts establish, while adding three things they cannot. It supplies the reception and reputation record, the 1965 acclaim and the later neglect, which the interviews only gesture at from inside. It gives the late biography and the death, closing the arc the earlier sources leave open. And it sharpens several recurring threads into their clearest form: William James as "a stream and not a track," Browning's monologues governing her poetry from first to last, the real wig-wearing woman behind Miss MacIntosh now named as Miss Alexander, and above all the political point latent in every interview, that understanding illusion is what keeps a person from blindly following a leader. It also confirms the Anais Nin relationship that the Ruas career interview names from Young's side, here shown from Nin's. Two cautions for the compilation: Neville gives the writing span as twenty years, another figure to set beside the "17 years and 8 months" and "eighteen years" of the interviews, and she records that the published Debs biography is a condensed, heavily edited text rather than the book Young intended, which matters for anyone who would cite it as her work.
