To All My Darlings

Chapter 31

On Chapter 31 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 30, 2026

← Chapter 30 · Home · Chapter 32 →

Summary

Catherine and Mr. Spitzer admire Cousin Hannah, whose great deeds made their own lives seem unfulfilled. Her secret grief is circled but never named: the mirrors of her house draped in black, her failed battle to rescue Catherine from opium, her indifference to both Spitzer brothers. She sent not one flower to Peron’s grave and ignored Joachim “as if it were he who had died.” Mr. Spitzer remembers his long faithfulness to her, knocking at a door that never opened. He struggles to write her epitaph, drafting and discarding version after version.

Key passages

From the research

  • Mr. Spitzer indulges Catherine’s moods as long as they are not of reality, believing reality would kill her as it had killed her canary, which she imagined flying over the waves. This suggests a delicate balance between fantasy and harsh reality in her life.
  • Their mutual admiration for Peron (who gambled his life away) and Cousin Hannah, an adventurer whose own life made their adventures seem unfulfilled, hints at the complex relationships and ideals binding these characters.
  • Cousin Hannah is described as direct, fierce, and adventurously restless, having fought losing battles with shadows and enduring harsh physical and emotional challenges, including suffrage campaigns and desert treks. She embodies “forlorn romance” and is portrayed as a great captain metaphorically armored by suffrage and armed with swords of ice and fire.
  • Her refusal to marry and the whispered secret of her life shadowed by grief add mystery and depth to her character. Her life is marked by dramatic adventures and a stoic endurance of suffering.
  • Despite her strength and battles, she could not “rescue herself,” and her death is framed as a “domestic war,” contrasting with the grand battles she fought. This passage suggests a tragic dimension to her life and death.
  • She sought to “carry” Vera’s mother away from opium dreams, fighting her losing battle with shadows, showing her as a protective but ultimately tragic figure.
  • She ignored Joachim, the lawyer, after Peron’s death, refusing any recognition or mourning, which underscores her complex relations and possibly her disdain for what she considered weak or insubstantial men. Also Mr. Spitzer may not be who he seems to be.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s persistent but unanswered visits to Cousin Hannah’s empty house symbolize a futile effort to maintain connection and control over the past. His rituals of knocking, leaving calling cards, and bringing important papers contrast with the silence and absence inside, emphasizing themes of loss and the elusiveness of presence.
  • Cousin Hannah’s adventures in deserts and mountains serve as metaphors for her restless spirit and unattainable quests, with imagery of desert storms, Islamic kingdoms, and un-climbable alpine peaks creating a mythical aura around her.
  • The desert beauty’s mirror she brought is a symbol of the transience of human life, reinforcing the chapter’s meditation on mortality.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s efforts to compose an epitaph for Cousin Hannah, his hesitation and inability to decide on words reflect his sorrow and the difficulty of capturing a complex life and character in simple terms. His proposed epitaphs evoke her warrior spirit, her suffering, and her ultimate solitude and death at home rather than on a battlefield.
  • The meditation on death and memory adds philosophical depth to the narrative.
  • His own uncertain status between life and death, as suggested by bureaucratic errors listing him dead, parallels the ambiguous boundary between presence and absence that permeates this chapter.
  • Cousin Hannah’s scorn for Vera’s mother’s love for a “shadow,” a fleeting and unsubstantial man, contrasts with Catherine’s belief that death is necessary to her love. This conflict highlights different understandings of love, reality, and illusion.
  • Cousin Hannah’s rejection of the external man and her suspicion of phantoms as rivals underscore the chapter’s theme of the elusive and often illusory nature of relationships and human connections.
  • The repeated imagery of knocking on doors, peering through keyholes, and the sensation of being watched by hidden eyes evokes a haunting atmosphere, symbolizing the barriers between people, the mysteries of identity, and the unseen presences that linger in memory and imagination.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • Mr. Spitzer indulges Catherine’s moods “so long as they were not of reality.” Why would reality kill her?
  • Why does Mr. Spitzer knock only when “he knew she was absent”?
  • “Here lies one who… could not rescue herself.” Which of his epitaphs is truest?

Characters

  • Cousin Hannah judged both brothers: “It was Hobson’s choice… a choice between death and death.” Do you agree?
  • Catherine asks “when had she ever seen a man on earth?” What does she love instead?
  • Mr. Spitzer chooses “the charity of his inaccuracy,” a headstone with no dates. Why?

Connections

  • The canary “she thought was flying over the waves” first appeared in Chapter 2. What else in this house dies without Catherine knowing?
  • A clerk listed Mr. Spitzer “among the dead” in Chapter 25, and here it happens again. What does the record know that he doesn’t?
  • Cousin Hannah’s secret grief is “a body covered by the snow.” How does it mirror Catherine’s lost husband in the Alps (Chapter 30)?

Reflection

  • Is “each one who lives… shrunken by another’s death”?
  • Can you mourn someone who ignored you?
  • What would your epitaph leave out?

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • A legal figure who serves as a connection between the living and the dead.
  • Indulges Catherine’s moods but is deeply affected by the passing of Cousin Hannah.
  • Exhibits profound grief, ritualistically visiting Cousin Hannah’s empty house and attempting to maintain legal and emotional ties.
  • His character embodies persistence, melancholy, and a struggle to reconcile presence and absence.
  • Despite Cousin Hannah’s death and absence, Mr. Spitzer continues to visit her empty mansion, knocking on doors and slipping calling cards through mail slots.
  • His visits symbolize the persistence of memory and the difficulty of accepting loss.
  • He contemplates an epitaph and memorial for Hannah, reflecting on her life as a warrior and adventurer.
  • He was “the only mourner” at her grave, “for the simple reason that most people thought she had died years before she had died.”

Catherine Cartwheel

  • A dreamer, living between reality and opium dreams.
  • Holds a love for a “shadow,” a dead or unreal man, rather than a concrete person.
  • Her relationship with reality is tenuous; she is entwined with mystical and illusory elements.
  • She and Mr. Spitzer share mutual admiration for Cousin Hannah and Peron.
  • Catherine’s love is for an elusive, intangible presence rather than a real man.
  • The metaphor of man as a shadow driven by the wind highlights themes of impermanence and spiritual longing.

Cousin Hannah

  • A formidable, adventurous woman, a “great captain” with a suffrage warrior’s spirit.
  • Known for her directness, fiery temper, and refusal to accept illusions or evasion.
  • Lived a nomadic life filled with symbolic and literal journeys across deserts, mountains, and forbidden cities.
  • She opposed Catherine’s love for the shadow, believing in facing harsh realities.
  • After death, she remains a spectral presence, her memory both revered and shrouded in secrecy.
  • Despite her public life and campaigns, she kept a secret of sorrow, which others hesitate to speak of.
  • Cousin Hannah disapproves of Catherine’s dreams and love for the “shadow.” Their conflict is ideological: directness and confrontation versus evasion and mystical longing.
  • She is portrayed as a mythic figure battling external foes and internal demons.
  • Her life and death symbolize the struggle between illusion and reality, love and loss.
  • The chapter describes her as a figure who fought battles greater than those recorded, with imagery of deserts, storms, and crusades.
  • She sent not one flower to Peron’s grave, neither a horseshoe of roses nor “two dice cubes in a lucky throw,” and ignored Joachim “as if it were he who had died.”
  • She called both brothers “equally insecure, as unsubstantial as flame and water.”

Research: Symbols and Imagery

The Empty Mansion

See: The House

  • The empty house, the broken lanterns, the snow-covered garden, and the silent rooms evoke a ghostly atmosphere of abandonment and mourning.
  • The Empty Mansion represents absence, memory, and the passage of time. The desolation and decay of the mansion mirror Cousin Hannah’s death and the fading of her presence.

The Desert and the Alps

See: Mountains · Exploration and the Poles

  • The Desert and the Alps symbolize harsh trials, isolation, and spiritual quests. Cousin Hannah’s travels through these landscapes symbolize her restless spirit and adventurous life.

Eyes and the Keyhole

See: Eyes and Blindness

  • “…he would be overwhelmed by the increasing certainty that there was another great, rounded eye staring at him…” The imagery of eyes symbolizes observation, mystery, and the interplay between seen and unseen forces.

Flame and Water

See: Illusion and Reality

  • “…the flame evaporates the water. The water extinguishes the flame.” Symbolizes the mutual destruction or futility of opposing forces, reflecting on the men in Catherine’s life.

The Butterfly Card

See: Butterflies and Moths

  • The “great eye” of the planet, the “butterfly” symbol of resurrection, and references to historical and exotic locations enhance the dreamlike and timeless atmosphere.

Storms, Deserts, Snow, and Stars

  • Storms, deserts, snow, castles, veils, horses, swords, and stars.
  • These images underscore the mystical, elusive, and epic qualities of the characters’ inner lives.

The Great Water Eye

See: Moon and Stars

  • The “Great Water Eye” of the planet is a recurring symbolic image that links the characters’ personal dramas to the vastness of the universe.

  • “saying that it should reveal the transience of human life”: The transience of human life is a philosophical concept emphasizing the impermanence and fleeting nature of existence, encourages mindfulness, acceptance of change, and a deeper appreciation for the present moment. Young portrays human life as a fragile, temporary phenomenon woven into the vast, ever-changing tapestry of nature and time. The imagery of natural cycles, the recognition of memory’s limits, and the contemplation of death’s relationship to life all serve to illustrate the profound transience of human existence in the novel.
  • “bearded human lions in their dens”: Daniel in the lions’ den (Daniel 6), and Scott’s “to beard the lion in his den” (Marmion, 1808). See The Bible.
  • “in love with no man on earth but with a fleeting shadow, the mere shadow of a man”: Shadows in scripture and Shakespeare. See The Bible, Ecclesiastes, The Book of Job, Shakespeare, and Love.
    • “Man is like to vanity: his days are as a shadow that passeth away” (Psalm 144:4). In the broader context of the chapter and the novel, this phrase highlights the idea that human life is insubstantial and temporary, underscoring the urgency to live fully and the acceptance of life’s inevitable end. It also connects with the novel’s exploration of the soul’s journey, memory, and the inescapable passage of time.
    • Ecclesiastes 6:12 speaks of “all the days of his vain life which he spendeth as a shadow.” It serves as a poignant reminder of the human condition’s vulnerability and the pervasive sense of impermanence that haunts the characters throughout the book.
    • “He cometh forth like a flower, and is cut down: he fleeth also as a shadow, and continueth not” (Job 14:2). This quote poignantly expresses the fleetingness of life and the inevitability of death, reminding readers of the fragile and transient nature of human existence that is central to the novel’s meditations on time, memory, and mortality.
    • “For the law having a shadow of good things to come, and not the very image of the things” (Hebrews 10:1). The phrase encapsulates a sense of hopeful anticipation and the recognition that earthly institutions, including the law, are provisional and imperfect, pointing toward a more complete reality or justice that remains to be realized.
    • “Which are a shadow of things to come; but the body is of Christ” (Colossians 2:17). The phrase encapsulates the novel’s meditation on the transient versus the eternal, urging a perspective that looks beyond the immediate shadows of life to the enduring spiritual substance found in Christ.
    • “Life’s but a walking shadow” is a line from William Shakespeare’s Macbeth (5.5). It signifies the novel’s existential reflection on the insubstantial, fleeting, and often bewildering nature of human life, where reality is elusive and individual existence may be more illusory than concrete.
  • “girding her loins with suffrage like that iron girdle worn by a great Crusader’s wife”: “Gird up now thy loins like a man” (Job 38:3). The chastity belt of Crusaders’ wives is largely a legend of later centuries. See The Book of Job and Women’s Suffrage.
  • “not one horseshoe of the red and white roses… two dice cubes in a lucky throw, seven and eleven”: Floral horseshoes and dice were set on gamblers’ graves. Seven and eleven are winning throws in craps. See Gambling and Playing Cards and Peron Spitzer.
  • “each one who lives is shrunken by another’s death”: “Any man’s death diminishes me, because I am involved in mankind” (John Donne, Devotions upon Emergent Occasions, Meditation 17, 1624).
  • “forbidden cities of brides, those who were held in thralldom by a cruel bridegroom”: It recalls Perrault’s “Bluebeard” (1697), whose dead brides hang in the forbidden room. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “those who would never be awakened by the kiss of any living man or love”: The Sleeping Beauty of Chapter 29. See the Chapter 29 resources.
  • “the old St. Bernard”: The monks of the Great St. Bernard Hospice bred their dogs to find travelers lost in Alpine snow. Barry (1800-1814) is said to have saved forty lives. The brandy cask was the invention of the painter Edwin Landseer (1820). See Dogs.
  • “the latticed shutters in those mysterious Oriental cities where the secret women look out”: The mashrabiya, a carved wooden lattice that lets women see without being seen.
  • “Or he might imagine that his veiled wife was beautiful”: Veiling and wearing a veil of clothing or hanging cloth has a long history in many societies across the world. It is intended to cover some part of the head or face, or an object of some significance. The practice has been prominent in different forms in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. The practice of veiling is especially associated with women and sacred objects, though in some cultures, it is men, rather than women, who are expected to wear a veil. Besides its enduring religious significance, veiling continues to play a role in some modern secular contexts, such as wedding customs. One view is that as a religious item, it is intended to honor a person, object or space. The actual sociocultural, psychological, and sociosexual functions of veils have not been studied extensively but most likely include the maintenance of social distance and the communication of social status and cultural identity. A veil also has symbolic interpretations, as something partially concealing, disguising, or obscuring. Young uses veiling as a rich symbol to explore themes of concealment versus revelation, the instability of identity, societal constraints especially on women, and the metaphysical boundaries between life, death, and perception. The veil is both protective and imprisoning, a literal and figurative barrier that characters both struggle with and embody. This motif deepens the novel’s meditation on illusion and reality, the known and unknown, and the complex interplay between appearance and essence.
  • “draped by veils of black chiffon in memory of some earlier death”: Mirrors were covered in houses of mourning, in Victorian custom and in the Jewish shiva. See Grief, Loss, and Mourning and Judaism.
  • “for what were earthly riches, great castles and lonely towers”: Luke 12:16-21, the Parable of the Rich Fool, shows how wealth can be a distraction from the true purpose of life. Riches distract from the emptiness or are a source of fleeting pleasure, ultimately failing to provide lasting fulfillment or true happiness. The parable is thematically woven into the narrative as a meditation on mortality, the vanity of materialism, and the necessity of grounding one’s life in deeper truths beyond the ephemeral world. Mr. Spitzer’s reflections echo the parable’s warning against greed and misplaced security, emphasizing humility, realism, and moral clarity in a transient world. See The Bible.
  • “Often he had seen the lunar rainbow”: A Lunar rainbow or Moonbow is a rainbow produced by moonlight rather than direct sunlight. See the Chapter 29 resources.
  • “his calling card embossed with the butterfly which was his sign of resurrection”: Mr. Spitzer’s calling card. See Butterflies and Moths and the Chapter 26 resources.
  • “the old State Street Bank and Trust”: Boston’s State Street Bank, founded in 1792 as the Union Bank.
  • “Here lies that great Crusader who rode against the Saracens”: The inclusion of women on both sides of the Crusades is a deliberate narrative choice that enriches the novel’s exploration of duality, feminine agency, internal conflict, mythic symbolism, and historical ambiguity. It underscores the novel’s themes of fragmented identity and the search for meaning in a world of contradictions.
    • Women played significant roles in the Crusades, either by leading armies, influencing political decisions, or promoting the cause: Eleanor of Aquitaine, Matilda of Tuscany, Melisende, Queen of Jerusalem, Margaret of Provence, Queen consort of France, Sibyl, Queen of Jerusalem, and Margaret of Beverly, who fought in the defense of Jerusalem against Saladin (1187). Ida of Austria led her own army in the Crusade of 1101, and the legendary Florine of Burgundy was said to have died fighting beside Sweyn of Denmark in 1097.
    • Women resisted during the Crusades: Shajar al-Durr, who ruled Egypt and organized its defense against the Seventh Crusade (1249-50), Anna Comnena, and the women of the Baltic Crusades. Ismat ad-Din Khatun, regent of Damascus, negotiated the end of the Crusader siege of Banias in 1174, and William of Tyre credited her with “courage beyond that of most women” (A History of Deeds Done Beyond the Sea, 20.31). At Shaizar, in Syria, when Assassins stormed the citadel in 1114, during the Crusade years though not by Crusaders, Usama ibn Munqidh’s mother armed the women, and an old servant, Funun, “covered herself with her veil, took up a sword and went into battle” (Usama ibn Munqidh, The Book of Contemplation, trans. Paul M. Cobb, 2008). Khawla bint al-Azwar, the most famous Arab woman warrior, fought the Byzantines at Yarmouk (636), five centuries before the Crusades.
  • “She raided the Etruscan towns”: Etruscan towns were a collection of independent city-states in central Italy, united by a shared culture and language. They thrived in the regions of Tuscany, Umbria, and Northern Lazio between the 7th and 2nd centuries BC. Important Etruscan cities include Tarquinia, Vulci, Populonia, Cerveteri, Volterra, and Chiusi. Many of these cities still have archaeological sites, necropolises, and museums that showcase their Etruscan heritage.
  • “Here lies one who searched all her life for her dead love, a body covered by the snow”: Young told Studs Terkel that Cousin Hannah came partly from an Alpine story in which two bloomer girls shed their skirts at a summit, and the younger, going back for hers, was lost: “the lady lost her life for a skirt.” She also drew on Margaret Fuller, who drowned off Fire Island in 1850 within reach of a sandbar (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See the Chapter 30 resources. See Cousin Hannah and Women’s Suffrage.
  • “Here lies one who shared our common lot of mortal frailty”: Heidegger’s “being-towards-death,” from Being and Time (1927), is a fundamental aspect of human existence, highlighting the finite nature of our being and the importance of confronting mortality to live authentically. Humans are in the world and constantly engaging and interpreting it. Humans are unique in that they are aware of their own mortality and this shapes their existence. Heidegger distinguishes between authentic and inauthentic ways of living. Authentic living involves confronting the inevitability of death and making choices that are meaningful to oneself, while inauthentic living involves being swept away by societal norms and expectations. One must confront mortality in order to live more fully and purposefully, making the most of the time one has. Young articulates a richly poetic and existential meditation on death that embodies Heidegger’s philosophy of “being-towards-death.” The chapter’s awareness of mortality disrupts ordinary existence, reveals the fragility and finitude of life, and through this confrontation, opens the possibility for authentic being. This chapter captures the essence of how the anticipation of death shapes human existence, infusing it with urgency, solitude, and profound insight, all central to Heidegger’s existential analytic. See Existentialism.
  • “She was not fallen in a foreign field”: “If I should die, think only this of me: / That there’s some corner of a foreign field / That is for ever England” (Rupert Brooke, “The Soldier,” 1914).
  • “She has gone over to the Islamites”: In the Islamic tradition burial is a sacred practice. Cremation is strictly forbidden. The body is buried as soon as possible after death, ideally within 24 hours, in a simple shroud, facing Mecca and without a coffin. There are no elaborate markers; simple flat markers are used instead. There is a ritual washing and prayer, and mourners each place three handfuls of soil. Post-mortem examinations are avoided. There is a 40 day mourning period.
  • “The rose of Sharon is growing in a desert place”: “I am the rose of Sharon” (Song of Solomon 2:1), and “the desert shall… blossom as the rose” (Isaiah 35:1). See The Bible and the Chapter 28 resources.
  • “Death snatched away her beautiful bride”: Young’s other source for Cousin Hannah was a story told by Charles Studin, a founder of the NAACP, about a suffragist “battle-axe” who fought men all her life, “one of the original bloomer girls,” yet for forty years brought home sealed trunks that proved at her death to hold “a complete bridal trousseau” each. “She had a dream too” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). In another telling the lawyer found “fifty wedding gowns,” and Young added that she “had read the histories of mountain climbers, of suffragette captains, of travelers to the Middle East” (Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs, “A Conversation with Marguerite Young,” Review of Contemporary Fiction 23, no. 1, Spring 2003 (1989 original)). See Cousin Hannah and Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “he had listed Mr. Spitzer’s name among the dead”: The clerk of Chapter 25. See the Chapter 25 resources.
  • “for Joachim, though he pretended to be so true, was weak as water”: “Unstable as water, thou shalt not excel” (Genesis 49:4). See Genesis.
  • “It was six of one and half a dozen of the other”: The phrase is in print in Captain Marryat’s The Pirate (1836).
  • “It was Hobson’s choice”: Hobson’s choice is a free choice in which only one thing is actually offered and is used to mean an illusion of choice. It serves as both a moral teaching and a reflection on the existential conditions faced by the characters, especially Mr. Spitzer and the brothers Cousin Hannah judged. It highlights the tension between illusion and reality, choice and necessity, and the acceptance of life’s inherent difficulties as the foundation for integrity and survival. Thomas Hobson (d. 1631), a Cambridge stable keeper, made each customer take the horse nearest the door.
  • “worth a candle’s flame in the headlong wind”: From the French proverb le jeu ne vaut pas la chandelle, “the game is not worth the candle,” from card players who paid for the candles they played by. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “her search for the external man”: “Though our outward man perish, yet the inward man is renewed day by day” (2 Corinthians 4:16). The phrase serves as a poignant meditation on mortality and the possibility of inner transformation, a central motif in the novel’s exploration of life, death, and the human soul. See The Bible.
    • Internalism and externalism are two opposite ways of integration of explaining various subjects in several areas of philosophy. These include human motivation, knowledge, justification, meaning, and truth. The distinction arises in many areas of debate with similar but distinct meanings. Internalism is the thesis that no fact about the world can provide reasons for action independently of desires and beliefs. Externalism is the thesis that reasons are to be identified with objective features of the world. (Wikipedia) The struggle to reconcile these approaches in the chapter reflects the broader philosophical challenge of integrating subjective experience with external reality, a theme explored through Mr. Spitzer’s divided self and his ambiguous relations to the world and memory.
  • “The external man, there was no doubt, had been Cousin Hannah’s foe”: Instead of seeking external validation, it’s important to focus on inner values, personal growth, and living a life that aligns with one’s beliefs and principles. Cultivating a strong sense of self-worth, independent of external opinions is crucial for personal well-being and resilience. This illustrates the importance of looking inward and living authentically. Cousin Hannah’s independence and moral rigor and Mr. Spitzer’s philosophical wrestling with illusion and reality convey that true life and growth come from aligning with one’s inner values rather than seeking external approval or being deceived by appearances. This chapter conveys that genuine selfhood and integrity arise from embracing one’s realities and responsibilities, no matter how harsh, rather than chasing illusions or the fleeting approval of others.
  • “to the sound of muffled drums”: “Our hearts, though stout and brave, / Still, like muffled drums, are beating / Funeral marches to the grave” (Longfellow, “A Psalm of Life,” 1838). See American Writers.
