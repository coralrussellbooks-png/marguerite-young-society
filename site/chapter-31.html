Summary

Catherine and Mr. Spitzer admire Cousin Hannah, whose great deeds made their own lives seem unfulfilled. Her secret grief is circled but never named: the mirrors of her house draped in black, her failed battle to rescue Catherine from opium, her indifference to both Spitzer brothers. Mr. Spitzer remembers his long faithfulness to her, knocking at a door that never opened. He struggles to write her epitaph, drafting and discarding version after version.

Key passages

From the research

  • Mr. Spitzer indulges Catherine’s moods as long as they are not of reality, believing reality would kill her as it had killed her canary, which she imagined flying over the waves. This suggests a delicate balance between fantasy and harsh reality in her life.
  • Their mutual admiration for Peron (who gambled his life away) and Cousin Hannah, an adventurer whose own life made their adventures seem unfulfilled, hints at the complex relationships and ideals binding these characters.
  • Cousin Hannah is described as direct, fierce, and adventurously restless, having fought losing battles with shadows and enduring harsh physical and emotional challenges, including suffrage campaigns and desert treks. She embodies "forlorn romance" and is portrayed as a great captain metaphorically armored by suffrage and armed with swords of ice and fire.
  • Her refusal to marry and the whispered secret of her life shadowed by grief add mystery and depth to her character. Her life is marked by dramatic adventures and a stoic endurance of suffering.
  • Despite her strength and battles, she could not "rescue herself," and her death is framed as a "domestic war," contrasting with the grand battles she fought. This passage suggests a tragic dimension to her life and death.
  • She sought to "carry" Vera’s mother away from opium dreams, fighting her losing battle with shadows, showing her as a protective but ultimately tragic figure.
  • She ignored Joachim, the lawyer, after Peron’s death, refusing any recognition or mourning, which underscores her complex relations and possibly her disdain for what she considered weak or insubstantial men. Also Mr. Spitzer may not be who he seems to be/
  • Mr. Spitzer’s persistent but unanswered visits to Cousin Hannah’s empty house symbolize a futile effort to maintain connection and control over the past. His rituals of knocking, leaving calling cards, bringing important papers contrast with the silence and absence inside, emphasizing themes of loss and the elusiveness of presence.
  • Cousin Hannah’s adventures in deserts and mountains serve as metaphors for her restless spirit and unattainable quests, with imagery of desert storms, Islamic kingdoms, and un-climbable alpine peaks creating a mythical aura around her.
  • The desert beauty’s mirror she brought is a symbol of the transience of human life, reinforcing the chapter’s meditation on mortality.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s efforts to compose an epitaph for Cousin Hannah, his hesitation and inability to decide on words reflect his sorrow and the difficulty of capturing a complex life and character in simple terms. His proposed epitaphs evoke her warrior spirit, her suffering, and her ultimate solitude and death at home rather than on a battlefield.
  • The meditation on death and memory adds philosophical depth to the narrative.
  • His own uncertain status between life and death, as suggested by bureaucratic errors listing him dead, parallels the ambiguous boundary between presence and absence that permeates this chapter.
  • Cousin Hannah’s scorn for Vera’s mother’s love for a "shadow," a fleeting and unsubstantial man, contrasts with Catherine’s belief that death is necessary to her love. This conflict highlights different understandings of love, reality, and illusion.
  • Cousin Hannah’s rejection of the external man and her suspicion of phantoms as rivals underscore the chapter’s theme of the elusive and often illusory nature of relationships and human connections.
  • The repeated imagery of knocking on doors, peering through keyholes, and the sensation of being watched by hidden eyes evokes a haunting atmosphere, symbolizing the barriers between people, the mysteries of identity, and the unseen presences that linger in memory and imagination.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • A legal figure who serves as a connection between the living and the dead.
  • Indulges Catherine’s moods but is deeply affected by the passing of Cousin Hannah.
  • Exhibits profound grief, ritualistically visiting Cousin Hannah’s empty house and attempting to maintain legal and emotional ties.
  • His character embodies persistence, melancholy, and a struggle to reconcile presence and absence.
  • Despite Cousin Hannah’s death and absence, Mr. Spitzer continues to visit her empty mansion, knocking on doors and slipping calling cards through mail slots.
  • His visits symbolize the persistence of memory and the difficulty of accepting loss.
  • He contemplates an epitaph and memorial for Hannah, reflecting on her life as a warrior and adventurer.

Catherine Cartwheel

  • A dreamer, living between reality and opium dreams.
  • Holds a love for a "shadow," a dead or unreal man, rather than a concrete person.
  • Her relationship with reality is tenuous; she is entwined with mystical and illusory elements.
  • She and Mr. Spitzer share mutual admiration for Cousin Hannah and Peron.
  • Catherine’s love is for an elusive, intangible presence rather than a real man.
  • The metaphor of man as a shadow driven by the wind highlights themes of impermanence and spiritual longing.

Cousin Hannah

  • A formidable, adventurous woman, a "great captain" with a suffrage warrior’s spirit.
  • Known for her directness, fiery temper, and refusal to accept illusions or evasion.
  • Lived a nomadic life filled with symbolic and literal journeys across deserts, mountains, and forbidden cities.
  • She opposed Catherine’s love for the shadow, believing in facing harsh realities.
  • After death, she remains a spectral presence, her memory both revered and shrouded in secrecy.
  • Despite her public life and campaigns, she kept a secret of sorrow, which others hesitate to speak of.
  • Cousin Hannah disapproves of Catherine’s dreams and love for the “shadow.” Their conflict is ideological: directness and confrontation versus evasion and mystical longing.
  • She is portrayed as a mythic figure battling external foes and internal demons.
  • Her life and death symbolize the struggle between illusion and reality, love and loss.
  • The chapter describes her as a figure who fought battles greater than those recorded, with imagery of deserts, storms, and crusades.

  • storms, deserts, snow, castles, veils, horses, swords, and stars.
  • These images underscore the mystical, elusive, and epic qualities of the characters’ inner lives.

Research: Resources

  • The transience of human life is a philosophical concept emphasizing the impermanence and fleeting nature of existence, encourages mindfulness, acceptance of change, and a deeper appreciation for the present moment. Young portrays human life as a fragile, temporary phenomenon woven into the vast, ever-changing tapestry of nature and time. The imagery of natural cycles, the recognition of memory’s limits, and the contemplation of death’s relationship to life all serve to illustrate the profound transience of human existence in the novel.
  • Heidegger’s philosophy “being-towards-death” is a fundamental aspect of human existence, highlighting the finite nature of our being and the importance of confronting mortality to live authentically. Humans are in the world and constantly engaging and interpreting it. Humans are unique in that they are aware of their own mortality and this shapes their existence. Heidegger distinguishes between authentic and inauthentic ways of living. Authentic living involves confronting the inevitability of death and making choices that are meaningful to oneself, while inauthentic living involves being swept away by societal norms and expectations. One must confront mortality in order to live more fully and purposefully, making the most of the time one has. Young articulates a richly poetic and existential meditation on death that embodies Heidegger’s philosophy of “being-towards-death.” Vera’s awareness of mortality disrupts ordinary existence, reveals the fragility and finitude of life, and through this confrontation, opens the possibility for authentic being. This chapter captures the essence of how the anticipation of death shapes human existence, infusing it with urgency, solitude, and profound insight, all central to Heidegger’s existential analytic.
  • “Man is like a breath; his days are like a passing shadow.” - Psalm 144:4 In the broader context of the chapter and the novel, this phrase highlights the idea that human life is insubstantial and temporary, underscoring the urgency to live fully and the acceptance of life’s inevitable end. It also connects with the novel’s exploration of the soul’s journey, memory, and the inescapable passage of time.
  • Luke 12 The Parable of the Rich Fool shows how wealth can be a distraction from the true purpose of life. Riches distract from the emptiness or are a source of fleeting pleasure, ultimately failing to provide lasting fulfillment or true happiness. The parable is thematically woven into the narrative as a meditation on mortality, the vanity of materialism, and the necessity of grounding one's life in deeper truths beyond the ephemeral world. Vera's reflections echo the parable's warning against greed and misplaced security, emphasizing humility, realism, and moral clarity in a transient world.
  • Instead of seeking external validation, it’s important to focus on inner values, personal growth, and living a life that aligns with one’s beliefs and principles. Cultivating a strong sense of self-worth, independent of external opinions is crucial for personal well-being and resilience. This illustrates the importance of looking inward and living authentically. Cousin Hannah’s independence and moral rigor, Mr. Spitzer’s philosophical wrestling with illusion and reality, and Miss MacIntosh’s practical wisdom all convey that true life and growth come from aligning with one’s inner values rather than seeking external approval or being deceived by appearances. This chapter conveys that genuine selfhood and integrity arise from embracing one’s realities and responsibilities, no matter how harsh, rather than chasing illusions or the fleeting approval of others.
  • In the Islamic tradition burial is a sacred practice. Cremation is strictly forbidden, The body is buried as soon as possible after death, ideally within 24 hours, in a simple shroud, facing Mecca and without a coffin. There are no elaborate markers with simple flat markers used instead. A ritual washing and prayer. Three handfuls of soil and no post-mortem examinations. There is a 40 day mourning period.
  • Etruscan towns were a collection of independent city-states in central Italy, united by a shared culture and language. They thrived in the regions of Tuscany, Umbria, and Northern Lazio between the 7th and 2nd centuries BC. Important Etruscan cities include Tarquinia, Vulci, Populonia, Cerveteri, Volterra, and Chiusi. Many of these cities still have archaeological sites, necropolises, and museums that showcase their Etruscan heritage.

  • Internalism and externalism are two opposite ways of integration of explaining various subjects in several areas of philosophy. These include human motivation, knowledge, justification, meaning, and truth. The distinction arises in many areas of debate with similar but distinct meanings. Internal– external distinction is a distinction used in philosophy to divide an ontology into two parts: an internal part concerning observation related to philosophy, and an external part concerning questions related to philosophy. Internalism is the thesis that no fact about the world can provide reasons for action independently of desires and beliefs. Externalism is the thesis that reasons are to be identified with objective features of the world. The struggle to reconcile these approaches in the chapter reflects the broader philosophical challenge of integrating subjective experience with external reality, a theme explored through Mr. Spitzer's divided self and his ambiguous relations to the world and memory.

  • Veiling and wearing a veil of clothing or hanging cloth has a long history in many societies across the world. It is intended to cover some part of the head or face, or an object of some significance. The practice has been prominent in different forms in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. The practice of veiling is especially associated with women and sacred objects, though in some cultures, it is men, rather than women, who are expected to wear a veil. Besides its enduring religious significance, veiling continues to play a role in some modern secular contexts, such as wedding customs. One view is that as a religious item, it is intended to honor a person, object or space. The actual sociocultural, psychological, and sociosexual functions of veils have not been studied extensively but most likely include the maintenance of social distance and the communication of social status and cultural identity. A veil also has symbolic interpretations, as something partially concealing, disguising, or obscuring. Young uses veiling as a rich symbol to explore themes of concealment versus revelation, the instability of identity, societal constraints especially on women, and the metaphysical boundaries between life, death, and perception. The veil is both protective and imprisoning, a literal and figurative barrier that characters both struggle with and embody. This motif deepens the novel’s meditation on illusion and reality, the known and unknown, and the complex interplay between appearance and essence.

It highlights the tension between illusion and reality, choice and necessity, and the acceptance of life’s inherent difficulties as the foundation for integrity and survival.
