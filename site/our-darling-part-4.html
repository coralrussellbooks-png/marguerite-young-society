Marguerite Young, Our Darling, Part 4: What It Reveals About Young and Her Work

Part of a reading of Marguerite Young, Our Darling (1994), a book of tributes and essays about her.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Part 3 · Marguerite Young, Our Darling: What It Reveals, Part by Part · Part 5 →

Source: Marguerite Young, Our Darling: Tributes and Essays, edited by Miriam Fuchs (Dalkey Archive Press, 1994). Part 4 completes Miriam Fuchs's essay on Young's utopias and then gives three more scholarly essays: Lillian S. Robinson on narrative and redemption, Peter G. Christensen's William Jamesian reading of Angel in the Forest, and Charles Ruas on the epic imagination. This is the densest critical stretch of the book, and the Ruas essay is the fullest account so far of how Young understood her own project. Only material that illuminates Young and her work is kept below. Quoted phrases from the book are marked with quotation marks.

The utopian pattern, completed

Fuchs finishes her argument by showing how deeply the theme of glorious failure runs through Young's whole imagination. She points to the giraffe poem in Moderate Fable, where Young celebrates a creature that survives “beyond the curve of probables,” and to a whole menagerie of anomalies, the unicorn, the deer-footed mouse, the flytrap flower, that stand for imperfection as the condition of existence itself: it is in confusion, Young writes, that “our only existence [is] possible.” These oddities are kin to the misfits of Miss MacIntosh, the blind painter and the one-legged dancer, the culture's dispossessed. Angel in the Forest is her tribute to two more failures, the utopian communities of George Rapp and Robert Owen at New Harmony, and Fuchs reads the book's meaning as fluctuating rather than fixed: the more failures Young chronicles, the more ubiquitous utopia becomes, so that utopia is constituted by all its collapsing attempts rather than by any single achieved one. Fuchs adds a useful compositional fact from Young herself: Angel began as sixty ballads, was rewritten in blank verse, and took its final prose form over roughly three years, a “poetic compilation of individual portraits and collective histories.” The governing law is that there must always be a flaw, the flaw that heightens the crystal, and the unfinished tale of the flour miller, folded into book after book, is its emblem. What is “no-place” and many places in Angel becomes an “everyplace” in Miss MacIntosh, whose only landscape, Vera says, is the soul's. Fuchs ends on the image of Cousin Hannah's forty locked trunks and forty keys, always one key too many or too few, the mystery that cannot be solved, and on Young's decision to break off her twelve-hundred-page novel abruptly, because no single revelation could ever contain her utopia.

A whole world made of stories

Lillian Robinson borrows a phrase from her own review of Margaret Atwood to name what Young achieves: “a book that is a whole world,” and, more rarely, one with a woman's inner life at its center. She maps the novel's cosmos as three layers, a limited or forgetful God at the top, the solid middle earth of geography and government where Miss MacIntosh herself is rooted, and the individual mind as microcosm of the whole. What binds those layers is narrative: the book is made of hundreds of stories, a patchwork quilt of them. Robinson sets it against the encyclopedic tradition of Dante, but marks the crucial difference in the ending. Where Dante's quest rises to divine love, Young's ends on one more small constituent story, the restaurant sign reading “Bee bak in a whale,” a deliberately unimportant line that turns the reader back into the text rather than up toward perfection. For Robinson the point is that Young lets women's stories matter simply as stories, an assurance she says rarely coexists with the creation of a comprehensive fictional world. She also reads the heroine's birthplace, What Cheer, Iowa, as the flat middle of the country, a “Plantation without the Providence,” standing for the plain and ordinary against which the novel's cosmic theology pushes.

Politics, prophecy, and Angel in the Forest

Robinson and Christensen together bring out the political spine of Young's work. Robinson argues that Miss MacIntosh is intentionally incomplete, one part of a larger whole that includes Angel in the Forest and the forthcoming Debs epic, “Prelude in the Golden Key”; these more explicitly political books share the novel's visionary character. She names Young's antecedents precisely: Blake, building a New Jerusalem against the dark Satanic mills, and Bunyan, whose Pilgrim's Progress is Miss MacIntosh's favorite text and lends its title to the Robert Owen chapter of Angel. Debs is prized as a prophet and seer; Robinson calls Eugene Debs in prison Young's moral equivalent of Jesus on the Mount. Cousin Hannah carries the same visionary charge into the suffrage struggle, her battles fought as much in literature as in electoral politics. Christensen reads Angel in the Forest through William James's Varieties of Religious Experience. He shows Father Rapp as the ruin of belief turned tyrannical, a man who listens to no voice but his own until, in Young's words, “As vice-regent of the Deity, Father Rapp left nothing to the Deity.” Rapp's obsession with apocalypse and election, his retributive religion, even the killing of his own son, all fail the everyday human world, and Young links his apocalyptic fire to real industrial devastation. Owen, opposing imperialism with social harmony, is the counter-figure, and Christensen ties Young's mockery of aggressive Christian empire to James's own anti-imperialism. His conclusion is that Angel is less a study of religious psychology than “a defense of socialism with a human face,” placing society over the individual through Young's own image: stop thinking about flour, the individual, and think about bread, the collective.

How Young placed herself

Charles Ruas's essay is the clearest record of Young's own account of her lineage and method. She described herself as belonging to a tradition of “objective imagination,” reaching back to Milton and Spenser and forward through a novelistic line she claimed as her own: Balzac's human comedy, and more especially Sterne, Gogol, Dickens, Melville, and Dreiser. Pointedly, she distanced herself from Gertrude Stein, arguing that Stein practiced automatic writing while her own repetition and incantation grew out of writing poetry, and she claimed instead to be a disciple of William James and his pluralism, with a debt to Henry James for sensibility. Her strongest statement of method is an alliance with Browning: like him, she said, “I don't believe in inventing characters,” pointing to The Ring and the Book, built from history, as her model. Ruas lays out the novel's architecture, circling the disappearance of Miss MacIntosh around four archetypal figures, the governess, the Opium Lady, Mr. Spitzer, and Esther Longtree, each an archetype large enough to contain its own opposite; a proposed paperback edition would have subtitled its volumes Inquest, Opium, Twin, and Mule. Young defined her character-making as projection: she pours into the heightened image of a figure everything she thinks it is and might become, and insists that even a minor character must have a problem, or it is only “a name on a page.” Ruas closes on the scale of the ambition, noting the chapters given to the last passenger pigeon and the monarch butterfly's migration, Young's reach toward geological time, and her acceptance, echoing McCullers's line that “the novel is the epic of modernity,” of her generation's challenge to write the Great American Novel, an American epic modeled on Joyce's epic of Dublin.
