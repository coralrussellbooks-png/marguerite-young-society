The Wheel and the Spokes: Introduction

Wheel and Spokes Home

Some books ask to be read. This one asks to be inhabited.

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is a novel about a bus ride. Vera Cartwheel is traveling from the coast of New England toward the interior of America, searching for her childhood nursemaid, a practical woman from What Cheer, Iowa who one September morning walked into the sea. The journey takes the entire book to complete. The book is 1,198 pages long.

That description is accurate and almost entirely beside the point.

What Marguerite Young built over the eighteen years she spent writing this novel (published in 1965, largely unread since) is less a story than a philosophy rendered in prose. Its central claim is that reality is not what we find but what we make; that the self is multiple and irresolvable; that grief does not move through stages toward resolution but accumulates, layer by layer, like sediment, until it becomes the ground we walk on. Young did not arrive at these ideas and then illustrate them. She found a form (the vast, spiraling, never-concluding sentence; the return to the beginning with what was learned) that enacts them from the inside.

The novel's characters exist at the edges of categories: the opium-dreaming mother who is neither mad nor sane, neither living nor dead; Mr. Spitzer, the lawyer-musician who may be his own dead twin; Miss MacIntosh herself, the enemy of illusion who turns out to be the most elaborately constructed person in the book. They are not exceptions to the social order. They are its evidence: the places where the seams show, where the fiction of a stable, singular, coherent self becomes impossible to maintain.

This philosophy is called Radical Immanence. The sacred, in Young’s world, does not live elsewhere. It lives here, in the grey bus, in the permanent grief, in the broken nose and the wig and the watch worn over a missing breast. Transcendence points not upward and out but deeper in, into the body, the loss, the ongoing sentence that refuses to conclude.

This is not an easy book. It demands the kind of attention we usually reserve for people we love: sustained, patient, willing to stay in the room without rushing toward resolution. Young asks you to read the way her characters live: accumulating, circling, returning, carrying forward what you cannot put down.

The reward is not a resolved story. It is something rarer: a prose that thinks the way grief thinks, that moves the way memory moves, that takes seriously the possibility that incompletion is not failure but the only honest form integrity can take.

Marguerite Young wrote Miss MacIntosh, My Darling as a container to enact her philosophy. It consists of 10 pillars and the 11th pillar is the form, the Crossing Point. A reader can also picture it as 10 spokes inside a wheel with the 11th pillar as the axis.

What Young's novel demonstrates, from its opening pages, is that the Eleven Pillars are not a framework imposed from outside; they are the discovered grammar of a consciousness that refuses the consolations of singularity, resolution, completion, and transcendence-as-escape.

Below is a summary of the Eleven Pillars of Radical Immanence that will be discussed in every chapter.

The Eleven Pillars of Radical Immanence

  • Pillar 1 Not One Self: You are not one person. The self is multiple, simultaneous, and irresolvable. This is not a problem to fix. It is what you are.

  • Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed: Reality is not what we find; it is what we make. The line between illusion and reality is itself a construction. There is no sunlit world outside the cave, only a larger cave more carefully lit.

  • Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End: Grief does not move through stages and arrive at resolution. It accumulates. It is the climate of a world where everything you love is always already changing. The honest life stops pretending otherwise.

  • Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act: The central ethical stance is not to fix or conclude, but to stay in the room. To see fully, without flinching, without rushing toward resolution. Attention is the practice. Refusal to simplify is the act.

  • Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary: You build anyway, knowing the building is provisional. You return to the beginning with what you learned. The building is never finished. That is not failure. That is how it works.

  • Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest: The unfinished is not failure. It is honesty. The demand for completion is its own violence against experience. Integrity looks like the ongoing sentence, not the concluded one.

  • Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth: The edges of categories are where the constructed nature of reality becomes most visible. The marginal person is not the exception. They are the evidence.

  • Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away: The past does not recede. It accumulates beneath us, layer on layer. What was lost is not behind us. It is beneath us, still pressing up through the ground we stand on.

  • Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible: Language is not a neutral vehicle for truth. It shapes what can be seen, who can exist, what counts as real. Honest language names its own location. Language that pretends to speak from nowhere speaks from somewhere in particular.

  • Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here: The sacred is here, inside the ordinary world, pointing deeper into the immanent rather than outward to a beyond. The grey bus, the permanent grief, the plural self in all its unresolved reality: this is where the sacred lives.

  • Pillar 11 The Crossing Point: You are not a self moving through time. You are where time crosses itself. You are constituted by what was deposited before you arrived, and you are constituting the ground the not-yet-born will stand on. This obligation is not chosen. It is structural. The ancestors are in the sediment. The carrying continues.
