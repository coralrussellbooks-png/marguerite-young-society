Chapter 22: All in All, There Is No News Today That Was Not Yesterday

← Chapter 21 · Guide Home · Chapter 23 →

Research notes: Chapter 22

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Philosophy: Augustine and Christian Theology · Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Exploration and the Poles · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest

Chapter 22 is the first full ordinary day after the revelation, and Young gives it to us in full. It is long and dense and wanders and doubles back on itself, and it does this deliberately: this is what it feels like to sit at a breakfast table with someone and try to act as if nothing has changed.

If you find yourself lulled by the rhythm of Miss MacIntosh’s newspaper recitations, her geography questions, her complaints about dust and housekeeping, that’s fine. That lulling is the point. And then the chapter breaks the lull, twice, with sentences so precise they stop you cold. Stay alert for those. The chapter earns them.

What actually happens in Chapter 22

It is the morning after. Vera sits at an unexpectedly festive breakfast table (the good Haviland china, a silver service, white roses in a hammered silver bowl) while Miss MacIntosh reads the morning newspaper aloud. Her wig is in place. The pact is holding.

Miss MacIntosh reads about Brazilian rebellions, Wall Street, Norwegian storms, horse races, cock fights, needle-workers’ strikes, the price of eggs, divorces, the deposition of Balkan kings. She asks Vera geography questions, creation questions, sports questions. She complains about the housekeeping, the dust, the unmatched socks, the angels in the hallways who need dusting. She talks about the shipwrecks off their coast, the things the sea keeps bringing in. She considers buying shirts from a flooded Boston basement.

Vera, watching the brick-red top of Miss MacIntosh’s wig above the newspaper, seething with a nameless irritation, asks twice about the party dress. Miss MacIntosh deflects both times with more questions.

Near the end, almost in passing, Miss MacIntosh says: “baldness is no cure for sorrow, my dear.” And later: sometimes “the inner man was all that counted—not the outer man, this poor visage and mockery of himself.”

The chapter ends with Vera wondering: was she all others?

The festive table

Young opens the chapter with something small and strange: the breakfast table is decorated as if for a special occasion. The Haviland china, the silver service, the hammered silver bowl of white cabbage roses. They never use these things. They are usually down to the broken crockery bought cheap at a fire sale.

Miss MacIntosh apparently does not notice. She sees only the old oilcloth of What Cheer, Iowa. She has “no eye for artificial beauty or anything” man has made, and she says so, as she always has.

But someone set that table. Someone brought out the good china and the silver bowl and the roses. Young does not say who. The chapter simply begins with Vera sitting before this festive spread, and Miss MacIntosh behind her newspaper, and the morning light on the red wig, and the pact quietly in effect. The table is the only acknowledgment either of them will make of what the night was.

Miss MacIntosh in full voice

Chapter 22 gives us the longest and most expansive portrait of Miss MacIntosh’s newspaper monologue in the novel. It goes on for most of the chapter, weaving between world affairs and geography lessons and housekeeping complaints and sports scores and creation theology, and it is worth reading as a performance, because it is one.

Miss MacIntosh is talking to keep the silence away. The questions come faster than usual, the complaints more insistent, the newspaper’s contents more varied. She reads about cock fights and dog races and steeplechases and shipwrecks and divorces and the price of tallow. She moves from God’s creation of the firmament on Monday to the greatest pearl ever grown to whether Siberian wheat can grow in the Dakotas to the rules of bowling, all without pausing.

Vera notices that their schedule seems “somewhat off course, perhaps by a point or two.” She notices that Miss MacIntosh has eaten before her, which has never happened before. She notices the rasping of Miss MacIntosh’s breathing, the difficulty of it, like a drowning man who sees a wall of water approaching.

Underneath the performance, Vera is listening for something else. And then, late in the chapter, Miss MacIntosh gives it to her: “baldness is no cure for sorrow, my dear.” “Spinach is the best bone-builder. We all require a little salt in our blood—but not much. Death has a salty taste—the same as tears. We came from the sea.”

It is the only direct acknowledgment either of them makes. It is delivered in the voice of a proverb, almost sleepily, “like the continuation of a dream.” And then the newspaper goes back up.

The inner man

Near the chapter’s end, Miss MacIntosh says something she has perhaps been building toward all morning: sometimes “the inner man was all that counted—not the outer man, this poor visage and mockery of himself, so every image should fade.” “The inner man might be an altogether different being from the poor outer man. Only the poorest fool would judge by the appearances, by the outer visage and these poor clothes a man must wear, these mortal garments so ill-fitting and not his choice at all.”

She applies this to herself immediately: “sometimes she thought the inner man was battered, too, impaired by much hard usage. Her soul itself must have, she laughed, this broken crown, these weak and watering eyes,” “this poor, broken nose, these false teeth, these knotted veins, the large hands, the large, long feet.”

This is Miss MacIntosh doing what she does in the night chapters, but in daylight, in her normal voice, filtered through the grammar of proverb and common sense. She is telling Vera, and telling herself, that the mortal garments are not the person. That the outer visage is not the choice. That the inner man and the outer man may be different beings.

It is as close as she will come, in daylight, to speaking directly about what Vera saw.

Was she all others?

The chapter’s final question (was she all others?) is one of the novel’s most compressed. Vera asks it without elaboration and Young does not answer it.

What does it mean? Vera has been watching Miss MacIntosh perform normalcy all morning, and she has been watching the performance crack at the edges: the festive table no one acknowledges, the schedule off course, the breathing like a drowning man, the line about baldness and sorrow. And she wonders: is Miss MacIntosh’s ordinariness not just a performance but a kind of universality? Is she, in her stripping bare, not one particular strange person but somehow everyone, every person carrying a secret the outer self works all day to conceal?

Young has been building to this question since Chapter 18, when Vera thought about the secret man hidden in the woman, and the secret woman hidden in the man. Now, at the breakfast table, in the ordinary morning light, the question arrives in its simplest form: was she all others? It opens outward into the rest of the novel, which will keep asking it, of character after character, in different rooms and different weathers.

What to watch for as you keep reading

Light spoilers: This section hints at what happens later in the novel. Skip it if you want to meet the story fresh.

The novel is about to expand. We are ten chapters away from the end of Volume One, and Young is going to begin widening the frame: bringing in other characters, other histories, other geographies. The Miss MacIntosh sequence has been the novel’s anchor. Everything that comes next will be understood against it.

Miss MacIntosh’s mention of her dead love, the silver-dollar watch “given to her by her dead love,” arrives so quietly in this chapter that you might miss it. It is the only time in the novel that this love is named directly. Young does not elaborate. She does not need to. She has already told you everything about Miss MacIntosh’s inner life. This is just one more detail of the inventory, placed without emphasis, in a sentence about how Miss MacIntosh is protected by her lack of pretence.

The shipwrecks off the coast accumulate in this chapter into a significant catalogue: “the captain’s table set for two” or three, the stringless harp washed up, the cardinal’s hat, the twelve rosaries strewn from here to the lighthouse. These are the things the sea keeps returning. Young is building the novel’s coastal mythology: a world where what has been lost keeps coming back, changed, barnacled, still recognizable.
