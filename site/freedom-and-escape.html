In the research: Chapter 12, Chapter 15, Chapter 16, Chapter 30, Chapter 35

Definition from research

Chapter 12 · Freedom, Innocence, and Joy

  • There is a moment of profound joy and freedom as Vera walks out into the night, celebrating the beauty and unity of nature.
  • This freedom is described as innocence and clarity of vision, untainted by past sorrow or fear.
  • Vera feels connected to the natural world, sensing stars, flowers, and animals as real and meaningful.

Chapter 15 · Youth and Experience versus Adult Convention

  • Vera experiences a moment of awakening, realizing the vastness and complexity of life beyond the restrictive, controlled world she has known.
  • She contrasts her youthful desire for experience and freedom with the narrow, methodical, and often fearful mindset of the adults, especially Miss MacIntosh.
  • The tension between youthful exploration and adult caution is central: Vera laughs at the warnings and rules, craving life’s fullness despite risks.

Chapter 16

  • “The fierce, exultant pride that was mine”: In “What Is It with Women and Horses?” (Intelligent Horsemanship, 2019), Dr. Rosie Jones-McVey, drawing on her PhD research among British horsewomen, writes: “In Victorian novels, as well as in the more modern genre of pony-girl books, it is often aboard a horse that girls find their wings, often proving some snot-nosed boy wrong in the process, or enabling them to escape various romantic dramas and find solace in the barn. Just like the pony-girl books, many of my participants’ life stories entwined horseyness with coming-of-age stories. Horses were the means through which many women first acted in a truly independent way, often pursuing equestrian endeavours despite/without their parents, or else, proudly taking up adult-like responsibilities in being the teacher, pilot, and carer of their ponies, rather than the one being looked after and told what to do.” She argues that horses give many women a space for independence and self-assertion that their everyday lives do not.

Chapter 16 · Imagination and Childhood Freedom

  • Vera’s ride on Falada is a metaphor for freedom and escape from her restrictive life.
  • The imaginative descriptions of the pony’s capabilities and their adventures reflect the boundless nature of a child’s mind.

Chapter 30 · Adventure and Escape

  • Cousin Hannah embodies the spirit of adventure, traveling to forbidden places and surviving countless dangers.
  • Her life serves as a counterpoint to Catherine's static, opium-induced dreaming.
  • The recurring imagery of ships, deserts, mountains, and deserts emphasizes journeys both physical and metaphysical.

Chapter 35 · Freedom and Emancipation

  • Cousin Hannah embodies the fight for women’s rights and freedom from traditional roles.
  • Her life is a metaphor for breaking boundaries: social, geographic, and existential.
  • The suffrage movement is portrayed as her husband, her master, and her true love.
