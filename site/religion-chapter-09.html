On Chapter 9 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Christianity and Religion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Allusions: Genesis · The Bible · The Book of Job

Definition from research

The "Twelfth Commandment" is Miss MacIntosh's own invention; no such commandment exists. What she hangs on it, fetch the needle, hem the handkerchief, cover our nakedness, turns her plain morality into a kind of scripture, with a glance back at the nakedness of birth and the shame of the Fall.

"naked as in that day when we came from our mothers' wombs" echoes Job 1:21, naked we come and naked we go, all things given and taken by God.

"Hope dies hard in the human breast" darkens Pope's "Hope springs eternal," trading his easy optimism for the stubborn refusal of hope to die even when it should.

Vera's refusal to believe Miss MacIntosh's childhood was unhappy is a small act of protective denial, of a piece with the stern Christian stoicism that bears suffering rather than dwells on it.

"if it was God's hand, was it still murder?" is the book's sharpest question of divine justice, asking whether an act God willed or allowed can still be a crime, and where human guilt begins and ends.

"Eve's eating an apple of the tree of knowledge" names the Fall, the disobedience in Eden that let sin and death into the world. The apple is later tradition, not the text itself.

"Who had divided the light from the darkness and created great whales" follows Genesis 1, God parting light from dark and making the great creatures of the sea.

The whales are bid to "be fruitful and multiply and fill the waters in the seas," the blessing of Genesis 1:22 on the life of the waters.

The genealogies Vera drills from the "Moody Bible," the sons of Levi, Judah, Er, Onan, and the rest, are the long descent of Jacob's line, learned by rote as the sediment of the faith. The name points to the evangelical Moody Bible Institute.

"the heave shoulder and the breast wave" are the priestly portions of Exodus and Leviticus, the breast waved and the thigh lifted before God and set aside for Aaron's line, an image the novel turns toward self-offering and dedication.

Those who "built cities in the wilderness and built arks" recall Noah's ark and David's city that housed the Ark of the Covenant, shelters raised against the flood and the wild.

"Death is not yet" is Miss MacIntosh's flat refusal to fear death before its hour, of a piece with her sober, present-facing faith.

"the canker eats the rose," with the greatest sailor swallowed by the very waters, gathers the book's images of a decaying, fallen world, beauty consumed and even the strongest taken under.

"Died Abner as a fool died" comes from David's lament in 2 Samuel 3, set here among the novel's long meditation on the many ways of dying and the one end that levels them all.

Asked how the wise man dies, Vera answers "As the fool died," refusing any comfort that wisdom might spare a person the common grave.

"When thou diest, I shall die with you" reworks Ruth's vow to Naomi in Ruth 1:17, loyalty unto death, turned here toward reckoning and reproof.

"There but for the grace of God" carries John Bradford's words at the sight of prisoners led to die, and behind them Paul's "by the grace of God I am what I am."
