Chapter 9

On Chapter 9 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Christianity and Religion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 24, 2026

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Allusions: Genesis · The Bible · The Book of Job

Vera's refusal to believe Miss MacIntosh's childhood was unhappy is a small act of protective denial, of a piece with the stern Christian stoicism that bears suffering rather than dwells on it.

"if it was God's hand, was it still murder?" is the book's sharpest question of divine justice, asking whether an act God willed or allowed can still be a crime, and where human guilt begins and ends.

“Desire be to thy husband”: God tells Eve, “thy desire shall be to thy husband” (Genesis 3:16). See Genesis.

“She was just the cross I had to bear”: “Whosoever doth not bear his cross, and come after me, cannot be my disciple” (Luke 14:27). See The Bible.

"There but for the grace of God" carries John Bradford's words at the sight of prisoners led to die, and behind them Paul's "by the grace of God I am what I am."

The "Twelfth Commandment" is Miss MacIntosh's own invention; no such commandment exists. What she hangs on it, fetch the needle, hem the handkerchief, cover our nakedness, turns her plain morality into a kind of scripture, with a glance back at the nakedness of birth and the shame of the Fall.

"naked as in that day when we came from our mothers' wombs" echoes Job 1:21, naked we come and naked we go, all things given and taken by God.

"Hope dies hard in the human breast" darkens Pope's "Hope springs eternal," trading his easy optimism for the stubborn refusal of hope to die even when it should.

“for we were put here to be tested and not found wanting”: The writing on Belshazzar’s wall reads, “Thou art weighed in the balances, and art found wanting” (Daniel 5:27). Miss MacIntosh sees life as a rigorous discipline, where even suffering and failure have their place but must be met with fortitude. The phrase underscores her belief in the necessity of accepting life’s challenges as part of one’s duty and not giving way to despair or weakness. See The Bible.

“Twelve hours in a day, twelve hours in a night”: “Are there not twelve hours in the day?” (John 11:9). See The Bible.

"Eve's eating an apple of the tree of knowledge" names the Fall, the disobedience in Eden that let sin and death into the world. The apple is later tradition, not the text itself.

“the apple was no sweet macintosh”: The McIntosh apple, found in Ontario in 1811, carries Miss MacIntosh’s name. Genesis never names the fruit; the apple came from the Latin pun malum, “apple” and “evil.” See Genesis.

"Who had divided the light from the darkness and created great whales" follows Genesis 1, God parting light from dark and making the great creatures of the sea.

The whales are bid to "be fruitful and multiply and fill the waters in the seas," the blessing of Genesis 1:22 on the life of the waters.

The genealogies Vera drills from the "Moody Bible," the sons of Levi, Judah, Er, Onan, and the rest, are the long descent of Jacob's line, learned by rote as the sediment of the faith. The name points to the evangelical Moody Bible Institute.

"the heave shoulder and the breast wave" are the priestly portions of Exodus and Leviticus, the breast waved and the thigh lifted before God and set aside for Aaron's line, an image the novel turns toward self-offering and dedication.

Those who "built cities in the wilderness and built arks" recall Noah's ark and David's city that housed the Ark of the Covenant, shelters raised against the flood and the wild.

“the whole world on his back”: Vera says the turtle is so old that it might be the “World Turtle.” This myth is found in different parts of the world and different cultures. In Hindu myth the turtle Akupara holds up the world, and the Lenape and Haudenosaunee tell of the earth built on a turtle’s back, Turtle Island.

“Solomon, Miss MacIntosh objected, had had too many wives”: Solomon may be named after King Solomon of the Bible, who was regarded as a wealthy and wise king. Solomon “had seven hundred wives, princesses, and three hundred concubines” (1 Kings 11:3), and his temple had tables of gold, lamps, snuffers, and pomegranates (1 Kings 7). See The Bible.

“sixteen bleeding Greek metropolitans”: Greek metropolitans are archbishops who head a province of the Orthodox Church. Some were killed and became martyrs and saints. In 1922, when Smyrna burned, Metropolitan Chrysostomos was handed to a mob and mutilated to death, one bleeding Greek metropolitan the church made a saint. The sixteen metropolitans may refer to the Greek genocide (1914-1922).

“Where were those seven years of lean? Who shall divide the wheat from the tares? Who hid his lantern under a bushel?”: Joseph’s seven lean years (Genesis 41), the parable of the wheat and the tares (Matthew 13:24-30), and the candle under a bushel (Matthew 5:15). See The Bible.

“A blind violinist and a deaf dancer”: There are many blind, deaf, and mute people in the book. This could be a play on the Three Wise Monkeys. The Three Wise Monkeys are a Japanese folk representation of the proverb “see no evil, hear no evil, speak no evil.” They symbolize prudence and purity, and are often seen as a call for discretion or willful ignorance. The three monkeys are Mizaru (see no evil), Kikazaru (hear no evil), and Iwazaru (speak no evil). The concept originates in Japan, with statues of the monkeys found in temples and shrines. The proverb “see no evil, hear no evil, speak no evil” is often interpreted as a way to avoid negative experiences or situations by choosing to ignore them. In Japan, the monkeys are seen as attendants of Koshin, the God of Roads, and their message is used to teach prudence and purity. In the West, the proverb is often used sarcastically to criticize those who deliberately ignore wrongdoing. The idea is rooted in Confucius: “Look not at what is contrary to propriety; listen not to what is contrary to propriety; speak not what is contrary to propriety” (Analects 12.1, trans. James Legge). Some representations include a fourth monkey, Sezaru, who “does no evil.” (Wikipedia) One misses the train by seeing it but not hearing its whistle, the other by hearing but not seeing. See the Chapter 8 resources and Deafness and Sign Language. Earlier in Chapter 8.

“this vale of tears”: “In hac lacrimarum valle,” from the hymn Salve Regina, after Psalm 84:6. See The Bible.

“It would be better to celebrate, not the day we were born into this world but the day we were born out of it”: “The day of death than the day of one’s birth” (Ecclesiastes 7:1). See Ecclesiastes.

“Was there even a dark angel at the gate? What horseman reining his horse at the door?”: Death rides a pale horse (Revelation 6:8). Lionel Johnson’s poem “The Dark Angel” (1893) is about a tempter who haunts the soul. See The Bible.

"Death is not yet" is Miss MacIntosh's flat refusal to fear death before its hour, of a piece with her sober, present-facing faith.

"the canker eats the rose," with the greatest sailor swallowed by the very waters, gathers the book's images of a decaying, fallen world, beauty consumed and even the strongest taken under.

“There is vomit at all tables”: “For all tables are full of vomit and filthiness” (Isaiah 28:8). See The Bible.

"Died Abner as a fool died" comes from David's lament in 2 Samuel 3, set here among the novel's long meditation on the many ways of dying and the one end that levels them all.

Asked how the wise man dies, Vera answers "As the fool died," refusing any comfort that wisdom might spare a person the common grave.

"When thou diest, I shall die with you" reworks Ruth's vow to Naomi in Ruth 1:17, loyalty unto death, turned here toward reckoning and reproof.

“Were not true marriages made in heaven”: “What therefore God hath joined together, let not man put asunder” (Matthew 19:6). See The Bible.
