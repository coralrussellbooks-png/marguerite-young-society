Moses Hunnecker: The Driver of the Crossing

Part of Teaching a Writing Masterclass, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← The Old Clam Digger · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Madge Capehorn Edwards →

Spoiler note: This portrait is written for readers who have finished the novel. It discusses the character’s whole story, including how it ends.

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Moses Hunnecker · Vera Cartwheel

He is the first person in the book, at the wheel, whistling, drunk, driving badly through the mist because he is the one steering the whole thing, the vehicle that carries Vera and the two sleeping lovers and the reader into the novel, and he is doing it half blind, toward a place that will not turn out to be the one anyone meant to reach.

Moses Hunnecker drives the Grey Goose bus into interior America, his long hair streaming like a curtain over his eyes, his fat shoulders sloping over the wheel, a whiskey bottle he drinks from openly with many calls upon God and the angels and the angel Gabriel. He whistles the mating calls of birds and sings that the old grey goose is dead, died in the morning with a pillow under her head. He talks the whole way about his old woman waiting at home, who will take his head off when she sees him, and Vera cannot tell whether the wife and the children and the ordinary happiness are his or something she dreamed up out of her own hunger for it. He may be a family man going home. He may be a bachelor, a nobody, a mad Don Quixote chasing windmills. He drives erratically through a mist that blots out the road, and he says he can see better drunk than the sober can see, because he has the rear-view mirror and can watch what comes from before and behind at once.

Ask the wrong question and he is a bit of local color, a comic driver to open on before the real story starts. Ask the right one and he is piloting the book's deepest claim. Radical Immanence holds that the meaning is not at the destination set out for but at the actual place a person ends up, the exact here-and-now, the crossing point where she happened to be standing. Moses is the man at the wheel of that idea. The question the book asks through him is not where is he taking her, but what is it to be carried, half blind, toward a place that will not be the one she was looking for, and to find that the wrong place is the only place there is?

Vera boards to find Miss MacIntosh, or the dead, or the truth, a journey with its destination out ahead of it. Moses does not take her there. He plows nowhere, through the mist, on a road that turns and seems to go back the way it came, and he sets her down in southern Indiana, a state as yet unknown to her, a place she never meant to reach. The bus was supposed to arrive somewhere. It arrives here. And here, the ordinary misted plain of the Middle West, the low fields, the toy train crossing the toy bridge, turns out to be the whole point, the real ground under the real moment, which is the only ground anyone ever stands on. Moses is the one who drives her off the map she was reading and into the place she is. He does not reach the destination because there is no reaching a destination. There is only being carried into the present, and he is the one doing the carrying.

His name says it twice. Moses is the man who leads the people the whole long way toward a promised land and does not himself set foot in it, who dies in sight of the country he spent his life crossing toward. Moses Hunnecker drives the same errand. He steers everyone toward an arrival that is always one more lap ahead. We are on the next to the last lap now, he says, and the next step is the end, or maybe it is the beginning, but which of us will live to know. He is the guide to a place the guide does not enter. The infant Moses was drawn out of the water and set adrift among the reeds, and this Moses pilots his bus across a plain the book keeps calling oceanic, a sea of mist with no boundary and no shore, ferrying his few passengers over the ambiguous waters of the first creation. The name is not decoration. It is the job.

And he is himself on the border he drives across. The whole ride he rages at old Doc, the doctor who brought him into the world and is dying now, or dead, killed by the revenge of all the patients he could not save, come back for him and riding in the rear-view mirror above the driver's shaggy head. Moses says he is living too long after he was dead, that some new mortal complication is on him, cold and burning at once, that he ought to be driving in his shroud. His cheeks have gone wooden and the woodpeckers bore holes in them, so that the living driver is turning into a carved effigy as he steers. He is the clam-digger's brother and Spitzer's, one more figure who cannot be called clearly alive or clearly dead, and it is exactly right that the crossing between those two states should be piloted by a man who is himself in the middle of making it. The one who drives the border belongs to both shores.

There is one more turn, and it is Vera's. She cannot verify a thing about him. The wife with the ample figure, the children, the home, the happiness she could not imagine to be real, may all be his, or may all be hers, laid over a stranger's back because she wants a world where people go home to someone. His ordinary life is either a fact she witnesses or a dream she builds, and the book refuses to say which. So even the man driving the crossing is himself a place where reality is made instead of found, a screen for the longing of the person he carries. He is real, and he is also whatever the passenger needs him to be, both at once, which is the condition of everyone in this book seen at their plainest.

The lesson for a writer is about who to put at the wheel. Young opens her enormous novel not with the sea-blackened mansion or the famous beauty or the lost governess but with a drunk bus driver singing about a dead goose, and she does it because the frame of the whole book, the journey that never arrives, needs a driver who is it rather than a symbol who stands for it. Moses does not represent the crossing. He drives it, half blind, into the present, toward a home he may or may not have, named for the guide who never reached the land. Put a figure like that at the very start and the reader is already inside the book's hardest claim before a single door of the great house has opened: that the reader is not going where she thinks, that the wrong place is the only place, and that someone not quite seen is carrying her there.
