Chapter 39

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel

Pillars: Not One Self · Unfinished Is Honest · Language Shapes What's Possible

The Questions

Catherine, eating an apple, says “I am eating myself.” What has happened to the word “I”?

She asks “which self of selves was true, reigning supreme.” What if the answer is that none is?

Her “self-knowledge, the knowledge of so many selves,” is a torment. Is the pain in the plurality, or in the demand to resolve it?

If the “I” does not name one thing, who is speaking when Catherine says it?

The pronoun comes apart in Catherine’s mouth. Eating an apple, she says, “I am eating myself,” and the strangeness is exact: if the “I” and the “myself” are both her, and the apple is also somehow her, then the word “I” has stopped naming a single bounded thing. The chapter says plainly that “the personal pronoun I did not always mean the same being or thing to Catherine.” This is Not One Self arriving at the level of grammar, where Language Shapes What's Possible meets it: “I” is a built word, a convenience that pretends to point at one fixed self, and in Catherine the pretense fails. She is “everybody and the rose petal, too,” and the little word cannot hold her.

She asks the question the demand for a single self always asks: “which self of selves was true, reigning supreme?” And the chapter’s weight falls on the possibility that none is. There is no true self reigning over the others, no original the counterfeits copy, no monarch among the crowd. The search for the one real self beneath the many is the search for a king in a republic, and it comes up empty not because the self is broken but because it was never a monarchy. The demand for a reigning true self is the finished blueprint turned inward, and like every finished blueprint it fails against the plurality it tries to rule.

This is why self-knowledge becomes torment rather than peace. “Self-knowledge, the knowledge of so many selves, had added to these wild waves of thoughts,” and the pain is precise: it is not the plurality that tortures but the demand to resolve it, to know at last which self is true. If one accepts the crowd, the many selves are simply what a person is. If one insists on finding the sovereign among them, the search never ends and never rests, and the knowledge of more and more selves only widens the failure. The torment is Unfinished Is Honest refused inward, the insistence on a finished self-knowledge that the self cannot supply.

So the chapter turns the plurality from affliction toward acceptance without ever pretending it is easy. Catherine suffers her many selves because she still, half, wants one. The book’s footing is the other way: the self is unfinished and unfinishable, a crowd with no king, and the peace it offers is not the peace of finally knowing which self is true but the peace of stopping the search for a true one. Catherine does not reach that peace here. She is caught between the plurality she lives and the sovereign she still looks for, and the looking is her suffering.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Catherine’s dissolving “I” as a symptom of madness to be cured back into a single self, and the reading that romanticizes her multiplicity as a freedom she enjoys. Neither the cure nor the celebration meets her actual torment.

The footing is that the pronoun “I” is a construction that pretends to name one fixed self and fails, so that Not One Self reaches all the way into grammar. There is no true self reigning over the others; the search for the sovereign self is the finished blueprint turned inward, and it comes up empty. Self-knowledge tortures not because the self is plural but because Catherine still demands it resolve into one, Unfinished Is Honest refused inward. There is no reigning true self, and the demand to find one is the source of the pain. Catherine does not reach the peace of ending the search, and the chapter leaves her caught between the crowd she is and the king she still wants.

For Reflection

When one says “I,” how sure is one that the word names a single thing?

What if there is no true self reigning over the others, and the search for one is the whole of the suffering?

Is the pain in being many, or in the demand to be one?
