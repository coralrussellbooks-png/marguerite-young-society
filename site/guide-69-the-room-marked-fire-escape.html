Chapter 69: The Room Marked Fire Escape

Research notes: Chapter 69

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Justice O’Leary · Miss MacIntosh · Moses Hunnecker · Mr. Weed · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Freedom and Escape · Guilt, Innocence, and Justice

Symbols: Angels, Devils, and Myth · Clothing, Hats, and Skirts · Fire · The House

Philosophy: Augustine and Christian Theology · Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Genesis · Revelation and the Apocalypse · Shakespeare · The Bible · The History of Opium

Vera has a room now. It is marked “Fire Escape.”

The bed is narrow and stuffed with corn husks. There is a warped mirror that shows only her eyes. There is a slop jar with sediment and blonde hair combings in it. On the wall, “a picture of a silver church steeple” with the words “Forget me not.” And a rocking chair that is already creaking, though no one is sitting in it.

This is where Vera will spend the night. She does not sleep. How could she? The Tavern is alive with presences: Mr. Weed’s shadow moving in the corridor, an old woman with canaries as big as turkey buzzards, a young girl named Tillie who will knit Vera a baby hood in the morning, and, through the thin wall beside her, the all-night anguish of Dr. Justice O’Leary, who is in there talking to someone named Sarah.

Sarah is dead. Or possibly not. That, as with everything in this novel, depends on your definition of real.

What Actually Happens

The landlord shows Vera to her room, taking the long way through the hotel’s corridors, pointing out the other inhabitants as he goes: Moses, who has a room here; Tillie, who lives across the hall and knits; the old woman with her terrible canaries. He tells Vera about Mr. Weed and his shadow: the shadow, apparently, being more talkative than the man himself. He is the kind of guide who tells you everything except what you actually need to know.

Once she is alone, Vera lies on her corn-husk mattress and listens. The hotel thinks out loud around her. And through the wall, Dr. Justice O’Leary begins.

He is old and failing, a physician of the old school, haunted and possibly mad. He is conducting a one-sided argument with Sarah: a woman who is dead, who may be a ghost, who arrives in his room smelling of cold breath and covered in sores, who he calls “baby” and “you old fool” and “the last disgrace of my life.” He curses her. He mourns her. He asks God why he has been abandoned. He tells her she should be ashamed. He cannot make her leave. He cannot stop talking to her. He also, in the course of the night, tells Vera (through the wall, through the strange permeability of this hotel) about his strangest case: a woman who believes she is “always pregnant with an imaginary baby” and suffers from miscarriage after miscarriage. Remember that detail. That woman will matter.

By morning, Vera is still awake. So, presumably, is Dr. O’Leary. Sarah, as always, is somewhere between.

The Room Marked Fire Escape

Vera’s room is not incidental. Young has given her a room designed for emergency egress (a room whose entire purpose is to get out, in case of catastrophe) and furnished it with the remnants of whoever was here before. The slop jar with its sediment and blonde hair combings. The rocking chair creaking on its own. The mirror that shows only eyes, not a face. The church steeple on the wall saying “Forget me not.”

Everything in this room is a trace of absence. Someone was here. They left things behind. The hotel has not cleaned them out. Forget me not: but the person who hung that picture is already forgotten, already sediment, already a combings-in-a-jar. The room is an escape and it is also a record. This is one of the novel’s recurring compressions: the thing that promises freedom is also the thing that documents everything you can’t escape from. Memory works this way. So does this hotel. So does this book.

The Landlord

He is loquacious: To All My Darlings uses that word and it is the right one. He has a tattooed chest that once had a lion on it, now faded. He has been a sailor, a soldier, a performer. He worked in intelligence during the Last War (the novel’s term for World War II, since World War I was the Great War). He has run this hotel through floods and fires. He listens to everyone. He trusts almost nothing.

He tells Vera about Mr. Weed’s shadow the way an innkeeper might pass along local legend: as information you’ll need, delivered casually, without alarm. In his telling, Mr. Weed is cautious and reserved; the shadow is the garrulous one, more forthcoming than the man himself. The shadow does his talking for him. This is the kind of doubling Young loves: the self and its unspeakable counterpart, the visible man and the part of him that moves freely in corridors at night.

He also mentions Tillie in passing: a very young girl who lives across from Vera’s room and will knit her a baby hood. Young slips this in without comment. A young girl in a decaying hotel, knitting baby hoods. We don’t know who the hoods are for. The landlord doesn’t say. He has moved on to something else.

Dr. Justice O’Leary and Sarah

This is the chapter’s heart, and it is one of the strangest and most affecting passages in the novel.

Dr. O’Leary is aging, bitter, professionally diminished, and haunted. His name is a small joke: his first name is Justice, and justice is exactly what he cannot locate, not in his own past, not in Sarah’s fate, not in the body’s failures, not in God’s apparent indifference. He has been a doctor his entire life and he does not know what Sarah is or how to treat her or how to make her stop appearing.

Sarah is described variously as a dead woman, a baby, a senile presence, something cold and covered in sores. She is not, or not only, a ghost. She is the thing Dr. O’Leary cannot stop carrying: a disgrace, a loss, a patient he failed, a person he loved and damaged, a past that will not close. Young does not explain her. You are not meant to have a resolved answer about what Sarah is. You are meant to understand what she does: she keeps him awake, keeps him talking, keeps him from the ordinary mercy of forgetting. He cries out in her voice the words of Christ on the cross: “My God! Why hast thou deserted me?” The line is from Psalm 22, spoken by Jesus in the Gospels at the moment of maximum abandonment. Dr. O’Leary uses it not for theological effect but because it is the most accurate description he has of his situation. He has been left. By Sarah, by competence, by faith, by the body’s cooperation, by whatever it is that used to make medicine feel like service rather than futility.

He also invokes “Let this cup pass from him”: Jesus in Gethsemane, asking God if there is any way to avoid what is coming. There is not. Dr. O’Leary knows there is not. He asks anyway, into the thin wall, all night.

Vera hears all of this. She doesn’t intervene. She is becoming a kind of collector of these voices (Mr. Weed last chapter, Dr. O’Leary tonight) people who are talking past their own capacity to resolve anything, who are saying the thing that cannot be said and saying it anyway because the alternative is silence and silence is worse.

The Strangest Case

Dr. O’Leary tells Vera (through the wall, in the middle of his long night of grief) about the strangest patient he ever had: a woman who believes herself “always pregnant with an imaginary baby,” who suffers miscarriage after miscarriage, who cannot stop expecting the child that is not coming.

Hold this. Young is introducing, in passing, through a physician’s corridor-monologue, the character of Esther Longtree, who will become one of the novel’s great figures in the chapters to come. You are being told about her before you meet her, in the voice of the man who failed to help her, in a hotel that already knows her by reputation. The novel prepares its characters this way: not with introductions but with rumors, with secondhand grief, with the doctor’s bafflement and the wall’s thin membrane.

A woman “always pregnant with an imaginary baby.” She is, in some sense, every character in this book: waiting for something that is perpetually expected and perpetually withheld. She is Vera, waiting for Miss MacIntosh. She is Catherine, waiting for her opium dreams to resolve into solid ground. She is the novel itself, always gestating, never quite arriving.

What the Tavern Is

By the end of this chapter you have a clearer picture of what the Tavern actually is. It is not simply a run-down hotel. It is a way station for the unresolved: people who are between things, who have not yet become what they are going to be or mourned what they have lost or finished the arguments they are having with the dead. Moses has a room here. Mr. Weed stays here. Dr. O’Leary apparently lives here. Tillie knits baby hoods here. The landlord says it is “a good enough port for a man in a storm.” He means this practically. Young means it cosmologically. Everyone in this hotel is in a storm. The port holds them without resolving anything. That is the most it can offer, and it is not nothing.

The hotel was “built in 1827 by God’s servants” and is now given over to thin walls and human-faced wasps and rocking chairs that move on their own. The faith that built it has become atmosphere. The walls remember everything. The slop jar holds what is left.

What to Hold Onto

The room marked Fire Escape. Vera’s room is for getting out, and she cannot sleep in it.

Mr. Weed’s shadow, moving in the corridor at night, more talkative than the man himself. The shadow that speaks what the body won’t. Dr. O’Leary and Sarah: a physician who cannot heal what haunts him, asking the same questions Jesus asked in the garden, all night, through a wall.

The woman “always pregnant with an imaginary baby.” She is coming.

And the picture on the wall (a silver church steeple, “Forget me not”) which is already, of course, the thing you cannot forget: that someone was here, in this bed, looking at this picture, and now they are gone. As everyone is gone. As Miss MacIntosh is gone. As the chapter ends before anything is resolved, and Vera lies awake in the dark, still listening.
