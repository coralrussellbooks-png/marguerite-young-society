Chapter 10. Language, Grammar, and the Grammar of Utopia

Part of a reading of Angel in the Forest (1945), Marguerite Young’s history of the two failed utopias at New Harmony, Indiana.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 9 · Angel in the Forest: A Reading in Fourteen Chapters · Chapter 11 →

A utopia is a grammar before it is a place. Before anyone lays a brick, the founders decide which words are allowed. They settle on a set of nouns that will count as real, and a set of verbs that people will be permitted to do, and everything that falls outside that vocabulary becomes not just discouraged but unsayable. And a thing that cannot be said, inside a community organized around saying, might as well not exist. This is the ninth pillar of Radical Immanence in its sharpest form. Language is not a set of labels for a world that is already there. Language is where the world gets decided. Marguerite Young understood this better than most historians ever have, and Angel in the Forest is, among other things, a long demonstration that no single grammar is big enough to hold a living world. Both utopias on that bend of the Wabash had a grammar. Both grammars broke, in the same place, on the same rock. The world would not hold still long enough to be named.

The Rappite grammar

Father Rapp’s Harmony spoke Scripture. Its nouns came straight out of Revelation. Angel, paradise, the New Jerusalem, the golden rose of Micah, the second Coming of One. These were not decorations. They were the entire inventory of what the community agreed was real. The permitted verbs were few, and all of them pointed the same way: to wait, to ascend, to be lifted up into the society of imperishable angels. What the grammar had no verb for was the individual will. Rapp said if the community was to be preserved, “the individual must be destroyed.” A grammar of pure waiting has no room for a person who wants something now.

The trouble is that the grammar could not hold its own content. Harmony was, by any measure, a place of staggering material abundance. It poured out wheat and wool and whisky and shoes and silk, and its barges went down the Mississippi loaded with the produce of a fabulously fertile nature. And all of this was made by a community whose official theology held the material world to be a snare, a thing to be endured until the real world arrived from the sky. The nouns said heaven. The warehouses said earth. The grammar denied the exact thing the community was best at.

You can watch the contradiction most clearly in a single tender image. Father Rapp kept a compound of wild deer that he fed by hand, and Young describes them stepping toward him like dancers, “their eyes almost like the eyes of luminous angels.” The old man who had his own son castrated for the crime of loving his wife would push spoiled lettuce and roses through the bars to the deer. The grammar had a word for the light in a deer’s eye. It had no word at all for what passed between John Rapp and his wife in the dark. Angel it could say. Marriage it could not. The tenderness the community forbade its members leaked out toward the animals, because tenderness does not disappear when you strike it from the dictionary. It only goes looking for somewhere it is still allowed to land.

The Owenite grammar

Robert Owen spoke a language that looked like the opposite of Rapp’s and worked in exactly the same way. Where Rapp had Scripture, Owen had the machine. His nouns were the vocabulary of the new science of society: circumstance, environment, improvement, the rational man. Man, he taught, is “the best of all possible machineries,” delicate but still a machine, all of whose parts can be known. From this it followed, with the clean click of a syllogism, that “to change human character, it is necessary only to change the circumstance.” Man was “solely the creature of his circumstance.” The permitted verbs were the mechanic’s verbs: to improve, to educate, to adjust, to form. What the grammar had no verb for was everything that could not be tuned. Belief. Grief. Longing. The wish to go home to Pittsburgh. The wish, at the end of a long life in pain, to walk into a lightning storm and be done.

And here too the grammar could not hold what the community actually was. Owen’s rational language drew some of the most intellectually alive people in America to that muddy townsite. It placed education at the center of everything and left real marks on how the country would come to think about schooling. It gathered reformers like Frances Wright, who fought for the freeing of slaves and for the equality of women. There was genuine light in the place. And the same community, run on the confident grammar of improvement, could not reliably house or feed the people it had gathered. It could theorize the perfect formation of character while children died of whooping cough in the mud outside the lecture hall. The nouns said progress. The graves said otherwise.

A grammar of obstacles

The clearest specimen of the Owenite grammar is the document Owen thought more important than the Declaration of Independence. Standing in Father Rapp’s old pulpit on the Fourth of July, he issued his Declaration of Mental Independence, and named what he called “a trinity of the most monstrous evils” ever combined to injure the human race: private property, irrational religion, and marriage founded on property. He was so sure this was the hinge of history that he started a new calendar. The community’s Gazette began dating its issues from the first year of Mental Independence.

Look at what a grammar of obstacles does. By naming the three things that stand between man and happiness, the declaration makes instantly visible everything it left off the list. What is not among the great obstacles? The body. Grief. Malaria in the river bottoms. A woman going blind at her sewing. An old man’s readiness to die. The desire to go home. Owen’s list is a list of ideas and arrangements, all of them things a rational reformer could dismantle. It has no line for the stubborn animal facts that were actually defeating the community while he spoke. Young sets the whole thing down and then delivers the quietest, most devastating judgment in the book: “the word ‘ought’ is a different thing from the word ‘is.’” People of fifty summers could not remake themselves into bald infants because a declaration told them to. The grammar of ought could describe a world that should exist. It could not touch the one that did.

The people the grammar cannot hold

This is where the seventh pillar comes in. The people who do not fit the grammar are not exceptions to the utopia. They are the evidence. When someone cannot be said in the community’s language, the seams of that language show, and you can see for the first time that it was made, not found. John Rapp, whose marriage the Scripture had no word for, is the proof that the Scripture was a human invention. Mrs. Pears, whose homesickness and ruined eyes had no place in the science of improvement, is the proof that the science was incomplete. Greenwood in the storm, carrying a truth his community could not let him speak, is the clearest proof of all. The margins are not the footnotes to these utopias. They are the exposed edge, where you can see that a grammar was drawn around some experiences and not others, and that the drawing was a choice, and that the choice left people out.

Young’s own grammar

Against both of these closed systems, Young writes a third kind of language, and it is the real argument of the book. Her prose refuses to freeze. It moves in catalogs, in lists that pour objects across the page faster than any category can gather them up: deerskins, hogskins, bearskins, wolfskins, horsehides, and on and on, until the sheer surplus of the world overflows the sentence meant to contain it. Her sentences are full of verbs and reluctant to settle into nouns. She asks questions and leaves them open. She holds two contradictory things side by side and declines to let either one win. This is not looseness. It is a grammar built on purpose to do what the other two could not, which is to describe a world made of change without pretending the change has stopped.

The theology closest to her method is the one heresy the book treats with real tenderness. Frederick Rapp, the pantheist smuggled into the heart of the nature-denying celibates, believed that God was everything, and therefore that God was “a huge animal without eyes,” because there is nothing outside God for God to look at, “not even one small, wet violet.” A God with no outside has no vantage point, no fixed place to stand and name things from. A God that is everywhere cannot be located, and a God that cannot see itself cannot freeze itself into an object. That is also a fair description of Young’s prose. It has no outside. It stands nowhere in particular and everywhere at once. It is a grammar of verbs for a world of processes, and it does not try to see itself, because it knows that being seen from the outside, is exactly the thing a living world can never be.

The world that would not hold still

Both utopias believed their grammar was reality itself. Rapp believed the New Jerusalem was on its way and the material world a passing illusion. Owen believed the human machine could be perfectly tuned by adjusting its circumstances. Each was describing a world that does not exist, because each grammar required the world to be a set of stable things doing permitted actions, nouns performing approved verbs, holding still to be named. The world Young actually shows is nothing like that. It is mud and malaria and abundance and grief and wild deer and dying children, all of it moving, none of it staying inside the words built to contain it.

Her prose is the counterargument, and it is an argument you feel rather than one she states. The sentence that will not resolve is not a failure of grammar. It is the only honest grammar for a world that does not resolve. Rapp’s New Jerusalem never came. Owen’s twelve months came and went and the world was not remade. But the deerskins are still piling up in the warehouse, and the violet is still wet, and the sentence, in Young’s hands, is still going.
