Chapter Seven: The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth · Across every book

Characters: Moses Hunnecker · The Stone Deaf Man

The seventh pillar is where the philosophical system encounters its own population.

"Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" is populated with characters who exist at the edges of the categories that social and political life uses to organize its population. These are not marginal figures in the sense of being incidental. They are the center of Young's philosophical attention, because the margins are where the categories show their seams, where the constructed nature of the social real becomes most visible, where the plural self cannot be forced into the singular mold without the forcing becoming undeniable. The marginal is not the exception in Young's system. It is the evidence.

Young had stated the principle as philosophy before she populated it with characters. In “The Contravalid” (1945) there are “No schemata exclusive of the cripple and accident,” and “The crippled dancer is the dance, at last, / The only truth.” Twenty years later the crippled dancer appears in the novel as a one-legged dancer among its company of the dispossessed.

Hannah Arendt's political philosophy, developed across The Human Condition and The Origins of Totalitarianism, offers the most rigorous available framework for understanding why the marginal is the evidence rather than the exception. Arendt argues that the political realm is not the realm of agreement but the realm of plurality. Political life, properly understood, is the life of beings who are fundamentally different from each other, who cannot be reduced to a common type or a common interest, and who nevertheless share a world. The space of appearance, Arendt's term for the political space, is the space in which plural beings appear to each other in their difference, not in their sameness.

Arendt's analysis of totalitarianism is directly relevant here. Totalitarian movements, she argues, destroy political life not primarily through violence, though they use violence, but through the destruction of plurality. They demand that every person become the same kind of person, that difference be eliminated, that the space of appearance be replaced by the uniform surface of ideological agreement. The destruction of plurality is the destruction of the political realm itself.

Young's novel enacts this analysis. The demand for the true self beneath the plurality, is a totalitarian demand in Arendt's sense. It destroys the space in which the plural self can appear honestly. It replaces the full complexity of the person with the simplified version that the demand can process. The community that holds difference without erasure is the community that refuses this demand.

The passengers on Moses Hunnecker's Grey Goose bus are not a community of shared identity. They are a collection of plural selves in transit, each carrying their own permanent grief and their own constructed reality, each appearing to the others in the partial visibility that the bus's shared space allows. What holds them together is not agreement. It is the shared condition of being in transit, of moving through a world that does not resolve, of carrying what they carry without the possibility of putting it down. The community of the bus is the community of witnesses to each other's plurality, not the community of people who have found their common ground.

Mikhail Bakhtin's theory of the dialogic offers a complementary framework. Bakhtin argues that meaning is not produced by a single voice speaking a single truth. It is produced in the encounter between voices, in the dialogue that occurs when different perspectives meet and respond to each other. "Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" by Marguerite Young is dialogic in Bakhtin's sense, but it pushes the principle further than Bakhtin's framework anticipates. The voices in Young's novel are not only the voices of different characters. They are the voices of different selves within a single character, the plural self in dialogue with its own plurality.

This is the seventh pillar's image: the Grey Goose bus as the model of community. Not the community that has arrived at its destination and agreed on what it found there. The community that is still in motion, still carrying its grief, still holding the difference of its members without demanding that they simplify.

Young’s work follows the margins from the weeds and the “old, blind, broken ass” of the 1930s poems, to “The Contravalid,” to the Ozark families camped at the edge of New Harmony, to Debs’s union of all workers “irrespective of race, color, or creed.” It also records what this chapter does not: the racial and other blind spots of Young’s period, present in every phase of her work, including the novel’s first page, where Black passengers are “beings unseen, merely sensed.” Her awareness of these limits is sharpest in the novel and the Debs book, where she names the failure to see.

Phase One, Childhood and the Early Poems (1908 to 1934)

Pillar 7: The Margins Reveal the Truth

The margins of this phase are Indiana's rural poor and old women: the women of Hawthorne Lane, the woman who chose china over bread, the farmer pulled from school who "tried to learn / The rules of English grammar by myself," the peddler woman, the hunter laughed at by his neighbors. These are the people the poems take seriously. No racial material appears yet.

Phase Two, The Chicago Years, Prismatic Ground, and the Graduate Essays (1935 to 1937)

Pillar 7: The Margins Reveal the Truth

Prismatic Ground turns firmly toward the edges. "To the Strangest Lady" brings freckled rocks and dried snakeskins to someone who "likes wind-ragged weeds and me; / She thinks both are beautiful." "Plea for Sodom" asks heaven to spare a small city "for the sake of birds... And the old, blind, broken ass." "Advertisement" asks for payment in "a rabbit's paw" and "a crow's caw-caw" instead of silver, an economy of worthless things. "Paradise Is Still Lost" finds the fallen angels among stool pigeons, addicts, and a woman "walking the windy streets." "Milton's Wife" shows the wife of a great poet shut away and forgotten, an early form of a judgment Young later made about utopias.

Complication: the same poems carry period stereotypes. "Paradise Is Still Lost" has "slant / dark eyes" and a "Chinese gentleman, heroin," and "Rosary in Discord" has the phrase "Return slant-eyed." The blind spots found later in the essays are present from Young's first book.

Phase Three, The Philosophical Poems (1938 to 1945)

Pillar 7: The Margins Reveal the Truth

"The Contravalid" (1945) states the pillar: "No schemata exclusive of the cripple and accident," and "The crippled dancer is the dance, at last, / The only truth, the double-headed dwarf of paradox." "That Chance" (1943): "There is no absolute... But deviation from the law / In lonely items of the real." A draft scorns logicians "Omitting salty spinsters by that sea." "The Long War" gives worth to "Whatever is small, whatever deceives." In "The Clinic," the condemned man might be "The highest good to which mankind attains." "The Priesthood of Motion" sees in an old Black man in the cotton fields a stillness that is "a deception / And an exodus from cotton fields."

Complication: the unpublished portraits of an old Black woman by the sea, "She was my mother then," feeding "a white child," turn love into the old type of the Black nurse.

Phase Four, "Angel in the Forest" and “Complex Life and Complex Letters” (1945 to 1946)

Pillar 7: The Margins Reveal the Truth

The Ozark families camp at the edge of town "like the lost children of Israel," and "God did never deliver these people out of any wilderness." Among a woman on a crutch and a grandfather with a "broken fiddle," the narrator finds "a feeling that man is in himself valuable, as man." Rapp's graveyard has no tombstones because he wanted no "individuality" carried into death, and Young saw the perfect society as one that shuts out "the fantastic, irrational, non-reasoning side of life."

Complication: Indigenous people appear mostly as the dead beneath the Rappite graves, described in the period's vocabulary of "papooses" and "squaws."

Phase Five, "Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" (written from the mid 1940s, published 1965)

Pillar 7: The Margins Reveal the Truth

The novel's company is the culture's dispossessed: the stone-deaf man, the blind veteran, the blind painter, the one-legged dancer (the "crippled dancer" of "The Contravalid," twenty years later), the bald and one-breasted nursemaid, the schizophrenic mother of dream children. The final voice is the deaf lover's: "we be the silences because we are all loves." A hotel landlady gives enforced sameness its own voice: "There are no Negroes in this county." Moore notes the book's following in the gay literary community.

Complication: the Black bus passengers on the first page are "beings unseen, merely sensed." But the narrator frames this as a failure of sight, "no one has ever seen the other person." Here Young names her own limit, which the essays and poems do not.

What the novel adds is a narrator who admits her own limit. The poems and essays look at the margins without asking what the one looking cannot see. The novel’s first page asks it.

Phase Six, The Occasional Prose, Interviews, and Self-Interpretation (1940s to 1995)

Pillar 7: The Margins Reveal the Truth

"Indiana has given birth to many individuals but to no types." "The World of Silence": "The tragedy of those born deaf is not that they do not hear but that others hear." The deaf apologized to her for being "in the minority," and she saw an affinity among "the blind, the deaf, the crippled," and "the Negro." "The Artist as Wanderer" reads the Wandering Jew as "a crime committed by all men who would kill the dreamer and the dream." She hoped "the lower elements will never be got rid of."

Complication: "The Midwest of Everywhere" mentions worms sold "in Negro shanty towns" as local color. "The Doll People" passes over "old mammy dolls with nut-brown faces." "The World of Silence" favors lip-reading in line with its time and includes a generalization about Jewish deaf and criminality. Her method of the "archetype" pulls against her rejection of types.

Phase Seven, "Harp Song for a Radical" (written in her last decades, published 1999)

Pillar 7: The Margins Reveal the Truth

Debs's union takes all workers "irrespective of race, color, or creed, excluding not even the least paint scraper or wheel wiper." On Chinese exclusion: "The souls of Chinese children are yellow butterflies." His fellow convicts' uncounted votes still have "value." The "working dead" are the book's center.

Complication: Kate Debs is satirized with her "nest egg" and "all her women's rights about her," and Mary Lincoln is "the squat black widow."
