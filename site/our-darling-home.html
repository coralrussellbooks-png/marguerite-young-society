Marguerite Young, Our Darling

Marguerite Young, Our Darling: What It Reveals, Part by Part

Marguerite Young, Our Darling · The Marguerite Young Society

A reading of the 1994 collection in seven parts, keeping what each tribute, essay and interview reveals about Young and her work.

Part 1

This covers the front matter, Fuchs's introduction, Martha J. Sattler's chronology, and the first tributes.

  • The shape of a life
  • How she worked
  • What the critics actually argue
  • The real people inside the fiction
  • In her own words

Part 2

Part 2 runs from the close of John J. Stephan's recollection through the tributes by Peter Marchant, Rob Wynne, Michael and Betty Higgins, Caroline Sandoz Pifer, Gail Cohen, LaVerne Harrell Clark, and Lee Harrod, and into Jean Petrolle's critical essay.

  • Her teaching and her aesthetic
  • The method and philosophy of the novel
  • Poetry and prose as one continuum
  • Influences and kinship
  • The world of the novel: voices and music
  • Reception, milieu, and later work

Part 3

Part 3 completes Jean Petrolle's essay, then gives Reinhard Friederich's account of rereading the novel, Erika Duncan's essay on Esther Longtree, and the opening of Miriam Fuchs's essay on Young's utopias.

  • Imagination makes reality, so fictions have stakes
  • How the novel is built, and how to read it
  • Esther Longtree and pregnancy as an epic subject
  • Utopia, loss, and the value of the incomplete
  • Against binary logic and the single self
  • Her own claim, and her place outside the canon

Part 4

Part 4 completes Miriam Fuchs's essay on Young's utopias and then gives three more scholarly essays: Lillian S. Robinson on narrative and redemption, Peter G. Christensen's William Jamesian reading of Angel in the Forest, and Charles Ruas on the epic imagination.

  • The utopian pattern, completed
  • A whole world made of stories
  • Politics, prophecy, and Angel in the Forest
  • How Young placed herself

Part 5

Part 5 gathers five scholarly essays on Miss MacIntosh, My Darling: Erika Duncan on the novel as elegy, Suzanne Oboler on the American dream, Martha J. Sattler on woman's experience, Fatima Shaik on the subconscious, and the opening of Susan Strehle on women's time.

  • An epic elegy for all creation
  • The American epic and its cultural reality
  • An epic of woman, and women's time
  • The poetry of the subconscious

Part 6

Part 6 completes Susan Strehle's essay on women's time, then gives two of the book's most theoretical pieces: Miriam Fuchs's “Liquescence as Form” and Steven Shaviro's “Exorbitance and Death.” Together they make the collection's strongest case for why Miss MacIntosh, My Darling matters and why the academy has resisted it.

  • Time without history
  • Liquescence as form
  • Feminine writing and the dispersal of patriarchy
  • Exorbitance, and why the academy resists it
  • A celebration of ordinary life

Part 7

This final part contains the two conversations that close the book, a long interview conducted by Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs on 23 March 1988 and a brief conversation with Fuchs at St. Vincent's Hospital on 1 June 1993, followed by the contributors' notes.

  • Everything she wrote is a search for utopia
  • The flour miller and the passage of the soul
  • Genre belongs to critics, and she is traditional, not experimental
  • “I don't make up anything”
  • Debs, Riley, and obsession as the engine
  • The rage against women, and her own neglect
  • Her closing self-definition
