Chapter 21: Let Bygones Be Bygones

← Chapter 20 · Guide Home · Chapter 22 →

Research notes: Chapter 21

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Symbols: Clothing, Hats, and Skirts

Philosophy: Augustine and Christian Theology · Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Chapter 21 is the last chapter of the birthday night sequence, and it does something no reader quite expects after chapters 17 through 20: it ends with comedy.

Not the dark comedy of the novel’s great surreal passages, but something lighter and more precise: the comedy of two people who have been through something enormous together, silently agreeing to say nothing about it, and returning to ordinary life with the particular relief of survivors.

Young earns this ending. Don’t be fooled into thinking it undoes what came before.

What actually happens in Chapter 21

Vera is still lying in Miss MacIntosh’s bed, still watching the bald head on the pillow, still composing her resolution to pretend. Young gives us one final extended description of Miss MacIntosh as she is in this moment: the gnarled hands and feet, the missing breast and its scar, the absent eyelashes and eyebrows, the woman’s clothes strewn in bundles on the floor, the “green hair ribbon looped over the bust of Martin Luther.” The Irish green ribbon on Luther. Young cannot help herself, and neither can we.

Then Vera falls back to sleep.

When she wakes, Miss MacIntosh is standing at the foot of the bed, fully dressed and wigged, her red hair shining, her silver-dollar watch blotted out by sunlight, in a remarkably good humor. The bedroom is neat. Nothing has changed. The pact is already in effect without a word being spoken.

Miss MacIntosh says Vera has “been very sick and unusually stubborn, but” has recovered. She scolds her mildly for dancing when there was no partner at the birthday party. She holds up the party dress Mr. Spitzer had sent (that filmy, floating creation) and says it cannot be mended and must be thrown away. “Into the ash bin it goes.”

The second inventory

Before Vera falls asleep, Young gives us one last full look at the body as it is in morning light, and it is the most complete description in the sequence. The stalk of a breast “cut away so early, the scar, the tissues like a baby’s fist.” “No eyebrows, no eyelashes, no single hair or feather, the hairless body shining in the” sound of surf. The “gnarled hands with veins knotted by old labors.” The “brassiere with a wad of cloth” concealing what is missing.

And in the middle of this: the pity, pity, pity. Young uses the word three times in close succession, and it is doing the work that nothing else in the sequence could do. Not grief, not outrage, not the rage of a child who has been deceived. Pity. The feeling that arrives when you understand something about another person’s life that they never chose to show you, that they have carried alone for years, that is simply larger than any response you have.

This is what Vera carries forward from this night. Not the shock, not the fight, not even the decision. The pity that is not the end but the beginning, only the beginning, of understanding another person.

Miss MacIntosh returns

The scene of Miss MacIntosh standing at the foot of the bed, restored, is one of the most carefully handled moments in the novel. Young gives us her return in full detail: the green hair ribbon, the pin-striped shirtwaist, the turtleneck “sweater with holes at the elbows,” the “faded woolen scarf pinned with a large black safety pin.” Every piece of the assembly back in its place.

And Vera’s response: she “looked at her without the slightest astonishment, completely forgetful that she had ever been different than now she seemed.” Not pretending to forget. Actually, momentarily, forgetting. The vision of the night slipping under the surface of the ordinary morning like something covered by a wave.

This is what Young has been building toward across five chapters. Not a confrontation, not a confession, not a reckoning. Two people standing in a bedroom in the morning, one of them asking what Mr. Spitzer was thinking when he sent that party dress, and neither of them saying anything about the bald head on the pillow an hour ago. The pact holds. The ordinary day begins.

The party dress

The chapter ends on the party dress, and Young gives it real weight. Mr. Spitzer sent it in a long white box. “It was a filmy, floating creation,” cloudlike. Vera was supposed to wear white roses in her hair. She “should have worn white roses in” her hair.

It cannot be mended. We should only tear it further. Into the ash bin.

The dress is the fourteenth birthday itself: the party that had no guests, the night that went so far from what it was supposed to be. Miss MacIntosh holds it up to the light and finds no hole in it, no seed pearl out of place, all the little ribbons intact, every rosette of yellowed lace exactly as it was when it came out of the box. The dress survived the night intact. The night did not.

Miss MacIntosh’s verdict is cheerful and final: it cannot be mended. Throw it away. There will be no new one. This is Miss MacIntosh at her most characteristic: pragmatic, slightly bleak, refusing to pretend that the unrepairable can be repaired. She has been making this assessment all her life, about things rather more significant than party dresses.

It is also, read against the night, the novel’s most compressed statement about what has happened: the dress is fine, but it cannot be worn again. The occasion is over. “Let bygones be bygones. We shall turn over a new leaf.”

What to watch for as you keep reading

The Miss MacIntosh sequence, chapters 13 through 21, is complete. The novel will not return to this night directly, though it will return to Miss MacIntosh constantly, in memory, in Vera’s search, in the questions the novel keeps circling. You now have the full picture. Everything else is aftermath and extension.

Notice, from here forward, how often other characters in the novel are described in terms that echo Miss MacIntosh’s situation: the disguised, the concealed, the person whose outer life is a performance built over a private reality. Young is going to show you this pattern in many different lives. You are now prepared to see it.

The “green hair ribbon looped over the bust of Martin Luther” will stay with you. It should. It is one of those small, exact, irreducible images that Young places in a scene and lets stand without comment. The ornament of the woman’s secret self (the Irish green, the softness of a ribbon) resting on the shoulders of the man who said here I stand, I can do no other. Young does not explain this. She never explains these. You carry them.
