The religious question underneath

These three chapters quote no scripture, but heaven, hell, the soul, and immortality all come up in Mr. Spitzer’s own private terms, and the word resurrected appears once. They are where he comes nearest to the question Chapter 29 states openly: whether anything rises after death. Chapter 29 then gives it the language of the Bible: Joseph’s coat, Aaron’s breastplate, the Word made Flesh.

Chapter 26: The Department of the Dead

Mr. Spitzer’s law practice is a ministry to the dead: wills, deeds, archives, lost heirs. The two Boston spinsters who each believe they are the reincarnation of Cleopatra, one leaving her fortune to Siamese cats under his supervision, are a comic version of immortality secured by legal instrument. Even the old caretaker is “like an old mummified pharaoh,” Egypt’s answer to death.

Mr. Spitzer states his theology plainly here. He can believe in “no hell more than this present individual life,” and he is not sure heaven is a place at all rather than a state of mind in which we have forgotten who we were. He has watched, as Peron never would, a dying soul pass from this world to the next, a next world he still believes is only coldness and darkness. Yet he speaks of his offer to Catherine as an “ideal and incorruptible marriage,” an immortal happiness too great for this mortal life. The language is Paul’s, from 1 Corinthians 15: the corruptible putting on incorruption, the mortal putting on immortality. Mr. Spitzer keeps the vocabulary of resurrection for a love, not a body.

The chapter ends on his thought that to reach the upper world one must first travel through the lower. That is the shape of the descent stories behind the whole book. In Book Six of the Aeneid, the Sibyl warns that going down to the underworld is easy and that climbing back is the real task, hoc opus, hic labor est, the words Joachim takes as his motto in Chapter 55. In Christian tradition the same shape belongs to the creed, the descent into hell before the rising on the third day.

Chapter 27: The Two Watches

The only resurrection in this chapter belongs to Catherine, who claims to have resurrected the phantasmagoric facts of Mr. Spitzer’s life. He, meanwhile, carries calling cards and “an out-dated passport for a journey he had not taken,” so that if he is ever found, even with his face altered, he will be recognized. The journey he has not taken is death, and the papers are his provision for being known as himself on the other side of it. Belief in resurrection has always raised the question of which self will rise, and Mr. Spitzer is no longer sure which self he is.

His two watches, Peron’s running and his own stopped near the hour of Peron’s death, put him in two times at once: ordinary time that keeps moving, and a stopped time in which the death is always happening. Peron, he remembers, was “impatient with the idea of eternity.” Mr. Spitzer uses Peron’s watch to time his own “eternal music.”

A stranger once asked him on a crowded street whether he wanted to die. Mr. Spitzer said no while shaking his head yes, and the stranger led him safely through the traffic. The stranger plays the part of the unrecognized guide, a figure that runs from the Book of Tobit to the road to Emmaus.

Chapter 28: Impalpable Butterflies

The butterfly has stood for the soul since the Greeks, who used one word, psyche, for both, and Christian art took it up as an emblem of the resurrection: the caterpillar as earthly life, the chrysalis as the tomb, the winged creature as the body raised. Young makes the link herself. Peron had no patience “to wait for the caterpillar to become the butterfly,” and gave no thought to a future life, while Mr. Spitzer thought only of “the impossible future.” After Peron’s death he gave up his butterfly collection, as if he could no longer bear an emblem of rising. His butterflies, when he remembers them, are “spirit messengers,” which is what the word angel means.

The chapter’s image of creation, a flame said to sleep in the eternal darkness, “weaving our bodies, weaving our separate souls,” recalls Psalm 139, where the body is woven in secret in the depths. And his observation that anyone called away at the crucial moment could never tell whether they had seen a birth or a death is the chapter’s central religious thought. From outside, emergence and dying look alike, and only faith says which one happened.

He admits, too, that he may be only one of Catherine’s hallucinations, truly alive only in her room in the evenings. A man who exists in someone else’s dreaming is a dark inversion of the believer who lives in God’s sight.
