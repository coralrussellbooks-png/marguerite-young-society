Chapter 72: Esther Longtree

← Chapter 71 · Guide Home · Chapter 73 →

Content note: The Esther Longtree chapters (72 to 81) deal with stillbirth and pregnancy loss, rape, infanticide, racist violence including lynching, and cruelty to children. This chapter centers on pregnancy and stillbirth.

Research notes: Chapter 72

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Jacqueline White · Justice O’Leary · The Chicago Detective · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Identity and the Self · Motherhood, Pregnancy, and Birth

Symbols: Birds · Pregnancy, Birth, and Children · Shadows

Philosophy: Hinduism

Allusions: Judaism · Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery · The Bible

She is the one Dr. O’Leary told you about through the wall in Chapter 69: the woman who believes herself always pregnant, who suffers miscarriage after miscarriage, whose condition baffled him and broke him and whose name, if he ever said it, you may have missed in the dark.

Her name is Esther Longtree. She is the waitress at the Greasy Spoon. She has “a bald spot shaped like a large coin” on her head, long black hair that flows loose at whatever cost to sanitation, ponderous red lips, a deformed foot, and a belly that has been swollen for as long as anyone in town can remember. She is perpetually pregnant. She has never carried a living child to term.

She is one of the great figures in this novel, and this chapter is hers entirely.

If you have been reading along, you already know something about what Esther represents: the woman always waiting, always gestating, always on the verge of the thing that never arrives. She was introduced as a rumor: a medical curiosity, the doctor’s strangest case, the woman no one quite understands. Now you meet her in full, and she turns out to be something larger than a case history. She is vast with grief and hope and philosophy and humor, enormous with the children she has lost and the one she still believes is coming. Chapter 72 is Esther’s monologue, Esther’s meditation, Esther’s world. Read it slowly.

The Setting: The Greasy Spoon

It is early spring, foggy, the morning after Vera’s sleepless night at the Tavern. The town is described in its morning configuration: an undertaker’s hearse, ghostly old automobiles, a grey wagon pulled by a bony mule. Between the undertaker and the beauty parlor, between death’s preparation and beauty’s, stands the Greasy Spoon, where Esther works.

She took the job from Jacqueline White, the previous waitress, who got sick. She is the passive policeman’s only daughter. Her parents think she is an old maid, a failure, a puzzle they cannot read. They tease her. They do not know what she carries. The Greasy Spoon is both a restaurant and a symbol. It is where ordinary life gets fed (truck drivers, salesmen, travelers) and where Esther has spent years standing between the living and the dead, serving food, carrying children no one ever sees born. The hollyhocks painted on the cracked walls reach toward the ceiling, straining upward against the decay around them. Young gives Esther exactly this setting: life insisting on itself in a place given over to grease and impermanence.

Esther Herself

She is described with a physicality Young lavishes on very few characters: plum-colored and square, ponderous red lips, breasts like milky breakers, hair she tosses freely. She has morning sickness and evening sickness. Her belly is enormous. She has a bald spot, a deformed foot, and she laughs uproariously at things others find distressing. She talks to herself. She talks to the unborn child. She talks to the stillborn children who haunt her, the ones she has buried under leaves and snow and in rivers and who come back to her in whispers. She is “brilliant but dumb,” “ugly outside but beautiful inside,” “experienced and sought after” yet also an old maid in her parents’ estimation. She keeps lovebirds. She knits without thread: a gesture of preparation for a child that may not come, or of faith in the act of preparation itself, regardless of outcome. She has made “a little black suit for the” child she believes will survive. She has prepared layettes. She is ready. She is not deluded in the way Catherine is deluded, or Dr. O’Leary. Her mind, Young tells us, is clear and luminous. She knows exactly what has happened to her: she has carried as many children as autumn leaves, and none of them lived, and she is pregnant again, and she believes this one might be different. This is not confusion. This is hope maintained in the full knowledge of its own history. It is one of the bravest things in the novel.

The Little Shadow Boxer

This is what Esther calls the child she is carrying now: “the little shadow boxer who in the waters of her womb” has grown and grown and has warded off every blow. She can feel the little shadow foot playing against her side. She can feel the heartbeat accelerating under her heart. Two hearts, she says: mine and his.

A shadow boxer fights an opponent who is not there. The fight is real; the opponent is invisible. This is precisely Esther’s situation: she is fighting for a life against the force that has taken every other life she has carried, a force she cannot see or name or locate. The child is fighting too, from inside, against the same invisible opponent. The image is also tender in a way Young rarely allows herself to be this directly. The little shadow foot against Esther’s side. The heartbeat. Two hearts. For all the novel’s philosophical and mythological weight, this is simply a woman feeling her baby move and loving it with the particular love of someone who knows she may lose it.

The Fathers

Esther has had many fathers for her children. They are almost all salesmen, which in this era means traveling men, men who pass through: the piano-tuner, the “horn-blower, the tin roofer, the soap salesman,” the rubber salesman, the featherweight boxer, the Chicago detective. They come through town, they stay a night or two at the diner or the hotel, and then they are gone. They are, almost without exception, sterile. Not always in the clinical sense (the pregnancies are real, the children are real) but in some deeper way that Young does not quite medicalize: the children of these men do not survive. They are born still. They haunt Esther. Some of the fathers write years later “asking if the child was ever born,” unaware or unwilling to accept that it was not, that it never will be. There is one exception in this chapter: a sad, stout drummer, a Fuller Brush salesman, who comes in crying. His grief is not about Esther’s children. His wife’s sister’s little girl died of scarlet fever, and he cannot recover from it. He abandoned his business, his family, his life, and now he wanders the roads selling brushes and carrying a bag of candy for a child who is dead and will never eat it. He sits at the counter, weeping. Esther serves him. He is the saddest man in the chapter, and possibly the most honest: he knows exactly what he has lost and cannot stop carrying it. The traveling salesmen collectively are one of Young’s sharpest observations about American masculinity: men in motion, men who sell things, men who pass through women’s lives and disappear before the consequences arrive. They represent, in aggregate, the absent father writ American: not villainous, not deliberate, simply gone.

Dr. O’Leary’s Verdict

Dr. O’Leary, whom you met in Chapters 69 and 71 in his dissolving state, turns out to have encountered Esther before his mind gave way. He came into the diner to get a drink (a nickel, which dates the encounter to sometime between 1930 and 1959) and Esther asked him about her condition. He told her she was “the immortal medical case.” He told her she would never die in childbirth; her labor would be easy, like passing a seed. He told her the problem was atmospheric: the unusual pressure, the thinning ether, the whirling stars. He told her the problem would end with her and would “not be seen again upon the earth.”

This is both the most and least helpful medical opinion Esther has ever received. It offers her everlasting hope. It explains nothing. It attributes her condition to forces beyond anyone’s control or comprehension and then reassures her that she will survive what it will not explain. This is Dr. O’Leary at his most compassionate and his most useless, which is to say, at his most human.

His attribution of Esther’s condition to “atmospheric pressure” and “thinning ether” is also, in its way, Young’s. The novel treats Esther’s pregnancy as operating on a cosmic scale: stars, moon, wind, the Milky Way. She is not merely a woman with a medical condition. She is a woman whose body is in conversation with forces so large they dwarf medicine.

Who Killed Cock Robin?

Near the chapter’s end, Esther poses the question from the old English nursery rhyme: “Who killed Cock Robin?” The rhyme is ancient, probably older than its first written records, and no one is certain what it originally meant; it may involve Celtic sun gods, may involve real historical figures, may be simply the nursery-rhyme recording of a universal truth about death. In the rhyme, everyone takes responsibility and no one is wholly to blame: I, said the sparrow, with my bow and arrow; I, said the fly, with my little eye.

Esther’s answer: nobody. The child “was killed by the death that was in him from the time of his birth,” by “the wind, the night, the day, the” atmospheric pressure. No one is solely responsible. Death is a universal and impersonal force that operates through all things simultaneously. This is not consolation exactly, it does not bring the children back, but it is a kind of philosophical peace: she did not kill them. The sterile fathers did not kill them. The cold cosmos killed them, impartially, as it kills everything eventually. She also says: “she was soon to have a sweet, sweet little baby that would cry in her arms and that would kill them all.” The living child (if it comes, when it comes) will supersede the dead. Will put to rest the haunting whispers of the stillborn. Will end the reign of ghosts with a cry. This is Esther’s defiant hope, stated simply, as fact. She is preparing for it. She has knitted the little black suit. She is ready.

The Stillborn

They are “as many as the autumn leaves.” They are under leaves, under snow, in rivers, in the earth. They whisper to her. They cry. They are not gone in any simple sense: they persist in Esther’s consciousness the way the dead persist throughout this novel, as presences that refuse to complete their departure. Young treats them with extraordinary delicacy. They are real, Esther has buried them with her own hands, and they are also luminous abstractions: snowflakes, stars, shadows. The novel refuses to let them be merely medical events, failures of physiology. They are children who existed and were lost, and their mother remembers every one of them, and they have come back to haunt her not with malice but with the simple fact of their having been.

This is one of the places where Young’s insistence that everything real is real, “even the unreal things were real”, carries its full weight. The stillborn are not real in the way the living are real. But they are not nothing. Esther has carried them, buried them, grieved them, and is still pregnant with all of them in the only sense that counts: she has not stopped loving them or waiting for the one who will live.

What to Hold Onto

“The little shadow boxer, the little” shadow foot against Esther’s side. Two hearts: mine and his. Keep this.

The bag of candy the sad Fuller Brush salesman carries for a child who is dead. He wanders the roads with it. He cannot put it down.

Esther knitting without thread. The act of preparation as an act of faith, regardless of outcome.

Dr. O’Leary’s diagnosis: atmospheric. The thinning ether. The whirling stars. The problem will end with her and will “not be seen again upon the earth.” And Esther’s answer to “Who killed Cock Robin?”: nobody. And: soon there will be “a sweet, sweet little baby that” will cry in her arms and kill them all.

She is the passive policeman’s only daughter. She is the immortal medical case. She is always pregnant. She is luminous. She is ready.
