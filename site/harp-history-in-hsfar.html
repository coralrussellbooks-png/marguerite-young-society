The History in Harp Song for a Radical

Harp Song for a Radical · The Marguerite Young Society · The History Companion

Harp Song for a Radical covers roughly the 1840s through the Great Railroad Strike of 1877, with flashes forward to Pullman, Haymarket, and Debs in prison. Young's method is to follow any thread that fed the world Debs was born into. So a chapter about Terre Haute can open onto a German tailor-communist in Iowa, a Mormon farmer mortgaging his land, or a Russian novelist facing a firing squad. What looks like digression is her argument: American radicalism came out of this tangle of utopian dreams, immigrant politics, war, and industrial violence. Below, the history is sorted into its main threads, with the context Young often assumes.

The utopian inheritance

Before there was a labor movement, there were communities that tried to live out a better world. Indiana was one of their main laboratories, which is partly why Young, a Hoosier herself, returns to them so often.

Father George Rapp led the Harmony Society, German Pietists who believed Christ's return was near. They practiced celibacy, held property in common, and were extremely productive farmers and manufacturers. They founded Harmony, Pennsylvania (1804), moved to New Harmony, Indiana (1814), then sold the whole town in 1825 and returned to Pennsylvania. The society lingered until 1905.

The buyer was Robert Owen, a Welsh mill owner who had run a model factory village at New Lanark, Scotland. His New Harmony (1825 to 1827) was secular and rationalist, meant to prove that people shaped by good conditions would cooperate. It collapsed within two years. But it drew scientists and educators by keelboat (the "Boatload of Knowledge," 1826), and Owen's son Robert Dale Owen later served Indiana in Congress. The younger Owen helped found the Smithsonian and pushed for married women's property rights in Indiana's 1850 constitution. Young wrote a whole earlier book, Angel in the Forest, on these two New Harmonys.

Frances Wright was a Scottish heiress, friend of Lafayette and a protégé of Jeremy Bentham. In 1825 she founded Nashoba, near Memphis, where enslaved people were to earn their freedom through labor and education. (Young places it on the Fox River and in 1827; most histories say the Wolf River and 1825.) The project failed, and in 1830 Wright took its enslaved residents to Haiti and freed them. She went on to become one of the first women in America to lecture publicly to mixed audiences, speaking for free thought, workers' rights, and birth control.

Étienne Cabet was a French communist whose novel Voyage to Icaria (1840) inspired a real emigration. His followers tried Texas in 1848, where fever wrecked the settlement. In 1849 they took over Nauvoo, Illinois, the town the Mormons had just abandoned. The group split in 1856, Cabet was expelled, and he died in St. Louis that year. Icarian offshoots survived in Iowa until 1898.

Zoar and Ebenezer, which Young mentions through Weitling's eyes, were German religious communes. Zoar, Ohio (1817 to 1898) was led by Joseph Bimeler. The Ebenezer community of New York moved to Iowa in 1855 and became the Amana Colonies, which stayed communal until 1932. The appliance company still carries the name.

Charles Fourier was a French theorist who planned society around cooperative units called phalanxes. His ideas swept America in the 1840s through Albert Brisbane and Horace Greeley's New York Tribune, and dozens of phalanxes were founded, including Brook Farm in its later years. The same ideas got Fyodor Dostoyevsky arrested in 1849 for belonging to a Fourierist discussion circle in St. Petersburg. He was marched out for execution, reprieved at the last moment, and sent to a Siberian prison camp, the basis for The House of the Dead. Young uses that scene to foreshadow Debs's own imprisonments, and she names The House of the Dead as one of the books Debs cared about.

The Mormons enter as another American millennial experiment. Martin Harris was the prosperous New York farmer who financed the first printing of the Book of Mormon in 1830 by mortgaging his farm. He also lost the first 116 manuscript pages after borrowing them to show his skeptical wife. He, Oliver Cowdery, and David Whitmer were the "Three Witnesses" to the golden plates. Brigham Young then led the migration to Utah, where the church experimented with cooperative economics. Young also brings in the Shakers (Ann Lee) and the Oneida Community (John Humphrey Noyes) as part of the same restless search.

The German radicals after 1848

When the European revolutions of 1848 failed, thousands of radicals fled to America. They became known as Forty-Eighters, and they brought socialism into American cities decades before most native-born workers heard the word.

Heinrich Heine, the German poet exiled in Paris and a friend of Marx, never came to America. Young uses him as the lyrical conscience of that European generation.

Wilhelm Weitling gets more pages than almost anyone except Debs. He was a German tailor, largely self-taught, and one of the first working-class communists. His books, especially The Gospel of a Poor Sinner, mixed communism with Christian feeling, and he clashed bitterly with Marx in 1846. In New York he edited Die Republik der Arbeiter (The Workers' Republic) from 1850 to 1855 and organized German workers' leagues. He also tied his hopes to Communia, a colony in Clayton County, Iowa, which collapsed in debt and lawsuits by the mid-1850s. He died in New York in 1871, largely forgotten. His story matters because it shows a socialism in America that was religious, utopian, and immigrant long before the Marxist version took hold.

August Willich was a Prussian officer who turned communist, fought in the 1849 Baden uprising, and broke with Marx in 1850. In America he became a Union general and commanded the 32nd Indiana, a German regiment. Joseph Weydemeyer, another Marx ally, also served as a Union officer. Friedrich Sorge, in Hoboken, ran the International Workingmen's Association after its headquarters moved to New York in 1872.

Indiana during the Civil War

This is some of the least-taught history in the book. Southern Indiana was deeply split, and the Ohio River connected more than it divided.

The Knights of the Golden Circle began as a pro-slavery society in the 1850s that dreamed of a slave empire around the Gulf of Mexico. During the war, it and related groups (the Order of American Knights, the Sons of Liberty) became networks of Northern Copperheads, Democrats opposed to the war, some plotting armed uprisings in the Midwest.

Lambdin P. Milligan was an Indiana lawyer and Copperhead who was arrested in 1864. A military commission, not a civilian court, sentenced him to hang. In Ex parte Milligan (1866), the Supreme Court ruled that civilians cannot be tried by military tribunals where civilian courts are functioning. It remains a landmark of civil liberties. Milligan later sued the officials involved and won five dollars in damages. The opposing lawyer was Benjamin Harrison, future president, which is the connection Young draws.

Oliver P. Morton, Indiana's wartime governor, faced a Democratic legislature that refused to pass a budget in 1863. He ran the state for two years on private loans and federal money, arguably outside the law. The press-smashing Young describes in Terre Haute belongs to this period.

Young also looks past the war's edges. At Sand Creek, Colorado, on November 29, 1864, militia under Colonel John Chivington attacked the peaceful Cheyenne and Arapaho camp of Black Kettle and killed well over a hundred people, mostly women, children, and the elderly. Black Kettle survived, only to be killed by Custer's troops at the Washita in 1868. In Mexico, France installed Maximilian as emperor while the United States was busy with its own war. He was executed in 1867, and his wife Carlotta lived out her life in madness in Europe.

Railroads, money, and the Gilded Age

Major George Washington Whistler was an American railroad engineer who built the St. Petersburg to Moscow railway for the czar with serf labor, and who had earlier worked on the Baltimore & Ohio. He was the father of the painter James McNeill Whistler, and of course the husband of the woman in Whistler's Mother. Young uses him to link American railroads with Russian autocracy.

Tom Scott, president of the Pennsylvania Railroad, was one of the most powerful men in the country. He is widely credited, though the quote may be legend, with saying strikers should get "a rifle diet." His railroad interests are also tied by historians to the backroom deal that settled the 1876 election.

Amasa Stone was a Cleveland industrialist. The Ashtabula bridge he designed collapsed under a passenger train in December 1876, killing about 92 people, and he took his own life in 1883. His son-in-law John Hay, Lincoln's former secretary, later wrote The Bread-Winners (1883), an anti-union novel shaped partly by the 1877 strike.

The Panic of 1873, set off by the failure of Jay Cooke's bank, began a depression that lasted until 1879. It is the economic backdrop for nearly everything in the book's second half. Charley Ross, the four-year-old kidnapped from Germantown, Pennsylvania, in 1874, appears as period texture. His was the first widely publicized ransom kidnapping in America, and he was never found.

Labor's hard decade

The Tompkins Square Riot took place on January 13, 1874. Thousands of unemployed workers gathered in Manhattan to demand public works jobs. The police permit was revoked without proper notice, and mounted and foot police clubbed the crowd. A young cigar maker named Samuel Gompers escaped by jumping into a cellarway. He later said the day shaped his cautious approach to labor organizing, and he went on to lead the American Federation of Labor.

The Molly Maguires were Irish miners in the Pennsylvania anthracite region, allegedly a secret society that used violence against mine bosses. The central figure against them was Franklin B. Gowen, president of the Philadelphia and Reading Railroad and its coal company. He hired Allan Pinkerton's agency, whose detective James McParlan went undercover as "James McKenna" inside the Ancient Order of Hibernians from 1873 to 1875. At the trials, Gowen himself served as a special prosecutor, and the juries excluded Irish Catholics. Twenty men were hanged between 1877 and 1879, ten of them on June 21, 1877, remembered in the region as the Day of the Rope. Jack Kehoe, the tavern keeper Young mentions, was hanged in 1878 and granted a posthumous pardon by Pennsylvania in 1979. Historians still argue over how much of the conspiracy was real, but the destruction of the miners' union in the Long Strike of 1875 is not in dispute.

Allan Pinkerton was himself once a radical. He was a Scottish Chartist, a supporter of workers' voting rights, who fled Glasgow in 1842 and became a barrel maker in Dundee, Illinois. He ran an Underground Railroad station, helped John Brown, and guarded Lincoln on the way to his first inauguration. His agency's logo was an open eye with the motto "We Never Sleep," the source of both Young's refrain "The Eye That Never Sleeps" and the phrase "private eye." His turn from Chartist to strikebreaker is one of the book's great ironies.

The Great Railroad Strike of 1877 was the first national strike in American history. It began on July 16 at Martinsburg, West Virginia, after the B&O cut wages again, and spread along the rail lines within days. In Pittsburgh, militia fired into a crowd, killing around twenty. The crowd then burned the Pennsylvania Railroad's yards, destroying over a hundred locomotives and more than a thousand cars. St. Louis saw a brief general strike run by the Workingmen's Party, sometimes called the St. Louis Commune. President Hayes sent federal troops, the same troops just withdrawn from the South under the Compromise of 1877 that ended Reconstruction. Roughly a hundred people died nationwide. In Terre Haute, Debs, then a young officer of the firemen's union, did not back the strike, a stance he would later outgrow. Federal Judge Walter Q. Gresham in Indianapolis used his authority over bankrupt railroads to jail strikers for contempt, an early version of the injunction later used against Debs in 1894.

Indiana politics in 1876 also get Young's comic treatment. The Republican candidate for governor, Godlove Orth, withdrew under scandal. The Democrat, James "Blue Jeans" Williams, was a six-foot-four farmer who campaigned in homespun and won.

Debs's Terre Haute circle

The Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen was founded in 1873 at Port Jervis, New York, originally more as an insurance and temperance society than a fighting union. Debs was a charter member of its Terre Haute lodge in 1875 and became editor of its magazine in 1880.

The Occidental Literary Club was where Debs gave early speeches and used his connections to bring national figures to town. They included Robert Ingersoll, the "Great Agnostic" and the most famous orator of the age; Wendell Phillips, the abolitionist who turned to labor reform after the war; and Susan B. Anthony, whom Debs hosted when much of Terre Haute shunned her.

Ida Husted Harper was Debs's school friend and ran the women's department of the firemen's magazine. She later became Susan B. Anthony's authorized biographer and co-wrote the last volumes of the History of Woman Suffrage. James Whitcomb Riley, the Hoosier poet of "Little Orphant Annie," and the humorist Bill Nye were close friends of Debs. The Reverend Myron Reed was a former Grand Banks fisherman turned Denver minister who became one of Debs's closest allies. Herman Hulman was a German-born Terre Haute grocer. His family's Clabber Girl baking powder fortune later bought the Indianapolis Motor Speedway.

Glimpses forward

Young repeatedly foreshadows events past 1877. In the Haymarket affair of 1886, a bomb at a Chicago labor rally led to the conviction of eight anarchists on thin evidence and four hangings. After the Preparedness Day bombing in San Francisco in 1916, labor organizer Tom Mooney was convicted on perjured testimony and spent over two decades in prison before his pardon in 1939. Coxey's Army was the 1894 march of the unemployed on Washington. The Pullman strike of 1894 put Debs in jail and was the chapter Young was writing when she fell ill.

Why this history feels hidden

Most of these stories fall between the categories schools use. Utopian communities get filed under religion or curiosities, Copperheads under Civil War footnotes, and the Molly Maguires and 1877 under "labor unrest," if they appear at all. Young's point is that they belong together: the same country produced New Harmony and the Pittsburgh roundhouse fire, and Debs was shaped by both the dream and the violence.

For reading in the same spirit as Zinn, several books go further on these threads. Jeremy Brecher's Strike! tells labor history from below. Robert V. Bruce's 1877: Year of Violence is still the fullest account of the Great Strike. Kevin Kenny's Making Sense of the Molly Maguires untangles myth from record. Carl Wittke's The Utopian Communist is the standard life of Weitling. Frank Morn's history of the Pinkertons borrows Young's own phrase for its title, The Eye That Never Sleeps. Frank Klement's Dark Lanterns examines the Copperhead secret societies. Nick Salvatore's Eugene V. Debs: Citizen and Socialist is the best conventional biography to set beside Young's lyrical one.

The book has no titled chapters. It runs in two parts (Part One, "Prelude in a Golden Key," and Part Two, "Harp Song for a Radical," beginning on page 179) made of roughly 170 short numbered sections, many only a few pages long. In the uploaded scan, many section numbers are too garbled to track reliably. So this plan groups the sections into 21 units by page range (using the 1999 Knopf first edition pagination), each built around a subject Young stays with for a stretch.

Unit 1, pp. 1-16: The prelude from prison

Entries for this unit: History Companion, Unit 1

Young opens at the end of Debs's life, then circles back.

  • The Espionage Act (1917) and its 1918 amendments, known as the Sedition Act, criminalized speech against the war. Debs was convicted under them for his June 1918 antiwar speech in Canton, Ohio.

  • Woodrow Wilson refused to pardon Debs. Norman Thomas was a Presbyterian minister and six-time Socialist presidential candidate, who later preached Debs's funeral sermon. Carl Sandburg was a Socialist Party organizer in Wisconsin before his fame as a poet.

  • Tom Mooney and the Preparedness Day bombing: a bomb at a pro-war parade in San Francisco on July 22, 1916 killed ten people. Mooney, a labor organizer, was convicted on perjured testimony and pardoned in 1939.

  • The Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) was the first federal law to bar immigration by nationality.

  • James Whitcomb Riley, the Hoosier poet and Debs's friend, appears here and throughout.

Unit 2, pp. 17-32: Colmar, and the dream of reason

Entries for this unit: History Companion, Unit 2

  • Daniel Debs and Marguerite Marie Bettrich ("Daisy") were Alsatians from Colmar. Daniel arrived in 1849 and they settled in Terre Haute in 1855.

  • Eugène Sue was a French novelist of the Paris poor, and Victor Hugo wrote Les Misérables. Debs was named for both.

  • Napoleon and Czar Alexander I stand for the Europe of empire and the post-1815 Holy Alliance of monarchs against revolution.

  • Jeremy Bentham, the English philosopher of utility, and Thomas Jefferson at Monticello stand for Enlightenment reason.

  • Robert Owen and Charles Fourier are the two great utopian planners, and Walt Whitman is the American poet of democracy. Young introduces all three as the book's intellectual ancestors.

Unit 3, pp. 33-48: Weitling's youth and Heine

Entries for this unit: History Companion, Unit 3

  • Wilhelm Weitling was born in Magdeburg in 1808. He was a journeyman tailor who became the leading voice of the League of the Just, the secret German workers' society that later became Marx's Communist League. He was arrested in Zurich in 1843, and the prison poems Young quotes date from that time.

  • Heinrich Heine was a German-Jewish poet in exile in Paris. His Germany: A Winter's Tale (1844) satirized Prussia.

  • Mathilde was Heine's French wife. Uncle Salomon was his wealthy Hamburg banker uncle, who supported him grudgingly.

Unit 4, pp. 49-80: Paris, Brussels, Marx

Entries for this unit: History Companion, Unit 4

  • Ludwig Börne was a radical German journalist in Paris and Heine's rival.

  • Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels: Marx came from Trier, and Engels managed his family's cotton mill in Manchester. Eleanor Marx, Marx's daughter, later toured America as a labor speaker (1886).

  • The 1846 Brussels confrontation: Marx attacked Weitling's moral, prophetic communism as unscientific, a split between two kinds of socialism that runs through the whole book.

  • Joseph Weydemeyer was Marx's ally who emigrated to New York and first published Marx's Eighteenth Brumaire there in 1852.

  • John Jacob Astor, the richest man in America, appears as the New York money power Weitling found on arrival.

Unit 5, pp. 81-104: Weitling in America, and the communes

Entries for this unit: History Companion, Unit 5

  • The Arbeiterbund (Workers' League) and Die Republik der Arbeiter were Weitling's New York organization and newspaper, 1850 to 1855. German workers' networks in Cincinnati, Milwaukee, Sandusky, and Dubuque were its base.

  • Communia was a communal colony in Clayton County, Iowa. Weitling tied his hopes to it, and it failed in debt and lawsuits by the mid-1850s.

  • The Oneida Community (1848 to 1881) was John Humphrey Noyes's perfectionist commune in New York.

  • Étienne Cabet and the Icarians: a disastrous Texas settlement in 1848, then Nauvoo, Illinois, from 1849.

  • Zoar, Ohio was a German Separatist commune (1817 to 1898) under Joseph Bimeler. Ebenezer became the Amana Colonies of Iowa.

Unit 6, pp. 105-120: The Rappites and the roots of Mormonism

Entries for this unit: History Companion, Unit 6

  • George Rapp's Harmony Society moved from Harmony, Pennsylvania, to New Harmony, Indiana (1814), then to Economy, Pennsylvania (1824).

  • Joseph Smith's early life as a treasure seeker in upstate New York included folk magic, seer stones, and tales of Captain Kidd's buried gold. Young links this to the angel Moroni and the Urim and Thummim, the seeing instruments Smith said came with the golden plates.

Unit 7, pp. 121-152: The Book of Mormon to Salt Lake

Entries for this unit: History Companion, Unit 7

  • Martin Harris was the Palmyra farmer who mortgaged his farm to pay for the 1830 printing and lost the first 116 manuscript pages.

  • Oliver Cowdery, David Whitmer, and Peter Whitmer: the church was formally organized in 1830 in Fayette, Seneca County, New York, at the Whitmer farm.

  • Brigham Young led the exodus to Utah (1846 to 1847). The Lion House was his Salt Lake residence for his plural family.

  • Mark Twain's Roughing It (1872) recounts his 1861 trip west with his brother Orion Clemens, secretary of Nevada Territory, including a visit to Salt Lake. William Dean Howells, editor of the Atlantic Monthly, praised it.

Unit 8, pp. 153-176: Weitling's later years

Entries for this unit: History Companion, Unit 8

  • Wilhelm Keil was a German tailor turned revivalist who founded communal colonies at Bethel, Missouri (1844) and Aurora, Oregon (1856).

  • August Willich was a Prussian officer and communist who broke with Marx and later became a Union general commanding the 32nd Indiana.

  • Weitling's inventions: in later life he earned patents on sewing machine improvements, set against Elias Howe and Isaac Singer's sewing machine patent wars.

  • Caroline Tödt was Weitling's wife. Friedrich Sorge in Hoboken led the American branch of the International Workingmen's Association.

  • Castle Garden was New York's immigrant landing station, 1855 to 1890, before Ellis Island.

Unit 9, pp. 177-200: Terre Haute and the coming war

Entries for this unit: History Companion, Unit 9

  • The Wabash and Erie Canal and the Vandalia Railroad were the transport lines that made Terre Haute. Daniel Debs's first job was laying ties.

  • Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857) ruled that Black Americans could not be citizens and that Congress could not bar slavery in the territories.

  • Richard Gatling of Indianapolis patented the Gatling gun in 1862.

  • Oliver P. Morton was Indiana's war governor, who ran the state without a legislative budget from 1863 to 1865.

  • The Knights of the Golden Circle were a pro-slavery secret society that became a Copperhead network in southern Indiana.

Unit 10, pp. 201-224: Treason trials and abolition

Entries for this unit: History Companion, Unit 10

  • John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry was in 1859.

  • Lambdin P. Milligan was a Copperhead lawyer sentenced to death by a military commission in 1864. Ex parte Milligan (1866) barred military trials of civilians where courts are open. Benjamin Harrison opposed him in his later damages suit.

  • Wendell Phillips was a Boston abolitionist who became a labor reformer after the war. Henry Ward Beecher was the celebrity Brooklyn preacher.

  • George Pullman's early career: he raised Chicago buildings out of the mud in the 1850s and launched his luxury sleeping car, the Pioneer, in 1865. The legend attached to it is that it carried Lincoln's body home.

Unit 11, pp. 225-256: Assassination, Reconstruction, Mrs. Lincoln

Entries for this unit: History Companion, Unit 11

  • John Wilkes Booth assassinated Lincoln in April 1865.

  • Andrew Johnson was impeached in 1868 and acquitted by one vote in the Senate. Reconstruction policy is the backdrop.

  • Black Kettle and Sand Creek (1864): Custer killed Black Kettle at the Washita in 1868.

  • Mary Todd Lincoln was committed to a private asylum in Batavia, Illinois, in 1875 at her son Robert's instigation. Tad Lincoln died in 1871.

  • Susan B. Anthony was arrested for voting in 1872. Victoria Woodhull was the first woman to run for president (1872). She was a stockbroker, spiritualist, and radical, and she exposed the Beecher adultery scandal.

Unit 12, pp. 257-272: Europe, Mexico, St. Louis

Entries for this unit: History Companion, Unit 12

  • Mary Lincoln lived in Pau, France, from 1876 to 1880. On her voyage home, Sarah Bernhardt caught her from a fall on the stairs. She died in 1882 at the Springfield home of her sister, wife of Ninian Edwards.

  • Maximilian was the Austrian archduke France installed as emperor of Mexico. He was executed in 1867, and his wife Carlotta went mad.

  • Joseph Pulitzer began his career in St. Louis.

  • Herbert Spencer coined "survival of the fittest" and was the philosopher of Social Darwinism, hugely popular with American industrialists.

  • Debs's own start: he left school at fourteen in 1870 to scrape paint in the Vandalia shops, then worked as a fireman.

Unit 13, pp. 273-304: The Brotherhood and the lecture circuit

Entries for this unit: History Companion, Unit 13

  • The Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen was founded in 1873 at Port Jervis, New York. Its grand master Joshua Leach signed Debs up in Terre Haute in 1875. It began as mutual insurance, since insurers would not cover railroad men.

  • Herman Hulman was a German-born Terre Haute wholesale grocer. Debs worked for his firm as a clerk after the Panic of 1873 cost him his railroad job.

  • Robert Ingersoll, the "Great Agnostic," was the most famous orator of the day. The Redpath Lecture Bureau booked speakers like him, Twain, and Phillips.

  • Elijah Lovejoy was an abolitionist editor killed by a mob in Alton, Illinois, in 1837. Phillips's career began with a speech about him.

  • Debs was elected Terre Haute city clerk as a Democrat in 1879. James Garfield was elected president in 1880 and assassinated in 1881. Cornelius Vanderbilt was the New York Central rail king.

Unit 14, pp. 305-352: Home life and the wider 1880s

Entries for this unit: History Companion, Unit 14

  • Charley Ross was kidnapped in 1874, the first famous ransom kidnapping.

  • The Beecher-Tilton scandal ended in an 1875 adultery trial with a hung jury.

  • Ida Husted Harper was a Terre Haute journalist who wrote the women's column in the Firemen's Magazine, later Anthony's biographer and a historian of suffrage.

  • Kate Metzel married Debs in 1885. Theodore Debs was Eugene's brother and lifelong aide.

  • The Statue of Liberty was designed by Bartholdi. Pulitzer's newspaper campaign funded its pedestal in 1885.

  • Oscar Wilde toured America in 1882. Joel Chandler Harris's Uncle Remus stories appeared in 1880.

  • Charles Stewart Parnell was the Irish nationalist leader ruined in 1890 by his affair with Kitty O'Shea. "Boycott" comes from Captain Charles Boycott, an Irish land agent shunned by tenants in 1880.

  • The lodge names Young lists (Nauvoo, Marais des Cygnes at Osawatomie) connect the union to older American dreams.

Unit 15, pp. 353-392: Rail kings and Hoosier politics

Entries for this unit: History Companion, Unit 15

  • Jay Gould was the most hated financier of the age. The Knights of Labor beat him in the 1885 Wabash strike and lost to him in the 1886 Great Southwest Strike.

  • The Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers was founded in 1863. Young follows Debs's resistance to the engineers' sense of superiority over firemen, which foreshadows his later push for one union for all rail workers.

  • Chauncey Depew was Vanderbilt's lawyer, later a senator.

  • John Perego was a fireman whose 1887 act of heroism saved President Cleveland's train.

  • Labor Day began with a New York parade in 1882 and became a federal holiday in 1894, days after Pullman.

  • Riley and Bill Nye toured together in joint readings.

  • Daniel Voorhees was Terre Haute's Democratic senator, the "Tall Sycamore of the Wabash." "Waving the bloody shirt" was the Republican habit of invoking the war against Democrats. Edward Hannegan was an expansionist Indiana senator who stabbed his brother-in-law to death while drunk in 1852.

Unit 16, pp. 393-432: Allan Pinkerton

Entries for this unit: History Companion, Unit 16

  • The Chartists were the British workers' movement for the vote. George Julian Harney, their fiery orator and a friend of Engels, later lived in Boston. Pinkerton was a Glasgow Chartist cooper who fled in 1842.

  • In Dundee, Illinois, on the Fox River, Pinkerton exposed counterfeiters. He became Chicago's first detective in 1849 and founded his agency in 1850.

  • The Illinois Central Railroad was an early client, where George McClellan was an officer and Lincoln a lawyer. Pinkerton also foiled the Baltimore plot against Lincoln in 1861.

  • The Reno brothers of Seymour, Indiana, carried out the first peacetime American train robbery in 1866. Three were lynched by vigilantes in New Albany in 1868.

  • Zerelda Samuel was Jesse James's mother. In 1875 a Pinkerton raid threw a device into her Missouri home that took her arm and killed her eight-year-old son Archie.

  • The Larches was Pinkerton's estate at Onarga, Illinois. William and Robert Pinkerton were his sons and successors.

Unit 17, pp. 433-472: The Molly Maguires

Entries for this unit: History Companion, Unit 17

  • The name came from Irish agrarian resistance. Many anthracite miners came from Donegal.

  • Franklin B. Gowen ran the Reading Railroad and its coal company and personally prosecuted the accused.

  • The Ancient Order of Hibernians was an Irish fraternal society, and James McParlan was the Pinkerton spy inside it.

  • Pat Dormer, Muff Lawler, and Jack Kehoe were a Pottsville tavern keeper, a Shenandoah lodge leader, and a Girardville tavern keeper. Kehoe was hanged in 1878 for the 1862 killing of mine foreman Frank Langdon and pardoned posthumously in 1979.

  • Thomas Duffy was among the executed.

  • Twenty men were hanged in total. Schuylkill County and Pottsville were the center.

Unit 18, pp. 473-512: Tompkins Square to the Great Strike

Entries for this unit: History Companion, Unit 18

  • The Tompkins Square Riot was in 1874. Samuel Gompers was there.

  • The Knights of Labor was a secret order from 1869 until 1881. Terence Powderly led it from 1879.

  • John Swinton was a New York labor journalist whose Striking for Life (1894) defended the Pullman strikers.

  • George Blair was a Knights of Labor box maker Young ties to the Tompkins Square organizing; confirm his role in a labor history, since Young's account is thin.

  • The Martinsburg walkout on the B&O began the strike on July 16, 1877. John W. Garrett was the B&O's president.

  • Pittsburgh and Tom Scott: the militia shooting and the burning of the Pennsylvania Railroad yards.

  • Amasa Stone and John Hay: see Unit 13 context. Hay's novel The Bread-Winners (1883) came out of this era.

  • General Philip Sheridan brought federal troops into Chicago in 1877.

  • Mr. Dooley and Hennessy were Finley Peter Dunne's fictional Chicago Irish bartender and his listener, the sharpest political satire of the 1890s.

Unit 19, pp. 513-544: Indiana in 1876 and 1877

Entries for this unit: History Companion, Unit 19

  • James "Blue Jeans" Williams won the 1876 governor's race after Republican Godlove Orth withdrew under scandal. Benjamin Harrison, mocked by Democrats as "Kid Gloves," replaced Orth and lost.

  • The Hayes-Tilden election was settled by the Compromise of 1877, which ended Reconstruction. The Greenbackers were the party of paper money and debtor relief.

  • Judge Walter Q. Gresham used federal receivership power against strikers. The Indianapolis Committee of Public Safety was a businessmen's body that organized armed citizens, with Harrison commanding a company.

  • The Vandalia strike in Terre Haute went ahead without the backing of Debs's Brotherhood.

Unit 20, pp. 545-576: Whistler and Dostoyevsky

Entries for this unit: History Companion, Unit 20

  • George Washington Whistler was a West Point engineer who worked on the B&O, then built the St. Petersburg to Moscow railway for Nicholas I. He died in Russia in 1849. His son James worked for the U.S. Coast Survey before becoming a painter.

  • Vissarion Belinsky was a radical critic. Dostoyevsky was arrested in part for reading Belinsky's banned letter to Gogol, author of Dead Souls, aloud.

  • The Petrashevsky Circle was a Fourierist discussion group. Its members were held in the Peter and Paul Fortress, subjected to a mock execution in December 1849, and sent to prison in Omsk.
