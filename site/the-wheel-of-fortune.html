Movement One: The Wheel Itself

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Homer Capehorn · Joachim Spitzer · Madge Capehorn · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Philosophy: Buddhism · Marguerite Young on Her Craft · Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres

Allusions: Astronomy · Ecclesiastes · Gambling and Playing Cards

Definition from research

Boethius wrote The Consolation of Philosophy in prison while awaiting execution. Lady Philosophy appeared to him and explained, among other things, the nature of Fortune: that she turns her wheel as she pleases, that the high are brought low and the low raised up, and that this turning is not cruelty but the fundamental condition of temporal existence. The wheel does not break the wise person because the wise person does not mistake Fortune's gifts for permanent possessions. What rises must fall. What falls was always going to fall. The wheel keeps turning whether we watch it or not.

Marguerite Young knew this work. She knew the medieval literary tradition that carried it forward, through Chaucer, through the mystery plays, through the long river of Western letters that treated the Wheel of Fortune not as a metaphor but as a diagram of how time actually works. She built that diagram into Miss MacIntosh, My Darling from the first page to the last, not as decoration or allusion, but as architecture. The Wheel is not something Young refers to. It is something she constructs the novel around, turning it through each of her five philosophical pillars until the wheel and the novel become indistinguishable from each other.

The clearest sign that this is structural rather than ornamental is that Young encodes it in the narrator's name before the novel properly begins. Vera Cartwheel. The name announces the novel's central preoccupation before a single chapter has unfolded: Vera, from the Latin for truth, attached to a wheel. Truth is a wheel. It turns. It does not stay. The self that seeks truth is herself part of the turning, cannot step outside it to observe from a safe distance, cannot stop the wheel to get a better look. Young names her narrator after the problem the novel is going to spend 1,198 pages not solving, because the Wheel of Fortune is not a problem that gets solved. It is the condition within which everything else happens.

The plural self is where the wheel does its most obvious work. In the Boethian framework, Fortune's wheel is impersonal: it does not turn for you specifically, it simply turns, and you happen to be on it. Young extends this logic inward. The self is not a fixed point around which fortune revolves; the self is itself multiple, turning, constituted by contradiction. When Vera observes that life must always have "these double motions like the progressions of delicate planets, circles within circles, wheels within wheels, orbs within orbs," she is not describing the external universe. She is describing the interior one. The self contains its own wheels within wheels, its own orbits that do not resolve into a single center. Mr. Spitzer is the clearest embodiment of this: he is always between two streams, unable to decide which way to turn, "undecided which way to turn, unable to stand still," his identity perpetually suspended in the moment before the wheel completes its revolution. The plural self is not a philosophical position Young's characters hold. It is the mechanical condition they cannot escape because they are made of the same stuff as Fortune's wheel: turning, multiple, never arriving.

Illusion as primary takes the Wheel of Fortune and asks what we actually see when we watch it turn. The rusted ferris wheel appears early in the novel, standing against a sky in a neighborhood of extreme poverty, and Vera observes that it "might be only a circle of sea birds." This is not whimsy. It is Young's epistemological claim: the wheel we see is always also something else, always already transformed by the perceiving mind into something the mind needs it to be. The ferris wheel rusted against the poverty sky is the Wheel of Fortune stripped of its grandeur, turned mundane and corroded, and yet still capable of becoming birds, becoming motion, becoming something other than what it materially is. Mr. Spitzer's watch, that jeweled instrument that marks "the hours, the minutes, the seconds, the days, the nights, the years, the revolutions of the planets around the sun," is itself described as "a starry wheel," making the measurement of time and the celestial mechanics of time into the same object, collapsing the practical and the cosmic into one thing held in one hand. Illusion is not the opposite of the wheel's turning. Illusion is how we survive the turning, the layer of perception that translates raw cyclical fate into something livable, something temporarily meaningful, something we can call home before the wheel takes it away.

Grief as permanent weather is where the Wheel of Fortune finds its emotional grammar in the novel, and Young is precise about this. In Boethius, Fortune's wheel produces grief because we attach to what it gives us and mourn when the wheel turns and takes it back. Young does not disagree with this, but she goes further: the grief does not end when we learn detachment. The grief is structural. The wheel broken at the cistern, an image drawn from Ecclesiastes into the fabric of the novel, names the grief not as a response to loss but as a permanent condition inscribed in the universe itself. The golden bowl broken. The wheel broken. These are not events that happened once. They are the description of how things are. The bus-driver's endless rounds through rain and darkness, his fat shoulders sloping over the wheel as he sleeps and drives, are not a metaphor for a life that once had meaning and lost it. They are the image of consciousness itself continuing after the wheel has broken, continuing because it has no other option, because the turning does not stop simply because the mechanism is rusted and the amusement park is empty and nobody is watching. Grief as permanent weather means the wheel keeps turning even when everyone has gone home.

The witness is the pillar that makes the Wheel of Fortune philosophically bearable in Young's architecture, and it operates here through the distinction between the wheel of chance and the wheel of cosmic order. Mr. Spitzer explicitly distinguishes between them: "the only implacable law was the divine law, the heavenly harmony, that which kept the planets in their course, that which controlled, with mathematical accuracy, the falling stars, the music of the spheres, the silent music, that which was not based on the roulette wheel stopping or games of chance, such as the marked cards, the loaded dice." His dead brother Peron had given himself to "the wastrel wheels of chance, the games of loaded dice, the croupiers," and it destroyed him. The witness does not play the roulette wheel. The witness observes the larger turning, the one that cannot be cheated, the one that produces the silent music rather than the noise of the gambling hall. This is not detachment in the Buddhist sense; it is a different quality of attention, one that can hold the wheel in view without trying to stop it, without betting on where it will land, without requiring the wheel to validate the self that is watching. The witness position is what allows grief to be permanent weather rather than permanent crisis.

Recursive construction is where Young's deployment of the Wheel of Fortune becomes most formally ambitious. The great potter who is looking the other way when some of them are turned upon the wheel, watching a flight of red-winged blackbirds instead of attending to his work, is one of the novel's most devastating images of how creation proceeds: not through deliberate intention but through recursive inattention, through the wheel turning while the maker's gaze is elsewhere. The wheel of blackbirds that becomes a wheel of stars that turns with the turning sun or moon, appearing in the bus-driver's dying vision, is the novel's formal logic made visible: one wheel becomes another becomes another, each image recursively generating the next, the turning structure reproducing itself at every scale from the cosmic to the creaturely. Madge and Homer are on the bus leaving the “dust-colored pottery town”. Madge is distressed over her pregnancy and that Homer “was not shaped by the great potter”. Madge insisted she would have been happier if “she had married the great potter”. Madge recalls the warning “He told me that all were clay, all men and some women.” The wheels within wheels of Catherine Cartwheel's father's factories and the wheels within wheels of the "hidden being" of the feminine nature and the wheels within wheels of the planetary progressions are not separate images. They are the same image at different magnifications, the novel's recursive architecture demonstrating through form what it argues through content: that the Wheel of Fortune is not one wheel but an infinite series of wheels turning within each other, each containing and generating the others, with no original wheel at the center and no final wheel at the edge.

Miss MacIntosh herself, the novel's emblem of stubborn linearity, who approved of nothing "outside of nature's wheel, though it should be a wheel casting foam and spray," is the figure against whom all this turning is measured. Her shoulders at the wheel, helping those who were not the children of fortune. Her straight back. Her plain sense. Her refusal of the infinite. She falls off the wheel entirely when her wig comes off and her bald head is revealed, and the novel never recovers from this, because the novel was always secretly about what happens when the one person who seemed immune to Fortune's turning turns out to have been turning all along, secretly, under her hat, the whole time. The wheel does not exempt the sensible. The wheel does not exempt anyone. That is Boethius's point. That is Young's point. They are, in this novel's long, turning argument, the same point.
