Definition from research

Chapter 1 · Mist and Grey Landscape

  • Representing ambiguity, the unknown, and the dissolution of familiar reality.

Chapter 1 · Miss MacIntosh’s Whaler’s Hat and Umbrella

  • Symbols of groundedness and practicality against the backdrop of illusion.

Chapter 3 · Corset

  • Represents discipline and the necessary constraints of life.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s tight corset is a physical embodiment of her moral and mental self-control.

Chapter 6 · The Stuffed Bird on Madge’s Hat

  • Symbolizes fragile consciousness, the artificiality of appearance, and the precariousness of Madge’s situation.

Chapter 6 · Madge’s dress and accessories

  • Madge’s dress and accessories are a kaleidoscope of symbols portraying a young woman caught in the painful tension between societal expectations and her grim reality.
  • Her artificial makeup and mismatched finery mask a deeper denial and despair about her pregnancy and future, while the sports motifs and exposed sexuality reflect a conflicted desire for life and normalcy.
  • The decayed and ill-fitting garments evoke the disintegration of her life and identity, and her restless sleep gestures toward her psychological torment and tragic fate.
  • These symbolic details richly underscore the themes of denial, vulnerability, and the tragic loss of innocence in Madge’s character within the novel.

Chapter 9 · The Party Dress and the Birthday Celebration

  • The party dress, described as filmy, sleeveless, and embroidered with seed pearls, symbolizes both Vera’s coming of age and the tension between innocence and emerging sexuality.
  • The empty birthday party and Vera dancing alone highlight loneliness, unfulfilled expectations, and the absence of community or connection.

Chapter 10 · Veil and Mustache

  • Play with gender roles and identity, challenging traditional expectations.

Chapter 10 · Old Empty Shoe

  • Could represent emptiness, absence, or abandonment.

Chapter 11

  • “made of pure Brussels lace”: Brussels lace is well known for its delicacy and beauty. Originally it was only made from the finest spun linen thread, which was spun in dark damp rooms to keep the thread from becoming too brittle. Only one ray of light was allowed into the room, and it was arranged so that it fell upon the thread. (Wikipedia)

Chapter 14 · Mr. Spitzer’s Hat and Garments

  • The black cape, high silk hat, silver buttons evoke the image of the coachman, suggesting blurred identities and roles.

Chapter 17

  • “her corset stays sticking out like the quills of an angry porcupine”: The Ghost tells Hamlet his tale would make “each particular hair to stand on end, / Like quills upon the fretful porpentine” (Hamlet 1.5). A few lines later, “My hair crawled,” and soon Vera’s hair stands “on end.”
  • “someone who wore a jumbo corset whale-boned”: Corsets were stiffened with whalebone, the baleen from the mouths of whales.

Chapter 17 · Corset and Armor

  • Miss MacIntosh’s metallic corset is described like a battleship or wrestler’s gear, symbolizing restraint, protection, and conflict readiness.

Chapter 19

  • “Her whale-boned, salmon-colored corset”: The “salmon-colored monstrosity” of Chapter 18, stiffened with whalebone.
  • “a grey fedora”: The fedora takes its name from Victorien Sardou’s play Fédora (1882), in which Sarah Bernhardt wore a soft, creased, brimmed hat. It was a woman’s hat first, and then a man’s.

Chapter 21 · The Party Dress

  • Represents innocence and celebration. Miss MacIntosh declares it beyond mending, though Vera “could not see a hole in it” or “a seed pearl which was not in its place.”
  • The destruction of the dress symbolizes the loss of innocence and the inevitability of change.

Chapter 27

  • “his high silk hat and black necktie and rosebud with maidenhair”: Maidenhair fern (Adiantum) was the usual green set behind a buttonhole rose.

Chapter 30

  • “Because of the loss of a skirt”: Young told Studs Terkel that this came from an Alpine story in which two bloomer girls shed their skirts at a summit, and the younger, going back for hers, was lost: “the lady lost her life for a skirt” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See the Chapter 31 resources.
  • A white skirt often symbolizes purity, innocence, mourning (in some cultures), new beginnings, versatility, symbol of femininity. Suffragettes wore white as a symbol of the movement to represent purity and unity.

Chapter 30 · Skirts and Whispers

  • Symbolize femininity, mystery, loss, and redemption.

Chapter 32 · The Veils, Parasols, and Skirts as Symbols of Femininity and Defense

  • The chapter richly describes feminine accouterments like “great garden hats decked with billowing plumes,” “secretive veils,” “frilled parasols,” and “great hoop skirts flounced with ribbons and lace.” These symbolize traditional femininity, societal expectations, and also the subtle weapons and shields women use in social and gender conflicts.
  • Cousin Hannah’s urging for women to wield their parasol “as her sword or her shield” and to “fly her skirts as her banners in her war against tyrannical man” transforms these delicate objects into instruments of rebellion. The parasol becomes a metaphor for female agency in a world dominated by men.

Chapter 39

  • He “changed his personality whenever he changed his high silk mourner’s hat.” Young told Studs Terkel, “William James made a beautiful statement, man is body, soul, and clothing” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).

Chapter 41

  • “She looked like a domino”: Patches of black silk or velvet, called mouches, flies, in France, were worn in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries to hide smallpox scars and to set off the whiteness of the skin. Addison reported Whig and Tory ladies at the opera patching on opposite sides of their faces (Joseph Addison, The Spectator, no. 81, June 2, 1711). A domino is the hooded cloak worn with a half mask at masquerades, and the game pieces, black with white spots, share the name. Catherine reverses them, a white face with black patches.

Chapter 42

  • “Cousin Hannah as the great bloomer girl”: Amelia Bloomer’s The Lily promoted the loose trousers worn under a short skirt in 1851, and the costume took her name. See Young’s account to Studs Terkel of the bloomer girls who shed their skirts, in the Chapter 38 resources.
  • “wearing an old boater and striped shirt”: The straw boater and the shirtwaist were the uniform of the New Woman and the Gibson Girl of the 1890s.
  • “creeping under some great skirt or crinoline”: See the crinoline in the Chapter 41 resources.

Chapter 43

  • “that high silk hat which was almost his only God”: The high silk hat used to be a clear symbol of authority and status in society. It represented wealth and prestige. Culturally it also represented capitalism, enterprise, and ambition. The first top hats were made of beaver fur felt. From the 1840s silk plush replaced beaver, which gave the silk hat its name. For Mr. Spitzer the hat is “almost his only God.”
  • Bloomers are divided women’s garments for the lower body. They were developed in the 19th century as a healthful and comfortable alternative to the heavy, constricting dresses worn by American women (Wikipedia). They take their name from their best-known advocate, the women’s rights activist Amelia Bloomer. It became a symbol of women’s rights in the early 1850s. See Amelia Bloomer in the Chapter 42 resources.
  • “Is that my love, my skirt moving through clouds”: Skirt has long been slang for a woman, as in “a bit of skirt.” The skirt Hannah lost “so long ago” may be both the garment she gave up for the cause and the woman she lost. See Young’s account of the bridal trousseaus under “a secret woman, a bride” in the Chapter 42 resources.

Chapter 43 · High Silk Hat & Black Cape (Mr. Spitzer)

  • Symbols of mourning, ritual, and his ambiguous status between life and death.

Chapter 43 · Skirt of Clouds and Waters

  • Symbolizes beauty, identity, and the suffragette’s fading presence.

Chapter 44

  • “that great bloomer girl”: Lucy Stone and other suffragists adopted the Bloomer costume, which consisted of a knee-length skirt worn over trousers or pantaloons, in the early 1850s. Stone loved the comfort and practicality of the outfit and wore it both at home and in public for about three years. However, the Bloomer costume attracted significant public scrutiny and ridicule, and critics of the women’s rights movement often focused on the reformers’ attire rather than their arguments for suffrage and other reforms. By late 1853, many prominent suffragists, including Elizabeth Cady Stanton, abandoned the Bloomer costume due to the negative attention it generated. Stanton persuaded Stone to follow suit. Stone resumed wearing long skirts, although she never again wore the restrictive corsets and voluminous dresses of the fashionable style. Other notable suffragists like Susan B. Anthony also stopped wearing Bloomers around the same time. Amelia Bloomer, the editor of The Lily newspaper and namesake of the costume, continued to wear it until 1858. Only Elizabeth Smith Miller, who had helped popularize the style, continued to wear bloomers longer, until 1861 (Wikipedia). See Amelia Bloomer in the Chapter 42 resources.
  • “I must find my skirt”: This is the Alpine story Young told Studs Terkel, of two bloomer girls who shed their skirts at a summit, and the younger, going back for hers, was lost: “the lady lost her life for a skirt” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).
  • “Was not this Mt. Blanc always in my heart?”: Mont Blanc, the “white mountain,” was first climbed by a woman, Marie Paradis, in 1808. Henriette d’Angeville climbed it in 1838 in men’s trousers, and Mark Twain teased that she “cramped their utility by adding her petticoat” (A Tramp Abroad, 1880).

Chapter 44 · Skirts, Laces, Petticoats

  • Symbolize femininity, freedom, and perhaps the burden of societal expectations. Cousin Hannah’s paradoxical final request for her skirt contrasts with her life’s advocacy for shedding such encumbrances.
  • Her lost love turned back on the mountain for her skirt: “I must find my skirt, my love. Farewell, farewell, my love.”

Chapter 45 · Skirt/Flounces

  • The lost love or spirit, something elusive that the suffragette seeks but cannot find.

Chapter 47

  • “What could he do for that old bloomer girl”: See Amelia Bloomer in the Chapter 42 resources.
  • “In the East where men wore skirts”: In much of Asia and the Middle East men wear wrapped or flowing garments: the sarong, the lungi, the dhoti, the thobe.

Chapter 47 · Symbolism of Clothing and Veils

  • The recurring motif of skirts, shrouds, and veils points to concealment, mystery, and the social/cultural framing of death and identity.

Chapter 47 · Skirt/Shroud

  • The veil of death, love hidden, mystery of identity.

Chapter 51

  • “He feared the naked valet who had clothed him”: The “naked valet” and the “mad couturier” who clothes him “with the colors of a dream” are outside forces shaping the perception of self and world, as in Plato’s cave, and Thomas Carlyle’s philosophy of clothes in Sartor Resartus (1833-1834) makes the body and the world garments of the spirit. “For the dream of life, even like that of death, must ever contain within it an element of the unrealized, the unrealizable—else all would be complete”: imperfection and the unattainable are part of existence, as existentialist thought holds. Mr. Spitzer fears eviction from the womb, the “citadel” where he heard “the beating of another heart above his heart,” and birth into a world where he will miss the smell of the rose, and the mother who is “a bearded man armored” unsettles fixed ideas of gender. The old jockey who dies of shock when his dream proves real shows the power of the unconscious, as in Freud. “We lived by an imperfect concatenation” echoes the Buddhist teaching of impermanence and the illusory self.

Chapter 51 · The Mantle and Cape

  • Mr. Spitzer’s mantle or cape is described as growing with him, winding like mother-of-pearl around his consciousness and unconsciousness, symbolizing the weight of existence and the intertwining of life and death.

Chapter 52 · Skirts and Veils

  • The suffragette’s lost skirt embroidered with forget-me-nots symbolizes lost love and identity. Veils and skirts also signify the blurred boundaries of gender and self.

Chapter 53 · The Black Cloak and Ivory Cane

  • Emblems of mourning, identity, and secondary intelligence. The cane serves as an extension of Spitzer’s self and his cautious, measured approach to life.

Chapter 54 · The Black Cape and High Silk Hat

  • These identify Mr. Spitzer’s figure as a ghostly, almost theatrical presence, marking him as an outsider and a man out of time.

Chapter 56

  • “many tailored suits from Savile Row”: Savile Row is a street in Central London that was originally famous for men’s tailoring.
  • “innumerable fine Steinkirk thrice-winding scarves”: Steinkirk is a kind of neckcloth worn in a loose and disorderly fashion. It is named for the Battle of Steenkerque (1692), where French officers, surprised by the enemy, went into battle with their cravats wound loosely and tucked through a buttonhole.
  • “huntsmen’s jackets, both green and red”: Fox hunters wear scarlet coats, called hunting pink, while those who hunt hares with beagles and harriers traditionally wear green.

Chapter 56 · Clothing and Tailoring

  • Represent identity, social roles, and continuity/death. Peron’s lavish suits contrast with Mr. Spitzer’s threadbare suit, symbolizing life and decay.

Chapter 57 · The Black Funeral Suit and Tailor’s Bill

  • Symbolizes unresolved debts, both literal and metaphorical. The suit represents Peron’s dignity and the past that Mr. Spitzer attempts to honor and settle.

Chapter 58

  • “his pearl-buttoned spats”: Pearl-buttoned spats were a popular, elegant fashion accessory for both men and women from the late 19th century to the 1920s. Originally designed to protect footwear from dirt on unpaved roads, they became a symbol of social status and high fashion, especially during the Victorian and Edwardian eras. Spats faded in the late 1920s, as paved streets and motorcars made them unnecessary and men’s dress grew more casual.

Chapter 58 · The Black Cape

  • Mr. Spitzer’s attire symbolizes his somber, reflective nature and his boundary state between darkness and light, life and death.

Chapter 59

  • “his long white Steinkirk”: See the Steinkirk scarf in the Chapter 56 resources.

Chapter 60

  • “dresses of bone lace or bird-cages”: Bone lace is bobbin lace, worked on a pillow with bobbins once carved from bone. A bird-cage was the hooped steel crinoline of the 1850s and 1860s.

Chapter 61

  • The Congress gaiter is an ankle boot with elastic gussets in its sides, patented by J. Sparkes Hall in 1837.

Chapter 62

  • “From all his musical rags, future musicians must stitch together a musical coat”: Compare the philosophy of clothes in Thomas Carlyle’s Sartor Resartus (1831), “the tailor retailored.”
  • “the long white Stein-kirk”: See the Steinkirk scarf in the Chapter 56 resources.

Chapter 65 · Mutilated Crockery and Black Gloves

  • Symbols of care, brokenness, and preparation for hardship or death.

Chapter 69 · Fire Escape Room

  • Symbolizes escape but also confinement, a place between safety and danger.

Chapter 71 · Shoes

  • The doctor’s office is littered with mateless women’s shoes from all eras, symbolizing lost or fragmented lives, unfulfilled journeys, and the absence of order.

Chapter 74

  • “a shroud with many, many pockets”: “Shrouds have no pockets”: what is hidden in life cannot be carried into death. An Italian proverb in English print since 1854, it is also a Yiddish saying.

Chapter 74 · The Shroud with Many Pockets

  • Symbolizes the secrets Esther keeps, the hidden aspects of her identity and history.

Chapter 81

  • “a little man wearing a bowler hat”: The bowler hat, or “derby” in the US, is a hard felt hat with a, rounded crown and slightly curled brim, originally designed in 1849 by Thomas and William Bowler for British gamekeepers to provide durable, close-fitting head protection. It became an iconic symbol of 19th-century British, and later American Western, style.
  • “under his rain-streaked ulster”: An ulster is a long, loose overcoat, often with a cape, first made of rough Irish frieze in Belfast and named for the province. Ulster is one of the four historical provinces of Ireland, located in the north of the island. It traditionally comprises nine counties: six form Northern Ireland (Antrim, Armagh, Down, Fermanagh, Londonderry, and Tyrone), which is part of the United Kingdom, while three (Cavan, Donegal, and Monaghan) are part of the Republic of Ireland.

Chapter 81 · Black Mourning Band and Old Rosebud

  • Symbols of death, mourning, and lost beauty.
