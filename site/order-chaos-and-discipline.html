Definition from research

Chapter 3 · Discipline, Labor, and Practicality

  • Life is portrayed as a testing ground requiring discipline, hard work, and acceptance of necessary deprivations.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s emphasis on practical tasks (mending clothes, building fires) and physical exercise reflect this ethos.

Chapter 3 · Social Order and Class

  • Miss MacIntosh rejects aristocratic airs and illusions of grandeur.
  • She advocates for the dignity of simple people and honest labor.

Chapter 9 · Discipline and Domesticity

  • Miss MacIntosh’s rigorous training prepares Vera for the traditional female role of housekeeping and marriage.
  • Emphasis on practical skills (sewing, household chores) contrasts with Vera’s imaginative inner life.
  • The discipline is strict, yet there is an undercurrent of rebellion and dissatisfaction.

Chapter 12

  • “a place for everything”: “A place for everything, and everything in its place,” the old household rule.

Chapter 14 · Law and Order vs. Chaos

  • Mr. Spitzer embodies legal order but is portrayed as overwhelmed by the disorder of life and his own ghosts.
  • Catherine’s world is chaotic, filled with shifting images, ghosts, and memories that resist neat categorization.

Chapter 18 · Return to Normalcy and the Persistence of Routine

  • Miss MacIntosh insists that the strange night is a dream and that normal life will resume with the morning.
  • The repeated references to daily routines, domestic tasks, and practical lessons reflect the human need to cling to order amid chaos.

Chapter 23 · Common Sense and the Middle Way

  • She values common sense and the "middle brow," the average life, as a guiding principle.
  • Despite her philosophical musings on vast, cosmic issues, she prides herself on practicality and sensibility.

Chapter 23 · Nature and the Cosmic Order

  • The novel references natural phenomena such as glaciers, tides, northern stars, and the changing seasons.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s talk about ice floes, winter preparations, and the Arctic environment symbolizes the fragility and impermanence of existence.
  • There is a tension between the cosmic, the remote, and the immediate, everyday life.

Chapter 40 · Time and Order

  • The chapter challenges conventional notions of time and causality (“the echo came before the song,” “death came before life”).
  • Time is cyclical, layered, and full of “refractions” and “oblique passages,” making logic and sequence unreliable.

Chapter 42 · The Search for Meaning Amid Chaos

  • Mr. Spitzer’s obsession with lost ships and records symbolizes humanity’s quest for order and meaning despite the inherent chaos of existence. The lost ships, passengers, and forgotten histories mirror Cousin Hannah’s own forgotten life.

Chapter 55 · Order vs. Chaos

  • Joachim’s life is governed by plans, maps, and precise arrangements designed to exclude chance and disorder.
  • Peron’s life is the antithesis: reckless, chaotic, and unpredictable.
  • The tension between these forces creates conflict and tragedy, illustrating the human struggle to balance these aspects.

Chapter 70 · Decay and Disorder

  • Her physical smell and appearance evoke decay (rotted apples, dead flies).
  • The disorder she brings contrasts with the previously orderly household.
  • Symbolizes the intrusion of chaos into a controlled environment.
