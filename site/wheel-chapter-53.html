Chapter 53

On Chapter 53 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of The Wheel and the Spokes.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 52 · Wheel and Spokes Home · Chapter 54 →

Research notes: Chapter 53 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 53

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

"if other people...did not always know which brother he was, how should he know, with absolute certainty, that he was Joachim"

The novel does not treat Mr. Spitzer's identity confusion as psychological pathology. It treats it as ontological truth. He is simultaneously:

  • Joachim the composer
  • Peron the gambler (in others' eyes)
  • The mourner
  • The lawyer
  • The ghost
  • The survivor who suspects he is the one who died

"he lived in two worlds at once, though in neither world was he at home, and he was only his sad visitor"

The phrase "sad visitor" is crucial. He does not inhabit himself; he visits himself. The self is not a home but a transit point. This is Not One Self not as philosophical abstraction but as lived, daily, exhausting experience. He cannot even change his clothing without risking becoming someone else entirely:

"he would be afraid to wear any other clothing — for fear he should put on a different body and lead an altogether different life"

Clothing, here, is not metaphor. It is ontological infrastructure. The self is assembled from external materials. Change the materials, change the self. This is Radical Immanence's Not One Self at its most literal.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

"what a pity it would be for all of us...if we had not lived also through a mistaken dream"

This single sentence is perhaps the most direct statement of Pillar 2 in the entire chapter. Young argues not merely that illusion is unavoidable but that it is constitutive: that a life lived only through verified experience would be an impoverished, diminished life. The mistaken dream is not the enemy of the real. It is the real.

The chapter multiplies illusions deliberately:

  • People mistake Joachim for Peron
  • Joachim mistakes himself for Peron
  • The city of Boston contains ships "he had supposed were sunken in far seas"
  • Hobble-skirted ladies and Victorian carriages appear in what should be contemporary streets
  • He crosses from Boston to Boston and finds "wild regions of purple mountains"

These are not hallucinations to be corrected. They are the primary texture of experience. The "sunlit world outside the cave" (the fixed, verifiable real) never appears. What appears instead is an endlessly larger cave, more elaborately lit, in which the dead walk alongside the living and the past refuses to become past.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

This pillar is perhaps most completely embodied in this chapter. The text never describes grief as a process with an arc. Instead:

"His grief had settled over everything, spreading like an ink blot over a page of his unwritten, most beautiful music"

The ink blot is not a wound that heals. It is a climate condition; it has "settled," it spreads, it takes different shapes (ace of spades, ace of clubs, ace of hearts) but it never lifts. The grief is not for a specific loss at a specific time. It is for:

  • His brother
  • His unwritten music
  • His unlived life
  • His lost youth
  • The love that never answered
  • Himself, whom he may have already buried

"his grief had been diffused, no doubt, having outlived its reason and its object"

This is the most devastating formulation: grief that has outlived its object. It no longer even has a specific referent. It has become the atmosphere itself. Pillar 3 says "the honest life stops pretending otherwise", and Spitzer, for all his evasions, never pretends. He does not claim to be recovering. He claims to be weather.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrative stance of this chapter enacts Pillar 4 structurally. The prose does not rush toward resolution. It accumulates, circles, returns, digresses through billiards and boxing and kite-fighting and chess, not to arrive anywhere but to stay in the room with Spitzer's condition.

Spitzer himself is a witness in the most radical sense:

"For he should walk the earth, and he should knock upon all doors, all ivory doors. He should stare through every window. He should stand upon every street corner"

He watches. He searches. He does not fix. He cannot fix. His entire existence is an act of sustained, unresolved attention: to his brother's absence, to the city's layered times, to the faces of strangers who may or may not be Peron.

The chapter's catalogue of sports knowledge (boxers, billiard players, wrestlers, golfers) is itself an act of witnessing. Spitzer accumulates these figures not to judge them or resolve their stories but simply to hold them:

"Poor fellows — all! And who would not pity them? Mr. Spitzer did — in his own sad, empty way — though yet his sympathy was one and the same as his indifference"

This is the Witness in its most honest form: sympathy that does not pretend to rescue, attention that does not pretend to change. "Sympathy...one and the same as his indifference"; this is not coldness. It is the recognition that true witnessing does not alter what it sees.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

"There were only these poor notes, marked by many segnos, many signs like his S which concealed other signs"

The segno in musical notation literally means "sign"; it marks a point to which the player returns. The music is structurally recursive: you build forward, you return, you build again. Spitzer's entire compositional and existential method is recursive:

  • He wanders the city and returns to the same corners
  • He thinks through the same problems of identity and never resolves them
  • He builds toward "crescendos of unbelievable climax" that are "saved for finales" that never arrive
  • He sets the breakfast table for two every morning, knowing Peron will not come

"he had achieved perhaps more than he had wished to achieve"

The building continues even when the builder doesn't intend it. The recursive construction is not chosen; it is structural. He returns to the beginning because there is no other movement available. This is Pillar 5's "you build anyway, knowing the building is provisional", except here, the builder is barely aware he is building. The building happens through him.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The unfinished music is the central symbol of the entire chapter:

"this lost musician with his unfinished song"

"too timid a composer to write a music which could be heard"

"the music not written for the ears of living men — and perhaps...not even for the dead, who were equally indifferent"

The incompletion here operates on multiple levels simultaneously:

  1. The music is unfinished because life interrupted it 2. The music is unfinished because it was always already beyond the reach of any keyboard that exists 3. The music is unfinished because completion would be a kind of death: the chess player who refuses to move his knight because "having moved his man, he would never move again" 4. The self is unfinished because identity is never resolved 5. The grief is unfinished because it has no terminus

The chapter explicitly frames the demand for completion as violence:

"like that mad man who had waited all his life to make one move toward final victory because it would be his absolute, final defeat, his death"

Completion = death. The unfinished is not failure; it is the condition of continued existence. Pillar 6's "integrity looks like the ongoing sentence, not the concluded one" is here made literal: the chapter itself is one enormous ongoing sentence, grammatically and philosophically refusing to conclude.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

The chapter is populated almost entirely by marginal figures, not as local color but as epistemological evidence:

  • The old billiard-hall king who miscued and drowned himself
  • The politician who died in a shelter house, discovered post-mortem to have been a woman
  • The featherweight who killed a man by touching him lightly, then retired forever
  • The fighter who thought he was battling his father's ghost
  • Old fighters on Skid Row, "babbling like old infants in the storm"
  • The baseball player who thought he was a football player carrying the moon

These figures are not exceptions to the social order. They are, as Pillar 7 states, the evidence: the places where the constructed nature of identity, gender, victory, and selfhood becomes most visible. The politician-woman is the most explicit case:

"His political cronies did not know whether he should be buried as a woman or as a man"

The categories fail at the margin. And Spitzer himself (twin, ghost, musician-lawyer, living-dead) is the supreme marginal figure, the one whose existence makes the constructed nature of every category visible.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This pillar may be most perfectly illustrated in the chapter's treatment of Boston:

"he was crossing strange frontiers — that this was not New England as he knew it — that these were wild regions of purple mountains...castles perched upon inaccessible peaks"

The city is not one time. It is all its times simultaneously, pressing upward through the present. Victorian carriages share streets with modern traffic. Ships "supposed were sunken in far seas" sail the harbor. Ladies in hobble skirts pass alongside "old gentlemen with faces the color of pumice stone".

The sedimentary metaphor becomes explicit in the treatment of Spitzer's knowledge:

"a knowledge seeming to spurt up from some underground, hidden source — perhaps his dead sportsman brother's realm"

The dead brother's knowledge literally rises through the living brother. Peron is in the sediment beneath Joachim. His gambling world, his sporting contacts, his shady acquaintances: all of it presses up through Joachim's consciousness unbidden. The past is not behind him. It is beneath him, and it erupts.

"Whole centuries might be a baby’s cry in the darkness"

Time is not linear progression but compression: centuries collapsed into a cry, a moment expanded into eternity. This is sedimentary time: not a river flowing past but layers accumulating, each one still present, still pressing.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The prose style itself enacts Pillar 9. Young's sentences are extraordinarily long, recursive, hedged, qualified; they perform the instability of language rather than pretending to transparent description:

"Sometimes it seemed to old Joachim that only one piano key was missing...sometimes that he had no keyboard, not even the pale, foaming, starlighted surf rolling on a distant shore"

"Sometimes...sometimes": the language refuses to fix. It names its own uncertainty, its own location in a consciousness that cannot stabilize its perceptions.

The chapter also contains a remarkable meditation on the way language categories fail:

he "knew that tallyho was a term in hunting and not in golf—that archery was employed originally only in hunting...that fistmele, which he might at one time have supposed was a term in boxing, was a term in archery"

This is not trivia. This is a sustained demonstration that words do not naturally belong to their referents; they are assigned, reassigned, confused, mistaken. Language is a system of provisional placements, not a map of fixed truths. Spitzer's entire knowledge-base is a comedy of misapplied categories, words that have wandered from their domains.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The chapter reaches repeatedly for transcendence (the music of the spheres, the singing stars, immortal love, the vast overture of finality) and locates it consistently inside the ordinary world, not beyond it:

"the logical music which should be played in empty halls"

Not in heaven. In empty halls. The transcendent music is immanent; it belongs to this world's spaces, even if no living ears can hear it.

"the slow city lamps went on one by one like the slow stars of a returning consciousness"

The city lamps are the stars. The transcendent is not elsewhere; it is the ordinary seen with full attention. The swan boats on the Public Gardens, the black swans quarreling with white swans, the purple balloon vendor, the coin for the ferryman; these are the sacred objects of this chapter. Not symbols pointing beyond themselves, but things dense with immanent significance.

"the sacred is here, inside the ordinary world, pointing deeper into the immanent"

Spitzer's entire life is this pointing (his music, his grief, his love for his brother, his walks through Boston) all of it pointing deeper into the world rather than out of it. The opium lady's face appears "in every cloud conformation or rippling shadow": the beloved is not in heaven but in the weather, in the water, in the light.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is perhaps the pillar that most completely describes Spitzer's metaphysical situation:

"as if his brother's train should pull out just as Mr. Spitzer's train pulled in...he would always miss connections"

Spitzer is the crossing point: the place where his brother's life and his own intersect without meeting, where the living and the dead share the same streets without touching, where past Boston and present Boston occupy the same geography.

"he was constituted by what was deposited before you arrived, and you are constituting the ground the not-yet-born will stand on"

This is enacted in Spitzer's relationship to his brother's debts, his brother's acquaintances, his brother's knowledge: all of it deposited in Joachim by Peron's life and death:

"He was simply amazed that he seemed to know, perhaps because of his dead brother whose circle had been a good deal larger than his own"

He did not choose to carry this knowledge. It was structurally deposited in him by the fact of being Peron's twin. And he carries it forward (paying Peron's debts, tipping his hat at Cockerel Church in memory of him, setting the table for two) constituting the ground on which Peron's memory continues to exist.

"the individual should not die so long as one remembered him"

This is the Crossing Point's ethics made explicit: the obligation to carry is not chosen. It is structural. Joachim is the place where Peron crosses from death back into the world. Without Joachim's memory, his grief, his daily acts of commemoration, Peron ceases entirely. The carrying continues.
