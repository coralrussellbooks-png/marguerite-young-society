Movement Six: What the Novel Teaches

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Moses Hunnecker · Peron Spitzer · The Stone Deaf Man

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Deafness and Sign Language · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · The History of Opium · Women's Suffrage

Definition from research

Midway through Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, Mr. Spitzer contemplates what his dead brother Peron may have established by dying: "a colony under no flag, perhaps upon the other shore, a community not of this world as now it was but of this world as once it was...or of this world as it might become tomorrow." The passage is dense with grief and longing, and it would be easy to read it as only that: a mourning man's wish that death might lead somewhere better than here. But Young is doing something architecturally more precise. She is embedding a constitutional vision inside an elegy. The community not of this world as now it was but as it might become tomorrow is not a description of the afterlife. It is a description of a just world. And the novel has been building toward it for five hundred pages before it arrives.

The phrase "colony under no flag" is Young's most compressed statement of what the AND philosophy requires politically. A flag represents sovereignty, jurisdiction, the authority of one group to define who belongs and who does not, whose body is protected and whose is expendable, whose voice counts and whose is silence. Young had spent seven years at New Harmony, Indiana, the utopian community where Robert Owen and the Harmonists tried to build a society organized on principles of common ownership, universal education, and the rejection of hierarchy. Angel in the Forest, her book about those experiments, was published in 1945. She began Miss MacIntosh, My Darling two years later, carrying everything she had learned about why utopias fail and what they nonetheless prove. The colony under no flag is what remains when you strip away the specific failures and look at the aspiration that kept generating new attempts. It is the world as it might become tomorrow.

The five pillars of Young's philosophy all produce specific constitutional requirements when they are applied to the question of how a society should be organized. The plural self pillar insists that no person is reducible to a single identity, which means no constitutional provision that treats a person as only one thing, only a worker or only a citizen or only a body, can be adequate. Every heart is the other heart: the political consequence is that your suffering is my obligation, not because you are like me but because the boundary between us is an illusion the dominant culture uses to manage which grief it will acknowledge and which it will ignore. The economic floor pillar, which Young makes explicit in AND: the novel's argument that the five pillars require a material floor as their precondition, is already present in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling in the figure of every person who cannot access what their humanity requires. Mr. Spitzer practices law without fee, "preferring the lost causes." Esther Longtree moves slowly behind the counter with a deformed foot and no one who will bear the cost of her care. The bus driver Moses Hunnecker will retire into nothing. The novel is full of people for whom the economic floor does not hold, and the colony under no flag is the world in which it does.

The witness pillar's political consequence is the most direct: a community that cannot see its own dispossessed members cannot address their condition. The novel insists on visibility for everyone it contains: the bus passengers, the pregnant waitress, the deaf man, the stillborn children who embarrass Esther because she cannot explain them. Young gives each of them the full weight of sustained, undiscriminating attention that the witness pillar requires. The colony under no flag is the community that has learned to witness without hierarchy, in which no life is too ordinary to be seen.

The grief as permanent weather pillar's political consequence runs through the novel as the question of what happens when grief is collective. The mother Catherine's opium grief, Miss MacIntosh's grief over her mastectomy and her isolation, Mr. Spitzer's grief over his brother, Hannah's grief over the love that became the suffrage movement, Esther Longtree's grief over forty years of stillborn children: none of these are private sorrows. They are the weather of a society that has not built the structures its members need to survive. The colony under no flag is the world organized around the permanent weather rather than the fiction that grief is temporary and individual and properly kept indoors.

The most unexpected element of the colonial vision in the novel is what Young gives it as a foundation: the stone deaf man at the novel's end, whose deafness became complete when he was fourteen and who was consoled by an old Black woman who told him: "All must suffers for something that be not their fault, you because your skin be white and we because our skin be black." This speech, which arrives late in the novel and which Young gives in the woman's own voice without correction or translation, is the closest the novel comes to a direct constitutional statement. The colony under no flag is the community that takes this woman's understanding as its operating premise: that suffering distributed by circumstances of birth is not natural order but injustice, that the white boy and the Black woman share a condition even when their specific oppressions differ, and that the appropriate response is "dream on, for life is imaginary, and this be an imaginary world." The imagination is not escapism. It is the faculty by which the world as it might become tomorrow is held in view against the world as now it is.

Young was writing this in 1947. The Civil Rights Act was seventeen years away. The Voting Rights Act was eighteen years away. The AND Philosophy, in which you would formally develop the constitutional provisions that the five pillars require, was decades away. But the colony under no flag is already in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, embedded in an elegy for a dead brother, spoken by a grieving man standing on a beach with music only he can hear, dreaming of a community that has not yet been built and may never be built and must nonetheless be imagined because the imagining is what keeps it possible. This is what Young's philosophy produces when it meets the question of how people should live together. Not a manifesto. Not a platform. A colony under no flag, upon the other shore, in the world as it might become tomorrow.
