Harp Song for a Radical

Unit 2, pp. 17-32: Colmar and the dream of reason

Part of a reading of Harp Song for a Radical (1999), Marguerite Young’s life of Eugene Victor Debs.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Unit 1 · Harp Song for a Radical: A History Companion · Unit 3 →

Background for this unit: The History in Harp Song for a Radical

Young follows Debs's parents from Alsace to New York in 1849, then widens to the Europe they left: a czar who flirted with reform, philosophers drafting constitutions, and utopians promising a "New Moral World" (p. 20). The unit introduces the planners whose communities fill Units 5 and 6.

Major entries

Daniel Debs and Marguerite Marie Bettrich ("Daisy") (pp. 17-20)

  • Who they were: Daniel Debs (1820-1906) was the son of a prosperous textile mill owner in Colmar, Alsace. Marguerite Bettrich worked in his father's mill. His family opposed the match across class lines.

  • What happened: Daniel emigrated in 1849, and Marguerite followed. They married in New York that year and settled in Terre Haute, Indiana, in the early 1850s, where they ran a small grocery from the front room of their house. Eugene was born there on November 5, 1855.

  • How Young uses them: She admits the childhood record is thin. She notes that Henry Schnittkind's 1929 biography supplied "an idyllic and symbolic way" of imagining it, "for which there was no documentation" (p. 17). Daniel's love of French literature explains Eugene's names.

  • Further reading: Nick Salvatore, Eugene V. Debs: Citizen and Socialist (1982), chapter 1.

Robert Owen and *A New View of Society (pp. 20-21, 25, 30)

  • Who he was: Welsh-born mill owner (1771-1858) who turned the cotton mills at New Lanark, Scotland, into a model community with schools, shorter hours, and no child labor under ten. His essays A New View of Society (1813-1816) argued that character is formed by circumstances, so a better environment makes better people.

  • Why it matters: Owen is a founder of both the cooperative movement and British socialism. In 1825 he bought the town of Harmony, Indiana, for his experiment at New Harmony, which fell apart by 1827.

  • How Young uses him: Owen lobbied kings as readily as workers. Young describes him approaching Czar Alexander I "at a convention of kings in Frankfurt" (p. 25).

  • Where the record differs: Owen presented his proposals to the allied sovereigns at the Congress of Aix-la-Chapelle in 1818, not at Frankfurt. He had earlier shown the future Czar Nicholas I around New Lanark in 1816.

  • Further reading: Arthur Bestor, Backwoods Utopias (1950).

Charles Fourier and Albert Brisbane (pp. 20-21, 25, 30)

  • Who they were: Fourier (1772-1837) was a French clerk and theorist who designed communities called phalanxes, about 1,600 people each, where work would be arranged around natural passions. Albert Brisbane (1809-1890) was an American who studied with Fourier and brought his ideas home.

  • Why it matters: Brisbane's book Social Destiny of Man (1840) and his column in Horace Greeley's New York Tribune set off an American craze. More than two dozen phalanxes were founded in the 1840s, including the North American Phalanx in New Jersey and Brook Farm near Boston. Young's "Brisbaneites" (p. 20) are these followers.

  • Further reading: Carl J. Guarneri, The Utopian Alternative: Fourierism in Nineteenth-Century America (1991).

Czar Alexander I (pp. 23-27)

  • Who he was: Emperor of Russia from 1801 to 1825. He came to the throne after conspirators murdered his father, Paul I, in the Mikhailovsky Castle in St. Petersburg, a plot he had allowed to go forward believing Paul would only be forced to abdicate.

  • Why it matters: Alexander began as a reformer who corresponded with Jefferson and courted Bentham and Owen. After defeating Napoleon he turned mystical and reactionary and founded the Holy Alliance of monarchs against revolution. That shift set the political climate the 1848 radicals rose against.

  • How Young uses him: Weitling's French father died in Napoleon's 1812 Russian campaign, so Alexander's Russia swallowed the father Weitling never knew. Young notes Alexander "had apparently died" (p. 27) at Taganrog in 1825. The word "apparently" nods to the Russian legend that he faked his death and lived on as the hermit Fyodor Kuzmich.

  • Further reading: Janet M. Hartley, Alexander I (1994).

Wilhelm Weitling's origins (pp. 22-23, 28-32)

  • Who he was: Born in Magdeburg, Prussia, in 1808, the illegitimate son of a housemaid and a French artillery officer in Napoleon's occupying army. The officer died in the 1812 Russian campaign. Weitling became a journeyman tailor who wandered Europe for work.

  • Why it matters: He was the first German communist to come from the working class itself. His Guarantees of Harmony and Freedom (1842) was praised by Marx before the two fell out.

  • How Young uses him: Weitling is the book's second hero, a mirror to Debs. Young describes his proposal that coins bear emblems of labor, "the hammer and the anvil and the chisel and the saw" (p. 32), in place of kings. His Swiss imprisonment of 1843 anticipates Debs's own. Unit 3 continues his story.

  • Further reading: Carl Wittke, The Utopian Communist: A Biography of Wilhelm Weitling (1950).

The Mormon mission to England and the Chartists (pp. 29, 31)

  • What it was: From 1840 to 1841 Brigham Young and other Mormon apostles preached in Lancashire and around Manchester. They founded the Millennial Star newspaper there and sent thousands of converts to America, advancing passage money to the poorest.

  • Why it matters: The mission ran at the height of Chartism, the British working-class movement for the vote, and both drew on the same distressed mill towns. Young's point is that religious and political hopes competed for the same people.

  • Further reading: James B. Allen, Ronald K. Esplin, and David J. Whittaker, Men with a Mission: The Quorum of the Twelve Apostles in the British Isles, 1837-1841 (1992).

Shorter entries

  • Henry Schnittkind (p. 17): Author of The Story of Eugene Debs (1929), an early, admiring biography. Young calls him "Uncle Henry Schnitkind."

  • The Shakespeare Hotel (pp. 20, 22): A New York hotel at Duane and William Streets frequented by German and French exiles. It returns in Unit 8 as the site of a banquet for Weitling.

  • The World's Convention at Clinton Hall (p. 20): Robert Owen organized a gathering of reformers in New York in October 1845. Young places the young Walt Whitman there as a reporter for the Brooklyn Eagle. Whitman joined the Eagle in 1846, so this detail is unverified.

  • The Perfectionists (p. 20): Followers of John Humphrey Noyes, who believed people could live without sin in this life. They founded the Oneida Community in 1848 (Unit 5).

  • Étienne Cabet and the Icarians (pp. 20, 30): Cabet's 1840 novel Voyage en Icarie inspired a communist emigration to Texas in 1848 and to Nauvoo, Illinois, in 1849. Unit 5 covers them.

  • Pierre-Joseph Proudhon (p. 22): French anarchist (1809-1865), author of What Is Property? (1840), with its answer "Property is theft."

  • Jeremy Bentham (p. 25): English philosopher of utilitarianism (1748-1832). Alexander I sent him a diamond ring, which Bentham returned with the seal unbroken, as Young reports. He lived at Forde Abbey in Somerset from 1814 to 1818.

  • Aaron Burr and Francisco de Miranda (p. 25): Burr, the former vice president, schemed to seize territory in the Southwest and Mexico and was tried for treason in 1807. Miranda was a Venezuelan revolutionary. Both sought Bentham's help.

  • Jefferson's last letter (p. 27): Written June 24, 1826, ten days before his death, to Roger Weightman, then mayor of Washington, declining an invitation to the Fourth of July jubilee. It holds his famous line that mankind was not born "with saddles on their backs." Young calls Weightman "librarian of the Patent Office."

  • The Barbary War and Tripoli (p. 24): The frigate USS Philadelphia ran aground off Tripoli in 1803 and its crew was held captive. Young credits Alexander with helping, and Jefferson thanked him, but the extent of Russian help is uncertain.

  • Félicité de Lamennais (p. 30): French priest (1782-1854) whose Words of a Believer (1834) joined Christianity to democracy. The pope condemned it, and Lamennais left the church.

  • Joseph Smith's death at Carthage (p. 30): The Mormon prophet and his brother Hyrum were killed by a mob at the Carthage, Illinois, jail on June 27, 1844 (Unit 7).

  • The Mexican War (p. 20): The 1846-1848 war that took California and the Southwest. Its gold rush drew many of the immigrants Young describes.

Allusions

  • "When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd" (p. 21): Whitman's 1865 elegy for Lincoln.

  • Icarus (p. 30): The Greek youth whose wax wings melted when he flew too near the sun. Cabet's Icaria takes its name from him.

  • The Rubicon (p. 30): The river Caesar crossed in 49 BC, meaning a point of no return.

  • Sphinx of the North (p. 23): A period nickname for Alexander I, for his inscrutable character.

  • City of God (p. 30): Saint Augustine's heavenly city, used for Nauvoo.
