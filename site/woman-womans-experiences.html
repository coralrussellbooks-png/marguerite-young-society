Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Woman's Experiences

← Chapter 82 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Young's contribution to feminist thought →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Madge Capehorn · Miss MacIntosh · Mrs. Hogden · Rosemary · Tabitha · The Chicago Detective · Vera Cartwheel · Walter

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling gathers into one book very nearly the whole range of what could happen to a woman: pregnancy and miscarriage and stillbirth, marriage and divorce and widowhood, rape and incest, the amputated body, the illegitimate child, the suffrage fight, the unpaid labor, the roles a woman is trained to perform and the ones she is punished for refusing. No entry below reaches for a theme the novel does not already carry. The breadth is real. Whether Young wrote the first novel to attempt this coverage is a question worth leaving open, since the claim is easy to overstate and hard to prove. What is not in doubt is what the breadth is for. Young does not catalog women's experience to document it. She catalogs it to go down through it. Each condition the culture imposes on a woman becomes, in her hands, a place to enter consciousness, and the sheer number of entrances is the method: she takes every road the female body and the female role open, and every one of them arrives at the same floor, where the categories that sort a self, man and woman, one and many, living and dead, stop holding. The entries that follow read each theme twice, once as the social condition the theorists describe, and once as the site where, in this novel, the self comes apart. The two readings are not rivals. The social condition is the door and the dissolution is the room, and Young's originality is that she found she could reach the room through any door a woman was given.

Pregnancy

Anchoring pillar: Unfinished is not failed (Pillar 3)

Esther Longtree is pregnant through the whole of her long section and delivers nothing. This is reproductive politics: the female body as the site where a culture stakes its claim, the woman defined and judged by what her body is made to carry. Shulamith Firestone, in The Dialectic of Sex, put the case at its sharpest, that the reproductive difference is the material base on which women's subordination was first built, so that pregnancy is never only a private event but the ground of a whole order. The novel does something Firestone's argument has no need to do. It takes the socially imposed condition and follows it down until it becomes a shape of consciousness. Esther is not pregnant toward a birth. She is pregnant as a permanent state, carrying without arriving, and the carrying is the point. This is unfinishedness made flesh: the incomplete not as a failure waiting to be corrected but as a way of being a self. Primary history stands behind the social reading. For most of the recorded past, pregnancy carried a real chance of death, and medieval women were known to write their wills on learning they had conceived. The novel remembers that nearness of birth to death, but it will not resolve the nearness into either one. It holds Esther in the middle, which is where consciousness, in this book, is found.

Miscarriage

Anchoring pillar: Permanent grief (Pillar 4), with unfinishedness (Pillar 3)

Miscarriage in the novel is not one event but a rhythm. Esther loses and loses, and the losses do not accumulate toward any lesson or end. In the middle of the twentieth century the culture around a woman was likely to greet a miscarriage with relief dressed as comfort, treating it as a blessing in disguise and hurrying her toward the next, healthier pregnancy. The loss itself was not to be dwelt on. Adrienne Rich's distinction, in Of Woman Born, between motherhood as lived experience and motherhood as institution, names exactly the gap the novel works in: the institution has no place to put this grief, so the experience is left to carry it alone. Young's contribution is to refuse the hurrying. She lets the grief stay. Esther's mourning does not close and does not convert into anything else, and that refusal to close is the pillar of permanent grief, mourning as a continuous state rather than an event with an end. The spectral children who gather around her, present and unborn at once, are not gothic decorations. They are the shape grief takes when a culture gives it nowhere to go: a standing population, a company of the almost-born who do not leave. The social reading explains why the grief is unhoused. The consciousness reading is what the novel adds: unhoused grief does not disappear, it becomes a permanent feature of the self.

Stillbirth

Anchoring pillar: Permanent grief (Pillar 4), touching the illusion of the individual (Pillar 8)

The stillbirths carry a weight the miscarriages do not: they raise the question of blame. Esther is accused, and half-accuses herself, of murder, and the novel lets the accusation stand without settling it. The social history here is real. Where reproduction is policed, a woman is held responsible for its outcomes in a way no man is, and Kate Manne's account of misogyny as the enforcement arm of a gendered order, the machinery that punishes women who fail to deliver what the order demands, explains why a stillbirth arrives already dressed as a possible crime. Esther lives under that suspicion, and a detective circles her section like the law itself waiting for a confession. But the novel will not let the question resolve into guilt or innocence, and the refusal is the reading. When the book asks "Who killed cock robin?" the answer is that "all people killed him." Blame diffuses until it belongs to no single person and therefore to everyone, which is the illusion of the individual reached from the side of harm: if every heart is the other heart, then no death has one author. The stillbirth is where the novel's permanent grief and its dissolution of the separate self touch. The loss never closes, and the guilt never lands, because the self that might be guilty has already been shown to have no firm edges.

Motherhood

Anchoring pillar: Plural selfhood (Pillar 1)

Motherhood in the novel is where the self stops being one. Mothers are figures of failed or absent nurture, and the social frame for that reading is Adrienne Rich's, whose Of Woman Born separates the experience of mothering from the institution that has scripted it, so that a woman may be a mother and still be crushed by motherhood. Catherine has gone so far into the institution's dream that she has forgotten she ever gave birth. Read through the pillar of plural selfhood it is something more exact. Catherine's "I" has already scattered across the guests of her opium dream, and a scattered self cannot hold the single, bounded relation the institution calls motherhood. Esther, at the other end, does not so much lose her children as multiply into them; her spectral offspring are extensions of her, versions of a self that will not stay one. Marilyn French's history, in From Eve to Dawn, tracks how motherhood was narrowed over centuries into a private, devalued, and total assignment, and that narrowing is the pressure the novel's mothers buckle under. But the buckling reveals the deeper thing. Where the culture demands one mother, one child, one clean line of care, the novel finds a self already too many to perform it. Motherhood becomes the plainest case of plural selfhood, because it is the relation in which one body is asked, and fails, to be exactly one person.

Marriage

Anchoring pillar: Plural selfhood forced to perform singularity, with the crossing point (Pillars 1 and 2)

Marriage is the institution the novel most enjoys taking apart. Gerda Lerner argues in The Creation of Patriarchy that the appropriation of women's sexual and reproductive capacity came before private property and lies at its foundation, and that the archaic state had an essential interest in the patriarchal family; marriage, in that account, is the oldest machinery of control, the point where a woman's capacity is transferred and held. Marilyn French adds the later turn, the moment when marriage was remade into a public ceremony that granted men more power and left women less say in when and whom they would wed. All of this the novel knows. But its own attack runs along a different line. In the swapped wedding, Mr. Spitzer stands as the bride and Miss MacIntosh as the groom, and the preacher lets the wedding rite and the funeral rite run together until neither is intact. The scene is not only mocking the institution's control. It is exposing what the institution requires: that two people each perform a single, fixed self, one man and one woman, each standing on an assigned side of a line. The novel's characters cannot do it. They are already plural, already crossing, and the ceremony that demands singularity from them collapses into its opposite. Marriage, in this reading, is the collision of plural selves forced to perform as one, and the funeral bleeding into the wedding is the sound of that performance failing. The social critique names the harm. The consciousness reading names why the harm cannot hold: a self that is many cannot be married to a self that is many and the result called one.

Widowhood

Anchoring pillar: The crossing point and the life-and-death boundary (Pillars 2 and 5)

Widowhood is the entry where the social history and the consciousness reading meet because the widow is, by definition, a person defined by a death that is not her own. The social facts are hard and can be stated without softening. Under coverture a wife had no separate legal person; her death, or her husband's, was one of the few events that returned any standing to her, and even then on terms set by his family. The widowhood effect is measurable: the surviving spouse's risk of death rises sharply in the months after the loss, most steeply for women, who were more often the ones left without means. Marilyn French's long historical account and Gerda Lerner's account of women's standing as mediated through their tie to a man both explain why the loss of that tie was so dangerous: a woman severed from the man through whom she held her place fell out of the order entirely. The novel takes this literal in-betweenness and makes it a structure of consciousness. Catherine, the book's central widow-figure, is not simply bereaved. She is living and dead at once, kept in a state the novel returns to again and again, watchful and wakeful on the wrong side of the line, holding both conditions in one body. Widowhood is the crossing point, the pillar's name, the seam between living and dead, made social first by law and then made metaphysical by the novel. What the culture imposes as a legal and economic limbo, Young inhabits as the very place her book wants to be: the threshold where the categories that sort the living from the dead stop holding.

Mastectomy

Anchoring pillar: The body as the place the self comes apart (Pillar 7)

Miss MacIntosh carries the novel's plainest wound: one breast amputated, the scar described with a clinical closeness, the head bald under a red wig she puts on and takes off. The surgical removal of the breast is old, but the modern management of the female body around it, the medical authority that decides, cuts, and interprets, sits inside the longer story Adrienne Rich and Marilyn French both tell, of women's bodies handed from their own keeping into the keeping of male expertise. Miss MacIntosh's body bears the marks of that history. But the novel is doing something the social reading alone does not reach. Her wounds are not only losses. They are the places where the self comes apart and shows what it is made of. The wig is a self that lifts off. The missing breast is the bounded body opened, the outline that was supposed to hold one person breached. When Vera sees the baldness and the scar, she is seeing, made literal in flesh, the dissolution the whole book performs elsewhere in dreams and doublings. Mastectomy is the body's version of the novel's central move: the person taken apart, not to be destroyed but to be seen without the covering that made her look whole and single. Miss MacIntosh, of all the characters the most grounded and the least given to illusion, turns out to carry the dissolution on her own body, which is exactly why the reader is meant to trust it.

Abortion

Anchoring pillar: The illusion of the individual (Pillar 8), touching the life-and-death boundary (Pillar 5)

Abortion barely happens in the novel as an act, and that is the point of how it is handled. It arrives instead as rumor, dream, and blur: the "mass murderess" abortionist who secretly kills unmarried mothers and their infants, and, at the far edge of the book's logic, a woman she murders who was "pregnant in her dreams" only. Andrea Dworkin's chapter on abortion in Right Wing Women corrects the record the culture tells about who sought abortions, that it was largely married women and not the "shameless sluts" of conservative fantasy, and that until reform the illegality itself was the danger, so that legalization in one state alone let far more women survive maternity. The social fact is a woman held responsible under law for a body the law will not let her govern. The novel takes that imposed condition and follows it into its own country. Where a pregnancy can be real or dreamed, ended or never begun, the question of whether an abortion even occurred dissolves, and with it the question of who is guilty of it. This is the illusion of the individual reached from the side of the unborn: if the pregnancy has no certain existence and the child no certain author, then no act has a single doer and no death a single cause. Abortion in the book is not a decision a bounded person makes. It is the place where the boundary between the conceived and the unconceived, like the boundary between one self and another, refuses to hold.

Birth

Anchoring pillar: The life-and-death boundary (Pillar 5)

Birth in the novel is never far from death, and Esther Longtree is where the two arrive together. She delivers stillbirths and miscarriages and, at the extreme, "the birth of nothing," labor that produces no child at all, and around these losses the book gathers cosmic imagery, "the Milky Way of many stars," the moon, the "starry universe," as if a birth were an event on the scale of creation and extinction rather than a private hour. The social history behind this is concrete and grim. For most of the recorded past, birth carried a real chance of death, medieval women wrote their wills on learning they had conceived, and the women-centered knowledge that once attended it was taken, over centuries, into the hands of male physicians, a transfer Marilyn French and Gerda Lerner both track as part of the larger appropriation of women's bodily authority, enforced at its cruelest by the killing of women called witches. Young remembers that nearness of birth to death and refuses to resolve it. Her births do not come out on the side of life. They hold at the seam, where creation and destruction are one motion, and "the birth of nothing" is the seam named. This is the life-and-death boundary in its first and most literal form, the threshold every other threshold in the book descends from: the moment a self is supposed to begin, held open so that beginning and ending cannot be told apart.

Child, Children, Childhood

Anchoring pillar: Real and dream as one country (Pillar 6)

Children in the novel are rarely present in the ordinary way. They are spectral, unborn, stillborn, or grown into accusation: Esther's "little shadow boxer," her son Walter who "grew and cast his shadow" and returns to charge her with his death, the "little toy salesman" who is a "bottle baby" that never had a mother's love. Childhood itself is described as a state "as elusive as a dream," made of "sediments, echoes and reflections." The social frame is real: the very idea of childhood as a protected, innocent stage was a historical invention, hardening only in the modern era and always shadowed by the child labor and early death it was meant to deny, and Adrienne Rich's separation of the experience of raising a child from the institution that scripts it explains why the mothers here can love and still fail their children utterly. But the novel does something the social history does not. It refuses to keep childhood and children on the daylight side of the real. The spectral children who "hover at the ivory gates between life and death" are not memories or fantasies set against fact. They are as present to Esther as anything the waking world offers, and the book grants them that standing without apology. This is the real and the dream as one country worked through the figure of the child: a childhood that is a dream one never fully wakes from, and children who are neither wholly imagined nor wholly born, inhabiting the single country where, in this novel, the dreamed and the real keep the same address.

Divorce

Anchoring pillar: The failure of the record (Pillar 10), touching the life-and-death boundary (Pillar 5)

Divorce runs through the novel as escape and as erasure at once. Vera notes an America where divorces may soon outnumber marriages. The social history is exact. Through most of the period the novel remembers, divorce required proving a "fault" and betraying an "innocent spouse," and under coverture a wife had had no separate legal person to dissolve in the first place, so that the whole apparatus of marrying and unmarrying was a record kept by and for men, in which a woman appeared as a transferred asset more than a party. Gerda Lerner's account of women's standing as mediated through their tie to a man explains why leaving that tie was never a clean act. The novel makes the point through what the record cannot hold. Madge has destroyed her marriage license and cast off the wedding ring, refusing the documents that would certify a marriage ever happened, and Mr. Spitzer, the lawyer, goes uncertain and silent about the laws and the spiritual weight of divorce, unable to say what the paperwork means. Divorce here is the failure of the record to fix a woman's status, the license burned, the ring gone, the law fallen quiet, and what the failed record exposes is a self that was never single enough to be bound or released on paper. The marriage that ends was already a fiction the document could not keep, and the divorce is only the moment the fiction stops being maintained.

Femininity

Anchoring pillar: The crossing point (Pillar 2)

Femininity in the novel is never a nature. It is always a surface put on and taken off. Vera's mother appears as a "plucked and naked bird" under her paint, her eyelids colored, her whole presentation an artifice laid over an inner barrenness, and Miss MacIntosh keeps a red wig she settles on her bald head and lifts away at will. The social reading is direct and the theorists agree: femininity is made, not born, a set of performances repeated until they look like the truth of a body, sustained, as Susan Brownmiller and Marilyn French both argue, at real cost to the women who must keep them up. The novel accepts all of this and pushes past it to the seam the performance hides. If femininity is a surface, then something is underneath it that is not feminine, and the book keeps finding, under its women, the "secret man" it says lives recessive in every woman as a hidden woman lives in every man. Cousin Hannah's strength is called almost masculine and she rides "disguised as an Arab king"; Miss MacIntosh under the wig is taken for "an old man"; the swapped wedding puts a bride's role on a man and a groom's on a woman. Femininity is the crossing point seen from one side, the assigned surface a person is made to wear, worn thin enough in this book that the reader sees the person cross underneath it. Young does not argue that femininity is constructed so much as she lifts the construction off and shows that the self beneath it was never one sex to begin with.

Incest

Anchoring pillar: Plural selfhood (Pillar 1), touching the life-and-death boundary (Pillar 5)

Incest enters the novel less as an act than as a collapse of the distances that are supposed to keep persons separate within a family. It is implied around several women, Madge Capehorn, Jackie White, and Esther Longtree, who wonders whether her own father fathered her child, and it saturates the account of Esther's origin. Her conception is called "unnatural," arranged through a go-between who carried a "silver spoon" between parents who never touched, and she is haunted from birth by Rosemary, the twin she is accused of having killed, "a little child before she was born." The taboo the culture places on incest is, among other things, a rule of sorting: it keeps the lines of a family distinct, who belongs to whom, whose child is whose, which body is which. The scholarship treats father-daughter incest as the most reported and among the most damaging forms of abuse, a real violence the novel does not minimize. But the book's deeper interest is in what the collapse of those family boundaries exposes. Esther and her dead twin are two versions of one self, the shadow and the substance, inseparable and confused; the parents' union produces a child by proxy, through a third body; the generations blur into doubles and shadows until no one in the line stands clear of the others. Incest is plural selfhood in the register of blood, the place where the family, which exists to keep persons sorted into distinct relations, dissolves into a single tangled self that cannot be parted into its members. The twin killed in the womb, present ever after, is where that dissolution touches the boundary between the living and the dead as well.

Infanticide, Neonaticide

Anchoring pillar: The illusion of the individual (Pillar 8), with permanent grief (Pillar 4)

Infanticide gathers the novel's reproductive losses to their hardest point, the point of a killing, and then refuses to let the killing have a killer. Esther half-accuses herself and is accused by others of murdering her children, born and unborn, sometimes by neglect and sometimes by act, and her mother charges her with a prenatal infanticide, the death of the twin in the womb, that predates Esther's own birth. A detective circles her section like the law waiting for a confession. The social reality is real and Kate Manne names its logic: where reproduction is policed, a woman is held answerable for its outcomes in a way no man is, and misogyny is the machinery that turns a dead infant, for the mother, into a suspected crime. The research even bears out that mothers are more often than fathers the hand behind a neonaticide, which only tightens the trap. But the novel will not let the trap close. Its infanticides are "quiet," unwitnessed, the dead never quite acknowledged as having lived, and when the book asks "Who killed cock robin?" the answer is that "all people killed him." Blame diffuses until it belongs to everyone and therefore to no one, which is the illusion of the individual reached from the side of harm and stated as plainly as the book ever states it: if every heart is the other heart, no death has a single author. What is left when the guilt will not land is the grief, which does not land either but stays, the killed children joining Esther's standing company of the almost-born, mourned without end and blamed on no one. (See Stillbirth, where the same refusal of blame first appears.)

Legitimate and Illegitimate Children

Anchoring pillar: The failure of the record (Pillar 10), touching plural selfhood (Pillar 1)

Legitimacy is a filing system. It records which child belongs to which father, whose name a child carries, whose property a child may inherit, and it exists to keep the line of descent clean and countable. Esther Longtree's children fall entirely outside it. They are stillborn or spectral, fathered by transient salesmen who are absent or sterile, unrecognized by anyone but her, and some of the fathers write to ask after children who were never born or send gifts for babies who do not exist. The social history is old and hard: under common law a child born outside marriage was a "bastard," a "natural" child with no claim, and Gerda Lerner traces how the control of legitimacy, of respectable and unrespectable birth, was among the earliest instruments for holding women's reproductive capacity as property. The novel exposes the system by overwhelming it. Esther is the "immortal case," the "deserted woman" with an entire population of children the record cannot hold, and the "legal father" she finally settles on is a "little toy salesman," an innocent stand-in who makes the paperwork of paternity look like the absurdity it is. What the failed record reveals is that her children were never separable, countable individuals to begin with. They are versions of her, extensions of one self that will not resolve into distinct, legitimate persons with distinct, legitimate fathers. Legitimacy asks the family to be a clean ledger of bounded individuals, and Esther's spectral offspring are precisely what such a ledger cannot enter: a motherhood too plural, too unfinished, and too dreamed to be written down.

Oophorectomy, Ovaries

Anchoring pillar: The body as the place the self comes apart (Pillar 7)

When Vera asks Miss MacIntosh where her ovaries are, the answer is that they would be found "in the bottom drawer," next to a spare corset. The reproductive organ is spoken of as a stored object, removable and set aside among the linens, and the joke carries. Medically the removal of the ovaries has long been named, in the literature, the biological equivalent of castration, and the history Adrienne Rich and Marilyn French tell, of the female body handed from a woman's own keeping into the keeping of male medical authority that decides and cuts and interprets, sits behind the casualness of the line. Firestone's argument that the reproductive difference is the base on which women's subordination was built makes the ovary the very seat of the condition. But the novel is doing what it does with every wound it gives Miss MacIntosh. It treats the organ not only as a site of loss but as evidence that the self can be taken apart and stored in pieces, that the bounded body which was supposed to hold one whole person can be opened and its contents drawered like anything else. The ovaries in the bottom drawer belong with the wig that lifts off and the breast that is gone, the body rendered as a set of detachable parts, the person disassembled and shown, without horror and almost with humor, to have never been the sealed single unit the culture insists a self must be. (See Mastectomy, where the same disassembly is read on the breast and the scalp.)

Rape

Anchoring pillar: The body as the place the self comes apart (Pillar 7), touching the illusion of the individual (Pillar 8)

Rape in the novel is a breach of the self's boundary, and it is written as violation and dissolution at once. Esther is taken by strangers and by figures of authority, the Chicago detective chief among them, "without knowledge" and "while sleeping," states that strip her of the will and the wakefulness a bounded self is supposed to guard, and the pregnancies that follow are the wounds made to bear fruit. Susan Brownmiller's Against Our Will, still the foundational account, reads rape as a mechanism of control, an assertion of male power and of the property idea of women, sustained by a whole apparatus of myth, the woman blamed, the woman assumed to lie, the woman told the crime was her desire, a lie Brownmiller traces to Freud and to Deutsch and her doctrine of female masochism. The social reading names the violence for what it is. What the novel adds is the register in which it shows the violence, not only as a crime against a person but as the forced opening of the bounded body, the outline that was supposed to keep one self sealed inside it breached from outside. Esther after her assaults is fragmented, her edges gone, her self dispersed into the spectral children the violation leaves, and the detective who "had not had anything to do with her condition" claims her anyway, a man laying hold of a body he did not make. Rape is the body as the place the self comes apart in its cruelest form, the dissolution that the wig and the amputation perform as revelation performed here as harm, and the self it breaches turns out, in the breaching, to have had no firm edge to defend.

Wife

Anchoring pillar: Plural selfhood (Pillar 1)

The wife in the novel is never a single figure and never a single self. She is scattered across Madge, trapped in a pregnancy and a marriage she did not choose and haunted by a rival; Catherine, whose married life has dissolved into opium and phantoms; Mrs. Hogden, the wife declined into grotesque and senile parody; and the recurring shape of the "ghostly triangle," in which a wife, a husband, and an "other woman" or lost love form the real unit of every marriage the book shows. Under coverture the wife had had, in law, no separate person at all: as the “Good wife” and then “the angel of the cult of domesticity”, she was defined wholly by the household and the man she served, a self absorbed into a role, and Marilyn French's history of that absorption, from Eve to Dawn and again in the 1950s marriages of The Women's Room, is the social ground here. The novel takes the role the culture insists must be one, one wife, one husband, one clean bond, and finds it always already three, always a triangle, always a self split among rivals and doubles and the ghost of who the husband really loves. The wife is plural selfhood in its domestic form, the person a marriage requires to be exactly one thing, guardian of the hearth, mirror of the husband, and who cannot be, because she is many, because the marriage is a triangle, because the singular wife the institution demands was never a self that existed. (See Marriage, where the same collision is read from the institution's side.)

Woman's Rights

Anchoring pillar: The illusion of the individual (Pillar 8)

Woman's rights sit at a genuine tension with everything else this book does. Cousin Hannah carries the cause, the "great suffrage captain" who has fought male domination across lands and eras and urges Vera's mother to "take flight away from these vain, narcotic dreams"; Miss MacIntosh's very baldness reads as a bodily autonomy, a refusal of the beauty the culture requires; Esther's story is a long exposure of how little protection a woman had over her own body and fate. The tradition these draw on, from Seneca Falls forward and theorized in Lerner's account of feminist consciousness, is built on a specific figure, the rights-bearing individual, the bounded, countable person who can hold property, cast a vote, own her own body, and be recognized before the law as one and entire. That figure is exactly the one the novel spends eighty-two chapters dissolving. Here is the tension: Cousin Hannah in the novel demands for women the standing of individuals, and Young does not believe the individual exists. The resolution is not that the novel abandons rights. It is that Young pushes rights past their own foundation. The claim underneath the vote and the property and the bodily autonomy is a claim to be a full self, and when Young goes down far enough into that self to find it plural, porous, and shared, she is not withdrawing the claim but grounding it deeper than language about rights can reach: not that women should be counted as individuals too, but that the individual on whose behalf the counting is done was always many, and the order that denied full selfhood to women denied what everyone is. Woman's rights is the illusion of the individual met at its most political, the one place the book's dissolution of the self could look like a retreat from justice and turns out, read closely, to be justice carried past what it was fighting over.

Women's Role, Gender Role, Women's Work

Anchoring pillar: Invisibility and erasure (Pillar 9), with the crossing point (Pillar 2)

Gender roles and women's work are two names for the same machinery, the assignment of tasks and behaviors by sex, and the labor that assignment hands to women and then declines to see. Miss MacIntosh trains Vera in it directly, in housekeeping and sewing and cooking, teaching her to be "the servant of another, waiting on him," and her own caregiving, the nursemaid's endless moral and physical labor, is the unpaid work the culture depends on and does not count. The social account is well established. Gender roles are learned by imitation and enforced by reward and punishment, and women's work, as Marilyn French's long history shows and as Caroline Criado Perez documents in the present, is systematically undervalued and left out of the record, "virtually invisible," a supporting labor that props up a world which then forgets it was done. The novel holds this invisibility and adds its own dimension to it. The women whose work sustains every household in the book, the nursemaids and chambermaids and waitresses, are exactly the figures it keeps at the edge of visibility, present and unaccounted for, the mute Tabitha on her knees cleaning the floors, Esther feeding a diner full of strangers while she carries her own unwitnessed grief. Their labor is erased in the same motion that erases them. And the roles themselves, learned and performed and punished, are a sorting the novel's women keep failing: Cousin Hannah will not stay in hers, Miss MacIntosh crosses out of hers under the wig, the assigned parts slip. Women's work is the invisibility the book grants its erased, seen here as labor, and the gender role is the crossing point in its daily, enforced form, the part a person is drilled to play and cannot, finally, be held inside.

Women's Suffrage

Anchoring pillar: The crossing point (Pillar 2), touching the failure of the record (Pillar 10)

Suffrage in the novel wears a single face, Cousin Hannah's, and it is a face that will not hold one form. She is the "great suffragette," the "great knight errant" and "great Crusader" who has "ridden through many distant capitals" with "silken petticoats as flags," fighting "man, who had held woman too long in his power," and she is also "disguised as an Arab king," a woman of "self-contradictions, many opposites, many hidden facets," married to the movement as to an "awesome husband." The historical cause behind her was long and grinding, seven decades from Seneca Falls to the Nineteenth Amendment, and Lerner's account of the slow construction of feminist consciousness is the ground she stands on. But the novel does not render suffrage as a campaign. It renders it as a person who crosses every boundary the culture would sort her by, sex and role and nation, until she cannot be filed under any of them, and then it lets that person die a death "shrouded in mystery and loss," uncontained to the end. Cousin Hannah is the crossing point given a cause, the militant for women's full personhood who is herself no fixed self, no stable sex, no single thing the record could enter under her name. That her death resolves nothing, that the record cannot quite hold her, is not the novel failing to honor the movement. It is the novel recognizing that the figure who fought hardest to be counted was the one least willing to become the countable, bounded individual the counting required. (See Woman's Rights, where the same tension is drawn out.)

Patriarchy

Anchoring pillar: The structural container, reframed toward consciousness

Patriarchy is the container the other entries sit inside. Gerda Lerner's The Creation of Patriarchy shows that patriarchy is not natural but made, built historically through the appropriation of women's reproductive capacity, enforced by law and by the split of women into the respectable and the unrespectable, and secured so thoroughly that it came to look like the order of things. Marilyn French's From Eve to Dawn carries that history across civilizations, and Kate Manne's Down Girl names its working parts in the present, misogyny as the system's enforcement, the punishment kept for women who will not stay in place. The novel knows all of this and depends on it. But Radical Immanence lets the term do one more thing. Patriarchy, at bottom, is a demand for sorting. It insists on stable categories: man and woman, one self per body, the bounded individual who can be counted, owned, and held responsible. Everything it does downstream, the control of marriage, of reproduction, of a woman's legal person, rests on that first insistence that the categories are real and fixed. This is why the novel's dissolution of those categories is not incidental to its politics but is its politics, carried out at the deepest level available. When Young goes down into consciousness until man and woman, one and many, living and dead stop holding, she is undoing the sorting that patriarchy requires in order to function. Lerner's epigraph, that "patriarchy is man's struggle to give birth to himself," points at the same nerve: it is a system built to deny that the self was ever plural, ever porous, ever born of others and dispersed back into them. The novel spends eighty-two chapters showing that it always was. That is the deepest sense in which this is a book reached through women and carried past them. It dissolves the categories on which the whole order of women's subordination was built, and it dissolves them not by argument but by going so far into a mind that the order simply loses its grip.

I have made a bold claim that Young has included, to the date of its publication, the most comprehensive list of what can happen to a woman in fiction and I further clarify that it is to demonstrate the bottoming out of everything under the weight of consciousness. Other books, fiction and non-fiction, that have done something similar to catalog what can happen to a woman are:

Beauvoir's The Second Sex a foundational text of modern feminist theory.

The woman-as-route-into-a-self-that-comes-apart exists in Clarice Lispector, whose The Passion According to G.H. appeared in 1964, almost alongside Young, and dissolves the "I" into the impersonal through a woman's consciousness.

Doris Lessing's The Golden Notebook of 1962, which breaks the unified female self into fragments, though neither carries Young's encyclopedic reproductive and bodily range.

The gender-crossing strand has Woolf's Orlando and Barnes's Nightwood.

The dissolution of the individual into a collective feminine was Wittig's Les Guérillères, but later, in 1969.
