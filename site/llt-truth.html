Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Truth

Part of Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Love · Life, Love, and Truth · The Figures and What They Carry →

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Moses Hunnecker · Vera Cartwheel

Pillars: Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary · Unfinished Is Honest

The word “truth” appears 130 times, a startling scarcity beside the thousands of lives and loves. That scarcity is the first thing a reader should notice, because it is already an argument. Young does not deal in settled truth, and the two terms that answer this third of Vera’s question tell us why: Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary and Unfinished Is Honest.

Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary begins from the recognition that nothing here is ever final, so the honest move is to build provisionally and keep revising. The thing to distrust is the frozen, finished blueprint. Vera goes looking for a single, absolute truth and keeps meeting chaos, not because truth is absent but because the fixed kind she wants is the kind that fails. She notes minds that are “erroneous” and experiences that turn out to be “another dream”; she finds that “the world was this deception played upon her, and it included Mr. Spitzer.” These are not proofs that truth is unreachable. They are the novel dismantling the fixed blueprint so that an open, revisable truth can take its place.

The novel is sharp about who freezes the blueprint and why. Miss MacIntosh’s “old-fashioned values,” for all their steadiness, harden into a construction sealed shut, a truth held so rigidly it can no longer be revised, and the book counts the cost in compassion. Mr. Spitzer’s legal work, bent to wealthy clients, and Moses the bus driver’s political dogma show truth corrupted by power and self-interest, constructions passed off as facts dug from the earth. Each is a blueprint someone has frozen and then called reality. Against them, Vera’s wish to “be freed of the past and of its obsessions” is the wish to keep building rather than to enshrine.

Unfinished Is Honest is the second term, and it turns the scarcity of the word into a principle. The unfinished is not failure. It is honesty, and anyone who claims to hold the complete and final answer is usually selling something. Miss MacIntosh sells old-fashioned certainty; Moses sells political dogma; each offers a finished answer, and each answer, being finished, fails. What Young trusts instead she phrases as “the beauty which would not be an aspect of the lie,” a truth bound up with beauty precisely because neither can be nailed down and kept. Truth and beauty stay alive by staying open.

So the rarity of the word is the answer, not an accident of counting. Young withholds “truth” because she will not pretend there is the fixed thing the word usually promises. Young’s reply to Vera’s search for “truth that does not fail” is that no fixed truth can avoid failing, and the only truth that holds is one kept under revision. The truth that does not fail is not the one set in stone. It is the one rebuilt at every crossing, honest about its own incompletion, open to the next hand that takes it up.
