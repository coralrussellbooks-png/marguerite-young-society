Identity and the Self

Definition from research

Chapter 5 · Identity and Resistance

  • The bus-driver’s refusal to cut his hair symbolizes holding onto personal identity and autonomy despite external pressures.
  • Hair represents a personal dream, a physical manifestation of selfhood that remains even when everything else decays.

Chapter 6 · Identity and Duality

  • Madge struggles with her identity, feeling split between her past self, her present, and the “other” face she must become.
  • The motif of doubles: Madge vs. Jacqueline, life vs. death, awake vs. asleep.
  • Madge’s painted face and changing appearances symbolize the fluidity and fragility of identity.

Chapter 10 · Marriage and Identity

  • The confusion and inversion of roles in the dream wedding hint at fluidity in gender and societal roles.
  • Marriage is portrayed ambiguously, blending union and separation, life and death.
  • The preacher’s confusing vows suggest the complexity and sometimes absurdity of social rituals.

Chapter 11 · Identity and Multiplicity

  • Catherine’s dream involves figures like Rameses I, II, and III, representing fragmented selves and layered identities.
  • The dream hints at disjointed time and space, and the coexistence of past and present selves.

Chapter 11

  • “partitioned into many selves without his knowing it”: The Egyptians held that a person was made of many parts: the ka, the life force; the ba, the soul that flies from the tomb; the name; the shadow; and the heart, weighed at judgment.

Chapter 14 · Memory, Identity, and Duality

  • The chapter explores fractured identities, especially through the figure of Mr. Spitzer and his deceased brother.
  • Catherine perceives multiple overlapping realities and versions of Mr. Spitzer, reflecting her confusion and the blurred boundaries of life and death.
  • The motif of “four Mr. Spitzers” suggests layered selves and mirrored images, raising questions about selfhood and existence.

Chapter 16 · Memory and Identity

  • Vera reflects on her orphanhood, her lack of parents, and her future hopes.
  • She shares her secrets aloud, many of which she herself is only understanding as she speaks.

Chapter 17 · Identity and Transformation

  • Miss MacIntosh’s sudden baldness and altered demeanor symbolize a profound change, calling into question the stability of identity.
  • Vera’s inability to accept this change reflects a struggle with understanding the self and others.
  • The motif of hair loss as a loss of femininity or vitality is potent and unsettling.

Chapter 18 · Fear, Vulnerability, and the Mask of Identity

  • Vera experiences fear and confusion confronting Miss MacIntosh’s bald, naked, and terrifying form, symbolizing raw truth stripped of pretense.
  • The “mask” everyone wears shields them from their true selves, leading to isolation and existential loneliness.

Chapter 20 · Identity and Self-Perception

  • Miss MacIntosh’s lack of “intuitive imagination” and her common sense shield her from the deeper, visionary truths Vera perceives; she is “content with things just as they were” and would deny or not suspect the “empty vision” beyond the commonplace.
  • Vera’s internal conflict involves a desire to pretend normalcy to protect both Miss MacIntosh and herself from confronting the full reality of her condition.
  • There is also a complex emotional dynamic, where Vera simultaneously sees her as an “obstacle” to her happiness and as someone deeply significant, a secret lover or cause of her “vain pursuits.”

Chapter 25 · Identity and Duality

  • The twin brothers symbolize duality: life/death, faith/faithlessness, decisiveness/hesitation.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s identity is fractured, as he carries both his own life and that of his dead brother.
  • The confusion over who he is and who he might become reflects existential uncertainty.

Chapter 27 · Dignity and Self-Image

  • Mr. Spitzer is deeply concerned with preserving his dignity and avoiding ridicule.
  • His physicality (notably his size) and the fear of losing control over his image are recurring concerns.

Chapter 28 · Memory and Identity

  • Mr. Spitzer’s struggle to understand “the actual man he was” highlights the elusive nature of self-knowledge and the effect of loss on personal identity.
  • Memory serves as a bridge to the past and as a means to preserve continuity, especially through his recollections of his brother.
  • The chapter suggests that memory may be deceptive or limited, with “things not possible” entering the mind through “the deceived memory of the unknown.”

Chapter 34 · Identity and Loneliness

  • Catherine feels isolated, spouseless, and robbed of love, reflecting a deep sense of loneliness and alienation.
  • Cousin Hannah represents a contrasting figure of strength and defiance, yet also one marked by solitude and an almost otherworldly existence.

Chapter 36 · Identity and Fragmentation

  • Characters experience fragmented selves, double lives, or counterfeit identities.
  • The death of a twin brother creates a split in Mr. Spitzer’s identity.
  • Cousin Hannah’s identity transcends death and conventional existence, becoming fluid and ambiguous.

Chapter 37 · Identity and Transformation

  • Identity is unstable and mutable, with images of people changing genders, faces, or even species.
  • This theme questions the essence of self, suggesting that we might be composites of many selves or none at all.
  • The recurring motif of masks, doubles, and shifting forms highlights the fracturing or multiplicity of identity.

Chapter 39 · Fragmented Identity and Multiplicity of Selves

  • Catherine perceives herself as many selves, sometimes contradictory, never unified.
  • She contemplates which self is true or dominant, fearing that choosing one would mean losing the many fascinating false selves that sustain her.
  • This fragmentation extends to her questioning of self-knowledge and sanity, recognizing her madness but finding it integral to her existence.

Chapter 40 · Identity and Multiplicity of Self

  • Catherine perceives herself as many selves, reflections, and chimeras, sometimes unable to distinguish her true self from her images or memories.
  • This multiplicity reflects the complexity of identity within the realms of memory, dream, and consciousness.

Chapter 41 · Memory, Identity, and Self

  • Identity is fluid; Catherine can be many persons at once, sometimes failing to recognize herself.
  • Memories and past experiences collapse into moments without clear distinctions between past and present.
  • She grapples with who she is, where she is, and what form she might take after death.

Chapter 42 · Identity and Transformation

  • Hannah's multiple identities (Hannah, Hamid, Al Hamid, Great Moor) and her shifting roles reflect the multiplicity of self and the transformative power of myth and legend. Mr. Spitzer’s own uncertainty about his identity parallels this theme.

Chapter 43 · Memory, Identity, and Illusion

  • The suffragette’s past is fragmented and elusive, mixing real and imagined events.
  • Both characters wrestle with who they are and who others have been.
  • Dreams and memories flow outside of linear time, suggesting the permeability of identity.
  • Reality and illusion are intertwined; the suffragette’s visions may be symbolic rather than literal truth.

Chapter 45 · Memory, Identity, and the Passing of Time

  • Mr. Spitzer struggles with memory’s frailty, likening it to fleeting natural phenomena.
  • The chapter meditates on the dissolution of identity.
  • Cousin Hannah’s secret life and true self remain ambiguous, veiled in clouds and mystery, reflecting the difficulty of truly knowing another person, especially after death.

Chapter 46 · Identity and Masks

  • The motif of masks and clothing in dreams suggests that identity is always layered, never fully naked or transparent. Ghosts and memories are always clothed in some form, and this applies to living beings as well.

Chapter 47 · Memory, Identity, and Loss

  • Mr. Spitzer struggles with memory and identity, feeling lost between two worlds, unable to anchor himself or find certainty.
  • The elusive nature of memory and the impossibility of fully capturing or commemorating a lost love.

Chapter 48 · Identity and Self-Consciousness

  • Mr. Spitzer questions the nature of identity, whether the self remains unchanged after death, and how one can recognize oneself or others beyond life.
  • The possibility that he and his brother might be one and the same or that identity is fragmented and uncertain.

Chapter 49 · Identity and Self-Perception

  • There is a profound ambiguity regarding Mr. Spitzer’s identity, “He knew not whether he was himself or an image contained within an image”.
  • The motif of doubling, shadows, and reflections recurs, contributing to his fragmented self-understanding.
  • He often quarrels with himself or his shadow, suggesting an internal conflict and fractured self.

Chapter 50 · The Fluidity and Ambiguity of Reality and Identity

  • Mr. Spitzer experiences a world where "everything seemed off key and out of course" and where familiar structures (rooms, stars, chairs) multiply or dissolve, making it impossible to distinguish reality from dream or hallucination.
  • The doubling of images and uncertainty about which chair to sit on symbolize the instability of identity and the multiplicity of existence. The text questions whether one can trust the senses or even oneself: "one chair might be only the dream, and one might be real".

Chapter 51 · Identity and Multiplicity

  • Mr. Spitzer is described as a man of many parts, many identities, reflecting a fragmented self that cannot be pinned down to a single essence.
  • There are references to his “dead twin,” suggesting a duality or multiplicity of selves.
  • His confusion over who he is (lawyer, doctor, musician, lamp-lighter) which symbolizes the fluidity and elusiveness of identity.

Chapter 54 · Duality of Identity and Madness

  • Mr. Spitzer oscillates between his own identity and that of his brother, sometimes confused about who he truly is. The chapter explores the thin line between sanity and madness, with Joachim embodying order and Peron chaos, yet both are reflections of each other.

Chapter 55 · Memory and Identity

  • Joachim’s identity is fragmented; his memory is unreliable, altered by grief and loss.
  • He questions whether he is himself or his brother, and whether he is awake or dreaming.
  • His life is a continuous search for meaning in the past and the present, often blurred and confused.

Chapter 56 · Identity and the Self

  • Mr. Spitzer struggles with his identity, feeling like a shadow or a poor copy of his brother, or even a puppet controlled by unknown forces.
  • He questions the nature of selfhood, pondering whether he might become his brother by adopting his clothing and mannerisms.
  • The metaphor of clothing is powerful: Peron’s elaborate wardrobe symbolizes identity and social roles, while Mr. Spitzer’s threadbare suit symbolizes decay, loss, and continuity of self.

Chapter 59 · Identity and Alienation

  • Mr. Spitzer questions his own identity, feeling alienated from his body and self.
  • The metaphor of a stolen or substituted skeleton underscores his existential dislocation.
  • This raises philosophical questions about what constitutes the self (body, soul, or spirit) and the instability of these concepts.

Chapter 60 · Identity and Self-Perception

  • Mr. Spitzer is uncertain about whether he is truly a musician or merely a gambler who has deceived himself.
  • His self-questioning reveals a deep internal conflict and a search for meaning in his life.
  • The duality of musician versus gambler serves as a metaphor for the struggle between art and chance, order and chaos.

Chapter 64 · Identity and Difference

  • Baldness symbolizes not only her physical appearance but also her difference and the struggle to be accepted.
  • Her attempts to wear wigs and create a semblance of normalcy reflect the universal human desire to fit in.

Chapter 65 · Identity, Appearance, and Reality

  • Miss MacIntosh’s wearing of wigs and her attempt to hide her baldness reflects issues of identity and societal perception.
  • The contrast between appearances and inner reality is a recurring motif.
  • Mr. Bonebreaker’s idealized view of her contrasts sharply with her own self-perception.

Chapter 66 · Identity and Physical Amputation

  • Both Miss MacIntosh and the Arctic explorer carry physical and emotional amputations (her baldness and removed ovaries; his lost leg), symbolizing loss and survival.
  • Their shared disabilities forge a silent kinship and mutual understanding of alienation.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s lost hair and wig symbolize the loss of traditional feminine identity and seduction.

Chapter 74 · Gender and Identity

  • The text contrasts Esther’s experiences with societal expectations of men and women, highlighting gendered double standards, especially concerning sexuality, motherhood, and blame.

Chapter 78 · Identity and Self-Perception

  • The story explores Esther’s struggle with her own identity. She questions her own existence, haunted by the twin she allegedly killed and by feelings of being an outsider within her own family.

Chapter 79 · Identity and Acceptance

  • Esther struggles with her identity, labeled “Little Jackass” and burdened by the legacy of her birth.
  • Her physical and emotional differences set her apart, and she grapples with feelings of shame, rejection, and the desire for love.
  • The story probes how origins influence self-perception and social acceptance.

Chapter 80 · Race and Identity

  • Walter's father was a black man, described metaphorically as "the prince of Africa" and "as black as the ace of spades."
  • Esther’s shock and societal stigma over this interracial relationship and child profoundly impact her life.
  • Walter’s blackness is a symbolic and literal source of conflict, shame, love, and fear for Esther, reflecting broader themes of racial tension and identity.
