Chapter 6: The Wedding That Was a Funeral

Research notes: Chapter 6

Characters: Homer Capehorn · Jacqueline White · Madge Capehorn · Miss MacIntosh · Moses Hunnecker · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Death and Mortality · Women, Gender, and Marriage

Symbols: Twins and Doubles · Weddings and Funerals

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Fairy Tales and Folklore · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · Music and Composers · Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery · The History of Opium

We have been watching Madge Capehorn since Chapter 1, asleep in her seat, pregnant, visibly unhappy, the stuffed bird on her hat bobbing in the dim light. She has been a figure in the background, part of the landscape of the bus. In Chapter 6, Young brings her forward. We step inside her.

What we find there is not comfortable. But it is one of the most honest portraits of a woman trapped by the circumstances of her time that you will encounter in this novel. Settle in. Madge has things to say.

What actually happens in Chapter 6

The bus moves through a desolate night landscape: broken gates, fallen pillars, a derelict windmill spinning in the wind, withered leaves. Moses is half-asleep at the wheel. And Madge Capehorn is crying in her sleep.

When she surfaces into a kind of wakefulness, we learn her story. She is five months pregnant. She left her hometown years ago, went to work in a pottery town, grew older than the girls around her, and ended up marrying Homer Capehorn, not because she loves him, but because she needed a father for her child. The real father is a man named Adam. Homer doesn’t know this.

Homer is not a bad man, exactly. He is young, placid, oblivious, physically strong, and emotionally absent. He is asleep for most of the chapter or half-asleep, dreaming of a girl named Jacqueline White, his high school sweetheart, who is dying of tuberculosis. Madge knows he loves Jacqueline. She has always known. She accuses him of it. He denies it, barely awake, insisting he hasn’t thought of Jacqueline in years. They both know this is not true.

In the middle of all this, Young gives us the tragedies stacked beneath the present one: Madge’s mother died in childbirth. Her father, years later, in a moment of grief-addled confusion, mistook Madge for her dead mother and ran out into a winter storm and drowned in the river. Homer’s father, on hearing that Homer’s mother was pregnant, went out with his hunting dogs and killed himself. Death and birth are not separate events in this chapter. They arrive together, the way they have always arrived in this family.

Madge believes she will die in childbirth, the way her mother did. She believes Homer will die young, the way his father did. She can see the shape of it all. She is driving toward her own end with her eyes open, and there is nothing she can do about it.

What Vera is thinking about

Vera observes all of this while listening to Madge’s half-dreaming monologue, watching the exchange between Madge and Homer, understanding things about both of them that they may not fully understand about themselves.

Remember that Vera is on this bus because she is searching for Miss MacIntosh, the woman who embodied reality, practicality, the plain truth of things. And here, in Madge, she is encountering reality of a very different kind: a woman for whom reality is a trap with no exits, a marriage that feels more like a funeral, a body being remade against her will.

Vera does not intervene. She never does. But she witnesses. And in a novel where so much is illusion and dream, witnessing, truly seeing another person’s suffering, is its own act of moral seriousness.

Madge and what her story is really about

To understand Madge, you need to understand what the world looked like for women in the era Young is writing about. This is not ancient history, it is the America of the 1930s and ‘40s, the America Young herself grew up in and observed.

Marriage was not one option among many for a woman then. It was the only option. It was the entire architecture of a viable life. Without it, a woman had no economic security, no social standing, almost no respectable path forward. Madge left her town, went to work in a pottery factory, which was already an act of independence, grew older, and found herself pregnant and unmarried. In that world, in that moment, her choices had already been made for her.

She married Homer not for love but for survival. She is not a fool. She knows exactly what she has done and why. And she knows that the life she has bought herself is not a life she would have chosen.

Young is not writing a tragedy about a weak woman who made bad choices. She is writing about what happens to a person when all of the choices available to them lead to the same place. Madge’s intelligence, her self-awareness, her fierce and furious understanding of her own situation, none of it is enough to change what is happening to her. That is the point.

Jacqueline White, and the ghost in the marriage

Homer loves Jacqueline White. She is dying of tuberculosis or “consumption,” they called it, the disease that turned people pale and thin and beautiful as it killed them. She is becoming an ideal precisely because she is disappearing: untouched by time, preserved in Homer’s memory as the girl she was, the love he didn’t pursue.

Madge cannot compete with this. No living woman can compete with a dying one in a man’s imagination. Jacqueline will always be young, always be the one he didn’t get to keep. She is becoming the kind of love that only works because it was never tested.

This is one of the more quietly devastating observations in the chapter: that the dead and the dying have an advantage over the living, because they never have to become ordinary. Miss MacIntosh understood something like this. So does Vera. And now Madge is living it from the inside.

The two faces

Madge believes she must maintain at least two faces: the one she was born with, and the painted one she puts on for the world. This is not vanity. It is a survival strategy.

Young knew, and stated in interviews, that everyone wears masks: an inner self and an outer self presented to the public. But for Madge, this doubling is not philosophical, it is compulsory. She cannot afford to show the world what she actually feels. She cannot afford to be the woman who is terrified and grieving and furious and exhausted. So she paints a face over that one, pins the stuffed bird to her hat, and gets on the bus.

The stuffed bird is a perfect image. It is decoration that was once alive. It was killed to become ornamental. It sits on Madge’s hat going nowhere.

The deaths on both sides of this marriage

Young piles up the deaths in this chapter with a kind of brutal patience. Madge’s mother: died in childbirth. Madge’s father: drowned after mistaking his daughter for his dead wife. Homer’s father: killed himself when he learned his wife was pregnant.

Notice what Young is doing here. The news of a new life coming into the world (pregnancy, the thing that is supposed to represent hope and continuation) is the event that triggers the deaths on Homer’s side of the family. His father could not face it. Madge’s family was destroyed by it from a different angle.

This is a chapter in which birth and death are not opposites. They are the same gesture, made in different directions. Madge carrying a child is also Madge moving toward the grave her mother already reached. She knows this. She feels it in her body.

What to watch for as you keep reading

Light spoilers: This section hints at what happens later in the novel. Skip it if you want to meet the story fresh.

The pottery town. Madge worked in a pottery factory before all of this, one of the Midwestern towns, possibly in Ohio, where the clay soil produced the ceramics industry. Young makes something of this: Madge came from a world of clay and making, of things shaped by hand into useful forms. And now she is the clay, being shaped by forces she didn’t choose.

Tuberculosis. Jacqueline White is dying of it, and in the mid-twentieth century it was still the leading cause of death in the United States. It was called consumption because it seemed to consume a person from within and also the “Great White Plague” for the pallor it gave its victims. It had a terrible romantic reputation, for exactly the reason Young is using it here: it made beautiful young people die beautifully. Madge is right to resent it.

Masking. The idea that people show a different face to the world than the one they actually have runs through this entire novel. Madge states it plainly here that she keeps at least two faces. Watch for this idea as the book continues. Almost every character in this novel is wearing something over their real face.

The animals. Chapter 6 is full of them: moths, stags, dragonflies, blackbirds, rabbits, pheasants. Young uses each one as a layer of meaning: transformation, death, fragility, change. You don’t need to decode all of them. Just notice that the natural world in this chapter is saturated with significance, that nothing is merely decorative.

What to hold onto

Chapter 6 can feel overwhelming, so many nested tragedies, so much grief packed into one woman’s half-dreaming monologue on a midnight bus. If you feel the weight of it pressing down, that is exactly right. That is Madge’s weight. You are supposed to feel it.

But here is what to hold onto: Madge is awake when everyone else is asleep. She is the one who sees clearly, who knows exactly what her life is and what it cost her. In a novel full of people fleeing into dreams and illusions, that clarity, however painful, is its own kind of power.

She cannot change her circumstances. But she knows what they are. And in Young’s world, that is not nothing.
