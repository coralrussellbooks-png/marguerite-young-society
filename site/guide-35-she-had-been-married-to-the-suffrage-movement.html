Chapter 35: She Had Been Married to the Suffrage Movement

← Chapter 34 · Guide Home · Chapter 36 →

Research notes: Chapter 35

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Exploration and the Poles · Women's Suffrage

Chapter 35 is the last chapter devoted primarily to Cousin Hannah, and it is the most expansive. Young returns to Hannah’s public life (the suffrage campaigns, the foreign adventures, the iron keys and metallic presence) but now through the lens of Catherine’s longing: what it would mean if Hannah came back, what Catherine understands now that she resisted then, and what the great captain’s life finally amounted to.

This chapter is also the one where Young brings the reader closest to understanding why Cousin Hannah could not rescue Catherine. The answer, she suggests, was that Hannah’s faith in the suffrage movement (absolute, consuming, bride-like) was itself a form of the same retreat from ordinary human love that Catherine had made in the other direction. They were mirror images who could not see each other clearly.

What actually happens in Chapter 35

Catherine imagines inviting Cousin Hannah back for a long winter season, and thinks through what their relationship was and what it might now be. She would greet her without reserve now, take off her plumed hat, ask her to be seated. She would understand now what “she had not wished to understand” then.

The chapter catalogs Hannah’s suffrage career in its most complete form: the campaigns on “all fronts known and unknown, domestic and foreign and undiscovered, the” riding through distant capitals already evacuated, the triumphing over empire-makers and demoniac hordes, the refusal of a stained glass window in her memory. It also catalogs what Hannah was not: not a dancer, not a conversationalist, not a domestic figure, not someone who had time for old wounds or intimate revelations or confessions “that one had loved in secret what one” denied in public.

Young gives us the chapter’s central statement near its end: Hannah had been married “to the suffrage movement, even as if she were a bride, as if the suffrage movement were her husband, her lord and master who had carried her away.” “She had been married to no other husband.”

Married to the movement

The image of Hannah married to the suffrage movement is Young’s most complete statement of how Hannah solved, or did not solve, the problem of her own nature. The movement was “her true love, her awesome husband, the masculine dream of her life, the abundant resource of her strength.”

Young places this with characteristic precision: Hannah could not have been persuaded to confess that “she had yearned for marriage, or she had loved a man. Or she had yearned for a homunculus, a little man, a” “gentleman’s gentleman to wait on her.” The substitution of the suffrage movement for personal love is Hannah’s parallel to Catherine’s substitution of the phantom bridegroom, or Miss MacIntosh’s substitution of practical ordinariness. All three women in the novel’s inner circle have built their lives around something that stands in for what cannot be named or lived directly. Hannah’s substitute was the grandest and most public. It was also, in its way, the most complete: she was, as Young says, “this great lord, scythe sword in hand, her head reaching among the stars, this mighty captain who had rescued so many fair ladies in distress, breaking their invisible chains.”

She became the husband she could not have. She rescued the women she could not be rescued by. The suffrage movement was her marriage, and like all the marriages in this novel, it was a wedding to an absence.

Hannah’s buried life

Young gives us, near the chapter’s end, the sharpest contrast between Catherine and Hannah: Catherine is haunted by Catherine’s “buried life—by a contingent existence, the inexpressible, a way of life she had not chosen or had abandoned.” “Hannah’s life had not been haunted by” Hannah’s buried life.

This is both Hannah’s strength and her limitation. She had followed seemingly only one course, had never divided herself into opposing factions, “had forgotten herself long ago, perhaps in the great battle of the white eunuchs with the black eunuchs.” “She did not need to wage war with herself.” She had an external enemy (man, physical and present) and this meant she never had to confront the battle within.

Catherine’s many selves (“the look-out in the crow’s-nest, the negress in the belfry, the human barometer,” “the lady in the phaeton, the figurehead in the foam”) are the price of Catherine’s interior complexity, her willingness to contain contradictions. Hannah’s single-mindedness, her unity of character, was what Catherine most admired and least resembled. It was also what made Hannah unable to fully reach her.

Young adds her most cautionary note here: “the extremities of faith in anything might always beget” its opposite. Hannah’s total faith in the suffrage movement may have contained, at its extreme limit, its own negation. “Death might be the parent of love—or love” the parent of death. Catherine had always known this. Hannah, apparently, had not.

What Hannah could not do

The chapter contains one of Young’s most careful negative portraits: all the things Hannah could not do or be. She could not dance. She could not take her place in a minuet, balancing between right foot and left, shifting weight from one side to the other. She had never appeared in a ballroom. She had escorted no lady to the dance.

She had no time for intimate revelation, for the moment when one confesses that one is “the opposite of what one seemed, that all one’s life” has been a living lie, “that one had loved in secret what one” denied in public. That is the confession she could not make. That is what the loss of the skirt was about.

Young also tells us that Hannah had refused a stained glass window in her memory; “she had believed in no image, neither the living nor the dead.” After five chapters of Hannah’s life, this detail lands with unexpected weight. The woman who had ridden through every country, who had seen everything, who had known the secrets of husbands whispered to dead wives and wives whispered to dead husbands: refused to be made into an image. She would not be fixed. She would not be memorialized. “She had believed in no image,” which means she had believed in no representation of herself as a single, stable thing.

What to watch for as you keep reading

With Chapter 35, the Cousin Hannah sequence closes, and Volume One of the novel is drawing toward its end. We are in the last chapters of the first movement. After the break, the novel will follow Vera on her bus journey and into the wider American landscape Young has been preparing us for.

The image Catherine uses of herself (these many selves, these fragmentations of partial selves, splintered mirrors and partial images) is the novel’s self-description. Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is a novel of partial selves and splintered mirrors. Every character is an incomplete portrait of the others. Each is complete only in combination with the rest. Young has been building this from the first page.

Hannah’s observation that it was colder at home (colder than the Antarctic, colder than the glacial mountains, colder than any place the great explorers had discovered and named after their wives) is the novel’s most compressed statement about why people leave. The sleeping lady in the bed of snow, “with the snow falling on a lacy counterpane”: this is what the great Antarctic explorers were escaping. This is what Catherine has been: the cold at the center of the house. The one who cannot be warmed.
