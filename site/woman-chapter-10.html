Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 10

← Chapter 9 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 11 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Vera cannot picture her own wedding, so she dreams of others', and the one that arrives is impossible on purpose. Mr. Spitzer stands as the bride. Miss MacIntosh, black umbrella in hand, stands as the bridegroom. This is the crossing point in its clearest scene, the place where man and woman trade. The swap is not a trick played on gender norms. It is the book showing that the categories the ceremony depends on, one fixed man and one fixed woman, were never solid to begin with.

The preacher runs the wedding rite and the funeral rite together until neither survives, and the two fuse exactly where this edition's marriage entry says they must. Marriage demands a single, stable self from people who are many, and when the demand meets the book's people it collapses into its opposite, the wedding into the funeral, the boundary between life and death giving way. When the sermon turns to "Oh, woman, where are thy breasts? Where is thy womb?" the gendered body itself is being mourned and unmade, the body coming apart, a line that reaches straight to Miss MacIntosh's own missing breast.

Mr. Spitzer carries the chapter's plural selfhood. He lies in two beds at once, he would "marry no one but his brother worm," and he nods no while he answers yes, a self at odds with itself before the reader has learned how many of him there are. Vera's laughter at the scene is not derision. It is recognition. And her closing fear of the "necromancers" and "old rapists" by the sea, set against her reach for what is "good, true, beautiful" beyond the clouds, is the book's real-and-dream country closing the chapter on the note it keeps: the ordinary world and the dreamed one are one place, and the seam between them is where these people live.
