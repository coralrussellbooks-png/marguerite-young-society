On Mother-Blaming and the Transferred Grief

The mother’s behavior toward Esther (the repeated accusation of infanticide, the cold withdrawal of nurture, the tying to the tree, the blow for the bee) is what To All My Darlings identifies as severe emotional abuse rooted in unprocessed grief. Psychological literature on parents who lose a child documents a range of responses in the surviving children: survivor guilt, distorted attachment, the child becoming the repository of the parent’s grief rather than the recipient of the parent’s love. Parents with narcissistic traits may be particularly prone to this displacement: the child’s existence becomes an affront to the lost child’s memory, and the parent’s emotional needs are met by blaming rather than by loving. Young does not use clinical language. She renders the abuse in Esther’s voice as part of the landscape she grew up in: the mother small and cold and fragile, everything in the house built to her size, the accusation delivered with simultaneous laughter and tears. The laugh-cry is significant; it is the affect of someone who has lost the boundary between grief and cruelty.
