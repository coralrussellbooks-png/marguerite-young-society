Pillar: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible · Across every book

Act III: The Flat Horizon

Words do not describe an already existing world. They build it, sentence by sentence, through their own long unfolding.

The everyday view of language is a camera. The world sits there, finished, and words take its picture. A good sentence is an accurate snapshot and a bad one is blurry. Yet language rarely behaves like a camera. "I do" makes a marriage. "You're fired" ends a job. A diagnosis changes how a person sees their own body. Words are not only pictures of the world. They are tools that build it.

The Framework

Ludwig Wittgenstein wrote in the Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus (1921) that "The limits of my language mean the limits of my world." In that early book he still imagined language as a kind of picture. Later, in Philosophical Investigations (1953), he changed his mind. Meaning, he argued, comes from use, from the many "language games" people play inside shared ways of living. The later Wittgenstein makes the Tractatus sentence even stronger. Language does not only mark the edge of the world. It is part of the machinery that builds the world, one game at a time.

Marguerite Young wrote as if she knew this in her hands. Her sentences in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling can run for a page, adding clause to clause, image to image, qualification to qualification. They do not report a scene that already exists. The scene comes into being as the sentence goes on, and it would vanish if the sentence stopped. This is what Radical Immanence means by construction through prolonged execution. The world of the novel is exactly as large as its sentences are long.

This is not only a literary trick. It happens in towns and households every day. In 2007 a children's dictionary in Britain dropped a set of nature words, such as acorn, bluebell, and kingfisher, to make room for words like broadband and blog. The writer Robert Macfarlane took the change as a warning. When children lose the names for things, he argued, they stop seeing the things. His book Landmarks (2015) gathers hundreds of old, local words for weather, water, and land, many close to disappearing. Each word is a small tool for noticing.

The same logic holds on the inside. A person who calls themselves "a failure" builds a different day than a person who says "I'm learning something hard." Changing vocabulary does not change facts, but it does change which facts become visible and which paths look open.

The Art Companion

The Lost Words (2017), by Robert Macfarlane with paintings by Jackie Morris, is a large illustrated book built from the words the dictionary dropped. Each word is summoned back with a spell-poem and a painting. Schools and communities across Britain raised money to place copies in every primary school in some regions. The book is a picture of language as rebuilding.

Gertrude Stein's Tender Buttons (1914) takes ordinary household objects, a box, a cup, a piece of coffee, and describes them in sentences that refuse normal sense. The effect is strange at first, then freeing. Familiar things become new because the words around them are new.

Reflection Questions

  • Which words do I use about myself that build a smaller world than the one I actually live in?

  • What in my daily surroundings have I never had a name for?

  • Which phrase from childhood still builds the walls of a room I live in?

  • When did a single sentence someone spoke change my world, for better or worse?

The Exercise: A Hyper-Local Dictionary

  1. Spend one day noticing things in the immediate environment that have no name: the patch of floor where afternoon light lands, the sound the radiator makes before it wakes, the corner where neighbors always stop to talk.

  2. Choose five of these and invent or borrow a word for each. Ask older relatives or neighbors whether an old local word already exists.

  3. Write each word on a card with a one-sentence definition. Post the cards somewhere visible.

  4. Use the words for one week, in a journal, in conversation, or with the household. At the end, write a short note on what changed. Did the named things become easier to see? Did the place feel different?
