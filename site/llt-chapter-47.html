Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 47

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: Not One Self · The Past Doesn't Go Away · Language Shapes What's Possible · The Sacred Is Here

The Questions

Both Cousin Hannah and Mr. Spitzer “deferred life to the next life.” What does the book make of a life postponed to a beyond?

“Could I have been a woman, could I have been a man, this great battle never would have taken place.” What is Cousin Hannah blaming, at the end, for her lost love?

Mr. Spitzer counts on his dead brother as “a Welcoming Committee of One” on the other side. Is the twin a comfort, or the beyond wearing a familiar face?

“At any moment he might walk out of his skin as out of an old cloak.” What is a self that can be taken off like a coat?

The chapter names the failing that even Mr. Spitzer shares. Cousin Hannah, dying, “had deferred her life to the next life,” and “even like himself, Mr. Spitzer thought,” she had done what he does: postponed the living to a beyond, loved a love “which should exceed this life, be in death that which it had not been in life.” The Sacred Is Here, which the last chapter found so firmly in the pebble underfoot, meets its counter-pull here, and the book is honest that the model of open living feels it too. Mr. Spitzer values the secular spark and defers his life to the next world in the same breath. No one, not even him, is only open; the yearning for the elsewhere is in the man who best knows the here.

Cousin Hannah’s last blaming falls on the categories. “Could I have been a woman, could I have been a man, this great battle never would have taken place.” She traces her lost love, at the end, to the fixed identities of man and woman, the built categories the dream-wedding first dissolved, and half-sees that the war of her life was a war those categories made. This is Language Shapes What's Possible arriving at a deathbed as grief: the nouns that fix a person as woman or man, the grammar she never thought to question, are what she now names as the thing that cost her the love. She cannot unmake them, and she dies inside them, but she sees, too late, that they were made.

The dead brother as “Welcoming Committee of One” is the beyond wearing the most familiar face. Mr. Spitzer counts on his twin to greet him “on the other side of this great void,” and the comfort is real and is also the promised elsewhere made intimate, the beyond furnished with the one person he most wants there. This is Not One Self and the deferral to the next life fused: the twin who blurs into him in this world waits for him in the next, so that the boundary he cannot find between himself and his brother becomes the boundary between this life and the beyond he keeps postponing his living toward. The comfort and the seal are the same brother.

And the self here can be taken off like a coat. “At any moment he might walk out of his skin as out of an old cloak.” The aging Mr. Spitzer, watching even the moon grow “imperceptibly older” and the stars lose weight, feels his own self as something removable, worn, not identical with him. This is Not One Self at its most loosely held: not a bounded thing he is but a garment he is in, that he might step out of at any moment. The Past Doesn't Go Away works on him as it works on the moon, everything aging together, and the self it ages is a cloak, not a core, one more thing worn for now and set down.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that keeps Mr. Spitzer purely the man of the here against Cousin Hannah’s beyond, and the reading that takes his dead brother’s welcome as a settled consolation rather than a temptation. Neither the flawless model nor the secured comfort is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that even Mr. Spitzer defers his life to the next life, so that the yearning for the beyond is in the man who best knows the sacred underfoot, and no one is only open. Cousin Hannah, dying, blames the built categories of man and woman for the love they cost her, Language Shapes What's Possible come to a deathbed as grief. The dead brother is comfort and the beyond wearing a familiar face at once, the twin who blurs into him here waiting for him there. And the self is a cloak that can be walked out of, worn for now, aged like the moon. Even the model of the here feels the pull of the elsewhere, and the self is a garment, not a core. The chapter holds the pull without endorsing the beyond.

For Reflection

What in one’s own living is quietly deferred to some next life, some later, some beyond?

Can a genuine comfort also be a way of postponing the present toward an elsewhere?

What would change if the self were felt as a cloak one wears rather than a core one is?
