Lorena: The Life Made From a Confession

← Cousin Hannah Freemount-Snowden · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Mr. Bonebreaker →

Characters: Jacqueline White · Lorena · Madge Capehorn · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Lorena is not summoned out of nothing the way the others are. She was real, and she was loved, and she comes into the book by accident, pulled out of Miss MacIntosh one morning by a question Vera did not expect to matter. Vera had always been "certain that Miss MacIntosh could have no past," that the plain, red-haired nursemaid who stood for truth to nature was exactly as much as she appeared and no more. Lorena is the past that certainty had erased. She is the one love of the one person in the novel who was supposed to have no secrets, and she is the crack that runs straight through the book's idea of the real.

She was a dancer. Dark, "very dark, fat and not thin," with black hair "like the midnight curtain concealing the star until the star was revealed," she was in the orphanage that Miss MacIntosh worked in, laughed at her, and ran off to become a strip-tease artist, a fan dancer with a feather fan. Years later she came back into Miss MacIntosh's life the way the lost come back in this book, by chance and by force, paroled into her care, so that the nursemaid was now the parole officer of the girl she loved. Then Lorena ran again. "I loved only once," Miss MacIntosh says, and it was this, a beautiful girl who laughed at her and left twice.

Losing Lorena is what unmade the one steady person in the novel. Miss MacIntosh cannot chase her, because she is the officer sworn to hold her, and so she does the only thing left, which is to come apart. "I wandered. I wandered on and on. I broke my nose. I lost my breast. I had no place. I walked into the traffic flow." The woman who was the moral guide, the anchor, the one who told Vera not to let her mind wander from reality, wandered herself, out of her post and into the road, because a fan dancer left her twice. The rock of the reality that Miss MacIntosh was supposed to represent turns out to have been cracked from the start by a love it could not hold.

The secret Lorena knew is worn on Miss MacIntosh's head. Miss MacIntosh is bald and hides it under a wig, and the wig is the truest object in the portrait, a made surface pulled over a bare truth, put on quickly when footsteps come and thrown off when they recede. She wore it black at first because Lorena's hair had been black, and only later red, so that the concealment was also a memorial, the beloved's color kept on the head that lost her. The novel's one figure of truth to nature spends her days managing a false surface over a real absence, which is as exact a picture of how this book thinks reality works as anything in it. The plain woman is a construction too, and the construction is where the grief is kept.

Lorena and Miss MacIntosh are opposite faces of one longing. "She sought the light as I have sought the shadow," Miss MacIntosh says, the dancer under the spotlight and the nursemaid under the umbrella, the one who took everything off and the one who could not take off even her wig. But the two orientations meet at the same bare place. Lorena "snatched them all bald until she became old and bald herself, bald as an old man," and Miss MacIntosh was bald all along, so that exposure and concealment arrived at the identical naked head. And it was Lorena who had seen that head first, who "had known, laughing behind her fan," the one person who looked under the cover and stayed long enough to laugh. The love and the exposure were the same act.

Vera never meets her. Everything of Lorena reaches the reader through Miss MacIntosh's guarded, self-interrupting telling, a confession she keeps trying to stop, breaking off when "she stopped herself suddenly as if she were going to tell me too much," saying they have talked long enough, wondering why she should think of Lorena dancing in the spotlight at all. To know Lorena is to receive the version a bald, grieving woman will allow, drawn out of her by a girl's persistence and pushed back down again. She is the known built by the knower one more time, except that here the known was real, and loved, and the knower would rather not build her at all, and does anyway because she cannot help it.

This is where the method turns on its own foundation. Miss MacIntosh was the reason Vera went searching, the solid thing at the bottom of a dreaming world, the one who was not to be taken in. Lorena is the proof that the anchor was a wig. If even the woman who stood for the plain and the real is a concealed surface over a bare and grieving head, kept in the color of a girl who laughed and left, then the real in this book is not the opposite of the made. It is a thing worn. The most trustworthy person in the novel is trustworthy the way a wig is, a careful cover pulled over a loss, and the truest fact about her is the one she spent her whole life keeping under it, which has a name, and the name is Lorena.

The turn for a writer is about excavation rather than invention. Madge was made from a blank sleeping face and Jacqueline from a name cried in sleep, both conjured out of nearly nothing. Lorena is the reverse operation. She was real, and loved, and she has to be dug out of the one person who would rather not speak of her at all, drawn up against that person's own resistance, and given only in the version a bald, grieving woman will permit before she "stopped herself suddenly as if she were going to tell me too much." That is the other half of the work, not building a figure out of air but drawing a buried real thing out of a guarded source, and accepting the partial, self-interrupting account, because the reticence is itself true to the teller. The deepest material is almost always the thing the source least wants to give, kept under the wig, worn in the black of a girl who laughed and left and later in memorial red. And Lorena turns the whole method back on its own foundation, because Miss MacIntosh was supposed to be the solid ground, the one figure who was not made up, the floor the invention stood on, and she proves to be a careful surface pulled over a loss like everyone else. A writer who trusts that some characters are simply the real ones, the reliable floor beneath the dreaming, should remember that the floor is wearing a wig, and that the steadiest person in the book was assembled over a grief she would not name until a patient listener drew it out of her one guarded sentence at a time.
