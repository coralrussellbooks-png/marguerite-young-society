Chapter 11: The Alphabet of Everything

← Chapter 10 · Guide Home · Chapter 12 →

Research notes: Chapter 11

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Philosophy: Augustine and Christian Theology · Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Ancient Egypt · Astronomy · Exploration and the Poles · Greek Mythology · Music and Composers · Sir Walter Raleigh · The History of Opium

Chapter 11 is one of the shortest in the novel. You could read it in five minutes. But don’t let that fool you into thinking it’s minor, because it contains one of the most quietly devastating sentences Vera will speak in the entire book, and it sets up the long comedy of Mr. Spitzer’s structural absence that will run through dozens of chapters to come.

Take a breath. We’re in good shape here.

What actually happens in Chapter 11

Almost nothing, in terms of external event. Vera pauses in her narration to reflect on her own situation. Her mother, Catherine, is somewhere deep in her opium voyage, working through the P’s, Q’s, and R’s of her alphabetical guest list. Phantom visitors from history, geography, mythology, and art are assembling in her velvet bedroom. Mr. Spitzer, as always, has missed the whole thing.

That’s it. But what Young puts inside that frame is extraordinary.

What Vera is thinking about

The chapter opens with Vera asking herself a question she has no answer to: “how should I endure this isolation”?

She cannot be like her mother. She knows that. Catherine has retreated entirely into hallucination, and the opium world she inhabits is complete and self-sustaining. But Vera can’t go there. Miss MacIntosh trained her too well. She will always be able to tell exactly where the dream ends and the reality begins, and that clarity, which sounds like a gift, turns out to be its own kind of sentence. If you can’t dissolve into illusion, and the real world is as bleak as the one Vera is living in, what exactly do you do with yourself?

Young doesn’t answer the question. She pivots, as she so often does, into the gorgeous absurdist theater of Catherine’s guest list. But hold onto that opening. It’s the emotional engine underneath everything that follows.

Catherine’s guests, and why the alphabet matters

Catherine has been working through her visitors alphabetically, and by Chapter 11 she is somewhere in the Q’s and R’s. This detail is funny, but it’s also doing real work.

The alphabet is humanity’s most basic tool for organizing everything. We sort libraries with it. We file documents. We impose order on the chaos of the world by putting it in A-B-C sequence. What Young is showing you is what happens when you apply that system to the entire universe: geography, history, mythology, the living, the dead, the imagined. The system keeps trying to hold, and the universe keeps overflowing it. The Q’s alone are “a naval battle, an island, and a continent.” The R’s sprawl into pharaohs, composers, portrait painters, constellations, and a headless Elizabethan aristocrat running an atheist society.

The alphabet cannot contain what Catherine contains. Nothing can. That’s the joke, and also the tragedy.

The guests worth knowing

Queen Charlotte Islands arrives quietly, exchanging a word or two with Catherine about the climate and a missing courtier. She is a real place, an archipelago off the coast of British Columbia, made into a person, a cold and polite acquaintance from another summer. Young loves this move: collapsing the geographical into the personal, turning landscape into someone you might have known.

Queen Maud Mountains is a different order of visitor entirely. This is an Antarctic mountain range (five hundred miles of frozen, phantasmal peaks) arriving in Catherine’s bedroom as the most imperious houseguest imaginable. She wears glacial white. She exchanges no words. Her skirt is “five hundred miles long and made of pure Brussels lace.” She carries a white parasol that happens to belong to Catherine.

That last detail is the one to hold. The guest has absorbed the hostess’s own possessions. The boundary between Catherine and her visitors is completely porous. What is hers and what has been dreamed into the room can no longer be distinguished.

Rameses I, II, and III arrive as a single bemused pharaoh who is in fact three pharaohs layered inside each other, each unwrapping to find an older king inside. He has been “partitioned into many selves without his knowing it, without his consent.” As Rameses III, he has been dreaming “the dreams of Rameses I who” is already dead. This is one of Young’s most precise images of what fragmented identity actually feels like from the inside: not dramatic, not chosen, just the quiet discovery that you are multiple, and that some of those selves preceded you.

Sir Walter Raleigh arrives last and most spectacularly. He wears white satin embroidered with seed pearls. He removes his hat, and removes his head along with it. He asks to be called “by his intimate name, Ocean or Water.” His detached head then explains that he presides over a secret society devoted to atheistic propositions, including the one that God is dog spelled backward.

Raleigh was actually beheaded in 1618, so his appearing with a removable head is grounded in historical fact, delivered with perfect Young deadpan. He really was known for facing his execution with unusual composure. Young takes that historical detail and runs it to its logical absurdist conclusion: if you can face the removal of your head with equanimity, you might as well carry it around with you.

Mr. Spitzer, who is always missing the party

Mr. Spitzer does not appear in this chapter. He is defined entirely by his absence from it.

Catherine catalogs, with tactless relish, everything he missed: Rimski-Korsakov, Sir Joshua Reynolds, the Great Ram constellation, “Raphael’s tomb, the songs of Ronsard,” and of course Sir Walter Raleigh with his detachable head. She reminds Mr. Spitzer of his absences the way you might remind someone that they missed the best party of the year, with the particular cruelty of someone who knows they will keep missing it.

And Rhadamanthus, one of the mythological judges of the dead, has already tried Mr. Spitzer in absentia and found him guilty of many forgotten crimes.

Poor Mr. Spitzer. He is always out performing dusty legal errands for people who don’t particularly want them performed. He is always arriving just after the extraordinary thing has happened. Young will spend hundreds of pages building this portrait of a man who is structurally, constitutionally, cosmically late to everything that matters. It is one of the novel’s great sustained jokes, and underneath the joke is something genuinely sorrowful.

What to watch for as you keep reading

The question Vera asks at the opening of this chapter (how do I endure isolation when I can’t dissolve into illusion?) is the question the entire novel is organized around. Every character in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is working out some version of it. Some dissolve. Some can’t. Some spend the entire book trying to decide.

The alphabetical guest list will keep going. As Catherine moves through the letters, the guests will keep arriving and multiplying, and Mr. Spitzer will keep missing them. Let the comedy build. It is doing something serious underneath.

Notice how often Catherine’s guests bring their own geography with them. Queen Charlotte Islands brings her climate. Queen Maud Mountains brings five hundred miles of skirt. Raleigh brings the Atlantic Ocean in his name. The outer world keeps collapsing into the bedroom, which is another way of saying: Catherine’s inner world is large enough to contain everything.
