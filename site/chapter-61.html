To All My Darlings

Chapter 61

On Chapter 61 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 11, 2026

← Chapter 60 · Home · Chapter 62 →

Content note: This chapter touches on suicide and Mr. Spitzer’s sense of living on as one who “had committed suicide and lived.”

Summary

In a thieves’ café, Mr. Spitzer befriends an old mute who has trained a frog to speak in his mouth. One rainy night the frog recites the Lord’s Prayer; later it asks for a light and a nickel for coffee and tells him to put his money on the Robins. When the frog dies on Maundy Thursday, Mr. Spitzer goes with the mute to bury it in a cigar box in a wild meadow far inland, addresses a funeral speech to the frog, and drops a red rose into the grave. On the walk home through the city at dawn he guides the grieving mute through the traffic, hears a bullfrog croak “Too deep, too deep,” and thinks about the deaf: what the mute has lost and what he has been spared, and whether a man who cannot hear is the ideal audience for Mr. Spitzer’s own unheard music, scattered on scraps all over Boston, Philadelphia, Trenton, Camden, and Asbury Park.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with the remarkable story of an old mute who, through a frog trained to sing in his mouth, can “speak” and “croak” human words and even the Lord’s Prayer. This passage is surreal and symbolic, underscoring themes of voice and silence, communication beyond normal means, and the miracle of expression despite physical limitations. The frog is both instrument and voice for the mute, representing a fragile connection to the world of sound and speech.
  • Mr. Spitzer accompanies the mute in burying the frog, which had been the mute’s only voice. The description of the burial site as an upland flower-strewn meadow with wildflowers, grasses, and symbolic plants is deeply symbolic of mortality and the cycle of life and death. Mr. Spitzer reflects on mortality, mourning not just the frog but the brevity and fragility of life itself. The mute’s grief is portrayed as profound and inexpressible, a voiceless sorrow that Mr. Spitzer tries to honor through silent companionship.
  • The mute’s expressive hands create a rich, complex sign language full of symbols (crosses, circles, directions, and gestures) that Mr. Spitzer tries to interpret. This passage highlights communication beyond words and the ambiguity and depth of silent expression. It stresses the idea that silence can be full of meaning, though often ambiguous and elusive.
  • Mr. Spitzer reflects extensively on the mute’s condition as a deaf mute living in a world full of sound and language. The mute is portrayed as a stranger in a foreign land, unable to access the rich auditory world around him. This section meditates on the isolating nature of silence and deafness, the loss of abstract concepts and sensory experiences, and the profound loneliness that comes with it. Mr. Spitzer envies the mute’s escape from the distractions and dissonances of sound, yet mourns the tragic loss of connection and communication.
  • Despite being a musician, Mr. Spitzer feels that his music is lost or unheard, especially by the mute who cannot hear. This intertwines the theme of isolation in the artist’s life with the mute’s silence. Mr. Spitzer’s compositions are described as “inaudible music,” music that exists more as internal experience than as shared sound. This passage emphasizes the paradox of creating art that may never be truly communicated or appreciated.
  • Throughout the chapter, Mr. Spitzer contemplates mortality, the fleeting nature of life, and the illusions of time. The mute, unable to hear clocks or the city’s awakening, exists outside conventional time, which deepens the symbolic resonance of death and silence. Mr. Spitzer’s philosophical musings on existence, memory, and the passage of time add a profound existential dimension to the narrative.
  • The chapter repeatedly contrasts the world of sound (the bustling city, music, natural noises) with the silence of the mute. Silence is portrayed not as mere absence but as a profound presence, “greater than sound.” This duality explores the limits of human experience and the mystery of what may lie beyond sensory perception.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • When the frog croaks “Got a light, mister?” it is “this simple question seeming the real miracle.” Why does the ordinary question seem more miraculous than the prayer?
  • Mr. Spitzer promises the frog, “thou shalt be a man, little frog, in the next world,” then asks himself “what ego was this.” What is he mourning, the frog or himself?
  • Mr. Spitzer weighs whether the bullfrog’s cry means “too, to, two.” How does the pun lead him back to Peron?

Characters

  • Young writes that “the mute had learned love.” What does his grief over a frog show about him?
  • Mr. Spitzer “could not help almost envying him.” What does he envy in the mute’s deafness, and is the envy fair?
  • Mr. Spitzer is irritated that “the way back had seemed shorter than the way they had come.” Why does this small complaint fit his whole life?

Connections

  • The frog “had asked for a match, a coin.” How does this chapter carry on Mr. Spitzer’s coin-giving from Chapter 59?
  • “Death was his coachman.” Where else in the novel does death drive a carriage?
  • Mr. Spitzer has scattered his music “all over Boston.” Why might Young wait so long to name his city?

Reflection

  • “Silence spoke with an almost human voice.” When does silence speak?
  • “Reality is language, Mr. Spitzer said.” Is there a reality beyond what can be said or signed?
  • Mr. Spitzer believes “a man was fortunate if he lived for one moment in twenty-four hours.” What makes such a moment?

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • An old, reflective, and solitary musician, a retired lawyer turned silent observer of life.
  • He is contemplative, mourning not only the frog and the mute but also larger existential losses.
  • Characterized by his flowing black mourning cloak, and his slow, deliberate movements.
  • His music is described as unheard, symbolic of his isolation and the futility of his art in a silent world.

The Old Mute

  • A silent figure who communicates through sign language and an extraordinary frog trained to “speak” by croaking words.
  • Embodies the theme of silence, loss, and the difficulty of communication.
  • His grief over the frog’s death is profound and inexpressible except through gestures.

The Singing Frog

  • A small frog trained to sing and speak by the mute, symbolizing voice, life, and the fragile link between silence and sound.
  • Its death symbolizes the loss of voice and communication, a tragedy for the mute.

Further notes from the text

  • The chapter opens on a chain of reversals, night as day, dawn as twilight, beginning as end, which sets the terms for Mr. Spitzer’s friendship with a man who speaks only in signs.
  • The frog that speaks the Lord’s Prayer is the comic center. See Comedy in Chapter 61.
  • The burial ground is an upland meadow a mile or two inland, among plovers, woodpeckers, and old fences, beyond the reach of the sea but not beyond its sound.
  • Mr. Spitzer is irritated that the way back is short and direct: if so, why did they go such a long way, circling through fog and mistaking reflections for the real thing? The complaint fits his whole life.
  • Sign language becomes a theology: many things said by one sign, many never expressed, “like the sign language of God,” who wrote the world in hieroglyphic birds, beasts, and flowers.
  • Testing the mute’s hearing, Mr. Spitzer holds up a watch to the man’s dead ear, then realizes he has lifted his own dead arm, on his deaf side, with the broken watch on his dead side.
  • Near the end comes a long catalogue of sea shells drifting from pole to pole, including the left-handed stromb, a rare shell that coils the wrong way.

Research: Resources

  • “And his night was his day, and his day was his night”: Young goes on: “his dawn was his twilight, and his moon was his sun, and his sun was his moon, and his beginning was his end, and his end was his beginning.” These statements encapsulate the core of the reversal philosophy: the collapsing of distinctions that we usually take for granted. See the philosophy of reversals entry in the Chapter 37 resources.
  • “the Lord’s Prayer, Our Father Who Art in Heaven”: “Our Father which art in heaven, Hallowed be thy name” (Matthew 6:9, KJV). Young gives the prayer in the Catholic and Book of Common Prayer form, “who art.” See The Bible.
  • “by labial and laryngeal and dental manipulations”: Labial is related to the lips. Laryngeal is related to the larynx. Dental sounds are made with the tongue against the teeth.
  • “Put your money on the Robins”: Mr. Spitzer wasn’t sure if he meant the song “When the Red, Red Robin (Comes Bob, Bob, Bobbin’ Along)” (Harry Woods, 1926) or if he meant the baseball team.
    • The Dodgers were founded in 1883 as the Brooklyn Atlantics. The team was known alternatively as the Bridegrooms, Grooms, Superbas, Robins, and Trolley Dodgers before officially becoming the Brooklyn Dodgers in the 1930s. The team moved to Los Angeles in 1958. They were the Robins from 1914 to 1931, named for their manager, Wilbert Robinson. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “his little frog like a mouth harp”: The Jew’s harp, also known as jaw harp, juice harp, or mouth harp, is a lamellophone instrument, consisting of a flexible metal or bamboo tongue or reed attached to a frame. Despite the colloquial name, the Jew’s harp most likely originated in China, with the earliest known Jew’s harps dating back 4,000 years ago from Shaanxi province. It has no relation to the Jewish people. Jew’s harps may be categorized as idioglot or heteroglot (whether or not the frame and the tine are one piece); by the shape of the frame (rod or plaque); by the number of tines, and whether the tines are plucked, joint-tapped, or string-pulled.
    • In America “mouth harp” usually means the harmonica. See Music and Instruments.
  • “an Enrico Caruso among the frogs”: Enrico Caruso was a famous Italian operatic tenor. His records made him the first great star of the phonograph; he died in 1921. See Music and Composers.
  • “meadows of Queen Anne’s lace”: Queen Anne’s lace is the wild carrot. Legend says Queen Anne pricked her finger while making lace, and the single dark red floret at the center of the white flower head is her drop of blood. See Roses and Flowers.
  • “the great bitterns booming in the clouds”: The American bittern’s booming spring call carries far across the marsh; country people called the bird the thunder-pumper and the stake-driver. See Birds.
  • “the lute turtle sleeping in the mud”: The leatherback sea turtle is also called the lute turtle, or luth, for the ridges along its shell, which reminded French naturalists of a lute. See The Sea and Water.
  • “the recording angel in the cloud”: See Sterne’s recording angel in the Chapter 55 resources.
  • “man is but a fleeting shadow and fades as grass”: “He cometh forth like a flower, and is cut down: he fleeth also as a shadow, and continueth not” (Job 14:2, KJV); “The grass withereth, the flower fadeth” (Isaiah 40:8, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “cloud cherubim riding sea-horses with their long, gilded necks”: The sea-horses of Greek myth, the hippocamps, are horses in front and fish behind; they draw Poseidon’s chariot over the waves. See Greek Mythology.
  • “a gentleman’s nosegay”: A gentleman’s nosegay is a small, sweet-scented bouquet of flowers and herbs, similar to a modern boutonnière, that was worn pinned to a man’s clothing. It was used historically for both practical and symbolic purposes.
  • “perhaps of mignonette”: Mignonette (Reseda odorata), “little darling” in French, is a small, sweet-scented garden flower long loved for nosegays. See Roses and Flowers.
    • Mignonette is a classic French sauce, primarily served with raw oysters and other seafood, that consists of red wine vinegar, finely minced shallots, and cracked black pepper.
  • “five-flowered gentian named in memory of a dead Illyrian king”: Five-flowered gentian is a North American plant, also called stiff gentian or agueweed, known for its light blue or violet-blue, 5-lobed flowers that often appear in clusters.
    • “dead Illyrian king” may refer to Gentius, the last king of the Illyrian Kingdom. He ruled from 181 to 168 BC and is known for his final, unsuccessful resistance against the expansion of the Roman Republic. His defeat marked the end of Illyrian independence, as the region was absorbed into the Roman Empire. Pliny says the plant was named for Gentius, who discovered its powers (Natural History, Book 25).
  • “an idee fixe”: In Berlioz’s Symphonie fantastique (1830), the idée fixe is the melody that stands for the beloved and returns, changed, in every movement. See Music and Composers.
  • “hedgehogs stand with their backs always to the sea”: Mr. Spitzer thought Aristotle said that hedgehogs always stand with their backs to the sea. Aristotle: History of Animals IX says they look out of their holes in the direction of the wind. See Aristotle.
  • “like many other old bindlestiffs”: Bindlestiffs is another word for a hobo who carries a bedroll.
  • “the Etruscans had set stones at the doors of the houses of the dead”: Etruscans marked graves differently depending on the sex of the deceased, most famously in the necropolis at Cerveteri. This practice used carved stone markers called cippi. While the form and style of grave markers varied by region and over time, distinct gender-specific shapes were a notable tradition in certain Etruscan communities. There is less evidence for marking graves differently based on the age of the deceased, although separate burials for children did exist.
    • The Sarcophagus of the Spouses from Cerveteri (c. 520 BC, Villa Giulia, Rome) shows a husband and wife reclining together as if at a banquet; Etruscan tombs held engraved bronze mirrors. See Weddings and Funerals.
  • “The Eskimos dug a hole in the ice”: The Inuit more often laid their dead on the land under cairns of stones, since the frozen ground could not be dug.
  • “Some of the Indians had lashed their dead to the boughs of trees”: North American First Nations and other indigenous peoples tied their dead to tree branches called a Burial tree.
  • “The Egyptians had put their dead into swan boats under the sand”: Boats were buried with Egyptian kings for the voyage of the dead; Khufu’s cedar boat, buried beside the Great Pyramid, was found in 1954. See Ancient Egypt.
    • Royal Egyptians were buried in funerary boats.
  • “he is wrapped in his sail and should have had a sea burial”: At sea the dead were sewn into a hammock or a length of sail, weighted with shot, and slid over the side while the captain read the burial service; by sailors’ custom the last stitch went through the nose. See The Sea and Water.
  • “milkwort which is believed to cause the milk to flow in the breasts of nurses”: Mr. Spitzer listed the different plants and their properties that were in the meadow: milkwort, cockscomb, moonwort, Dutchman’s pipes and breeches, jack-in-the-pulpit, coneflowers, cosmos, orchis, hedge hyssop, nightshade, heliotrope, cattails, oxlip, bladder campion, frog lilies, snakeroot, crowfoot, couch grass, crab grass, pectoral grass, mouse ear, snakemouth, yellow marsh mallows, periwinkle, penny-royals, marsh flags, star grass, starflowers, wild rambler roses, moon vines. The plants mentioned are not just decorative details but carry symbolic meaning linked to life, death, mourning, transformation, and the boundary between worlds. See On the meadow plants.
    • Milkwort: Traditionally associated with nurturing and fertility (believed to increase milk flow in nurses), milkwort symbolizes nurturing life and the continuation of existence beyond death. Its presence at the frog’s grave suggests a hope for renewal or sustenance beyond mortality.
    • Cockscomb: Its bright, flame-like shape often symbolizes boldness, uniqueness, or the vibrancy of life. Within the mournful scene, it contrasts the somber tone, perhaps hinting at the persistence of life’s vividness amid death.
    • Moonwort: A fern associated with the moon and mystical powers, moonwort evokes mystery, transformation, and the hidden realms of the night. It symbolizes the passage from life to death, the “moonlit” or shadowy journey of the soul.
    • Dutchman’s pipes and breeches, Jack-in-the-pulpit: These uniquely shaped plants are often symbolic of the strange, peculiar, and sometimes spiritual or supernatural. They may represent the oddity of existence, the spiritual “voices” or signs that speak silently, fitting the mute’s world.
    • Coneflowers, Cosmos, Orchis: These flowers are often linked to harmony, beauty, and spiritual order. Orchis, an orchid, is tied to fertility and rebirth. Cosmos stands for order and peace. Their presence suggests the coexistence of death and spiritual continuity and harmony.
    • Hedge hyssop: Traditionally connected with protection and purification, this plant could symbolize a guarding of the soul or the sacredness of the burial site.
    • Nightshade: A poisonous plant, nightshade often symbolizes danger, death, and the dark side of life. Its inclusion points to mortality and the ever-present shadow of death.
    • Heliotrope: Known for turning towards the sun, heliotrope symbolizes devotion and eternal love. This flower’s orientation towards the sun might represent a soul’s yearning for light beyond death.
    • Cattails, Oxlip, Bladder campion: Wetland plants like cattails and marsh flowers connect to themes of liminality or places between water and land, life and death. These plants symbolize transition, the fluid boundaries between states of being.
    • Frog lilies: Named evocatively, these lilies spotted with rainy light tie directly to the frog’s character and his watery world, symbolizing the fragile beauty of life and the connection between the mute, the frog, and nature.
    • Snakeroot, Crowfoot: Plants with names associated with animals or mystical traits may symbolize danger, cunning, or the cyclical nature of life and death.
    • Couch grass, Crab grass, Pectoral grass, Mouse-ear, Snakemouth: Hardy, common grasses and plants embody persistence, survival, and the mundane continuity of life amid the extraordinary event of death.
    • Yellow marsh mallows, Periwinkle, Penny-royals, Marsh flags: These marsh plants reinforce the watery, liminal setting, symbolizing cleansing, healing, and the mysteries of the marshland as a space of passage.
    • Star grass, Starflowers: Clearly symbolic of the stars, the heavens, and the eternal. They evoke immortality, the soul’s journey to the cosmos, and the vastness beyond earthly life.
    • Wild rambler roses: Roses are classic symbols of love and mourning. Wild, they suggest untamed nature and spontaneous beauty amid sorrow.
    • Moon vines: As climbers associated with the moon, they symbolize growth, reaching upward, and the connection between earth and sky, life and the unknown. Overall, the meadow, richly populated with these plants, forms a symbolic landscape at the threshold of life and death, reinforcing the themes of mourning, transformation, liminality, and the hope for immortality or continuation beyond the mortal realm. The plants embody a complex web of meanings: nurturing life, signaling death’s danger, hinting at spiritual mysteries, and evoking the eternal cycle of nature and the soul’s journey.
    • The flowers and plants carry rich symbolic meaning related to life, death, memory, and transformation. The imagery of wild flowers, grasses, mosses, and herbs like milkwort, cockscomb, nightshade, and heliotrope evoke a natural cycle of birth, decay, and renewal. These plants symbolize the fragility and transience of life. The recurring references to flowers and leaves suggest the ephemeral nature of existence and the blurred boundaries between life and death. The plants and flowers also convey the intertwining of the physical and spiritual realms. Natural imagery is also linked to cycles of hope and despair. They symbolize the delicate, often painful process of life and transformation, the persistence of memory and hope amidst loss, and the mingling of the tangible and intangible aspects of existence. They symbolize the need for perseverance, vitality, resilience, ambition, and reaching new heights.
    • Young’s moonwort “with tiny buds and crescent moon seeds” may be honesty (Lunaria), also called moonwort, whose round, silvery seed pods shine like moons.
    • The frog lily is the yellow pond lily (Nuphar) of ponds and slow streams.
  • “flowers pale as tussock moths”: Tussock moths are found on every continent except Antarctica. See Butterflies and Moths.
  • “the black-crowned night heron splashed”: The black-crowned night heron feeds at dusk and by night; its call is a flat “quok.” See Birds.
  • “staring into a scum-covered pond as if it were a mirror”: See Narcissus and Echo in the Chapter 58 resources. See Mirrors and Reflections.
  • “some great loom weaving heaven and earth together”: See Goethe’s loom of Time in the Chapter 58 resources.
  • “it was Maundy Thursday when the frog had died”: Maundy Thursday is Holy Thursday during Holy Week that commemorates the Washing of the Feet and Last Supper of Jesus Christ with the Apostles. “Maundy” comes from the Latin mandatum: “A new commandment I give unto you, That ye love one another” (John 13:34, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “as if there were a frog in his throat”: The idiom “a frog in his throat” means hoarseness; it is also used for a voice choked by emotion. Harvey Newcomb used it in How To Be A Man: A Book for Boys (1847), but it appears in America at least as early as 1808, when the Salem Gazette toasted France, wishing that she “may find a frog in her throat” when she tried to raise her voice in American councils (Word Histories). See On a frog in his throat.
  • “as if he were evolving into one great sea shell, spiral above spiral”: “Build thee more stately mansions, O my soul” (Oliver Wendell Holmes, “The Chambered Nautilus,” 1858). See American Writers.
  • “mimetic flowers like faces in clouds”: Mimetic flowers are flowers that deceive pollinators by mimicking other organisms or objects to attract them without offering a reward. This mimicry can involve copying the appearance, scent, or texture of a model, such as a rewarding flower, rotting flesh, or even a female insect, to trick insects into acting as pollinators.
  • “music for the wind harp”: The wind harp, or Aeolian harp, is a box of strings set in a window or a tree for the wind to play; “And what if all of animated nature / Be but organic Harps diversely fram’d” (Samuel Taylor Coleridge, “The Eolian Harp,” 1795). See Romantic Poets and Music and Instruments.
  • “A swan when it is dying has the right to sing”: The swan song myth is an ancient belief that swans, typically silent throughout their lives, sing a beautiful song just before they die. The term is now used metaphorically to describe a final, often brilliant, performance, work, or gesture before retirement or death. This myth has roots in Greek and Roman mythology, appearing in the writings of Aeschylus and Plato, and was later referenced in English literature by Chaucer. The English phrase “swan song,” a translation of the German Schwanengesang, dates from 1831; Thomas Carlyle is credited with popularizing it in his satire Sartor Resartus, written in 1831. See On the swan song. See also Plato’s singing swans in the Chapter 53 resources. See Plato and Platonism.
  • “there were these two deaths which every man must die”: “And death and hell were cast into the lake of fire. This is the second death” (Revelation 20:14, KJV). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “Death was his coachman”: “Because I could not stop for Death— / He kindly stopped for me— / The Carriage held but just Ourselves— / And Immortality” (Emily Dickinson, c. 1863). See The Black Coachman and American Writers.
  • “for this world, he had said to the little frog, is but the tadpole state”: The passage uses rich natural and musical imagery to meditate on mortality as both an end and a transformation. It reflects Mr. Spitzer’s deep philosophical engagement with death as a complex, multifaceted process, intertwined with life and memory, filled with beauty, solitude, and mystery. The metaphor of the frog’s death and hopeful transformation encapsulates the novel’s exploration of human fragility, the limits of consciousness, and the hope for continuation beyond ephemeral physical existence.
  • “many signs which Mr. Spitzer had not understood”: American Sign Language grew from the school that Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet and the deaf French teacher Laurent Clerc opened in Hartford, Connecticut, in 1817. See Deafness and Sign Language.
  • “there was no being but becoming”: This expresses a deep philosophical reflection on the nature of existence and reality. This passage suggests that life and existence are not fixed or static (“no being”) but are continuous processes of change and transformation (“but becoming”). The “wheel” symbolizes the cyclical, ongoing movement of life, time, and experience: constantly turning without a definitive endpoint or beginning. The “images flowing like water into these images” evoke the idea of impermanence and fluidity, where one state or moment seamlessly merges into the next, much like water flowing and blending. It is meditating on the transitory nature of reality and identity, where everything is in flux, and what we consider “being” is actually a dynamic process of becoming something else. There is no fixed, immutable self or reality, only an endless flow of images, moments, and transformations. In 1913 Sir Thomas Oliver, a professor of medicine, said “becoming is better than being.” See On no being but becoming and Heraclitus and Flux.
    • Quote Investigator traces the saying to his paper read at Newcastle-upon-Tyne in December 1913; Carol Dweck repeated it in Mindset (2006, after 1964).
  • “is that which never was and never will be”: Mr. Spitzer asks, what “is that which never was and never will be and is not given in reality and if it were given would not be itself but a silent plain, empty and silent and vast?” is a philosophical reflection on the nature of infinity and reality. Mr. Spitzer is pondering the concept of infinity as an empty void: an expanse without color, form, or tangible existence. It is described as a “silent plain,” emphasizing its vastness and emptiness. This infinity is something that cannot be fully realized or given in concrete reality because if it were, it would lose its essential quality of being infinite and become simply a silent, empty space. He then contrasts this with the idea that reality itself is language: “image of image, image of the rose.” This suggests that reality is not the bare infinite void but is constructed through symbols, representations, and interpretations, much like language creates meaning through images and words. The “image of the rose” symbolizes how reality is mediated by perception and conceptualization rather than being a direct or absolute existence. This passage explores the tension between the infinite void as a pure, unformed potential and reality as a constructed, symbolic world filled with images and meanings. It highlights Mr. Spitzer’s philosophical and metaphysical concerns about existence, perception, and the limits of understanding.
  • “Reality is language, Mr. Spitzer said”: Compare Ludwig Wittgenstein: “The limits of my language mean the limits of my world” (Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus, 5.6, 1921). See Wittgenstein.
  • “the shadow of the mantling hawk”: In falconry a hawk mantles when it spreads its wings over its prey, or stretches a wing and a leg down one side. See Birds.
  • “He had pulled out his hair by the handfuls”: Ancient mourning tore the hair and beat the breast. When Achilles hears that Patroclus is dead, he pours dust on his head and tears his hair with his hands (Homer, Iliad, Book 18). See Homer’s Epics and Grief, Loss, and Mourning.
  • “Pax vobiscum”: Pax vobiscum, “peace be with you,” is said by the priest during the Mass. Young turns it into “silence be thine.”
  • “the way back had seemed shorter than the way they had come”: This is called the “Return Trip Effect,” which is a combination of familiarity, predictability, and reduced pressure to arrive on time. On the outbound journey a traveler focuses on the destination, which makes the trip feel longer, while the return is often more familiar and less time-pressured, making it seem to pass more quickly. (Niels van de Ven and others, “The Return Trip Effect,” 2011, after 1964.)
  • “like star-nosed moles”: The star-nosed mole of eastern North American wetlands has a ring of twenty-two fleshy rays around its snout, the most sensitive touch organ known in any mammal.
  • “it was an airplane light intermittently gleaming through the clouds”: Through most of the novel the events around the characters, especially Mr. Spitzer, seem to take place in the past or in a time around the 1900s. One of the clearest glimpses of modern times comes in this paragraph where Mr. Spitzer thinks he sees a moving star but it is an airplane light seen through clouds.
  • “one of his Congress gaiters was broken”: Congress gaiters were a popular boot for men and women in the 19th and 20th century.
    • The Congress gaiter is an ankle boot with elastic gussets in its sides, patented by J. Sparkes Hall in 1837. See Clothing, Hats, and Skirts.
  • “a silver helmet with beaver down to hide his face”: The beaver is the face guard of a helmet; of the ghost of Hamlet’s father, “he wore his beaver up” (William Shakespeare, Hamlet, 1.2). See Shakespeare.
  • “a florin or crusado”: A florin was a former coin of various countries, most notably a gold coin from Florence and later a British two-shilling coin equivalent to ten new pence. Today, the Aruban florin is the official currency of Aruba, and some European countries like the Netherlands have used “florin” as another name for their guilder, though its use is declining.
    • The crusado, or cruzado, is primarily an old Portuguese gold or silver coin, named for the cross that was typically on its reverse.
  • “the Bank of Sea Men and Others”: “Bank of Sea Men” metaphorically represents a kind of spiritual or otherworldly institution where coins (perhaps symbolic of value, memory, or passage) are exchanged or held. It evokes imagery related to sailors, the sea, and maritime life, fitting with the novel’s frequent motifs of water, journeys, and transitions between life and death.
    • Compare the Seamen’s Bank for Savings, founded in New York in 1829 for sailors’ wages.
  • “Carthago est delenda”: Carthago est delenda may refer to Carthago delenda est, Carthage must be destroyed, which is said to have been the last line of Cato the Elder’s speech advocating for war between Rome and Carthage. The war between Rome and Carthage, known as the Punic Wars, was a series of three conflicts from 264 BCE to 146 BCE that resulted in Roman victory and the destruction of Carthage. The wars were fought over dominance in the western Mediterranean, with the first and third wars fought over Sicily and later over the total destruction of Carthage. The Second Punic War is famously known for Hannibal’s invasion of Italy by crossing the Alps, but it ultimately ended with Carthage’s defeat at the Battle of Zama. See On Carthago est delenda.
    • Marcus Porcius Cato, also known as Cato the Censor, the Elder and the Wise, was a Roman soldier, senator, and historian known for his conservatism and opposition to Hellenization. He was the first to write history in Latin with his Origines, a now fragmentary work on the history of Rome. His work De agri cultura, a treatise on agriculture, rituals, and recipes, is the oldest extant prose written in the Latin language. His epithet “Elder” distinguishes him from his great-grandson Senator Cato the Younger, who opposed Julius Caesar.
  • “moving by summation”: A sea anemone’s nerve net acts by summation: one touch may do nothing, but repeated touches add up until the animal contracts. A medusa, or jellyfish, swims by pulsing its bell. See The Sea and Water.
  • “Too deep, too deep, this old bull frog had croaked”: The paragraph is a deeply poetic and philosophical reflection on the nature of existence, using the bullfrog’s croak as a catalyst for Mr. Spitzer’s meditation on duality, mortality, perception, and the blurred boundaries between life and death. It emphasizes the complexity of identity, the ambiguity of time, and the limits of human communication and understanding, resonating with the novel’s central themes of illusion and reality.
  • “hearing with their eyes, their skin, their hair”: Studies of deaf adults find heightened attention to the edges of the visual field and to touch (Daphne Bavelier and others, “Do deaf individuals see better?” 2006, after 1964). See Deafness and Sign Language.
  • “in cloud cuckooland”: In Aristophanes’s The Birds (414 BC), the birds build Cloudcuckooland, a city in the air between gods and men. See Birds.
  • “standing guilty when accused, standing mute”: A prisoner who stood mute and refused to plead could be pressed under weights; Giles Corey of Salem was pressed to death in 1692 for refusing to plead at the witch trials.
  • “the tapestry figures of the emperor and the unicorn”: The unicorn traditionally symbolizes purity, innocence, and something rare or unattainable, often connected to mystical or idealized qualities. The emperor, on the other hand, represents worldly power, authority, and earthly rule. Together, the “emperor and the unicorn” juxtaposes earthly power with mystical purity.
    • Compare the Unicorn Tapestries at The Cloisters in New York (c. 1495-1505) and The Lady and the Unicorn at the Musée de Cluny in Paris.
  • “a silent attendant to a Turk”: The Ottoman sultans kept mutes, the dilsiz, at the Topkapı Palace; they spoke among themselves in signs and sometimes served as executioners. See the Kafes in the Chapter 42 resources. See Deafness and Sign Language.
  • “a paid mourner at a funeral, walking behind the corpse”: In England the hired attendants who stood at the door and walked before the hearse were called mutes; Oliver Twist is put to work as one for the undertaker Mr. Sowerberry (Charles Dickens, Oliver Twist, 1838). See Weddings and Funerals.
  • “He did not hear the umbrella mender’s cry”: See The Umbrella.
  • “for none should toll for him”: “Never send to know for whom the bell tolls; it tolls for thee” (John Donne, Meditation XVII, 1624).
  • “even with tongues of angels and of men”: “Though I speak with the tongues of men and of angels, and have not charity, I am become as sounding brass, or a tinkling cymbal” (1 Corinthians 13:1, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “like a cabalistic language few could understand”: The Kabbalah is the Jewish mystical tradition that reads hidden meanings in the letters and numbers of Scripture; “cabalistic” came to mean secret and hard to understand. See Judaism.
  • “the hermit thrush’s speckled breast”: The hermit thrush sings the elegy in Walt Whitman’s “When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom’d” (1865), his poem on the death of Lincoln. See American Writers and Birds.
  • “the King’s English”: The King’s English most commonly refers to the standard or correct form of English, especially when a king reigns, or it can refer to a book on English usage by H.W. Fowler.
    • “Abusing of God’s patience and the king’s English” (William Shakespeare, The Merry Wives of Windsor, 1.4). See Shakespeare.
  • “a Spanish deaf mute lip-reader in Boston”: Boston was a center of oralism, which taught deaf children to read lips and speak and drove out sign language. Alexander Graham Bell taught his Visible Speech at the Boston School for Deaf Mutes in 1871, and the Milan congress of 1880 voted to ban signing in deaf schools. See Deafness and Sign Language.
  • “an English lip-reader in Seville”: Seville is the capital and largest city of the Spanish autonomous community of Andalusia and the province of Seville. It is situated on the lower reaches of the River Guadalquivir, in the southwest of the Iberian Peninsula.
  • “as silent as Babylon or Nineveh or Tyre”: “Babylon” may refer to the ancient Mesopotamian city in modern-day Iraq. The ancient city was the capital of two empires, known for its scientific and architectural achievements.
    • Nineveh was a major ancient Mesopotamian city, serving as the final capital of the Neo-Assyrian Empire around 700 BCE until its destruction in 612 BCE. It was a significant cultural and political center known for its grand palaces, gardens, and Ashurbanipal’s library. The city is also prominent in the biblical story of Jonah. Tyre is a city in Lebanon, and one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world. It was one of the earliest Phoenician metropolises and the legendary birthplace of Europa, her brothers Cadmus and Phoenix, and Carthage’s founder Dido.
  • “The idea occurred to Mr. Spitzer that the mute”: This paragraph poignantly illustrates the mute man’s alienation not only from sound but from society and even human connection. Mr. Spitzer’s reflections reveal a deep empathy and sorrow for the mute’s condition, while also contemplating the broader human condition of isolation and the limits of perception and communication. The mute’s silence is not just the absence of sound but a profound existential state that alienates him from the world’s richness and human relationships. Mr. Spitzer’s role as a guide and companion highlights the tenuousness of connection for those so isolated, and the paragraph underscores the tragic loneliness of those who inhabit the margins of sensory experience. This passage contributes to the novel’s exploration of perception, reality, and the boundaries between life and death, presence and absence, sound and silence.
  • “he was born deaf but had been made mute”: Most deaf people have working voices. The old word “deaf-mute” came from the fact that a child born deaf did not learn speech by ear, and many Deaf people today reject the term. See Deafness and Sign Language.
  • “his mother had been a fan dancer”: Sally Rand made the fan dance famous at Chicago’s Century of Progress fair in 1933, dancing behind two great ostrich-feather fans.
  • “sound of the water ouzel”: A water ouzel is the traditional name for the American Dipper, a medium-sized, gray songbird known for its aquatic lifestyle, as it dives into cold, fast-moving streams to hunt for food. It is the only North American songbird that forages underwater, where it walks along the stream bed to catch aquatic insects and other prey. John Muir wrote it a hymn of praise, “The Water-Ouzel” (The Mountains of California, 1894). See Birds and American Writers.
  • “the bird of time forever winging”: “The Bird of Time has but a little way / To flutter—and the Bird is on the Wing” (Edward FitzGerald, The Rubáiyát of Omar Khayyám, 1859). See Time.
  • “when the graves gave up their dead”: “And the sea gave up the dead which were in it; and death and hell delivered up the dead which were in them” (Revelation 20:13, KJV). Young’s trumpets “at the four corners of the clouded sky” recall the “four angels standing on the four corners of the earth” (Revelation 7:1, KJV). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “with bright eyes flashing in the lights like Aurora”: See the aurora in the Chapter 60 resources.
  • “Look to the end, Mr. Spitzer said”: Respice finem is Latin for consider the end or look to the end. The medieval Gesta Romanorum gives it as “Quidquid agis, prudenter agas, et respice finem,” whatever you do, do it wisely, and look to the end. See On Respice finem.
  • “Should that be added which had never been?”: “If any man shall add unto these things, God shall add unto him the plagues that are written in this book: And if any man shall take away from the words of the book of this prophecy, God shall take away his part out of the book of life” (Revelation 22:18-19, KJV). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “as the dolphin’s rainbow colors faded”: See Byron’s dying dolphin in the Chapter 56 resources. See Romantic Poets.
  • “the old milk-white horse”: Moon blindness is a recurring inflammation of a horse’s eyes, once blamed on the phases of the moon.
  • “more than twelve knots a day”: A knot is already a speed, one nautical mile an hour, so “knots a day” is Young’s joke on Mr. Spitzer’s slowness.
  • “to be the great conchologist”: A conchologist is a person who studies and collects shells, typically those of marine mollusks. See The Sea and Water.
  • “brigantines of the opium trade”: Boston and Salem fortunes were made in the China trade. Russell & Company, run largely by Boston families such as the Forbeses and the Delanos, was the leading American firm in the opium trade with China. See The History of Opium.
  • “ships from the Barbary coast”: The “Barbary Coast” most commonly refers to the historical coastal region of North Africa, inhabited by Berbers, from which pirates and privateers launched raids on European shipping and coastal towns from the 16th to the 19th century. The term is also used to describe the raucous waterfront district of San Francisco during the late 19th and early 20th centuries, known for its saloons, gambling, and brothels. The North African region was notorious for its Barbary corsairs, who captured ships and people for ransom and the slave trade.
  • “the Labrador coast”: Labrador is a geographic and cultural region within the Canadian province of Newfoundland and Labrador. It is the primarily continental portion of the province and constitutes 71% of the province’s area but is home to only 6% of its population.
  • “the great Arctic birds who round this globe each year, going from pole to pole”: The Arctic tern flies each year from its Arctic nesting grounds to the Antarctic and back, the longest migration of any bird. See Birds and Exploration and the Poles.
  • “left-handed stromb”: A “left-handed stromb” is a very rare, naturally occurring left-handed snail shell. In most gastropods, including the Florida fighting conch, shells grow in a spiral that is right-handed, making a left-handed version a rare genetic anomaly.
  • “sea shells are found on the mountain tops”: Leonardo da Vinci argued in his notebooks that fossil shells on mountains could not have been carried there by Noah’s Flood, and Charles Darwin found fossil shells high in the Andes in 1835.
  • “roar like a demented sea shell remembering that sea which never was”: There is a common folk myth that if one holds a seashell to the ear the person can hear the ocean. This is because of seashell resonance. See On seashells and the ocean. See also the sea heard in a shell in the Chapter 58 resources.
  • “Mr. Spitzer’s susurrus”: Susurrus means whispering, rustling, or murmuring.
  • “here at the Hub”: Oliver Wendell Holmes wrote that the “Boston State-House is the hub of the solar system” (The Autocrat of the Breakfast-Table, 1858), and Boston has been the Hub ever since. See American Writers.
  • “a multiplicity of Tritons blowing their distant spiral horns”: “Or hear old Triton blow his wreathèd horn” (William Wordsworth, “The World Is Too Much with Us,” 1807). Triton, son of Poseidon, calms or raises the sea with his conch. See Greek Mythology and Romantic Poets.
  • “the cries of rickshaw boys in Peking”: Compare Lao She’s novel Rickshaw Boy (1937), whose English translation (1945) became an American bestseller.
  • “great hermit mollusk door”: Young may have in mind hermit crabs (crustaceans) that use mollusk shells for protection. Hermit crabs lack their own protective shell and rely on empty shells from marine snails (gastropods) to protect their soft, vulnerable abdomens from predators and the elements.
    • A snail’s own door is its operculum, the horny or limy plate it closes over the opening of its shell.
  • “sometimes Trenton, Camden, Asbury Park”: Trenton is the capital city of New Jersey and the county seat of Mercer County. It is historically significant as the site of George Washington’s first military victory during the American Revolutionary War in 1776.
    • Camden is a city in Camden County, in the U.S. state of New Jersey. It is part of the Delaware Valley metropolitan region. The city was incorporated on February 13, 1828. Walt Whitman spent his final years in Camden, where he died in 1892. He is buried in a tomb at Harleigh Cemetery in Camden. See On Camden, New Jersey and American Writers.
    • Asbury Park is a small seaside city on the New Jersey coast. It’s known for its sandy shore and beachfront boardwalk lined with shops, arcades and cafes. The city’s live music venues include the 1920s Paramount Theatre. The Silverball Museum’s video games, arcade games and pinball machines date from the 1930s to the present. (The Silverball Museum is after 1964.) Stephen Crane spent his boyhood in Asbury Park.
