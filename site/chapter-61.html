Chapter 61

On Chapter 61 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Summary

In a thieves' café, Mr. Spitzer befriends an old mute who has trained a frog in his mouth to speak for him, even to recite the Lord's Prayer. When the frog dies, the two men bury it in an upland meadow, and the walk back seems shorter than the walk there. Mr. Spitzer wonders whether the mute is his ideal audience. He thinks about silence, and about the music he has scattered on scraps across Boston and other cities.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with the remarkable story of an old mute who, through a frog trained to sing in his mouth, can “speak” and “croak” human words and even the Lord’s Prayer. This passage is surreal and symbolic, underscoring themes of voice and silence, communication beyond normal means, and the miracle of expression despite physical limitations. The frog is both instrument and voice for the mute, representing a fragile connection to the world of sound and speech.
  • Mr. Spitzer accompanies the mute in burying the frog, which had been the mute’s only voice. The description of the burial site as an upland flower-strewn meadow with wildflowers, grasses, and symbolic plants is deeply symbolic of mortality and the cycle of life and death. Mr. Spitzer reflects on mortality, mourning not just the frog but the brevity and fragility of life itself. The mute’s grief is portrayed as profound and inexpressible, a voiceless sorrow that Mr. Spitzer tries to honor through silent companionship.
  • The mute’s expressive hands create a rich, complex sign language full of symbols (crosses, circles, directions, and gestures) that Mr. Spitzer tries to interpret. This passage highlights communication beyond words and the ambiguity and depth of silent expression. It stresses the idea that silence can be full of meaning, though often ambiguous and elusive.
  • Mr. Spitzer reflects extensively on the mute’s condition as a deaf mute living in a world full of sound and language. The mute is portrayed as a stranger in a foreign land, unable to access the rich auditory world around him. This section meditates on the isolating nature of silence and deafness, the loss of abstract concepts and sensory experiences, and the profound loneliness that comes with it. Mr. Spitzer envies the mute’s escape from the distractions and dissonances of sound, yet mourns the tragic loss of connection and communication.
  • Despite being a musician, Mr. Spitzer feels that his music is lost or unheard, especially by the mute who cannot hear. This intertwines the theme of isolation in the artist’s life with the mute’s silence. Mr. Spitzer’s compositions are described as “inaudible music,” music that exists more as internal experience than as shared sound. This passage emphasizes the paradox of creating art that may never be truly communicated or appreciated.
  • Throughout the chapter, Mr. Spitzer contemplates mortality, the fleeting nature of life, and the illusions of time. The mute, unable to hear clocks or the city’s awakening, exists outside conventional time, which deepens the symbolic resonance of death and silence. Mr. Spitzer’s philosophical musings on existence, memory, and the passage of time add a profound existential dimension to the narrative.
  • The chapter repeatedly contrasts the world of sound (the bustling city, music, natural noises) with the silence of the mute. Silence is portrayed not as mere absence but as a profound presence, “greater than sound.” This duality explores the limits of human experience and the mystery of what may lie beyond sensory perception.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • An old, reflective, and solitary musician, possibly a lawyer turned silent observer of life.
  • He is contemplative, mourning not only the frog and the mute but also larger existential losses.
  • Characterized by his flowing black mourning cloak, and his slow, deliberate movements.
  • His music is described as unheard, symbolic of his isolation and the futility of his art in a silent world.

The Old Mute

  • A silent figure who communicates through sign language and an extraordinary frog trained to "speak" by croaking words.
  • Embodies the theme of silence, loss, and the difficulty of communication.
  • His grief over the frog’s death is profound and inexpressible except through gestures.

The Singing Frog

  • A small frog trained to sing and speak by the mute, symbolizing voice, life, and the fragile link between silence and sound.
  • Its death symbolizes the loss of voice and communication, a tragedy for the mute.

Isolation and Alienation

Further notes from the text

  • The chapter opens on a chain of reversals, night as day, dawn as twilight, beginning as end, which sets the terms for Mr. Spitzer’s friendship with a man who speaks only in signs.
  • The frog that speaks the Lord’s Prayer is the comic center. See Comedy in Chapter 61.
  • The burial ground is an upland meadow a mile or two inland, among plovers, woodpeckers, and old fences, beyond the reach of the sea but not beyond its sound.
  • Mr. Spitzer is irritated that the way back is short and direct: if so, why did they go such a long way, circling through fog and mistaking reflections for the real thing? The complaint fits his whole life.
  • Sign language becomes a theology: many things said by one sign, many never expressed, “like the sign language of God,” who wrote the world in hieroglyphic birds, beasts, and flowers.
  • Testing the mute’s hearing, Mr. Spitzer holds up a watch to the man’s dead ear, then realizes he has lifted his own dead arm, on his deaf side, with Peron’s broken watch.
  • The chapter ends in a long catalogue of sea shells drifting from pole to pole, including the left-handed stromb, a rare shell that coils the wrong way.

  • "The wheel turned, and that was all. Perhaps there were only these images flowing like water into these images—there was no being but becoming" This expresses a deep philosophical reflection on the nature of existence and reality. This passage suggests that life and existence are not fixed or static ("no being") but are continuous processes of change and transformation ("but becoming"). The "wheel" symbolizes the cyclical, ongoing movement of life, time, and experience: constantly turning without a definitive endpoint or beginning. The "images flowing like water into these images" evoke the idea of impermanence and fluidity, where one state or moment seamlessly merges into the next, much like water flowing and blending. It is meditating on the transitory nature of reality and identity, where everything is in flux, and what we consider "being" is actually a dynamic process of becoming something else. There is no fixed, immutable self or reality, only an endless flow of images, moments, and transformations. In 1913 Professor Thomas Oliver said “becoming is better than being”.
  • Mr. Spitzer asks, what "is that which never was and never will be and is not given in reality and if it were given would not be itself but a silent plain, empty and silent and vast?" is a philosophical reflection on the nature of infinity and reality. Mr. Spitzer is pondering the concept of infinity as an empty void: an expanse without color, form, or tangible existence. It is described as a "silent plain," emphasizing its vastness and emptiness. This infinity is something that cannot be fully realized or given in concrete reality because if it were, it would lose its essential quality of being infinite and become simply a silent, empty space. He then contrasts this with the idea that reality itself is language: "image of image, image of the rose." This suggests that reality is not the bare infinite void but is constructed through symbols, representations, and interpretations, much like language creates meaning through images and words. The "image of the rose" symbolizes how reality is mediated by perception and conceptualization rather than being a direct or absolute existence. This passage explores the tension between the infinite void as a pure, unformed potential and reality as a constructed, symbolic world filled with images and meanings. It highlights Mr. Spitzer's philosophical and metaphysical concerns about existence, perception, and the limits of understanding.
