Cousin Hannah Freemount-Snowden: The Life Made From a Real One

← The Canary · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Lorena →

Spoiler note: This portrait is written for readers who have finished the novel. It discusses the character’s whole story, including how it ends.

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Jacqueline White · Joachim Spitzer · Madge Capehorn · Mrs. Hogden

The others in this set were built out of almost nothing, a sleeping face, a cried name, an absence, a turned-away head. Cousin Hannah is the exception, and that is what makes her the hardest case in the book. She was a real person, a real cousin who really lived and really died, and more than that she was the mother's opposite in every direction, the one who scorned dreams and insisted on the hard world. If the book can fold even her into the dream, it has proved the thing it keeps trying to say.

She was everything the mother was not. Where the mother lay in bed for decades, Hannah crossed the actual earth, "a Captain Derring-Do who engaged in those adventures my mother only dreamed of," a suffragist and duelist and mountaineer who rode by camel where no woman had gone, knew the Alpine and Himalayan passes the way other people know their own street, and turned up prescient, having heard of the archduke's murder hours before the news. She was self-sufficient to the point of being her own pair, able to be "both herself and a gentleman," a woman who needed no fourth at the table because she was already two. The mother looked at her and saw "an emancipated extension of herself," the vertical life she might have led if she had ever stood up and got out of the bed.

And Hannah scorned the whole apparatus the mother lived inside. She "had not been persuaded by dreams and visions." She cut through "those delicate threads binding us to reality" as if they were nothing. She had come to the dark house to drag the mother out of it, to break up the opium dreams, to say plainly that the mother loved only "the mere shadow of a man" and should leave him for the open road. She beat the bridegroom with her white umbrella and tried to carry the bride off. She fought the dream directly, in the open, the way she fought everything, and "the valiant heart had fought her losing battle with shadows." She lost. The dreamer stayed in bed, and the realist could not move her.

What made Hannah easy to keep after death was that she had never been present in life. She was always somewhere else, always leaving, and "Cousin Hannah was never known to leave a forwarding address," so that when she died nothing about her changed for the mother, who goes on telephoning the empty house and inviting her to card games and asking her to search the Alps for the lost husband on her next crossing. The great line of the whole portrait is the quiet one: "Death seemed scarcely a greater absence than life had been." A woman that absent does not need to be alive to be summoned. She had already spent her life being unreachable, so the mother simply keeps reaching, exactly as before.

Then the book does the thing it has been building toward. It rewrites her death. Hannah, who lived by the sword and "should have died by one sharp blow, a martyr to her cause," does not. "She had suffered for years from frostbite. She had died after many years of semi-invalidism," in a bed, slowly, like the mother. And on that bed she recants everything. She confesses that the foreign wars were nothing, mere incidents, that "the greatest battles were fought in the domestic wars by those who had never left home," that the man who leaves is the coward and the woman who stays is supreme. The adventurer, dying, hands the dreamer the argument. The one who spent a life proving that reality is out there to be crossed says at the end that the real war was the one the mother was fighting all along, lying still.

The catch is that none of it could have happened. "It was impossible to know how the great captain could have made this death-bed confession," because Hannah never answered the telephone, and the two houses were far apart, and the mother "had visited her only in a dream," carried to the deathbed in the carriage of the dead black coachman. The confession is invented. The mother built Hannah's surrender the way she built James and the coachman, and got her own conscience to drive her there to collect it. Even the secret the portrait circles, the grief that shadowed Hannah's unmarried life, "the great secret of Cousin Hannah's life" that Catherine and Mr. Spitzer bury and "skirt around," stays sealed, an interior no one can open, which is its own admission that a person is never fully found, only partly made by whoever is doing the remembering. And later the mother is not even sure Hannah has stayed one person, wondering whether the stranger Mrs. Hogden "must be my Cousin Hannah." The realist ends as raw material, recycled, reassigned, made and remade.

This is where the set closes, because Hannah is the strongest possible test and she fails it the way everyone does. Madge and Jacqueline and James and the coachman were built from little and fooled no one about it. Hannah was real, and she resisted, and she insisted on the exact opposite of everything the dreamer believed, and the dreamer built her anyway, down to the change of heart on the deathbed she was not there to witness. If the woman who cut the threads to reality can be woven back into someone else's dream, and made to say the dream was right, then the book has said there is no one so real they cannot be made. To be made by someone who loved her, or needed her to be wrong so they could be right, is not the opposite of having lived. It is the ordinary fate of anyone remembered by a person who cannot stop dreaming.

The turn for a writer is the most tempting and the most dangerous in the set. Madge and James were built out of almost nothing and never pretended otherwise. Hannah was real, and she resisted, and she held the exact opposite of everything the dreamer believed, and the dreamer remade her all the same, down to a deathbed recantation she was never there to give. That is the writer's most seductive power, the ability to take the very person who fought the dream and, in the telling, make her come around. The adventurer who scorned every illusion is made to say with her dying breath that the illusion was right, that the woman who never left her bed was the braver of the two. A writer reaches for this move whenever a remembered opponent conveniently softens, concedes, admits at the last that the writer had it right all along, and the convenience is the tell. A confession that happens to hand the teller the argument is the teller's need doing the writing, not the person. What keeps Hannah honest, and what a writer should learn to keep, is the thing that would not come out. "The great secret of Cousin Hannah's life" stays sealed, skirted, buried by the very people remembering her, and that locked place is the proof that a real person was there at all. A made up surrender always flatters the one who made it. A real person is known by the part that cannot be persuaded to speak.
