Chapter 80

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Esther Longtree

Pillars: The Past Doesn't Go Away · The Sacred Is Here

The Questions

A white butterfly makes Esther think “a dead child had returned, that this was the spirit.” What has happened to the line between the dead and the living?

She “made little clothes for no one to wear—set out bread crumbs for the… spirits.” What are these small ordinary acts doing?

She is “the go-between, going between two worlds.” What is a life lived on the crossing between the living and the dead?

Why does the book give this bridging work to the plainest of ordinary tending?

The chapter turns the dead into present company, and the butterfly is the sign. “A snow-white butterfly drifting over the grass could cause Esther such still anguish… making her think that a dead child had returned, that this was the spirit.” The dead children are not gone but present, arriving as butterflies, whispering in the stirred leaves “when there was no wind.” This is The Past Doesn't Go Away in Esther’s daily world: the past, the lost children, pressed into the present so completely that they drift through the grass on white wings, and the butterfly, which carried the wandering soul through the earlier chapters of the book, returns here as the returning dead child, the psyche in flight, the lost one come back as a small white thing over the grass.

Her small ordinary acts are the bridge she builds to them. She “made little clothes for no one to wear,” “set out bread crumbs for the other birds, the spirits,” “fed her own hand at her breast.” These are The Sacred Is Here doing the work the book most trusts it to do: plain, humble, everyday acts, sewing, feeding, setting out crumbs, that tend the dead and the living at once, that reach across to the spirits not through any grand rite but through the small tending of the here. Esther does not pray toward a distant heaven for her dead; she makes them little clothes, leaves them bread, and in the ordinary gestures of care she keeps the bridge open, the sacred done in the plainest domestic acts.

She is the go-between, and the crossing is where she lives. “She was the go-between, going between two worlds,” moving between the living and the dead, this world and the other, doing “what could be done here, too,” always keeping both. This is Esther as the crossing point in its plainest form: not sealed into either world but living on the passage between them, tending the dead with ordinary acts and staying, at the same time, in the ordinary world of the restaurant and the customers. She does not choose the beyond over the here or the here over the beyond; she goes between, a woman on the bridge, keeping the traffic open both ways, belonging to neither shore and serving both.

The book gives this bridging to the plainest tending for its deepest reason. The connection between the living and the dead, the traffic across the great division, is not entrusted here to a priest or a seer or a grand vision but to a waitress making little clothes and scattering crumbs. The Sacred Is Here holds that meaning is here, underfoot, in the world, and this chapter extends that all the way to the dead: even the bridge to the other world is built of ordinary acts, humble domestic care, the small tending that asks no beyond. Esther crosses between worlds by sewing and feeding, and the chapter insists that this, the plainest care, is how the crossing is actually kept, not by transcendence but by bread crumbs and little clothes.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Esther’s dead-child butterflies and spirit-feeding for mere derangement, and the reading that lifts her bridging into a grand mysticism, a seer’s communion with the beyond. Neither the derangement nor the grand mysticism is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that the dead are present rather than gone, arriving as butterflies and whispering leaves, The Past Doesn't Go Away in Esther’s daily world, the lost child returned as the wandering soul on white wings; that her small ordinary acts, the little clothes, the bread crumbs, are The Sacred Is Here building the bridge to the dead through domestic care rather than grand rite; and that she is the go-between, living on the crossing between two worlds, sealed into neither, keeping the traffic open both ways. The dead are near, and the bridge to them is built of ordinary tending. Esther crosses between worlds by sewing and feeding, and the chapter keeps her on the bridge, belonging to both shores.

For Reflection

Where has the line between the dead and the living grown thin enough that the lost seem to return in small ordinary things?

What plain, humble acts of tending build a bridge to what has been lost, without any grand rite at all?

What would it be to live as a go-between, on the crossing between two worlds, sealed into neither?
