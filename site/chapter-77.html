Summary

Esther asks who killed the heavens and the earth and love itself. The detective sends her an old clipping about a mass murderess, an abortionist who killed unmarried mothers and their newborns. She was caught after killing a woman who was pregnant only in her dreams. Esther reveals that her first child was fathered by a Black man, which makes the detective's indifference to Black children personal. Sterile salesmen keep sending gifts for babies who may be dead.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter begins with a profound meditation on who "had killed the heavens," the earth, and love itself images symbolize the destruction of innocence, life, and hope. Esther Longtree, the "stillborn mother," embodies silent suffering and unanswered cries, reflecting a deep personal and universal grief. This sets the tone for the chapter's exploration of death and loss, both literal and metaphorical.
  • Esther is described as pregnant but in a strange, watery, almost void-like condition: "pregnant with death," carrying something intangible rather than a living child. This paradoxical pregnancy symbolizes sterility and sorrow, emphasizing her unique, mysterious condition. Her physical state is intertwined with her emotional and psychological torment. Her isolation is emphasized by her work in the restaurant and her efforts to forget the "moths" and "stillborn who came to her at night".
  • The detective who pursues the mystery of children's deaths but only those of white children, ignoring the black children, whom he deems too many and unimportant. This racial divide in justice and attention highlights societal neglect and racial injustice. The detective's selective interest and fear of "black mysteries" contrast sharply with Esther's own encompassing sorrow that transcends race.
  • Esther recalls a faded newspaper clipping about an old woman abortionist who killed poor unmarried mothers and babies under her care. The story is haunting, especially the detail about a pregnant woman who was only "pregnant in her dreams," leading to a fatal mistake that exposed the murderer. This story resonates with Esther's own experience of pregnancy, loss, and the theme of death hidden beneath appearances.
  • Esther reflects on sending letters to the detective, possibly as a way to confront or taunt him. She recalls being raped by him, left for dead, and how since then murder has "grown upon this landscape." This passage reveals deep personal trauma and implicates the detective as both pursuer and perpetrator which adds complexity to their relationship and to the themes of guilt and accusation.
  • Throughout the chapter, there are repeated images of shadows, blackbirds, and stillborn children, symbolizing death, mystery, and things that never fully manifest. Esther sees the landscape as perhaps the "world unborn," hovering on the threshold of realization, reflecting her own liminal existence between life and death, birth and loss.
  • The passage listing exhibits A through U as letters standing for objects, feelings, and accusations (e.g., A for agony, M for murder, N for Negro, S for shadow) is a powerful symbolic enumeration of all the elements that accuse or haunt Esther. It illustrates the complexity of her guilt, the societal judgment upon her, and the intertwining of personal and collective tragedy.
  • Despite all her suffering and the detective's threats, Esther asserts her preference for silence, mystery, and the void over explanation or judgment. She wants to remain enigmatic, outside the reach of "old human laws" and inquisitions, embracing her unique condition and sorrow as a "great serenity".
  • The chapter ends with Esther questioning who should be responsible for the deaths and losses she embodies. She acknowledges the many "fathers," the salesmen, census-takers, and others, but ultimately feels her own stubbornness and sorrow are unique. She cherishes the natural world and wonders why she should be punished for "a few mistakes" when she has already suffered so much.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Esther Longtree

  • The protagonist; a pregnant woman symbolizing loss and mystery.
  • Complex, haunted by past pregnancies and the deaths of children, real or imagined.
  • Her perspective is lyrical, introspective, and fragmented, blending reality and metaphor.

The Chicago Detective

  • Represents societal judgment and racial bias.
  • Obsessed with solving murders of white children but indifferent to black children’s deaths.
  • Seen by Esther as both a threat and possibly the real murderer.

Old Doctor

  • A figure who once tried to aid Esther, representing medical authority and perhaps failed understanding.
  • He recognizes Esther’s exceptional nature and mystery.

The Miller

  • The Miller is a symbolic and tragic figure embedded in the narrative’s broader themes of loss, death, and the elusive nature of identity and legacy.
  • The Miller is described in the context of a flour miller who experiences epileptic seizures, during which he falls into the flour bin, covering himself in flour that then falls from his hat and clothes like snow. This imagery evokes a surreal, almost ghostly presence as people mistakenly believe it is snowing when it is actually flour. The miller’s role and condition emphasize themes of fragility and the blurring between life and death, reality and illusion.
  • The Miller is burdened with the expectation that a secret "thumb print" (a unique mark or identity) will be passed down from father to son, symbolizing lineage and continuity. However, this expectation is shattered when the old miller dies, and the son realizes that no one can truly pass on such a mark upon intangible things like the sunset, starlight, or water. This failure to transmit identity or legacy underscores the sense of loss and futility pervasive in the chapter and in the novel. The son’s epileptic fits may symbolize the psychological and physical turmoil resulting from this loss of meaning and continuity.
  • The Miller represents the human desire for permanence and identity amid the ephemeral and often cruel realities of life. His character evokes the tension between hope and despair, the tangible and the intangible, and the tragic consequences when the expected inheritance of identity and belonging is denied or impossible.
  • The narrative situates the Miller within a landscape of death, mystery, and unanswered questions, linking his personal tragedy to the larger themes of stillbirth, lost children, and the "quiet murders" of existence that haunt Esther Longtree and the novel’s world. The Miller Boy’s condition and the symbolism of flour as falling snow contribute to the chapter’s atmosphere of haunting ambiguity, where the boundaries between life, death, and memory dissolve.
  • The Miller is a poignant emblem of lost legacy and identity, reflecting the novel’s meditation on mortality, memory, and the ineffable mysteries of human existence as presented through Vera’s lyrical and fragmented narration.

The Little Boy

  • The Little Boy character emerges as a poignant and symbolic figure deeply intertwined with Esther Longtree’s complex emotional and psychological landscape.
  • The Little Boy is described as an exception among the many stillborn or lost children that haunt Esther. He is distinct because he “grew and cast his shadow,” unlike the others who remained stillborn or unmanifested. His crying is described as “like the night wind, like the falling leaves,” evoking a mournful, haunting presence that unsettles Esther. She desires to “reach him once with her hands” and to “still him,” but she cannot, as he mistrusts her, his own mother, and actively obstructs her path, causing her to stumble, fall, and suffer further losses.
  • This mistrust and obstruction suggest a fractured or troubled relationship, symbolizing perhaps Esther’s guilt, grief, and inability to fully reconcile with the loss or absence represented by her children. The Little Boy’s role as an accuser is implicit; he embodies the accumulated sorrow and unanswered questions about motherhood, loss, and abandonment that weigh heavily on Esther. The chapter intimates that he is part of the “evidence of sadness” in her life, a living shadow of the grief that has shaped her existence .
  • The Little Boy's shadow symbolizes the tangible imprint of loss and memory, as well as the burden of the past that follows Esther. His presence amplifies the themes of mystery and guilt that pervade the narrative. While Esther is “always pregnant,” carrying death and loss within her, the Little Boy serves as a manifestation of that sorrow made visible, a constant reminder of what is lost and unresolved.
  • The Little Boy character in this chapter is a complex symbol of loss, accusation, and the haunting persistence of grief in Esther Longtree's life. He is both a real and spectral figure who represents the emotional weight of her stillbirths and unfulfilled motherhood, embodying the tension between memory, guilt, and the desire for reconciliation that Vera, the narrator, carefully unfolds.

The Old Woman Abortionist

  • Mentioned in an old newspaper clipping; accused of killing unmarried mothers and babies.
  • Symbolizes institutional cruelty and hidden atrocities.

  • The detective’s pursuit symbolizes societal judgment and the invasive quest for truth. Esther fears exposure but also wishes for some form of public trial to assert control over her narrative.

Newspaper Clippings

  • Represent external judgment, historical atrocities, and persistent societal violence.
  • Esther Longtree's parents are portrayed as a mismatched, barren couple who never consummated their marriage, representing a union without real love, flesh, or genuine connection. The father is a large, lumbering, passive policeman (symbolizing authority but with a lonely, ineffectual life) while the mother is a small, delicate, child-like woman, proud of her fragile appearance but emotionally distant and incapable of nurturing.
  • Symbolically Esther's parents might represent the fraught and dysfunctional relationship between dominant societal forces and marginalized or oppressed groups in America, especially in terms of race and gender relations.
  • The father, a "passive policeman" who is "big—her mother, small," could symbolize the established power structures (law enforcement, government authority) that are large and imposing but ineffective or disconnected in truly addressing human needs or justice. His passivity and loneliness may reflect institutional failure or indifference.
  • The mother, fragile and child-like, might represent the traditional, constrained role of women or the idealized but ineffective domestic sphere, unable to assert real agency or nurture true growth. Her narrowness and coldness can symbolize limitations imposed on women or the societal denial of genuine human connection.
  • Together, their "unnatural conception" of Esther Longtree, where the "third person" (a go-between woman) carries a silver spoon between them to enable conception, reflects a forced, artificial union that produces a complicated, burdened offspring (Esther), who embodies both the failures and contradictions of this relationship.
  • Given the racial elements in Esther's story, her first child born of a black man, and the tensions around race and identity, the parents can also be seen as symbolic of the racial divisions and unfulfilled promises of American society prior to the Civil Rights Movement of the 1960s. The father as authority and the mother as fragile tradition suggest the racial and social dynamics that left many marginalized and created fractured identities.
  • Esther's parents symbolize the fractured, unequal, and dysfunctional societal foundations of America, marked by racial segregation, gender inequality, and institutional failures. This interpretation is supported by the detailed symbolic narrative of their personalities and relationship, reflecting broader social and historical themes.
  • Esther's parents symbolize the divided and dysfunctional social order in America, embodying the tensions between authority and vulnerability, racial and gender divides, and the consequences of an artificial, loveless union producing a troubled next generation.

Research: Resources

  • Esther Longtree in this chapter is portrayed as a deeply tragic and complex figure, embodying sorrow, loss, mystery, and a profound connection to themes of fertility, death, and motherhood. Esther is described as a large, pregnant waitress, often weighed down not only by her physical condition but by the immense emotional burden of repeated stillbirths and lost children she carries within her memory and body. Her pregnancy is symbolic, described as “watery” and “wild,” with her body “pregnant with death,” indicating a continuous cycle of creation and loss intertwined in her existence. She is caught between hope and despair, weighted down "by the death of a small thing," her body swollen but also fragile, her spinal column curved by the weight of memory. Esther represents the paradox of fertility and sterility simultaneously. Though she is repeatedly pregnant, the children are stillborn or lost, rendering her motherhood incomplete and haunted by absence. This condition makes her a symbol of the "Song of Songs of the sterility which is the creation," where creation is intertwined with loss and death. Her pregnancy is not just physical but metaphysical, involving the vastness of the universe, stars, and the earth, all of which seem to be "killed" or destroyed, mirroring her personal tragedy. Her mental state is marked by suspicion, confusion, and a profound sense of mystery. She contemplates who might have "killed the heavens," the earth, and love itself, reflecting a cosmic grief that parallels her personal losses. She imagines a detective who is both a pursuer and a murderer, embodying the threat of exposure and judgment, while the children she once had or never had remain elusive, "the child that never was". Esther’s relationship with the detective is complex: she suspects him of having raped her and leaving her for dead, yet he is also the one who threatens to uncover her secret sorrows. Her narrative includes a haunting sense of being pursued and judged unfairly, and she is both victim and accuser in her own story. The chapter also touches on racial issues through the detective's selective interest in "white" deaths while neglecting "black" ones, revealing societal biases and injustices. Esther, being associated with the "quiet, black mystery," is marginalized and ignored by official justice, highlighting themes of racial invisibility and systemic neglect. Esther is haunted by old murders, tragedies, and lost children, both personal and historical. She reflects on a mass murderer, abortionist and other cases of maternal and infant death, drawing parallels to her own suffering and guilt. These old stories serve as a backdrop to her present torment and sense of isolation, deepening the theme of a past that refuses to be forgotten. Despite her suffering, Esther shows moments of defiant acceptance and even joy, laughing in her sleep and finding some beauty in the natural world and her own strange, wild condition. She is stubborn, proud, and insists on her innocence in the face of accusation. Her condition is "no dream" but a persistent reality, and she prefers the mystery and silence of her pain to exposure or judgment. Esther Longtree is a figure of immense sorrow and mystery, embodying the tragic intersection of life and death, fertility and sterility, hope and despair. She is pregnant with loss, carrying both physical and metaphysical burdens, haunted by memories, accusations, and societal neglect, especially in the face of racial injustice. Her story is a poignant, symbolic exploration of motherhood, tragedy, and the human condition.
  • Esther Longtree symbolizes a tragic and complex history of motherhood, loss, and social neglect in the United States, especially reflecting the fates of unmarried or marginalized women and their children. Her story evokes the suffering of countless women who experienced stillbirths, miscarriages, or the loss of children under harsh social conditions. She is "always pregnant" yet often gives birth to "nothing," representing the grief of "stillborn" children and the immense sorrow that accompanies such loss. This condition is described as a vast, mysterious, and diffused crime or guilt that is both personal and cosmic in scale, involving death and sterility in creation itself. Esther Longtree's narrative intertwines with the grim history of illegal or dangerous abortions, as suggested by the story of the old woman abortionist who killed unmarried mothers and their babies in a hospital disguised as care. This history reflects the brutal reality many women faced in times when reproductive rights and safe medical care were inaccessible. The abortionist's killing of a woman who was "pregnant in her dreams" but had no child underscores the terror and stigma surrounding female reproduction and the societal condemnation of women’s bodies. Esther’s interactions with the white detective reveal the racial and social divisions in America’s history of violence and injustice. The detective focuses only on the deaths of white children, ignoring the countless black children lost or murdered: a commentary on racial indifference and systemic racism. Esther’s world is described as a "quiet, black mystery," where black children's deaths are overlooked, symbolizing the historical neglect and invisibility of black lives in American social and legal systems. Her burden of loss and the constant shadow of death she carries symbolize not only individual grief but also a collective history of women’s suffering, social marginalization, racial injustice, and the silencing of certain kinds of pain. Esther Longtree is a figure embodying the intersection of these historical realities: motherhood, loss, racial discrimination, and societal neglect in the US.
  • Who Killed Cock Robin is a common association with the mythology of Lugh, a warrior, a king, a master craftsman and a savior. He is also associated with Mercury and Baldr in Norse mythology. He was killed by Bran the Blessed, the god of winter in the form of a raven. Lugh is believed to have been given a Chirstian continuation through Saint Mologa in Ireland. Cock Robin appears seven times in Esther Longtree’s chapters. "Who killed Cock Robin?" repeatedly asked in the novel is not just about a single culprit but a symbol of the pervasive and inscrutable nature of death, loss, and guilt. It is a question without a definitive answer, reflecting the novel's exploration of mystery, suffering, and the silent tragedies that haunt Esther and the world she inhabits.

  • The Song of Songs is an erotic poem. It is unique within the Hebrew Bible in that it celebrates sexual love. The two are in harmony, each desiring the other and rejoicing in sexual intimacy. Jewish tradition reads it as an allegory of the relationship between God and Israel In Christianity, it is treated as an allegory celebrating the love between God and Church or Christ and his bride, the Church. The Christian church's interpretation of the Song is evidence of God's love for his people, both collectively and individually. Over time a moral element and the bride as Virgin Mary were added. It is unique in that these conclusions are not found in the overtly theological books of the Bible. Those books reveal an imbalance in the relationship between God and man, but Song of Songs is a theological metaphor in which the two partners are equals, bound in a committed relationship. In modern times the poem has attracted the attention of feminist biblical critics, with Phyllis Trible's Depatriarchalizing in Biblical Interpretation and the Feminist Companion to the Bible series edited by Athalya Brenner and Carole Fontaine.

  • The Chicago detective sent Esther a “yellowed newspaper clipping” that read “Mass Murderess Apprehended Accused by Dead Mothers and Dead Children.” There was nothing about the fathers. It was a “...woman abortionist and her timid little assistant…” who had killed “...all the poor, unmarried mothers and their babies, too, in a hospital which she ran for that purpose.” The mothers were described as well treated right up until their delivery. When the mother was supposed to deliver, mother and baby were killed “...by the cruel, old abortionist, a large woman, weighing almost three hundred pounds.” The babies were buried with lime.
  • Lime is used at mass grave sites to capture the scent of decay and keep soil pH high. Low pH soil is an indicator for a mass grave as the decomposition products are acidic and lower the soil pH. Adding lime reduces this acidity.
  • The newspaper was dated as happening during the “horse and buggy age.” The “old woman abortionist” kept at her trade until she murdered a “...woman who was not, who never could have been a mother.” This story may have its roots in real events.
  • Baby farming is the historical practice of accepting custody of an infant or child in exchange for payment in late-Victorian Britain and, less commonly, in Australia and the United States. Some baby farmers adopted children by paying lump-sum payments, while others cared for infants for periodic payments. There are several women or couples in the past that murdered or allowed the adopted children to die through starvation. Even the Catholic run Bon Secours Mother and Baby Home in Ireland illegally adopted children without their mother’s consent and the Home had an abnormally high child death rate with malnourished, emaciated babies. A significant amount of children’s remains were discovered in a septic tank on the property. There was also mistreatment and abuse of the mothers.
  • One of the most famous baby farming child murderers was Amelia Dyer. She murdered infants in her care over a thirty-year period during the Victorian era in the United Kingdom. Trained as a nurse and widowed in 1869, Dyer turned to baby farming to support herself. She initially cared for the children legitimately, then began directly murdering children she took in, strangling at least some of them, and disposing of the bodies to avoid attention. She was caught because the body of a child was found in the river. The “timid assistant” may refer to Dyer’s 23-year-old daughter Polly who assisted in the child murder of Doris Marmon.
  • “...was natural and a mother should suffer—she would just deliver the child then in such a way that it would be born dead —and would kill the mother—but only after it had been the agony of birth.” This may refer to Genesis 3:16 as Eve and women’s punishment for being cast out of the Garden of Eden.
  • Esther may be suffering from False pregnancy or pseudocyesis. It is the appearance of clinical or subclinical signs and symptoms associated with pregnancy although the individual is not physically carrying a baby. The mistaken impression that one is pregnant includes signs and symptoms such as tender breasts with secretions, abdominal growth, delayed menstrual periods, and subjective feelings of a moving fetus. False pregnancy has a prominent psychiatric component as well as physical manifestations of pregnancy. It can be caused by physical or mental trauma, a chemical imbalance of hormones, and some medical conditions.

  • Once when he thought he was dreaming he “delivered a real baby...and said—Oh, I see it all now! Life is a dream, and I have been wrong and much wronged, and I must give up this practice!” This line may refer to a play by Pedro Calderon de la Barca called Life Is A Dream. Barca was a Spanish dramatist, poet, writer and knight of the Order of Santiago. Life Is A Dream is a philosophical allegory regarding the human situation and the mystery of life. The play's central themes are the conflict between free will and fate, as well as restoring one's honor. Other themes include dreams vs. reality and the conflict between father and son.
