To All My Darlings

Chapter 77

On Chapter 77 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 12, 2026

← Chapter 76 · Home · Chapter 78 →

Content note: The Esther Longtree chapters (72 to 81) deal with stillbirth and pregnancy loss, rape, infanticide, racist violence including lynching, and cruelty to children. This chapter describes the murder of mothers and newborns and of two little girls, one white and one Black, and the detective’s racism; Esther’s alphabet of accusations includes “a Negro, the night, a noose.”

Summary

Esther asks who killed the heavens, the earth, and love, and decides that cock robin killed cock robin. She has scrubbed the restaurant clean, though the epileptic flour miller, who learned that no one can pass a thumb print down, walks the streets white with flour; her bedroom, with its empty cradle, could not bear the jury’s search. Once she found a rain-stained alphabet book in the hole where she had hidden a dead child, and every letter became an exhibit against her, from A, the agony, to N, “a Negro, the night, a noose,” and R, a rope. The white detective cares only for the deaths of white children: finding a murdered white girl and, farther on, a murdered Black girl in the same woods, he ran screaming and never told the police. He sends her clippings of old murders, among them an old woman abortionist who killed unmarried mothers and their newborns and was caught only after killing a woman who was pregnant in her dreams. Esther remembers that the detective raped her and left her for dead, sees the shadow of murder everywhere, and wonders why the sterile salesmen send ear muffs for babies who may be dead and growing.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter begins with a profound meditation on who “had killed the heavens,” the earth, and love itself. Esther Longtree, the “stillborn mother,” embodies silent suffering and unanswered cries, reflecting a deep personal and universal grief.
  • Esther is described as pregnant but in a strange, watery, almost void-like condition: “pregnant with death,” carrying something intangible rather than a living child. Her isolation is emphasized by her work in the restaurant and her efforts to forget the “moths” and “stillborn who came to her at night.”
  • The detective pursues the mystery of children’s deaths but only those of white children, ignoring the black children, whom he deems too many and unimportant. This racial divide in justice and attention highlights societal neglect and racial injustice. The detective’s selective interest and fear of “black mysteries” contrast sharply with Esther’s own encompassing sorrow that transcends race.
  • Esther recalls a faded newspaper clipping about an old woman abortionist who killed poor unmarried mothers and babies under her care. The story is haunting, especially the detail about a pregnant woman who was only “pregnant in her dreams,” leading to a fatal mistake that exposed the murderer.
  • Esther reflects on sending letters to the detective, possibly as a way to confront or taunt him. She recalls being raped by him, left for dead, and how since then murder has “grown upon this landscape.” This passage reveals deep personal trauma and implicates the detective as both pursuer and perpetrator.
  • Throughout the chapter, there are repeated images of shadows, blackbirds, and stillborn children. Esther sees the landscape as perhaps the “world unborn,” hovering on the threshold of realization.
  • The passage listing exhibits A through U as letters standing for objects, feelings, and accusations (e.g., A for agony, M for murder, N for Negro, S for shadow) is a powerful symbolic enumeration of all the elements that accuse or haunt Esther. It illustrates the complexity of her guilt, the societal judgment upon her, and the intertwining of personal and collective tragedy.
  • Despite all her suffering and the detective’s threats, Esther asserts her preference for silence, mystery, and the void over explanation or judgment. She wants to remain enigmatic, outside the reach of “old human laws” and inquisitions, embracing her unique condition and sorrow as a “great serenity.”
  • The chapter ends with Esther questioning who should be responsible for the deaths and losses she embodies. She acknowledges the many “fathers,” the salesmen, census-takers, and others, but ultimately feels her own stubbornness and sorrow are unique. She cherishes the natural world and wonders why she should be punished for “a few mistakes” when she has already suffered so much.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “Cock robin had killed cock robin, so the mystery was solved.” Is it solved?
  • The alphabet asks, “Mother, why did you kill me? Mother, why did you not kill me?” Why both questions?
  • “Her body was pregnant with death which moved its little hand inside of her.” Whose hand is it?

Characters

  • Finding a murdered white girl and a murdered Black girl in the same woods, the detective “had run screaming, stumbling through the woods, and had never written to the police.” What is he afraid of?
  • Esther calls hers “this quiet, black mystery.” What does she mean?
  • The miller’s son learns that “no one could pass a thumb print down.” Why did Young say she put him into everything she wrote?

Connections

  • The old woman abortionist is caught because she killed a woman “pregnant in her dreams.” How is that woman like Esther?
  • “Exhibit N, a Negro, the night, a noose.” What history do these words call up?
  • Esther would rather be “the scarlet woman” in the courtroom than be searched in her bedroom. Why?

Reflection

  • “Who could find the heart of a mystery that had no heart?”
  • “Should a woman ever answer a man’s questions?” What is Esther refusing?
  • “The men, oh, what children they were!” Is she right?

Research: Characters

Esther Longtree

  • The protagonist; a pregnant woman symbolizing loss and mystery.
  • Complex, haunted by past pregnancies and the deaths of children, real or imagined.
  • Her perspective is lyrical, introspective, and fragmented, blending reality and metaphor.

The Chicago Detective

  • Represents societal judgment and racial bias.
  • Obsessed with solving murders of white children but indifferent to black children’s deaths.
  • Seen by Esther as both a threat and possibly the real murderer: “This detective was a murderer.”
  • He “had raped her before she could cry out” and left her for dead, “the dung of blackbirds caked upon her face.”

Old Doctor

  • A figure who once tried to aid Esther, representing medical authority and perhaps failed understanding.
  • He recognizes Esther’s exceptional nature and mystery, telling her “that she was immortal and could not testify.”
  • Once, called out at night, he delivered a real baby he thought he was dreaming, and declared, “Life is a dream, and I have been wrong and much wronged, and I must give up this practice!”

The Miller

  • The miller falls into the flour bin in his epileptic seizures and walks the streets “with the flour falling like snow from his hat.” His son had been told that his father would give him “the secret of his exact thumb print upon the flour,” passing it down as father to son, but when the old miller dies the son learns that no one can set such a mark on sunset, starlight, or water. Young drew him from a real flour miller who tried to pass on his thumbmark to his son; see “He had been told by his father” above.
  • The Miller is a poignant emblem of lost legacy and identity, reflecting the novel’s meditation on mortality, memory, and the ineffable mysteries of human existence as presented through Vera’s lyrical and fragmented narration.

The Little Boy

  • The Little Boy character emerges as a poignant and symbolic figure deeply intertwined with Esther Longtree’s complex emotional and psychological landscape.
  • The Little Boy is described as an exception among the many stillborn or lost children that haunt Esther. He is distinct because he “grew and cast his shadow,” unlike the others who remained stillborn or unmanifested. His crying is described as “like the night wind, like the falling leaves,” evoking a mournful, haunting presence that unsettles Esther. She desires to “reach him once with her hands” and to “still him,” but she cannot, as he mistrusts her, his own mother, and actively obstructs her path, causing her to stumble, fall, and suffer further losses.

The Old Woman Abortionist

  • Mentioned in an old newspaper clipping; accused of killing unmarried mothers and babies.
  • Symbolizes institutional cruelty and hidden atrocities.

Research: Themes

Motherhood and Loss

See: Grief, Loss, and Mourning · Motherhood, Pregnancy, and Birth

  • Esther is “always pregnant,” but the pregnancies are metaphorical and literal, filled with loss and the absence of children (stillborn or never born).
  • The grief of lost children haunts her, symbolized by “little bones,” a “little book,” and the shadow of death inside her womb.
  • Her pregnancy represents both creation and sterility, life and death simultaneously.

Guilt and Innocence

See: Guilt, Innocence, and Justice

  • Esther feels guilt for the “murder” of her children, though this guilt is ambiguous: is she guilty or a victim of fate or society?
  • The detective’s pursuit symbolizes societal judgment and the invasive quest for truth. Esther fears exposure but also wishes for some form of public trial to assert control over her narrative.

Racism and Social Injustice

See: Guilt, Innocence, and Justice · Society, Class, and Race

  • The white Chicago detective is interested only in the deaths of white children, ignoring black children’s deaths, revealing racial prejudice and societal neglect.

Mystery and the Unknown

See: Faith, God, and the Supernatural

  • The nature of Esther’s pregnancies, the deaths, and the detective’s role create a pervasive sense of mystery.
  • The chapter explores the limits of understanding and the unknowable facets of human experience.

Nature and Cosmic Imagery

See: Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos

  • Constant references to stars, clouds, birds, leaves, and celestial phenomena frame the narrative in a cosmic context of creation and destruction.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Pregnancy and Stillbirth

See: Pregnancy, Birth, and Children

  • Not just physical but symbolic of potential, loss, and unfulfilled possibility.
  • Esther’s “watery pregnancy” and “wild pregnancy” evoke fragility and chaos.

The Little Book (ABC’s)

  • Symbolizes innocence, learning, and memory.
  • Its presence in the grass where no child is physically found evokes haunting loss.

Birds (Robins, Blackbirds, Moths)

See: Butterflies and Moths · Birds

  • Represent life, death, innocence, and societal neglect.
  • Blackbirds especially tie to racial themes and death ignored by authorities.

Shadows and Darkness

See: Lamps, Candles, and Light · Shadows

  • Recur throughout as metaphors for hidden truths, guilt, and fear.
  • Esther’s shadow and the detective’s shadow symbolize identity and the unknown.

Newspaper Clippings

  • Represent external judgment, historical atrocities, and persistent societal violence.

Esther’s Parents

  • Esther Longtree’s parents are portrayed as a mismatched, barren couple who never consummated their marriage, representing a union without real love, flesh, or genuine connection. The father is a large, lumbering, passive policeman (symbolizing authority but with a lonely, ineffectual life) while the mother is a small, delicate, child-like woman, proud of her fragile appearance but emotionally distant and incapable of nurturing.
  • Symbolically Esther’s parents might represent the fraught and dysfunctional relationship between dominant societal forces and marginalized or oppressed groups in America, especially in terms of gender.
  • The father, a “passive policeman” who is “big—her mother, small,” could symbolize the established power structures (law enforcement, government authority) that are large and imposing but ineffective or disconnected in truly addressing human needs or justice. His passivity and loneliness may reflect institutional failure or indifference.
  • The mother, fragile and child-like, might represent the traditional, constrained role of women or the idealized but ineffective domestic sphere, unable to assert real agency or nurture true growth. Her narrowness and coldness can symbolize limitations imposed on women or the societal denial of genuine human connection.
  • Together, their “unnatural conception” of Esther Longtree, where the “third person” (a go-between woman) carries a silver spoon between them to enable conception, reflects a forced, artificial union that produces a complicated, burdened offspring (Esther), who embodies both the failures and contradictions of this relationship.
  • Young read the parents’ artificial conception of Esther as Esther’s own loss: “That’s the loss she’s been trying to replace over and over again through continual pregnancies” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983). Of Esther’s lover she said, “this is not a race story because her lover was white in the moonlight and black at dawn. It was her schizophrenia” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). The passive policeman came from W. C. Fields. See the Chapter 72 resources.
  • Esther’s parents symbolize the divided and dysfunctional social order in America, embodying the tensions between authority and vulnerability, gender divides, and the consequences of an artificial, loveless union producing a troubled next generation.

Research: Resources

  • “Who had killed the heavens, rolling them up like a scroll”: “And all the host of heaven shall be dissolved, and the heavens shall be rolled together as a scroll” (Isaiah 34:4, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “the stillborn mother, stifling her cries”: Esther Longtree in this chapter is portrayed as a deeply tragic and complex figure, embodying sorrow, loss, mystery, and a profound connection to themes of fertility, death, and motherhood. Esther is described as a large, pregnant waitress, often weighed down not only by her physical condition but by the immense emotional burden of repeated stillbirths and lost children she carries within her memory and body. Her pregnancy is symbolic, described as “watery” and “wild,” with her body “pregnant with death,” indicating a continuous cycle of creation and loss intertwined in her existence. She is caught between hope and despair, weighted down “by the death of a small thing,” her body swollen but also fragile, her spinal column curved by the weight of memory. Esther represents the paradox of fertility and sterility simultaneously. Though she is repeatedly pregnant, the children are stillborn or lost, rendering her motherhood incomplete and haunted by absence. This condition makes her a symbol of the “Song of Songs of the sterility which is the creation,” where creation is intertwined with loss and death. Her pregnancy is not just physical but metaphysical, involving the vastness of the universe, stars, and the earth, all of which seem to be “killed” or destroyed, mirroring her personal tragedy. Her mental state is marked by suspicion, confusion, and a profound sense of mystery. She contemplates who might have “killed the heavens,” the earth, and love itself, reflecting a cosmic grief that parallels her personal losses. She imagines a detective who is both a pursuer and a murderer, embodying the threat of exposure and judgment, while the children she once had or never had remain elusive, “the child that never was.” Esther’s relationship with the detective is complex: she suspects him of having raped her and leaving her for dead, yet he is also the one who threatens to uncover her secret sorrows. Her narrative includes a haunting sense of being pursued and judged unfairly, and she is both victim and accuser in her own story. The chapter also touches on racial issues through the detective’s selective interest in “white” deaths while neglecting “black” ones, revealing societal biases and injustices. Esther, being associated with the “quiet, black mystery,” is marginalized and ignored by official justice, highlighting themes of racial invisibility and systemic neglect. Esther is haunted by old murders, tragedies, and lost children, both personal and historical. She reflects on a mass murderer, abortionist and other cases of maternal and infant death, drawing parallels to her own suffering and guilt. These old stories serve as a backdrop to her present torment and sense of isolation, deepening the theme of a past that refuses to be forgotten. Despite her suffering, Esther shows moments of defiant acceptance and even joy, laughing in her sleep and finding some beauty in the natural world and her own strange, wild condition. She is stubborn, proud, and insists on her innocence in the face of accusation. Her condition is “no dream” but a persistent reality, and she prefers the mystery and silence of her pain to exposure or judgment. Esther Longtree is a figure of immense sorrow and mystery, embodying the tragic intersection of life and death, fertility and sterility, hope and despair. She is pregnant with loss, carrying both physical and metaphysical burdens, haunted by memories, accusations, and societal neglect, especially in the face of racial injustice. Her story is a poignant, symbolic exploration of motherhood, tragedy, and the human condition. See Esther Longtree.
    • Esther Longtree symbolizes a tragic and complex history of motherhood, loss, and social neglect in the United States, especially reflecting the fates of unmarried or marginalized women and their children. Her story evokes the suffering of countless women who experienced stillbirths, miscarriages, or the loss of children under harsh social conditions. She is “always pregnant” yet often gives birth to “nothing,” representing the grief of “stillborn” children and the immense sorrow that accompanies such loss. This condition is described as a vast, mysterious, and diffused crime or guilt that is both personal and cosmic in scale, involving death and sterility in creation itself. Esther Longtree’s narrative intertwines with the grim history of illegal or dangerous abortions, as suggested by the story of the old woman abortionist who killed unmarried mothers and their babies in a hospital disguised as care. This history reflects the brutal reality many women faced in times when reproductive rights and safe medical care were inaccessible. The abortionist’s killing of a woman who was “pregnant in her dreams” but had no child underscores the terror and stigma surrounding female reproduction and the societal condemnation of women’s bodies. Esther’s interactions with the white detective reveal the racial and social divisions in America’s history of violence and injustice. The detective focuses only on the deaths of white children, ignoring the countless black children lost or murdered: a commentary on racial indifference and systemic racism. Esther’s world is described as a “quiet, black mystery,” where black children’s deaths are overlooked, symbolizing the historical neglect and invisibility of black lives in American social and legal systems. Her burden of loss and the constant shadow of death she carries symbolize not only individual grief but also a collective history of women’s suffering, social marginalization, racial injustice, and the silencing of certain kinds of pain. Esther Longtree is a figure embodying the intersection of these historical realities: motherhood, loss, racial discrimination, and societal neglect in the US.
  • “cock robin had killed cock robin”: Some folklore readings connect Who Killed Cock Robin with the mythology of Lugh, a warrior, a king, a master craftsman and a savior. The Romans identified him with Mercury, and his slaying of Balor has been compared with the death of the Norse god Baldr. In Celtic studies he is often paired with Brân the Blessed, the Welsh giant-king whose name means “Raven.” St. Mologa has been theorized to be a Christian continuation of Lugh. “Cock robin” appears seven times in Esther Longtree’s chapters. “Who killed Cock Robin?” repeatedly asked in the novel is not just about a single culprit but a symbol of the pervasive and inscrutable nature of death, loss, and guilt. It is a question without a definitive answer, reflecting the novel’s exploration of mystery, suffering, and the silent tragedies that haunt Esther and the world she inhabits. See the Chapter 72 resources and the Chapter 76 resources. See Fairy Tales and Folklore and Birds.
  • “hiding his head under his wing”: “The north wind doth blow, / And we shall have snow, / And what will poor Robin do then, poor thing? / He’ll sit in a barn, / And keep himself warm, / And hide his head under his wing, poor thing.” See Fairy Tales and Folklore and Birds.
  • “the universe which, O’Leary said to her, might be quite, quite small”: See Justice O’Leary.
  • “He had been told by his father that his father would give him the secret”: “When I started to write about New Harmony, the utopia, I was inspired to do so by an account of a flour miller who tried to pass on his thumbmark to his son. That cannot be done, and the son ended by committing suicide.” Young wrote a short story about him that failed, and “I have put that flour miller and this thumb into ‘Miss MacIntosh, My Darling’ somewhere. In fact, I’ve put that flour miller and his thumb into everything I’ve ever written since,” “the idea of identity and passage of the soul” (Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs, “A Conversation with Marguerite Young,” Review of Contemporary Fiction 23, no. 1, Spring 2003 (1989 original)). See Marguerite Young on Her Craft.
  • “If the jurymen came to her bedroom”: Young reminds us that the jury will consist of men. Mississippi, in 1968, was the last state to let women serve on juries, and in Taylor v. Louisiana (1975) the Supreme Court ruled that jury pools excluding women violated the Sixth Amendment. See Women, Gender, and Marriage and Guilt, Innocence, and Justice.

  • “she would be the doll, the scarlet woman”: “And the woman was arrayed in purple and scarlet colour… MYSTERY, BABYLON THE GREAT, THE MOTHER OF HARLOTS” (Revelation 17:4-5, KJV). See Revelation and the Apocalypse and Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “raped by a thief in the night”: “For yourselves know perfectly that the day of the Lord so cometh as a thief in the night” (1 Thessalonians 5:2, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “Many an old maid has dreamed thus of being raped”: The idea of women dreaming or fantasizing about being raped comes from a long history supported by male dominated institutions where women are trained to be rape victims which is still being challenged today. The rape fantasy in women is man-made. Women use the rape fantasy to find their sexuality within the context of male sexuality or power drive. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “the Song of Songs of the sterility which is the creation”: The Song of Songs is an erotic poem. It is unique within the Hebrew Bible in that it celebrates sexual love. The two are in harmony, each desiring the other and rejoicing in sexual intimacy. Jewish tradition reads it as an allegory of the relationship between God and Israel In Christianity, it is treated as an allegory celebrating the love between God and Church or Christ and his bride, the Church. The Christian church’s interpretation of the Song is evidence of God’s love for his people, both collectively and individually. Over time a moral element and the bride as Virgin Mary were added. It is unique in that these conclusions are not found in the overtly theological books of the Bible. Those books reveal an imbalance in the relationship between God and man, but Song of Songs is a theological metaphor in which the two partners are equals, bound in a committed relationship. In modern times the poem has attracted the attention of feminist biblical critics, with Phyllis Trible’s “Depatriarchalizing in Biblical Interpretation” (1973) and the Feminist Companion to the Bible series edited by Athalya Brenner and Carole Fontaine. See The Bible, Judaism, and Augustine and Christian Theology.
  • “Exhibit A, the agony, exhibit B, the birthday”: Esther’s alphabet of accusation echoes the New England Primer (about 1690), the first schoolbook of American children, whose alphabet begins with guilt: “In Adam’s Fall / We sinned all.” See The Bible.
  • “exhibit J, a jack-in-the-pulpit, a Jew’s harp”: The hangman carries a Jew’s harp (“A Jew’s harp is not Jewish”) in the Chapter 68 resources, and in Chapter 71 he always plays it before a hanging.
  • “exhibit N, a Negro, the night, a noose”: The night, the noose, and the rope that follows at R: the alphabet calls up lynching. The Tuskegee Institute recorded 4,743 lynchings in the United States from 1882 to 1968; 3,446 of the dead were Black. See Lynching and Racial Violence and Society, Class, and Race.
  • “But he only dealt with the deaths of little white children”: When Young wrote this the civil rights movement had been going on since 1954. See Society, Class, and Race and Lynching and Racial Violence.
    • In 1955 fourteen-year-old Emmett Till was lynched in Mississippi; an all-white jury acquitted his killers in about an hour. In 1963 a Klan bomb at the Sixteenth Street Baptist Church in Birmingham killed four Black girls, Addie Mae Collins, Cynthia Wesley, Carole Robertson, and Carol Denise McNair; no one was convicted until 1977.
  • “had found first her brown mary-janes”: Mary Janes originated in the early 1900s, named after a character in Richard Outcault’s Buster Brown comic strip (1902), and were popularized by the Brown Shoe Company. Originally, brown or black, low-cut strap shoes were worn by both young boys and girls as formal attire. They transitioned into popular women’s fashion in the 1920s-30s and became a 1960s mod icon.
  • “The detective had sent her a yellowed newspaper clipping”: The Chicago detective sent Esther a “yellowed newspaper clipping” that read “Mass Murderess Apprehended Accused by Dead Mothers and Dead Children.” There was nothing about the fathers. It was a “...woman abortionist and her timid little assistant…” who had killed “...all the poor, unmarried mothers and their babies, too, in a hospital which she ran for that purpose.” The mothers were described as well treated right up until their delivery. It said the fathers sent bouquets. When the mother was supposed to deliver, mother and baby were killed “...by the cruel, old abortionist, a large woman, weighing almost three hundred pounds.” The babies were buried with lime. See The Old Woman Abortionist.
    • Lime is used at mass grave sites to capture the scent of decay and keep soil pH high. Low pH soil is an indicator for a mass grave as the decomposition products are acidic and lower the soil pH. Adding lime reduces this acidity.
    • The newspaper was dated as happening during the “horse and buggy age.” The “old woman abortionist” kept at her trade until she murdered a “...woman who was not, who never could have been a mother.” This story may have its roots in real events.
    • Baby farming is the historical practice of accepting custody of an infant or child in exchange for payment in late-Victorian Britain and, less commonly, in Australia and the United States. Some baby farmers adopted children by paying lump-sum payments, while others cared for infants for periodic payments. There are several women or couples in the past that murdered or allowed the adopted children to die through starvation. Even the Catholic run Bon Secours Mother and Baby Home in Ireland illegally adopted children without their mother’s consent and the Home had an abnormally high child death rate with malnourished, emaciated babies. A significant amount of children’s remains were discovered in a septic tank on the property. There was also mistreatment and abuse of the mothers.
    • One of the most famous baby farming child murderers was Amelia Dyer. She murdered infants in her care over a thirty-year period during the Victorian era in the United Kingdom. Trained as a nurse and widowed in 1869, Dyer turned to baby farming to support herself. She initially cared for the children legitimately, then began directly murdering children she took in, strangling at least some of them, and disposing of the bodies to avoid attention. She was caught because the body of a child was found in the river. The “timid assistant” may refer to Dyer’s 23-year-old daughter Polly who assisted in the child murder of Doris Marmon.
    • Abortion has been used to separate good women from bad women. This stigma punishes the bad woman in society. Even if the abortion is necessary to save a woman’s life, religion often stipulates that a woman must sacrifice herself. It is better to die than live with the stigma of having an abortion. The only good heroine is a dead one. When Young was writing this over eighteen years, abortion was illegal, and contraception was restricted by law until Griswold v. Connecticut (1965).
  • “was natural and a mother should suffer”: This may refer to Genesis 3:16 as Eve and women’s punishment for being cast out of the Garden of Eden. “In sorrow thou shalt bring forth children” (Genesis 3:16, KJV). See Genesis and Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery.
  • “pregnant in her dreams even though there was neither child nor father”: Esther may be suffering from False pregnancy or pseudocyesis. It is the appearance of clinical or subclinical signs and symptoms associated with pregnancy although the individual is not physically carrying a baby. The mistaken impression that one is pregnant includes signs and symptoms such as tender breasts with secretions, abdominal growth, delayed menstrual periods, and subjective feelings of a moving fetus. False pregnancy has a prominent psychiatric component as well as physical manifestations of pregnancy. It can be caused by physical or mental trauma, a chemical imbalance of hormones, and some medical conditions. See pseudocyesis in the Chapter 75 resources. See Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery.
  • “a wall of brass between her and the bodies of creation”: “And I will make thee unto this people a fenced brasen wall” (Jeremiah 15:20, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “and the sun blotted out and become as black as sackcloth of hair”: “And the sun became black as sackcloth of hair, and the moon became as blood” (Revelation 6:12, KJV). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “the bathtub drownings of the brides”: In England George Joseph Smith married and drowned three brides in their baths for their money, the “Brides in the Bath” murders of 1912 to 1914; he was hanged in 1915. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “the father’s skull that was brought home to play with the children”: Esther thought he only solved the “white mysteries.” Esther guessed it might be dead brides or the missing father where the children played with the skull judging by the newspaper clippings the Chicago detective sent her. The missing father may refer to the story Madge told Homer during the bus ride in Chapter 7 about Mr. and Mrs. Tidings.
  • “Life is a dream, and I have been wrong and much wronged”: Once when he thought he was dreaming he “delivered a real baby...and said—Oh, I see it all now! Life is a dream, and I have been wrong and much wronged, and I must give up this practice!” This line may refer to a play by Pedro Calderon de la Barca called Life Is A Dream. Calderón (1600-1681) was a Spanish dramatist, poet, writer and knight of the Order of Santiago. Life Is A Dream is a philosophical allegory regarding the human situation and the mystery of life. The play’s central themes are the conflict between free will and fate, as well as restoring one’s honor. Other themes include dreams versus reality and the conflict between father and son. La vida es sueño was first performed in 1635.
  • “to death which is another form of love”: There are several characters, like Miss MacIntosh and Mrs. Hogden, that have manipulated their arguments so they are never wrong or never appear to be wrong.

A man serving on a jury.
Lair
A wild animal’s den; a hiding place.
Mary Janes
Low, strapped shoes for children.
Nettle
A plant with stinging hairs.
Oilskin
Cloth made waterproof with oil; a raincoat of it.
Remorseless
Without pity; relentless.
Riddance
Freedom from something unwanted, as in good riddance.
Sackcloth
A coarse cloth worn in mourning or penance.
Tallow
Animal fat used for candles and soap. Tallow-colored is a dull, yellowish white.
Tuning fork
A two-pronged steel fork that sounds one pitch when struck.
Uterus
The womb.
Vortex
A whirlpool or whirlwind.
