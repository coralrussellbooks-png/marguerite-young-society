Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Primary Text

Young, Marguerite. Miss MacIntosh, My Darling. New York: Scribner's, 1965. 1,198 pp.

The central primary text for the entire philosophical argument developed across all chapters. Referenced throughout as the source of the ten-pillar system, including the plural self, illusion as primary, grief as permanent weather, the witness, and recursive construction. The novel's formal properties, including sentence length, recursive structure, and refusal of linear resolution, are treated as philosophical arguments in their own right.

Secondary Philosophical and Theoretical Sources

Hannah Arendt

Arendt, Hannah. The Human Condition. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1958.

Cited in relation to the seventh pillar, the ontology of the marginal, and the concept of the space of appearance as the political space in which plural beings appear to each other in their difference rather than their sameness. Also cited in the map of philosophical conversations convened by the ten pillars, under both the plural self and the witness.

Arendt, Hannah. The Origins of Totalitarianism. New York: Harcourt, Brace, 1951.

Cited alongside The Human Condition in the discussion of the seventh pillar. Arendt's analysis of totalitarianism as the destruction of plurality, rather than primarily the use of violence, is applied to Young's treatment of the demand for the "real" Miss MacIntosh beneath the wig as a totalitarian demand in Arendt's sense.

Mikhail Bakhtin

Bakhtin, Mikhail. The Dialogic Imagination: Four Essays. Translated by Caryl Emerson and Michael Holquist. Austin: University of Texas Press, 1981.

Cited in the discussion of the seventh pillar for the theory of the dialogic, in which meaning is produced in the encounter between voices rather than by a single voice speaking a single truth. The text extends Bakhtin's principle to argue that the voices in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling by Marguerite Young are not only the voices of different characters but the voices of different selves within Vera herself. Cited again in the map of philosophical conversations under the ontology of the marginal.

Walter Benjamin

Benjamin, Walter. The Arcades Project. Translated by Howard Eiland and Kevin McLaughlin. Cambridge: Belknap Press of Harvard University Press, 1999.

Cited in the discussion of the sixth pillar, the ethics of incompletion, as a supreme example of the unfinished work whose incompleteness is not incidental but integral to its argument. The Arcades Project's fragmentary form is presented as enacting the same principle Young's system names: that the honest engagement with a world that does not resolve produces work that does not resolve.

Benjamin, Walter. "Theses on the Philosophy of History." In Illuminations: Essays and Reflections, edited by Hannah Arendt, translated by Harry Zohn. New York: Schocken Books, 1968.

Cited extensively in the discussion of the eighth pillar, sedimentary time, for the image of the angel of history facing the accumulated wreckage of the past while being blown forward into the future by the storm of progress. Benjamin's critique of the progressive view of history is directly connected to Young's permanent grief extended to the scale of history. Cited again in the map of philosophical conversations under both grief as permanent weather and sedimentary time.

Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann

Berger, Peter L., and Thomas Luckmann. The Social Construction of Reality: A Treatise in the Sociology of Knowledge. Garden City, NY: Doubleday, 1966.

Cited in the map of philosophical conversations convened by the ten pillars, under the discussion of illusion as primary.

Judith Butler

Butler, Judith. Works on grievability and public mourning, including Precarious Life: The Powers of Mourning and Violence. New York: Verso, 2004.

Cited in the map of philosophical conversations under grief as permanent weather, in relation to the political implications of Young's third pillar. [[25]]

Butler's work on language and gender is also implicitly referenced in the discussion of the ninth pillar and the map of philosophical conversations under language as construction, within the tradition of feminist language theory from Dale Spender through Judith Butler.

René Descartes

Descartes, René. Meditations on First Philosophy. 1641.

Cited as a representative of the philosophical tradition Young refuses, specifically the tradition that protects the assumption of the unified self. The evil demon thought experiment is referenced in the discussion of the second pillar, illusion as primary, as the nightmare that Young reframes: not the demon but the dream of escaping the demon.

Michel Foucault

Foucault, Michel. The Order of Things: An Archaeology of the Human Sciences. English translation. New York: Pantheon Books, 1970. (Originally published in French as Les Mots et les choses, 1966.)

Cited in the discussion of why the tradition missed Young, as part of the poststructuralist conversation that would have been most equipped to receive her work but arrived in American intellectual culture too late to create a context for Miss MacIntosh, My Darling by Marguerite Young.

Jacques Derrida

Derrida, Jacques. Of Grammatology. Translated by Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak. Baltimore: Johns Hopkins University Press, 1976. (Originally published in French, 1967.)

Cited alongside Foucault in the discussion of timing and the tradition's failure to receive Young. The poststructuralist dismantling of the unified subject and the critique of presence are identified as the philosophical conversation that would have known what to do with her work.

Patricia Hill Collins

Collins, Patricia Hill. Works on Black feminist epistemology, including Black Feminist Thought: Knowledge, Consciousness, and the Politics of Empowerment. Boston: Unwin Hyman, 1990.

Cited in the map of philosophical conversations under illusion as primary, in relation to the epistemological implications of Young's second pillar.

David Hume

Hume, David. A Treatise of Human Nature. 1739-1740.

Cited in the discussion of the first pillar, the plural self, for Hume's famous introspective discovery that he could never catch himself without a perception, finding the bundle of experiences with no experiencer underneath. The text notes that Hume retreated from the full implications of this discovery, while Young does not.

William James

James, William. Works on the stream of consciousness and the self as process, including The Principles of Psychology. New York: Henry Holt, 1890.

Cited in the discussion of the first pillar for James's naming of the stream of consciousness and his understanding that the self was a process rather than a thing. The text argues that Young takes James more seriously than most of the tradition did, extending the stream until it becomes clear that it does not flow in one direction. Cited again in the map of philosophical conversations under the plural self.

Immanuel Kant

Kant, Immanuel. Critique of Pure Reason. 1781.

Cited as a representative of the philosophical tradition that protects the assumption of the unified self, specifically through the transcendental subject, the organizing "I" that makes experience possible by being prior to it.

Robin Wall Kimmerer

Kimmerer, Robin Wall. Braiding Sweetgrass: Indigenous Wisdom, Scientific Knowledge, and the Teachings of Plants. Minneapolis: Milkweed Editions, 2013.

Cited in the discussion of the tenth pillar, immanent transcendence, for the insistence that the world is not a collection of objects but a community of subjects, and that attention to the non-human is not sentiment but epistemology. Kimmerer's work is connected to Young's practice of giving the Indiana landscape the same density and interiority as her human characters. Cited again in the map of philosophical conversations under immanent transcendence.

John McPhee

McPhee, John. Works on deep time and geology, including Annals of the Former World. New York: Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 1998.

Cited in the map of philosophical conversations under sedimentary time, as part of the tradition of deep time thinking that informs the eighth pillar.

Iris Murdoch

Murdoch, Iris. The Sovereignty of Good. London: Routledge, 1970.

Cited in the map of philosophical conversations under the witness, alongside Simone Weil's ethics of attention.

Plato

Plato. The Republic. c. 375 BCE.

Cited as the origin story of the Western philosophical tradition's foundational opposition between the real and the illusory, specifically the allegory of the cave. Young's second pillar is presented as a direct answer to Plato: there is no sunlit world outside the cave, only a larger cave more elaborately lit.

Adrienne Rich

Rich, Adrienne. "Notes Toward a Politics of Location." In Blood, Bread, and Poetry: Selected Prose 1979-1985. New York: Norton, 1986.

Cited in the discussion of the ninth pillar, language as construction, for the argument that political speech is always located, always spoken from a particular body in a particular place with a particular history, and that the pretense of speaking from nowhere is a form of domination rather than universality. Cited again in the map of philosophical conversations under language as construction.

Dale Spender

Spender, Dale. Man Made Language. London: Routledge and Kegan Paul, 1980.

Cited in the map of philosophical conversations under language as construction, as part of the tradition of feminist language theory.

Gertrude Stein

Stein, Gertrude. Works including poetry, prose experiments, and theoretical essays. Key texts include Tender Buttons (1914), The Making of Americans (1925), and Lectures in America (1935).

Cited in the discussion of the first pillar as the thinker Young is most directly in dialogue with, identified as William James's student. Stein's investigation of repetition, the continuous present, and identity as something that happens rather than something that is, is described as running parallel to Young's project with different formal tools. Where Stein strips language down to show the self as a grammatical construction, Young piles language up until the construction becomes visible through its own excess. Cited again in the discussion of the ninth pillar for the investigation of the relationship between grammatical structure and the structure of experience, and in the map of philosophical conversations under both the plural self and language as construction.

Simone Weil

Weil, Simone. Works on attention and ethics, including Waiting for God. Translated by Emma Craufurd. New York: Putnam, 1951, and "Reflections on the Right Use of School Studies with a View to the Love of God." In Waiting for God.

Cited in the discussion of the fourth pillar, the witness, for the ethics of attention: the argument that the foundation of ethics is not duty or utility or virtue but attention, the capacity to truly see the person in front of you without the distortions of projection or self-interest. Weil's attention is described as an act of love understood as a practice rather than a feeling. Young's witnessing is described as Weil's attention made systemic. Cited again in the map of philosophical conversations under the witness.

Alfred North Whitehead

Whitehead, Alfred North. Process and Reality: An Essay in Cosmology. New York: Macmillan, 1929.

Cited in the discussion of the fifth pillar, recursive construction, as the most rigorous available philosophical framework for the world Young's fiction describes. Whitehead's central claim that reality is composed of processes rather than things, that the fundamental units of existence are events and occasions of experience rather than stable objects, is presented as Young's plural self in the language of metaphysics. The self in Whitehead is described as a process that is its experiences, the verb rather than the noun. Cited again in the map of philosophical conversations under both recursive construction and immanent transcendence, in relation to process theology.

Notes on the Philosophical Conversations Map

The text provides an explicit map of philosophical conversations convened by the ten pillars, which is reproduced here for reference and cross-indexed with the full bibliography above:

On the plural self: William James, Gertrude Stein, Gloria Anzaldua, Hannah Arendt on plurality as the condition of political life.

On illusion as primary: Plato (as the tradition Young refuses), Descartes (as the tradition Young refuses), Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann on the social construction of reality, Patricia Hill Collins on Black feminist epistemology.

On grief as permanent weather: Walter Benjamin on the accumulated wreckage of history, Judith Butler on grievability and public mourning, the tradition of indigenous philosophy on the ongoing presence of historical loss.

On the witness: Simone Weil on attention as an ethical act, Iris Murdoch on the sovereignty of good, Hannah Arendt on the space of appearance as the space of honest presence.

On recursive construction: Alfred North Whitehead on becoming rather than being, the constitutional tradition of democratic revision, the feminist tradition of ongoing political construction.

On the ethics of incompletion: Walter Benjamin on the incomplete project, the tradition of process philosophy, Young herself as the supreme exemplar.

On the ontology of the marginal: Hannah Arendt on plurality and political life, Mikhail Bakhtin on the dialogic, the tradition of coalition politics developed in feminist and anti-racist organizing.

On sedimentary time: Walter Benjamin on the angel of history, John McPhee and the tradition of deep time thinking, indigenous philosophy on the ongoing presence of the past.

On language as construction: Gertrude Stein on the grammar of being, Adrienne Rich on the politics of location, the tradition of feminist language theory from Dale Spender through Judith Butler.

On immanent transcendence: Robin Wall Kimmerer on the intelligence of the living world, the tradition of process theology developed from Whitehead, Young herself as the philosopher of the sacred without the beyond.

Note on Gloria Anzaldua

Anzaldua, Gloria. Borderlands/La Frontera: The New Mestiza. San Francisco: Aunt Lute Books, 1987.

Cited in the map of philosophical conversations under the plural self. Anzaldua is not discussed at length in the body text but is listed as a key interlocutor on the question of plural selfhood and identity.

Note on Missing Publication Details

Several sources are referenced in the text in ways that identify the thinker and argument but do not specify individual titles, including the traditions of indigenous philosophy, coalition politics, and process theology, as well as the constitutional tradition of democratic revision and the feminist tradition of ongoing political construction. These are cited as traditions rather than individual texts, reflecting the text's approach of situating Young within broad intellectual communities rather than limiting her to specific academic conversations.
