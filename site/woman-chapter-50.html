Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 50

← Chapter 49 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 51 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

The universe will not hold still in this chapter, and its instability is the pillar. A "snow bird" veers in the wind, "central fires of jewels" flicker, "relationships which were never to be found" dissolve among a multiplicity of "mirrors" and "phases," and the woman's uncertain place in the world and the plural, unfixed self arrive as one shifting weather. Mr. Spitzer loses himself in "clouded rooms," "one room so like the other," a "rose withering in a vase where there had been no rose before," two golden chairs of which one may be only a dream, and the doubling that will not resolve is the illusion of the individual seen from inside a mind coming apart.

Instruments without strings run through the chapter, "a lyre without strings, a trumpet without a throat," and Mr. Spitzer fears he is himself "only the dream," an "imaginary bird" caught in the strings. This silencing and loss of expressive power is invisibility and erasure and it is the music that goes unheard, the instrument unstrung, the voice with no throat. His complaint against God for making "dice without markings," for a life lived "by the dreams of others," is women's imposed passivity and it is the book's larger claim that no self authors itself, each living inside another's dream.

The "beautiful opium lady" holds the chapter's center, chattering "madly," her "imaginary dawns" and her inability to "keep a chart of time's dimensions" or to tell life from death making her the fullest figure of a woman held between the two, real and dream as one country ruled from a drugged bed. Mr. Spitzer's return to her, "body and soul and spirit crying in the wind," and his faith that "when life was over, it would begin" on a star where "living love was united with dead love," is dependency and longing and it is the dissolving boundary reached toward as a home, the place where the categories of living and dead finally give way. Her "disassociations and mistakes of senses" are her powerlessness and her way of knowing at once, the two never separated.

The star toward which the chapter reaches is a "keyhole star," a "door of truth" "never opened by man," where "no eye stared on either side," and the woman's exclusion from knowledge and the self's exclusion from its own truth are one locked door. Life is "scarcely understood before it was gone," a "flying sand grain" becoming a star, and "all that happened happened only once," the irretrievability that falls hardest on women. The chapter ends on the "old suffragette captain" from whom Mr. Spitzer "had learned much," her "hyphenated name more important than the two names it joined," her "broken melody" she would not "take up in life but perhaps in death," a woman whose fulfillment was deferred past living, her mind dying before her heart, and the "nocturne" written for her is the unheard song the book keeps composing for its erased.
