On Pre-Roe Reproductive Law

Esther’s situation (facing potential criminal prosecution for the disappearance of her stillborn children, in a legal environment with no precedent for her case) reflects the actual legal landscape for women’s reproductive lives before the 1973 Roe v. Wade decision. Abortion was a criminal offense in most American states, punishable by fines and imprisonment, permitted only to save the mother’s life and even then restricted in access. Miscarriage and stillbirth occupied a legal gray zone: a woman whose pregnancy ended without a living child could, depending on circumstances, face investigation for infanticide or criminal abortion. Women who sought illegal abortions risked not only their health and lives but prosecution. The stigma attached to any reproductive loss (miscarriage, stillbirth, abortion) was that of moral failure or criminality. Esther is not accused of abortion specifically, but the machinery pursuing her (the detective, the undertaker’s complaint, the imagined jury) is the same machinery that operated against women whose reproductive lives did not conform to what the law permitted and what society approved.
