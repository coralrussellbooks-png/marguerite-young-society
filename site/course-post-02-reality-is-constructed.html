Lesson 2: Reality Is Constructed

Pillar: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed · Across every book

Act I: The Fractured Interior

Your private delusions and memory fabrications are not errors of the brain. They are your reality.

The modern West keeps a strict ledger. On one side sits objective truth, measurable and shared. On the other sits illusion, the stuff to be corrected, medicated, or debunked. The task of a mature mind, we are told, is to move everything from the second column into the first. Marguerite Young thought the ledger itself was the fantasy.

The Framework

Catherine Cartwheel, the mother at the center of Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, spends her life in a shuttered seaside mansion, dreaming on opium. Her visitors, her lovers, and her conversations may never have happened. Young does not frame this as a tragic departure from the real. She writes it with the same weight, detail, and dignity as anything else in the book. The novel's quiet argument is that every character lives this way, and Catherine is only more honest about it.

The Spanish playwright Pedro Calderón de la Barca put the idea on stage in Life Is a Dream (1635). His prince Segismundo, drugged and moved between a prison and a palace, can no longer tell which life is the real one. His famous speech ends: "¿Qué es la vida? Una ilusión, una sombra, una ficción... que toda la vida es sueño, y los sueños, sueños son." In English: "What is life? An illusion, a shadow, a fiction... for all of life is a dream, and dreams are only dreams."

Indian philosophy gave this insight a name, Māyā. In the Advaita Vedanta tradition, the world of forms is appearance, a veil over the single reality of Brahman. Here Young makes her radical turn. Vedanta keeps a truer world behind the veil. Young removes the behind. There is no backstage. The veil is the whole theater, and it is where we actually live.

Memory research quietly agrees. Each time a memory is recalled, the brain rebuilds it, and the rebuilt version is what gets stored. Psychologist Elizabeth Loftus showed how easily people come to remember events that never occurred. Our pasts are not archives. They are workshops.

The cultural anthropologist Ernest Becker argued in The Denial of Death (1973) that people build personal myths, which he called hero projects, to hold off the terror of mortality. Read beside Young, these myths are not cowardice. They are shelter, made by hand, from the weather of being alive and finite. The question is never whether to live inside a myth. The question is whether to live inside it with open eyes.

The Art Companion

Akira Kurosawa's film Rashomon (1950) tells one crime four times, through four witnesses, and each account is sincere and incompatible. The film never names the true version. Watch it as a portrait of how every mind builds the world it can bear to live in.

René Magritte's The Treachery of Images (1929), the painted pipe labeled "Ceci n'est pas une pipe," makes the same point in one frame. The picture is not the thing, yet the picture is all we are ever handed.

Reflection Questions

  • What story about my family do I repeat often but have never checked?

  • Which memory of mine has shifted with retelling, and what did the shift protect?

  • What private myth helps me get out of bed in the morning?

  • If one of my illusions disappeared tomorrow, what would rush in to fill the space?

The Exercise: Two Ledgers and a Blessing

  1. Choose one memory that shaped the person who grew out of it. Write it quickly, exactly as it lives in the mind, in about half a page.

  2. On a second page, list only what can be confirmed: dates, photographs, a sibling's version, a letter. Notice where the two pages disagree.

  3. On a third page, answer one question: what has the remembered version given me that the confirmed version could not?

  4. Close with a short paragraph that honors the remembered version as a made thing, built with care, the way a person might honor a house their grandparents built.
