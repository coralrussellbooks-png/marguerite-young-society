Chapter 7: Excavating the Substrate

I. The Philosophy: Your Sound Has a History and It Did Not Go Away

It is comforting to treat a fresh sample, a new plugin, or a new guitar as a neutral beginning. You buy it, you load it, you start. Whatever happened before it reached your hands feels like someone else's story, finished and behind us. Radical Immanence says time does not work that way. The past does not recede behind you as you move forward. It accumulates, layer by layer, pressing down on the present. Historical injustice is not a past event. It is a geological fact about the ground you are standing on right now. You cannot build fairly on ground you are pretending is neutral.

The sound in your hands is not a blank starting point. It is the top layer of a deep stack. The scales and the swing you reach for carry the labor, the worship, and the survival of people whose music was taken and resold without credit. Equal temperament carries the industrial flattening of older tunings into one standard the factories could mass-produce. Your instrument carries the colonial trade in tonewoods, ivory, and shellac. Your laptop and interface carry the lithium, cobalt, and coltan dug out of the ground far away, and the hands that assembled the boards. None of this is a footnote to the gear. It is the gear's actual composition, as real as the spec sheet.

This is not an argument for guilt or for refusing to make anything. It is an argument for honesty. When you act as if your sound arrived from nowhere, you are building on ground you have agreed to pretend is neutral, and that pretense is exactly how the old harm stays buried and keeps working. To excavate the substrate is to ask where this came from and let the answer change how you handle it. The honest maker does not get to start clean. The honest maker starts by knowing what is underfoot.

II. Deep Journal Prompts

The Origin You Don't Ask About. Pick the sound you reach for most often without thinking: a sample pack, a preset, a scale, an instrument. Write down everything you actually know about where it came from: who played it first, who recorded it, who was paid, who was not. Then write down how much of that is a real answer and how much is a brand story printed to keep you from asking. Sit with the gap. What does it cost the work to not know?

The Cheap Sound. Write about a tool you use because it is cheap: a free sample library, a cracked plugin, a budget subscription. Trace, as honestly as you can, why it is cheap. Cheapness is never free; someone or somewhere absorbed the cost. Follow the price down through the layers until you reach the ground it was paid from. Then decide, plainly, what you want to do with that knowledge.

III. Studio Sonic Exercises

The Material Core Sample

Choose one sound you are about to work with and trace it back as far as you genuinely can before you record a single note. Read the label, then go past the label. Find the player, the recording, the country, the maker of the instrument, the lineage of the scale. Where the trail goes cold, mark exactly where your knowledge ends, because that gap is part of the truth too. Build a short piece that records the journey as layers, the oldest origin at the bottom, your own hands at the top, with a marked band for everything you could not find out. The piece is a core sample of the sound's history, including the parts that were hidden from you.

The Honestly Sourced Piece

Make one piece using only sounds whose history you can actually name out loud: an instrument you know the maker of, your own voice, the room you are sitting in, a sample you recorded yourself or that lives in the public domain with a known past. The constraint will be hard and will make the piece smaller and plainer than you wanted. That smallness is the lesson. Notice how much of your usual practice depends on not knowing, and let this one honest piece show you what working on known ground feels like.

IV. Cross-Medium Interference

The Layered Liner Note

Write the history of one finished track as a timeline that reads from the deep past up to your hands, every stage you know and every stage you do not. Print it as a liner note, or write it into the file's metadata, and attach it so it travels with the release. Most liner notes tell a clean origin story meant to sell. This one tells the geology. When someone reads it, they meet the track standing on its actual ground, not the ground the market wanted them to imagine.
