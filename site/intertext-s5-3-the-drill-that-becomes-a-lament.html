Intertextuality in MMMD

3. The Drill That Becomes a Lament

← Previous · Intertextuality in MMMD Home · Next →

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Allusions: Deafness and Sign Language · The Bible

Definition from research

One set of references travels further than any other and changes its meaning at each stop: the genealogies of Israel.

In chapter 9 they are schoolwork. Miss MacIntosh prefers the begats to the Bible's stories because they need no interpretation. Vera must memorize "all the genealogies of the Moody Bible, the sons of Levi, Judah, Er, Onan, Issachar, the sons of Tola, Phuvah, Zebulun." The threat attached is practical: they will memorize "from Abraham to Abraham" and skip the afternoon drive.

In chapter 7 the same names come from the bus-driver, and they are grief. He is speaking of the old doctor and "the poor humanity that was dead before it was ever born." Then comes the roll call: "Reuben, Gad, Manasseh, Jahaziel, Eliezer, Luke, Dan, Saul, blind as a bat." He adds "the children of Ham, deaf as a post, all these people begetting, begetting, begetting shades." The genealogy that should prove continuity has become a list of the dead and the never-born.

In chapter 10 the names return as questions: "Where, my dearest heart, is Issachar? Where is Nepthalin? Where is Reuben who begot a horde?" The questions run into the burial service, "earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust."

Then in chapter 18 the drill turns on its teacher. Miss MacIntosh, stripped of her disguise, cries out the names she once assigned as homework: "Where is Issachar, my dear, my dear of dears? Where is Reuben? Where is Nabal? Where is Mr. Spitzer?" She slips a living man in among the patriarchs. The memorized list has become a roll call of everyone lost, and it has entered her own life.

This is one of Young's most exact strategies. She does not change the text. She changes who says it and when. A list learned for use becomes a lament once the person reciting it has lost something. The same names serve the schoolroom, the barroom, the funeral, and the breakdown. Section 2 describes meaning as passing through everything a reader has read. Young makes that happen inside the novel, four times, with one list.
