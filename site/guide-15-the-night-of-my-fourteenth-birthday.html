Chapter 15: The Night of My Fourteenth Birthday

← Chapter 14 · Guide Home · Chapter 16 →

Research notes: Chapter 15

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Dreams and Imagination · Motherhood, Pregnancy, and Birth · Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos

Symbols: Pregnancy, Birth, and Children · The Sea and Water

Allusions: Deafness and Sign Language · Ecclesiastes · Gambling and Playing Cards · Revelation and the Apocalypse · The Bible

After the long, enclosed darkness of Chapter 14 (that claustrophobic bedroom, Catherine and Mr. Spitzer circling each other in the lamplight) Chapter 15 throws open a door and sends you outside.

We are back with Vera. It is still the night of her fourteenth birthday. She is standing at the sea.

This chapter is the last breath before the revelation. Something extraordinary happens at its close, and Young earns it by spending the chapter inside the particular texture of being fourteen and alive and standing at the edge of the Atlantic in the dark, feeling like the universe has just become available to you.

What actually happens in Chapter 15

Vera has wandered from the garden to the shore. The night is wild: fog, a skull-white moon, black roaring water, phosphorescent surf. She runs into the waves. She is soaked, her hair streaked with salt. She doesn’t care. She laughs aloud.

In this state of exhilaration, her mind turns to Miss MacIntosh, but differently than before. For the first time in the novel, Vera turns a critical eye on her. She thinks about what Miss MacIntosh doesn’t know: the undertow, the waywardness of water, the deeper things of life. She thinks about Miss MacIntosh’s strange habits: the umbrella carried indoors, the windows flung open in winter, the bare cold bedroom with its broken-down brass bed. She replays the long lecture Miss MacIntosh delivered all day on her birthday: proverbs about death, about marriage, about disorder and men and the proper life of an industrious ant.

And then the sea performs something extraordinary. Young gives us a passage of apocalyptic beauty: the water turning to glass, the clouds pregnant with impossible colors, the harpers harping on the sea at the end of time. It lasts only a moment. Then the waters go black again.

The chapter ends with a blind sheep dog barking at the waves.

Vera’s revolt

This is the first time we’ve heard Vera think critically about Miss MacIntosh rather than yearningly. It is important, and it feels true to being fourteen.

What Vera notices, standing in the surf on her birthday night, is that Miss MacIntosh’s practical wisdom has limits. She couldn’t swim a stroke. She avoided the greatest waves. “She would have been dragged down at the first blow by her rainproof” and her sea boots and all her needles and pins. All that famous common sense, and she couldn’t breast the roaring tide.

There is something fond in this critique, even as it sharpens into something harder: “Miss MacIntosh knew less than I did, hers being” “a deadness, a dullness, a coldness of heart.” That is a fourteen-year-old's verdict, delivered with a fourteen-year-old's certainty, standing wet in the ocean at midnight feeling immortal. Young knows exactly what she’s doing. This is not the novel’s judgment. It is Vera’s judgment on this particular night, in this particular mood, before she knows what she is about to find out.

The revolt is the preparation for the grief.

Miss MacIntosh’s birthday lecture

The chapter gives us, in Vera’s memory, the full length of Miss MacIntosh’s speech on the day of her fourteenth birthday. It is extraordinary. It goes on all day, through breakfast and dinner and in between, and it is almost entirely about death and marriage in that order, as if they were the same subject.

“Death, like our mother and our father,” “is an old harpoonist who will capture the swimmer in narrow streams.” “Our cradle stands in the grave.” Death “loves a young maid and an old man. Death loves a shining mark.”

On marriage: all women should certainly marry, but no man. “Perhaps no man should marry woman.” Before marriage, keep your eyes open; after marriage, “keep them half shut for fear of what” you might see. True marriages are “few and far between due to the sins of our first parents and the high cost of living.”

And woven through: wheat markets, bowling scores, black hens laying white eggs, the sinking of the Titanic, Ecclesiastes, the Farmer’s Almanac, Benjamin Franklin, John Bunyan. Miss MacIntosh does not separate the sacred and the practical. They are the same shelf.

What makes this extraordinary is that Young has given us, in this extended monologue, Miss MacIntosh in full voice, not filtered through Vera’s admiration or grief, but rendered directly, on a grey rainy birthday morning, in all her sour and loving and slightly unhinged glory. She is funny. She is bleak. She is preparing Vera, in her own completely idiosyncratic way, for a world she knows is hard. And underneath every word, if you listen, is the sound of a woman who has made her peace with having been loved by no one.

The vision at the sea

Young saves the chapter’s most extraordinary writing for the end, and it arrives like weather: suddenly, fully, and then it passes.

The sea turns to oily green and gold. The sky lights up with colors outside the mortal spectrum. The waves slow, almost stop. Vera hears what she describes as “harpers harping on those seas of glass which man shall behold at the end of time when the heavens are parted, when the sky rolls back like a scroll.”

Then it’s over. The waters go black again. The clouds fade. A dead seagull lies cold on a rock. The marble statue of Saul, we learn in passing, was once swept out to sea by a musical tide and never returned. A strange marble head came in and took his place.

This is Young at her most formally ambitious: a moment of genuine apocalyptic vision, framed on either side by the mundane, the barking sheep dog, the dead seagull, the empty playing cards the sea leaves behind. The heavens part and the harpers play, and then the sheep dog barks at the waves. Young will not let the transcendent stand alone. She always puts it back in the world.

The secret dream

Briefly, almost in passing, Vera mentions a secret dream she has been carrying: a false wedding, one that begets no other wedding, where Mr. Spitzer is married to the bridegroom. The only guests are “the northern light beams, the stars, and droves of seals on a solitary shore.”

Young plants this without explanation. We are not told whose wedding it is, what the bridegroom represents, why Mr. Spitzer appears in it. It feels significant, and it is, but the novel will take its time with it. For now, file it away. Vera carries a dream about a wedding that cannot produce another wedding, and it makes her laugh in the dark.

What to watch for as you keep reading

Light spoilers: This section hints at what happens later in the novel. Skip it if you want to meet the story fresh.

The sea has now been established as the novel’s site of revelation and dissolution. It sweeps statues away. It brings unknown heads in their place. It briefly parts to show you something outside the mortal spectrum and then closes again. Every time you encounter the sea in the chapters ahead, remember what it did on the night of Vera’s fourteenth birthday.

Miss MacIntosh’s bedroom (bare, cold, the windows open in winter, the three-mirror dressing table she never looks into) has been described here for the first time with some strangeness. Why does she carry her umbrella indoors? Why does she refuse to look in mirrors? Why does she sleep in the cold? These are the details that will matter when Chapter 16 finally delivers what has been withheld since Chapter 13.

The blind sheep dog barking at the waves is the last image of the chapter, and it echoes Chapter 13’s garden of the deaf. An animal whose sight is gone, barking at something it cannot see, that cannot hear it. Young places this image carefully at the close of the chapter. It is not decorative.
