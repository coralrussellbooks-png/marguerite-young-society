Chapter 53: The Pale Replica

Research notes: Chapter 53

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Themes: Death and Mortality · Isolation and Connection · The Search for Meaning

Symbols: Cards, Games, and Chance

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft · Phenomenology · Plato and Platonism

Allusions: Gambling and Playing Cards · Suicide in History and Literature

Chapter 52 gave us the secret in its physical form: the trunks, the gowns, the no slippers. Chapter 53 gives us the other secret: the one that has been the novel's deepest structural question since Chapters 45 and 47 first began to surface it.

Peron apparently died by suicide.

And since Peron's death, Mr. Spitzer, Joachim, has been mistaken for him. Has been addressed as Peron. Has been seen as Peron by people who knew them both. Has been walking through the world in the afterimage of his dead twin, unable to assert his own identity against the force of the resemblance, perhaps no longer entirely certain which of them he is.

He describes himself as a pale replica. A mask or dream rather than flesh and blood. And this chapter gives us the most explicit statement yet of what the novel has been implying: Joachim “divided himself between himself and his dead brother.” Since Peron's death he has access to knowledge he should not have: the underworld of gambling, speakeasies, boxing, billiards, the shady and illegal and flamboyant life that was Peron's domain, not his. He has become, somehow, both.

What actually happens in Chapter 53

Mr. Spitzer meditates on “the difference between himself and his” brother, and on the dissolution of that difference since Peron's death. Peron was volatile, colorful, many-sided: a chameleon changing colors, a crazy patchwork of identities and experiences. Joachim was opaque, timorous, marked by a search for equilibrium and an inability to make the irreversible move. He was the timid chess player afraid to take the final action that would mean his definitive defeat.

He has “missed his brother by only a minute.” Trains keep passing the wrong station. Connections are not made. He wanders through empty landscapes seeking a place of reunion that does not arrive.

But something has changed since the death. Mr. Spitzer knows things he should not know. He knows the boxing records with the precision of someone who was at the fights: the 110-round bout of 1893, the fighters who continued with broken hands, the featherweight champions and their exact reigns. He knows the gambling underworld, the speakeasies, the shady corners of the city where Peron moved and Joachim never went. He has absorbed his brother through mourning, or through the dissolution of the border between them, or through the possibility that he was always Peron and only thought he was Joachim.

Peron's apparent suicide

Young gives us this information almost in passing, the way she gives all the most important information: in subordinate syntax, surrounded by other matter, requiring you to slow down and re-read. Peron's apparent suicide. Apparent: the word is doing work. Either the death was made to look like a suicide and was not, or the suicide itself was somehow ambiguous, uncertain, not definitively established. Young uses apparent and does not explain it.

What it adds to the Peron/Joachim question: if Peron died by suicide, then the identity substitution becomes even more unstable. Suicide is an act of will: a decisive, irreversible choice of the kind Joachim is described as incapable of making. A timid chess player who cannot take the final action. And yet if Peron, the reckless one, the one who burned brightly, made that choice, and if Joachim has been living as Peron since, then who made the choice? The impulsive twin or the cautious one? The one who lived by chance or the one who calculated every move?

Young does not answer. She gives you apparent and lets the word sit there, doing its work.

The chameleon and the black velvet pall

Young gives the two brothers contrasting textures: Peron is a chameleon, shifting colors to match every environment, a crazy patchwork of many-sidedness. Joachim is a black velvet pall: the cloth draped over a coffin, fixed, dark, the thing that marks the boundary between the living and the dead and does not change its nature regardless of what is placed beneath it.

The chameleon is Peron's gift and his instability. He could be anything. He was many things. His identity was performed and re-performed in the gambling halls and boxing clubs and speakeasies: a different suit for every occasion, a different face for every game. What was true of him was that he was always changing, which means it is very difficult to say what the truth of him was.

The black velvet pall is Joachim's identity: the mourner, the keeper, the one who marks the death and stands at its boundary. He is the thing that does not change while everything beneath him changes. And yet: since Peron's death, Joachim has absorbed Peron's knowledge, Peron's world, Peron's shady acquaintances and gambling records. The pall is beginning to take on color. The fixed is beginning to shift. The two identities are becoming what they always were in some deeper sense: not two but one, expressed through alternating facets.

Knowledge from the other side

Mr. Spitzer's knowledge of the boxing world deserves attention because it is so specific. He does not know boxing in the abstract. He knows the 1893 fight between Andy Bowen and Jack Burke: 110 rounds, seven hours and nineteen minutes, ending as a no-contest when neither fighter could come out of his corner, Burke having broken all the bones in both hands. He knows Henry Armstrong, who held world championships in three different weight classes simultaneously. He knows the fighters who continued after their hands broke, who went the distance when the spectators had fallen asleep.

This is not research. This is memory. And if it is memory, it is not Joachim's memory. Joachim, the cautious musician and lawyer, the man who valued his dignity more than his life, does not attend 110-round prize fights in New Orleans or follow featherweight title reigns. Peron did. Peron moved in those circles, knew those people, had that knowledge. And now Joachim has it.

Young is giving us the phenomenology of becoming someone else through grief: the way the mourner absorbs the dead, takes on their knowledge and their habits and their way of being in the world, until the boundary between mourner and mourned becomes unreliable. Mr. Spitzer, the pale replica, is not just wearing his brother's identity. He is, in some increasingly real sense, becoming it.

The missed connection, over and over

He “missed his brother by only a minute.”

Young's image of the perpetually missed connection (trains passing the wrong station, encounters that nearly happen and don't, the minute that keeps coming between Mr. Spitzer and the reunion he needs) is one of the chapter's most persistent and precise metaphors. He is always a minute late. He has always been a minute late. This is his existential condition rendered in schedule form: the train that was supposed to connect him to his brother, to the life he was supposed to have, to the moment of recognition he needed; it keeps passing without stopping. And he keeps arriving at the platform exactly one minute after it leaves.

To All My Darlings notes that recent research suggests humans perceive the world on approximately a fifteen-second delay: the brain processing and organizing experience slightly behind the moment it occurs, to prevent sensory overload. Mr. Spitzer's minute-late condition is this delay at its extreme: a man perpetually out of sync with the present moment, always processing what has just passed rather than what is happening. He could not have caught the train even if he had tried. He was built to arrive after it left.

The timid chess player

Mr. Spitzer is “a timid chess-player, afraid to” make the move that would mean his final defeat.

This is one of Young's most compressed portraits of a certain kind of consciousness: the person who will not make the decisive move because the decisive move would resolve the game. As long as the move is not made, the game continues. As long as the game continues, neither winning nor losing has been confirmed. The timid chess player's strategy is not to win. It is to defer the moment of determination indefinitely.

This is also Mr. Spitzer's strategy with his own life. He will not vote because “the dead cast no vote—and” he is not certain which side of that line he stands on. He will not declare himself Peron or Joachim because to declare would be to resolve the question and live with the resolution. He will not turn to Cousin Hannah at the moment when he almost could, because turning would mean the move that changes the board. He defers. He defers. He arrives a minute late to everything, and the game continues, and he is still in it, technically, having not yet made the move that ends it.

Death danced with the lady

Near the chapter's close, the novel returns to Cousin Hannah: death danced with the lady. Young reaches back to the danse macabre tradition: death as the intimate partner, leading everyone regardless of rank or accomplishment in the inevitable procession. But for Cousin Hannah, the dance was not simply imposed. She went toward it. She climbed mountains. She flew. She crossed every border. She lived in such intimate proximity to risk and extremity that death was less a surprise arrival than a companion she had been dancing with for years before it finally led her off the floor.

Mr. Spitzer, watching from the sidelines as he always has, knows this dance. He collected the alpenhorn. He kept the secret. He opened the trunks. He is still here, a minute late, having missed every connection, having never been certain whether he was the dancer or the audience. The pale replica. The one who stands at the edge of the floor holding the other's coat.

What to hold onto

Chapter 53 is the chapter where the novel's great structural secret comes as close to the surface as it will come: Peron died, apparently by suicide, and since then Joachim has been living in the space the death left, absorbing his brother's knowledge and world and possibly his identity. The pale replica. The mask or dream.

What Young is doing with this is more than a mystery plot. She is asking whether a person's identity is theirs in any absolute sense, or whether it is always partly composed of those they have lost, whether the mourner, by mourning completely enough, becomes in some real way a continuation of the mourned. Mr. Spitzer is the answer she proposes: not definitively either twin, but the space between them, the echo that forms when two things that were almost the same finally become one.

The train keeps passing. He keeps arriving a minute late. The game continues. He has not yet made the final move.
