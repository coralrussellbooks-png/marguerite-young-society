The Legend and Its Stone Guest

Don Juan is one of the foundational figures of Western literature: the libertine, the seducer, the man who pursues every woman and commits to none, who kills the father of one of his conquests and then, when the dead man's statue comes to dinner and invites him to return the visit, laughs and goes. In the original telling and in Mozart's Don Giovanni, Don Juan meets the statue at the graveyard, takes its stone hand, and is dragged down to hell unrepentant. He could have repented. He refuses. The defining characteristic of Don Juan is not his seductiveness but his refusal of the final reckoning: he will not account for what he has done. He will not change. He is dragged down without yielding.

The Don Juan tradition has a stone guest: the murdered father returned as a monument, as weight, as the inescapable consequence of the seducer's actions made material and permanent. The stone guest comes to dinner. The stone guest invites him to supper in return. Don Juan goes. He is taken. The stone guest is what happens when you refuse accountability long enough: the consequence becomes architecture, becomes something that can grip your hand and pull.

Mr. Spitzer at the Deathbed

In Chapter 43, Mr. Spitzer is at Cousin Hannah's deathbed. She is dying, crying out in the snow and cloud of her last delirium, and he has been “witness to this great lady's screamings, babblings which had revealed so much of vanished life.” Mr. Spitzer is managing his own grief and tactfulness at once. It seemed better to him “that she should speak not of the future but of the problematical past, of cities buried under the desert sand,” and he was “happy that she could not contemplate the great abyss toward which she headed now.” He is doing what he always does: witnessing, accommodating, apologizing for his presence, making himself useful at the edge of something he cannot enter.

And in the middle of this, he wonders: “was it possible that she had always been this pale Don Juan beneath her mask, this gentleman so frail, changeable, uncertain, evasive that his might have been a lady's trembling heart and tendency to faint, fall, slip into a simulated unconsciousness where all might be permitted which was never permitted by consciousness?”

This is one of the novel's most compressed sentences. Cousin Hannah is the great captain of the suffrage movement, the rescuer of fair ladies, the woman who in the East “was Al Hamid” (Chapter 42). Mr. Spitzer is asking whether, beneath that mask, she had always been a frail gentleman with a lady's trembling heart. The next lines take the question further: “If she should sleep, should she not love? Perhaps she had always been more fragile than some of those great ladies she had tried to carry away from monstrous loves, mirrors, clouds, doors.”

The Female Don Juan

The gender inversion is everything. Don Juan is by definition masculine: his project is the conquest of women, his refusal of consequence is coded as male prerogative, his punishment is being dragged to hell by a dead man's stone hand. He moves through the world. He pursues. He refuses to be held.

Cousin Hannah turns the figure inside out. Like Don Juan she rode out, wore the mask, and carried ladies away. She was the cavalier, Al Hamid in the East, “this virgin warrior who would seem to evoke no answering romance” (Chapter 42). But Don Juan pursues women to conquer them, and Cousin Hannah rode out to free them. Beneath the armor, Mr. Spitzer now suspects, the captain was as frail as anyone she ever rescued.

Her dying cries reveal the love behind the campaigns. “Oh, have you seen my love, dead love? Did she lift her veil embroidered with the stars, and did you see her eyes shining through a cloud?” “Then where's my love that disappeared, her hair like the white feathers drifting through snow, her gold eyes staring through a cloud?” (Chapter 43). “False modesty killed my love, she cried, and not I, not I, my love” (Chapter 44). Her epitaph, as Mr. Spitzer composes it, names the same search: “Here lies one who searched all her life for her dead love, a body covered by the snow” (Chapter 31). The love Cousin Hannah cries for is a woman lost in the mountain snow. The pale Don Juan beneath the mask is a woman who loved a woman, and who says so only as she dies.

The Stone Guest Who Never Came

In the Don Juan legend, the stone guest comes. The dead father, whose death was Don Juan's fault, returns as stone, comes to dinner, issues the invitation, arrives at the graveyard supper, takes the hand, drags him down. Don Juan is punished because the stone guest comes.

Cousin Hannah's stone guest never comes. Her dead love does not return as monument and consequence. She returns only in the dying woman's cries, a veiled figure with eyes “shining through a cloud.” She does not grip any hand or drag anyone anywhere. The same house holds a second love of this kind: Cousin Hannah “had said that my mother's love was dead, but she did not seem to know that death was necessary to my mother's love” (Chapter 31). The captain who fought for years to pull Catherine away from a dead love was keeping one of her own. Mr. Spitzer at the deathbed is watching the pale Don Juan die without ever having been dragged to hell. This is not a triumph. It is not a failure either. The stone guest did not come. The hell was not incurred. The great captain died “in her own old tent bed,” with her faithful witness beside her, still crying for her love, and the stone hand never gripped her wrist, and she went down anyway, because everything goes down, with or without the stone guest, because the abyss is there whether Don Juan laughs at it or not.

The Radical Immanence Reading

The pale Don Juan identification is Pillar 1 (Not One Self) applied to the question of how desire organizes a life. Cousin Hannah has been read throughout the novel as pure force: the captain, the marcher, the rescuer, the virgin warrior. Mr. Spitzer's deathbed question proposes that the armor was also a mask. Beneath the cavalier was a frail gentleman with a lady's trembling heart, and beneath the gentleman a woman grieving a woman she had lost in the snow. She is all of these at once, and none of them cancels the others.

Pillar 3 (Grief Doesn't End): the love organized around permanent absence is not a failure of love. It is one of the most sustained loves in the novel. She searched for her dead love all her life, and she is still crying for her at the end. The love has not faded. It has not transferred. It has not compromised. It has simply required that its object be unavailable, which the dead are. The rescuer who could not rescue herself dies still in that love, the pale Don Juan whose hell never arrived, whose love was exactly as permanent and exactly as organized around absence as the grief of everyone who loved her.
