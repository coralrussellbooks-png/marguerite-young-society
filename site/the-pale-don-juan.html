The Legend and Its Stone Guest

Don Juan is one of the foundational figures of Western literature: the libertine, the seducer, the man who pursues every woman and commits to none, who kills the father of one of his conquests and then, when the dead man's statue comes to dinner and invites him to return the visit, laughs and goes. In the original telling and in Mozart's Don Giovanni, Don Juan meets the statue at the graveyard, takes its stone hand, and is dragged down to hell unrepentant. He could have repented. He refuses. The defining characteristic of Don Juan is not his seductiveness but his refusal of the final reckoning: he will not account for what he has done. He will not change. He is dragged down without yielding.

The Don Juan tradition has a stone guest: the murdered father returned as a monument, as weight, as the inescapable consequence of the seducer's actions made material and permanent. The stone guest comes to dinner. The stone guest invites him to supper in return. Don Juan goes. He is taken. The stone guest is what happens when you refuse accountability long enough: the consequence becomes architecture, becomes something that can grip your hand and pull.

Mr. Spitzer at the Deathbed

Mr. Spitzer is at the opium lady's deathbed. She is dying. She is in the final hours, babbling, revealing vanished life, speaking more often of the remote past than of the future. Mr. Spitzer is managing his own grief and tactfulness simultaneously: keeping her talking about cities buried under desert sand rather than tomorrow's weather, encouraging her to take another city by storm rather than contemplate the great abyss toward which she headed. He is doing what he always does: witnessing, accommodating, apologizing for his presence, making himself useful at the edge of something he cannot enter.

And in the middle of this, he wonders: was it possible that she had always been this pale Don Juan beneath her mask, this gentleman so frail, changeable, uncertain, evasive that his might have been a lady's trembling heart and tendency to faint, fall, slip into a simulated unconsciousness where all might be permitted which was never permitted by consciousness?

This is one of the novel's most architecturally compressed sentences. Mr. Spitzer is asking whether the opium lady, whom everyone in the novel has experienced as the one who was loved and pursued and courted and visited, was actually herself the seducer: the one who was always frail and changeable and evasive precisely because commitment was what she refused, who slipped into unconsciousness when reality pressed its claim, who permitted in dreams what she denied in waking life, who has been conducting, all this time, a Don Juan career from her bed.

The Female Don Juan

The gender inversion is everything. Don Juan is by definition masculine: his project is the conquest of women, his refusal of consequence is coded as male prerogative, his punishment is being dragged to hell by a dead man's stone hand. He moves through the world. He pursues. He refuses to be held.

The opium lady does not move. She has not left her bed in years. She does not pursue anyone; they come to her. She does not seduce in any conventional sense; she is visited, entertained, courted by an endless procession of the living and the dead. But Mr. Spitzer's question proposes that this is the more perfect form of the Don Juan strategy: not to pursue but to attract, not to move but to be the fixed point around which everyone orbits without ever reaching the center, to be so frail and changeable and evasive that no one can pin down what she wants, what she loves, what she would accept, whether she is present or absent, real or dreamed.

She has loved, by her own account, one man: her dead husband. But this love is organized entirely around his absence. He is dead. He cannot come back. The love that is organized around permanent absence is the perfect Don Juan love: it requires no commitment because the beloved cannot be present to demand it. She can love without limit because the limit has been set by death. She can be the pale Don Juan because her one true conquest is already a ghost, already a stone guest who cannot arrive for dinner, and she is therefore permanently safe from the reckoning that dragged the original Don Juan down.

The Stone Guest Who Never Came

In the Don Juan legend, the stone guest comes. The dead father, whose death was Don Juan's fault, returns as stone, comes to dinner, issues the invitation, arrives at the graveyard supper, takes the hand, drags him down. Don Juan is punished because the stone guest comes.

The opium lady's stone guest never comes. Her dead husband does not return as monument and consequence. He does not come to dinner. He is not a stone guest. He is a dream guest, a fog guest, an opium guest who arrives in the bedroom as a vision and does not grip any hand and drag anyone anywhere. The husband's love was dead, as Cousin Hannah told her: but she did not seem to know that death was necessary to her love. The death is what makes the love safe. The stone guest cannot drag you to hell if the stone guest never materializes. The opium lady has found the Don Juan strategy's one successful variant: love the dead man, who will never become a stone guest, who will never demand the reckoning, who will never grip the wrist. Mr. Spitzer at the deathbed is watching the pale Don Juan die without ever having been dragged to hell. This is not a triumph. It is not a failure either. It is what life looks like when it ends on its own account without anyone's permission or objection or desire, as Mr. Spitzer describes his own slow suicide. The stone guest did not come. The hell was not incurred. The woman who slipped into simulated unconsciousness where all might be permitted died in her bed, in her tower, with her imaginary visitors and her ghost husband and her faithful witness sitting in the wing chair beside her, and the stone hand never gripped her wrist, and she went down anyway, because everything goes down, with or without the stone guest, because the abyss is there whether Don Juan laughs at it or not.

The AND Philosophy Reading

The pale Don Juan identification is the AND Philosophy's plural self applied to the question of how desire organizes a life. The opium lady has been read throughout the novel as the passive figure: the one who lies in bed, the one who is loved and visited, the one who requires care and management. Mr. Spitzer's deathbed question proposes that this reading has the agency exactly backward. The frail, changeable, uncertain, evasive figure is not the passive recipient of the story. She is its engine. She has been running a Don Juan operation from her bed for decades: attracting rather than pursuing, being elusive rather than direct, permitting in dreams what consciousness refuses, organizing everyone else's life around the impossibility of reaching her.

The grief as permanent weather pillar: the love organized around permanent absence is not a failure of love. It is the most sustained and consistent love in the novel. She has loved her dead husband without interruption for the entire narrative. The love has not faded. It has not transferred. It has not compromised. It has simply required that its object be perpetually unavailable, which the dead are. This is the pale Don Juan's resolution of the Don Juan problem: the seducer who cannot commit finds the one beloved who cannot demand commitment, and loves them without limit, and slips into the unconsciousness where all is permitted, and the stone guest never comes, and the love continues, and she dies in it, still in it, the pale Don Juan whose hell never arrived, whose love was exactly as permanent and exactly as organized around absence as the grief of everyone who loved her.
