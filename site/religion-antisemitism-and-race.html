Antisemitism and Race

By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Definition from research

The novel treats prejudice as a symptom. Its bigots are the lost, the raving, and the frightened, and their hatred looks less like conviction than like a way of imposing order on lives they cannot control by blaming someone outside. Young sets the ugliness in unreliable mouths and lets it condemn the speaker.

Racism in the book is not only spoken but built into a role. The "white detective" will investigate only the deaths of white children, since "the deaths of the little black children were not his business." Coming upon a murdered Black girl, he simply drops the case. His prejudice is a professional habit, the machinery of justice quietly refusing half the dead.

Esther Longtree lives the cost of this. Her Black lover has to flee "for fear the white people would see him and kill him," a fear the book confirms with an attempted lynching. Esther hides their child, dreads what her parents will do if they learn the baby is "coffee-colored," and travels to the "Negro section" of a Southern town to give birth, hunted and unsafe. Even mixed heritage is treated as a family scandal to be buried.

Set against the bigots are the ones who resist. Miss MacIntosh teaches Vera about the "injustices done to the Africans working in diamond mines," disputes Mr. Bonebreaker's slurs to his face, and hopes aloud for the end of the British Empire. Yet the book is honest about the cost of resistance too: her "tactful, hopeful silence" during his worst rants, and the "regret" that follows, show how hard it is to speak even for someone who knows better. Cousin Hannah, remembered as an abolitionist and a crosser of every border, is another, stranger figure of resistance, though the terms in which the novel recalls her carry their own period prejudice.
