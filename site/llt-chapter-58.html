Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: The Sacred Is Here

The Questions

Mr. Spitzer’s elegy is “the silent music he was always composing in his mind… finished only when he was dead.” What is a music that has no sound and no end?

The old violinist draws “his imaginary bow across his imaginary strings.” Is soundless playing still music?

Mr. Spitzer “knew immediately what music he was playing, soundless though it was.” How is a thing communicated with no medium to carry it?

The unheard duet happens on the public Library steps in muggy weather. Why set the transcendent in so ordinary a place?

This is the chapter the book has been reaching toward, and it turns on a music with no sound. Mr. Spitzer carries always “the silent music he was always composing in his mind, his elegy which would be finished only when he was dead.” The elegy has two impossibilities folded into it, and both are the book’s own. It is silent, a music that does not need the ear, the non-aural music he wondered after earlier; and it is finishable “only when he was dead,” an unfinished work that stays open the whole length of a life, completing at the exact moment there is no longer anyone to hear it complete. The meaning lives beneath the medium, and the work stays open until it no longer matters that it is.

The old violinist makes the silent music visible without making it audible. He draws “his imaginary bow across his imaginary strings… played his imaginary notes upon the imaginary box,” with real gestures of rapture and no sound at all. The playing is complete in every motion and empty of every tone, and the chapter does not treat this as a madman miming. It treats it as music, real music performed soundlessly, all of the piece present in the gesture and the knowledge, none of it in the air. If meaning lives beneath its medium, then the medium can be dropped and the meaning remain, and the violinist’s soundless playing is that claim enacted on a public step.

The communion between the two men is the chapter’s wonder. Mr. Spitzer, watching, “knew immediately what music he was playing, soundless though it was,” and knew it to be a piece so obscure it had “nearly passed away from human consciousness,” one only he remembered. Nothing passed between them but gesture and shared knowledge, and yet the exact music passed, understood completely, an obscure elegy recognized without a note. This is communication beneath language, the understanding the book found in the languages of the dead, arriving here between two living strangers on a step: meaning transmitted with no medium at all, carried by attention and inward knowing across the gap that sound would ordinarily cross.

And the setting is the argument. This soundless communion, this reaching of the transcendent, happens not in a heaven of silent harmonies but on “the Library steps,” in weather “muggy, sultry and warm,” between a caped old lawyer and a buttonless derelict violinist. The book has been wary of the beyond, the promised elsewhere where the losses are undone, and here it brings the silent music down out of the beyond and sets it on the public steps in the heat. The sacred is not in a distant paradise. It is here, soundless and complete, in an unheard duet two ordinary men play and understand on a city stair. The Sacred Is Here and the yearned-for beyond meet in the one soundless piece, and the meeting place is the street.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes the silent music for mere madness or delusion, two men imagining a concert, and the reading that takes it for a purely otherworldly transcendence, the beyond breaking through. Neither the delusion nor the distant heaven is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that meaning lives beneath its medium, so a music can be silent and still be music, complete in gesture and inward knowledge with nothing in the air; that such meaning can pass between people with no medium to carry it, an obscure elegy understood soundlessly, communication beneath language arriving between the living; and that this reaching of the sacred happens on the ordinary Library steps in the heat, not in a beyond, so the transcendent is brought home to the public stair. The sacred is soundless, shared, and here. Mr. Spitzer and the derelict play and understand an unheard elegy on a city step, and the chapter asks for no concert hall and no heaven to make it real.

For Reflection

Can something be fully communicated with no medium at all to carry it, only attention and shared knowing?

Is there a meaning that lives beneath sound, sight, and word, and would survive their loss?

Why might the transcendent be truer set on a public step in the heat than in any imagined beyond?
