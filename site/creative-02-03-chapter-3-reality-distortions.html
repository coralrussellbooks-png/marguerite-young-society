Chapter 3: Reality Distortions

Part of The Creative Radical Collection, on Radical Immanence, the philosophy Coral Russell draws from the work of Marguerite Young (1908 to 1995).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 2: The Logic of the Zealot · The Creative Radical Collection · Chapter 4: The Closed Loop →

"The illusion was the only reality, and our self-deception was the only rose."

The most effective form of control doesn't put you in a cage. It makes you build one yourself, because it has convinced you that the walls are not there.

Before a system can dictate your behavior, it has to dismantle your confidence in your own perception. This is not a metaphor. It is a documented, repeatable process. It works on individuals, on families, on congregations of thousands, and on entire nations. The mechanism is the same at every scale: deny what the person saw, replace it with an authorized version, and repeat until the person's own memory becomes the enemy.

We call this gaslighting, a word that has been softened by overuse. The clinical reality is harder. When your perception of events is systematically contradicted by an authority you have been taught to trust, your brain does not simply decide who is right. It experiences the contradiction as a threat to survival. We are social animals. To be told that what we saw did not happen, by someone with institutional power over us, registers in the nervous system as isolation. Isolation, historically, means death. The brain will rewrite its own memories to avoid that outcome. It will choose the authorized version not because it is true but because agreeing is the only way to stay inside the room.

This is not stupidity. This is the mind doing exactly what it evolved to do. And it is exactly what abusers, cults, and institutions count on.

The Pennsylvania grand jury report released in August 2018 is one of the most thorough public records of institutional gaslighting in American history. Over two years, investigators examined more than half a million internal Catholic Church documents across six dioceses and interviewed dozens of witnesses. The report documented credible abuse allegations against more than 300 priests, with identifiable victims exceeding 1,000 over a period of 70 years. What the report made visible was not just the abuse. It was the architecture built to make victims doubt themselves.

Church officials consistently used euphemisms like "boundary issues" to describe assaults. The grand jury concluded that diocesan leadership had constructed what it called "a playbook for concealing the truth." Tactics included reassigning accused priests to new parishes without explanation, leaning on local law enforcement to drop investigations, and framing the abuse in language so mild that victims who came forward were left without words to describe what had been done to them. One victim, quoted in reporting on the grand jury, asked the question that the entire system was designed to produce: "Who would have believed me? A priest, in 1948?"

That question is the system working as intended. The victim is not asking whether it happened. The victim already knows it happened. The victim is asking whether his own testimony has the right to exist in the same room as institutional authority. The answer the Church spent seventy years delivering was: it does not.

Pennsylvania Attorney General Josh Shapiro named the pattern directly: abuse, deny, cover up. The cover-up was not incidental to the institution. It was, as the grand jury found, sophisticated, coordinated across dioceses, and extended to the Vatican. The files were kept in what the Church called its "secret archives," with the bishop holding the only key.

This is what institutional gaslighting looks like when the documents survive. Most of the time they don't. Most of the time the person sitting across from an authority figure with no paper trail has only their own memory, which the system has already spent months or years teaching them not to trust.

The political version requires no secret archive. It happens in full public view. Donald Trump's claim that the crowd at his 2017 inauguration was the largest in history, contradicted immediately by aerial photographs, by Metro ridership data, and by the testimony of anyone present, was not a mistake. It was a test. The question being posed was not factual. It was hierarchical: whose account of reality gets to stand? When administration officials appeared on television the following day to describe the photographs as showing "alternative facts," the gaslighting was complete. The goal was never to convince anyone that the crowd was large. The goal was to demonstrate that the authorized version did not require evidence to displace the one you saw with your own eyes.

Populations that learn to accept this trade, their perception for the leader's version, become manageable in ways that populations anchored in shared reality do not.

The action: Keep a record the system cannot edit. When something happens that contradicts the authorized version, write it down immediately: what you saw, what was said, the date, any witnesses. Not for a confrontation. Not as evidence in an argument you are planning to have. Write it down for yourself, so that when the authorized version comes for your memory, you have a document in your own handwriting that says: this is what I saw. Your perception is not a democracy. It does not require a majority vote to be valid.
