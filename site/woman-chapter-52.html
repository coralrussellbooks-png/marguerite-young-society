Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 52

← Chapter 51 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 53 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Veils and masks open this chapter, and the ambiguity they hold is its pillar. The old suffragette's identity and losses are shrouded, a "sea shell born without a mantle," a "brook without water," and the feminism of absence and yearning and the self that cannot be fixed arrive together, a woman defined by what is missing from her. Mr. Spitzer cannot say whether she was mother or father to him, source of "amputated loves," and his thought that "perhaps she should be the husband and not the bride" is the crossing point stated plainly, the categories of the marriage rite dissolving on the woman who would not enter it.

Mr. Spitzer is a "pawnbroker who had pawned himself," a "locksmith in a street of dreams where there was neither key nor lock," "dominoes of which there were no spots," "no name, no face," and the alienation of the marginalized and the self dissolving toward blankness are one figure, a man who has hocked himself into the vanished trades and old crafts the suffragette is merged with. The "mother-of-pearl paved court" and the "veiled dancing girls" carry the objectification and veiling of women, literal and figurative, into the same dream.

The suffragette's fluidity is the chapter's engine. She is "indifferent to the election," howling in a snowstorm, her "lost skirt" the lost femininity or autonomy the skirt both grants and confines, her multilingual cries of "Long live woman!" carrying feminism across every border. She dresses as a man, climbs "old mountains as if they were new" with practical gear, and may be "the bridegroom to her bride," the crossing point again destabilizing the marriage rite and the fixed self. Her adventures, challenging prime ministers, sleeping among Laplanders, flying over the Alps, make her an iconoclast and a "lost star," a "black knight," and the Swiss fear of her "disrupting mountains and skies," the "blackamoor" nickname, carry feminism's disruptive force and its intersection with race and the exotic into the myth.

Trunks and locked boxes hold the chapter's last figures, "bridal gowns and veils" behind keys, feminist archives hidden and inaccessible, a "multiplicity of bridal gowns without slippers" marking a marriage refused or unrealized. She is a "type of the uncreated creation," a woman who "achieves without love and loves without realization," and the paradox is the illusion of the individual at the edge of being, a self that is and is not, present and absent, bride and bridegroom. Whether she was "male or female" the chapter will not say, and it closes with her reading an old romance of unattainable lovers and pennies laid on her eyelids, the crossing point and the mystery held to the last, a life the record could not sort and the book will not pretend to.
