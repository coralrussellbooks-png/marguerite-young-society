Harp Song for a Radical

Unit 5, pp. 81-104: Weitling in America and the communes

← Unit 4 · Harp Song for a Radical: A History Companion · Unit 6 →

Background for this unit: The History in Harp Song for a Radical

Weitling tours the German towns of the Great Lakes and the Mississippi, then pins his hopes on Communia, a small colony in Iowa. Along the way Young surveys the rival communities that dotted the Midwest around 1850: Oneida, Nauvoo, Icaria, Zoar, and Ebenezer. It is a map of American utopia at its peak.

Major entries

Communia, Iowa (pp. 81, 85-90, 102)

  • What it was: A communal colony founded in 1847 near Elkader in Clayton County, Iowa, by the Volga River, a tributary of the Turkey River. Its founder, Heinrich Koch, was a German radical who had come to America after the revolution of 1830 and edited a freethinking paper in St. Louis.

  • What happened: Koch left the presidency accused of taking colony money for his family (p. 86). Weitling's workers' league in New York invested in the colony around 1851, and Weitling arrived, as he reported in his paper, "on Good Friday" (p. 88). Quarrels over money and control followed, the league's funds were lost, and lawsuits dragged on until the colony dissolved in the mid-1850s.

  • Why it matters: Communia was the one place where Weitling's ideas met real soil. Its failure ended his public career. It is typical of dozens of small immigrant communes that vanished with almost no record.

  • Further reading: Wittke, The Utopian Communist (1950), chapters 13 and 14.

Die Republik der Arbeiter* and the Arbeiterbund (pp. 82-88)

  • What it was: Weitling's monthly, later weekly, newspaper, The Workers' Republic, published in New York from 1850 to 1855. It served the Arbeiterbund, a league of German workers' associations in cities such as Cleveland, Sandusky, Toledo, Detroit, Chicago, and Milwaukee.

  • Why it matters: The league promoted a "labor exchange bank" and cooperative workshops, an early attempt at a workers' economy inside capitalism. Young notes a cooperative tailor shop in Cleveland where the women did the sewing "when the men had failed" (p. 82).

  • Further reading: Bruce Levine, The Spirit of 1848 (1992).

Nauvoo, Illinois (pp. 93-96, 99, 102-103)

  • What it was: The Mormon city on the Mississippi. The site began as a village called Venus, then Commerce (p. 95). Joseph Smith bought land there after the Mormons were driven from Missouri in 1838-1839 and renamed it Nauvoo, from a Hebrew word for "beautiful."

  • Why it matters: By 1844 Nauvoo had perhaps twelve thousand people, rivaling Chicago. After Smith's murder that June, Brigham Young led the Saints west in 1846, and the Icarians bought part of the emptied town in 1849. One site thus held two utopias in succession, the religious and the secular.

  • Further reading: Glen M. Leonard, Nauvoo: A Place of Peace, a People of Promise (2002).

Étienne Cabet and the Icarians (pp. 96-102)

  • Who he was: A French lawyer and deputy (1788-1856) exiled to England in 1834 for his radical journalism. There he absorbed Owen's ideas. His novel first appeared in 1840 as the "travels and adventures of Lord William Carisdall in Icaria," presented as a translation from an English author, Francis Adams (p. 99). It described a communist republic run by elected committees.

  • What happened: On Owen's advice Cabet bought Texas land sight unseen (p. 97). The first party of sixty-nine men sailed from Le Havre in February 1848, landed at New Orleans, and found scattered parcels, fever, and failure. Cabet brought the survivors to Nauvoo in March 1849. The community split in 1856, Cabet was expelled, and he died in St. Louis that November. Successor colonies lasted in Iowa until 1898.

  • Why it matters: Icaria was the longest-lived secular commune in American history. Its members were mostly French artisans, the counterparts of Weitling's German tailors.

  • Further reading: Robert P. Sutton, Les Icariens: The Utopian Dream in Europe and America (1994).

Zoar, Ohio, and Ebenezer (pp. 103-104)

  • What they were: Zoar was founded in 1817 by German Separatists from Württemberg under Joseph Bimeler, in the Tuscarawas valley of Ohio. They went communal in 1819 to pay for their land and earned much of their capital by building a section of the Ohio and Erie Canal. They practiced celibacy for part of the 1820s and dissolved in 1898. Ebenezer was the settlement near Buffalo, New York, of the Community of True Inspiration, which moved to Iowa in 1855 and became the Amana Colonies.

  • Why it matters: These pietist communes were the most durable of all. Weitling admired that their women worked in the fields alongside the men (p. 103), which he saw as the key to their harvests.

  • Further reading: Kathleen M. Fernandez, Zoar: The Story of an Intentional Community (2019).

Coxey's Army (p. 104)

  • What it was: In 1894, during the worst depression the country had yet known, Jacob S. Coxey, a businessman from Massillon, Ohio, led a march of unemployed men to Washington. With his partner Carl Browne he called it the "Commonweal of Christ" (Young's "Army of Christ"). They demanded federal road building to create jobs, paid for with paper money.

  • What happened: About five hundred marchers reached the Capitol on May 1, 1894. Coxey was arrested for walking on the grass.

  • Why it matters: It was the first protest march on Washington and the seed of public works programs of the 1930s. It unfolded the same spring as the Pullman strike.

  • Further reading: Carlos A. Schwantes, Coxey's Army: An American Odyssey (1985).

John Peter Altgeld (p. 83)

  • Who he was: Born in Germany in 1847 and raised on an Ohio farm, he became a Chicago judge and governor of Illinois (1893-1897).

  • Why it matters: In 1893 he pardoned the three surviving Haymarket defendants, declaring the trial unjust, which ended his political career. In 1894 he protested President Cleveland's sending of federal troops against the Pullman strikers. Vachel Lindsay's poem "The Eagle That Is Forgotten" mourns him.

  • How Young uses him: She pictures him as a sorrowful German boy and calls his path "a transcendental and yet secular socialism" (p. 83), a bridge from the 1848 exiles to Debs.

  • Further reading: Harry Barnard, Eagle Forgotten: The Life of John Peter Altgeld (1938).

Shorter entries

  • John Humphrey Noyes and the Oneida Community (pp. 80-81): Noyes's Perfectionists practiced "complex marriage," in which all adults were considered married to one another, and group criticism. The community prospered making traps and silverware and gave up communal life in 1881.

  • The Millerites (p. 81): Followers of William Miller, who predicted Christ's return in 1843-1844. The failure of October 22, 1844, is called the Great Disappointment. The Seventh-day Adventists grew from it.

  • Cyrus Hall McCormick (p. 83): Inventor of the mechanical reaper, who moved his factory to Chicago in 1847. His works later saw the 1886 strike that led to Haymarket.

  • John Hay (p. 82): Lincoln's private secretary, later a Cleveland resident by marriage (Unit 18).

  • George Miller (p. 94): A Mormon bishop who refused to follow Brigham Young after Smith's death.

  • Massillon, Ohio (p. 104): Named, as Young says, by the wife of founder James Duncan after the French bishop Jean-Baptiste Massillon. He opened his funeral oration for Louis XIV with "Dieu seul est grand," God alone is great.

Allusions

  • Midas (p. 91): The king whose touch turned everything to gold.

  • Noah's Ark (p. 93): Joseph Smith taught that the ark had sailed from America.

  • Herod (p. 97): The biblical tyrant, used for Cabet's persecutors.

  • Lot and Zoar (p. 103): In Genesis, Zoar is the small city where Lot took refuge from the destruction of Sodom. The Ohio commune took its name from it.

  • Stygian (p. 102): Of the river Styx, dark and gloomy.

  • Father of Waters (p. 86): The Mississippi River.
