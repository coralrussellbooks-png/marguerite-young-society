Pillar: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here · Across every book

Characters: Miss MacIntosh

The tenth pillar is the most delicate of all the pillars and the one that required the most time to name accurately.

"Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" is suffused with a quality that can only be called sacred: a sense that the ordinary world, all of it is charged with a significance that exceeds ordinary description. But the significance does not point beyond the world. It points deeper into it. Young offers a way of experiencing the world as sacred that requires no transcendence, no beyond, no god outside the becoming. The sacred is immanent or it is nothing. This is the tenth pillar, and it is, in some ways, the one that holds all the others together.

Immanence is the philosophical term for what is present within rather than transcendent beyond. It is the opposite of the move that most philosophical and religious systems make when they encounter the difficulty of existence: the move upward, outward, beyond, toward the foundation that does not shift, the ground that holds still, the truth that exists outside the flux and can therefore anchor it. Young refuses that move at every level of her system. There is no transcendent ground in "Miss MacIntosh, My Darling". Everything that exists, exists here, in the immanent world, in the middle of the becoming, in the room with the contradiction.

Radical is the modifier that specifies how far the immanence goes. A moderate immanence might acknowledge that most of what we know is available within experience while leaving open the possibility of some transcendent anchor. Young's immanence is not moderate. It goes all the way down. The immanence is the whole of it. What is here is what there is, and what is here is plural, constructed, grief-saturated, and alive in ways that the search for transcendence has always prevented us from fully seeing.

The novel states this on its first page: “there is perhaps no God but what we dreamed.” Miss MacIntosh says it more bluntly: “God loves the wind, the empty places. There is no God.” Steven Shaviro, reading the novel, finds a vision “in no way transcendent or otherworldly,” a celebration of something “available to us, at every moment, in ordinary life.”

This is not nihilism. The refusal of transcendence is not the refusal of meaning. It is the insistence that meaning is made here, in the immanent world, by plural selves building provisional structures with the full knowledge that the structures are provisional. That is a harder and more honest account of meaning than any transcendent guarantee could provide. Young understood this.

Robin Wall Kimmerer's work on reciprocity and the intelligence of the living world enters the conversation around the tenth pillar. Kimmerer's insistence that the world is not a collection of objects but a community of subjects, that attention to the non-human is not sentiment but epistemology, rhymes with Young's own practice of giving the Indiana landscape the same density and interiority she gives her human characters. Every participant in the novel's world, carries the same weight of accumulated time and grief as any human character.

The tenth pillar is where the system becomes, if the word is not too large, spiritual, but spiritual in a way that requires no god outside the becoming, no transcendent ground beneath the flux. The sacred is the ordinary world attended to with the full quality of attention the witness brings.

That is the tenth pillar. That is radical immanence. That is what the title names.

No pillar travels farther in Young’s work than this one. It follows from the nature-worship of “Worship” (1927), which still addresses God and finds him “In stars and flowers and shining eyes,” through the graduate essay in which God “would hardly be missed,” to “Noah’s Ark” (1943), where God is “creation’s flaw,” to the novel, and finally to the sacred as labor in "Harp Song for a Radical". Young used a vocabulary that never left her: the angel she insisted “is real,” her pull toward the Catholic Church, and resurrection, the word she never gave up.

Phase One, Childhood and the Early Poems (1908 to 1934)

Pillar 10: The Sacred Is Here

"Worship" (1927) is the starting point: "God, I don't pray to you in church," and "I see you only / In stars and flowers and shining eyes, / In dewdrops and in pearls." In "Twilight" the garden's quiet could "Be peopled by angels even." "Door-Way" puts death, the threshold of the beyond, on an ordinary street.

Complication: this God is still addressed as "Thine." It is theist immanence, God found in nature, which Young's later work would take apart.

Phase Two, The Chicago Years, Prismatic Ground, and the Graduate Essays (1935 to 1937)

Pillar 10: The Sacred Is Here

This is the pillar that moves most in Phase Two. The Patmore essay says that "God as God, if subtracted from that cosmos, would hardly be missed." The Night Thoughts essay ends by equating the mystic's eternity with the modern unconscious. The poems bring heaven down. "Wild Orchard" may be Young's clearest early image of immanence: "For man is rooted at the last, / He is rooted and held fast, / Confounded by the strangest sainthood / Blowing in wind, wild orchard." Holiness is rootedness in earth. In "Sky of Grasses," "Heaven has shrunk to the dim size / Comprehended by insects' eyes." In "Fall of Man" the angels fall "Among brier roses" and "The blue deep was a field of clover." In "Strange Prophecy" the skull becomes "a hive for honey bees." "Lenten Feast" warns that anyone who will not feast on the world's small beauties becomes bones that "Rattle in void eternity."

Complication: "Rosary in Discord" still prays to the Trinity, though the prayer opens out to "the multiform, / The crowded sea / And populous storm." "Lover" ends "dust will never / Be all of him." "The Visit" closes on "The balm in Gilead for me." The theist God of "Worship" is fading but not gone.

Phase Three, The Philosophical Poems (1938 to 1945)

Pillar 10: The Sacred Is Here

Here Young goes furthest. "Noah's Ark" (1943): "God is the impotent old man, and withered," and "He is creation's flaw / And celibate, nor passenger of this uniting ark." "The Contravalid": "Of heaven, we do not need to go there to be there this night." "Elegy": "our God is not the God of Weight / But ourselves." Drafts: "we are God and little pepper moths," and "God could not compass His own death." "The White Rat" puts the pillar's question in Augustine's mouth inside a laboratory cage: "O, my God, art Thou without or within?" "The Trains" (1942): "there is an angel mental as man." "Marriage in Heaven" weds the soul to an earth that "has no home."

Complication: the pull toward transcendence is strongest here too. "Night Watches" (1942): "exiled is man... Whose Orient is origin not earth." A draft imagines life arriving as "the super-sensible cell, / All futurity from another planet fallen." Berkeley hopes God may "be real / And not postulated merely as our theory."

Phase Four, "Angel in the Forest" and “Complex Life and Complex Letters” (1945 to 1946)

Pillar 10: The Sacred Is Here

The title angel is reduced: "There was no angel, nor even a way to deduce one from such a primitive landscape." Instead "there was a wild turkey, almost as handsome." The storekeeper puts on Rapp's spectacles and "By God, he almost saw an angel." Thomas Say "sought nothing supernatural but the Spirit of Nature." Owen locates worship in "inexpressible feelings of wonder."

Complication: Robert Dale, the rationalist's son, ends haunted by spirits, with "no harmony at all in the universe, no center but whirlwinds." Of the two visions merging, the narrator says, "Perhaps not." The supernatural returns in the children of unbelief.

Phase Five, "Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" (written from the mid 1940s, published 1965)

Pillar 10: The Sacred Is Here

The first page: "there is perhaps no God but what we dreamed, and there is no train." Miss MacIntosh: "God loves the wind, the empty places. There is no God." As Shaviro puts it, what the novel celebrates is available "at every moment, in ordinary life."

Complication: the last page is full of Gabriel's horn, the golden cock, and the old white mule, though the horn will be "a silent horn for us." The vocabulary remains, and its meaning moves to earth.

What the novel adds is the earthly turn stated in a character’s voice rather than argued in verse. The poems of the war years reached God as “creation’s flaw” by reasoning. The novel opens by assuming it. The pillar is still partial here, since the vocabulary of the beyond remains on the last page.

Phase Six, The Occasional Prose, Interviews, and Self-Interpretation (1940s to 1995)

Pillar 10: The Sacred Is Here

On Broch's dying Virgil: "We are the only God we know of." The essays find the marvelous in the ordinary: the Midwest's dead whale in a boxcar and sow at the wheel of a Ford, Marianne Moore's strawberry "that's had quite a struggle," Raggedy Ann with her nose forgotten. "Angels are the dreams of men." "As long as you're still dreaming, you're alive."

Complication: she was "very, very drawn to the Catholic Church," read the Bible daily, studied the mystics, defended "angels and unearthly creatures, spirits without bodies," told Neville "the angel is real," named "transmutation and transmigration, metempsychosis" and "resurrection" as her meaning, saw Poe's "astral body," and was visited nightly by Henry James. "The Artist as Wanderer" calls art "an act of faith."

Phase Seven, "Harp Song for a Radical" (written in her last decades, published 1999)

Pillar 10: The Sacred Is Here

The sacred moves into labor: the "divine spark" in the dead souls of America, "the proletaire of Galilee," railroad ties as crosses for "any railroad Christ." Weitling is "an agnostic who had remained under the spell of the icons of Catholicism," which also describes Young. Resurrection, the word she never gave up, now means organizing the working poor.
