Angel in the Forest

Chapter 8. Grief That Does Not Resolve

← Chapter 7 · Angel in the Forest: A Reading in Fourteen Chapters · Chapter 9 →

Marguerite Young / Angel in the Forest, Chapter 8

There are two ways to write about a thing that failed, and they feel completely different. One is tragedy. The other is elegy. Angel in the Forest is an elegy, and understanding the difference is the key to the emotional life of the book.

Tragedy resolves. It has a shape that completes itself: a rise, a fall, a moment of recognition, and then the strange peace that comes when the arc closes. Tragedy takes suffering and converts it into form, and the form is a consolation. You watch the hero go down, and something in you is purged and released, and you walk out having been through something and come out the other side. The doom means something. The ending settles.

Elegy does not resolve. It is mourning without the catharsis. The loss is not turned into meaning and handed back to you as a lesson. It is simply carried. An elegy does not end so much as it trails off, the way grief itself does not end but only thins and returns. There is no purge, no release, no redemption of the loss. You just go on missing the thing.

Young chose elegy, and she chose it for a hard reason. Tragedy needs the doom to be significant, needs the fall to matter cosmically, needs a flaw grand enough to ennoble the ruin. Young denies all of that. As this study has traced, the utopias were not brought down by a tragic flaw that dignifies them. They were doomed from the start by a plain structural error, a fixed account of human nature that could not hold the actual people. There is no catharsis available because there is nothing to purge; the failure was in the premise. And yet, here is the difficulty of the book, she mourns them anyway. She grieves, fully and without irony, something she also understands completely to have been impossible from the first day. That is the emotional structure of Angel in the Forest: grief that lives alongside total understanding of why the thing had to fail, and is not cancelled by that understanding.

The happiness she refuses to correct

The clearest instance is a small one, near the end. Young reports that years after the collapse, a few old citizens still confessed that the Owen period, for all its chaos and uncertainty, had been the happiest time of their lives. They were younger then, she writes, and filled to the brim with hopefulness. There had been, in her phrase, a morning hour upon all sublunary things.

Watch what she does not do. She does not correct this memory. She could. A different writer would step in and explain that the happiness was a delusion, that the community was already unraveling underneath them, that they had been deceived by a charismatic man and a doomed idea. Young lets the remembered happiness stand, whole and uncorrected, right next to the complete record of the failure. Both are true. The happiness was real. The failure was real. And crucially, the happiness is not made false by the failure. This is the elegiac move in its purest form. The sweetness of the lost thing is not diminished by the knowledge that it was doomed. If anything the doom is exactly what makes it elegy and not mere nostalgia. They were happiest during the one thing that could not last, and knowing it could not last does not make them fools for having been happy. It makes them human, and it makes the loss permanent.

A landscape that is grief itself

Young's 1940 town is the same grief written into a place. The ten-cent store cobwebbed and insubstantial. The abandoned Catholic church where swallows have nested in the belfry and flutter back and forth like bells. The maze of Hebrew and Greek letters that was put up hastily to symbolize the transience of man, itself already crumbling. The outhouses leaning with time. The water tower under its ivy.

It would be easy to read this as picturesque decay, the pleasant melancholy of an old ruin, and Young shuts that reading down. This is not scenery. It is grief made visible in a landscape. The town does not charm; it mourns. The decay is the physical form the loss takes, the way a grieving person carries the dead in their body, and Young names the reason directly: nothing lingers so like the memory of failure. She adds the turn that makes New Harmony heavier than any ordinary ruin, which is that this failure had reached for the extreme perfection. The ruins of an ordinary town are just ruins. The ruins of a town that tried to build heaven are grief, because the height of what was wanted is the exact measure of what was lost. New Harmony's grayness weighs more than another town's because the dream that failed there was so much larger.

The one dynasty that does not fail

In a book about grief, the single permanent institution is the undertaker. Young notes that his was the only unbroken dynasty in New Harmony, and she is not making a dark joke. She is stating what survives failure. Every utopia that came through this place promised, one way or another, to defeat death, and every one of them was buried, and the man who buried them stayed in business the whole time. Burial is the one human activity that never becomes obsolete, never runs short of work, never fails. The undertaker is the elegiac condition turned into a profession: the one whose living depends on the permanence of loss.

And Young draws him with real tenderness. He is, she says, the kindest man on earth, holding mortgages he will not foreclose, always on his way from the grave to the cradle. That tenderness is not incidental. The keeper of grief is not a ghoul. He is a gentle man, because grief handled honestly is an act of care, and the person who gives the dead dreams a decent burial is doing something closer to love than to profit.

A frame that will not close

The deepest expression of all this is the shape of the book. It opens with a chapter called "New Harmony Today," a glimpse of the ruined town in the summer of 1940. It closes, four hundred pages later, with "Farewell to New Harmony," set on a summer day in the same ruined town in the same year. The book ends where it began. Nothing has been resolved.

A tragic structure would close that frame. We would return to the opening scene transformed, carrying the understanding we had earned, the arc complete, the catharsis delivered. Young returns us to exactly the same place with exactly the same question still hanging in the air, the one she asked on the first page and never answered: what dream among dreams is reality? The frame is left open on purpose. This is formal grief, mourning built into the architecture. You do not come out the other side of this book. You come back to where you started, still grieving, except that now the whole weight of the history is beneath you, pressing up. The elegy's trailing-off has been turned into a structure. The book refuses to end because grief does not end, and Young will not lie about that even in the shape of her chapters.

The grief is not for a fixable thing

Here is the point that holds the book together, the grief in Angel in the Forest is not grief for the loss of a correct utopia. It is not "if only they had done it right, it would have worked." That would be a fixable grief, a grief with a cure, and a grief with a cure is not really this book's grief at all.

Young is mourning something that has no cure. She is grieving the permanent human condition of wanting something that cannot be made permanent. The Rappites and the Owenites are not wept over because their particular blueprints failed. They are wept over because they are the sharpest possible instance of the universal thing: the longing to build something that will not change and will not die, held by creatures who live in a world where nothing holds still and everything they love keeps changing. That longing is not an error to be corrected. It is simply what people are. And it will always be defeated, every single time, because the impermanence it runs up against is the one truly permanent fact. So the grief cannot resolve, because the condition it mourns cannot resolve. You do not get over grieving the impermanence of what you love. The impermanence is forever. New Harmony is only the place where this ordinary, universal, endless grief was made unusually visible, because the people there wanted permanence so nakedly, and lost it so completely, that you can see in their small failed town the shape of everyone's.

That is why the book had to be an elegy and not a tragedy. Tragedy would have let us off. It would have converted the failure into meaning and sent us home purged and released. Young will not let us off, because to resolve the grief would be to lie about the condition that produces it. So the frame stays open, and the town stays gray, and the question stays unanswered, and the grief stays exactly where it is. And there is something in that refusal that is not bleak at all. To carry a loss honestly, without converting it into a consolation you have not earned, is itself a form of faithfulness to the thing you lost. The elegy that does not resolve is the truest love the dead can be given.
