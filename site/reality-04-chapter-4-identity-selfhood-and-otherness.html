Reality and Illusion in MMMD

Chapter 4: Identity, Selfhood, and Otherness

← Chapter 3: Reality, Illusion, and the Nature of Perception · Exploring Reality and Illusion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling · Chapter 5: Mortality, Death, and the Afterlife →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Lisa Lunde · Miss MacIntosh · Mrs. Hogden · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

The sentence this book keeps returning to, “The individual is the one illusion,” is usually quoted as a verdict. In the novel it arrives as something more fragile. Vera does not announce it from a height. She reaches it at the end of a long, lonely question: “Who knew even his own divided heart? Who knew all hearts as his own?” She asks whether, “among beings strange to each other,” who “have turned stonily away,” there might be, “in the vision of some omniscient eye, a web of spidery logic establishing the most secret relationships, deep calling to deep.” Only then do the four short sentences come: “Every heart is the other heart. Every soul is the other soul. Every face is the other face. The individual is the one illusion.” And the very next sentence takes it back: “I had walked alone, searching.”

The famous line, then, is born out of isolation. It is the hope of someone who has not been answered, and elsewhere Vera admits that there has been “no soul which answered mine like deep calling to deep.” This matters for how the chapter reads the novel’s handling of identity. The line carries two claims that are easy to confuse. The first is that separateness is an illusion, that beneath the boundaries all souls are one. The second is that the self is an illusion, that what looks like one person is a crowd. Young pursues both, but the second is the one her characters actually live. They do not melt into a shared ocean of being. They find themselves plural, split, substituted, and assembled from other people, and they must go on living that way.

The framework this book follows has a name for that second claim, Not One Self: a person is a crowd, not a single fixed identity, and the many selves are not a malfunction to correct but the ordinary condition of being alive. The novel tests this claim on nearly every character it has. Some are destroyed by their plurality, some are freed by it, and one is made entirely of it.

The Pronoun That Would Not Hold

Catherine takes plurality further than anyone. Chapter 2 describes her world and Chapter 3 her reasons for it. What concerns this chapter is what the coma does to the word “I.”

“The personal pronoun I did not always mean, of course, the same being or thing to my mother.” Eating an apple, she would say “I am eating myself.” She would say “I am a cloud eating the moon,” and warn visitors not to sit in a certain golden chair, “for she was the chair.” She was “the divan, the mirror, the lamp, the rug.” She was “everybody and the rose petal, too, the cause of this dissolution which was this creation.” In another passage “she was many selves, many beautiful, volatile Psyches wandering about and she was lying in bed, propped up among satin pillows,” both at once, the plural soul and the single inert body.

This is Not One Self with no floor under it. Catherine does not merely contain many selves. She has become the room itself, so that there is no one left to meet. Her daughter can speak to the body in the bed, but the “I” that might answer has scattered into the furniture. The novel lets this be beautiful, since the dissolution is also “this creation,” and it lets it be terrible, since a self that is everything cannot be anyone’s mother. Catherine shows that plurality has an outer limit. Past that limit, the crowd becomes a fog.

The Third Body

Mr. Spitzer lives at the opposite edge. Chapter 2 sets out his situation and Chapter 7 gives it a full meditation: the dead twin Peron, the two iron beds, the case of Spitzer versus Spitzer. What he adds to this chapter is his fear.

His brother’s life runs through his own as “the plural melody which was not Mr. Spitzer’s composition or choice, the melody not single or alone.” He did not choose to be two. Yet two is not what frightens him most. “That which Mr. Spitzer feared with his most overwhelming fear was probably a third body when he should be neither the one Mr. Spitzer nor the other Mr. Spitzer when he should be nothing familiar to him.” Being doubled he can bear, because each half still has a name. What he dreads is becoming something with no name at all, “so inconclusive and indefinite that, like a cloud continually changing,” it could not be defined “for it would change before the definition had changed.”

Then comes one of the most exact sentences in the book: “When the definition was correct, the thing defined should be incorrect. When the thing defined was correct, the definition should be incorrect.” A self cannot be named and caught at the same moment. By the time the word arrives, the thing has moved.

Late in the novel, Mr. Spitzer draws up his will as two men at once. One is the musician, “signing with his clef and writing with his left hand.” The other is the attorney “writing with his right hand which wore his brother’s milky blue stone ring.” It is the most legal of documents, the one meant to fix a person’s wishes forever, and he can only produce it by splitting his hands between two selves, one of them wearing the dead man’s jewelry.

Naming as a Late Arrival: Derrida and Deleuze

Jacques Derrida built much of his philosophy on the gap Mr. Spitzer describes. His coined word, différance, joins two senses of the French verb différer: to differ and to defer. Meaning, for Derrida, is never fully present in a word. A word means what it means only by differing from other words, and its full sense is always postponed, carried forward along a chain that never closes. Identity works the same way. No self is ever wholly present to itself, because it is made up of differences from others and of traces of what it is not. Mr. Spitzer is that idea given a body. He is who he is only by differing from Peron, so the dead brother is built into his identity. Every attempt to name himself arrives, in his own words, after the thing named has changed.

Gilles Deleuze, a near contemporary of Derrida, fits the novel at a different point. In Difference and Repetition he argued that identity is not the ground of things but an effect, something produced by difference and never prior to it, and that repetition never truly repeats the same thing. Each return is a variation. Young’s world runs on this kind of repetition. The canary that Mr. Spitzer keeps replacing is always “the” canary and never the same bird. Mrs. Hogden takes Miss MacIntosh’s place, as the next section shows, and does not become her. Vera’s “chaos begetting the chaos” is repetition that produces difference rather than sameness. Both philosophers lead to the same conclusion the framework reaches under the heading Language Shapes What's Possible: nouns freeze what is actually moving. The name “Mr. Spitzer” is a noun laid over a man who is really a verb.

Hannah in Boston, Hamid in the East

Cousin Hannah, the Great Suffragette, is the novel’s most spirited picture of a plural self, and a useful counterweight to Mr. Spitzer’s dread. She is dead before the novel begins, alive only in Catherine’s memory and in Mr. Spitzer’s recollection of her deathbed. Catherine calls her “a soul of vast duplicity,” and means it half as praise. At a card table in the other world, Catherine muses, Hannah “could be two partners being both herself and a gentleman.” Her “entire history, it seemed now, had been a history of skirts.”

Mr. Spitzer knew the fuller story. “Though she was Hannah in Boston, this virgin warrior” who “here in Boston she had fought against man, she was Hamid in the East.” In fact, “she was Al Hamid,” and she was known in the mountains as “the Great Moor.” She crossed deserts where no woman was supposed to go and lived there under a man’s name. In Boston she campaigned against men. In the East she was taken for one of them.

The novel does not treat this as a disguise to be exposed. It treats it as a life. Mr. Spitzer concludes that “no man had ever really known her,” and that fame had blinded people to “her essential beauty, that of deprivation, of absence of being.” At her bedside he is “reminded of many self-contradictions, many opposites, many hidden facets of Cousin Hannah’s life, some he might never have known.” For once, a divided self is not a wound. Hannah chose her selves, moved between them, and kept her secret to the end.

She also illustrates another principle in the framework, The Margins Reveal the Truth: the people who do not fit the categories see the categories most clearly. A woman who could be Hannah or Hamid depending on the continent knew better than anyone that “woman” and “man” were roles assigned by place and custom rather than facts written in the ground. She fought the conventions in Boston because she had seen, in the East, how easily they could be put on and taken off.

A Creature Compounded of Illusions

If Cousin Hannah is a plural self that chose its plurality, Mrs. Hogden is a self made almost entirely by other people. She arrives at Catherine’s house after Miss MacIntosh has vanished and Vera has run away, so Vera never meets her. She is “a Mrs. Hogden who, though I never saw her, implored my attention.” Everything Vera knows of her comes secondhand, through Lisa Lunde, the young companion who took Vera’s place in the house.

What arrives at the door is less a woman than a pile. She wears “at least four coats of seemingly graduated sizes, the one fitting snugly over the other,” along with a smashed hat, beads, cardboard boxes tied with string, Javanese temple bells, and “an empty bird cage, held in the crook of her little finger.” Her name will not hold still: she is “Mrs. Hogden or Mrs. Logden.” She introduces herself as “a teacher of arithmetic.” Lisa Lunde decides she is a shoplifter. Vera sums her up in one line: “Oh, she was a creature compounded of illusions.”

Mrs. Hogden is also a substitute. In Vera’s dreams “she would seem to be another than she was, a substitute for the true subject of my dreams.” She slides into Miss MacIntosh’s place “like the moon appearing where there had been the sun in the sky, Mrs. Hogden being the lesser radiance.” And the substitutions do not stop with her. On arrival she takes Lisa Lunde “to be the daughter of the house,” and in a sense she is right, since Lisa has taken Vera’s place. Lisa’s own mother, “poor and mad,” dreams “that she was rich, even as my mother dreamed that she was poor.” The household has become a set of positions (the nurse, the daughter, the canary in its cage) filled and refilled by whoever arrives next.

That is the most unsettling version of Not One Self the novel offers. Identity here is less something a person carries than a place a person occupies. Mrs. Hogden’s empty birdcage is exact. She comes equipped with the container before there is anything to put in it.

An Honest Woman or an Honest Man

Miss MacIntosh appears to be the one single, solid self in the book, and the earlier chapters have shown how that solidity was made. Chapter 2 tells the story of the wig and the baldness, Chapter 3 follows her late confession about illusion, and Chapter 7 reads the unmasking as the novel’s great doubling. Only one point needs adding here.

Vera’s first words for the bare head are “She was other than herself.” The phrase is precise. The shock is not that Miss MacIntosh lied, though Vera does call her “the bald pretender.” It is that the one person Vera believed to be exactly one person turned out to be at least two: the helmeted nursemaid of the daytime and the frail, hairless woman “drifting like some pale plume upon the darkness.” Miss MacIntosh’s own later reckoning goes further: “I should have been an honest woman or an honest man.” Even the simplest division of all, the one her corset and hair were meant to hold in place, does not hold for her. Her unity was never given. She kept it up with laces and a wig, every morning, for decades.

Reaching for the Tangible

Vera stands among all these selves and cannot find her own edges. “All my life I had been reaching for the tangible, and it had evaded me, much like the myth of Tantalus, much as if the tangible itself were an illusion.” Her life, she says, has been “made up of just these disrelated, delusive images hovering only for a moment at the margin of consciousness.”

What she does not say outright, but the novel shows, is that her self has been assembled from the people around her. She dreams because her mother dreamed. She mends and measures because Miss MacIntosh taught her to. Jean-Paul Sartre described the self as partly a “being-for-others,” made in part by how others see and name it. Vera is that kind of self, made from her mother’s dreaming and her nursemaid’s rules, and when one of those people disappears, part of Vera disappears with her. Chapter 6 takes up the ethical side of Sartre’s thought. Here the point is simpler. Vera’s search for Miss MacIntosh is also a search for the missing piece of herself, the part that was built in the nursemaid’s image and left without a model when the model walked into the sea.

Crowd and Ocean

The novel’s two readings of “the individual is the one illusion” come together here. Vera hopes for the ocean, “all barriers dissolving, all souls as one and united.” What she finds is the crowd: a mother scattered into furniture, a lawyer signing with two hands, a suffragette with two names, a nurse in four coats, a nursemaid who was always other than herself. The ocean never arrives. The crowd is everywhere.

Yet the novel does not treat the crowd as a failure to reach the ocean. It suggests that the crowd is the only way the ocean is ever glimpsed. Deep calls to deep only between people who have admitted how deep and divided they are. Vera cannot love Miss MacIntosh until the nursemaid stops being one solid thing and becomes a bald, frightened, contradictory woman. The recognition she had been waiting for, the “other heart” known “as their own,” happens at the moment she sees how many hearts Miss MacIntosh contained.

The characters who suffer most are those who demand to be one: Mr. Spitzer, terrified of the third body, and Miss MacIntosh, laced tight into a single self. The character who dissolves completely, Catherine, is lost in the opposite direction. Between them lies the narrow path the novel keeps pointing toward, and the one Not One Self describes: to be many without pretending to be one, and without scattering into everything. Cousin Hannah walked it for a lifetime. Vera spends the novel learning to.

Reflection Questions

  1. Vera’s line “The individual is the one illusion” comes out of loneliness, and she follows it with “I had walked alone.” Does knowing that change what the line means?

  2. Mr. Spitzer fears “a third body” more than being two. Why might it be easier to live as a divided self than as an undefined one?

  3. Cousin Hannah was “Hannah in Boston” and “Hamid in the East.” Which roles in ordinary life shift with place and company, and which, if any, stay fixed?

  4. Mrs. Hogden is known to Vera only through what others report. How much of any person’s identity is built by other people’s accounts of them?

  5. Miss MacIntosh says she “should have been an honest woman or an honest man.” What would an honest relationship to one’s own many selves look like?

  6. Catherine becomes everything and so becomes no one to meet. Where is the line between a self that is rich in selves and a self that has dissolved?
