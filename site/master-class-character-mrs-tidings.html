Mrs. Tidings: The Woman Who Invented Her Own Dead

Part of Teaching a Writing Masterclass, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Esther Longtree's Parents · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Musidora →

Spoiler note: This portrait is written for readers who have finished the novel. It discusses the character’s whole story, including how it ends.

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Gertrude Hathaway Tidings · Musidora Tidings · Rosemary

She keeps a parrot in the window, and the town says "the things that parrot could tell if that parrot could talk," which is the town admitting it knows nothing for certain and suspects everything. Mrs. Tidings is the mother of Musidora and Gertrude, and she is the household in the gossip that most exactly rhymes with the great house by the sea, because inventing the people she needs and keeping the ones she killed.

The stories about her will not agree with themselves, and the disagreement is the portrait. There never was a Mr. Tidings that anybody knew, no one ever saw him, and "they say she just invented him." And in the same breath, there was a Mr. Tidings, and "they say she killed him." Both are held at once, a husband who never existed and a husband she murdered, and no one can say which. What is agreed is the relic. She "kept his old coats and his old hats," and she "would not bury" the skull, "some old rabbit-hunter's skull," which was "all that was ever found of him," and she let the children play with it on the floor and set it on the mantelpiece. She is "a pillar of the church," the gossip adds, "though I don't know which church."

Ask the wrong question and Mrs. Tidings is a witch out of a cautionary tale, the murderer with the skull. Ask the right one and she is the second pillar of Radical Immanence told in the register of a crime the town cannot prove. Reality is constructed rather than found, and Mrs. Tidings is the town's Catherine, a woman who built the husband she required and moved him into the house, coats and hats and all.

The invented Mr. Tidings is the gossip's version of the mother's James, the chauffeur built out of an absence, kept present precisely by never arriving. Mrs. Tidings does the same domestic magic in a poorer house. Whether the man was ever there hardly matters to the result, because the coats hang in the closet either way and the skull sits on the mantel either way, and a husband made of relics keeps as well as a husband made of memory. The mother kept James by never letting him come back. Mrs. Tidings keeps Mr. Tidings by never letting him be buried. Both refuse the one thing that would end it, which is admitting the man is gone.

And she carries the book's other obsession, the wrong one lost. She meant to kill "the one she thought was weak-minded," and "aimed for the wrong one," and killed instead "the baby that was never named," while Musidora, the weak-minded one, is "the one that lived." "She always did know she had killed the wrong one." This is the Rosemary knot, the perfect child dead and the wrong child surviving, and it is the Hathaway knot, the wrong brother lost, sounded a third time. Everywhere in this book someone is left with the survivor they did not choose and the loss they did not intend, and Mrs. Tidings is the coldest instance, a mother who priced her weak daughter's life and missed. For she did price it. The old man "bought her from her mother for sixteen dollars" and a new roof he promised, paid "twelve dollars down," "still owes four," never built the roof, and Mrs. Tidings "was going to sue." That is the whole economy of the marginal women laid bare in one transaction, a daughter traded for four dollars and a roof that never came, the invented husband and the sold daughter kept in the same ledger.

The turn for a writer is in the contradiction the book refuses to fix. A careful storyteller resolves the record, decides whether Mr. Tidings was invented or murdered, and reports the finding. Young leaves both standing, because the town leaves both standing, and the doubled, self-canceling rumor is truer to how a person is actually known than any settled fact would be. Mrs. Tidings exists in the space between she made him up and she killed him, and the space is not a gap in the story, it is the story. A writer can build a whole character out of two accounts that cannot both be true, and the character will be more alive for the impossibility, because a real person is the one the evidence keeps disagreeing about. The skull on the mantel is the emblem of the method, a thing that is either all that remains of a husband or with no husband behind it, played with on the floor by children who do not care which, kept by a woman who needed a dead man in the house and made sure she had one.
