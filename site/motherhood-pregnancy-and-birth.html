Motherhood, Pregnancy, and Birth

Part of To All My Darlings, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Definition from research

Chapter 6 · Life, Death, and Rebirth

  • The narrative blurs the lines between living and dead, birth and death.
  • Madge’s pregnancy is a metaphor for birth and death intertwined; she “dies” in giving birth.
  • Death is portrayed as a complex continuation rather than an end.

Chapter 28 · Philosophical Reflections on Birth, Death, and Time

  • The chapter explores the ambiguity between birth and death, suggesting moments in life where beginning and end are indistinguishable.
  • Mr. Spitzer muses on how one could never be sure if a certain moment signifies death or birth, reflecting on the cyclical and interconnected nature of existence.
  • This ambiguity inspired his lifelong study of the "impalpable butterflies," symbolizing ineffable and transient moments in life.

Chapter 71 · Birth and Creation vs. Decay

  • The doctor’s instruments and imagery of birth contrast with the barrenness of the landscape and the decay in the hotel.
  • The pregnant man rumor symbolizes disruption of natural order, reflecting anxieties about identity and transformation.

Chapter 74 · Motherhood and Loss

  • Esther experiences repeated pregnancies that result in stillbirths, symbolizing loss, unfulfilled potential, and emotional trauma.
  • Her womb is described as swollen with “empty waters,” a metaphor for hope and despair coexisting.

Chapter 75 · Motherhood and Loss

  • Esther is pregnant but plagued by miscarriages and stillborn children, some of whom return as haunting apparitions in her dreams.
  • The passage frequently contrasts the physical reality of her miscarriages with her emotional and spiritual yearning to be a mother.
  • The idea of motherhood is both a source of hope and despair; she desires motherhood passionately but is tormented by repeated loss.

Chapter 76 · Motherhood and Infanticide

  • Esther’s experience with pregnancy is fraught with loss, as she has many stillborn or miscarried children, which she refers to as “quiet mysteries.”
  • The concept of murder is extended metaphorically to include unborn or unconceived children, highlighting complex feelings of guilt and responsibility.

Chapter 77 · Motherhood and Loss

  • Esther is "always pregnant," but the pregnancies are metaphorical and literal, filled with loss and the absence of children (stillborn or never born).
  • The grief of lost children haunts her, symbolized by "little bones," a "little book," and the shadow of death inside her womb.
  • Her pregnancy represents both creation and sterility, life and death simultaneously.

Chapter 79 · Unnatural Conception and Birth

  • Esther’s birth is the result of an unnatural process involving a silver spoon, a third person, and years of effort without conjugal love or union.
  • The story contrasts the natural processes of life and love with a barren, mechanical, and painful conception.
  • This theme explores the impact of artificiality on identity and human connection.

Chapter 80 · Motherhood and Pregnancy

  • Esther is perpetually pregnant, both literally and metaphorically. Her body swells with pregnancies that often end in stillbirths or miscarriages, yet she carries an enduring hope for a living child.
  • The physical and emotional toll of her pregnancies is immense; she experiences nausea, swelling, and an overwhelming sense of waiting and expectation.
  • Pregnancy is symbolic of life, death, and the cyclical nature of existence. It is her "widow’s weeds," representing loss and continual renewal.

Chapter 81 · Motherhood and Loss

  • Esther’s endless pregnancy symbolizes the liminal state between creation and destruction.
  • Her history of stillborn children and hidden losses underlines the pain of motherhood.
  • The little shadow boxer inside her represents hope and uncertainty.
