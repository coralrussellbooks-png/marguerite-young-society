Definition from research

Chapter 13

  • “the life-sized chessmen”: In Lewis Carroll’s Through the Looking-Glass (1871), Alice crosses a countryside laid out as a chessboard with living pieces. The chessmen have stood in this garden since Chapter 1.

Chapter 13 · Chessboard and Chessmen

  • Symbolize the complexity and hidden order of life’s movements and events despite apparent confusion.

Chapter 25

  • “all the people who were more than three miles tall should be put out of the house”: The King’s “Rule Forty-two. All persons more than a mile high to leave the court,” in Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland (1865).

Chapter 26

  • “all horses should come in first”: The Dodo’s verdict on the Caucus-race: “Everybody has won, and all must have prizes” (Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland, 1865).

Chapter 37

  • “But who was the dreamer”: “You’re only a sort of thing in his dream!” Tweedledee tells Alice of the sleeping Red King (Lewis Carroll, Through the Looking-Glass, 1871).

Chapter 38

  • “the life-sized chessmen”: In Through the Looking-Glass (1871) Alice finds the chessmen alive and walks into a life-sized game of chess.

Chapter 41

  • “the rabbits wearing lace veils and silver petticoats, walking upright”: Alice first sees “a White Rabbit with pink eyes” run close by her, and Young’s rabbits have “pink eye’s” (Lewis Carroll, Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland, 1865, chapter 1).
  • “so that she could not get through the door”: Alice, after eating the cake, grows “more than nine feet high,” too large for the little door into the garden, though she holds its golden key (Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland, chapter 2).
  • “Once there was a purple feather fan”: The White Rabbit, startled, “dropped the white kid gloves and the fan” (Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland, chapter 2). Catherine’s visitor leaves a fan and then a pair of gloves.

Chapter 48

  • “much like the effect preceding the cause”: The White Queen lives backward: “There’s the King’s Messenger. He’s in prison now, being punished: and the trial doesn’t even begin till next Wednesday: and of course the crime comes last of all,” and “It’s a poor sort of memory that only works backwards” (Lewis Carroll, Through the Looking-Glass, 1871, chapter 5).
  • “Did the house expand”: “What a curious feeling!” said Alice; “I must be shutting up like a telescope” (Lewis Carroll, Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland, 1865, chapter 1). Like Alice, Mr. Spitzer is in a house of doors and keyholes, and later “Perhaps he had passed through a keyhole.”
  • “the portmanteau personality carrying another soul within his soul”: Portmanteau is a large trunk or suitcase opening into two halves, or a word blending the sounds and combining the meanings of two others, a sense Lewis Carroll gave it: “You see it’s like a portmanteau—there are two meanings packed up into one word” (Through the Looking-Glass, 1871, chapter 6).

Chapter 49

  • “chessmen who had walked on squares of black and gold”: See the life-sized chessmen of Through the Looking-Glass in the Chapter 13 resources.

Chapter 50

  • “dream dreamed by another dreamer”: A dream dreamed by another dreamer points to multiple levels of perception and existence where the boundaries between dream and reality blur. It belongs to the novel’s themes of illusion, memory, and the intertwining of life and death, where Vera and the other characters struggle to tell what is real from what is dreamed. Each life may be a fragment within a greater, shared dream. Tweedledee tells Alice that the Red King is dreaming her: “You’d be nowhere. Why, you’re only a sort of thing in his dream!” (Lewis Carroll, Through the Looking-Glass, 1871, chapter 4). See Zhuangzi’s butterfly in the Chapter 49 resources.

Chapter 51

  • “the withering of the flower before the blooming of the flower”: Effect before cause, like the White Queen’s messenger punished before the crime; see the Chapter 48 resources.

Chapter 54

  • “Peron had been mad as a mad hatter”: Hatters were said to go mad from the mercury used to cure felt, which poisons the nerves; Lewis Carroll’s Hatter in Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland (1865) made “mad as a hatter” famous.

Chapter 55

  • Mr. Spitzer is describing several maladies: tinnitus, diplopia, micro/macropsia (Alice in Wonderland syndrome) that can be caused by different things, including psychological.

Chapter 56

  • “if the dreamer was dead, how should he know”: In Lewis Carroll’s Through the Looking-Glass (1871), Tweedledum and Tweedledee tell Alice that she is only a sort of thing in the sleeping Red King’s dream, and that if he woke she would go out like a candle.

Chapter 58

  • “a croquet player knocking his ball through the last wicket”: At the Queen’s croquet ground in Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland (1865), the mallets are flamingos and the balls hedgehogs, and the soldiers bend double to make the arches.

Chapter 69

  • “He was as mad as a March hare”: “Mad as a March hare” is an idiom meaning extremely silly, eccentric, or crazy, originating from the frenzied, unpredictable behavior of European hares during their spring breeding season (March), which includes frantic chases, high leaps, and “boxing” matches where females swat males away to discourage unwanted advances. This phrase was popularized by Lewis Carroll’s Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland, featuring the eccentric March Hare at the Mad Hatter’s tea party, though the behavior itself is a real, albeit misunderstood, part of hare mating rituals, not actual madness.
