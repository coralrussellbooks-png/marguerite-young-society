In the research: Chapter 55, Chapter 69

Definition from research

Chapter 55

  • “The Musical Sensibilities of the King Walrus” may refer to Lewis Carroll’s poem "The Walrus and the Carpenter" which first appeared in 1871. Its rhythmic narrative has been set to music multiple times over the decades, including the 1951 Walt Disney film Alice in Wonderland. In Disney's animated classic, the poem "The Walrus and the Carpenter" is set to a musical sequence. The song and lyrics, performed by J. Pat O'Malley, evokes a vaudeville or light-operatic style, which was a common feature of Disney's musical animation of the mid-20th century. The musical performance acts as a narrative piece, driven by the rhythmic cadence of Carroll's original verse. This focus on clear, storytelling lyrics reflects the musical theater and show-tune conventions of the era. The song portrays the Walrus as charming but villainous. The tune is catchy and inviting, contrasting with the Walrus's predatory intent toward the oysters.

Chapter 55

  • Mr. Spitzer is describing several maladies: tinnitus, diplopia, micro/macropsia (Alice in Wonderland syndrome) that can be caused by different things, including psychological.

Chapter 69

  • “mad as March Hare” is an idiom meaning extremely silly, eccentric, or crazy, originating from the frenzied, unpredictable behavior of European hares during their spring breeding season (March), which includes frantic chases, high leaps, and "boxing" matches where females swat males away to discourage unwanted advances. This phrase was popularized by Lewis Carroll's Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, featuring the eccentric March Hare at the Mad Hatter's tea party, though the behavior itself is a real, albeit misunderstood, part of hare mating rituals, not actual madness.
