Chapter 34

On Chapter 34 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Woman in Young's Magnum Opus.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah

Catherine imagines herself married in black rather than white, and the inversion is the chapter's pillar: marriage as social death, the rite that should begin a life staged instead as its end. She cannot tell whether the ceremony is "a wedding, a funeral, or an auction," and the confusion is the whole feminist charge, marriage as a rite of passage that is also a passing away, the "death of a woman's soul" the great suffrage captain names. The prophetic St. Bernard delivering the Viaticum blurs the sacraments until the bride's forward motion into marriage and her motion toward the grave are the same motion.

Cousin Hannah stands in the chapter as realism against Catherine's opium fantasy, insisting she is "spouseless" and bereft of love, and the two women divide again along the militant and dreamer seam the novel keeps drawing. Hannah is the "great captain" and "old warrior" who persists though there is "no defense, no answering cannon ball," a woman fighting systemic silence with a body that will not surrender; the "adamantine" mountains of wedlock are rigid and imprisoning, and her indomitable spirit outlasts them. Her strength, "like a finely tempered blade," "cold and nipping as the winter air," is an elemental femininity refusing mortal frailty, and Catherine both envies and is estranged from it, the complicated distance between women who have chosen different survivals.

The chapter deconstructs masculine authority as it goes, man reduced to "ephemeral" and a "broken sea shell," fragile and illusory beside the enduring strength of a woman like Hannah who has transcended "merely mortal needs" and "the idea of a man." Catherine's flirtations with imaginary lovers, her "magician" power to make and unmake reality, place female imagination as both refuge and trap. The chapter closes on marriage as the death of female autonomy and on two women, one militant and one dreaming, refusing that death from opposite ends of the same conviction.
