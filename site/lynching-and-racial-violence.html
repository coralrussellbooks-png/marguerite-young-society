Definition from research

Chapter 54

  • “Wall Street broker” The history of Wall Street brokers traces the evolution of securities trading from informal outdoor dealings to the high-tech, regulated global financial markets of today. The profession has shifted from being a specialized service for the wealthy to a digitized industry, leading traditional brokers to focus on financial advising rather than trade execution. In the late 1700s, merchants and speculators met on Wall Street to trade U.S. government bonds and shares of early banks. Business was initially conducted informally, often taking place under a buttonwood tree outside 68 Wall Street or in nearby coffeehouses. To formalize their business and edge out the auctioneers who had previously dominated securities trading, 24 brokers signed the Buttonwood Agreement in 1792. This established a fixed commission rate and required them to give preference to each other in negotiations. This agreement became the foundation of what would later be the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE). As the market grew, trading split between the formal NYSE and an informal "curb" market for riskier stocks. For much of the 19th century, brokers who traded stocks for small, new, or speculative companies operated in the streets of lower Manhattan. These "curbstone brokers" specialized in securities for emerging industries like railroads, mining, and oil. The Open Board of Stock Brokers, founded in 1864 by former curb brokers, competed with the NYSE before merging with it in 1869. In 1921, the curb market moved indoors and eventually became the American Stock Exchange (AMEX). The introduction of the stock ticker in 1867 brought real-time stock prices to brokerage offices, a vast improvement over pneumatic tubes and runners. The "Roaring Twenties" and the crash of 1929 saw an explosion in brokerage activity and a dramatic increase in public participation in the market. Leaders of firms like Merrill Lynch began encouraging middle-class Americans to invest in common stocks, fueling market growth. Brokers aggressively promoted speculative investments and margin trading (borrowing money to buy stocks) to boost their commissions. This pushed stock prices to unsustainable levels. The stock market crash of 1929, triggered by panic selling after prices began to fall, led to widespread broker bankruptcies and financial ruin for many clients. In the aftermath, government regulation was introduced to prevent such events in the future. After the crash, the industry faced greater oversight and eventually underwent a technological transformation. The Securities Exchange Act of 1934 created the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) to regulate the securities industry and protect investors.

Chapter 68

  • Oliver Moore was lynched in North Carolina in 1930. Oliver Moore was accused of assaulting two young white girls, Ethel, age 5, and Lucile Morgan, age 7, daughters of Mr. and Mrs. E. F. Morgan, who lived on the county line between Wilson and Edgecombe Counties, on the night of 18 July 1930. Moore evaded capture for nearly a month but was finally caught and arrested on 15 August 1930 and jailed in Tarboro, NC, in the county jail. At around midnight on 18 August 1930, a mob of masked men broke Moore out of jail and took to a wooded area on the Wilson-Edgecombe County line just inside Wilson County not far from the scene of the alleged assault, hung him from a tree with a rope around his chest, and shot him repeatedly until he was dead. By the next morning when it appears this photo was taken, thousands of people from all around the area had gathered to view the lynching aftermath and Moore's body hanging riddled with bullets from the tree.

Chapter 68

  • “The Negro was hanged for sleeping with a white woman” describes a story told by the Christian hangman Mr. Weed about a botched lynching of a Black man in Kentucky. According to the tale, the man was hanged for the accusation of sleeping with a white woman, an accusation he likely did not deserve. The hangman describes how the man was cut down from the noose a split second too soon, meaning he was still technically alive and was later seen pushing a baby buggy, frightening the local white community. The hangman reflects on the "inefficiency" of the hanging, suggesting that his role was to make death as swift and painless as possible, but here the man suffered unnecessarily by surviving the hanging. This passage echoes real historical accounts of lynching in the United States, particularly in the South, where Black men were often falsely accused of crimes, especially of violating racial codes like alleged relations with white women. Lynchings were extrajudicial killings by mobs, commonly by hanging, sometimes with brutal torture. There are documented cases where victims were cut down prematurely or survived the initial hanging, leading to further suffering before death or even escape, heightening the terror of such acts. The passage metaphorically highlights the cruelty and injustice of these acts, along with the hangman's conflicted role: he sees himself as a Christian bringing mercy through a swift death, yet is part of a brutal system that perpetuates racial violence and injustice. The "unofficially alive" status of the man symbolizes the liminal, tortured state of lynching victims: condemned by society, yet clinging to life in a world that refuses them justice or dignity. This passage serves as a fictional reflection on historical racial violence in America, underscoring the themes of injustice, suffering, and moral ambiguity surrounding lynching and the societal roles that participated in or failed to prevent such atrocities.

Chapter 71

  • Vera pictured Marie hanging and her unborn baby hanging by its umbilical cord. Pregnant Black women were among the many victims of lynching during the era of racial terror in the United States. While the exact number is not known, the case of Mary Turner is one of the most widely documented instances of this particularly brutal violence. In May 1918, a white farmer named Hampton Smith was killed by one of his Black employees, Sidney Johnson, in Brooks County, Georgia. An enraged white mob began a week-long rampage, killing several Black people, including Mary Turner's husband, Hayes Turner. After her husband was lynched, a grieving Mary Turner, who was eight months pregnant, publicly denounced the murder and threatened to have the mob members arrested. Her statement enraged the mob, which turned its fury on her. On May 19, 1918, a mob of hundreds of people captured Turner and dragged her to Folsom Bridge over the Little River. There, they tied her ankles, hung her upside down from a tree, and set her on fire. While she was still alive, a man cut open her abdomen with a knife, causing her unborn baby to fall to the ground. A mob member then stomped the baby to death. Turner's body was then riddled with hundreds of bullets. No one was ever held accountable for the murders of Mary Turner and her unborn child. The grotesque nature of her lynching revealed the level of dehumanization and impunity with which Black women were treated during this period. Her case was widely publicized by the NAACP and became a symbol in the anti-lynching movement. The lynching of Black women is a less-documented aspect of racial terror, but historians have uncovered evidence that it was not uncommon. The violence against Black women, especially pregnant women like Mary Turner, served as a tool of white supremacy to terrorize the Black community and suppress any form of dissent. The sexualized and gendered violence inflicted upon women's bodies was also used to communicate power and control. Many instances of women being lynched were underreported or not documented at all. The details of Mary Turner's lynching only became widely known because of the investigation by Walter F. White of the NAACP and the subsequent national anti-lynching campaign. The murder of Mary Turner remains a potent and horrific symbol of the racial and gender-based violence that was inflicted upon Black women during the era of racial terror.

Chapter 80

  • Esther Longtree symbolizes a deep and tragic history of racial and social injustice in the United States, particularly relating to the legacy of slavery, interracial relationships under oppressive conditions, and the ongoing consequences of systemic racism. Her story reflects the painful history of a black father, described as a "Negro, a Negro, a Negro, poor young fellow, black as the black bough, the ace of spades," who was a fugitive, possibly an ex-convict, victimized by white supremacy: "the white people there had tried to kill him because he was black and had wanted to sleep with a white woman... they had tried to hang him from a white-blossoming tree for revenge, but the rope had broken". This narrative evokes the historical reality of lynching and racial violence in the American South. Esther’s pregnancy with this black man’s child, her subsequent abandonment of the child, and her lifelong torment symbolize the fraught and often violent intersections of race, gender, and power in America. Her child, Walter, who is black and whom she both loved and feared, becomes a haunting presence in her life: a symbol of the legacy of interracial unions condemned and punished by society, and the personal and social consequences of such relationships. The repeated motif of Esther being "always pregnant," carrying the memory and burden of lost or stillborn children, reflects the cycles of trauma and loss imposed on black and mixed-race families by systemic racism and social rejection. Her incarceration in the "Greasy Spoon" restaurant, her social marginalization, and her sense of isolation also symbolize the economic and social disenfranchisement experienced by many African Americans and women in American history. Esther’s struggle with guilt and the haunting presence of her first child, who lives outside her physically but not emotionally, represents the unresolved historical wounds of slavery’s legacy: the unacknowledged and hidden suffering passed down through generations. Esther Longtree embodies the history of racial violence, forbidden and punished interracial love, maternal suffering, social exclusion, and the enduring scars of America's racial past. Through her narrative, Young confronts the complexity of identity, memory, and historical trauma in the United States.

Chapter 80

  • Interracial marriage in the United States has only been legal since the 1967 U.S. Supreme Court decision Loving v. Virginia that held anti-miscegenation laws were unconstitutional by the 14th Amendment established in 1868. The court's landmark decision has been commemorated and celebrated every year on the Loving Day, June 12th. The number of interracial marriages in 1967 was three percent. The first interracial marriage in what is today the United States was that of Pocahontas, who married tobacco planter John Rolfe in 1614. The first ever law prohibiting interracial marriage was passed by Maryland in 1691. The Quaker Zephaniah Kingsley married, outside the US, a black enslaved woman that he bought in Cuba. He also had three black common-law enslaved wives. He freed all four. They had to flee Florida for Haiti when it became a US territory. The prospect of black men marrying white women terrified many Americans before the Civil War. It was magnified into the greatest threat to society. The first legal black-white marriage in the United States was that of African-American professor William G. Allen and a white student, Mary King, in 1853. When their plans to marry were announced, Allen narrowly escaped being lynched. Their marriage was secret, and they left the country immediately for England, never to return. In 1957 actor Sammy Davis Jr. faced backlash for his relationship with a white woman, actress Kim Novak. In 1958, Davis briefly married a black woman, actress and dancer Loray White, to protect himself from mob violence.

Chapter 80

  • Jo Reed was an African American man who was lynched in Nashville, Tennessee, on April 30, 1875, where he was taken by a white mob from the county jail after being arrested for killing a police officer in a confrontation. He was hanged from a suspension bridge but, after the rope broke, Reed survived the attempted lynching, escaped via the river, and left Nashville to go West. A reference to part of the history of lynching in America is included at the end of this chapter. In 1955 the brutal lynching of fourteen year old Emmett Till for allegedly flirting with a white woman was a catalyst for the Civil Rights Movement after his mother permitted an open casket and photographs were published nationwide.
