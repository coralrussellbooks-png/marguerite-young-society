Content note: The Esther Longtree chapters (72 to 81) deal with stillbirth and pregnancy loss, rape, infanticide, racist violence including lynching, and cruelty to children. This chapter describes cruelty to a young child and a stillbirth.

Summary

Esther was called Little Jackass from birth, because she was born kicking and stubborn. Her tiny, cold mother blamed her for the stillbirth of her twin, Rosemary, the perfect child, saying Esther kicked her out of the womb. Esther grew up lonely, often tied to a tree in the yard. Her father was a large, timid policeman who patrolled the graveyard to warn off lovers rather than arrest them. She wonders whether she herself is the shadow, the one who never truly lived.

Key passages

From the research

  • The opening lines explain why Esther was called "Little Jackass" because she was born stubborn, kicking, awkward, and reluctant to be born. This sets the tone for her lifelong struggle with her own existence and nature. The metaphor of being born kicking and fighting reflects her resistance to life’s hardships and her difficult personality.
  • Esther’s father is described as a passive, slow, and timid policeman who patrols the graveyard at night to warn off lovers. Despite his loneliness and bad luck, Esther feels pity and love for him. She sees herself as a "chip off the old block," sharing his nature but contrasting with her mother. This passage contrasts the warmth and vitality Esther inherits from her father with the coldness of her mother.
  • Her mother is portrayed as small, childish, and cold, with a narrow heart and fragile possessions. Everything in the house is built to her size and easily broken. Esther’s awkwardness and physical attributes are contrasted with her mother’s delicate, fragile nature. This passage helps explain Esther’s difficult upbringing and lack of maternal warmth.
  • Esther’s childhood is marked by physical accidents, deformities (like her crippled foot), and social abuse by other children. She is described as always stung by insects, reflecting a deep, ongoing pain and isolation. The story of the bee flying up her skirts and the buzzing under her skin symbolizes persistent inner torment and vulnerability.
  • There is an important passage about Esther’s father’s mother, who gave birth many times, often without realizing it, and whose children mostly survived. This contrasts with Esther’s own fate, where she is haunted by the loss of her stillborn siblings. This contrast emphasizes the unnaturalness and tragedy of Esther’s birth and life.
  • Central to the chapter is the revelation that Esther had a twin sister, Rosemary, who was born prematurely and dead because Esther had "kicked her out" of the womb. This accusation, made repeatedly by her mother, defines much of Esther’s shame, guilt, and identity. Rosemary is described as perfect, delicate, and the child the mother would have preferred. Esther’s lifelong feeling of being a murderer and a shadow is tied to this traumatic event.
  • The mother constantly blames Esther for killing Rosemary before birth and accuses her of being ungrateful and awkward. Esther lives with this accusation, which shapes her self-perception as a mulish, sullen child who broke things and was not properly loved or nurtured. This deep maternal rejection is a key to understanding Esther’s emotional struggles.
  • Throughout her life, Esther looks for signs of Rosemary or the "other," digging in the ground, finding small objects buried under trees, and interpreting shadows and noises as traces of the lost twin. This search symbolizes Esther’s attempt to reconcile with her past and her own identity, haunted by the loss and mystery of her twin.
  • Esther sometimes wonders if she is the shadow and if the other twin was the one truly born. She doubts her own reality and existence, feeling like an elusive being or ghost. This passage highlights the depth of Esther’s psychological torment and alienation from herself and the world.
  • The chapter closes with Esther’s reflection on loneliness, the emptiness of life, and the idea that no heart is as great as "non-heart," no being as beautiful as "non-being." She sometimes feels like a shadow, never fully found or understood. This somber, poetic ending encapsulates the tragic and existential themes of the chapter.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Esther Longtree

  • She was called "Little Jackass" due to her stubborn, awkward nature and her difficult birth. She was born kicking and fighting against life itself, a symbol of resistance and struggle. Her childhood was lonely, marked by accidents, physical scars, and emotional neglect. She found solace only in nature, making friends with birds like woodpeckers and bluejays.
  • Esther wrestles with her identity, sometimes wondering if she is the “shadow,” the lesser, the one who should not have lived. She is caught between the life she has and the ghost of the twin she lost, a metaphor for the conflict between existence and non-existence, presence and absence.
  • A complex figure embodying strength, stubbornness, and vulnerability. She is deeply affected by her family’s past and her own mysterious shadow, the lost twin. Her physical awkwardness and emotional turmoil underscore her unique resilience and tragic circumstances.

Esther’s father

  • He is a passive policeman, seen as a lonely, timid man whose life is confined and unfulfilled. He patrols the graveyard to warn off lovers to avoid arrests, embodying a man trapped by circumstance and routine. Her mother is a small, childlike woman with little understanding of life or Esther’s struggles. Their marriage lacks love, and Esther inherits nothing from her mother but much from her father’s robust, natural lineage.

Esther’s mother

  • She blames her for the death of her twin sister, Rosemary, who was born prematurely and dead. Esther is accused of killing this twin by kicking her in the womb, an act that haunts her throughout her life. This accusation shapes Esther’s self-perception as a murderer even before birth and contributes to her lifelong feelings of guilt and alienation.

Rosemary

  • Rosemary represents the idealized child who never lived, who would have been perfect and brought ease to the family. Esther’s mother mourns Rosemary’s loss and envies her, constantly reminding Esther of what she supposedly destroyed. Esther, in contrast, struggles with this shadow, often questioning her own reality and identity.

Research: Resources

  • Esther Longtree is portrayed in this chapter as a deeply tragic and complex character, burdened by loneliness, loss, and a profound sense of guilt that shapes her entire existence. Esther is introduced as a "mulish," stubborn, and awkward individual, nicknamed "Little Jackass" because she was born kicking and resisting life from the very beginning. This obstinacy is both physical, shown in her accidents and bruises, and emotional, manifesting in her defiance against her circumstances and her family dynamics. Despite her struggles, Esther tries hard to embrace life, described poetically as a red rose "blooming in the heart of death" or a "white piano in a tinkling stream," symbolizing her vitality and resilience amid suffering. Her family background is marked by estrangement and emotional coldness. Her father, a passive, slow, and timid policeman with a sad, isolated life, contrasts with Esther’s fiery nature. He patrols the graveyard to warn off lovers rather than arrest them, embodying a gentle but resigned presence. Esther inherits her strength and naturalness from her father’s lineage, which includes Irish, Indian, and possibly Negro ancestry, known for their robust fertility and natural births. In contrast, her mother is tiny, childish, emotionally cold, and narrow-hearted, representing a limiting and oppressive force in Esther’s life. A central element of Esther’s tragic identity is the burden of a lost twin, Rosemary, who was stillborn before Esther’s birth. Esther’s mother accuses her repeatedly, laughing and crying, that Esther "killed" Rosemary by kicking her out of the womb, condemning Esther as a murderer even before she was born. This accusation haunts Esther throughout her life, coloring her self-perception with guilt and shame. She grows up overshadowed by the memory of Rosemary, an idealized "perfect" child who would have been obedient, delicate, and beloved, the daughter her mother longed for. Esther, by contrast, is awkward, large, and defiant, and constantly reminded of her failure to be the child her mother wanted. Esther’s relationship with motherhood and fertility is deeply fraught. She is often pregnant or swollen but has lost many children, described metaphorically as "little birds," "snowflakes," or "stillborn," which reflects the repeated cycle of hope and grief she endures. She carries a secret sorrow for these lost children, which she can never openly mourn or explain, and she battles both physical and emotional pain, including an enlarged heart and a crippled foot, which further symbolize her suffering. Her internal world is haunted by shadows, rustlings, and the presence of the lost children, hinting at a mystical or spectral dimension to her grief. Esther digs holes in the earth, searching for remnants of the past, the "shadow" of her twin, and the lost children, but never fully finds peace or closure. She experiences a profound alienation, sometimes doubting her own reality and identity, wondering if she herself is the shadow or the lesser existence, while Rosemary is the "real" one who should have lived. Despite all this, Esther is not without moments of fierce joy and strength. Her laughter can be loud and powerful, shaking the snow from clouds, and she fiercely protects and claims love for the children she does carry, even if they do not survive. Her stubbornness and vitality make her a figure of tragic endurance, struggling against the "nameless grave" and the weight of familial expectations and curses. Esther Longtree is a figure of intense contradiction: strong yet vulnerable, life-affirming yet haunted by death, natural yet marked by unnatural loss. Her story in this chapter reveals themes of maternal grief, the burden of inherited trauma, and the search for identity amid family secrets and personal suffering. Her character embodies the tragic complexity of fertility and loss, the interplay of life and death, and the painful legacies passed down through generations.
  • Esther Longtree symbolizes a deep and painful history of struggle, loss, and marginalization that can be read as a reflection of certain aspects of American history, particularly those involving the complex legacies of race, family, and survival. Her lineage is described as mixed: "partly Irish and partly Indian and maybe even had a Negro streak in them," suggesting the multiracial and multicultural heritage that is part of the American fabric, especially in the context of colonial and frontier histories where Indigenous peoples, European settlers, and African descendants intersected. This mixed heritage points to the tangled histories of racial identity and the often painful intersections of different communities in the United States. Esther’s life is marked by hardship, loneliness, and repeated loss: she has borne many children who have died before or shortly after birth, symbolizing the high rates of infant and maternal mortality historically faced by impoverished, marginalized, or rural populations in the US. Her mother’s accusation that Esther "killed a little child before she was ever born," referring to the death of her twin Rosemary, suggests the tragic fate of many families who suffered such losses quietly and without support. Her father, a passive policeman whose "life was a prison," represents the ordinary working-class man trapped in a limited existence, disconnected from emotional life and unable to protect or nurture fully. This dynamic echoes the social and economic constraints faced by many in American history, especially in small, insular communities where traditional roles and isolation prevailed. Esther’s story also touches on the theme of women’s experiences of oppression and misunderstanding: her mother’s coldness, the abuse from schoolmates, and her own physical and emotional pain symbolize the harsh realities women have faced, including neglect, judgment, and the burden of reproductive suffering. Esther’s "mulish" nature and stubbornness reflect resistance and survival despite these adversities, embodying the resilience necessary to endure "long trial, long suffering". Esther Longtree’s narrative can be understood as a microcosm of the American historical experience involving racial mixture, frontier hardship, the struggles of working-class families, the tragedy of loss, and the resilience of women caught in cycles of suffering and survival. Her character embodies the silent histories of pain and endurance that underlie much of the American story.
  • Vera narrates the harsh and painful parental abuse Esther Longtree endured from a very early age. Esther's mother is portrayed as a cold, narrow-hearted woman who blamed Esther for the death of her twin sister, Rosemary, even before Esther was born. Her mother repeatedly accused her of having "killed a little child before she was ever born," labeling her guilty of infanticide in the womb, which created a deep sense of shame and guilt in Esther throughout her life. This accusation was a source of constant reproach and emotional cruelty, with her mother both laughing and crying as she reminded Esther of this supposed crime. The mother also expressed disappointment and resentment toward Esther, comparing her unfavorably to the lost twin, Rosemary, who was idealized as perfect, delicate, and the daughter her mother wished she had. This kept Esther in a state of embarrassment and alienation, burdened by the shadow of the lost child and her mother's cold judgment. Esther's mother physically abused her as a child, exemplified when she once hit Esther for screaming about a bee that had flown up her skirts, not believing her and dismissing her cries. Esther was also tied by a rope in the yard under a tree by her mother, indicating neglect and harsh treatment. The mother is described as childish in mentality and size, living in a house too small for both her and Esther, filled with fragile objects that seemed to symbolize the fragile and oppressive environment Esther was subjected to. The emotional abuse extended to lack of nurturing; Esther's mother never nursed her at the narrow breasts or stayed to comfort her tears. She also mocked Esther’s awkwardness, physical deformities, and stubborn nature, further isolating her emotionally. The mother’s coldness was a constant presence, leaving Esther to grow up lonely and unloved, intensifying her suffering. Esther’s maternal abuse was a complex mixture of emotional cruelty, neglect, physical punishment, and harsh judgment rooted in the tragic loss of her twin and her mother’s inability to love her fully, which deeply affected Esther’s sense of self and her life trajectory.
  • Esther Longtree endured significant bullying from her schoolmates and other children during her childhood. She was nicknamed "Little Jackass" because of her stubborn, awkward nature and physical deformities, including a deformed foot and crossed eyes. The children made fun of her for these traits as well as for her "streaming hair" and her mulish head, which she was always sticking into old wasps' nests, a behavior her mother had criticized as well. Esther was often stung and worried by wasps, bees, and flies, which added to her distress. Her physical awkwardness and accidents (she was always falling, her knees scarred, and her body black and blue) made her a target for ridicule. The children’s cruelty was compounded by Esther’s own suffering and her mother’s lack of understanding or protection, leaving her lonely and often crying in the dark. The bullying was not just verbal but tied to Esther’s physical and emotional isolation, making her childhood unhappy and lonely.
  • An icebox is a compact non-mechanical refrigerator which was a common early-twentieth-century kitchen appliance before the development of safely powered refrigeration devices. Before the development of electric refrigerators, iceboxes were referred to by the public as refrigerators. The terms icebox and refrigerator were used interchangeably in advertising as long ago as 1848.
  • Esther Longtree's lack of food is described as a persistent and painful condition that has been part of her life even before she was pregnant. She is characterized by an empty stomach because she could not eat enough to give her strength. The chapter notes that even before pregnancy, Esther was always hungry and resorted to stealing food from the icebox when her mother wasn’t looking. This chronic hunger is part of her broader suffering and physical weakness, contributing to her difficult existence marked by accidents, bruises, and a crippled foot. Esther's experience of hunger and deprivation can be seen as a personal reflection of broader themes of scarcity and struggle that many individuals have faced historically. The narrative situates Esther’s hunger within a familial and emotional context of neglect, poverty, and hardship as her mother’s household is described as small and fragile, with delicate objects easily broken, and her father as a passive policeman with little home life, suggesting an environment of limited resources and emotional deprivation. The history of child hunger in the U.S. includes its public "discovery" in the late 1960s, which led to the creation of federal programs like the Special Food Service Program (now the Summer Food Service Program) and the Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children (WIC). The 1980s saw the development of metrics like "food insecurity" to better measure the problem, and despite fluctuations, child hunger remains a significant issue, with families of color and single-parent households disproportionately affected.
  • Esther says her mother is a “pallid descendant of George Washington’s sister.” This may refer to Betty Washington Lewis who was the younger sister of George Washington and the only sister that survived childhood. Betty Washington was born into the Washington Family as the first daughter of Augustine Washington and Mary Ball Washington.
  • The novel portrays children, especially characters like Esther Longtree and Musidora’s children, as burdened by premature or inappropriate knowledge of sexual realities. This knowledge is often linked to trauma, loss, and the failure of adult protection. Esther Longtree is depicted as a woman who has been "always pregnant," with many stillborn children and a pervasive sense of loss, loneliness, and burden. She is surrounded by the imagery of children who have suffered or died, and these children represent both literal and symbolic figures of innocence lost and corrupted sexuality. The children are often shown as victims of adult failures, such as unloving or absent parents, societal neglect, and the consequences of adult promiscuity or irresponsibility. They are described as "wise" beyond their years, aware of harsh truths that should be hidden from them, and sometimes as reproachful presences haunting their mothers or the community. Vera, and other characters, like Madge, observe that these children are burdened with knowledge and experiences they cannot comprehend or control, symbolizing a premature confrontation with adult realities. This is reflected in phrases like children "playing at hanging themselves," or being "mysterious shrouded presences" who cause confusion and suffering. The presence of sexually aware children also ties into the motif of pregnancy and birth, which in the novel is often depicted as fraught with mystery, loss, and sometimes death. The characters’ struggles with conception, stillbirths, and motherhood are interwoven with a cultural critique of the consequences of repressed or mismanaged sexuality. The motif symbolizes the erosion of childhood innocence in a world where adult hypocrisies, failures, and social decay impinge on the young. It reflects a cultural anxiety about the premature sexualization of children and the failure of society to protect them. These children embody the consequences of broken families, absent or irresponsible parents, and a society that neglects its vulnerable members. Their knowledge of sex is not empowering but burdensome, linked to trauma rather than understanding. The novel critiques the social conditions that produce such outcomes: poverty, neglect, and moral confusion. The children represent lost potential and fragmented identities, paralleling the novel’s themes of multiplicity and fractured selves. Their awareness of sex and its consequences contrasts with their powerlessness and the mysteries surrounding birth, life, and death. The motif also connects with the novel’s larger exploration of dreams, illusions, and altered states of consciousness. The children’s premature knowledge is part of a distorted reality, where what is known and unknown, real and unreal, is blurred. Their sexual awareness is part of the complex interplay between truth and illusion that defines the novel’s atmosphere. By highlighting the plight of children burdened with adult knowledge, the novel implicitly critiques the social hypocrisies surrounding sex, marriage, and reproduction. It questions the societal failure to provide clear, honest guidance and protection, instead leaving children to navigate complex, often harmful realities alone.
  • Esther’s mothers behavior is a form of severe emotional abuse with devastating psychological consequences for the surviving child. The parent's actions are often rooted in their own inability to cope with the trauma, grief, and feelings of helplessness surrounding the death. Individuals with narcissistic or antisocial traits may lack empathy and view their children as objects to be used to regulate their own emotions. In this context, blaming the child can serve the parent's emotional needs rather than the child's.
  • Elizabeth "Betty" Washington Lewis (1733–1797) was George Washington’s only sister to survive to adulthood and a dedicated patriot. She managed the family estate, Kenmore, in Fredericksburg, Virginia, while her husband, Fielding Lewis, supported the Revolution. She faced financial hardships and managed the household after her husband's death.
