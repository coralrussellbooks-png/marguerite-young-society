Angel in the Forest

Chapter 2. The Catalog as Philosophical Instrument

← Chapter 1 · Angel in the Forest: A Reading in Fourteen Chapters · Chapter 3 →

Marguerite Young / Angel in the Forest, Chapter 2

Marguerite Young makes lists where another writer would make an argument. Open Angel in the Forest to almost any page and you find them: long runs of nouns, joined by commas, pouring past faster than you can hold them. It is easy to read these as exuberance, a poet who fell in love with her material and could not stop naming things. Mark Van Doren more or less says as much, describing the objects that stream across the book like cosmic dust. But the lists are not an overflow. They are the main tool Young works with, and they are doing a specific and deliberate job: they are proving, over and over, that the categories we sort human life into cannot actually hold human life.

It helps to be plain about what a list is, structurally, because the whole argument of this chapter turns on it.

An argument subordinates. It has a main point and supporting points. It ranks things. It moves in a direction, toward a conclusion, and along the way it throws out whatever does not serve the conclusion. That is what makes an argument feel like knowledge: it cuts. A list does the opposite. It coordinates. Every item sits at the same level as every other item, strung along by commas and the word "and," and nothing is subordinate to anything else. Nothing is the main point. Nothing is the conclusion. Where an argument builds a hierarchy, a list lays everything flat and keeps it all.

Once you see that, you can see why Young reaches for the list at exactly the moments when another historian would reach for a thesis. She does not want to rank the people of New Harmony or reduce them to a type. She wants to keep all of them. And keeping all of them, refusing to cut, turns out to be an argument in its own right, made at the level of grammar.

The inhabitants who fit nowhere

Look at how she names the people who passed through the town. She calls the roll of an angel like a hermaphrodite butterfly, a butterfly catcher, a Daniel Boone of the infinite, a finite Elijah, a herb doctor, Lot's wife, and on through clouded dreamers, a celibate breeder of horses, poor Yahoos, the Spirit of Nature, rational man, irrational men, patriarchs, and undertakers.

Read that as an argument and it looks like nonsense. Read it as a catalog and it becomes precise. Nearly every figure on the list is a person who breaks a category by standing inside two at once. The angel is a hermaphrodite and a butterfly, crossing sex, species, and the line between earth and heaven in a single phrase. A Daniel Boone of the infinite yokes the most local of frontiersmen to the boundless. A finite Elijah does the reverse, pinning a prophet who ascended into the sky back down to a finite life. Lot's wife is the woman who looked back at what she was told to leave behind and paid for the looking. These are not random. They are, one after another, people the sorting machines cannot sort.

That matters because the two utopias were nothing but sorting machines. Father Rapp's schema turned people into future angels. Robert Owen's schema turned people into machines shaped by circumstance. Between them they claimed to have accounted for what a person is. Young's catalog answers by naming a town full of people who are neither angels nor machines, who spill out of both systems, and it makes the answer without a word of overt disagreement. She simply lists the ones who do not fit, and the list is longer than either schema can absorb.

This is the point the philosophy calls attending to the margins. The people at the edges of a category, the ones whose bodies or minds or lives cross the lines the system drew, are usually treated as exceptions, footnotes, problems to be explained away. The edge is exactly where you can see that the line was drawn by someone, that it was made and not found. When Young pours out her hermaphrodite angels and finite prophets, she is not decorating the history. She is showing the seams. The catalog holds difference without flattening it into a type, which is the one thing an argument, with its need to conclude, can never do.

The flesh that came back as commerce

Her most quietly devastating list is an inventory. When she describes what the Rappite community sold, she lists the goods: wheat, corn, oats, hides, furs, butter, wax, horses, cattle, hogs, and so on down the line, then a second cascade of every skin and animal product a frontier economy could render down, deerskins and bearskins and wolfskins, otter and muskrat and beaver and mink, honey and eggs and bristles and tallow and feathers.

On its face this is dull, the kind of trade record a normal historian summarizes in a sentence and moves past. Young does not summarize it. She lets it run, and letting it run is the point because of who these people were. The Rappites were celibate. They had renounced the flesh, renounced marriage and children, and believed all things fleeting but themselves. And here is the community they built, generating a torrent of flesh: fur, hide, fat, bone, feather, egg. The list is dense with animal bodies and animal increase, an overwhelming record of fecundity produced by people who had officially given up on the body. Young names the irony without arguing it. She calls the whole cascade the products of a marvelously fecund nature, operated by celibates. The list has already made the case. You cannot renounce the flesh. It comes back, if not as sons and daughters then as bearskins and honey, as commerce, as the sheer productive weight of the world reasserting itself through the very people who tried to step out of it. No sentence of analysis could carry that as plainly as the inventory carries it.

The utopia that contained everything it excluded

The third list is the widest. Describing what the two communities somehow held inside half a square mile of wilderness, Young writes a catalog that yokes the cosmic to the trivial with no transition at all: the burning of Rome, city planning, explosion of stars, a new calendar, anarchy, a New Jerusalem, moneyless Eden, a three-hour working day, exaltation of horses, infinite regress, the united nations of earth, and, a few beats later in a companion list, good dentistry and baby owls.

The juxtapositions are the argument. Set the explosion of stars beside good dentistry, or a New Jerusalem beside the falling of autumn leaves, and you get the point without being told it: any real attempt to reorganize human life ends up containing all of human life, the sublime and the petty and the contradictory all at once. A utopia sets out to exclude the mistaken parts of existence, to keep only the reasonable, the elevated, the sortable. And the more completely it tries, the more completely it fills back up with everything it meant to leave out. Young says this outright, in one of the few places she lets the catalog resolve into a claim: such perfectionist orders, which would have excluded much of mistaken life, seem mistaken life itself, with all its infinite variety. The list is how she earns the right to say it. She shows you the infinite variety first, item by item, and only then names what it means.

What accumulation knows that argument cannot

So the honest question is the formal one. What does a list know that an argument does not?

An argument buys its coherence by cutting. It gives you a clean line of reasoning and a conclusion you can carry away, and the price is everything that got left on the floor to make the line look neat and clean. A list refuses to pay that price. It keeps the contradictions in the same breath, on the same level, unranked and unresolved, and what it gives you in return is not a conclusion but a whole thing, contradictions included. That is a real kind of knowledge, and it is not the kind synthesis produces. Synthesis tells you what a thing means once the mess has been cleared away. The catalog tells you what the thing is while the mess is still in it, which is the only condition the thing ever actually exists in.

This is also why the list is the right grammar for what Young believes. Standard sentences assume a world of stable things that do stuff, a subject that governs and a verb that acts and an object that receives, a small machine for turning the world into causes and effects. Young does not think the world holds still like that. Her catalogs are almost all noun and almost no verb, and crucially they refuse to let any single noun become the governing one. There is no subject ruling the sentence, no main term the others serve. Everything is a coordinate, everything is kept, nothing is allowed to settle into the stable center that ordinary grammar demands. This is the same instinct the first chapter traced in her restless sentences, working now at a different scale. Where the standard vocabulary makes it hard to describe a world of process and contradiction, Young builds a grammar of accumulation that can hold exactly that.

The list, in other words, is not the poet losing control of the history. It is the philosopher choosing the only instrument honest enough for the job. To witness a place without rushing it toward a conclusion, to hold everything that was really there without deciding which parts were allowed to be real, you do not make an argument. You make a catalog, and you let it stay open.
