Research notes: Chapter 58 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 58

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

Mr. Spitzer is never singular. He is simultaneously the musician who cannot play, the mourner who cannot stop mourning, the observer who cannot simply observe, the critic who cannot separate admiration from pity. The chapter explicitly states:

"For probably Mr. Spitzer was so constituted that his admiration would always be his pity — and his pity, his admiration."

This is not psychological ambivalence in the ordinary sense. It is ontological plurality: the self as a site where contradictory orientations coexist without resolution. He is the catcher and the pitcher, the musician and the audience, the living and the possibly dead. The old violinist himself multiplies across the chapter's famous catalogue of transformations (violinist, baseball player, tennis player, shepherd, judge, king, conductor) demonstrating that identity is not a stable property but a function of which instrument, which wand, which stance one happens to hold at a given moment:

"Ah, what miracles could be achieved with a simple wand! What forms of being might evolve."

Not One Self is not presented as a crisis to be resolved. It is presented as the fundamental condition of existence. The self is whatever crossing of roles and memories happens to be activated in a given moment. Mr. Spitzer contains his dead brother Peron, the old violinist, all the old sportsmen, the recording angel, the loggerhead turtle, the walking concert program. He is, structurally, a crowd.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The entire encounter is built on layers of unreality that are treated with complete seriousness. The violinist plays an imaginary violin with an imaginary bow producing imaginary sound. Mr. Spitzer, who may himself be beyond ordinary hearing, responds to this non-sound with genuine emotion:

"it was the most beautiful music he had never heard, making his heart shake in the great silence as the tears came stinging to his eyes"

The music is more beautiful because it is not heard. The chapter explicitly argues that hearing would diminish it:

"something which would be less beautiful if heard by living ears, for it would lose in the hearing"

This is not a paradox the text tries to escape. It is its central thesis. The constructed, the imagined, the illusory is not a degraded version of the real; it is the primary form of the real. The cave is not a deficiency. It is the condition of all experience. Mr. Spitzer's dream of the sea shell that bit off his arm is treated with equal ontological weight as any waking event:

"if a thing had happened to him in a dream...it had happened to him, there was no doubt in Mr. Spitzer's mind"

The line between illusion and reality is not merely blurred here; it is declared structurally non-existent.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Grief in this chapter is not an episode. It is the atmospheric condition in which everything occurs. Mr. Spitzer's grief for his brother Peron saturates every perception, every association, every act of attention:

"Alas, Mr. Spitzer could remember no one unless he remembered his brother, with whom he was still obsessed and always would be — increasingly, no doubt — to the extent, he sometimes sensibly acknowledged, of an almost absolute madness"

Notice the word increasingly. Grief does not diminish with time in this world. It grows. It takes on new meanings, new dimensions, new forms. The dead brother is not behind Mr. Spitzer; he is inside every perception Mr. Spitzer has. The old violinist becomes a meditation on Peron. The old sportsmen become extensions of Peron's memory. The chapter makes this accumulative structure explicit:

"he had gained his understanding of life and was reminded of his brother by so many things that he would nearly forget himself"

And Peron's death itself, described as a "lyric leap", is not resolved, explained, or concluded. It hovers. It is the permanent weather through which everything else is perceived. The grief is also specifically described as something that grows old alongside the griever:

"It grows, and it even grows old."

This is grief as climate, not event. The honest life, as Radical Immanence demands, does not pretend otherwise.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Mr. Spitzer's primary activity throughout this chapter is watching. He does not intervene, does not speak, does not offer coins, does not ask questions. He witnesses, with extraordinary, almost painful attention:

"he had stood with his colorless mouth opened and his eyes rounded and his head turned to one side with an attitude of attentive listening"

The ethical weight of this posture is made explicit when the text explains why he does not applaud, does not speak, does not interrupt:

"he had hesitated — though several times, of course, nearly forgetting himself, he had nearly broken into an applause...and doubtless would have done so if he had not been afraid to interrupt the silence with a sound"

The silence is more important than his response to it. The witnessing is more important than the intervention. And crucially, the text acknowledges the epistemological humility that genuine witnessing requires; he cannot be sure whether the violinist is performing, dying, already dead, or having a fit:

"how could Mr. Spitzer know...how could he judge or know the difference between the living and the dead?"

The Witness does not resolve. The Witness stays in the room. Mr. Spitzer also articulates the anti-elucidation principle that is central to this pillar:

"a problem of existence was not illuminated — it was darkened by elucidation"

This is a direct philosophical statement of Radical Immanence's fourth pillar. To explain is to damage. To witness fully, without rushing to conclusion, is the ethical act.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Mr. Spitzer is described as always composing an elegy that will only be finished when he is dead. This is the recursive structure made explicit: the building continues precisely because it cannot be completed while the builder lives. The chapter itself performs this recursion stylistically: sentences return to their beginnings, qualifications are layered over qualifications, first impressions are corrected by second impressions which are then corrected again:

"as always when he checked with his first impressions, he must correct them by other impressions laid over the first, even if sometimes, after numerous corrections, returning to his first thoughts when he could remember them"

This is not indecision. This is the honest structure of thought operating in time. The building is provisional. The return is not failure but method. The chapter's extraordinary length and syntactic complexity, its refusal to arrive, is itself a formal enactment of recursive construction. Young's prose builds and returns and rebuilds throughout, mirroring Mr. Spitzer's own compositional process.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The unfinished is everywhere honored in this chapter. The elegy will be finished only in death. The musical notes written on Mr. Spitzer's shirt front are "fragments of reproachful phrases". The old violinist plays music that is "a music made of many approximate refrains", not complete themes but approximations. The chapter explicitly frames Mr. Spitzer's entire life as a perpetual preparation rather than an achievement:

"all his life, Mr. Spitzer had hovered, only preparing himself for the ultimate music"

This hovering is not failure. It is integrity. The demand for completion (for the concluded symphony, the finished elegy, the resolved grief) is implicitly identified as a violence against the actual texture of experience. The most beautiful music is the music never heard. The most honest composition is the one still being written. The walking concert program that Mr. Spitzer becomes is itself an image of Unfinished Is Honest: he is not the music, he is the announcement of the music, always pointing toward something that has not yet sounded.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Both Mr. Spitzer and the old violinist are figures of radical marginality. The violinist is described as a "musical derelict and outcast", "one of so many lost and indistinct musicians in a great city". His coat is torn "like that of a scarecrow". He plays without instrument, without audience, without payment, without recognition. Mr. Spitzer himself is a figure who barely inhabits the ordinary social world; he wears a black cape and pearl-buttoned spats, carries an ivory-headed cane, writes musical notes on his shirt front, and may or may not be able to hear.

The chapter peoples itself with marginal figures: old sportsmen discredited and broken, blind street-singers, fallen divas, a one-handed pianist who left his wooden hand playing after death, the old King of Cracow under the railway bridge. These are not curiosities or comic relief. They are, as Radical Immanence insists, evidence: evidence of the constructed nature of the categories that exclude them. The one-handed pianist playing on after death makes visible the arbitrary boundary between the living musician and the dead one. The silent violinist makes visible the arbitrary boundary between music and silence, between performance and madness.

The marginal figures in this chapter do not illustrate a thesis about marginality. They are the thesis. Their existence at the edges of category is where the constructed nature of all categories becomes most legible.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This is perhaps the pillar most organically present throughout the chapter. The past does not recede for Mr. Spitzer. It accumulates beneath every present moment, pressing upward:

"This was an old song which Mr. Spitzer, trembling when he thought of Joachim, remembered from his buried childhood"

The word buried is precise and important. The childhood is not gone. It is buried, which is to say, it is still there, beneath the surface, capable of being exhumed by a particular set of gestures. The old violinist's silent playing excavates it. The music "would be less beautiful if heard by living ears, for it would lose in the hearing", because what makes it powerful is precisely its sedimentary quality, its existence as something "scarcely ever heard in modern times", something that has sedimented down through time and now presses upward through the present moment.

Peron's death similarly does not recede. It is described as something that grows and grows old; it sediments, it accumulates meaning, it becomes more rather than less present. The chapter's description of Mr. Spitzer as covered in chalk marks and bird dung and accretions is a direct image of sedimentary time applied to the self; he is a surface on which time has deposited its layers visibly.

"as if he were streaked with chalk marks like some old cliff corroded by the tide — or covered with the accretions of whitened bird dung where the feathery clouds rested upon the pinnacle"

He is not a person moving through time. He is a geological formation that time has moved through.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter is formally self-aware about language as a constructive rather than descriptive act. The catalogue of transformations, bow to baseball bat to cue-stick to cane to staff to mallet to gavel to pole to mace to sceptre to baton, is not merely playful. It demonstrates that the name of a thing, the category assigned to it, determines what it is and what it can do. Change the word, change the ontology:

"reality was but this fugitive thing...It depended upon the moment. Reality was made up of just so many sticks and not one stick more"

The musical notes written on Mr. Spitzer's shirt front in "spidery musical hieroglyphs" are language that knows its own constructed, provisional status: fragments, approximations, notes toward something never completed. The chapter's own prose performs this: the sentences are so long, so heavily qualified, so layered with subordinate clauses that they draw attention to themselves as constructions, as things made rather than things found.

The text also explicitly acknowledges that language shapes what can exist:

"he thought he himself was only a walking musical concert program to remind all people that what they were, they were not"

Language here is not description but negation and possibility simultaneously; it marks the gap between what is named and what is real, and that gap is where meaning lives.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The transcendence in this chapter is entirely immanent. There is no beyond, no other world, no resolution in a higher realm. The sacred is located precisely in the ordinary, the broken, the marginal, the silent. The most transcendent musical experience Mr. Spitzer has ever had occurs on Library steps, in a city, watching a ragged old man mime playing an instrument he does not have:

"Never had he been — at least for many years — so shaken, so moved — yet serene."

The serenity is not otherworldly. It is produced by maximum attention to this world: to these gestures, this silence, these clouds, this old man. The chapter's imagery consistently locates the sacred in the immanent: the bellwether's bell, the tinkling prisms, the sea shells buzzing like bees, the pebbles that "never sang before" suddenly singing. These are not symbols pointing outward to transcendence. They are the transcendence itself, located in the ordinary world's capacity for unexpected revelation.

The description of music that "came before consciousness, before the ear was added to the man, before the ear was the entrance to the soul" (and its counterpart, music that comes "after the ear was no more") frames the sacred as something that brackets ordinary consciousness from both sides, not something located above it. The transcendence is deeper immanence, not escape from it.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is the pillar that gives the chapter its deepest structure. Mr. Spitzer is explicitly described as constituted by what came before him (his brother, his childhood, Joachim, the buried music, the old sportsmen) and as constituting the ground for what comes after:

"he thought he himself was only a walking musical concert program to remind all people that what they were, they were not"

He carries the past forward into the present. He is not a self moving through time but a crossing point where multiple temporal streams intersect. The sediment of his brother's death presses upward through every present perception. The buried childhood music surfaces through the old violinist's gestures. The one-handed pianist's wooden hand plays on after the musician is dead: the crossing point persists beyond the individual life.

The chapter's most explicit statement of this structural obligation comes in the description of the abacus:

"he was keeping the score, always keeping the score, counting, counting, counting to the numbers beyond the numbers"

The keeping of the score is not chosen. It is structural. Mr. Spitzer does not decide to remember Peron, to carry the buried music, to witness the old violinist. He is constituted as the place where these things are carried. The obligation is not ethical in the ordinary sense; it is ontological. He is the crossing point. The carrying continues because he continues, and when he stops, the wooden hand will play on.
