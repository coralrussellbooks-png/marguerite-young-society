Chapter 4: The Long Sleep of Empty Years

← Chapter 3 · Guide Home · Chapter 5 →

Research notes: Chapter 4

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · Suicide in History and Literature · The Bible · The History of Opium

You have just spent Chapter 3 falling a little in love with Miss MacIntosh. Young made sure of that. She gave you the red hair and the black umbrella and the fireflies in a jar, the corset and the sensible shoes and the cold morning swims. She gave you a woman who was the truest thing in the house.

And then she reminded you, as she did in Chapter 1, and now again here, that Miss MacIntosh is gone.

Chapter 4 is about what that loss does to Vera. And it is, in its quiet way, one of the most honest portraits of grief in the novel.

What actually happens in Chapter 4

Miss MacIntosh has disappeared. Her clothes were on the beach. “The two pennies were in her purse.” Vera sits by the shore for what feels like years: crying, barely functioning, her body described as “slothful and immense,” her mind “employed by nothing but the dream.” She tries to write a book. She lights fires on the beach. She talks to fishermen and sailors, asking questions no one can answer.

Her mother, Catherine, watches this and makes her own diagnosis: Vera is losing her sanity. Catherine’s solution is the one she has always had: the opium, the withdrawal, the beautiful obliteration. She offers Vera a vision of eternal youth and dreamless sleep, jewels and supernatural servants, an “opium paradise” where nothing can hurt her anymore. She is, in her way, trying to be a good mother. This is simply the only medicine she knows.

Vera refuses. She leaves by way of Grand Central Station, heading toward What Cheer, Iowa, and whatever she might find there.

On the way, she encounters “a fat, soft-faced lady on” a train who offers her a warning: the place she is going is desolate. Out of this world. “No social life, no communication, no human” warmth. Just cold and silence and decay. The golden bowl, the lady says, is broken there.

Young also gives us, in the middle of all this, a brief and haunting story about a young woman who jumped from the Brooklyn Bridge in her wedding veil, suitcase in hand, on her way to be married. She disappears into the dark water. The novel doesn’t linger on it. It doesn’t need to.

What Vera is thinking about

Grief is the engine of this chapter, but Young is not interested in simple grief. She is interested in what grief does to a person over time, how it can become its own kind of opium, its own withdrawal from the world.

Vera has been lost before. She grew up lost, in a house where reality was optional. Miss MacIntosh was the thing that tethered her. And now the tether is gone, and Vera is discovering something terrifying: that without it, she is capable of floating away just as her mother did. That the boundary between herself and Catherine is thinner than she thought.

This is why Catherine’s offer of opium is so dangerous in this chapter, not because it is obviously wrong, but because it is almost tempting. Vera can see the appeal. She can see how a person ends up in that bed, in those dreams, in that beautiful nowhere. She is close enough to the edge to feel the pull.

What saves her, if anything saves her, is the stubbornness of Miss MacIntosh’s memory. The ivory knitting needles she finds in the sand. The fire she lights on the beach. The specific, physical, irreducible realness of the woman she is looking for. You cannot grieve a dream the way Vera grieves Miss MacIntosh. This grief is proof that something was real.

So she gets on a train. She goes toward Iowa. She carries her grief with her like a suitcase.

The woman on the Brooklyn Bridge

Young drops this story into the chapter without explanation or fanfare, and it is easy to read past it. Don’t.

A young woman a working class, about to be married, carrying her suitcase and wearing her veil jumps from the Brooklyn Bridge into the East River. She is never named. We are told very little about her. And then Young moves on.

But the image stays. A woman in a wedding veil, the symbol of a life about to begin, choosing instead the dark water. Young is placing this alongside Vera’s own crisis point: the moment when the pull of oblivion is strongest. She is not saying Vera will make the same choice. She is saying this is the landscape they all inhabit, these women in this novel: a landscape where the alternative to the life you have been handed is sometimes the river.

The reality of women’s lives in the era Young is writing about is part of what this image carries. Marriage was not simply an option, it was, for most women, the entire structure of a viable life. There was no other architecture. The woman on the bridge was on her way to that architecture. And she chose the water instead. Young does not judge her. She just shows you the bridge.

Catherine, again and why she matters here

It would be easy to read Catherine as simply a cautionary tale: this is what happens when you surrender to illusion. But Chapter 4 asks you to look at her more carefully.

Catherine genuinely loves Vera. The opium paradise she offers her daughter is not cruelty, it is the best gift she has. She has found a way to stop hurting, and she wants to give it to the person she loves most. The jewels, the supernatural servants, the eternal youth: these are not a trap she is setting. They are her idea of heaven.

What Young is doing here is complicated: she is asking you to feel the appeal of what Catherine is offering, even as you understand why Vera must refuse it. Illusion is not always ugly. Sometimes it is gorgeous. Sometimes it is the only mercy a person has found. The novel does not let you dismiss Catherine. It makes you understand her.

Vera understands her. That is part of what makes leaving so hard.

The fat, soft-faced lady on the train

She arrives as a voice of experience, warning Vera about where she is headed. What Cheer or wherever Vera is going is not what she imagines. It is cold and silent and the community has collapsed in on itself. The golden bowl is broken.

She is not wrong, exactly. But notice what she represents: the voice that says don’t go. The voice of reasonable caution, of hard-won disillusionment, of the person who has already made the journey and come back diminished. Vera listens to her. And then goes anyway.

This is who Vera is. This is, arguably, who she has to be.

What to watch for as you keep reading

Light spoilers: This section hints at what happens later in the novel. Skip it if you want to meet the story fresh.

The ivory knitting needles. Vera finds them in the sand, Miss MacIntosh’s, left behind like everything else. She keeps them. They become a kind of relic, a physical proof of a life that was real. Watch what happens to objects in this novel. Young uses them the way a poet uses images: precisely, with accumulated weight.

Vera’s fires on the beach. She lights them to signal to Miss MacIntosh, to call her back, to mark the place of loss. Fire in this novel is almost always about wanting to be seen across a great distance. Notice when it appears and who is trying to reach whom.

Iowa’s cold. When Vera remembers Miss MacIntosh’s descriptions of What Cheer (the frozen ears, the frozen horses, the frozen smoke) she is not just receiving travel information. She is receiving a portrait of a life stripped of illusion all the way down to the bone. Iowa, in this novel, is reality at its most unadorned. That is why she has to go there.

Lazarus. Young references the Biblical story of a man raised from the dead after four days in the tomb. Vera is not thinking about miracles here, she is thinking about the desire for one. About the hope that what has disappeared might come back. About the difference between resurrection and delusion. The novel will keep returning to this question.

What to hold onto

Chapter 4 can feel like a long dark passage: Vera at her lowest, pulled toward oblivion, surrounded by people and images that say give up. If you feel the weight of it, that is intentional. Young wants you in the fog with Vera.

But here is what to hold onto: Vera gets on the train. After all of it, the years of grief, the mother’s beautiful offer of oblivion, the warning from the fat soft-faced lady, she gets on the train and goes toward What Cheer, Iowa. She carries her loss with her and she keeps moving.

That is not nothing. In this novel, that is almost everything.
