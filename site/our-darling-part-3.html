Marguerite Young, Our Darling, Part 3: What It Reveals About Young and Her Work

← Part 2 · Marguerite Young, Our Darling: What It Reveals, Part by Part · Part 4 →

Source: Marguerite Young, Our Darling: Tributes and Essays, edited by Miriam Fuchs (Dalkey Archive Press, 1994). Part 3 completes Jean Petrolle's essay, then gives Reinhard Friederich's account of rereading the novel, Erika Duncan's essay on Esther Longtree, and the opening of Miriam Fuchs's essay on Young's utopias. This is the most critically dense stretch of the book so far. Only the material that illuminates Young and her work is kept below; personal digression and pure praise are left out. Quoted phrases from the book are marked with quotation marks.

Imagination makes reality, so fictions have stakes

Petrolle closes her reading by drawing the ethical edge out of the novel's premise. If imagination and language actually produce the reality a person lives in, then the fictions a culture chooses matter. She distinguishes two kinds of fiction-maker in the book. Catherine Cartwheel knowingly creates her own fictions and so lives inside beautiful ones; Miss MacIntosh accepts the culture's ready-made fictions, the supposed facts of geography, history, science, and morality, without recognizing them as fictions at all, and so cannot escape the ugliness they impose on her, down to her belief that her own baldness is ugly. From this Petrolle raises the book's implicit challenge to the inherited canon: if writers, readers, and dreamers determine reality by incantation, why keep telling the old stories in which only men are adventurers and only women are raped. She places Young in a modernist strand marked by formal experiment and dense self-reflexive texture, alongside H.D., Joyce, Anna Kavan, Nabokov, Proust, Dorothy Richardson, and Woolf, and finds its philosophical cousins in Wittgenstein, Santayana, Dewey, and Mead, thinkers who put imagination and language at the center of being.

How the novel is built, and how to read it

Reinhard Friederich's essay is the most useful firsthand account of what reading Miss MacIntosh is actually like. He had failed to finish the fourteen-hundred-page novel four or five times over twenty-five years, always stalling partway. What finally worked was reading fast, a “leap-and-linger” motion that matched the book's own central movement, the rapid bus journey through night and day carrying interlaced memories and dreams. He describes the prose as a cumulative, symphonic thing, “a fractal method of harmonizing variations” that refuses to be recapitulated or excerpted, so that reading dissolves into a kind of seeing. His governing term is the novel's “life-world,” a totality that has to be taken in whole before analysis is possible, and he ranks the book with Tristram Shandy, Broch's Death of Virgil, Williams's Paterson, Murasaki's Genji, and Dostoevsky. Within that flow he singles out the novel's range of registers: the frog-musician grotesque, which opens as a tall tale in the American vein of Twain and Faulkner before turning meditative, and the hangman's tale, evidence of Young's “terse precision” when she wants it. His conclusion is thematic as well as technical: in this book loss is what creates splendor, and nothing concludes so much as it opens. He ends by placing Young with Whitman, Sandburg, and Dickinson, and even with Bessie Smith and Charlie Parker, as a maker of a democratic and difficult joy.

Esther Longtree and pregnancy as an epic subject

Erika Duncan's essay centers on Esther Longtree, the novel's perpetually pregnant woman, forever carrying the never-to-be-born and the shadow child. Setting aside Duncan's personal frame, the critical claim is striking: she argues that Young may have written serious literature's first full exploration of pregnancy, and that it could only be done inside an epic of this scale. Duncan reads the roughly hundred-page Esther section as the fruition of everything seeded across the earlier thousand pages, built by doubling and told largely in reverse, so that the murder that would explain everything surfaces only long after the reader feels its weight. She connects it to Adrienne Rich's Of Woman Born: it is precisely Esther's near-unconscious killing of her one live-born child that opens the many-sided treatment of the pregnant psyche, widening to take in all who have borne children and all who never have. Duncan also supplies a working detail, that while writing the novel Young was immersed in her biography of James Whitcomb Riley, “the little orphan Annie poet,” and drawn to figures like Fourier, the apostle of universal love who died alone with only his cats. The essay's closing sickbed scene yields two of Young's own lines: that the whole enterprise is “all a matter of research,” and, in Duncan's phrase, that Young was always “an opponent of the medium and the mean.”

Utopia, loss, and the value of the incomplete

Miriam Fuchs's essay reads all of Young's work as utopian in a particular sense: it enacts both the longing for an ideal and the impossibility of reaching it. Young fills her books with bungled jobs, confused messages, lost causes, and abandoned paradises, from the failed communities of George Rapp and Robert Owen at New Harmony in Angel in the Forest to Miss MacIntosh walking off into the Atlantic, leaving behind a corset, a red wig, and glassless spectacles. Fuchs argues that for Young partial success is the only success there is, and that her characters' broken visions are, in the novel's own words, “more complete in their incompletion than if they had been whole.” She points to a revealing habit: Young has said she never managed to finish the story of a flour miller trying to pass his thumbprint to his son, so she has folded it into each successive manuscript, never telling it whole, because the whole version would be the illusion. Creation is always flawed, and Young enacts the flaw rather than hiding it, echoing the undertaker in Angel in the Forest who warns that no one can remove the flaws from the universe without destroying it.

Against binary logic and the single self

Fuchs frames Young's deepest subversion as an assault on the either/or categories of Western thought, the fixed oppositions of masculine and feminine, order and chaos, self and other. In Young's world every soul is also the other soul, so nothing is singular; her characters are mutable, never fully themselves and never quite the other either. Fuchs aligns this with Hélène Cixous's critique of how fiction “fetishizes” a single privileged self, and reads Catherine Cartwheel's dissolving first person, the wealthy opium addict who says she is eating herself, is the divan and the mirror and Brooklyn Bridge, as prose that refuses to settle on any coherent subject. Passages like this run for chapters, doubling and multiplying their images rather than resolving.

Her own claim, and her place outside the canon

Fuchs uses Young's own words to explain why the late turn to biography is no departure. Young insists the clean lines separating poetry, prose, fiction, nonfiction, realism, and experiment belong “not to writers, but to critics,” because the poetry of nonfiction is as fabulous as any poetry she could write in fiction. Fuchs is candid that this refusal of category has cost Young her standing: as an experimental writer who is also a woman, she is twice removed from the mainstream, and despite William Goyen's 1965 praise of the novel as a work of “stunning magnitude and beauty,” she remains neglected by critics and unsanctioned by the canon. The book gathers the counterclaims of her admirers: Duncan calls the work an epic elegy for all creation, and Balakian ranks Young among the most innovative novelists of the age, what Gertrude Stein was to its beginning but, in Balakian's phrase, “so much more human, more poetic, visionary.”
