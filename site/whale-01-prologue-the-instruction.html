Be Bak in a Whale

Prologue: The Instruction

Be Bak in a Whale · Chapter One. Not One Self: I Will Kill You If You Tell →

Characters: Esther Longtree · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

“If you don’t have obsessions, don’t write.”

Marguerite Young

“I believe that all my work explores the human desire or obsession for utopias,

and the structure of all my works is the search for utopias lost and rediscovered.

All my writing is about the recognition that there is no single reality.

But the beauty of it is that you nevertheless go on, walking towards utopia,

which may not exist, on a bridge which might end before you reach the other side.”

Marguerite Young

I embraced obsession. I became obsessed with this book. – Coral Russell

⁂

The first time I read Miss MacIntosh, My Darling I was drowning. Not in the romantic sense that gets used about difficult books, the pleasurable submersion, the willingness to be overwhelmed. I mean the other kind. Face just above the water. Treading as hard and fast as I could. Going under anyway. The feeling was still so strong after the third reading of the book that I made a meme about it.

I had read all the big books. I am not afraid of big books and I love the ones considered difficult. Difficulty is not the problem. I had also spent my professional life teaching people to read and write, which means I had built my identity on a particular belief that I knew what I was doing with a book. That if I brought sufficient attention and sufficient stubbornness to any text, I would find my way through it.

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling put that belief underwater alongside me.

There was confusion. There was, I will say it because it is true and because Young would want the honest account, there was boredom. There were stretches where I could not locate the thread, could not find the current that was supposed to carry me forward, and I wondered whether there was a message here at all, or whether I had finally met the book that had defeated me. I am completely stubborn. My nickname was Pitbull. When I latch on to something I do not let go. I do not acknowledge or accept that there are things that can’t be done. I do not walk away from books. But I also could not understand this one, and I had no framework yet for understanding why.

And then I reached the last two paragraphs.

I became emotional in a way I could not explain. It would take eight years and five readings of the book before I had the language to explain it. I read the last line. And I knew, without being able to say why I knew, that I would be reading and studying this book until I die. I had found something. I did not have a single clear word for what I had found. I knew only that I had found it, and that the finding had happened below the level of understanding, in some place that understanding had not yet reached.

That experience reorganized something in me about reading itself. I had spent a lifetime reading everything, moving from book to book, accumulating. And what Young's last page made me understand, before I understood anything else about the novel, was that all of that moving might have been a form of consumption. That the big books are saying something and you need to pick one and stay with it. That immensity is not the same as depth, and that I had been measuring the wrong dimension.

This was my book. I did not choose it. It chose me in the way that the things that matter choose you by refusing to let you go even when you haven't yet understood what is holding you.

⁂

I grew up in a small town. I knew from early on, the way certain children know, that I was going to leave. Not because the town was bad or the people were lesser, but because I had already made the decision that Vera Cartwheel made at fourteen: that life was going to be a grand adventure or nothing at all. Helen Keller said it first. I claimed it young and held it hard. The circumscribed life was the one thing I was unwilling to accept.

And then Young gave me Esther.

Esther was the character I kept returning to across five readings, the one I could not fully account for, the one who would not resolve into something manageable. A waitress. A person of circumscribed circumstances, real pain, real limitation, the kind of inner story you tell yourself when the outer story has been largely decided by the conditions you were born into. I grew up knowing people like Esther. I knew the texture of that life, the dignity of it and also the grief of it, the way imagination becomes not a launching pad but a dwelling place when there is nowhere to launch toward.

I could understand all the other characters in some provisional way. I could locate them. Esther kept slipping away from me. Not because she was obscure or underdeveloped, but because Young was doing something with her that I could feel before I could see it, something that had to do with what it means to witness a life that the world has decided does not count as a life worth witnessing.

Young ends the novel with Esther. Not with Vera, who made the grand gesture, who threw the wedding at the end that invited everyone and everything and left no one out of the accounting. With Esther. The waitress. The one who is going out to lunch. The one who will hang a sign in the restaurant window.

Owt to luntsch. bee bak in a whale.

For she could not spell either.

I read that line the first time and felt something I could not name. I read it the second time and began to understand that the misspellings were not incidental. I read it the third time and understood that Young had put the entire philosophy into those two spelling errors. I read it the fourth time and saw Jonah's whale: the creature that swallows you and keeps you alive in the dark and eventually returns you, transformed, to the world. I read it the fifth time and understood that Esther going back into the whale is not a form of defeat. It is an honest life. You write the note as best you can. You go back in. You keep going.

It wasn't until after multiple readings that Jonah's whale surfaced in my understanding. But intrinsically, on the first reading, before I had any of the language for it, I knew the book was calling me back to the beginning. That the last line was not an ending. That it was an instruction.

⁂

This book is the record of five readings and everything they built.

It is not a reading guide. The guide exists as a free PDF (A Guide to Reading Miss MacIntosh, My Darling) that moves through all eighty-two chapters and holds the reader's hand through the novel's considerable demands. It is not a philosophical treatise. AND: The Philosophy of Marguerite Young (also available as a free PDF) makes the case that Young built a complete philosophical system and names its five load-bearing pillars. Both of those books were necessary steps. This book is the step they were building toward.

What this book does is go inside the construction. It takes each of the five pillars and returns to the novel to show exactly how Young builds them. I share my personal experiences while reading the novel that connected with it. Like all readers do. It is reading in the oldest and most demanding sense with the reader and the text in the same room with full attention brought to what is on the page and what Young is actually doing there.

I am the right person to write it for one reason that has nothing to do with credentials and everything to do with the fault line I walked in carrying before I opened the novel the first time and I came to Young as Vera and stayed because of Esther. I was the reader who chose the grand adventure and spent five readings learning to truly see the person who didn't get to choose. That tension is not my backstory. It is the epistemological ground of everything that follows.

Young told her students: if you don't have obsessions, don't write. She was right about that the way she was right about everything: not as advice but as description. The obsession is not the engine of the work. It is the epistemological condition without which the work cannot see what it needs to see.

I have been inside this novel for years. Each reading found what the previous reading had walked past. That is not a testament to the book's difficulty, though it is that too. It is a testament to what Young built: a structure so complete and so internally consistent that it keeps generating new surfaces to read, new depths to descend to, new ways of understanding what you thought you had already understood.

The questions the first reading left me with was, what is it? What did I find on that last page that I couldn't name but couldn't walk away from?

Five chapters follow. They are the answer.

We begin, as Young begins, at the beginning. A woman on a bus. A journey without arrival. A nurse who turns out to be a wig. And the question underneath all of it, the one that will take 1,198 pages to ask properly and five chapters here to show how it was asked: what do you do when the solid ground turns out to be a performance, the foundation a construction, and the real Miss MacIntosh nothing but a wig on the back of a crab?

I never belonged anywhere until I read this book. Not because it gave me somewhere to belong. Because it gave me the philosophy I had already been living without knowing what to call it. Young’s system was the first framework I had ever encountered that was capacious enough to hold the full complexity of who I am without asking me to simplify. The not-belonging was never the wound. It was the condition of seeing. And this novel, which also belongs nowhere and refuses every category that would contain it, was the proof.

The reader who picks up this book and feels the same recognition deserves to find that sentence waiting for them. Because that reader exists. I know they exist because I was that reader and there was nothing waiting for me.

This is what was waiting.
