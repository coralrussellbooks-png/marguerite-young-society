The political geology of cotton, wool, and dye (Pillar 8)

I. The Philosophy: Your Fiber Has a History and It Did Not Go Away

It is comforting to treat a skein of yarn as a fresh, neutral beginning. You buy it, you open it, you start. Whatever happened before it reached your hands feels like someone else's story, finished and behind us. Radical Immanence says time does not work that way. The past does not recede behind you as you move forward. It accumulates, layer by layer, pressing down on the present. Historical injustice is not a past event. It is a geological fact about the ground you are standing on right now. You cannot build fairly on ground you are pretending is neutral.

The fiber in your hand is not a blank starting point. It is the top layer of a deep stack. Cotton carries the long history of plantation slavery and the colonial systems built to grow and move it cheaply. Wool carries the enclosures, the moment commons were fenced off and people were pushed off shared land to make room for sheep that were worth more than they were. Dye carries colonial extraction, the indigo and cochineal routes, and later the chemical runoff that still colors rivers downstream of the mills. None of this is a footnote to the material. It is the material's actual composition, as real as the fiber content on the label.

This is not an argument for guilt or for refusing to make anything. It is an argument for honesty. When you act as if your cotton arrived from nowhere, you are building on ground you have agreed to pretend is neutral, and that pretense is exactly how the old harm stays buried and keeps working. To excavate the substrate is to ask where this came from and let the answer change how you handle it. The honest maker does not get to start clean. The honest maker starts by knowing what is underfoot.

II. Deep Journal Prompts

The Origin You Don't Ask About. Pick the fiber you reach for most often without thinking. Write down everything you actually know about where it came from: the animal or plant, the land, the people who grew or processed it, the dye, the distance it traveled. Then write down how much of that is a real answer and how much is a brand story printed to keep you from asking. Sit with the gap. What does it cost the work to not know?

The Cheap Skein. Write about a material you buy because it is cheap. Trace, as honestly as you can, why it is cheap. Cheapness is never free; someone or somewhere absorbed the cost. Without flattening into a lecture, follow the price down through the layers until you reach the ground it was paid from. Then decide, plainly, what you want to do with that knowledge.

III. Studio Fiber Exercises

The Material Core Sample

Choose one fiber you are about to work with and trace it back as far as you genuinely can before you make a single stitch. Read the label, then go past the label. Find the farm, the mill, the country, the dye source. Where the trail goes cold, mark exactly where your knowledge ends, because that gap is part of the truth too. Build a small sample that records the journey as layers, the oldest origin at the bottom, your own hands at the top, with a marked band for everything you could not find out. The piece is a core sample of the material's history, including the parts that were hidden from you.

The Honestly Sourced Piece

Make one piece using only materials whose history you can actually name out loud: local fleece you know the farm of, plant dye you gathered or can trace, undyed thread, unraveled yarn from a worn-out garment with its own known past. The constraint will be hard and will make the piece smaller and plainer than you wanted. That smallness is the lesson. Notice how much of your usual practice depends on not knowing, and let this one honest piece show you what working on known ground feels like.

IV. Cross-Medium Interference

The Layered Label

Write the supply chain of one finished piece as a timeline that reads from the deep past up to your hands, every stage you know and every stage you do not. Stitch or print it onto a label and attach it to the work so it travels with the piece. Most labels tell a clean origin story meant to sell. This one tells the geology. When someone reads it, they meet the cloth standing on its actual ground, not the ground the market wanted them to imagine.
