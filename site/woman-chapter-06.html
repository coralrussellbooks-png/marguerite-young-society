Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 6

← Chapter 5 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 7 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Homer Capehorn · Jacqueline White · Madge Capehorn · Miss MacIntosh

Madge's long chapter is where the book first shows a self coming apart in real time. It is the coming-apart as a catalog of what patriarchy does to women and the structure underneath it is plural selfhood. Madge is "herself and the other," she is "breaking into two," her thoughts run "like a mouse in an intricate cage," and when she dances she dances "with her shadow." She is not one person suffering through an institution. She is a person the book has already split, and the splitting is the subject.

Marriage is where the split is forced, and the chapter treats it as the demand that a divided self perform as one. Madge's marriage is "empty and hollow and ghostly," the wedding bells are funeral bells, the "circle of creation" is a wedding ring and a grave at once. It is marriage as social death and it runs the wedding and the funeral together, the same fusion Chapter 10 will stage openly, so that marriage becomes the place where the boundary between living and dead gives way. The "dead child dancing" and the grinning skull under Madge's painted face are that boundary made visible.

Her resistance to the pregnancy belongs to the interrupted birth. She would give birth, she says, only "to an urn, to a stone angel," and she is "coming to her early grave"; the pregnancy is dread and the birth is death, the shape the book gives every pregnancy it touches. The armor she fashions against all this, the "golden mail" and "mailed gloves," is the self as mask, the same armor Miss MacIntosh wears as helmet-hair and corset.

The chapter's long meditation on Jacqueline White, the dying rival, is where the illusion of the individual surfaces. The men's love for Jacqueline is "lost in a crowd," the triangle of Madge, Homer, and the dead girl keeps folding into new triangles, and Homer reframes death itself as "birth" and eternal youth. It is the male denial of female mortality and love with no single object, selves that will not stay distinct, and death and birth trading places. Homer is not failing to understand Madge. He is speaking the book's thesis without knowing it.
