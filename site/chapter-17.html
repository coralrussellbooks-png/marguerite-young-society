Summary

Miss MacIntosh is bald, her skull gleaming like an ivory ball in the moonlight. Vera, shocked and half triumphant, cries out that she is a man. A struggle follows, real or partly imagined. Vera is knocked down, lifted, and laid on the bed, and drifts in and out of consciousness, wondering whether she is wrestling a dummy, a gladiator, or the angel Jacob met. In the morning her braids are neatly plaited, and the only scratches on her cheeks could have come from leafless branches.

Key passages

From the research

  • Miss MacIntosh is portrayed almost as a spectral, otherworldly figure standing by the tall windows with blowing curtains. Her corset stays resemble quills of a porcupine or rays of moonlight, and her head is replaced by a "cold and dented" moonlike dome. This surreal image evokes a sense of alienation and transformation, suggesting that she is no longer fully human or recognizable. The metaphor of her body being "more angular than curved" and "ready to meet the opponent" sets the tone of confrontation and tension.
  • The silence, the white curtains blowing like wings, and the "faint tappings of a spectral branch" create an eerie atmosphere, hinting at supernatural or psychological disturbance. This passage is important because it introduces the unsettling and uncanny presence of Miss MacIntosh, foreshadowing the dramatic events to follow.
  • A key moment is the revelation that Miss MacIntosh is bald "as the egg or as the rock where nothing grows," her face "absolutely impersonal and expressionless." This stark image symbolizes loss of identity, vitality, and femininity. Vera is horrified by this transformation, seeing her as a "monster" or a "phantasmagoric being," a "bald pretender" who deceives the senses.
  • The contrast with the memory of her "red, shining hair, tawny as an August sunset," her eyeglasses, and umbrella underscores the dramatic change, suggesting that this is either a ghostly apparition or a symbolic representation of death, decay, or betrayal.
  • This passage is crucial as it marks the psychological crisis of Vera, confronting a beloved figure who is now alien and threatening, evoking themes of identity, disguise, and existential fear.
  • Vera reflects on proverbs and parables, such as "the mountains shall never meet with the mountains," "you shall praise the bridge that carried you," and "man is not born to happiness." These reflections highlight the philosophical and existential underpinnings of the chapter, emphasizing themes of inevitability, loss, and the fragile nature of human happiness.
  • Vera struggles with the notion that either Miss MacIntosh has changed or they themselves have changed, questioning the nature of reality and perception. This self-doubt and questioning of identity deepen the psychological tension and ambiguity.
  • This extended passage details a physical and symbolic struggle between Vera and the bald figure (Miss MacIntosh or a phantom). The fight is described in vivid, almost cinematic terms, with references to boxing moves ("left jab," "straight right," "rabbit punch") and wrestling.
  • Vera experiences disorientation, pain, and a sense of duality ("my body seemed divided as if now there were two of us"). The imagery of stars falling "like dried seed pods," "dark horsemen," and a black "cloud extending from pole to pole" evokes apocalyptic or mythic grandeur.
  • Importantly, Vera questions the identity of the attacker, wondering if it is a "fallen angel," a "Christian knight," or merely a "sandbag dummy come to life." This uncertainty blurs the line between reality, dream, and hallucination.
  • This confrontation symbolizes an internal psychic battle, possibly reflecting trauma, fear of loss, or the struggle with self-identity and acceptance of harsh truths.
  • Vera describes being overpowered, feeling hair pulled out "by handfuls," yet experiencing no pain but a speculative wonder about being "snatched bald." This loss of hair symbolizes a profound loss of beauty, femininity, and power, likened to a "terrible mistake, God’s error."
  • Despite this, Vera notes that "the next morning, of course, my long braids were exactly braided down my back," suggesting the experience may have been a dream, vision, or hallucination, blurring reality and illusion.
  • This passage is significant as it encapsulates the chapter's themes of identity loss, transformation, and the ambiguous boundary between nightmare and waking life.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Miss MacIntosh

  • Initially described as a familiar and trusted figure, a nursemaid, a guardian.
  • Transforms into a bald, expressionless, ghostly figure, described as “bald as the egg or as the rock where nothing grows,” symbolizing loss, change, or an unsettling void.
  • Portrayed with contradictory imagery: both fragile and monstrously strong, spectral yet physically violent.
  • Vera struggles to reconcile this apparition with the Miss MacIntosh they knew, suspecting deception and a hidden identity.
  • Symbolizes a “bald pretender,” a false self, or a shadowy force challenging Vera.

Vera Cartwheel

  • A young girl, around fourteen years old, caught between terror and fascination.
  • Experiences a physical and psychological battle with the transformed Miss MacIntosh.
  • Reflects on memories, identity, and the nature of illusion versus reality.
  • Shows moments of wild laughter, despair, and confusion, indicating a complex emotional state.
  • Vera’s perception is unreliable or dreamlike, blending corporeal reality with surreal visions.

Research: Themes

Identity and Transformation

See: Identity and the Self

  • Miss MacIntosh’s sudden baldness and altered demeanor symbolize a profound change, calling into question the stability of identity.
  • Vera’s inability to accept this change reflects a struggle with understanding the self and others.
  • The motif of hair loss as a loss of femininity or vitality is potent and unsettling.

Illusion vs. Reality

See: Illusion and Reality

  • The chapter blurs the lines between waking life, dream, and hallucination.
  • Vera questions what is real, what is illusion, and the nature of perception.
  • The fight with the “bald opponent” may also be symbolic, reflecting an internal conflict or a confrontation with an aspect of the self.

Fear and Madness

See: Madness and Sanity · Fear and Courage

  • The setting at midnight, the eerie descriptions, and the “zero hour” evoke a sense of dread.
  • Vera’s fear escalates into a violent struggle, suggesting a descent into madness and trauma.
  • The chapter explores how strange events can “leave us mad” or shatter sanity.

Power and Vulnerability

  • Miss MacIntosh’s physical power contrasts with her ghostly, fragile appearance.
  • Vera, though young and vulnerable, refuses to be defeated quietly.
  • The battle becomes a metaphor for the fight between opposing forces within or external threats.

Memory and Perception

See: Memory and the Past

  • Vera recalls past moments with Miss MacIntosh, contrasting them with the present terrifying vision.
  • There is an awareness that memory and perception can deceive or change over time.
  • Vera’s reflections on proverbs and parables hint at larger philosophical questions.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Baldness

See: Hair, Wig, and Baldness

  • Represents loss, emptiness, exposure, and transformation.
  • Compared to “the egg,” “the rock where nothing grows,” “the rose stripped of all its petals,” and “bare as the winter winds,” emphasizing barrenness and desolation.

Moonlight and Darkness

See: Moon and Stars · Lamps, Candles, and Light

  • The moonlight creates a cold, distant atmosphere.
  • The interplay of light and shadow symbolizes the tension between knowledge and ignorance, reality and illusion.

Corset and Armor

See: Clothing, Hats, and Skirts

  • Miss MacIntosh’s metallic corset is described like a battleship or wrestler’s gear, symbolizing restraint, protection, and conflict readiness.

Animal Imagery

  • Falada, Vera’s old horse, a present from Mr. Spitzer, represents loyalty and alertness but is powerless.
  • Vera’s comparison of Miss MacIntosh to a “codfish swept up upon a barren shore” and “old man” adds to the grotesque transformation.

Physical Combat

  • The fight is depicted with detailed boxing terminology (rabbit punch, jab, left hook), emphasizing the visceral and brutal nature of the struggle.
  • The encounter may also symbolize internal psychological conflict or a battle with one’s fears or identity.

Stream of Consciousness

  • The narration flows with intense, fragmented, and poetic sentences reflecting Vera’s emotional turmoil.

Surreal and Symbolic

  • The imagery is often dreamlike, blending the real with the fantastical.

Emotional Intensity

  • The tone fluctuates between terror, disbelief, wild laughter, and despair.

Use of Parables and Philosophical Reflections

See: Mirrors and Reflections

  • Vera contemplates proverbs and existential questions, adding depth and universality.

Research: Resources

  • King Magnus was an illegitimate son who became king of Norway and Denmark. He functions as a symbolic figure representing power, challenge, and the monumental nature of Vera's confrontation with Miss MacIntosh's transformed, imposing presence
  • The “holy angel with whom Jacob struggled at Peniel” is in Genesis 32:22-32 and Hosea 12:3-5 It symbolizes a universal confrontation with the self or with higher powers, embodying the challenges of self-knowledge, division within the soul, and the search for unity and resolution in a fragmented world.
