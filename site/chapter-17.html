To All My Darlings

Chapter 17

← Chapter 16 · Home · Chapter 18 →

Summary

In the moonlight Miss MacIntosh stands by the window, and Vera sees that she is bald, “bald as the egg or as the rock where nothing grows,” with “only one breast.” Vera knows her only by her broken nose. Terrified and wildly triumphant at having always pictured her with a mustache and cast her as the bridegroom, Vera screams, “Miss MacIntosh, you are a man!” A struggle follows, told in the language of the boxing ring. Vera is knocked down, her mouth covered by “his hoary hand,” and laid on the bed, and she wonders whether her attacker is a fallen angel, a gladiator, the sandbag dummy come to life, or the angel Jacob wrestled. She calls it the work of someone “who should take advantage of a poor, ignorant girl, only fourteen years old.” She calls for her mother and for Mr. Spitzer, but no one comes. In the morning her braids are exactly braided, and the only scratches on her cheeks could have come from leafless branches.

Key passages

From the research

  • Miss MacIntosh is portrayed almost as a spectral, otherworldly figure standing by the tall windows with blowing curtains. Her corset stays resemble quills of a porcupine or rays of moonlight, and her head is replaced by a “cold and dented” moonlike dome. This surreal image evokes a sense of alienation and transformation, suggesting that she is no longer fully human or recognizable. The metaphor of her body being “more angular than curved” and “ready to meet with the opponent at a single move” sets the tone of confrontation and tension.
  • The silence, the white curtains blowing like wings, and the “faint tappings of a spectral branch” create an eerie atmosphere, hinting at supernatural or psychological disturbance. This passage is important because it introduces the unsettling and uncanny presence of Miss MacIntosh, foreshadowing the dramatic events to follow.
  • A key moment is the revelation that Miss MacIntosh is bald “as the egg or as the rock where nothing grows,” her face “absolutely impersonal and expressionless.” This stark image symbolizes loss of identity, vitality, and femininity. Vera is horrified by this transformation, seeing her as a “monster” or a “phantasmagoric being,” a “bald pretender” who deceives the senses.
  • The contrast with the memory of her “red, shining hair, tawny as an August sunset,” her eyeglasses, and umbrella underscores the dramatic change, suggesting that this is either a ghostly apparition or a symbolic representation of death, decay, or betrayal.
  • Vera reflects on proverbs and parables, such as “the mountains shall never meet with the mountains,” “you shall praise the bridge that carried you,” and “man is not born to happiness.” These reflections highlight the philosophical and existential underpinnings of the chapter, emphasizing themes of inevitability, loss, and the fragile nature of human happiness.
  • Vera struggles with the notion that either Miss MacIntosh has changed or she herself has changed, questioning the nature of reality and perception. This self-doubt and questioning of identity deepen the psychological tension and ambiguity.
  • This extended passage details a physical and symbolic struggle between Vera and the bald figure (Miss MacIntosh or a phantom). The fight is described in vivid, almost cinematic terms, with references to boxing moves (“left jab,” “straight right,” “rabbit punch”) and wrestling.
  • Vera experiences disorientation, pain, and a sense of duality (“my body seemed divided as if now there were two of us”). The imagery of stars falling “like dried seed pods,” “dark horsemen,” and a black “cloud extending from pole to pole” evokes apocalyptic or mythic grandeur.
  • Importantly, Vera questions the identity of the attacker, wondering if it is a “fallen angel,” a “Christian knight,” or merely a “sandbag dummy come to life.” This uncertainty blurs the line between reality, dream, and hallucination.
  • Vera describes being overpowered, feeling hair pulled out “by handfuls,” yet experiencing no pain but a speculative wonder about being “snatched bald.” This loss of hair symbolizes a profound loss of beauty, femininity, and power, likened to a “terrible mistake, God’s error.”
  • Despite this, Vera notes that “the next morning, of course, my long braids were exactly braided down my back,” suggesting the experience may have been a dream, vision, or hallucination, blurring reality and illusion.
  • “She was only this old man who had only one breast and no hair and no future prospect.” Chapter 19 confirms that one breast was amputated. The novel’s clues to Miss MacIntosh’s illness, the lobster’s claws upon her forehead in Chapter 10 and the blue lips in Chapter 12, come to this.
  • Vera’s account of the struggle, her mouth covered, laid on “the bed of death or something which might be worse,” the work of someone “who should take advantage of a poor, ignorant girl, only fourteen years old,” is the language of assault. That the morning shows no trace leaves open what happened, not whether Vera was harmed.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “I refused to believe that she had not changed, that only I had changed.” Which is true?
  • “What shall we do when, fleeing from illusion, we are confronted by illusion?” How does Vera answer?
  • Her “body seemed divided as if now there were two of us, one who fought, one who lay still and watched it all.” What does that division tell us?

Characters

  • Vera laughs and feels “no pity for her but only a kind of wild triumph.” Why?
  • Vera calls Miss MacIntosh “the bald pretender” who “had been pulling the wool over my eyes.” Who has been deceived?
  • She knows Miss MacIntosh “by her broken nose.” What does it mean to know someone by a wound?

Connections

  • Vera has pictured Miss MacIntosh with a red mustache (Chapter 9) and as the bridegroom (Chapter 10). How does that prepare for “you are a man”?
  • The sandbag dummy of Chapters 12 and 15 comes “to life.” What has Vera been training for?
  • Falada’s head speaks after death in Chapter 16. What does Miss MacIntosh’s bare head say?

Reflection

  • Why might a child react with cruelty when someone she trusted is suddenly exposed?
  • Can a person be harmed by an event she cannot be sure happened?
  • How does illness change how others see a body?

Research: Characters

Miss MacIntosh

  • Initially described as a familiar and trusted figure, a nursemaid, a guardian.
  • Transforms into a bald, expressionless, ghostly figure, described as “bald as the egg or as the rock where nothing grows,” symbolizing loss, change, or an unsettling void.
  • Portrayed with contradictory imagery: both fragile and monstrously strong, spectral yet physically violent.
  • Vera struggles to reconcile this apparition with the Miss MacIntosh she knew, suspecting deception and a hidden identity.
  • Symbolizes a “bald pretender,” a false self, or a shadowy force challenging Vera.
  • She has “only one breast,” and Vera knows her only “by her broken nose” from the Spokane bus (Chapter 9).

Vera Cartwheel

  • On the night of her fourteenth birthday, caught between terror and fascination.
  • Experiences a physical and psychological battle with the transformed Miss MacIntosh.
  • Reflects on memories, identity, and the nature of illusion versus reality.
  • Shows moments of wild laughter, despair, and confusion, indicating a complex emotional state.
  • Vera’s perception is unreliable or dreamlike, blending corporeal reality with surreal visions.
  • She screams, “Miss MacIntosh, you are a man!” and feels “a kind of wild triumph,” though “I should have cried.”

Research: Themes

Identity and Transformation

See: Identity and the Self

  • Miss MacIntosh’s sudden baldness and altered demeanor symbolize a profound change, calling into question the stability of identity.
  • Vera’s inability to accept this change reflects a struggle with understanding the self and others.
  • The motif of hair loss as a loss of femininity or vitality is potent and unsettling.

Illusion versus Reality

See: Illusion and Reality

  • The chapter blurs the lines between waking life, dream, and hallucination.
  • Vera questions what is real, what is illusion, and the nature of perception.
  • The fight with the “bald opponent” may also be symbolic, reflecting an internal conflict or a confrontation with an aspect of the self.

Fear and Madness

See: Madness and Sanity · Fear and Courage

  • The setting at midnight, the eerie descriptions, and the “zero hour” evoke a sense of dread.
  • Vera’s fear escalates into a violent struggle, suggesting a descent into madness and trauma.
  • The chapter explores how strange events can “leave us mad” or shatter sanity.

Power and Vulnerability

See: Fear and Courage · Women, Gender, and Marriage

  • Miss MacIntosh’s physical power contrasts with her ghostly, fragile appearance.
  • Vera, though young and vulnerable, refuses to be defeated quietly.
  • The battle becomes a metaphor for the fight between opposing forces within or external threats.

Memory and Perception

See: Memory and the Past

  • Vera recalls past moments with Miss MacIntosh, contrasting them with the present terrifying vision.
  • There is an awareness that memory and perception can deceive or change over time.
  • Vera’s reflections on proverbs and parables hint at larger philosophical questions.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Baldness

See: Hair, Wig, and Baldness

  • Represents loss, emptiness, exposure, and transformation.
  • Compared to “the egg,” “the rock where nothing grows,” “the rose stripped of all its petals,” and “bare as the winter winds,” emphasizing barrenness and desolation.

Moonlight and Darkness

See: Moon and Stars · Lamps, Candles, and Light

  • The moonlight creates a cold, distant atmosphere.
  • The interplay of light and shadow symbolizes the tension between knowledge and ignorance, reality and illusion.

Corset and Armor

See: Clothing, Hats, and Skirts

  • Miss MacIntosh’s metallic corset is described like a battleship or wrestler’s gear, symbolizing restraint, protection, and conflict readiness.

Animal Imagery

See: Horses and Carriages

  • Falada, Vera’s old pony, a present from Mr. Spitzer, represents loyalty and alertness but is powerless.
  • Vera’s comparison of Miss MacIntosh to a “codfish swept up upon a barren shore” and “old man” adds to the grotesque transformation.

Physical Combat

See: Fear and Courage

  • The fight is depicted with detailed boxing terminology (rabbit punch, jab, left hook), emphasizing the visceral and brutal nature of the struggle.
  • The encounter may also symbolize internal psychological conflict or a battle with one’s fears or identity.

Style

  • The narration flows with intense, fragmented, and poetic sentences reflecting Vera’s emotional turmoil.
  • The imagery is often dreamlike, blending the real with the fantastical.
  • The tone fluctuates between terror, disbelief, wild laughter, and despair.

Use of Parables and Philosophical Reflections

See: Fairy Tales and Folklore

  • Vera contemplates proverbs and existential questions, adding depth and universality.

Further notes from the text

  • Miss MacIntosh first appears as a silhouette: corset stays sticking out like the quills of an angry porcupine, and in place of a head another moon, an ivory ball “such as perhaps a god should play with.”
  • The baldness arrives as a litany of similes, bald as the egg, as the rock where nothing grows, as the rose stripped of its petals, and then as a list of missing things: her red hair, her eyeglasses, her black umbrella, her Pilgrim’s Progress.
  • Vera’s first response is laughter and “a kind of wild triumph” at having been right all along in imagining Miss MacIntosh with a red, curled mustache and in casting her as the bridegroom, a direct callback to the dream wedding in Chapter 10.
  • The figure Vera fears is the lurking rapist Miss MacIntosh had warned her about, the drunken sailor reeling out of the fog. The protector and the danger she warned against have become one figure in the dark.
  • The struggle is told in boxing language: the left jab, the straight right, the rabbit punch, the left hook, the southpaw. It is the sandbag dummy of Chapters 12 and 15 come to life, and also, Vera thinks, the angel with whom Jacob wrestled at Peniel.
  • Vera calls for help that cannot come: her mother, and Mr. Spitzer, who “was gone from Boston to Boston.” She says she will be asking the questions of this night long years afterward, when she is old and grey.

Research: Resources

  • “her corset stays sticking out like the quills of an angry porcupine”: The Ghost tells Hamlet his tale would make “each particular hair to stand on end, / Like quills upon the fretful porpentine” (Hamlet 1.5). A few lines later, “My hair crawled,” and soon Vera’s hair stands “on end.” See Shakespeare and Clothing, Hats, and Skirts.
  • “a figure of a tomb”: She stands “her hands crossed against the flatness of her breasts,” the pose of a tomb effigy. See the Chapter 16 resources.
  • “that zero hour, the hour of midnight when strangest things do often happen”: “Zero hour” was the soldiers’ name for the hour of attack in the First World War. “’Tis now the very witching time of night, / When churchyards yawn” (Hamlet 3.2). See Shakespeare.
  • “as if I were entombed in fields of everlasting ice”: At the bottom of Dante’s Hell, the traitors are frozen in the ice of Cocytus (Inferno 32-34). Vera is about to feel betrayed. See Dante.
  • “she was bald. She was bald as the egg”: Alopecia, the total loss of hair, was popularly blamed on “some sudden shock or amazement or illness.” The hair passage of Chapter 12 prepared for this, and in Chapter 19 Vera finds Miss MacIntosh’s red wig. See Hair, Wig, and Baldness, Miss MacIntosh, and the Chapter 12 resources.
  • “a great mirror where one should see one’s self if one looked closely”: Her bald head as a mirror. See Mirrors and Reflections.
  • “I knew her by her broken nose”: The broken nose from the Spokane bus. See Miss MacIntosh and the Chapter 9 resources.
  • “many a game of battledore and shuttlecock”: Battledore and shuttlecock is the old paddle game, the ancestor of badminton.
  • “the mountains shall never meet with the mountains”: “Friends may meet, but mountains never greet,” an old proverb. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “you shall praise the bridge that carried you”: “Praise the bridge that carries you over,” an English proverb. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “man is not born to happiness and is a bubble”: Homo bulla, “man is a bubble,” and “man is born unto trouble, as the sparks fly upward” (Job 5:7). See The Book of Job and the Chapter 15 resources.
  • “old pharaohs voyaging to heaven upon clouds of incense”: The Pyramid Texts tell of the king rising to heaven on the smoke of incense. See Ancient Egypt.
  • “where was her Pilgrim’s Progress?”: Miss MacIntosh’s Bunyan. See the Chapter 3 resources.
  • “Better it had been King Magnus, the fleshless being!”: It is not clear which Magnus Young means. Magnus the Good (1024-1047), an illegitimate son of St. Olaf, became king of Norway and Denmark. St. Magnus of Orkney, an earl and martyr, whose skull, split by an axe, was found hidden in a pillar of Kirkwall Cathedral in 1919, would fit “the fleshless being.”
  • “who knows not the grinning skull?”: Yorick’s skull: “Where be your gibes now?… Not one now, to mock your own grinning?” (Hamlet 5.1). See Shakespeare, Death and Mortality, and the Chapter 7 resources.
  • “some Irish hooligan or drunken sailor”: “Hooligan” came into use in 1890s London, said to be from a rowdy Irish family named Houlihan. The word carries the era’s contempt for the Irish poor. See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “visualizing her with a red, curled mustache, that I had been right in assigning to her the role of the bridegroom”: The red mustache of Chapter 9 and the wedding dream of Chapter 10. See Weddings and Funerals, the Chapter 9 resources, and the Chapter 10 resources.
  • “this old man who had only one breast”: Halsted’s radical mastectomy (1894), which removed the breast and the muscles beneath it, was the standard treatment for breast cancer for most of the twentieth century. Chapter 19 confirms the loss. In Chapter 10, Vera imagined “claws upon her forehead like the claws of a lobster,” and cancer takes its name from the crab. See Miss MacIntosh, Women, Gender, and Marriage, and the Chapter 10 resources.
  • “It was a horse ballet, a Spanish carousel with many mirrors”: Baroque courts staged horse ballets, and the carrousel was first a tournament of horsemen, like Louis XIV’s Grand Carrousel of 1662. See Horses and Carriages and the Chapter 16 resources.
  • “the moon which draws the waves in its wake and has no life of its own”: The moon raises the tides and shines only by reflected sunlight. See Moon and Stars.
  • “she was the ring-master”: The circus of Vera’s bareback dream in Chapter 16 returns, Miss MacIntosh with her “steel embroidery hoops.” See the Chapter 16 resources.
  • “one rose which canker shall not eat”: “And loathsome canker lives in sweetest bud” (Shakespeare, Sonnet 35). See Shakespeare.
  • “one door which shall not open to our knocking”: An inversion of “Knock, and it shall be opened unto you” (Matthew 7:7). See The Bible.
  • “the left jab which annoys, hurts, and stings, the straight right”: The fight follows boxing under the Marquess of Queensberry rules (1867). The rabbit punch, a blow to the back of the neck named for the way hunters killed rabbits, is illegal. The Sunday punch is a fighter’s best blow, and a southpaw fights left-handed. The little shadow boxer returns as a character in Chapter 72. See The Little Shadow Boxer.
  • “The stars fell like dried seed pods upon a dark and barren plain”: “And the stars of heaven fell unto the earth, even as a fig tree casteth her untimely figs” (Revelation 6:13). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “Myriads of dark horsemen thundered against a sky”: The four horsemen of Revelation 6. See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “was it some fallen angel, some livid creature of the deep, or was it the angel of light”: “For Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light” (2 Corinthians 11:14). See The Bible.
  • “Where was Mr. Spitzer? He was gone from Boston to Boston.”: The preacher’s words in the dream of Chapter 10: “from Boston to Boston.” See Joachim Spitzer and the Chapter 10 resources.
  • “some ancient Sumerian or Greek wrestler or Roman gladiator”: In the Epic of Gilgamesh, from ancient Sumer and Babylon, Gilgamesh wrestles the wild man Enkidu, who becomes his friend. See Greek Mythology.
  • “someone who wore a jumbo corset whale-boned”: Corsets were stiffened with whalebone, the baleen from the mouths of whales. See Clothing, Hats, and Skirts.
  • “even that holy angel with whom Jacob struggled at Peniel”: Genesis 32:22-32 and Hosea 12:3-5: “And Jacob was left alone; and there wrestled a man with him until the breaking of the day” (Genesis 32:24). See Genesis and the Chapter 6 resources.
  • “the sandbag dummy come to life”: The sandbag dummy Vera was made to box in Chapters 12 and 15, now “the shadow boxer moving with such rapidity that his blows were impossible to block.” The little shadow boxer returns as a character in Chapter 72. See The Little Shadow Boxer, the Chapter 12 resources, and the Chapter 15 resources.
  • “my wailings and gnashings of teeth”: “There shall be weeping and gnashing of teeth” (Matthew 8:12). See The Bible and the Chapter 10 resources.
  • “the anesthesia provided by nature before she strikes her final blow”: David Livingstone, mauled by a lion, wrote that the shock “caused a sort of dreaminess, in which there was no sense of pain nor feeling of terror” (Missionary Travels, 1857).

Vocabulary

Abyss
A bottomless pit or chasm.
Anesthesia
Loss of feeling or sensation, natural or induced.
Apparition
A ghost, or a ghostlike image of a person.
Battledore
A small paddle used to hit a shuttlecock; also the game itself.
Beseechment
Earnest pleading.
Biceps
The large muscle at the front of the upper arm.
Breastplate
A piece of armor covering the chest.
Canker
A blight or ulcer that eats away at a plant or the body; anything that corrupts from within.
Carousel
A merry-go-round. Earlier, a tournament in which horsemen performed exercises and displays.
Clinch
In boxing, a hold in which the fighters grip each other.
Codfish
A large food fish of cold northern seas.
Combatant
One who fights.
Corrugated
Shaped into ridges and furrows.
Corset
A close-fitting undergarment, stiffened with whalebone or steel and laced tight, worn to shape the waist.
Girded
Encircled or bound tightly, as with a belt or girdle.
Gladiator
A fighter in the arenas of ancient Rome.
Goose pimples
Bumps on the skin raised by cold or fear.
Gossamer
A fine film of cobweb; anything light and delicate.
Hoary
Gray or white with age.
Hooligan
A rough, lawless young man.
Invulnerable
Unable to be wounded or harmed.
Irrevocably
In a way that cannot be undone.
Jab
A quick, short punch.
Livid
Bluish-gray, the color of a bruise. Also furiously angry.
Myriad
A countless number.
Parable
A short story that teaches a lesson.
Phantasmagoric
Like a phantasmagoria, a shifting series of dreamlike or nightmarish images. A phantasmagoria was an eighteenth-century magic-lantern ghost show.
Pinioned
Held or bound fast, especially by the arms.
Pretender
One who pretends; also, someone who claims a throne or title without a rightful claim.
Preternatural
Beyond what is natural.
Rabbit punch
An illegal blow to the back of the neck.
Ravishing
Enchanting; also, seizing by force.
Shuttlecock
A feathered cork struck back and forth in battledore or badminton.
Southpaw
A left-handed boxer.
Spectral
Ghostly.
Speculative
Thoughtful and wondering; based on guesswork.
Stays
Strips of whalebone or steel that stiffen a corset.
Sunday punch
A boxer’s hardest, best blow.
Translucence
The quality of letting light pass through without being clear.
Vanquish
To defeat thoroughly.
Whalebone
Baleen, the flexible plates from the mouths of certain whales, once used to stiffen corsets.
Zero hour
The hour set for an attack to begin.
