Research notes: Chapter 66 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 66

Characters: Lorena · Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Titus Bonebreaker · Old Friday

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

Miss MacIntosh presents herself as the embodiment of radical singularity ("absolutely normal," she insists, "just like other people") and yet she is constitutively multiple and irresolvable. She is simultaneously:

  • A nursemaid who dresses as a whaler
  • A socialist who haunts the Stock Exchange
  • A bald woman who wears a red wig
  • A woman without ovaries who obsesses over recipes
  • A woman without a breast who lectures on household arts
  • A woman who preaches common sense while drowning herself at dawn

The narrator notes that she "took on every color except my own", which is itself a description of the plural self: the child narrator has no fixed self either, only a reflective surface absorbing Miss MacIntosh's multiplicities. But Miss MacIntosh herself is the deeper demonstration: she cannot be resolved into a single coherent person because she is not one. Her bald head under the red wig is not a secret hidden from her true self. It is one layer of a self that is all layers simultaneously.

The Arctic explorer, who sits beside her at the Stock Exchange, mirrors this plurality back at her. He is a Great Dane, a Greater Dog, a man who killed twin sisters with an arrow, a man with a wooden leg who has "eaten his dead master", a man whose eyes cannot close. He is not one thing either. Their encounter is not between two unified persons but between two unresolvable multiplicities who briefly orbit each other.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The entire epistemological structure of the chapter rests on this pillar. Miss MacIntosh's defining characteristic, the one the narrator names as the revelation that reorganizes everything, is her baldness, the fact that her red hair was a wig. But the wig is not simply a disguise. It is the primary construction by which a self becomes livable.

The narrator says: "I thought her red hair was her own, of course, and never looked at her too closely. I had not yet discovered her without her wig like the red sunset upon the waters." The wig is not the lie and the bald head the truth. Both are real. The wig is the construction that allows Miss MacIntosh to exist in social space, to teach lessons, to walk in the surf lecturing on socialism, to visit the Stock Exchange, to be a person at all. Without it, she is exposed to a kind of ontological nakedness that the text treats as catastrophic, not because the bald head is shameful in itself, but because the social world requires the construction of a self that can be seen.

Her imaginary investments at the Stock Exchange extend this logic. She makes "imaginary money", keeps her investments on "slips of brown wrapping paper, neatly cut, made with a red pencil". She knows they are imaginary. She does it anyway. The imaginary fortune is not a delusion; it is a practice, a way of inhabiting the world's structures without being destroyed by them. The line between illusion and reality is, as the philosophy states, itself a construction. Miss MacIntosh lives this truth more honestly than most.

The Arctic explorer intensifies the theme: he claims to have reached the North Pole, but Miss MacIntosh suspects both he and Captain Cook and Commander Peary of being "liars" about having reached the center, "which was surrounded by five hundred miles of mush and no solid ice to uphold any foot". The Pole (the fixed point, the center, the truth) cannot be stood upon. It is mush. There is no sunlit world outside the cave.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The chapter's emotional climate is grief, but it never announces itself as such. It accumulates in the background of every lesson, every booming question, every piece of grey knitting intended for an orphan in a cold country, who is, the narrator quietly notes, "herself, no doubt".

Miss MacIntosh grieves:

  • Her baldness, which she has carried since birth
  • Her mastectomy, her removed ovaries: the reproductive body she never had
  • The King of Sweden, who "promised much to her" and misled her
  • Mr. Bonebreaker, who fled before the honeymoon upon seeing her bald head
  • The Arctic explorer, who disappeared "without her, leaving no name"
  • Lorena, who knew "before seeing this baldness": a figure whose full story the text withholds, but whose knowing is described with the tenderness of intimacy
  • What Cheer, Iowa, the lost origin, the plain and simple place that no longer exists as she remembers it

None of this grief resolves. The text does not move Miss MacIntosh through stages toward acceptance. Instead, it accumulates: lesson upon lesson, question upon question, wig upon bald head upon recipe for walrus heart upon Eskimo cookbook upon silk nightgown. The grief is the weather of the chapter, the atmospheric pressure under which every scene is set.

The final image makes this explicit. The shore after her drowning is not a scene of resolution. It is a scene of accumulation: her corset on a rock, her watch among sand dollars, her grey socks unknitting at the tide's edge, her red wig on a horseshoe crab. These are not the remnants of a completed life. They are grief made visible as sediment.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator's function in this chapter is precisely that of the Witness. She does not fix Miss MacIntosh. She does not conclude her. She watches.

"Not that she cared for the answers. But our lessons must go on in spite of the storm and the moon drowned in the sky and the carcasses on the beach."

The narrator receives Miss MacIntosh's confessions during "that last month of her life" without judgment, without interruption, without resolution. She records the Arctic explorer's long monologue, Miss MacIntosh's imaginary finances, her social welfare concerns, her loneliness in New York City, her visits to Grant's Tomb, her terror at the cafeteria for mutes: all of it held in the same attentive, non-simplifying gaze.

The ethical weight of this witnessing becomes explicit at the end:

"Yet once more, Miss MacIntosh, my darling, and once more — old Friday barking by the sea..."

The repetition of "once more" is not nostalgia. It is the act of witnessing refusing to conclude. The narrator will not arrive at a tidy grief. She will not summarize Miss MacIntosh. She will stay in the room (which is now the sea, the shore, the echo) and keep looking. The refusal to simplify is the act.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Miss MacIntosh's lessons are the clearest image of recursive construction in the chapter. She asks the same questions again and again: Who was Martin Luther? What is the altitude of Tibet? Who is John L. Sullivan?, not because she expects new answers, but because the asking is the building. The lessons are provisional, acknowledged as such by both teacher and student, and yet they continue.

"In spite of death itself, our lessons must go on."

This is the recursive structure stated plainly: you build anyway. The building is never finished. The narrator confesses she never knew the answers then, but the lessons "took on a new light in memory" after Miss MacIntosh was gone. The building continues after the builder has drowned. What was constructed in the nursery during storms is still being constructed in the act of remembering and narrating.

Miss MacIntosh's imaginary Stock Exchange fortune follows the same logic. She builds it, knowing it is imaginary, covering "her investments with her investments", hedging "herself about with paper mills". The building is provisional. That is not failure. That is how it works.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter's form enacts this pillar structurally. The lessons are never completed. The Arctic explorer's proposal is never answered. The story of Lorena is never fully told. Mr. Bonebreaker's umbrella is left behind but he himself is not explained. Miss MacIntosh's body is never found.

"There being no sign of her upon the water or the sand where her old mackintosh must lose its colors."

The demand for completion (for a body, for a resolution, for an explanation of why she walked into the sea) is refused. The text treats this refusal not as a failure of narration but as its highest honesty. Miss MacIntosh herself enacts this ethic: she never finishes her grey knitting, never cooks the recipes she collects, never places her first ten cents in the actual market, never goes to the North Pole or anywhere else.

Her life, as rendered here, is a series of ongoing sentences. The concluded life, the finished woman, would be a falsification. The unfinished is not failure. It is honesty.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Miss MacIntosh is the Pillar 7 figure made fully explicit. She exists at the intersection of multiple marginalities:

  • Bald in a world that requires hair as femininity
  • Mastectomized and oophorectomized: her body surgically removed from the reproductive order
  • Poor in a world of the idle rich she serves
  • From What Cheer, Iowa: a place so marginal it is almost a joke, a place whose very name is an irony
  • A woman who dresses as a whaler but is not one
  • A socialist who haunts the Stock Exchange but invests nothing
  • A nursemaid who loathes children

"She had always been lonely for adult companionship and had always had a private loathing for children and orphans."

This is the marginal person as evidence. Miss MacIntosh does not fit any category cleanly, not woman, not man, not rich, not quite poor, not teacher, not explorer, not wife, not mother. At every categorical edge, the constructed nature of the category becomes visible. Her baldness is not incidental. It is the physical sign of her categorical instability: the place where the construction shows.

The Arctic explorer is a second marginal figure: a man who killed women with an arrow and has never recovered, who cannot close his eyes, who is partly wooden, who has "eaten his dead master", who lives in a cold water flat on lower Broadway. Together, Miss MacIntosh and the explorer constitute not exceptions to the human but its evidence: the places where the seams show.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The temporal structure of the chapter is entirely sedimentary. The narrator is remembering from "long years after Miss MacIntosh's disappearance", but the past does not recede; it presses upward through every sentence.

Miss MacIntosh herself is a figure of accumulated sediment. Her body carries the layers: the removed breast, the removed ovaries, the broken nose, the bald head covered by a red wig, the eye going blind. Each loss is a geological stratum. She is not moving through time toward a future. She is standing on the accumulated weight of everything that has been taken from her.

The What Cheer of her childhood is not behind her. It is beneath her: pressing up through her obsession with oil lamps over electric lights, her disdain for zippers, her belief in mended fences and honest poverty. The sectarian world of What Cheer, where women washed men's feet with their hair, is not past. It is the sediment that determines her present posture, including the specific way she cannot kneel before a man, having no hair to dry his feet with.

The Emperor Constantine who "changed the calendar" is the chapter's figure for the violence of trying to escape sedimented time. Miss MacIntosh cannot endure this. Time "must have hung heavy on his hands, or he would not have changed time." The attempt to restructure time is read as a symptom of corruption, of having too much: time, power, the luxury of tampering. Miss MacIntosh, who has too little of everything, knows that time cannot be changed. It accumulates.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter's language performs its own argument. Young's prose is deliberately recursive, accumulative, refusing the clean sentence. Consider the passage about Miss MacIntosh's hair:

"...having no hair of seduction, no hair bright or dark, no illusion, not even a hair of the sunlight, moonlight, starlight, and surely she could not be attractive with her broken nose or with the breast of her amputation..."

The accumulation of "no hair of seduction, no hair bright or dark, no illusion" is not decorative. It is a demonstration that language constructs what it names: each phrase adds a new dimension to what hair means, revealing that hair was never simply hair but a whole system of constructed femininity, seduction, illusion, celestial metaphor. To name the absence is to reveal the construction.

Miss MacIntosh's own language (her booming questions, her "Faugh!" and "Phaw!" and "Oh, faugh!") is language that knows its own location. It speaks from What Cheer, Iowa. It speaks from a body that has been surgically altered. It speaks from poverty and loneliness and the specific historical moment of a woman who cannot place her ten cents. It does not pretend to speak from nowhere.

The Arctic explorer's language, by contrast, is language that claims transcendence; he speaks of the place "where nothing was known, there was nothing unknown", of a land "horizonless", of forgetting her name, of no boundary between the living and the dead. His language reaches for the beyond. Miss MacIntosh's language stays resolutely here, in the nursery, in the storm, asking what are the states adjoining Kansas.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter lives entirely within the ordinary and the marginal. It does not live in the Emperor Constantine's changed calendar, or in the King of Sweden's promises, or in the Arctic explorer's six months of darkness. It lives in:

  • The hurricane lamp in the nursery during the storm
  • The grey knitting intended for an orphan in a cold country
  • The silver-dollar watch among the sand dollars on the beach
  • Old Friday barking at the waves
  • The red wig laid over the horseshoe crab crawling in the sand

These objects are not symbols pointing upward to a transcendent meaning. They are the sacred itself, located in the immanent. The wig on the horseshoe crab is not a symbol of Miss MacIntosh's vanity or her tragedy. It is Miss MacIntosh: her constructed self, her illusion, her survival mechanism, now returned to the natural world that she walked into.

The final elegy makes this explicit:

"Yet once more, Miss MacIntosh, my darling, and once more — old Friday barking by the sea, her watery bier, great, blowing tides and storm clouds knotted which were her coffin, its lid the star-studded sky, its shores all shores..."

The coffin is the sea. The lid is the sky. The sacred is not elsewhere. It is here, in the water and the sand and the barking dog and the waves "like sheep with a thousand bells". The transcendence is immanent, pointing deeper into the world, not out of it.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The narrator is the Crossing Point made visible. She is constituted by Miss MacIntosh, by the lessons she could not answer, by the questions boomed above the storm, by the discovery of the bald head, by the last month of confessions. She did not choose this constitution. It was deposited in her before she could evaluate it.

"I took on every color except my own."

This is the Crossing Point described from the inside: the self as the place where prior depositions cross, where Miss MacIntosh's loneliness and What Cheer's sectarianism and the mother's opium dreams and the Arctic explorer's proposal all intersect in the body of a child who cannot name the author of their freedom.

Miss MacIntosh herself is a Crossing Point between What Cheer and the wider world, between the sectarian past and the socialist present, between the body she was born with and the body that was surgically altered, between the self she presents (red hair, capable nursemaid, normal woman) and the self beneath the wig.

Her drowning is not an exit from the Crossing Point. It is its completion: the moment when the accumulated sediment, the plural self, the permanent grief, the provisional constructions all return to the element from which they came. And the narrator, carrying all of it forward in language, becomes the ground the not-yet-born will stand on.

"The amputated feet, Miss MacIntosh once said, will walk by themselves, walk by themselves through sands and surfs and flowering meadows and orchards of golden apples."

The carrying continues.
