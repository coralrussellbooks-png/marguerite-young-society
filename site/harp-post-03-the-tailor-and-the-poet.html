Chapter 3. The Tailor and the Poet

← Chapter 2 · The Unfinished Harp: Reading Harp Song for a Radical · Chapter 4 →

Why a biography of an Indiana railroad man spends sixty pages in Germany and Paris

On page 22 of Harp Song for a Radical, Daniel Debs is waiting in New York. It is 1849. He has crossed the Atlantic ahead of Marguerite Bettrich, the mill girl he loves, and has already been swindled out of most of his inheritance by a confidence man. He is staying at the Shakespeare Hotel on Duane Street, a lodging house full of French and German radicals who fled Europe after the failed revolutions of 1848.

A reader expects the book to stay with Daniel. Instead, Young notices another guest at the Shakespeare Hotel, the wandering German tailor and prophet Wilhelm Weitling, and follows him. She follows him back to his birth in Magdeburg, into a Swiss prison, into a Hamburg bookshop where he meets the poet Heinrich Heine, and then to Brussels, where Karl Marx pounds a table at him. Daniel Debs does not return for a long time. Eugene will not be born for another hundred and sixty pages.

Most readers will find this the hardest stretch of the book to accept. It is also where Young demonstrates what she is doing.

The tailor

Weitling was, in Young's blunt word, a bastard. His father was a young French artillery officer who marched into Russia with Napoleon and never came back. His mother was a housemaid. As a boy, he helped his grandmother peddle matches and chicory coffee to Cossack soldiers. At twelve he was apprenticed to a tailor, and at eighteen he took to the roads as a wandering journeyman.

Young traces his politics to a child's question in a cathedral. Looking at the two thieves crucified beside Christ, he wondered why they were never thieves of mines and mills, and why instead they were always "petty thieves of bread crumbs for their starving children" (27). Those who stole a country's wealth from the poor were never punished, or were fined less than they had gained.

This is Reality Is Constructed where the law decides which taking counts as theft, and the law is made by the people doing the larger taking. Weitling's answer was childlike and exact. He wanted coins stamped not with the heads of kings but with a hammer, an anvil, or a saw, and with words saying what the coin would buy: one loaf of bread, one pound of meat (32). Money, which presents itself as a fact of nature, turns into an agreement the moment someone proposes changing the picture on it.

Young is not sentimental about him. He was vain, grandiose, and half persuaded he was the returning Christ. He threw his tailor's scissors into Lake Zurich, swearing never to sew again, "But alas, he was to be pursued by scissors the rest of his life, even unto his grave" (29). His certainty was total: "Utopia was that which was outside of history. It came because it must" (32). Young records that sentence without endorsing it. Everything else in her book argues against it.

She also records his generosity. He believed that "There was no such thing in nature as a bastard left upon a rock, for all children were the same in the eyes of God" (28). The Margins Reveal the Truth could hardly be more personal. The illegitimate tailor, sorted from birth into the lowest box, is the one who sees that the boxes are man-made.

The crime of the open mouth

In June 1843, Zurich police arrested Weitling at three in the morning. He was jailed "for having committed no crime but the crime of spreading his anarchic socialist or communist messages" (32). His prison chapters are among the most painful in the book. He hears knocking in the walls. He is sure an Austrian detective watches him through two peepholes. He refuses a clean nightshirt in case the jailers mean to hang him by its sleeves, and he refuses water in case it is poisoned. He writes poems with titles like "Forty-eight Hours in the Dark" and "Do Not Despair."

When he is finally sent home in chains, Young sums up the case in a single line: "He had committed no crime but that of failure to keep his big mouth shut" (38).

That line explains why Weitling belongs in a life of Debs. Nineteen pages earlier, Young described Debs as "one of the jackass Socialists who would not keep their mouths shut" (4). The two sentences rhyme deliberately. Seventy-five years before Woodrow Wilson jailed Debs for a speech, a Swiss court jailed a German tailor for a pamphlet. The prison at the start of the book has a floor beneath it, and Weitling's cell is one of the layers.

This is The Past Doesn't Go Away as structure. Young is showing that Debs's fate was not a strange American accident. It had been rehearsed, in other languages, by people whose names his admirers would never learn.

The poet

After his release, Weitling went to Hamburg to see his prison poems through the press. In the bookshop of his publisher, Julius Campe, he found Heinrich Heine, home from Paris on a rare visit to his mother.

Young stages the meeting as comedy. Weitling, in a red cap and red shoes with their soles tied on by rope, flops onto a bench and props his bare legs up one at a time, scraping away the prison mold and the sores. He lectures the startled poet on the coming kingdom of heaven. Heine, fastidious and fragile, dislikes being slapped on the back by strangers "lest he who was so fragile should be knocked over" (47). He later complained to Marx about the tailor's "nauseating familiarity" (51).

Heine is the other half of the utopian temperament, and Young gives him more room than anyone else in Part One. He wanted plenty for everyone. His theory was that "there would be plenty in this world for everybody if only there were fair play" (51), wine and roses and bread for all. He saw clearly how the rich "raised up a barricade of laws by which to protect themselves from the starving poor" (51). He also feared the leveling that radical politics might bring, and he "came increasingly to view communism as that which would cut down the very flower of civilization" (51).

Young does not resolve that contradiction, because Heine did not. She describes a man with "space for many selves including the masks of himself and his double" (40), whose "horizons were never made of a choice between the Either on the one hand and the Or on the other" (49). Of his inner life she writes, "Illusion might be reality, and reality might be illusion" (49). This is Not One Self given a face. Heine is not confused. He is several people at once, and he does not pretend otherwise.

One of the Heine poems Young retells looks straight ahead to Debs. A white horse in a farmyard watches a steam locomotive rattle past, trembling, and understands that there is no future for horses. Men's hearts are hard, and once they no longer need horses, no one will feed them (50). Young places the poem thirty pages before Debs's birth. The boy who will shovel coal into that locomotive has not been born yet, but the machine and the fear it brings are already on the page.

Heine spent his last eight years on what he called his mattress grave in Paris, paralyzed and nearly blind. Young reports that he meant to ask God why He had given him such a sense of humor and then such suffering, and that he considered bringing God to trial before the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals (56). He died on February 17, 1856. Debs was three months old.

The theorist

Marx enters the story as Heine's friend and Weitling's enemy. In Brussels in 1846, Weitling announced at Marx's table that all their theory was unnecessary, because he had already worked everything out. Marx rose and brought his fist down so hard that the wine goblets and candlesticks jumped. Arousing fantastic hopes in workers, he declared, could lead only to their ruin (69).

Young treats this as the famous quarrel it was, and she lets each man score his points. To Marx and Engels, Weitling was a windbag. To Weitling, Marx was an excellent future lexicographer who would never found a world anyone could live in. Engels suspected Weitling of wanting money, and Young cannot resist the irony: "Money, it seemed, was required in order to found a moneyless utopia" (68).

What interests Young most about Marx is not his theory. She is drawn to his poverty and his grief. She describes a family pawning its china, a father who cannot afford paper and stamps for the articles he sends to Horace Greeley's New York Tribune, and the death of his small son Edgar. Of Marx's mourning she writes, "He could not dismiss a bundle of pink sand to nonbeing, of course" (59). Of Jenny Marx, "She was never to be consoled" (60). This is Grief Doesn't End. The prophet of historical necessity is, in these pages, a father who cannot get over his son.

Young also notes something about Marx that turns back on her own book. Marx believed "his writings could be published only when they were an artistic whole so well rounded that he could see the entire pattern" (52). Marx never finished Capital. Young never finished Harp Song. The man who wanted the finished whole and the woman who wrote in spokes both died with the work open on the table.

Three kinds of certainty

By the end of this stretch, Young has set three figures side by side. Weitling is sure by instinct, Marx is sure by science, and Heine is sure of nothing. Weitling believes utopia "came because it must." Marx's Hegelian inheritance, as Young puts it, held not so much "that what was rational would be but that what was rational must be" (53). Heine, meanwhile, knew early that the revolutionaries "would not live to realize their dreams of the dawn which was to come" (46).

Young sides with none of them, and her refusal is a position. The distrust of the frozen blueprint, the principle this study calls Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary, is how she separates the dream from the dreamers' certainty. Of Weitling's American Zion, which he never built, she writes that it "took a long time dying as in a dream that never really dies as some other dreamer must take over the dream, which transcends the individual" (48–49).

That sentence is the hinge of Part One. Weitling fails. His dream passes on to someone else. Young has just spent sixty pages showing whose hands it will pass through on its way to a boy in Terre Haute.

The vibrating string

In the middle of the Heine chapters, Young quotes a long passage from Denis Diderot about how the mind works. Thought, as she summarizes it, "depended upon associations of images streaming at random through the dreamer's mind like the music caused by the vibrations of strings" (49). One string sets another vibrating, and "an idea once aroused may sometimes set vibrating a harmonic at an inconceivable distance."

This is as close as Young comes to explaining her own method, and it may explain her title. A harp is a set of strings that sound one another. Weitling's cell in Zurich and Debs's cell in Atlanta are strings far apart that vibrate together. The white horse and the locomotive, Marx's lost son and Heine's mattress grave, the tailor's coins and the fireman's wages all sound in sympathy. The digressions are not a failure of focus. They are the instrument.

The crossing point

Weitling sailed for America late in 1846, went back to Europe for the revolution of 1848, and returned to New York in 1849, to the Shakespeare Hotel, where Daniel Debs was waiting for his bride. Young never claims the two men spoke. She puts them under the same roof and lets the reader feel the weight of it.

That hotel is the Crossing Point in its most literal form. Everything Young has traced so far, from the Napoleonic officer lost in the snow to the crucified thieves and the Brussels table, arrives at one address in lower Manhattan. Beneath the future Debs lies the ground these people packed down. Daniel will name his son after two French writers of social conscience, Eugène Sue and Victor Hugo. The boy will inherit the dream along with the names.

Checking the record

Young's broad outline holds. Weitling was born in Magdeburg in 1808, ten years before Marx, the illegitimate son of a French officer. He published Guarantees of Harmony and Freedom in 1842 and was arrested in Zurich in June 1843. The Brussels confrontation with Marx took place in 1846 and was recorded by the Russian visitor Pavel Annenkov, as Young says. She is also right that Heine described religion as a kind of opium before Marx made the phrase famous. Heine's meeting with Weitling in Campe's shop is real, and Heine told his own version years later in his Confessions, with the same tactless scene of the tailor tending the marks on his legs.

A few points need care:

  • Weitling's prison poems. The collection was published in German as Kerkerpoesien (Prison Poems). Young's English title shifts from "Wounds and Balsam" (39) to "Wounds and Blossoms" (43) within four pages, the kind of inconsistency an editor would normally catch.

  • Heine and baby Jenny Marx. Young sets this rescue in Brussels, with Heine turning the child upside down to dislodge a fish bone (52). The more common account places it in Paris in 1844, with Heine saving the infant from convulsions by calling for a warm bath.

  • Edgar Marx's death. Young places it after Heine's death, which would put it in 1856. Edgar died in April 1855, ten months before Heine.

  • Owen's "phalanstery." Young calls New Harmony Robert Owen's "phalanstery" (30). That was Fourier's term for his planned communities. Owen used different language, though Young, who wrote Angel in the Forest about New Harmony, would have known that. She may be blurring the utopias on purpose, as she often does. The blur still needs pointing out.

In the next chapter, Weitling heads west into the American interior, where the Icarians, the Rappites, and Robert Owen's New Harmony are waiting. It is ground Young had covered once before, in Angel in the Forest, and she comes back to it here.
