Chapter 23: We Be Creatures of the Darkness

← Chapter 22 · Guide Home · Chapter 24 →

Research notes: Chapter 23

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Light and Darkness

Symbols: Lamps, Candles, and Light · Snow, Ice, and Winter

Philosophy: Aristotle · Augustine and Christian Theology · Descartes and Dualism · Empiricism (Locke, Berkeley, Hume)

Allusions: Gambling and Playing Cards · Judaism · The Bible · The Book of Job

Chapter 23 is the electric lights chapter, and if you read it only as a comic tirade against modern convenience you will miss what it is doing.

Miss MacIntosh spends most of this chapter behind her newspaper, blaming Benjamin Franklin for the ruin of private life. She objects to floodlights, headlights, footlights, tail lights, traffic lights, searchlights, fluorescent lamps, and the two-way lighting systems she sees advertised as available in Boston. It is funny. Young means it to be funny.

It is also a woman who was seen three nights ago, looked at without her wig by a child holding a candle, arguing at enormous length that no one should have to be looked at.

Hold both of those at once and the chapter opens.

What actually happens in Chapter 23

Nothing, in the sense that nothing happened in Chapter 22. It is the same breakfast table, the same August morning, the same newspaper held up between two people who have agreed without discussion to say nothing.

Miss MacIntosh begins on vain man: the Emperor Constantine, the Slough of Despond, the heart that was locked from the inside. She moves to Caesar crossing the Rubicon and asks who alive ever saw him. History lies, she says, and geography is uncertain. She declares her faith in common sense, “the middle brow, the middle way, the average life.”

Then she arrives at Benjamin Franklin and stays there for most of the chapter. Franklin flew a kite, harnessed lightning he was never asked to harness, paved the way for electric lights, and now there is “nowhere a man can hide his” face. She works through the consequences: no dark corner on Skid Row, no privacy in a man's own bedroom, midnight baseball, the poor paying the light bills. She argues that darkness once protected marriage, that Helen of Troy would not have been Helen of Troy if someone had switched on a lamp, that Ulysses would never have come home if “he had seen Penelope under electric” light. She would give Thomas Edison a piece of her mind if it were ever her doubtful pleasure to meet him.

She asks where Baffin Bay is. She gives Vera advice about igloos.

Vera, meanwhile, does something she has never done before. She works out what the talk is for.

The chapter ends with Miss MacIntosh quoting the Book of Job and crying out that “the stars have lighted the heavens from pole to pole.”

Vain man

Vain man giving himself “immortal airs, imagining what is not.” And then, immediately: “when the door of the heart slams shut, it will not be opened by man again. The heart, my dear, was locked from the inside, and that is that.”

She says this at breakfast, to a fourteen year old, apropos of Constantine.

Notice also the shape of the skepticism. “History lies, and geography is uncertain,” says the woman who quizzes Vera on geography every single morning and has done so for years. She is a teacher calling her own curriculum unreliable, and she does it without any apparent sense of contradiction. “Ignorance of the law is no excuse,’ she” adds, and Young tells us she says it without conviction.

This is the chapter's first quiet signal. Miss MacIntosh's certainties have started saying things she does not entirely believe.

Why the electric lights matter

The tirade runs for pages and Young lets it run. Taken as argument it is crank material, the grumbling of a woman who has decided that a century went wrong. Taken as autobiography it is one of the most exposed passages in the novel.

Every complaint is the same complaint. There is no dark corner left. There is “nowhere a man can hide his ugly face.” God's starlight “left us the privacy in which a poor man may die.” Before all this tampering there was always a lightless alley, a street without lamps, a “bedroom where a man could hide” his face from everyone but God.

Three nights ago a child stood over her bed with a candle and saw what she had spent a lifetime covering. Miss MacIntosh has not mentioned it and she will not mention it. She is talking about Benjamin Franklin instead. But everything she says about light is about being seen, and everything she says about darkness is about the mercy of not being seen, and she says it for most of a chapter without once saying I.

Young does not underline this. She simply puts the tirade three days after the candle and trusts you.

The ugly woman safe in her husband's arms

The marriage passage is where the defense stops being general.

In the old days, she says, when darkness fell and it certainly did fall, “an ugly woman was safe in her husband’s arms,” since he could not see what he could not touch. He might even think her handsome. Couples undressed in the dark, each being modest. “Many a wife never saw her husband except when he was clothed. Many a husband never saw his wife.” Then the electric lights come on, and a blushing bride is shown up for what she is, sometimes on the wedding night, sometimes the night before, and look at the divorce rate.

Read this as a joke and it is a good one. Read it as a woman with a mastectomy scar and a bald head explaining why she never married and it is something else entirely. “The wisest men are never married,” she says a few lines later, and moves on.

“Beauty is as beauty does, my dear.” She offers it as folk wisdom. It is the only comfort she has ever been able to give herself, and she is handing it across the breakfast table to a girl who has just learned what it cost.

The igloos

Then, with no transition at all, she tells Vera how to keep house at the top of the world.

Always have two igloos. Keep the permanent one “closed against wind and drifting snow.” Keep “a good supply of frozen seals and” kitchen matches. A new broom sweeps clean. Never build the second igloo on an old ice floe, because it may “move away in a long night’s darkness.” Wait until the ice has stopped moving. “Listen for the crackle of distant ice,’ she said, ‘like the screaming of wild geese.”

And then the sentence the chapter has been circling: “the ice might seem quite firm where we are,” and be in motion “miles behind us or miles ahead.” These appearances are often deceptive. Old “floes have a way of splitting into two. We might lose ourselves. We might lose our sleds and our dogs.”

That is not housekeeping advice. That is a woman describing her own life in the only vocabulary she permits herself, and it is as close to a confession as she comes. Always keep a second shelter. Never trust ground that is not moving yet.

Notice too the small physical detail Young drops in the middle of it. “She coughed into her napkin, and then she seemed to scream.” It is one clause, easily missed, and it belongs with the rasping breath Vera heard in Chapter 22. The performance holds. The body does not.

Covering her hand

Vera's recognition arrives in the sunlight, at the table, in the middle of the geography.

These cosmic subjects, she thinks, the phantasmagoric glaciers and submerged continents and the latitude and longitude of the Austral Islands, are dreams, impersonal and vast, and they cover her hand, “as is said in gambling circles.” They conceal her limitations, the thing missing from the essential human, the thing she does not want Vera to see.

Then comes the harder line. “Of herself, of herself alone, what had she ever said that was personal and sad and revelatory?”

Fourteen years. The woman who raised her, who tested her every morning on rivers and capitals and the first day of creation, has never once told her anything about her own life. Vera notices this for the first time at this breakfast, and it is the notice that makes Chapter 23 a turning point rather than a second helping of Chapter 22. In Chapter 22 Vera watched a performance and wondered whether Miss MacIntosh was all others. Here she works out what the performance is for.

She is always evading something, Vera thinks, something too subtle to evade.

Winter in August

The chapter's last movement belongs to Vera, and it is one of the loveliest things in Volume One.

Miss MacIntosh announces that they “must get ready for winter’s blast,” mend the old woolen drawers, keep a nip of brandy for warmth. It is “the cold hand of death for those who are not ready.”

And Vera asks the obvious question. Why are we talking about winter in “August sunlight, heavy with the perfume of rain-washed flowers, and was there to be no season of transition, no gradual change, no autumn, no Indian summer like the Indian summer of the human soul, no time” for farewell before the world goes colorless?

Then she answers herself with a list. Winter wears “its coat of many colors, too,” dimmed though they are: “the bronze of beach grass, the whiteness of snow upon the heads of the blackened spruce and hemlock, the pale iridescence of the seagull’s eye, the purple and mauve and grey and gold of the winged seed heads. There would be the luminous colors of the winter sea like a great opal seen through” fog.

That is the lesson of the birthday night arriving in Vera's own voice, without anyone teaching it to her. A stripped world is not a world without color. It is a world whose colors have gone quiet. She has just watched a woman be stripped down to the bare fact of herself, and this is what she has made of it.

What to watch for as you keep reading

The blind man in the final lines looks like a throwaway joke. “A blind man saw me, certainly, and without electric lights.” It is not a joke. Miss MacIntosh worked in a house for the blind, and Young has been building that history since the garden chapters. It is also the chapter's actual argument, delivered in one sentence after several thousand words of complaint about light bulbs: being truly seen has nothing whatever to do with illumination. The one person who ever saw her could not see.

Watch the doubling. Two igloos. Two lighting systems in the Boston advertisement. Dwarves who dream they are giants and giants who dream they are dwarves. The novel is full of twins, doubles, and second selves, and Miss MacIntosh keeps producing them in pairs without ever noticing she does it.

Watch who she talks to. She would say this to Benjamin Franklin if she met him in the darkness. She would give Thomas Edison a piece of her mind. She would have a word with Caesar, were it her doubtful pleasure. Every argument she wins is with someone who cannot answer. Then turn the page to Chapter 24 and watch Catherine place telephone calls to Augustine and King Canute and Cousin Hannah. Two women in one house, both talking at length to the unreachable, one of them certain she is the sensible one. Young put those chapters side by side deliberately.
