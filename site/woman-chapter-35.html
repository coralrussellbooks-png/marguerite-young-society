Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 35

← Chapter 34 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 36 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

Cousin Hannah out-plays Mr. Spitzer at cards, and that small mastery opens the chapter's pillar: female authority inverting male power, the woman commanding a domain the man cannot read. His suspicion that she cheats because he cannot follow the game, his cynicism that "death would not improve her honesty," is the distrust that meets women who excel, and Catherine's admiration of Hannah's honor answers it. Against his bitterness and jealousy, his passivity, Hannah is active, audacious, unyielding, the "corporeal" obstacle of Mr. Spitzer set against a woman who commanded respect even from historical male authorities.

The chapter imagines her sovereign, a house of "vacant rooms" and "gilded footmen" ready to serve her, a reversal that puts a woman in power with men attending, and it frames suffrage as her one consuming love. She is the "true love" of the cause, wanting no romance or domesticity, and her all-consuming activism, her travels and confrontations, her riding "through deserts and cold lands" against "despotic, one-eyed ogres," makes feminism a transnational siege. She rejects the "old maid" stereotype not by marrying but by refusing the frame entirely, and her knowledge of "why explorers and husbands leave home" exposes the coldness inside domestic life, the unawakened woman whose heart has never been reached.

What sets her apart from Catherine is unity: Hannah has "no internal conflicts that divided her," her enemy is "physical rather than psychological," while Catherine's self is fragmented into dream and rivalry. The chapter credits Hannah's focused resistance without pretending it costs nothing; her fear that "yielding once would mean permanent defeat" names the stakes and the strain together. Catherine's tentative wish to understand the suffrage message "now, after years of resistance," registers feminist consciousness slowly permeating even the dreamer, so that the separation between the two women begins, barely, to close. The chapter ends on Hannah's "hardy temper" and "contempt for physical hazard," a woman whose courage the novel names outright, and whose singular purpose stands as its own answer to a world that divides women against themselves.
