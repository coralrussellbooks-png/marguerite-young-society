Chapter 51: The Trumpeter or the Trumpeter's Cry

← Chapter 50 · Guide Home · Chapter 52 →

Research notes: Chapter 51

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Mr. Mouse

Themes: Dreams and Imagination

Philosophy: Freud and Jung · Hinduism · Marguerite Young on Her Craft · Plato and Platonism · Time, Physics, and Chaos Theory

Allusions: Astronomy · Deafness and Sign Language · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · Music and Composers · Women's Suffrage

There is a mouse in Mr. Spitzer's papers.

His name is Mr. Mouse, and he has been busy. He has jumbled symphonies. He has eaten all Mr. Spitzer's chords. He has torn the music to ribbons and made a bed from scraps of old lawsuits and is currently wearing the paper lining of Mr. Spitzer's hat. He has also, in a moment of accidental grandeur, taken the Great Dipper of stars and shaken it like a rattle, pouring out new and unintended constellations across the sky.

Mr. Mouse is chaos. Mr. Mouse is the principle of disorder that is also, because Young always insists on this, the principle of creation. You cannot have new constellations without the shaking of the old ones. You cannot have the ribbons without the torn music. The order that Mr. Spitzer has spent his life defending (law, music, the careful preservation of documents) is being gnawed through from the inside, and the thing doing the gnawing is making something beautiful by accident.

Chapter 51 is the longest and most sweeping of Mr. Spitzer's interior chapters, and it gives him, at last, his fullest expression of what he is and is not. He does not know whether he is “the trumpeter or the trumpeter’s cry, the cry or the echo.” He is the lawyer to starfish. He is twelve cloaks. He is “the sail stalled in the mist, or the sail broke into flame.” He is a legal fiction. He is, at the very bottom of it all, the dream that is dreaming itself, even when there is no counterpart, even when there is nothing to dream about.

What actually happens in Chapter 51

Mr. Spitzer continues his long meditation in the borderland, but Chapter 51 extends that meditation into a catalogue, almost a complete inventory of every role, every form, every self he might be or have been or is not. He is the conductor lifting the baton, or perhaps only the lamplighter, or perhaps only the oarsman striking waves of darkness. He is the musician of silence: music “ever of the silence flowing under the sound of accidental or incidental music.” He is “the spectre dreaming upon the spectre.”

He is the lawyer to the dead: a lawyer to starfish, a lawyer to a dead coachman, a musician who “struck two chords together with one motion of his dead, jeweled hand.” He has stamped documents with the wrong seal by mistake: the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad instead of the President's seal, the “seal of the New England Lighthouse Underwriters” instead of the Grand Union Army. The errors compound. The paperwork of the dead is full of mistakes. Mr. Spitzer, the executor of estates and keeper of wills, has been certifying things incorrectly for longer than anyone has noticed.

The chapter catalogues the people Mr. Spitzer encounters in his wandering: seldom does he meet with anyone who does “not suffer from some form of complex illusion beyond necessity.” The old woman who believes she is a birdcage. The piano tuner who declares all foreheads hollow. The blind portrait painter. The deaf music critic who hears errors without hearing music. The old woman found with a golden arrow through her breast, who believed she was a reindeer. The chapter people these margins of normal consciousness with figures who are, in their particular delusions, only slightly more transparent than everyone else.

It closes on the one great dream: perhaps there was “only one great dream informing all dreamers and all dreams, one great dreamer dreaming through” us all. And the recognition that there is “no threshold between world and world.”

Mr. Mouse and the new constellations

Young's Mr. Mouse is not a metaphor for anything simple. He is not merely entropy, not merely the passage of time, not merely the chaos that undoes careful human work. He is the impresario, the one who produces the show, even while he destroys the score.

Young understood something about the relationship between destruction and creation that runs through her novel's entire structure. The novel itself is a kind of Mr. Mouse operation: it takes the careful categories of realism (chronological narrative, reliable narrators, settled boundaries between what is real and what is dreamed) and gnaws through them, and in doing so creates something that those categories never could have contained. The new constellations Mr. Mouse makes by shaking the Great Dipper are not planned. They are not what anyone would have designed. They are what happens when the old arrangements are released.

Mr. Spitzer opposes Mr. Mouse's will with his will. This is the contest that has defined his life: the lawyer against the mouse, order against chaos, the carefully filed document against the creature who eats documents and builds nests from them. He cannot win. Mr. Mouse will always eat faster than Mr. Spitzer can file. But the opposition itself is the music, Young suggests. The contest between them produces, in its friction, something that neither pure order nor pure chaos could generate alone.

The trumpeter or the trumpeter's cry

“He did not know whether he was the trumpeter or the trumpeter’s cry, the cry or the echo.”

This is the chapter's central question, and it is not rhetorical. Young means it as a genuine puzzle about the nature of identity: are you the source of what you produce, or are you what is produced? Is the musician the player or the music? Is the self the generator of experience or the experience itself?

For Mr. Spitzer, who has spent his existence at a second remove from everything (hearing sounds a minute late, living by other people's dreams, possibly inhabiting his dead brother's identity) the question of whether he is the trumpeter or the cry is especially acute. He has never been certain he was the original. He has always suspected he was a consequence of something that came before him, a sound that arose from a source he cannot locate. If he is the cry, then he disperses when the air stops vibrating. If he is the echo, then he exists only in relation to something that has already happened, and he will fade as the surfaces that reflect him fade.

The trumpeter would be the self that produces. The cry would be the self that is produced. Mr. Spitzer cannot determine which he is. Neither can Young. This is honest.

The lawyer to starfish

He is a lawyer to starfish. A lawyer to a dead coachman.

These are not jokes exactly, though they have the structure of jokes: the absurdist juxtaposition of a legal profession with creatures and figures that cannot benefit from legal representation. But Young means them seriously as a description of what Mr. Spitzer actually does. He is the executor of estates whose owners cannot dispute the terms. He is the keeper of wills whose testators are unavailable for consultation. He handles the legal affairs of the departed, which means he handles the formal arrangements of a world that no longer has a living stakeholder.

The starfish cannot engage with Mr. Spitzer's services. The dead coachman has no interest in the outcome of his estate. But Mr. Spitzer continues to process the paperwork, file the documents, press the wrong seals by mistake. The law continues regardless of whether the parties it serves are present or capable of benefiting. This is what law does. It persists past the point of usefulness as an act of form: a form that Mr. Spitzer maintains because the form is what he has, and without it there is only “the void where he would not be at least this shadow.”

Life as a legal fiction

“Was life a legal fiction, a realm which could never be proved, disproved, an imaginary house with its intricate ivory porches no waves should ever reach, its lights already lost?”

A legal fiction is a fact that is assumed to be true for the purposes of the law, regardless of whether it is actually true. Courts use legal fictions all the time: the fiction that corporations are persons, the fiction that certain presumptions hold unless disproved, the fiction that jurisdiction is established when it technically is not. Legal fictions are useful lies; they allow the law to function in situations where strict adherence to factual reality would prevent any decision from being made.

Young asks: what if life itself is this kind of fiction? What if the assumption that we are alive, that experience is real, that identity is stable: what if all of this is the legal fiction that allows the proceeding to continue? Mr. Spitzer, the executor of impossible estates, the lawyer to starfish, is perfectly positioned to recognize this. He has been processing legal fictions his entire career. He knows how they work. He knows how to proceed as though they are true even when they are clearly not. His life is an instance of proceeding as though the fiction holds.

The old woman who believed she was a reindeer

Young peoples Chapter 51's margins with figures of elaborate and particular delusion: the old woman who believed she was a birdcage, the piano tuner who declared all foreheads hollow, “the blind portrait painter, the deaf” music critic. And then the one that Young lingers on longest: the old woman found dead with a golden arrow through her breast, who had believed she was a reindeer.

She is not played for comedy. She is played for the full weight of what Young means by illusion: the old woman lived in a world where she was a reindeer. That world was as vivid and as internally coherent as any other world. The golden arrow was real: real enough to kill her. The delusion was complex and sustained and particular enough to generate a death that made sense only within its own terms.

Young is not saying all perceptions are equally valid. She is saying that the boundary between the old woman's world and the ordinary world is not as firm as it might appear. Mr. Spitzer, who struggles to distinguish which chair is real and which is its dream, who is “agonized by attributing consciousness to everything,” who cannot tell whether he is alive or dead; he is several degrees away from the old woman who was a reindeer, not infinitely far from her. The novel has been insisting, since Chapter 1, that the borders between these states are thinner than convention requires.

One great dream informing all dreamers

Perhaps there was “only one great dream informing all dreamers and all dreams, one great dreamer dreaming through” us all.

This is the chapter's philosophical summit, and it has a long history. The Upanishads describe Brahman as the universal consciousness in which all individual minds participate. Jung's collective unconscious is the reservoir of shared archetypes and images that every individual psyche draws from. Plato's theory of Forms proposes a realm of perfect Ideas from which all individual instances are imperfect copies. Young arrives at this convergence point not through any of these traditions specifically but through the logic of Mr. Spitzer's particular condition: a man who may be dreaming someone else's dream, who may be an image within an image, who has experienced enough of the world's multiplicity to wonder whether what looks like infinite individual consciousness is actually one thing manifesting in many temporary forms.

Young does not endorse this as doctrine. She gives it to Mr. Spitzer as a possibility that arrives in the borderland between life and death, where the usual distinctions have worn thin enough to let the light through. If he is only dreaming, he notes, then he is accompanied by a great deal of company. All the other dreamers. The one great dream.

No threshold between world and world

“There was no threshold between world and world.”

Young has been approaching this formulation since the novel's first chapter: the grey bus moving through the grey Midwest, the fog that dissolves borders, the household where Catherine has made the distinction between waking and dreaming so permeable that it no longer functions as a distinction. But Mr. Spitzer's version is harder and more absolute than Catherine's: not that the threshold is blurred or difficult to find, but that it does not exist.

We live “by an imperfect concatenation, even as we died—by echoes, tremblings, reverberations, sourceless thoughts.” The threshold we imagine between the living and the dead, between this world and whatever comes after, between the self and the dream of the self; these are the legal fictions that allow the proceeding to continue. They are useful. They may even be necessary. But they are not features of the territory. They are features of our maps.

For Mr. Spitzer, who has no reliable map, who has been stamping documents with the wrong seals for decades, this is not a consolation. It is simply what he has found. “There was no threshold between world and world.” He moves through the rooms of the dead and finds them continuous with the rooms of the living. The mouse eats the music and makes new stars. The legal fictions continue to be processed. And somewhere in all of this, barely visible, the dream keeps dreaming itself.

What to hold onto

Chapter 51 is the novel's most comprehensive portrait of Mr. Spitzer, not as a character in a story but as a form of consciousness, one of the novel's possible answers to the question of what a person is when the usual scaffolding of identity, continuity, and the reliable boundary between states has been removed.

What to carry forward: Mr. Mouse and the new constellations, chaos as the engine of unexpected creation. The trumpeter-or-cry question, which is not resolved and should not be. The lawyer to starfish, keeping the forms of law alive past the point where any living stakeholder can benefit. The old woman who was a reindeer. And the one great dream, dreaming through all the smaller dreams simultaneously, with no threshold between them.

The dream continues. The mouse continues. The music continues in its silence. Mr. Spitzer continues, a supernumerary star, beyond the normal count, giving off a light that may or may not be his own.
