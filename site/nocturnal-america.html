An American Book

Anaïs Nin called Miss MacIntosh, My Darling the voice of “nocturnal America,” as Don Quixote was the voice of Spain and Ulysses of Ireland (the Dowling memoir). Young herself placed the book in what she called “the Grand Tradition, clear back to Don Quixote,” and named Poe among the writers who shaped her (conversation with Friedman and Fuchs). She set the novel near Boston, though she hardly knew New England, because of transcendentalism and “my beloved Emerson,” with Hawthorne and Henry James behind him: “It had to be Boston” (Observation Point, 1965).

The American writers come into the novel the way all Young's sources do, in her own words and mostly without their names. To All My Darlings traces them through more than thirty chapters. This essay follows the four who matter most, Poe, Melville, Whitman, and Emerson, and then the others who pass through.

Poe: The Dream Within the Dream

Young felt close to Poe. Living in Greenwich Village, she said, she saw him at Sheridan Square “any rainy night,” or at least “his astral body” (1983 interview). His best-known lines on dreaming end, “All that we see or seem / Is but a dream within a dream” (“A Dream Within a Dream,” 1849). In Chapter 8, on the bus, Vera asks whether she has come to “a land of eternal night,” of “dead souls, unsoluble mystery, that fleeting image, that dream within the dream?” In Chapter 57, Mr. Spitzer goes one step further: “he only dreamed that he was dreaming—but it was true, this dream of life.”

Poe's tales and poems run under the whole book. Vera's dream of a plain Iowa life in Chapter 4 promises “births and weddings and wedding anniversaries, natural death at the end of a long life, the silver, the golden, never the leaden bells,” the life cycle Poe rang through in “The Bells” (1849), from silver sleigh bells and golden wedding bells to the iron bells of the funeral. The long-haired bus driver of Chapter 5 “had been told of people buried alive and crying in their graves,” the terror of Poe's “The Premature Burial” (1844). In Chapter 44, Mr. Spitzer wonders of the dead Cousin Hannah, “Might there not be heard as in a distant cavern the beating of her dead heart?” Poe's murderer in “The Tell-Tale Heart” (1843) cries, “It is the beating of his hideous heart!”

In Chapter 37, Catherine asks of Cousin Hannah, “Had she not gone to the court of the emperor worm?” Poe's “The Conqueror Worm” (1843) ends with the worm crowned as the hero of the human play. In Chapter 41, Catherine is “the whirlpool, but she was also the still center at the heart of the whirlpool,” and in Poe's “A Descent into the Maelström” (1841) the one who lives is the one who stops fighting and lets the whirlpool carry him. In Chapter 48, Mr. Spitzer hears his own “plucked heart-strings like the lute in the whirlpool.” “The Fall of the House of Usher” (1839) opens with a heart that is a hanging lute: as soon as it is touched, it sounds.

Poe is the American poet of mourning that will not end, the lover in “The Raven” asking questions he knows will be answered “Nevermore.” In that, he is the nearest American cousin to Catherine's house, where nothing dead is ever quite gone.

Melville: The Whale and the Dead Letter

“Moby Dick is my favorite of all American novels,” Young told Studs Terkel in 1965. She lived on Bleecker Street, she said, “in what is probably the exact site of his birthplace,” and she agreed that her novel, like Melville's, is “anti-Calvinist” (Terkel interview). The whale surfaces all through her book. In Chapter 47, Mr. Spitzer walks through drowned towns “where the old spouters spouted like the whales they once had caught.” Ishmael spends his first night in New Bedford at the Spouter-Inn.

In Chapter 55, Mr. Spitzer's law library includes “Laws Governing Whaling,” with its rule “that the man who found the whale was its owner and not the man who put his iron into it—for the man who put his iron into it might be dead a hundred years before the whale was found.” In Moby-Dick the whalemen's law runs the other way: “A Fast-Fish belongs to the party fast to it” (chapter 89). Young turns it over. The whale belongs to whoever finds it last, the way the dead belong to whoever remembers them.

Melville's quiet stories are here too. In Chapter 33, a man who writes harp music for the wind on a bridge goes “to the post office day after day to inquire for a dead letter,” a letter he mailed to himself years ago. Melville's Bartleby once worked in the Dead Letter Office: “Dead letters! does it not sound like dead men?” In Chapter 48, Mr. Spitzer sometimes feels like “a customs officer at an empty port.” Melville was a customs inspector in New York for nineteen years, and Hawthorne before him kept the custom house at Salem.

Whitman: The One and the Many

Young named Whitman directly. Vera, who takes in every stranger she meets until she is all of them, carries what Young called “a Walt Whitman theme, the one and the many,” and Young called herself “a sad Walt Whitman” (Terkel interview). Whitman wrote, “Do I contradict myself? / Very well then I contradict myself, / (I am large, I contain multitudes.)” (“Song of Myself,” section 51).

In Chapter 45, Cousin Hannah, dying, says: “Forgive, too, the self-contradiction of life and death, for both were contained within us.” In Chapter 49, Mr. Spitzer is the multitude: “None knew what the individual was, he being many countries, many times, many dimensions, one of whom no unitary statement could be made.” And in Chapter 50 he is “a handkerchief dropped from the hand of God.” Whitman, looking at the grass, guessed that it was “the handkerchief of the Lord, / A scented gift and remembrancer designedly dropt” (“Song of Myself,” section 6).

The difference is the sadness Young named. Whitman's multitudes are a celebration. Young's are a celebration and a grief at once. Mr. Spitzer contains multitudes because he cannot tell himself from his dead twin.

Emerson and the Transcendental Boston

Emerson is the reason the novel is set where it is. His essay Nature (1836) declares, “I am the lover of uncontained and immortal beauty.” In Chapter 41, Catherine complains that “nature had been unkind to her, an immortal beauty none had ever loved.” In Chapter 49, Mr. Spitzer asks, “Should not the slayer be the slain?” Emerson's poem “Brahma” (1857), drawn from the Bhagavad Gita, begins, “If the red slayer think he slays, / Or if the slain think he is slain.” The transcendentalists taught that the divine lives in nature and in each person, not above them. Young's novel keeps that faith and tests it against grief.

Death the Coachman, and the Others

Emily Dickinson's “Because I could not stop for Death – / He kindly stopped for me –” sends Death to the door as a gentleman caller with a carriage. Young's book is full of carriages, and Death drives them. In Chapter 37, Catherine thinks of Cousin Hannah: “Death had been, at last, her bridegroom.” In Chapter 55, Mr. Spitzer waits calmly, sure “that the oldest coachman will stop and take on another passenger.” In Chapter 61, of the little frog musician: “Death was his coachman.” And when Mr. Spitzer, in Chapter 49, says “For life was itself this indirection, going not by a straight road,” he is close to Dickinson's “Tell all the truth but tell it slant.”

Many others pass through. Vera's images in Chapter 1 pass “like ships in the night,” Longfellow's phrase. Madge's story in Chapter 7 has “the headless horsemen riding with their torches,” out of Washington Irving's Sleepy Hollow. In Chapter 14, “Mr. Spitzer had attended Mr. Spitzer’s funeral,” as Tom Sawyer and Huck Finn watched their own. In Chapter 49, a man “died because of a road not taken,” decades after Robert Frost's poem. Oliver Wendell Holmes's chambered nautilus, his one-horse shay, and his Boston “Hub” all turn up in Mr. Spitzer's chapters. Young treated Twain and Dreiser, she said, as “great thinkers, great dreamers” (Ruas interview), and she let all of them dream in her book.

The Radical Immanence Reading

In the language of Radical Immanence, Whitman's one and many, Mr. Spitzer “being many countries, many times, many dimensions,” is Pillar 1 (Not One Self). Poe's mourning that answers “Nevermore,” the dead heart still heard beating, is Pillar 3 (Grief Doesn't End). And Emerson's divine in nature, Whitman's grass as the handkerchief of the Lord, Mr. Spitzer as “a handkerchief dropped from the hand of God,” are Pillar 10 (The Sacred Is Here).
