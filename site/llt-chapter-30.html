Chapter 30

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Eustace · Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: Not One Self · The Past Doesn't Go Away

The Questions

Eustace is “many persons” at once, shifting at Catherine’s whim. Is a construction kept deliberately unfinished still a sealed dream?

Cousin Hannah devotes herself to “the overthrow of the dream and of the dreamer.” Is confronting the dream the opposite of Catherine’s sealing, or another seal of its own?

Cousin Hannah moves through the world in a dozen disguises. Is that a plural self, or a single certainty wearing costumes?

What does the book gain by setting a woman of pure action beside a woman of pure dream?

Catherine’s invention produces Eustace, and Eustace is strange in a way worth marking: invisible, shape-shifting, “many persons” at once, a mockingbird and an old woman and Wellington and a blind astronomer, all dictated by Catherine’s whim. Within her sealed dream, this one construction is kept deliberately unfinished, never allowed to settle into a single form. It is Not One Self as plaything, a figure built to stay many. And yet it lives inside the seal; Eustace is open within a closed world, plurality furnishing a locked room. The chapter shows that keeping a construction fluid is not the same as keeping it open, if the whole house around it is sealed.

Against Catherine the chapter sets Cousin Hannah, and sets her as the opposite: not dream but action, not retreat but “the overthrow of the dream and of the dreamer.” She travels dangerous places, defies every expectation, decides fast and lives by her decisions. She is genuinely magnificent, and the surface of the chapter offers her as the vigorous answer to Catherine’s opium sleep. But her certainty is worth watching. To devote one’s whole life to overthrowing the dream is to build a blueprint of one’s own, a sealed conviction that action is the answer and the dream the enemy, and a blueprint that decisive can seal as tightly as any dream. The book is planting a doubt it will collect later, at her deathbed, when she calls her great battles as nothing.

Her disguises complicate the plural-self reading in a useful way. Cousin Hannah appears as an Arab king, a lazar, a dozen others, and this looks like Not One Self, a woman who will not be sorted into one identity. But it may be the opposite: a single, fixed certainty wearing costumes, one unwavering will moving through many outfits. Where Eustace is genuinely plural and Mr. Spitzer genuinely porous, Cousin Hannah may be genuinely singular, sealed around one conviction, and merely disguised. The chapter leaves the question open, and the openness is the point: a costume-change is not the same as a plural self, and the book is teaching the reader to tell them apart.

Time runs loose around both women. Catherine invites the dead to play cards; Cousin Hannah knows events before they happen; the sea captain goes down into “that creation from which no man returned.” The Past Doesn't Go Away works on the dreamer and the woman of action alike, the dead as near as the living, the future as available as the past. Neither the dream nor the deed escapes it. Whatever Cousin Hannah’s vigor overthrows, it does not overthrow the layered time both women swim in.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Cousin Hannah as its answer, action and decision as the cure for Catherine’s dream, and the reading that takes her disguises as a settled instance of the plural self. Neither the vigorous cure nor the easy plurality is secure.

The footing is that keeping a construction fluid, as Catherine keeps Eustace, is not the same as keeping it open, if the house around it is sealed. Cousin Hannah’s devotion to overthrowing the dream is itself a blueprint, a certainty that can seal as tightly as any dream, and her many disguises may be one fixed will in costumes rather than a plural self. And The Past Doesn't Go Away works on the woman of action as on the dreamer; the deed escapes the layered time no better than the dream. Fluidity within a seal is not openness, and certainty about action is its own construction. Whether Cousin Hannah is plural or merely disguised is left standing, a question the book will return to.

For Reflection

Is a construction kept endlessly fluid the same as one kept open, or can flux itself be sealed inside a closed room?

Can certainty about the value of action seal as tightly as any dream?

What is the difference between a plural self and a single will wearing many costumes?
