Movement Six: What the Novel Teaches

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Chandelier · Mr. Res Tacamah · Mr. Titus Bonebreaker · Vera Cartwheel

Symbols: The House

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest

Definition from research

Marguerite Young taught writing for decades: at the Iowa Writers' Workshop, at the New School for Social Research, at Fordham University. She was, by her students' accounts, a beloved and transformative teacher. When asked in an interview whether she would write a book about writing, she said she would not live long enough. She was wrong about that only in the most technical sense. She had already written it. Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is that book, embedded inside a novel, operational on every page, teaching by example at such a level of sustained mastery that the instruction is invisible to the casual reader and inescapable to the attentive one.

Young tried to leave pebbles along the road so that no one could get lost. This is a teacher's description of a text. The pebbles are the literary devices, not deployed as ornament but as navigation: here is how you follow this sentence, here is how you track this motif across fifty chapters, here is how you know when a repetition is structural rather than accidental. The devices are the map. What Anaïs Nin calls the "cellular expansion" of the novel, the weaving of "each connecting cell, with unbroken bridges, from word to word, image to image, phrase to phrase," is the same thing described from the reader's position rather than the writer's. From the writer's position it is architecture. From the reader's position it is instruction. The novel teaches you to read it while you are reading it, which is the only pedagogy that works at this scale.

The five pillars of Young's philosophy require five different reading skills, and the novel teaches each one through the device it deploys most insistently in the service of that pillar. Understanding this relationship is not an academic exercise. It is the practical argument for why this novel, 1,198 pages long, does not fail its reader despite its scale. Every device is a pebble. The road holds.

The plural self pillar requires the reading skill of tracking identity across discontinuity: following a character who is not the same person on page 900 as on page 14, recognizing the persistence of an identity that has no stable center. Young teaches this through her handling of Mr. Spitzer, using juxtaposition as the primary device. He is placed beside his dead brother, beside Catherine, beside Vera, beside the old clam-digger, beside himself at different ages and states of consciousness, and the reader is never allowed to resolve these juxtapositions into a single coherent portrait. Young said: "If I removed the repetitions I would remove one of the motifs of life itself." The repetitions are not failures of editing. They are the curriculum. Each time Mr. Spitzer appears in a new juxtaposition, the reader's skill at holding a plural identity in view is tested and developed. By the novel's end, the reader has been trained to inhabit a consciousness that does not require coherence, which is the philosophical claim the pillar makes and the practical skill a writer needs to create characters of genuine complexity.

The illusion as a primary pillar requires the reading skill of sustained suspension of judgment: the ability to hold two contradictory explanations of a scene simultaneously without resolving them. Young teaches this through her radical use of personification, which in this novel is not a decorative technique but an epistemological one. When Mr. Chandelier becomes the mother's companion, when the drug bottle Mr. Res Tacamah develops ears to hear her commands, when the invisible elephant in the corridor becomes a perfectly logical subject of legal argument about the burden of proof, Young is not being fanciful. She is teaching the reader that the mind's constructions are real objects in the world of this novel, that there is no safe position from which to dismiss them as mere delusion. The personification trains the reader to treat all perceptions with equal seriousness. This is essential craft knowledge: the writer who dismisses the inner life of a character's imagination as less real than their external actions has already failed. Young's personification teaches otherwise, by practice, over hundreds of pages.

The grief as permanent weather pillar requires the reading skill of sustained attention to accumulation: the ability to notice that what looks like repetition is actually deepening, that the fifth time a motif appears it is not the same as the first time, that meaning is not stated but deposited. Young teaches this through her anaphora, most concentrated in the passage where Miss MacIntosh teaches Vera that when nothing has happened, one is supposed to admit that "not a leaf had fallen, that not a blow had struck, that not a wave had broken, that not a shadow had stirred." The anaphora is not rhetorical amplification. It is the structure of grief itself: the same absence named again and again in different forms, each naming not diminishing the previous but adding to it, until the accumulated weight of "not" becomes the presence it denies. Young told interviewers she could not shorten the novel because obsession is what possesses people. She understood that grief does not compress. The reader who has been trained by the novel's anaphoric structures has learned to write grief that accumulates rather than grief that summarizes, which is the difference between literature and sentiment.

The witness pillar requires the reading skill of attention without intervention: the ability to observe a scene fully without the narrative voice pressing toward a conclusion. Young teaches this through her imagery, which is always concrete and never interpreted within the sentence that contains it. "What was the cause of that great conflagration? What was the date? How many were unburied? How many were not identified?" These are Miss MacIntosh's questions to Vera, cascading without answer, and the narrative does not resolve them. The images accumulate without a settling commentary. The reader is trained, through hundreds of such sequences, to stay in the act of witnessing rather than rushing to judgment, to let the image do its work without the assistance of the authorial voice explaining what the image means. This is one of the most difficult skills in prose fiction to teach. Young teaches it by enacting it across 1,198 pages without flinching.

Recursive construction requires the reading skill of recognizing structural echo: the ability to notice that the thing happening now has happened before at a different scale, that the novel is building a pattern of patterns. Young teaches this through her extraordinary use of motif, which in this novel never stays at a single level of meaning. The umbrella is Miss MacIntosh's practical protection from weather and also her entire philosophical position made portable and also the object that appears waterlogged on the beach at the end and also Mr. Bonebreaker's prophetic instrument and also the novel's recurring emblem of what it means to try to carry common sense through a world that keeps raining illusions. The reader who has been trained by Young's motif-work knows how to build an object in fiction that accumulates meaning across a narrative without being reduced to symbol, that remains itself while also being everything it has gathered. "Anything which was worth happening at all was worth happening twice," Mr. Spitzer reflects, "that nothing which happened once was real." This is Young's structural principle stated as philosophy: the novel builds its reality through repetition with variation, and the reader who has learned to track the variations has learned recursive construction from the inside.

Young said she would not live long enough to write the book about writing. She had already written it, structured it, hidden it inside the novel she spent sixteen years building. The book about writing is the novel itself, in the relationship between its devices and its pillars, in the way each device teaches the reading skill the corresponding pillar requires. The pebbles along the road are the literary devices. The road is the five pillars. The destination, which the novel never quite arrives at and never stops moving toward, is what Young called the fullness and completeness of the motion: "It is in the fullness and completeness of the motion that one achieves understanding." She left everything she knew on the page. A writer who reads it carefully enough is reading a masterclass that has been running since 1965 and has not stopped.
