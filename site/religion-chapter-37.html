Chapter 37

On Chapter 37 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Christianity and Religion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 24, 2026

The chapter dissolves the old border, "there being no limits between heaven and hell," refusing the clean division of the two realms.

“But who was the dreamer”: “You’re only a sort of thing in his dream!” Tweedledee tells Alice of the sleeping Red King (Lewis Carroll, Through the Looking-Glass, 1871). See Alice in Wonderland. Zhuangzi dreamed he was a butterfly and woke not knowing “whether I am now a butterfly, dreaming I am a man” (Chuang Tzŭ, trans. Herbert A. Giles, 1889). See Taoism and Zhuangzi.

“trees of enormous crystal growing with their roots in the clouded sky”: The Bhagavad Gita (15.1) speaks of an eternal fig tree, the ashvattha, with its roots above and its branches below. See Hinduism.

“birds who could speak ecclesiastical Latin”: Ecclesiastical Latin was developed to discuss Christian thought. In Chapter 34 Catherine was married by a St. Bernard who spoke church Latin. See the Chapter 34 resources. Earlier in Chapter 34.

"The cross had turned into the immortal rose," and the rose back into a cross, a gibbet with creaking chains, weds the crucifixion to the rose of divine love, beauty and torment turning into each other until madness rules.

“Her head was eaten by great birds”: “The eye that mocketh at his father, and despiseth to obey his mother, the ravens of the valley shall pick it out, and the young eagles shall eat it” (Proverbs 30:17). See The Bible.

“that which was void and without form”: “And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep” (Genesis 1:2). See Genesis. Earlier in Chapters 1, 12, 15 and 20.

“the tormented whirlwinds turning upon spindles”: “The infernal hurricane that never rests / Hurtles the spirits onward in its rapine” (Dante, Inferno 5, trans. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, 1867), the circle of the lovers. See Dante.

“she had lifted her veil”: In Thomas Moore’s “The Veiled Prophet of Khorassan,” from Lalla Rookh (1817), the prophet Mokanna hides his face behind a silver veil, and when he lifts it, it is hideous. See Romantic Poets.

“The last conch trumpet had already blown”: Triton blew his conch to call back the waters of the flood (Ovid, Metamorphoses 1). “In a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trump: for the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised incorruptible” (1 Corinthians 15:52). See Greek Mythology and The Bible.
