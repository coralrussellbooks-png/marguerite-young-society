Chapter 59: The Borrowed Skeleton

← Chapter 58 · Guide Home · Chapter 60 →

Research notes: Chapter 59

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Duality and Doubles · Fate and Chance · Light and Darkness · Music, Sound, and Silence · Order, Chaos, and Discipline

Symbols: Lamps, Candles, and Light · Music and Instruments · Twins and Doubles

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Music and Composers · Suicide in History and Literature · The Bible · The History of Opium

If Chapter 58 left you with the image of two men making invisible music together on library steps, Chapter 59 takes you somewhere deeper and stranger: inside Mr. Spitzer's growing conviction that the body he inhabits is not his own.

Stay with him. This is one of the novel's most philosophically rich chapters, and it arrives exactly when you're ready for it.

What actually happens

Mr. Spitzer walks the city, tossing coins to street musicians and beggars with indiscriminate generosity. He cannot always tell who is poor and who is wealthy, who is living and who is dead, who is a professional musician and who is destitute. He gives anyway. And as he walks, he thinks: about his dead brother Peron, about chance and randomness and order, about the strange fact that he cannot be certain the skeleton inside his body is his own.

By the chapter's end, he arrives at a provisional, tentative conclusion: perhaps everything would be explained by simply admitting that he is already dead.

The stolen skeleton

This is the image that will stay with you from Chapter 59. Mr. Spitzer becomes convinced that at some point, he cannot say when, there was a "substitution": his original skeleton was replaced by another, and the body he moves through the world in is not quite his. He feels like “a shadow trembling in the air,” a man wearing a borrowed mask.

Young is not playing this for comedy or horror. She means it philosophically. If you cannot be certain that your own body is yours, what exactly is "you"? Mr. Spitzer distinguishes between three things: the body (which he suspects is illusion), the soul (which is all-informing, the deep water of a person), and the spirit (which is temporary as the wind). He suspects that transformations between these can happen without a person noticing. That you could wake up, or not wake up, as someone slightly other than who you were.

And here is the twist: Mr. Spitzer suspects this isn't unique to him. "Every man and every woman might have suffered a substitution like his own," he thinks. He distrusts himself and therefore cannot trust others, not because they are dishonest, but because they may not know what they are either.

This is Young's deepest quarrel with the stable, bounded self. She doesn't think there is one.

The horn-blower's double life

Among the street musicians Mr. Spitzer passes is an old horn-blower: ragged, playing glassy jazz, apparently destitute. Mr. Spitzer gives him coins. Then he discovers that this same man is also a member of a prestigious symphony orchestra.

The man is simultaneously a beggar and a professional. Both are true. Neither cancels out the other. Young offers this not as an ironic twist but as evidence for something she has been building across the entire novel: that appearances are unreliable not because people are deceitful, but because people genuinely contain multitudes that don't resolve into a single identity.

Mr. Spitzer already knew this, of course. He is both a lawyer and a composer. He is both alive and, possibly, already dead.

His brother Peron

Peron Spitzer (Mr. Spitzer's twin brother, now dead) appears throughout this chapter as a kind of shadow twin, a constant companion to every thought. Where Mr. Spitzer fears the length of life, Peron feared its brevity. They are mirror images: one man terrified life would go on too long, the other terrified it would end too soon. Between them, they cover the entire territory of human anxiety about time.

Peron's suicide comes up here as a meditation, not a wound. Mr. Spitzer turns it over carefully: what if suicide were not a negative impulse, but a creative one? What if it were, his words, "the most creative act, even like sexual love begetting another person"? A desire not to escape life, but to transcend its limits, to seek an immortal love beyond what living allows?

This is not a position Young endorses or condemns. It is what Mr. Spitzer genuinely wonders, as he walks and gives away coins and feels the creaking of a skeleton that may not be his.

Darkness as illumination

Peron, we learn, feared darkness. Mr. Spitzer has made a different peace with it. He has come to experience darkness as his "only light," his illumination. In the dark, he can see things the daylight conceals: the glimmering fins of invisible fish, the iridescence of hidden stars, the interior gleamings that ordinary perception misses.

This is one of Young's recurring arguments: that those who dwell closest to darkness, to loss, to dissolution, can perceive things the comfortably solid cannot. Vera, living in her mother's opium-haunted house, learned to navigate hallucination while others had never needed to. Mr. Spitzer, carrying his dead brother and his borrowed skeleton, can hear silent music and see in the dark. The loss is real. So is the seeing.

Chance and the music of billiard balls

Chapter 59 is one of the novel's most explicit meditations on chance, and it's worth slowing down for. Mr. Spitzer composes music based on the patterns of migratory birds in flight, or the trajectories of billiard balls struck at random: patterns that cannot be exactly repeated, that produce something beautiful precisely because they are unrepeatable.

He is asking: is life like this? Are we governed by chance? And if so, does chance produce chaos, or does it produce its own kind of order, like foam patterns on surf that are never twice the same but are always, unmistakably, foam?

He cannot answer this. He is afraid of everything (afraid of loss, of silence, of thresholds, of his own shadow) and he admits that he "would be most afraid if he had no fear," because his fear is the sign that something in him is still paying attention, still alive to what might be lost. Fear, for Mr. Spitzer, is not the opposite of courage. It is the proof of engagement.

No dividing line

The chapter keeps returning to a single, insistent claim: there is "no dividing line, no distinction between life and death." Birth and death are a "wild sea without a shore." The living and the dead are not in separate rooms. They are in the same room, breathing the same air, and the walls between them are thinner than we pretend.

Mr. Spitzer is not certain he is on one side of this line or the other. And by the end of the chapter, he arrives at his tentative conclusion: perhaps the simplest explanation, the one that would make everything else cohere, is that he is already dead. Not dramatically. Not with trumpets. But quietly, in some way he passed over without noticing, and has been walking through the aftermath ever since.

This is not despair. It is, he says, his "only wisdom." The confusion is not failure. It is what he knows.

What to hold onto

“Mr. Spitzer in his black cape,” walking through the city, giving coins to musicians he cannot quite see clearly, carrying a skeleton that may not be his own, hearing bells ringing underwater, unable to find the shore between life and death. He is afraid of everything. He keeps walking. He keeps giving.

That is Chapter 59.
