Summary

On an early spring morning, Vera enters the Greasy Spoon, between the undertaker's and the beauty parlor, and meets Esther Longtree. She is a big, crossed-eyed waitress with a bald spot, a deformed foot, and a belly swollen for as long as anyone remembers. She believes she is always pregnant, now with "the little shadow boxer," whose two hearts she can feel. His fathers were traveling salesmen. Dr. O'Leary once called her the immortal medical case. She ponders who killed Cock Robin and answers that everyone and no one did.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a vivid description of an early spring morning in a foggy, desolate town. The undertaker’s hearse, old ghostly automobiles, a grey wagon with a bony mule, and the "Greasy Spoon" restaurant set an eerie, liminal atmosphere between life and death, beauty and decay. This scene foreshadows the themes of birth, death, and waiting that pervade the chapter.
  • Esther is introduced as a robust, vibrant woman whose vitality contrasts with her bleak surroundings. She is perpetually pregnant, known for her "everlasting pregnancy" which is at once a burden and a source of hope. Her physical descriptions (ponderous red lips, flowing black hair, swollen belly, and signs of morning and evening sickness) highlight her liminal state between life and death, fertility and barrenness. Her relationship with the stillborn children she has carried and lost is deeply emotional and symbolic.
  • A central passage concerns Esther’s countless stillborn children, "as many as the autumn leaves," and the sterile, puny salesmen who fathered them. This theme addresses infertility, loss, and the void of unfulfilled potential. Esther’s maternal love extends even to these lost children, and her loneliness is compounded by the lack of recognition or mourning from others. The stillborn are described haunting her, whispering and crying, emphasizing the tragic and ghostly nature of her pregnancy.
  • Old Dr. O’Leary’s character provides a counterpoint as a doctor who is senile and confused but insists Esther is a unique “immortal medical case.” He predicts she will never die in childbirth and attributes much to atmospheric, cosmic influences. This passage blends medical realism with mythic and cosmic symbolism, reinforcing Esther’s extraordinary, almost otherworldly condition.
  • Esther reflects on her parents’ ignorance and misunderstanding, their teasing, and their failure to comprehend her life and condition. The passage reveals her social isolation, the stigma she endures, and the gulf between her interior reality and outward appearances. Her parents see her as a failure and an old maid, yet she has lived a secret, wild life full of pregnancies and losses unknown to them.
  • There are various men (salesmen, horn-blowers, detectives, and others) who have passed through her life, many being sterile fathers of her stillborn children. This passage underscores the transient, impersonal nature of her relationships and the loneliness at the heart of her condition. The repeated references to these men also highlight the theme of absence and loss.
  • Despite the repeated losses, Esther maintains hope for a living child. She has prepared layettes, toys, and clothes, knitting a little black suit for the child she expects to survive. Her hope is described as “luminescent,” and she embraces her condition with a mixture of strength, sorrow, and resilience. This passage is crucial for understanding her perseverance and the symbolic weight of her pregnancy.
  • The chapter delves into Esther’s psychological and emotional life, her conversations with the stillborn, the lovebirds she raises, and the voices she hears. She is portrayed as deeply connected to the unseen world of the lost children and haunted by their presence. Her actions (knitting without thread, playing piano, and caring for lovebirds) symbolize her ongoing struggle to nurture and give meaning to her experience.
  • Near the end, Esther poses the question “Who killed cock robin?” This classic riddle symbolizes the mystery of death, loss, and culpability. She concludes that nobody alone is to blame; the child was killed by “the death that was in him from the time of his birth” and by the forces of nature like the wind, night, day, atmospheric pressure. This acceptance of death as an inevitable cosmic force is a poignant thematic resolution for the chapter.
  • The last passage reveals Esther’s acceptance of her condition and her defiant hope. She knows she has been “always pregnant,” never the murderer of her children, and she prepares for the winnowing test of childbirth. Her strength, despite sorrow and isolation, is emphasized as she continues her life amid the bleak landscape.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Esther Longtree

  • Esther is heavily pregnant, with a “bald spot shaped like a large coin” and “long black hair flowing free”.
  • She is described as “plum-colored but square,” with “ponderous red lips” and “lustrous, dull but shining” eyes.
  • Her body is both a source of strength and burden; she experiences morning and evening sickness, swollen breasts, and a deformed foot.
  • Esther is simultaneously robust and vulnerable, embodying vitality and suffering.
  • She is known as the “passive policeman’s only daughter,” indicating a background of isolation and misunderstanding.
  • She broods over her “everlasting pregnancy,” symbolizing continuous loss and hope.
  • Despite her hardships, Esther is compassionate, loving, and hopeful, often talking to herself and her unborn child.
  • Esther’s pregnancy is metaphoric and literal, reflecting repeated stillbirths and lost children.
  • She refers to the “little shadow boxer” inside her, a foetus representing hope, fear, and the unknown.
  • The pregnancy signifies the struggle between creation and destruction, life and death, hope and despair.
  • Salesmen and transient men pass through her life, often fathering her stillborn children.
  • Her parents misunderstand her, seeing her as a failure or beast of burden.
  • She holds a secret inner life that no one fully comprehends, except the “stillborn and the white birds”.
  • Esther experiences loneliness, resilience, and a profound sense of mystery about her condition.
  • She wrestles with feelings of failure and hope, often laughing, crying, and brooding.
  • She fears being discovered by her mother but also finds solace in her secret world, including her lovebirds.

Doctor O’Leary

  • Dr. O’Leary is portrayed as an aging, somewhat senile physician who embodies both a figure of traditional medical authority and a symbol of the limitations of human knowledge and mortality.
  • Dr. O’Leary is described as "drunkenly leaning on his cane," with "old eyes frozen" and suffering from "hardening of the artery of the brain" a condition that makes him senile and confused. His physical frailty contrasts sharply with the vitality and mysterious condition of Esther Longtree, the central character in this chapter. He is aware of his own decline and admits that he has "retired from practice" because of his confusion and failing faculties.
  • Despite his failing health and mental clarity, Dr. O’Leary offers Esther "everlasting hope," encouraging her that she "will never die in child birth" and that her labor will be "easy" and "like passing a seed." He calls her "the immortal medical case," suggesting that her condition, her "everlasting pregnancy" and her strange, continuous fertility is an anomaly beyond his medical understanding. He believes that although she is "over-hopeful and bloated with no form within the form," her problem will end with her, and her disappointments will "pass away and not be seen again upon the earth." Yet, he cannot fully explain her condition, reflecting his own limitations as a doctor.
  • Dr. O’Leary also represents the conflict between science and imagination or superstition. He once believed in a simple, anatomical understanding of the human body but now sees "false eyes, false cartilage, false heart," and "false generations," causing him confusion and leading to his withdrawal from active practice. His perspective underscores the mysterious, almost supernatural aspects of Esther’s condition, which defy conventional medical explanation.
  • Dr. O’Leary’s comments on atmospheric pressure, the whirling stars, and the cold ether suggest a more cosmic or metaphysical dimension to Esther’s pregnancy and to human life and death generally. He attributes some medical phenomena to "the unusual atmospheric pressure" and "the thin ether," implying that natural forces beyond human control or understanding influence human fate.
  • Dr. O’Leary is a tragic yet compassionate figure as an old doctor who has seen many failures and losses but still offers hope and comfort. His character illuminates the themes of mystery, the limits of knowledge, and the intersection of science and the supernatural in the novel. His presence in the chapter emphasizes Esther Longtree’s extraordinary condition and the enigmatic quality of life, birth, and death in the story.

The little shadow boxer

  • The "little shadow boxer" is a deeply symbolic and poignant figure intimately connected to Esther Longtree's experience of perpetual pregnancy and loss. This character represents the unborn child whom Esther carries within her womb as a silent, elusive presence that embodies both hope and despair.
  • Esther refers to the little shadow boxer as a persistent, almost ghostly entity: "the little shadow boxer who in the waters of her womb had grown and grown and had warded off every blow". This suggests a resilient spirit, a life force that struggles to survive against overwhelming odds, such as the sterility of the fathers and the harshness of the environment. The child is portrayed as having a pulse and movements that Esther can feel the "little shadow foot" playing against her side and the heartbeat "accelerated" under her heart indicating a tangible yet intangible presence.
  • The name "shadow boxer" itself implies a figure fighting an invisible, perhaps impossible battle since shadow boxing is the act of fighting an opponent that is not there. This metaphor reflects Esther's ongoing struggle with her repeated stillbirths and miscarriages, a battle against loss and absence. The child remains hidden, "crouched where I place my hand," with folded arms and knees drawn up, symbolizing both vulnerability and endurance.
  • Esther’s relationship to the little shadow boxer is complex: she is filled with "everlasting hope" for its birth, yet haunted by the memory of many other stillborn children that "tease" her and whose fathers were sterile or absent. The fetus is described as growing "opalescent and unforgiving," a luminous and mysterious presence within her, evoking both beauty and sorrow. The child embodies the tension between life and death, presence and absence, reality and imagination.
  • The little shadow boxer is linked to Esther’s identity and sense of self. She often refers to her pregnancy as "everlasting" and describes herself as carrying "two hearts, mine and his". This duality emphasizes the inseparability of mother and child, and the emotional and physical weight Esther bears. Despite her loneliness, misunderstanding, and the skepticism of others, including her parents, Esther nurtures this secret life with love and care, preparing for the child's arrival by knitting clothes and collecting toys, even though she has suffered repeated losses.
  • The child also symbolizes a broader existential struggle. Esther contemplates the possibility that "only the dead gave birth" in her world, reflecting on the harshness of her environment (the Arctic glare, the cold starlight, the thinning ether) and the seeming impossibility of birth and renewal. The little shadow boxer stands as a figure of hope against this bleak backdrop, a potential miracle amid despair.
  • The little shadow boxer is a powerful symbol of Esther Longtree's enduring hope, maternal love, and the tragedy of repeated loss. It encapsulates the themes of life and death, visibility and invisibility, and the deeply personal yet universal experience of longing and sorrow in the face of infertility and stillbirth. This character's presence shapes Esther's identity and gives meaning to her perpetual state of pregnancy, making the child both a source of suffering and a beacon of hope throughout the chapter.

Sad father sub-plot

  • The sad father subplot is embodied in the figure of a stout, fat drummer who visits Esther Longtree. This man, once happily married with a business and a good home, has suffered a profound personal tragedy: the death of his wife’s sister’s little girl from scarlet fever. This loss devastates him so deeply that he abandons his business and family to wander the roads as a Fuller brush salesman, haunted by the memory of the child he lost. He stops at Esther’s restaurant, tearful and remorseful, carrying a bag of candy for the little girl he cannot forget. His story is a poignant reflection on grief, loss, and the enduring impact of death on the living.
  • The subplot serves several purposes in the narrative. First, it parallels Esther’s own experience of loss and endless pregnancy with no surviving child, deepening the theme of sorrow and the human struggle with infertility and stillbirth. The man’s sorrowful pilgrimage underscores the loneliness and heartbreak that accompany the absence of children, mirroring Esther’s isolation and the burden of her perpetual pregnancy. His presence highlights the shared grief of those connected by loss, even if their circumstances differ.
  • Second, this subplot reveals the complex interactions between Esther and the men in her life, who are often “the fathers” of her stillborn children: men who come and go, sometimes with remorse, sometimes with indifference. This father’s visit, marked by his visible sorrow and his offering of candy, contrasts with others who are more detached, emphasizing the varied ways men respond to loss and responsibility.
  • The sad father subplot enriches the atmosphere of the chapter by adding a human face to the abstract themes of death, loss, and the failure of reproduction that permeate the text. It personalizes grief, making it tangible through the story of a man overwhelmed by his inability to protect or retain a child he loved. This subplot deepens the reader’s understanding of Esther’s world as one filled with ghosts, disappointments, and fragmented hopes.
  • The sad father subplot is a poignant narrative thread that complements and intensifies the chapter’s exploration of loss, grief, and the tragic realities of stillbirth and infertility experienced by Esther Longtree and those around her. It brings emotional depth and humanizes the broader themes of the novel.

  • Esther Longtree may symbolize the sexual revolution in the United States by considering her character’s embodiment of themes such as fertility, sexuality, societal repression, and the struggle for female autonomy.
  • Esther is depicted as "everlastingly pregnant," carrying multiple stillborn children, representing an ongoing, unresolved fertility and reproductive issue. She is physically swollen, with symptoms of pregnancy (morning sickness, evening sickness, swollen breasts) but no viable birth occurs. This paradox of constant pregnancy without fulfillment can symbolize the tension in the 1960s between traditional expectations of women centered on motherhood and the emerging desire for sexual freedom and reproductive control. Esther’s "everlasting pregnancy" may reflect the societal burden placed on women to reproduce and the frustration when that role is unfulfilled or controlled by external forces like sterile fathers or societal norms.
  • Esther is described as robustly alive and free in some ways like her hair that flows loose "at whatever cost to sanitation," and she tosses it regularly as if to preserve her freedom. This image evokes a woman resisting social constraints, symbolic of the sexual liberation movement where women began to assert control over their own bodies and sexuality. Yet, she is also trapped in a "womb" that is both a restaurant and a grave, a liminal space between life and death, fertility and barrenness, reflecting the societal repression of female sexuality and the stigma around pregnancy out of wedlock or outside accepted norms.
  • The numerous stillborn children Esther carries (hidden under leaves, buds, snow, rivers) represent unrealized potential, lost possibilities, and the silencing of female sexual and reproductive expression. The fact that the fathers are "sterile" or "impotent" men, often traveling salesmen or transient figures, can symbolize the failure of patriarchal structures to support or recognize women's full sexual agency. The stillborns haunt Esther, much like the repressed desires and consequences of sexual control haunt society during the sexual revolution.
  • Esther is simultaneously described as "brilliant but dumb," "ugly outside... but beautiful inside," "experienced and sought after" yet also an "old maid" misunderstood by her own parents. This duality reflects the conflicted social status of women during this time who were beginning to break free from traditional roles but still faced judgment, misunderstanding, and marginalization. Esther’s education and cultural refinement contrast with her physical and social marginalization, symbolizing the new woman’s struggle to reconcile intellect, sexuality, and societal acceptance.
  • Esther’s "immortal medical case" status, with the old doctor predicting she will "be pregnant until the stars had fallen," suggests the ongoing, unresolved nature of women’s sexual liberation. The "everlasting pregnancy" can metaphorically represent the period of social transformation as an era pregnant with possibilities but fraught with struggle, loss, and hope. Her hopefulness despite repeated disappointments mirrors the spirit of the sexual revolution, which challenged existing norms and sought new forms of expression and liberation.
  • Her affinity with natural and mythical elements (wild mares pregnant by the wind, the great skull, the starry heavens, the moon, and the stars) links Esther to a broader symbolic framework of fertility, creation, and cosmic forces. This elevates her personal reproductive tragedy to a universal level, reflecting the countercultural embrace of naturalism, mysticism, and a redefinition of human sexuality as part of a cosmic order or liberation from mechanistic, repressive traditions.
  • Esther Longtree symbolizes the complexities and contradictions of the sexual revolution in America: the tension between fertility and barrenness, freedom and repression, hope and despair, tradition and change. Her character embodies the social anxieties about female sexuality, the quest for autonomy, and the redefinition of womanhood during a period of profound cultural upheaval.
  • Her story encapsulates the bodily, social, and legal struggles over reproduction, economic survival, and patriarchal oppression that shaped the conditions the women’s rights movement sought to change through activism.

  • The spelling "foetus" entered English from Late Latin (around the 16th century) as a "hypercorrection" based on Latin words like amoenus, adding the 'o' where it wasn't etymologically correct, though it became common in British/Commonwealth English and medical fields for a time. The true Latin root is fetus, meaning "offspring," making "fetus" the etymologically correct form, now the standard globally in medicine and preferred in the U.S., while "foetus" persists in non-medical UK usage.

  • Who Killed Cock Robin is an English nursery rhyme. Although the earliest known record of the song is from the mid-eighteenth century, there is some evidence that it is much older. Versions of the story appear to exist in other countries, including Germany. There are a number of theories that have been advanced to explain the meaning of the rhyme. According to Celtic traditions, Lugh, the sun god who dies as the nights get longer after the summer solstice, is marked in the old Celtic pictographic calendar with a bow-and-arrow shape. Lugh was the primary god representing the red sun and was also known in Welsh as Coch Rhi Ben, which when anglicized would mean Cock Robin (coch meaning red, rhi meaning lord, ben meaning leader) This may be a reference to the belief that souls became birds after death. The sparrow who kills him with his bow and arrow may represent Brân the Blessed, the god of winter in the form of a raven.

  • The different salesmen, such as the piano-tuner, the horn-blower, the tin roofer, the soap salesman, the rubber salesman, the featherweight, the Chicago detective, and others highlight the diversity yet uniformly disappointing nature of these transient relationships. Each is portrayed as a figure who fails to fulfill the biological or emotional role of a father, symbolizing the sterile, fractured modern world where connections are fleeting and fruitless. Their various professions suggest a broad cross-section of society, emphasizing that this sterility and failure transcend social or occupational boundaries. The salesmen symbolize the broader theme of displacement and rootlessness. They are drifters, passing through, unable or unwilling to form lasting bonds. They also embody the void and the mystery of loss that permeates Esther’s life, as they often write years later to inquire if the child they fathered was ever born, unaware or unwilling to accept the reality of their sterility and the stillbirths.
