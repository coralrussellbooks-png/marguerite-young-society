Chapter 38: The Loony Bin

On Chapter 38 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of A Guide to Reading Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Research notes: Chapter 38

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Love · Music, Sound, and Silence · Women, Gender, and Marriage

Symbols: Angels, Devils, and Myth · Moon and Stars · Music and Instruments

Philosophy: Heraclitus and Flux · Marguerite Young on Her Craft · Plato and Platonism · Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres

Allusions: Revelation and the Apocalypse · The History of Opium · Women’s Suffrage

There is a moment in Chapter 38 when Vera (not abstractly, not philosophically, but with the matter-of-fact clarity of someone who has been living inside a situation long enough to name it) refers to her mother's household as a loony bin.

It lands.

Young has been building this house for thirty-seven chapters: the opium, the bed Catherine never leaves, Mr. Spitzer arriving a minute behind each moment he inhabits, Cousin Hannah present only as memory and legend and the fading echo of iron keys. You have been inside these walls long enough to have forgotten, perhaps, that from the outside, this is what it looks like. A loony bin. A place where the usual rules of consciousness don't apply.

Chapter 38 is the chapter where the novel steps back, just briefly, and lets you see the house from that vantage. And then it steps back inside, because the inside is where the real work of the novel lives.

What actually happens in Chapter 38

Catherine talks about Cousin Hannah. Mr. Spitzer contributes anecdotes. Vera narrates her mother's interior: its shape, its contradictions, its magnificent and exhausting unreliability.

We learn more about Catherine's love life, or rather her love's absence: Peron, the man she loved, whose neglect she has reinterpreted as a form of devotion too large for expression. His silence was his love. His distance was his tribute to her power. Catherine has constructed this interpretation with great care and considerable creativity, and she has been living inside it for years.

We learn that Catherine does not believe in women's suffrage. She never did. This is not incidental: Young places it here deliberately, in the long meditation on Cousin Hannah's life's work, so that you feel the full weight of the irony. The woman Hannah loved most, the woman whose household she visited, whose memory she haunts: that woman thought the cause was wrong.

And we get Cousin Hannah herself, arriving in this chapter as she always arrives: fully armed, whistling, trailing snowstorms, carrying iron keys and jeweled swords. A figure of myth assembling itself out of Catherine's guilty admiration.

Obtuse and acute

Young gives us, in Chapter 38, a geometric vocabulary for the novel's central contrast. Catherine and Mr. Spitzer are described as obtuse: wide-angled, blunt, unable to cut. Cousin Hannah is acute: sharp, incisive, a finely tempered blade.

These are called, in the text, masculine virtues. Notice that Young says this with some irony. Cousin Hannah's sharpness is called masculine not because it belongs to men but because the culture has assigned it to them: shrewdness, incisiveness, the willingness to press forward without softening the edges. Cousin Hannah claimed these qualities anyway. She did not ask permission to be acute.

Catherine could not admire these traits without feeling their rebuke. Every quality she admired in Hannah was a quality she did not have, could not have, had perhaps chosen not to have by retreating to the bed and the opium and the beautiful, inaccurate dreams. The admiration and the guilt are inseparable. You cannot love someone for what they are without sometimes feeling diminished by the comparison.

Catherine's love, and the dead love that never was

The passage about Peron is one of the novel's quiet heartbreaks, and it deserves to be read slowly.

Catherine loved Peron. Peron did not love her back, or did not express it, or expressed it through neglect, which Catherine has, over years of opium-assisted interpretation, come to understand as a love too great for conventional demonstration. His silence was greater than hatred. His distance was a tribute. The love between them was a “dead love which never was and never would be real,” and was therefore, Catherine reasons, paradoxically eternal. What never happened cannot be lost. It is still there, unchanged, in the place where unrealized things live.

You might want to argue with this. Young wants you to feel both the argument against it and the deep, exhausted human logic that produces it. People do this. They construct the love they needed out of the materials available, which sometimes means constructing it out of absence and silence and the stories silence permits. Catherine is not deluded in any simple sense. She is doing what the mind does when the heart's actual history is too painful to live inside directly.

Cousin Hannah, Young tells us, never loved anyone, or loved only the image of the dead. Two women in the same house, both unable to love the living. One because she gave everything to a cause. The other because the living disappointed her, or frightened her, or simply were not the dream.

Catherine against suffrage

Let's stay with the fact that Catherine did not believe in women's right to vote.

This was not unusual among wealthy white women of her era and class. The arguments they made were various: that suffrage would disrupt the family, that women lacked the necessary political temperament, that doubling the electorate without adding new perspective served no purpose. Some opposed it because of racism, because they understood, correctly, that the logic of universal suffrage could not be contained to white women alone, and they did not want that. Some opposed it out of loyalty to the husbands and fathers and social arrangements that had, in their particular cases, been kind.

Young does not excuse Catherine. But she also does not make Catherine a simple villain for holding this view. She is interested in the specific texture of the irony: that Cousin Hannah devoted her life to a freedom the woman she loved most did not want for herself. That the relationship between them was genuine, complicated, full of real feeling on both sides, and built over this foundational disagreement. That people can love each other deeply across a chasm of this kind, and that the love does not resolve the chasm.

This is one of the places where the novel becomes historical without being didactic. Young is not delivering a lesson. She is showing you a room.

Cousin Hannah as the red cross knight

Chapter 38 reaches for medieval imagery to describe Cousin Hannah: she is a crusader, an armored figure, a knight of the suffrage campaigns riding into battle against the ogres of social arrangement. Young connects her explicitly to the Redcrosse Knight of Edmund Spenser's The Faerie Queene: a figure of allegorical virtue, armored righteousness moving through a landscape of monsters and temptations toward a goal that is always receding slightly ahead of him.

What does it mean to frame a suffragette as a knight errant? It means Young is placing the women's movement inside the oldest literary tradition for heroic struggle, the quest narrative, and insisting it belongs there. Cousin Hannah is not a footnote to the real history. She is the thing the quest was always about: the battle against the forces that would keep people from their full humanity.

And like the Redcrosse Knight, she is not without paradox. She fights for liberation while harboring her secret wish for the domestic life she publicly rejected. She embodies virtue while playing a mean game of cards. The armor has something complicated inside it. That is what makes her a knight worth following.

The music of the spheres, and what Mr. Spitzer hears

Mr. Spitzer contributes to this chapter a meditation on musica universalis: the ancient idea, traced through Pythagoras and Plato to Boethius and Kepler, that the mathematical relationships governing the cosmos express themselves as harmonics, as a music too vast and too perfect for human ears to detect directly.

Mr. Spitzer cannot hear it. He hears everything a minute late, and he hears it off-key. “The music of the spheres, the” harmony that supposedly underlies all of existence, is available to him only as an idea, a concept he can describe but not experience. He is constitutionally cut off from the very order he most wants to believe in.

Young uses this to deepen the portrait of a man who is both the most invested in the idea of order and the least capable of accessing it. His cosmic music plays on without him. He sits in the silence after the last requiem, hearing the absence of what he cannot quite reach.

Zeno's arrow, and the problem of getting anywhere

The chapter engages Zeno's paradoxes: specifically the question of whether motion is real. Zeno argued that before an arrow can reach its target, it must first travel half the distance, and before that half, half again, infinitely, which should make motion impossible. Neil deGrasse Tyson put the contemporary version of this simply: there are an infinite number of steps to a finite destination.

Young is using this not as a puzzle about physics but as a map of her characters' inner lives. Catherine cannot get anywhere from the bed. Not because she lacks the will, exactly, but because each step toward the real world requires another half-step of preparation, and another, and the destination keeps dividing ahead of her. Mr. Spitzer is always in transit, always arriving too late. Even Cousin Hannah, for all her relentless forward motion, was never fully reconciled with peace, always in the next battle, never at rest, the destination perpetually just over the next horizon.

The novel does not offer you a way to solve Zeno's paradox. It offers you three people living inside it.

What to hold onto

If Chapter 38 feels like a great deal of philosophy stacked on top of a simple domestic situation, here is what anchors it: this is a chapter about two women who loved each other genuinely and could not fully reach each other. One because she was always moving toward the next campaign. The other because she was always retreating into the next dream.

They admired qualities in each other they could not embody themselves. They disappointed each other in ways they could not fully name. They shared a household and a history and a disagreement so fundamental it was almost structural, and they loved each other anyway, across all of it.

Young writes about this kind of love (the love that doesn't resolve its contradictions, that carries its irony inside it like a stone) with more patience and precision than almost anyone. Chapter 38 is one of the places where that patience pays off.
