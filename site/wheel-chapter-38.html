Research notes: Chapter 38 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 38

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The chapter refuses to stabilize any single subject. The narrator, the mother, Mr. Spitzer, Cousin Hannah, and the dead Peron exist in a constant state of mutual interference, none achieving the coherence of a unified "person."

The mother is simultaneously:

  • A beautiful young traveller
  • A bedridden opium dreamer
  • A great courtesan with snow birds in her hair
  • A jeweled corpse
  • A Sleeping Beauty

"she would imagine that she was this great courtesan with a snow pompadour and snow birds in her hair"

This is not delusion presented as pathology. The text refuses to adjudicate between these versions. The mother's multiplicity is presented with the same narrative weight as any "factual" description. Mr. Spitzer is similarly plural: dead yet living, present yet obliterated, attentive yet abstracted. The chapter never resolves which version of any character is the "real" one, because the text implicitly denies that such resolution is available or even desirable.

The most radical statement of plural selfhood comes in the treatment of the two brothers (Peron dead, Joachim living) and the mother's admission that whichever had lived, she would have missed the absent one:

"whichever one had lived, she would have missed the absent one, this sense of her ineffable sorrow being so necessary to her and to her continued happiness"

The self here is constituted by its lacks, not its presences. Identity is the shape of the missing, plural and irresolvable.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter does not present illusion as the mother's personal failure or pathological condition. It presents illusion as the structural condition of all perception, including the narrator's and Mr. Spitzer's.

"truth seeming only another precarious organization of the fleeting and mistaken dream"

This is the philosophical core stated explicitly. But more importantly, the text enacts it. The narrator cannot verify when the mother last saw Cousin Hannah, cannot verify what year anything happened, cannot verify whether Cousin Hannah's reported speech was real or dreamed:

"So it was not to be believed that Cousin Hannah had greeted my mother by remarking — Babylon is fallen. My mother undoubtedly misunderstood as to time, place, person"

Yet the text continues to use this unverifiable speech as narrative material. The cave does not have an outside. The narrator's corrections of the mother's illusions are themselves offered from within another layer of illusion. This is not nihilism, the text is deeply invested in these figures, but it is a rigorous refusal of the claim that any account stands on solid ground.

The larger cave being more carefully lit appears in the description of the house itself (shrouded, windows broken, centaur sleeping in the garden) which travellers interpret either as the lady gone to Italy or as the lady dead. Both are constructions. Neither is privileged.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

This is perhaps the pillar most completely embodied in the chapter's texture. Grief here does not move. It is the climate of every sentence.

The mother's grief for Peron is not a stage she is passing through. It is the organizing principle of her existence, and crucially, the text reveals that it was always already structural: present even when Peron was alive, since his defining characteristic was absence and neglect:

"He had expressed his love by his silence, his distance. Death had surely given no voice to that which had not been expressed in life."

The grief pre-existed its object. It accumulated before the loss that supposedly caused it. This is grief as permanent weather, not responsive to events but constitutive of the self that experiences events.

Mr. Spitzer's grief is equally permanent and equally without resolution. He writes a nocturne in memory of Cousin Hannah, but the text presents this not as mourning-toward-closure but as mourning as ongoing practice, mourning as the form his life takes.

Even the house grieves:

"why the jewel boxes glittered with dim jewels, why the mirrors remembered the faces of the dead"

The mirrors do not merely reflect. They remember. Grief has infected the inanimate. This is not metaphor deployed for effect; it is the text's literal ontology. The world is made of accumulated loss.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator's ethical stance throughout is precisely the witness stance: staying in the room, refusing to simplify, refusing to conclude. The chapter never judges the mother's opium dreams as simply pathological, never endorses Cousin Hannah's activism as simply heroic, never dismisses Mr. Spitzer's abstraction as simply failure.

The witnessing is most explicit in the treatment of Cousin Hannah near the chapter's end:

"Not until Cousin Hannah was old and sick and dying, in fact, had my mother wondered as to that great lady's life in a personal sense, finding that, though illusions dissolved in the aqua fortis of memory, yet mystery increased, and other questions must be asked. Perhaps no human being could ever be entirely dismissed or understood."

This is the witness stance as explicit doctrine. The great suffragette (apparently the most legible, most unambiguous character in the narrative) turns out, under sustained attention, to be as mysterious as anyone else. She wept at snowflakes. Tears made great rivulets down her granite cheek. The witness refuses the reduction of any person to their social function or public identity.

The refusal to simplify extends to the prose itself: the endless qualification, the "perhaps," the "or possibly," the "at least," the "though it was not to be trusted, my mother's point of view". These are not stylistic tics. They are the formal enactment of the witness's refusal to conclude prematurely.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The chapter's structure is itself recursive. It begins with Cousin Hannah, moves to the mother's dreams, returns to Cousin Hannah, moves to the mother's imagined alternative lives, returns to Cousin Hannah's character, returns again to the mother; each return arriving with what was learned in the previous movement, building a more complex portrait without ever completing it.

Mr. Spitzer writing his nocturne is the clearest image of recursive construction: he builds a musical structure in memory of someone he can barely remember, someone who never valued music, building anyway, knowing the building is provisional, returning to the beginning with what he has.

The mother's imagined alternative life, what would have happened if she had accepted Cousin Hannah's invitations, is itself a recursive construction:

"if my mother had accepted these insistent invitations... her life must surely have taken a different course"

She builds an entire alternative existence, knowing it is counterfactual, knowing it will never be verified, building it anyway because the building is the practice. The provisional structure is not nothing. It is what there is.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Every sentence in this chapter demonstrates the ethics of incompletion formally. The prose refuses the concluded sentence. Clauses multiply, qualifications accumulate, em-dashes open new directions before previous ones are closed. This is not stylistic excess; it is ethical commitment to the ongoing sentence as the only honest form.

The most explicit statement:

her "fitful inaccuracy which, in the ultimate sense beyond experience, as Mr. Spitzer had always timidly realized, might be more nearly accurate than truth itself would ever be"

The incomplete, the inaccurate, the fitful; these are revalued here. The demand for completion, for accuracy, for conclusion is itself revealed as a violence against the complexity of experience. The mother's wandering, unfinished mental life is not presented as failure but as a form of integrity; she refuses the false closure that a more "functional" life would impose.

Cousin Hannah's life, by contrast, while heroic, is described in terms that suggest the violence of completion; she cut away the non-essentials, she lived in only one dimension. Her singular purposefulness, admirable as it is, is also a kind of foreclosure. The text mourns this even while admiring it.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Every character in this chapter is marginal. The bedridden opium-dreaming mother. The half-dead lawyer who is "as one dead". The suffragette who sacrificed her life to a cause that had not yet succeeded. The dead gambler Peron whose love was expressed entirely through absence.

The text positions these marginal figures not as exceptions to normal life but as evidence of what normal life conceals. The mother's inability to distinguish reality from dream is presented not as her personal pathology but as the general condition made visible:

"most people, it had been Mr. Spitzer's unfortunate observation, were at some level disoriented, unaware of the existing situation with reference to such apparently clear matters as time, place, identity of persons, even themselves"

The mother is not the exception. She is the evidence. Her extremity makes visible what ordinary social functioning requires people to suppress or deny. The marginal person (the woman who never left her bed, the woman who never married, the man who is "as one dead") these figures reveal the constructed nature of the categories (sanity, productivity, presence, identity) that the center depends upon.

The house itself is marginal; neither inhabited nor abandoned, neither alive nor dead, seen by passing travellers as evidence of departure or death, never correctly interpreted, existing at the edge of legibility.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This is perhaps the most beautifully realized pillar in the chapter. The past does not recede here. It is present in layers, each pressing upward through the one above it.

The mirrors remember the faces of the dead. The house accumulates snow on its roofs. The mother's bedroom contains Cousin Hannah walking back and forth as if no time had passed:

"She dreamed of Cousin Hannah who, in my mother's bedroom, had walked back and forth, rattling great iron keys, striking against a stone wall, striking against the empty air, or fighting a mirror with her jeweled sword as if no time had passed or dimmed this glass."

Time has not dimmed the glass. The past is not behind; it is beneath, pressing upward. The dead Peron is more present than the living Mr. Spitzer:

"Sometimes she thought that only he lived."

The sedimentary logic is explicit in the treatment of the mother's imagined past (the many loves, the many travels, the many countries visited) which are simultaneously things that never happened and things that are more real than what did happen. The sediment of the unlived life presses upward through the lived one.

The chapter's temporal structure enacts this; we cannot determine when anything happened, what year Cousin Hannah last visited, when Peron died. Time does not proceed linearly. It accumulates, layer on layer, the earlier layers pressing up through the later ones, so that the mother's childhood beauty and her current state as jeweled corpse coexist in the same sentence.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter is acutely self-aware about language as construction, most explicitly in its treatment of the narrator's own unreliability:

"One who had followed her descriptions of anything would have found himself much confused, even in a city which he might know like his own sad, empty heart"

But this is attributed to the mother's distortions, and then the text immediately undercuts the narrator's implied superiority by demonstrating that the narrator's own account is equally constructed, equally unable to verify its claims, equally dependent on a perspective that cannot be located outside what it describes.

The language used to describe Cousin Hannah shifts registers constantly: military metaphor (captain, troops, fortified castles), natural metaphor (live coal, sparks), mechanical metaphor (finely tempered blade), mythological reference (Clovis, Cuthbert, Richard Coeur de Leon). No single language is adequate. Each construction reveals something while concealing something else. The text does not find the right language for Cousin Hannah; it accumulates inadequate languages, each naming its own location, each honest about its partiality.

The repeated "perhaps," "possibly," "it was not to be trusted"; these are language naming its own location, acknowledging that it speaks from somewhere in particular, not from nowhere.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter is entirely immanent: located in the ordinary objects and experiences of this bedridden life, pointing deeper into the world rather than outward to a beyond.

The music of the spheres, which Mr. Spitzer can only imagine, is not transcendent in the conventional sense; it fills space, it is the vibration of the cosmos, but it is heard (or not heard) here, in this silence, in this dreamland. The seven-stringed harp, the polar lights with their whistling and crackling; these are sacred presences within the natural world, not beyond it.

Most powerfully, the mother's love for the dead Peron (which never found expression, which was never realized) is presented as the one undying love precisely because it remained immanent, never reaching toward any transcendent realization:

"This dead love which never was and never would be real was the one undying love."

The sacred is in the unrealized, the unexpressed, the immanent and incomplete, not in any achieved transcendence. The grey bus of ordinary grief, the permanent climate of loss, the plural self in its unresolved reality; this is where the sacred lives in this text. The centaur sleeping in the garden is the perfect image: mythological, sacred, but here, pillowed on stone, in this specific garden, in this specific house, immanent in the ordinary world.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This pillar is perhaps the most structurally embedded in the chapter. The narrator is constituted by what was deposited before their arrival: the mother's dreams, the dead Peron, the great-aunt Cousin Hannah, Mr. Spitzer's grief. These are not background. They are the ground the narrator stands on.

"the memory of the memory of music"

This phrase is the crossing point made explicit. The narrator does not have direct access to the music; they have the memory of Mr. Spitzer's memory of Cousin Hannah's relationship to music. They are where multiple times cross. They are constituted by sediment they did not choose and cannot fully excavate.

The structural obligation is visible in the narrator's continued attention to these figures: the carrying continues not because it is chosen but because it is what the narrator is. The ancestors are in the sediment:

"those who, though long hidden under the frozen ground, yet continue, bearing with every spring new flowers, sad as the memory of a preternatural spring"

The perennial dead continue. They bear flowers. The narrator is the spring through which they continue bearing. This is not chosen. It is structural. The carrying is the narrator's existence, not their project.
