To All My Darlings

Chapter 30

On Chapter 30 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 30, 2026

← Chapter 29 · Home · Chapter 31 →

Summary

Catherine and Mr. Spitzer discuss unreal persons as if they were real, especially Eustace, an invisible visitor who takes many shapes. Then Cousin Hannah Freemount-Snowden enters through memory. She was a Boston suffragette, archer, duelist, climber, and traveler who crossed deserts disguised as an Arab king, and she seemed to foresee assassinations. She is now dead and buried. She never cared for the Spitzer twins. Hannah once rode in Arab disguise against Spanish soldiers to defend a caravan of Riffs, and she was dedicated to “the overthrow of the dream and of the dreamer.” Catherine longs for her company and imagines sending her to search the Alps for Jock, Catherine’s husband, who vanished while mountain climbing.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with Catherine and Mr. Spitzer endlessly discussing unreal persons, treating the dead as if they were alive and present. Catherine converses with an invisible figure named Eustace, who takes on many forms symbolizing the fluidity and multiplicity of identity, and the suspension of natural laws in her mental state. This passage highlights her detachment from the tangible world and immersion into a dreamlike or hallucinatory realm.
  • Catherine imagines inviting ancient relatives and historical personages to play card games. Cousin Hannah, a figure of vast duplicity and adventurous spirit, is considered an ideal partner. This fantasy reflects Catherine’s desire for companionship and the bridging of past and present through imagination, as well as her yearning for connection beyond loneliness.
  • A substantial portion of the chapter is devoted to a vivid, expansive biography of Cousin Hannah. She is depicted as a fearless, resourceful, and mercurial adventurer who defied conventional limits of gender and geography. Her life spans numerous cultures and eras symbolically: an archer, dueler, mountain climber, yachtsman, and traveler through remote deserts and cities. Her knowledge of global politics, secret whispers, and historical assassinations underscores a mystical or prescient quality. Importantly, Cousin Hannah embodies a rebellious spirit dedicated to overthrowing dreams and illusions, including those of Catherine.
  • Despite facing numerous mortal dangers, Cousin Hannah survives through her prescience and determination. The narrative contrasts her survival with the fate of others lost to the elements or violence. Her life is portrayed as a continuous journey through perilous lands, reinforcing themes of resilience and the confrontation of fate.
  • Catherine romanticizes and mourns her lost husband, Jock Cartwheel, imploring Cousin Hannah to search for him in the Alps and other remote places. This longing underscores Catherine’s refusal to accept finality and death, blending memory, fantasy, and hope. The imagery of snow-capped peaks, dark valleys, and silent towers evokes a haunting atmosphere of loss and the persistence of love beyond death.
  • Catherine imagines herself sailing two ships simultaneously, one for the living and one for the dead, symbolizing a liminal existence between life and death, consciousness and unconsciousness. The dialogue with the star watchman, reflecting on stars, tides, and the nature of shore and shorelessness, deepens the metaphysical dimension of the chapter, contemplating the mysteries of existence, transition, and the cosmos.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “The real persons were apparently the only persons who were unreal to my mother.” Who is real in Catherine’s room?
  • Cousin Hannah was “dedicated to the overthrow of the dream and of the dreamer.” Why does Catherine admire her?
  • “Because of the loss of a skirt… her entire life had been perhaps an act of sorrow and redemption.” What might that loss have been?

Characters

  • Hannah “had never cared for the Spitzer boys” and called both brothers “profligates.” Is she right?
  • Hannah rides against the Spanish soldiers, “forgetting that she was a stockholder of American firearms.” What does that detail say about her?
  • Catherine asks of one “not likely to respond,” “Have you seen my husband?” What does she refuse to accept?

Connections

  • Catherine tried to telephone Cousin Hannah on a number “disconnected long ago” (Chapter 24). How does this chapter answer that call?
  • The blackamoor messenger returns from Chapter 24, now “in a fringe-topped surrey.” What do these servants of her dreams have in common?
  • Catherine sails “one ship for the living, one for the dead.” Where else has a ship carried the dead in this novel?

Reflection

  • Can a life of adventure be its own kind of escape?
  • What does it mean to be “an emancipated extension” of someone else?
  • “Watchman, where is the shore?” To whom do we put that question?

Research: Characters

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • Portrayed as a figure lost between reality and fantasy.
  • Engages in conversations with unreal or invisible persons such as “Eustace” who changes forms and identities.
  • She seems to live in a dreamlike state, haunted by memories and by the presence of the dead.
  • Experiences sudden emotional outbursts and imagines elaborate scenarios involving historical and mythical figures.
  • Wishes for company and distraction, often imagining card games with ghostly or imaginary partners.
  • Struggles with loss, particularly the absence of her husband, and clings to hope that he might still be found or returned.

Eustace

  • An invisible, multi-faceted figure in Catherine’s mind.
  • Takes on various guises: a mocking bird, an old woman, an old bearded man, a Venetian valet, a murdered gentleman, a blind astronomer, even Wellington.
  • Represents the fluidity of identity and the suspension of natural laws in Catherine’s opium dreams.

Mr. Spitzer

  • A companion to Catherine, often silent and seemingly unable to anchor her in reality.
  • His presence is uncertain, described as an abstraction yawning like cosmic ideas.
  • Catherine often ignores or addresses him tentatively.
  • He “seemed to indulge, often through his silence, her every mood, even her ignoring him.”

Cousin Hannah Freemount-Snowden

  • In stark contrast to Catherine, she is a robust, adventurous, and mercurial woman.
  • Known for her vast, almost mythical experiences across the globe, including deserts, mountains, and ancient cities.
  • Has a reputation for prescience, having foreseen many historical assassinations and events.
  • Skilled in various martial and survival arts: archery in Persia, dueling in France, mountain climbing, horseback riding, balloon ascensions.
  • Has traveled extensively in disguise, infiltrated hostile territories, and defied dangers.
  • Represents a force of action, clarity, and determination, dedicated to overthrowing dreams and illusions.
  • Symbolizes a bridge between myth and history, reality and fantasy, embodying strength and resilience.
  • She rode in Arab disguise against Spanish soldiers to defend a caravan of Riffs, for “her proud heart could not endure injustice of any kind in this world.”
  • She is buried in a New England churchyard, “her jeweled sword at her side,” in “her old grey army coat” worn in “wars for the emancipation of woman.”

Jock Cartwheel

  • Late husband of Catherine who is described as a philanderer who enjoyed dogs, yachts, and mountain climbing.
  • He disappeared while mountain climbing.
  • Catherine imagines him “lifting a beautiful lady’s skirts in clouds and winds,” “philandering,” or “racing dogs through snow clouds.”

Research: Themes

Reality versus Illusion

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Catherine’s conversations with invisible and shifting figures highlight the tension between what is real and imagined.
  • The suspension of natural laws around Eustace suggests the fluidity of identity and reality within opium dreams.
  • Cousin Hannah’s practical and adventurous life contrasts with Catherine’s dreamlike existence, yet even Hannah’s life borders on legend.

Memory and the Past

See: Memory and the Past

  • The narrative dwells on historical figures and events (e.g., Russian czar’s murder, archdukes’ assassinations, Saladin, Solymon the Magnificent).
  • Cousin Hannah’s knowledge spans centuries and cultures, emphasizing the weight of history and memory.
  • Catherine’s longing for her lost husband and her imaginary invitations for card games with ancestors suggest an attempt to connect with the past.

Death and Immortality

See: Death and Mortality

  • Catherine imagines the dead as still living and interacting.
  • Cousin Hannah’s death is ambiguous, with no clear date or place, emphasizing the elusive nature of mortality.
  • Catherine’s hope that her husband still exists somewhere reflects denial of death and the desire for immortality.

Adventure and Escape

See: Freedom and Escape

  • Cousin Hannah embodies the spirit of adventure, traveling to forbidden places and surviving countless dangers.
  • Her life serves as a counterpoint to Catherine’s static, opium-induced dreaming.
  • The recurring imagery of ships, deserts, and mountains emphasizes journeys both physical and metaphysical.

The Feminine and the Skirt

See: Women, Gender, and Marriage

  • Repeated references to skirts, including the loss of a skirt as symbolic sorrow and redemption.
  • Cousin Hannah’s life is linked metaphorically to skirts, suggesting themes of femininity, identity, and transformation.

The Cosmic and the Mystical

See: Faith, God, and the Supernatural · Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos

  • Frequent cosmic imagery: stars, planets, moons, constellations, and celestial events.
  • Catherine’s dialogue with cosmic elements (stars, waves, tides) reflects a mystical quest for meaning beyond the earthly.

Research: Resources

  • “She would rather address her solicitous remarks to Eustace”: Eustace is a name of Greek origin that means fruitful or abundant in grain. It can also mean steadfast or stable. Eustace may be Saint Eustace since she mentions “golden-antlered stags.” St. Eustace converted to Christianity while out hunting after seeing a cross between the deer’s antlers and hearing the voice of God. The golden-antlered stags also recall the Ceryneian Hind with golden antlers, Heracles’ third labor. See Eustace and Greek Mythology.
  • “an old man bearded like the cypress”: The cypress is the tree of graveyards. In Ovid, the boy Cyparissus, grieving a pet stag he killed, becomes a cypress, the tree of mourning (Metamorphoses 10). See Grief, Loss, and Mourning.
  • “Wellington riding at the head of his troops”: Arthur Wellesley, first Duke of Wellington was a famous military leader and political figure in 19th century England. See Napoleon and Wellington.
  • “Sometimes he was a Venetian gentleman’s murderous valet”: A Venetian gentleman’s murderous valet may refer to Lord William Russell who was a British aristocrat and murdered by his valet, Francois Benjamin Courvoisier (1840).
  • “He was a blind astronomer”: Galileo went blind in 1638. See Astronomy.
  • “Eustace, Eustace! Oh, is it you, Eustace dear”: Interviews and articles about her teaching describe Young calling out to dead authors and poets during class, inviting them to come join in. See Marguerite Young on Her Craft.
  • “a game of whist or loo or poker”: Whist, whose rules Edmond Hoyle published in 1742, and loo, the gambling game of Pope’s age. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “a satrap clothed in Saturnian gold or a wild, bearded Cossack”: A satrap was a Persian provincial governor. Saturn ruled the golden age.
  • “who had gone to join her fathers long ago”: “So David slept with his fathers” (1 Kings 2:10). See The Bible.
  • “never worn the white plume of cowardice”: This is a reference to the White Feather Campaign during World War I where women in Britain gave white feathers to men they perceived as shirking their duty to enlist in the war effort, symbolizing cowardice. The white feather symbolism borrowed the idea of a white feather in a game bird’s tail being a sign of inferior breeding. It became a symbol of cowardice and a way to shame men who did not volunteer for service. Women would publicly give white feathers to men in civilian dress, implying they were cowards for not enlisting. The campaign was a powerful tool in the propaganda efforts of the time, aiming to mobilize men into the war effort before conscription was introduced in Britain. The white feather campaign also had an impact on the understanding of masculinity and gender roles during the war. It is the subject of A. E. W. Mason’s 1902 novel The Four Feathers, whose hero receives white feathers when he resigns his commission before his regiment goes to war. It symbolizes the visible sign of choosing the easy, cowardly path rather than the difficult path of bravery and virtue. Henry of Navarre’s white plume, by contrast, was a sign of courage: “Rally to my white plume” (Ivry, 1590). See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “blowing like Gabriel’s skirts”: Gabriel, the archangel of the Annunciation (Luke 1:26) and, in later tradition, of the last trumpet. See Angels and Archangels.
  • “She had known of the murder of the Russian czar and royal family”: The murder of the Russian czar and royal family may refer to the execution of the Romanov family (1918).
  • “the murder of the archduke at Sarajevo”: The murder of the archduke at Sarajevo may refer to the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand which started World War I (June 28, 1914).
  • “great sultans of the past such as Saladin”: These rulers and military figures symbolize various aspects of history, power, and the passage of time, as well as the blending of reality and illusion. They represent authority, conquest, and legacy, but also the futility and ephemeral nature of power. They symbolize the interplay between history, memory, illusion, and identity that is central to the novel’s exploration of reality and dream, life and death.
    • Salah ad-Din Yusuf ibn Ayyub or Saladin was the founder of the Ayyubid dynasty. Hailing from a Kurdish family, he was the first sultan of both Egypt and Syria. He was an important figure of the Third Crusade. At the height of his power, the Ayyubid realm spanned Egypt, Syria, Upper Mesopotamia, the Hejaz, Yemen, and Nubia. See Ancient Egypt.
    • Suleiman the Magnificent was the Ottoman sultan between 1520 and his death in 1566. Under his administration, the Ottoman Empire ruled over at least 25 million people.
    • Sardanapalus was, in Greek legend, the last king of Assyria, a decadent who died in a pyre of his own treasures. The name is probably a corruption of Ashurbanipal (reigned 669-c. 631 BC). Byron’s play Sardanapalus (1821) and Delacroix’s painting The Death of Sardanapalus (1827).
    • Seleucus I Nicator was a Macedonian Greek general, officer and successor of Alexander the Great. He went on to found the Seleucid dynasty.
    • Seleucus II Callinicus was a ruler of the Hellenistic Seleucid Empire. His reign was a time of great turmoil and fragmentation of the Seleucid Empire.
    • Seleucus III Ceraunus was the eldest son of Seleucus II Callinicus and Laodice II and for a few years was king of the Seleucid Empire. He was assassinated in Asia Minor by courtiers during a campaign against Attalus I of Pergamon.
    • Seleucus IV Philopator was a ruler of the Hellenistic Seleucid Empire. He was assassinated by Heliodorus, one of his leading bureaucrats. He is mentioned in 2 Maccabees in the Old Testament.
    • Seleucus V Philometor was the ruler of the Hellenistic Seleucid kingdom. The epithet Philometor means “mother-loving” and in the Hellenistic world usually indicated that the king’s mother acted as co-regent; here it was his mother, Cleopatra Thea. He was killed by his mother in order to advance his younger brother, Antiochus VIII to the throne.
  • “many Bengal lancers”: The Bengal Lancers were cavalry of British India. The phrase is from Francis Yeats-Brown’s memoir The Lives of a Bengal Lancer (1930), filmed in 1935.
  • “Cousin Hannah had known the politics of all those nations”: Most of the main characters recite or list facts, real or imaginary. This may be a critique of school learning or rote memorization or The three Rs. The three Rs are three basic skills taught in schools: reading, writing and arithmetic. The phrase appears to have been coined at the beginning of the 19th century. The skills themselves are alluded to in St. Augustine’s Confessions where students learn to read, and write, and do arithmetic. See Augustine and Christian Theology.
  • “by a blackamoor in a fringe-topped surrey”: See the Chapter 24 blackamoor. The phrase recalls “The Surrey with the Fringe on Top” (Oklahoma!, 1943). See the Chapter 24 resources.
  • “She had been a great archer in Persia, a great dueler in France, an expert with sword and gun in Rumania”: Young’s inclusion of women engaged in various sports and non-traditional roles for their time carries significant thematic weight. This imagery reflects the novel’s broader exploration of gender roles, identity, and societal expectations, particularly highlighting the contrast between conventional femininity and more liberated or unconventional expressions of womanhood. These women vividly illustrate an active, robust female presence engaged in physical and public life, which was unconventional at the time. This challenges the era’s typical expectations of women’s domestic confinement and passive social roles. The active, sporty women serve as a symbol of independence and agency, affirming a woman’s right to self-determination and physical presence in the world. The references to sporty and athletic women suggest an ideal of physical health, vitality, and engagement with life, counterbalancing the decay, passivity, and dream states that afflict other characters. By incorporating women in roles and activities traditionally reserved for men, Young subtly reflects the social transformations occurring in the early 20th century, including the women’s suffrage movement and the gradual expansion of women’s rights and public roles. This is echoed in the figure of Cousin Hannah, a suffragette and adventurer who defies conventional female roles through her militant activism and boundary-crossing journeys, contrasting with the passive, isolated femininity of other characters. It also emphasizes the complexity of female identity. These external symbols of activity and engagement contrast with internal struggles over self-perception, societal acceptance, and the tension between appearance and reality, which are central themes in the novel. Young’s references to women in various athletic and unconventional roles serve to critique restrictive gender norms, celebrate female strength and autonomy, and reflect the complex, often contradictory realities of womanhood. They help to illuminate the novel’s central concerns with reality versus illusion, freedom versus confinement, and the evolving social landscape for women during the period. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
    • Persian women fought and were warriors until the Arab conquest of Persia in 651. A great archer of Persia may have been Banu, Wife of Babak who fought alongside her husband against the Arab Caliphate for 23 years until they were betrayed.
    • A great dueler in France may be Julie d’Aubigny. Her father taught her academic subjects and also trained her in fencing since the age of 12 where she competed successfully against men. She became an assistant to fencing master Seranne. Because she is said to have had relationships with men and women, some modern-day sources refer to her as bisexual or queer.
    • An expert with sword and gun in Romania may be Ecaterina Teodoroiu who fought on the front and died in World War I and is regarded as a heroine of Romania. She initially worked as a nurse but decided to be a front-line soldier partly influenced by the death of her brother.
    • There were famous female sailors: Lagertha, Jeanne de Clisson, Sayyida al Hurra, Lady Mary Killigrew, Grace O’Malley, Mary Read and Anne Bonny, Jeanne Baret, Zheng Yi Sao, Anne Jane Thornton, Mary Lacy, Grace Darling, Mary Patten.
    • Women sailors from New England: Mary Louisa Brewster, Martha Smith Brewer Brown, Mary Chipman Lawrence, Eliza Azelia Williams, Clara Clifford Kingman Wheldon.
    • A great Swiss mountain climber may refer to Lucy Walker (who was the first woman to climb the Matterhorn) and Hettie Dyhrenfurth (who took part in two major expeditions to the Himalayas in 1930 and 1934).
    • Lucille Mulhall (1885-1940), the first woman called a “cowgirl,” could rope and tie a steer with the best of the men. There were so many famous cowgirls it is impossible to list them all here.
    • A great Chinese horsewoman may refer to Hua Mulan, a legendary warrior who disguised herself as a man to join the army.
    • A tall Tibetan jockey may refer to Ani Pachen (Nun Big Courage). She was a Tibetan nun who inherited her father’s position as clan chieftain and led her people in armed resistance against Chinese rule.
  • “a marksman who could shoot with unwavering aim through the ace of hearts at a distance of twelve paces”: Annie Oakley (1860-1926) shot through playing cards tossed in the air. A free pass punched with holes came to be called an “Annie Oakley.”
  • “She had made many balloon ascensions”: Famous female balloonists: Sophie Blanchard, Elisabeth Thible, Nellie Thurston, Wilhelmine Reichard, Jeannette Piccard, who flew to the stratosphere in 1934.
  • “where slept the white-pronged unicorn”: The unicorn of the tapestries. See the Chapter 24 resources and the Chapter 28 resources.
  • “a Captain Derring-Do”: Derring-do is an old-fashioned word meaning boldness or daring, often with a hint of showiness or foolishness. It’s a noun, not a proper noun like a person’s name, and describes the quality of a person’s actions rather than a specific individual. The word began as Chaucer’s “dorring don,” “daring to do,” misread by Spenser and made popular by Scott’s Ivanhoe (1819).
  • “chauffeurs blew the horns of old Renaults”: Renault was founded in France in 1899 by the brothers Louis, Marcel, and Fernand Renault, and its cars were prized imports in American cities before the First World War. Renault taxis became famous in 1914, when hundreds of them carried French troops to the front at the First Battle of the Marne.
  • “the genealogies of Beacon Hill”: Beacon Hill is one of Boston, Massachusetts’s most picturesque areas. It is the home of Boston’s old families, the Lowells and Cabots of Chapter 24. See the Chapter 24 resources.
  • “the mountains of the moon”: Ptolemy’s Mountains of the Moon, the fabled source of the Nile.
  • “her poor husband, who was lost while walking between two frozen mountain peaks”: Catherine’s husband. See Jock Cartwheel.
  • “the elephants crossing the Alps with Hannibal”: Hannibal was a general and statesman of Carthage in what is now Tunisia. He crossed the Alps in 218 BC. See the Chapter 24 resources.
  • “where Childe Roland had blown his horn before a dark tower”: Childe Roland is a character in a poem by Robert Browning. The character Roland in the poem makes a journey to the Dark Tower much like the journey of Pilgrim’s Progress and the Divine Comedy. The name Child Roland comes from the Shakespeare play King Lear Act 3 Scene 4 Lines 195-197. Browning’s poem is “Childe Roland to the Dark Tower Came” (1855). The horn also recalls Roland of The Song of Roland, who blew his horn Oliphant at Roncevaux (778). See Shakespeare and Dante.
  • “marked only with the sign of the codfish”: The “codfish aristocracy” was a derogatory term in reference to “the ostentatious and vulgar display of that wealth, comes later, in the 1840s and 1850s, and most prominently in the debate over slavery” (Donna Seger, “Codfish Aristocracy,” Streets of Salem, April 2, 2019). Some argued that the codfish aristocracy’s economic interests in the slave trade and the sugar trade were intertwined with their wealth.
    • The Sacred Cod is a four-foot-eleven-inch carved-wood effigy of an Atlantic codfish, painted realistically and hanging in the House of Representatives chamber of Boston’s Massachusetts State House. The one in the House chamber today was installed in 1784.
  • “so many foreign and domestic wars for the emancipation of woman”: The white women’s suffrage movement, primarily organized and led by middle-class educated white women, focused on gaining voting rights for white women, culminating in the passage of the 19th amendment in 1920. The movement came out of the abolitionist movement with leaders being: Elizabeth Cady Stanton, Susan B. Anthony, Alice Paul, Carrie Chapman Catt, and Sojourner Truth (who played a significant role in the movement’s background). Suffragists employed various tactics, including lobbying, organizing marches and parades, and civil disobedience. After decades of activism, the 19th Amendment to the US Constitution was ratified in 1920 granting some women the right to vote. On paper the 19th Amendment covered all women. In practice it did not secure the vote for women of color. Black women in the South were kept from the polls by poll taxes, literacy tests, and terror. Native American women were not citizens until 1924. Asian immigrant women could not become citizens until 1952. It wasn’t until the Voting Rights Act of 1965 until all women’s voting rights were protected. See Women’s Suffrage.
    • A white skirt often symbolizes purity, innocence, mourning (in some cultures), new beginnings, versatility, symbol of femininity. As far as suffragettes they wore white as a symbol of the movement to represent purity and unity. The white skirt is a richly symbolic image representing a lost love, the ideal of purity and womanhood, and the spiritual journey through life, death, and memory. It is less a physical object and more a poetic emblem of the suffragette’s inner life, loss, and the mystery that surrounds her final moments. See Clothing, Hats, and Skirts.
  • “disguised as an Arab king”: There were female explorers in the Middle East: Gertrude Bell (a British explorer, archaeologist, and writer) and Freya Stark who was another British explorer and writer. Other explorers: Isabella Bird, Lady Anne Blunt, Lady Hester Stanhope. Isabelle Eberhardt (1877-1904) rode the Sahara dressed as an Arab man, and Lady Hester Stanhope lived among the Arabs in men’s dress. See Exploration and the Poles.
  • “she had been disguised as a lazar ringing his bell”: A lazar is a person afflicted with a repulsive disease like leprosy. The word comes from Lazarus, the beggar “full of sores” (Luke 16:20). Lepers carried bells or clappers to warn others away. See The Bible.
  • “the ruins of Persepolis”: Persepolis was the ceremonial capital of the Achaemenid Empire. It was burned by Alexander the Great in 330 BC.
    • Darius the Great was the third Persian King of Kings of the Achaemenid Empire.
    • Isfahan is a city in central Iran.
    • Shiraz is in the southwest of Iran.
  • “over the red hills of Moab and over the Dead Sea”: Moab, the biblical kingdom east of the Dead Sea, land of Ruth (Ruth 1:4). See The Bible.
    • The Dead Sea is a salt lake bordering Jordan, Israel, and the West Bank.
  • “inside Arabia Deserta”: Arabia Deserta is the title of a travel book by Charles Montagu Doughty and admired by TE Lawrence (Lawrence of Arabia). Doughty’s book is Travels in Arabia Deserta (1888).
  • “where she had heard an archangel blowing a ram’s horn three times”: In Islamic tradition, the archangel Israfil will sound the trumpet of Judgment. The shofar is the ram’s horn of Jewish worship. See Angels and Archangels.
  • “and she had slept in Babylon, Nineveh, Crete”: Babylon was the ancient capital city of the Babylonian empire.
    • Nineveh was an ancient Assyrian city of Upper Mesopotamia.
    • Crete is the largest island in Greece.
  • “Her white horse was named El-Burraq”: Burraq is a creature in Islamic tradition that provides transport for certain prophets. In tradition, Muhammad tethered Buraq at the Western Wall on the Night Journey.
  • “a caravan of lonely Riffs”: Riffs are members of a number of Berber tribes around Er Rif. The Rif War (1921-26), in which Abd el-Krim’s Republic of the Rif fought Spanish and French colonial armies.
    • The town of the Third Market Day may refer to the Rif War or one of the Spanish colonial wars in Morocco. Moroccan market towns are named for their market day. Souk el-Tleta is “the market of the third” day.
  • “Charon’s empty boat drifting on the midnight tide”: Charon, ferryman of the dead across the Styx. See Greek Mythology.
  • “Moroccan pirates returning from raids upon the sleeping coast of Spain with beautiful captive girls”: The Barbary corsairs of Salé and Algiers raided the coasts of Europe and sold captives into slavery. See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “Watchman, where is the shore”: “Watchman, what of the night?” (Isaiah 21:11). See The Bible.
  • “Arcturus, Andromeda, rainy Hyades”: Job’s “Arcturus, Orion, and Pleiades” (Job 9:9), and Tennyson’s “Ulysses” (1842): “Thro’ scudding drifts the rainy Hyades / Vext the dim sea.” See Astronomy, The Book of Job, and Greek Mythology.
    • Arcturus is the brightest star in the constellation of Bootes.
    • Andromeda is the daughter of King Cepheus and Queen Cassiopeia in Greek Mythology. The constellation is named for her.
    • Hyades is the nearest open star cluster.
  • “the argosy of the moving stars”: Argosy is a large merchant ship. The word comes from Ragusa (Dubrovnik), the merchant city.
  • “winter-rimed Orion”: Orion is a prominent constellation located on the celestial equator.

Grazier
One who grazes cattle. Here it means the herds.
Imploring
Begging earnestly.
Inscrutable
Impossible to understand or interpret.
Lazar
A diseased beggar, especially one with leprosy.
Leeward
On the side sheltered from the wind.
Loo
An old card game played for stakes.
Mercurial
Quick and changeable in mood. From the swift Roman messenger god Mercury and the liquid metal named for him.
Minaret
A tall, slender tower of a mosque, from which the call to prayer is given.
Mirage
An optical illusion, common in deserts, in which hot air bends light so that distant water or cities seem to appear.
Muezzin
The man who calls Muslims to prayer.
Nocturnal
Of or happening in the night.
Oblivion
The state of being forgotten, or of being unaware of everything.
Peaked
Thin and pale, as from illness.
Pinnacle
The highest point; a tall, pointed mountain peak or the slender spire on a building.
Prescience
Knowledge of events before they happen.
Problematical
Doubtful; hard to settle.
Profligate
A reckless, immoral spendthrift.
Sangfroid
Coolness in danger.
Satrap
A Persian provincial governor.
Solicitous
Anxious and attentive.
Stanchly
Firmly; loyally.
Surrey
A light four-wheeled horse-drawn carriage with two seats, often with a fringed canopy on top.
Taloned
Having talons, the sharp claws of a bird of prey.
Tentative
Hesitant; not certain.
Tethered
Tied with a rope or chain.
Turret
A small tower on a building or castle.
Whist
A card game for four players in pairs.
