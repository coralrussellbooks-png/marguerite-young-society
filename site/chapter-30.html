Summary

Catherine and Mr. Spitzer discuss unreal persons as if they were real, especially Eustace, an invisible visitor who takes many shapes. Then Cousin Hannah Freemount-Snowden enters through memory. She was a Boston suffragette, archer, duelist, climber, and traveler who crossed deserts disguised as an Arab king, and she seemed to foresee assassinations. She is now dead and buried. She never cared for the Spitzer twins. Catherine longs for her company and imagines sending her to search the Alps for Jock, Catherine's husband, who vanished while mountain climbing.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with Catherine and Mr. Spitzer endlessly discussing unreal persons, treating the dead as if they were alive and present. Catherine converses with an invisible figure named Eustace, who takes on many forms symbolizing the fluidity and multiplicity of identity, and the suspension of natural laws in her mental state. This passage highlights her detachment from the tangible world and immersion into a dreamlike or hallucinatory realm.
  • Catherine imagines inviting ancient relatives and historical personages to play card games. Cousin Hannah, a figure of vast duplicity and adventurous spirit, is considered an ideal partner. This fantasy reflects Catherine’s desire for companionship and the bridging of past and present through imagination, as well as her yearning for connection beyond loneliness.
  • A substantial portion of the chapter is devoted to a vivid, expansive biography of Cousin Hannah. She is depicted as a fearless, resourceful, and mercurial adventurer who defied conventional limits of gender and geography. Her life spans numerous cultures and eras symbolically: an archer, dueler, mountain climber, yachtsman, and traveler through remote deserts and cities. Her knowledge of global politics, secret whispers, and historical assassinations underscores a mystical or prescient quality. Importantly, Cousin Hannah embodies a rebellious spirit dedicated to overthrowing dreams and illusions, including those of Catherine.
  • Despite facing numerous mortal dangers Cousin Hannah survives through her prescience and determination. The narrative contrasts her survival with the fate of others lost to the elements or violence. Her life is portrayed as a continuous journey through perilous lands, reinforcing themes of resilience and the confrontation of fate.
  • Catherine romanticizes and mourns her lost husband, Jock Cartwheel, imploring Cousin Hannah to search for him in the Alps and other remote places. This longing underscores Catherine’s refusal to accept finality and death, blending memory, fantasy, and hope. The imagery of snow-capped peaks, dark valleys, and silent towers evokes a haunting atmosphere of loss and the persistence of love beyond death.
  • Catherine imagines herself sailing two ships simultaneously, one for the living and one for the dead, symbolizing a liminal existence between life and death, consciousness and unconsciousness. The dialogue with the star watchman, reflecting on stars, tides, and the nature of shore and shorelessness, deepens the metaphysical dimension of the chapter, contemplating the mysteries of existence, transition, and the cosmos.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • Portrayed as a figure lost between reality and fantasy.
  • Engages in conversations with unreal or invisible persons such as “Eustace” who changes forms and identities.
  • She seems to live in a dreamlike state, haunted by memories and by the presence of the dead.
  • Experiences sudden emotional outbursts and imagines elaborate scenarios involving historical and mythical figures.
  • Wishes for company and distraction, often imagining card games with ghostly or imaginary partners.
  • Struggles with loss, particularly the absence of her husband, and clings to hope that he might still be found or returned.

Eustace

  • An invisible, multi-faceted figure in Catherine’s mind.
  • Takes on various guises: a mocking bird, an old woman, an old bearded man, a Venetian valet, a murdered gentleman, a blind astronomer, even Wellington.
  • Represents the fluidity of identity and the suspension of natural laws in Catherine's opium dreams.

Mr. Spitzer

  • A companion to Catherine, often silent and seemingly unable to anchor her in reality.
  • His presence is uncertain, described as an abstraction yawning like cosmic ideas.
  • Catherine often ignores or addresses him tentatively.

Cousin Hannah Freemount-Snowden

  • In stark contrast to Catherine, she is a robust, adventurous, and mercurial woman.
  • Known for her vast, almost mythical experiences across the globe, including deserts, mountains, and ancient cities.
  • Has a reputation for prescience, having foreseen many historical assassinations and events.
  • Skilled in various martial and survival arts: archery in Persia, dueling in France, mountain climbing, horseback riding, balloon ascensions.
  • Has traveled extensively in disguise, infiltrated hostile territories, and defied dangers.
  • Represents a force of action, clarity, and determination, dedicated to overthrowing dreams and illusions.
  • Symbolizes a bridge between myth and history, reality and fantasy, embodying strength and resilience.

Jock Cartwheel

  • Late husband of Catherine who is described as a philanderer who enjoyed dogs, yachts, and mountain climbing.
  • He disappeared while mountain climbing.

Research: Resources

  • Eustace is a name of Greek origin that means fruitful or abundant in grain. It can also mean steadfast or stable. Eustace may be Saint Eustace since she mentions “golden-antlered stags.” St. Eustace converted to Chrisitanity while out hunting after seeing a cross between the deer’s antlers and hearing the voice of God.
  • Young is described as calling out to dead authors and poets while she was teaching a class for them to come join in.
  • “white plume of cowardice” - is a reference to the White Feather Campaign during World War I where women in Britain gave white feathers to men they perceived as shirking their duty to enlist in the war effort, symbolizing cowardice. The white feather symbolism borrowed the idea of a white feather in a game bird’s tail being a sign of inferior breeding. It became a symbol of cowardice and a way to shame men who did not volunteer for service. Women would publicly give white feathers to men in civilian dress, implying they were cowards for not enlisting. The campaign was a powerful tool in the propaganda efforts of the time, aiming to mobilize men into the war effort before conscription was introduced in Britain. The white feather campaign also had an impact on the understanding of masculinity and gender roles during the war. It was also a representation of conscientious pacifism in AEW Mason’s 1902 book, The Four Feathers. It symbolizes the visible sign of choosing the easy, cowardly path rather than the difficult path of bravery and virtue.
  • A white skirt often symbolizes purity, innocence, mourning (in some cultures), new beginnings, versatility, symbol of femininity. As far as suffragettes they wore white as a symbol of the movement to represent purity and unity. The white skirt is a richly symbolic image representing a lost love, the ideal of purity and womanhood, and the spiritual journey through life, death, and memory. It is less a physical object and more a poetic emblem of the suffragette’s inner life, loss, and the mystery that surrounds her final moments.
  • The white women’s suffrage movement, primarily organized and led by middle-class educated white women, focused on gaining voting rights for white women, culminating in the passage of the 19th amendment in 1920. The movement came out of the abolitionist movement with leaders being: Elizabeth Cady Stanton, Susan B. Anthony, Alice Paul, CArrie Chapman Catt, and Sojourner Truth (who played a significant role in the movement’s background). Suffragists employed various tactics, including lobbying, organizing marches and parades, and civil disobedience. After decades of activism, the 19th Amendment to the US Constitution was ratified in 1920 granting some women the right to vote. The 19th Amendment did not include women of color. It wasn’t until the Voting Rights Act of 1965 until all women’s voting rights were protected.
  • These rulers and military figures symbolize various aspects of history, power, and the passage of time, as well as the blending of reality and illusion. They represent authority, conquest, and legacy, but also the futility and ephemeral nature of power. They symbolize the interplay between history, memory, illusion, and identity that is central to the novel's exploration of reality and dream, life and death.
  • Salah ad-Din Yusuf ibn Ayyub or Saladin was the founder of the Ayyubid dynasty. Hailing from a Kurdish family, he was the first sultan of both Egypt and Syria. He was an important figure of the Third Crusade. At the height of his power, the Ayyubid realm spanned Egypt, Syria, Upper Mesopotamia, the Hejaz, Yemen, and Nubia.
  • Suleiman the Magnificent was the Ottoman sultan between 1520 and his death in 1566. Under his administration, the Ottoman Empire ruled over at least 25 million people.
  • Sardanapalus was the last king of Assyria (although Ashurbanipal holds that distinction and Sardanapalus is probably a corruption of Ashurbanipal). He was supposed to have lived in the 7th century BC, and is portrayed as a decadent figure who spends his life in self-indulgence and dies in an orgy of destruction. The legendary decadence of Sardanapalus later became a theme in literature and art, especially in the Romantic era.
  • Seleucus I Nicator was a Macedonian Greek general, officer and successor of Alexander the Great. He went on to found the Seleucid dynasty.
  • Seleucus II Callinicus was a ruler of the Hellenistic Seleucid Empire. His reign was a time of great turmoil and fragmentation of the Seleucid Empire.
  • Seleucus III Ceraunus was the eldest son of Seleucus II Callinicus and Laodice II and for a few years was king of the Seleucid Empire. He was assassinated in Asia Minor by courtiers during a campaign against Attalus I of Pergamon.
  • Seleucus IV Philopator was a ruler of the Hellenistic Seleucid Empire. He was assassinated by Heliodorus, one of his leading bureaucrats. He is mentioned in 2 Maccabees in the Old Testament.
  • Seleucus V Philometor was the ruler of the Hellenistic Seleucid kingdom. The epithet Philometor means “mother-loving” and in the Hellenistic world usually indicated that Catherine acted as co-regent for the prince. He was killed by his mother in order to advance his younger brother, Antiochus VIII to the throne.
  • Young's inclusion of women engaged in various sports and non-traditional roles for their time carries significant thematic weight. This imagery reflects the novel’s broader exploration of gender roles, identity, and societal expectations, particularly highlighting the contrast between conventional femininity and more liberated or unconventional expressions of womanhood. The detailed references to women participating in sports such as tennis, skiing, horseback riding, and even archery, as well as wearing attire adorned with sports motifs, underscore a break from the traditional, passive roles often assigned to women in earlier eras. These women vividly illustrate an active, robust female presence engaged in physical and public life, which was unconventional at the time. This challenges the era’s typical expectations of women’s domestic confinement and passive social roles. Miss MacIntosh’s advocacy for physical exercise such as bowling, horseshoes, and skipping ropes, and her disdain for luxury and affectation, emphasize a robust, self-reliant female identity that contrasts with the fragile, dreamlike femininity embodied by Vera’s mother, Catherine. The active, sporty women serve as a symbol of independence and agency, affirming a woman's right to self-determination and physical presence in the world. The references to sporty and athletic women suggest an ideal of physical health, vitality, and engagement with life, counterbalancing the decay, passivity, and dream states that afflict other characters. By incorporating women in roles and activities traditionally reserved for men, Young subtly reflects the social transformations occurring in the early 20th century, including the women’s suffrage movement and the gradual expansion of women’s rights and public roles. This is echoed in the figure of Cousin Hannah, a suffragette and adventurer who defies conventional female roles through her militant activism and boundary-crossing journeys, contrasting with the passive, isolated femininity of other characters. It also emphasizes the complexity of female identity. These external symbols of activity and engagement contrast with internal struggles over self-perception, societal acceptance, and the tension between appearance and reality, which are central themes in the novel. Young’s references to women in various athletic and unconventional roles serve to critique restrictive gender norms, celebrate female strength and autonomy, and reflect the complex, often contradictory realities of womanhood. They help to illuminate the novel’s central concerns with reality versus illusion, freedom versus confinement, and the evolving social landscape for women during the period.
