Chapter 52

On Chapter 52 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Christianity and Religion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Moses Hunnecker

Allusions: Angels and Archangels · Revelation and the Apocalypse

"a hammer struck rock dividing cherubim" sets the angels in stone, split by a blow.

“Should doubt move mountains which faith had not moved”: Mr. Spitzer inverts the saying that faith can move mountains: “If ye have faith as a grain of mustard seed, ye shall say unto this mountain, Remove hence to yonder place; and it shall remove; and nothing shall be impossible unto you” (Matthew 17:20). The suffragette’s search for a lost skirt, which leads to the death of her lover, suggests the absurdity of human pursuits, as in existentialist thought. She cries “Long live woman! Vive la femme! Viva la donna!” in many languages, “perhaps even in Sanskrit which was the language written upon a silent snowflake,” yet there are “secret languages never to be translated into words.” Her decision to dress as a man asserts her freedom against social norms, and her practical clothing against the other climbers’ finery sets materialism against idealism. The footman cutting a golden stairway from a frozen lake to the mountains of the moon may recall the ascent from Plato’s cave toward the light, and her climbing the Transcendentalist journey beyond the material world. See Plato and Platonism. Earlier in Chapter 44.

“like the sea giving up its dead”: “And the sea gave up the dead which were in it; and death and hell delivered up the dead which were in them: and they were judged every man according to their works” (Revelation 20:13, KJV). See Revelation and the Apocalypse. Earlier in Chapters 6 and 49.

"the infant Moses sleeping in his little boat among the reeds," "hidden away by women from a cruel king," recalls the child saved on the water, and marks Cousin Hannah's childless house by what it lacks.

"the ancient Moses who had led the children of Israel... through desert storms" is the Exodus, Moses guiding his people through the wilderness.

“I might fish up a minor prophet”: The twelve Minor Prophets of the Bible, Hosea through Malachi, are called minor for the shortness of their books, not their importance. Their message is judgment and salvation, social justice and the treatment of the poor, and concern for divine judgment in the future. One of them was fished up indeed: “And the LORD spake unto the fish, and it vomited out Jonah upon the dry land” (Jonah 2:10, KJV). See The Bible.

"scallop shells attached to her belt" carry the emblem of Saint James, the pilgrim's badge.

"Where now you see these flowers, there was once a great city which has disappeared," spoken from a cloud, echoes the ruined cities of Scripture and the promise of a city restored, judgment and the New Jerusalem behind it.

“Who heard the knock of her dead heart?”: “Behold, I stand at the door, and knock: if any man hear my voice, and open the door, I will come in to him, and will sup with him, and he with me” (Revelation 3:20, KJV). Here the knock comes from within, and no one opens. See Revelation and the Apocalypse.

"the unmelting snow-flake in the heart of the sea of burning fire" holds a figure of purity untouched in the very lake of fire of Revelation, incorruptible in the midst of judgment.

“The mountains skipped about like rams”: “The mountains skipped like rams, and the little hills like lambs” (Psalm 114:4, KJV). See The Bible.

“Perhaps she was the ladder personality”: “And he dreamed, and behold a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven: and behold the angels of God ascending and descending on it” (Genesis 28:12, KJV). See The Bible. Earlier in Chapters 14, 29, 36, 38 and 48.

“a four-faced cherub one hundred cubits high”: “And every one had four faces, and every one had four wings” (Ezekiel 1:6, KJV). See The Bible.

“in creation there was division as in division there was creation”: Genesis tells creation as a series of divisions: “and God divided the light from the darkness” (Genesis 1:4, KJV). Life begins the same way: in nearly all animals the fertilized egg, or zygote, splits again and again, a process called cleavage. See The Bible. Earlier in Chapter 29.

“Who knew the womb of a snowflake?”: “Hath the rain a father? or who hath begotten the drops of dew? Out of whose womb came the ice? and the hoary frost of heaven, who hath gendered it?” (Job 38:28-29, KJV). See The Bible.

“where none had moved over the face of the waters”: “And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters” (Genesis 1:2, KJV). See The Bible. Earlier in Chapters 1, 12, 15, 20, 37, 47 and 48.

“who should find the doors which held back the sea”: “Or who shut up the sea with doors, when it brake forth, as if it had issued out of the womb?” (Job 38:8, KJV). Young follows it at once: “The sea flowed like the waters from the womb.” See The Bible.

“who were born by the ear of the dead”: In the Christian bible Mary is supposed to be impregnated by the Holy Spirit and not by a mortal man, thus giving birth to Jesus. A medieval belief held that Mary conceived through her ear when she heard the angel’s word, conceptio per aurem, and paintings of the Annunciation sometimes show a ray of light or the dove of the Holy Spirit entering her ear. See The Bible.

“a sterile woman had conceived because another woman had shouted at her”: This may echo the barren Hannah of 1 Samuel, whose name Cousin Hannah shares, taunted by her husband’s other wife: “And her adversary also provoked her sore, for to make her fret, because the LORD had shut up her womb” (1 Samuel 1:6, KJV). Hannah prayed at Shiloh, “and the LORD remembered her,” and she bore Samuel (1 Samuel 1:19-20, KJV). In Luke, when the long-barren Elisabeth heard Mary’s greeting, “the babe leaped in her womb” and “she spake out with a loud voice” (Luke 1:41-42, KJV). See The Bible.

“like that Spanish don”: It evokes a man whose bride leaves him not for another man but for the convent, to become a bride of Christ. Like the don’s bride, the suffragette left traditional roles for solitary journeys and quests. It may refer to José Zorrilla’s play “Don Juan Tenorio,” Don Juan makes a bet that he can conquer more women than Don Luis. Don Luis challenges Don Juan to conquer a woman about to take her vows as a nun, and an engaged woman. Here is a Spanish don whose bride became a nun. In the play, Don Juan’s target, Doña Inés, is a novice nun whose innocence captivates him and leads him to contemplate redemption.

“the tree rooted in clouds with its branches growing downward”: The Bhagavad Gita (15.1) and the Katha Upanishad (6.1) describe the eternal ashvattha, the sacred fig, with its roots above and its branches below. See Hinduism.

A "door of stone" recalls the sealed tomb of Easter, the stone that would be rolled away.

“let the dead bury the dead”: “But Jesus said unto him, Follow me; and let the dead bury their dead” (Matthew 8:22, KJV). See The Bible. Earlier in Chapters 3 and 48.

“Eloise et Abelard”: The twelfth-century philosopher Peter Abelard and his student Héloïse secretly married, were parted when her uncle had Abelard castrated, and lived out their lives in the cloister, joined afterward by their letters. Young names them again among parted lovers in Chapter 65; see the Chapter 65 resources. Peter Abelard was a medieval French scholastic philosopher, leading logician, theologian, teacher, musician, composer, and poet. In philosophy he is celebrated for his proposed logical solution to the problem of universals via nominalism and conceptualism and his pioneering of intent in ethics. Often referred to as the “Descartes of the twelfth century,” he is considered a forerunner of Rousseau, Kant, and Spinoza. He is sometimes credited as a chief forerunner of modern empiricism. In Catholic theology, he is best known for his development of the concept of limbo, and his introduction of the moral influence theory of atonement. He is considered (alongside Augustine) to be the most significant forerunner of the modern self-reflective autobiographer. He paved the way and set the tone for later epistolary novels and celebrity tell-alls with his publicly distributed letter, The History of My Calamities and public correspondence. In history and popular culture he is best known for his passionate, tragic love affair and intense philosophical exchange with his brilliant student and eventual wife, Héloïse d’Argenteuil. After they secretly married, Abelard sent Héloïse to the convent at Argenteuil; her uncle and guardian Fulbert, believing she had been cast off, had Abelard ambushed and castrated, and both entered religious life. Roscellinus and others made fun of him. Their son was named Astrolabe. (Wikipedia) See the problem of universals in the Chapter 49 resources. Héloïse d’Argenteuil was a French nun, philosopher, writer, scholar, and abbess. Héloïse was a renowned “woman of letters” and philosopher of love and friendship, as well as an eventual high ranking abbess in the Catholic Church. She achieved approximately the level and political power of a bishop in 1147 when she was granted the rank of prelate nullius. She is famous in history and popular culture for her love affair and correspondence with the leading medieval logician and theologian Peter Abelard, who became her colleague, collaborator, and husband. She is known for exerting critical intellectual influence upon his work and posing many challenging questions to him such as those in the Problemata Heloissae. Her surviving letters are considered a foundation of French and European literature and primary inspiration for the practice of courtly love. Her erudite and sometimes erotically charged correspondence is the Latin basis for the bildungsroman genre and serve alongside Abelard’s Historia Calamitatum as a model of the classical epistolary genre. Her influence extends on later writers such as Chrétien de Troyes, Geoffrey Chaucer, Madame de Lafayette, Thomas Aquinas, Choderlos de Laclos, Voltaire, Rousseau, Simone Weil, and Dominique Aury. She is an important figure in the establishment of women’s representation in scholarship and is known for her controversial portrayals of gender and marriage which influenced the development of modern feminism. When Abelard gave a rationalistic interpretation of the Trinitarian dogma he was charged with heresy. Theologia by Peter Abelard refers to his three treatises on the Trinity, written in the 12th century. These works, titled Theologia “summi boni,” Theologia Christiana, and Theologia “scholarium,” explore the nature of God and the Trinity using dialectical reasoning. Theologia Christiana is considered the most influential of the three. However, Abelard’s Theologia was also controversial, with the first version being condemned as heretical and burned in 1121. Abelard employed logical arguments and philosophical analysis to examine theological concepts, particularly the Trinity. He adopted the Boethian sense of theologia as discourse about the divine nature, according to a study from Oxford Academic. The core of his work revolves around understanding the nature and relationship of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Abelard’s approach, which emphasized reason and challenged traditional interpretations, led to conflicts with Church authorities. His works were condemned at the Council of Soissons (1122) and the Council of Sens (1141). Abelard proposed that Christ’s death was primarily a demonstration of God’s love, rather than a satisfaction of divine justice. This view is now known as the moral influence theory. Despite the controversies, Abelard’s work had a lasting impact on medieval philosophy and theology, particularly in the development of scholasticism. (Wikipedia) The Letters of Abelard and Heloise are two series of passionate and intellectual correspondences apparently written in Latin during the 12th century. The purported authors, Peter Abelard, a prominent theologian, and his pupil, Heloise, a gifted young woman later renowned as an abbess, exchanged these letters following their ill-fated love affair and subsequent monastic lives. The letters reveal the personal and intellectual relationship between Abelard and Heloise, and provide an intimate glimpse into the societal context of 12th-century Europe. They’ve played a significant role in the development of Western epistolary literature, attracting attention from historians, literary scholars, and general readers alike. The Letters of Abelard and Heloise also serve as primary source documents on questions of medieval gender roles, love, and monastic life. (Wikipedia) Earlier in Chapter 49.
