Reality and Illusion in MMMD

Chapter 6: The Search for Meaning and the Ethical Life

← Chapter 5: Mortality, Death, and the Afterlife · Exploring Reality and Illusion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling · Chapter 7: Narrative, Time, and Memory →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Lorena · Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Titus Bonebreaker · Vera Cartwheel

If illusion is primary, the obvious next question is how to live. A world built from agreements and kept standing by repetition could seem to excuse anything. If nothing is bedrock, why be good? Miss MacIntosh, My Darling never asks the question in those words, but every character in it answers it. Catherine answers with the dream, Miss MacIntosh with work, and Mr. Bonebreaker with the promise of heaven. Vera answers with a search she cannot finish. This chapter reads those answers as ethical positions and asks what kind of goodness is possible in a world that is made rather than given.

The Basting Stitch

Miss MacIntosh’s ethic fits into one sewing lesson. “A rich girl should be taught to sew like a poor one, to make her basting stitches straight and even.” Then comes the reason, which is the most revealing sentence she ever speaks about how to live: “Large, straight basting stitches were more easily pulled out than small or crooked ones.”

A basting stitch is temporary by design. It holds a garment together while the real seam is decided, and it is meant to come out. Miss MacIntosh does not teach Vera to sew permanent things. She teaches her to sew in a way that can be undone cleanly. The whole of her morality has this shape: plain, orderly, useful, and built for revision. Her creed is “Common sense is the finest sense,” and her road through life is “a granite road and not the sea road.” Yet the stitch she values most is the one that comes out.

The framework this book follows has a principle for this, Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary: since nothing is ever truly final, the honest move is to build anyway, provisionally, and keep revising, and to distrust the frozen, finished blueprint. Miss MacIntosh would never have put it that way. She believed her rules were granite. But her hands knew better than her proverbs. She built a life for Vera the way she basted a sleeve, firmly enough to hold, loosely enough to be pulled out and redone.

The Rules and Their Edges

Her ethic has hard edges, and some of them cut. She teaches that “Life was hard, and it was intended to be hard.” She holds that “God had doubtless put us where He had intended,” so that everyone should keep to their place. She calls marriage “the only normal state for a woman” and advises Vera to marry a little shoemaker and expect little. She trains Vera in basting and bookkeeping for a domestic life that Miss MacIntosh herself never had.

The irony is plain once the rest of the novel is known. The woman teaching the center’s rules lived at the edges of every one of them. She was unmarried and bald. She had loved a dancing girl named Lorena. Late in life she says she “should have been an honest woman or an honest man.” The framework calls this The Margins Reveal the Truth: the people who do not fit the categories see the categories most clearly. Miss MacIntosh saw them clearly enough to teach them perfectly, and she lived outside them in secret. Her lessons were not hypocrisy so much as protection. She handed Vera the ordinary life as a shelter she could not have herself.

The Umbrella Was Mr. Bonebreaker’s

One detail on the beach where Miss MacIntosh disappeared changes the meaning of her most famous possession. Her black umbrella, the sword and shield she carried against weather and illusion alike, is named in that final scene as “Mr. Bonebreaker’s umbrella.”

Mr. Bonebreaker was the “Gospel salesman” from Chicago to whom she was once engaged. He preached that “the end of the world is at hand, like a shadow running backward,” and dreamed always “of that heavenly crown, that golden raiment.” In Miss MacIntosh’s own judgment, “he could not bear to face the truth that he was poor.” He broke with her. Looking back, she “could see how she had been taken in,” though in the end she blamed life rather than him: she “had been betrayed without forewarning betrayed by life.”

So the most practical woman in the novel carried, for the rest of her life, a relic of its most extravagant dreamer. The instrument she held up against illusion was a gift from the man who deceived her with promises of a world to come. The novel does not explain this. It simply sets the umbrella on the sand, turned inside out “like a trumpet.” Even Miss MacIntosh’s realism was sheltered under something left over from a dream, which fits the pillar at the heart of this book: the solid thing turns out to be made, and made partly from what it opposes.

Mr. Bonebreaker also clarifies the ethical difference between the two kinds of faith. His looks to a golden beyond. Hers looks to the next task. The framework calls the second The Sacred Is Here: meaning is here, in the actual world underfoot, and the appeal to a distant beyond is the oldest way to justify sacrifice in the present. Miss MacIntosh learned that lesson by being on the wrong end of it.

Bad Faith and Ambiguity: Sartre and de Beauvoir

Jean-Paul Sartre gave the name bad faith to a particular kind of self-deception: the lie a person tells to escape their own freedom, usually by pretending that their situation, their role, or their nature leaves them no choice. Catherine looks like the textbook case. She withdraws into opium and lets the dream decide everything.

Yet the novel complicates the diagnosis. Chapter 3 noted that Catherine “pretended that the real was the dream.” She knows what she is doing. Bad faith in Sartre’s sense requires hiding the choice from oneself, and Catherine hides nothing. Her coma is a chosen sovereignty, openly held. Miss MacIntosh comes closer to Sartre’s model, because she denied for decades that her own solidity was a performance.

Simone de Beauvoir offers the more useful frame. In The Ethics of Ambiguity she argued that human beings are irreducibly ambiguous, free and bound, subject and object at once, and that the ethical task is to live inside that ambiguity rather than escape it. Every serious flight from ambiguity is a moral failure, whether it runs toward total freedom or toward total rule. By that measure Catherine and Miss MacIntosh fail in mirror image. Catherine resolves the ambiguity by dreaming it away. Miss MacIntosh resolves it by legislating it away. Vera is left with the ambiguity itself, which is the only place, de Beauvoir would say, where an ethical life can start.

Virtue as a Made Thing

Aristotle held that virtue is not born but built. A person becomes brave by doing brave things and just by doing just things, until repeated action hardens into a stable disposition. Character, in his account, is habit that has become second nature.

Read beside Young, the idea has a striking consequence. Virtue is exactly the kind of construction Pillar Two describes, a reality kept up by repetition. Miss MacIntosh’s steadiness was not a natural fact about her. She made it every morning with her tasks, her corset, her exercise, her proverbs, and her umbrella, just as she made her red hair every morning with a wig. The novel does not treat this as an exposure that cancels her goodness. It treats it as the way goodness always works. A virtue can be constructed and still be real. It is real in the way a basted seam is real: holding for now, because someone keeps holding it.

The Detective and the Quiet Mystery

The novel’s sharpest ethical scene has nothing to do with Vera’s household. It belongs to Esther Longtree, the waitress whose uncounted pregnancies were followed in Chapter 5, and to the detective who torments her.

He sends her “old newspaper clippings of old murders” by mail, as warnings, and threatens “her with exposure for having hidden away his little children.” Esther cannot tell the investigator from the crime: “Maybe, she guessed, he being the murderer he, the detective!” Her question to him is the question of the whole novel: “The truth that she was always pregnant? The truth that she was never pregnant?”

The detective is the novel’s portrait of a certain kind of knowing, the kind that can only see what its categories allow. “Things were either white or black to him.” He spends his life on “all the white mysteries,” but “the black mysteries were too quiet even for him and scared him away.” Esther’s sorrow is one of those quiet mysteries. His method has no place for a grief without evidence, so he treats it as a crime.

Here the argument of this book turns ethical. A person who mistakes a construction for bedrock, in this case the investigator’s sorting of the world into white and black, solvable and unsolvable, does real harm to the people who fall between its lines. Esther is the novel’s clearest case of what the framework calls The Margins Reveal the Truth, and the detective is its clearest case of what happens when a system refuses to look there.

The Ethics of Attention

What does the novel offer in place of the detective’s method? It offers listening. In the last chapter Vera could have told Esther everything: that her mother is dead, that the house has gone into the sea, that she is “in mourning for three deaths.” “But I spoke not,” she says, “for I listened as one might listen for one lonely heartbeat in the midst of the great silence.”

Simone Weil, whose account of perception Chapter 3 drew on, put attention at the center of ethics. For her, attention meant holding one’s own mind open and empty enough to receive another person as they actually are, without filling the gap with one’s own imaginings or judgments. The framework names the same stance the Witness: staying in the room with what is painful and complicated without rushing to fix it or wrap it up. Mr. Spitzer at every deathbed, Vera at Esther’s table, and Miss MacIntosh at the edge of Vera’s dreaming all practice some version of it.

None of them finishes the work. Mr. Spitzer never settles his brother’s case. Miss MacIntosh never completes Vera’s education. Vera never finds the woman she is looking for. The framework has a principle for this too, Unfinished Is Honest: the unfinished is not failure but honesty, and anyone who claims to hold the complete and final answer is usually selling something. In this novel that someone is Mr. Bonebreaker with his golden crown. Everyone else keeps basting.

Reflection Questions

  1. Miss MacIntosh prefers “large, straight basting stitches” because they “were more easily pulled out.” What would it mean to hold one’s own principles that way?

  2. Miss MacIntosh taught Vera an ordinary life she did not live herself. Is it hypocrisy to teach a rule one cannot follow, or can it be a form of protection?

  3. The realist’s umbrella was a gift from a dreamer who deceived her. What in ordinary life shelters people that came from something they have since rejected?

  4. Catherine knows she is pretending. Does knowing make a retreat from reality more honest, or only more deliberate?

  5. The detective sees “things” as “either white or black.” Where do systems of judgment fail the people who fall between their categories?

  6. Vera “spoke not” and listened to Esther instead. When is listening a stronger ethical act than speaking?
