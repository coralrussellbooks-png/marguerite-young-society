Research notes: Chapter 24 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 24

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

"There was an Irish housemaid who would never open her mouth to speak if there was more than one person present... She spoke only to herself, and she was several fine ladies and a swarm of fly-catching warblers"

The servants are the most explicit demonstration of the plural self; each one has fractured into multiple simultaneous identities. But the mother is the central case. She is simultaneously a telephone exchange, a social hostess, a dying woman, a dreamer, and perhaps the only genuine unbeliever in her own dreams:

"My mother herself might be, of course, the only genuine unbeliever, as everyone recognized, for she was very clever. My mother placed her faith only in the unreal."

This is not madness in the clinical sense; it is the honest condition of selfhood made visible. The mother contains contradictions that cannot be resolved: she both believes and disbelieves, she is both the dreamer and the skeptic of her own dream. The narrator herself is plural; she is child, witness, questioner, and the one who cannot find a stable interlocutor:

"To whom should I have turned that sun-lit morning, asking those personal questions which burned like low fires in my mind"

Even Mr. Spitzer is plural; he is simultaneously a roundhouse, a ghost ship, a bumble bee, a busy signal. No character in this chapter occupies a single, stable identity. The pillar is not illustrated; it is structurally enacted through the prose's refusal to fix anyone in place.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

This is perhaps the chapter's most explicit theme, but Young complicates it beyond simple statement. The servants' illusions are not contrasted with a stable reality; they are the operating reality of the household:

"In fact, just to hear the servants talk, anyone might have thought they were all real, my mother's opium dreams, especially when they were unreal."

That sentence performs the pillar. The qualifier "especially when they were unreal" collapses the distinction rather than reinforcing it. The illusions are most real precisely in their unreality, which means the category of "real" has been quietly dissolved.

The larger cave argument appears in the description of the house itself; it is not presented as a deviation from a normal world outside. There is no outside referenced. The house with its imaginary chandeliers and famous guests sleeping tier upon tier is simply the world as it is constituted. The narrator does not stand outside it looking in. She is inside it, asking her questions from within:

"My imagination wandered like a dark and formless cloud, seeking that certitude it might never have."

The certitude is not available because there is no position outside the constructed world from which to obtain it. The cave has no exit. There is only the quality of the lighting.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Grief in this chapter is not an event; it is the atmosphere. The bronze chrysanthemums are "the flowers for the dead" ; the fires are lit "to greet those guests who would never come" ; the telephone calls are placed to the dead who never answer:

"What of Uncle Ronald who was dead? What of Uncle Bernard who had died in Moscow while bringing a message to the czar?"

The mother does not grieve these losses in any sequential sense; she simply keeps calling. She keeps leaving messages. She keeps adding names to lists. Grief here is not a stage to be passed through but the very medium of her existence. She cannot stop calling because stopping would mean accepting the finality that her dreaming refuses.

The servants' grief is structural too; they polish shoes for guests who left no shoes, they wait on Wellington who is not there, they light fires for arrivals that will never come. The whole household is organized around the labor of perpetual anticipation and perpetual disappointment. This is grief as permanent weather; everyone lives inside it, and no one names it as grief because it has become simply the climate.

The narrator's grief is quieter but present in the question she cannot find anyone to answer:

"questions as to the secret purpose of life, questions as to love and marriage and reproduction and death"

These questions are not asked in hope of resolution. They burn "like low fires": persistent, low-level, ongoing. Not acute grief but chronic grief. The weather kind.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator's position in this chapter is almost entirely that of the witness. She does not intervene in her mother's dreams, does not correct the servants, does not attempt to restore order. She watches, catalogs, and asks questions she knows may not be answerable:

"Then who, I wondered, should offer any contrast to these wild illusions and selfish or selfless deceptions"

The wondering is the practice. She does not conclude. She does not diagnose her mother as simply mad and move on. She stays in the room, metaphorically and literally, attending to the full complexity of what she sees.

The ethical weight of this witnessing is made explicit in the contrast with Mr. Spitzer, who evades:

"he whose powers of evasion were greater than even he could realize"

Evasion is the failure mode. The narrator refuses it, even when what she witnesses resists resolution. The description of the servants (detailed, patient, accumulative) is itself an act of witnessing. Each servant gets their particularity noted: the one who polishes shoes, the one who thinks he waits on Wellington, the one who lives in a forest of cuckoo clocks, the one who is Hannibal. None is dismissed. All are seen.

The refusal to simplify is the act. Young's prose enacts this at the sentence level; it never arrives at a clean summary of what the mother is or what the household means. It keeps adding, qualifying, circling.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The mother's endless list-making and planning is the clearest image of recursive construction:

"My mother would discuss endlessly with her absent social secretaries these invitations and arrangements"

She builds the guest list knowing (at some level, as the "only genuine unbeliever") that the guests will not come. She builds anyway. She returns to the lists, the telephone directory, the arrangements. Morning slips into afternoon into evening, and the building continues. It is never finished. That is not presented as tragedy; it is simply her mode of being.

The servants participate in the same recursive construction. They arrange the Renaissance chairs "very intimately for conversation" that will not happen. They polish the brass fire-dogs in preparation for fires that will greet no one. They return to these tasks. The tasks are never completed because completion is not the point. The building is the point.

Even the narrator's questioning is recursive; she circles the same questions about life and death and love without arriving at answers, and the chapter ends not with resolution but with her imagination wandering "like a dark and formless cloud", still seeking, still building toward a certitude that remains out of reach.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter is formally incomplete; it does not conclude, it accumulates and then stops. This is not a failure of craft. It is an ethical stance toward the material.

The mother's telephone calls are the most poignant illustration:

"She had called the Bishop of Hippo, but he had always just stepped out, was always expected to return in an hour."

The Bishop of Hippo, Saint Augustine, has always just stepped out. The call is never completed. The connection is never made. And yet the mother keeps calling, keeps leaving messages. The incompletion is not failure; it is the honest condition of trying to reach across time and death.

The demand for completion is identified as its own kind of violence in the description of what happens to "the most simple and apparently sensible persons" who enter this employment:

"did they not seem to undergo, after an hour or a year or many years in this employment, a sea change, a transformation, to be other than themselves"

The implication is that the simple, sensible person, the one who demands completion and resolution, is the one who becomes most destabilized. The household's incompletion is not what damages people. The attempt to impose completion on it is.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Every character in this chapter is marginal: the opium-dreaming mother, the servants who have fractured into historical fantasies, the narrator who cannot find a stable interlocutor, Mr. Spitzer who evades his own memories. But the pillar's deepest application here is structural: the marginal characters are not exceptions to a normal world. They are the evidence of what the world actually is.

The valet who waits on Wellington, the gardener who believes he is Potiphar's spouse, the housemaid who is several fine ladies; these figures are not presented as deviations from sanity. They are presented as the most visible instances of something universal: the self's construction of its own reality.

"The most simple and apparently sensible persons, did they not seem to undergo... a sea change, a transformation, to be other than themselves, to reach for door knobs which were not door knobs, to open doors which were not doors"

The "most simple and apparently sensible" person is not immune. They undergo the same transformation, only more slowly. The marginal servant who thinks he waits on Wellington is not different in kind from anyone else; he is just further along a continuum that everyone is on. His marginality makes visible what is always already true.

The mad lady retouching her makeup in the second powder room (who may or may not exist, who may be a unicorn wearing a necklace) occupies the most marginal position in the chapter, and she is also the most haunting. Her existence cannot be confirmed or denied. She is the edge of the category "real person," and at that edge, the constructed nature of the category becomes fully visible.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This pillar is everywhere in the chapter's temporal structure. The past does not recede; it accumulates, presses upward, populates the present:

"there was a mad lady retouching her make-up each night in the second powder room... Who left, each night, dusty face powder streaking the dark mirrors of Venetian glass, dead rose petals upon a rain-washed marble stair?"

The dead rose petals are physical evidence of the past pressing into the present. The face powder on the mirrors is the sediment of previous nights. The past is not behind anyone in this household; it is beneath them, still active, still leaving traces.

The telephone directory is the most explicit image of sedimentary time:

"she, still wearing her garden hat, sat poring over the lists... some antiquated copy of the Boston or Philadelphia telephone directory, perhaps even of Amsterdam or London or Prague, circa 1905"

The 1905 directory is the sediment. The mother dials numbers from it as if the people listed there are still reachable. They are not behind her; they are beneath her, still pressing up. The calls that go unanswered are not failures of communication across distance. They are failures of communication across the accumulated layers of time. The servants who polish shoes left by imaginary guests, who arrange chairs for conversations that belong to previous centuries, are living inside sedimentary time. The past is not past. It is the ground they stand on and work within.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Young's prose is itself the argument here. The language of this chapter does not describe a pre-existing reality; it constructs the reality as it goes. Consider:

"she thought that Mr. Spitzer was a roundhouse of the trains which run no more, the broken trains, the trains of ruined sequences, the trains of vanished thoughts and dreams"

This is not metaphor in the conventional sense: a comparison between two separate things. It is language constituting what Mr. Spitzer is. He is the roundhouse, in the mother's dreaming world, and the mother's dreaming world is the world the chapter inhabits. The language does not point to a reality outside itself. It is the reality.

The mother's awareness that her telephone connects only to the servants' quarters is a moment of language's location becoming visible; she has been speaking as if she were calling across time and the world, but the line runs only to the next room. The pretense of speaking from nowhere (calling Athens, calling the Bishop of Hippo) is revealed as speaking from somewhere very particular (a bedroom in a large house, connected to servants who may or may not reply).

The servants' language is equally constructive, by discussing Wellington, they make Wellington present. By naming the old valet "Wellington," they constitute him as Wellington. Language here is not describing the social world of the household. It is making it.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter does not point outward to a beyond. It lives inside the ordinary: inside the bronze chrysanthemums, the polished fire-dogs, the dusty face powder on the Venetian mirrors.

"The servants moved about in the dimmed evening light, arranging flowers in crystal vases, flowers as big as human heads, the bronze-colored chrysanthemums which are the flowers for the dead, lighting fires in jeweled fireplaces to greet those guests who would never come."

This is a liturgical passage. The servants perform a ritual (flowers, fire, arrangement, preparation) for an arrival that will not come. The ritual does not become meaningless because the guests are absent. It becomes more sacred. The care lavished on an empty house, the attention given to guests who are dead or imaginary, is the sacred living inside the immanent.

The mother's endless party-planning is the same; she is not planning a party in any practical sense. She is performing devotion to a world she has constructed and believes in more than she believes in the "real" world. Mr. Spitzer's "euphemistic certainties that an ultimate Heaven should unite us all" are explicitly contrasted with this: his transcendence points outward, to a beyond. The mother's transcendence points deeper into the immanent world of her dreams, her guests, her chrysanthemums, her telephone calls.

"She should die in the midst of her dreams and not be awakened, never know when she died"

This is immanent transcendence as the ideal death, not a crossing into another world, but a deepening into this one until the boundary between dreaming and dying dissolves.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is perhaps the most structurally embedded pillar in the chapter. The narrator is explicitly constituted by what was deposited before she arrived:

"The refractions of that drugged, dazzling atmosphere produced snow crystals with their faces half inclined toward my mother"

The narrator is a refraction of her mother's atmosphere. She did not choose this. It is structural. The mother's opium dreams, the servants' fantasies, the household's accumulated sediment: all of this is the ground the narrator stands on, and it was deposited before she could have consented to it.

The telephone calls to the dead relatives (Cousin Hannah, Cousin Wilbur who disappeared at sea, Uncle Ronald who was dead, Uncle Bernard who died in Moscow) are the ancestors in the sediment. The mother keeps calling them not because she expects them to answer but because the obligation to call is structural. They are in the ground. The carrying continues.

The narrator's final position in the chapter is that of someone who is constituted by all of this and is now, in the act of witnessing and narrating, constituting the ground that comes after:

"My imagination wandered like a dark and formless cloud, seeking that certitude it might never have."

She is the crossing point, where the mother's world of illusion crosses with Miss MacIntosh's world of practical reality, where the dead cross with the living, where the question crosses with the unanswerable. She does not resolve this. She carries it. That is the structural obligation of the crossing point. It is not chosen. It continues.
