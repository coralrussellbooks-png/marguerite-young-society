Chapter 82

On Chapter 82 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Christianity and Religion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 24, 2026

Characters: Esther Longtree · The Stone Deaf Man

Allusions: Deafness and Sign Language · The Bible

“Thou hast wrapped me in Thy fleece”: The hymns Miss MacIntosh sings reveal a profound spiritual dimension to her character. While she keeps her doubt to herself (“Her doubt was her own wound”) and will not be “the stumbling block in another’s path,” these songs from her childhood represent an enduring, if conflicted, faith. They expose her inner struggles with vulnerability, physical and emotional wounds, and the existential human condition of being lost and in need of guidance. These moments of singing, even as she performs mundane tasks, highlight the tension between her practical life and her deep, unarticulated spiritual longings, demonstrating that her common sense and brusqueness are often a defense against profound personal suffering and a search for meaning in a chaotic world. See Miss MacIntosh and The Bible. “Lo, she would lift her eyes to the hills from whence cometh help.” This references Psalm 121, often the basis of hymns about looking to the hills for help. “I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills, from whence cometh my help” (Psalm 121:1, KJV). “Thou didst turn Thy face from me, and I was troubled” echoes Psalm 30:7: “thou didst hide thy face, and I was troubled” (KJV). “For Thou art my strong rock and my castle, so also be my guide.” This refers to God as a “strong rock and castle,” a common biblical metaphor used in hymns. In the psalter of the Book of Common Prayer (Coverdale’s translation), Psalm 31:3 reads, “For thou art my strong rock, and my castle: be thou also my guide,” nearly Miss MacIntosh’s very words. “Thou hast covered me, and Thou hast numbered the hairs of my head, and Thou hast closed the wound of my side. Thou hast plucked out the arrows.” These lines evoke biblical imagery associated with God’s protection and healing, reminiscent of hymns about divine care and salvation. “The very hairs of your head are all numbered” (Matthew 10:30); “thou hast covered me in my mother’s womb” (Psalm 139:13); “one of the soldiers with a spear pierced his side” (John 19:34); “thine arrows stick fast in me” (Psalm 38:2, KJV). “Song of the shepherd who would lead all sheep home, losing none in narrow defiles” alludes to the Good Shepherd theme common in Christian hymns. “I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep” (John 10:11, KJV). “Song of the Rock of Ages cleft for me” directly mentions the famous hymn “Rock of Ages.” Augustus Toplady’s hymn (published 1775): “Rock of Ages, cleft for me, / Let me hide myself in thee.” “Songs of the Rugged Cross” refers to the hymn “The Old Rugged Cross,” focused on the crucifixion. George Bennard wrote it in 1912. Songs “of the shaded valley, of the still stream” evoke traditional hymn imagery of peaceful pastoral scenes symbolizing God’s care. “He leadeth me beside the still waters” (Psalm 23:2, KJV); “As the hart panteth after the water brooks” (Psalm 42:1, KJV). “Songs of those who reel to and fro and stagger like a drunken man, of the lost soul, of the pinioned wings” suggest hymns that address human frailty, being lost, and spiritual struggle. “They reel to and fro, and stagger like a drunken man, and are at their wit’s end” (Psalm 107:27, KJV). Songs “of the lighthouse beam which lights the way to the poor wanderer tossed upon the rolling waves far from any harbor” is a metaphor often used in hymns about guidance and salvation. “Awake, thou lute and harp, she sang. I myself will awake right early.” This is Psalm 57 in the psalter of the Book of Common Prayer: “awake, lute and harp: I myself will awake right early.” The King James has “awake, psaltery and harp: I myself will awake early” (Psalm 57:8). “The waters have drowned us. The stream has gone over our soul.” is Psalm 124 in the psalter of the Book of Common Prayer: “Yea, the waters had drowned us: and the stream had gone over our soul.” Earlier in Chapters 15, 20 and 65.

The book closes on a run of psalms. Esther "lifts her eyes to the hills from whence cometh help" (Psalm 121), calls God "my strong rock and my castle" (Psalm 31), and sings of one who has "numbered the hairs of my head" (Matthew 10) and "closed the wound of my side" (John 19).

She sings "of the Rugged Cross," from the old hymn, and of waters that have drowned us, "the stream has gone over our soul" (Psalm 124), the last images of a soul going under.

The final Christian note is the stone-deaf man's "My God Who art my enchantment, we be in the wrong hotel," a God who is pure enchantment and a life that has wandered into the wrong place, the book ending on misplacement and wonder together.

“when Gabriel blew his horn”: “For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God” (1 Thessalonians 4:16, KJV); The Bible does not name the angel who sounds the last trump; the spiritual “Blow Your Trumpet, Gabriel,” printed in Slave Songs of the United States (1867), gives it to Gabriel.
