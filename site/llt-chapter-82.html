Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 82

← Chapter 81 · Life, Love, and Truth · Epilogue →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Pillars: Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act · The Sacred Is Here

The Questions

The opium paradise has crumbled; “the sea had swept over the dark house.” What happens to a sealed world when the flood comes?

Of everything, “the only property which was left to me was this string of pearls.” What remains after the seal is gone?

Vera is “in mourning for three deaths” and yet still “in love with an older love than memory provided.” How does she carry grief and desire at once?

She could confess to Esther but does not. Where does the book leave its seeker at the very end?

The novel ends by drowning the sealed world. Vera could tell Esther “that my mother was dead, that the opium paradise had crumbled, that the sea had swept over the dark house with its golden turrets and towers, that all had been carried away.” Catherine’s sealed dream, the shuttered house of stopped clocks and imaginary guests, the invention closed against loss, is simply gone under the flood, swept away, carried off. The book has said all along that the sealed construction fails; here, at the end, it shows the greatest of its sealed worlds literally destroyed, the sea taking the dark house and everything in it, the opium paradise that admitted no theory of loss lost entirely to the water. What is sealed does not survive. The flood takes it.

What remains is a remainder, small and ordinary and worn. Of the crumbled paradise, “the only property which was left to me was this string of pearls I wore,” and the chandeliers “strewn for miles at the sea’s edge.” The sealed world is gone and what is left is a strand of pearls on Vera’s neck, the scattered glass at the tide line, the remainder of a vast construction. This is The Sacred Is Here at the book’s close: after the grand sealed thing is swept away, what endures is the small worn ordinary object, the pearls Vera actually wears, the here-and-now remainder she can carry. Not the paradise but the string of pearls, not the golden house but the glass at the water’s edge, the ordinary surviving the sealed.

Vera carries grief and desire together, unresolved and unsealed. She is “in mourning for three deaths,” and in the same breath “in love with an older love than memory provided,” grieving and longing at once, the losses not closed and the desire not extinguished. This is the seeker at the end of her education, holding what the book has taught her to hold: grief that does not resolve, kept open as honest weather, and alongside it a love, a longing, an openness toward something older and further than anything memory can supply. She does not seal her grief into a finished mourning or her desire into a fixed object. She carries both, open, the way Mr. Spitzer carried his, the way the book has said a life must.

And the book leaves its seeker on the crossing, still open, having become the Witness. Vera could confess her own losses to Esther but does not, because Esther is not interested, and so Vera stays the listener, the one who has heard another’s woe all morning and keeps her own; she has become the Witness the book trained her to be. She set out asking for a truth that does not fail, and she ends not with a sealed answer clutched in her hand but standing at the crossing point, in mourning and in love, carrying her string of pearls, having learned to witness, having watched every sealed world fail and this last one drown. The book does not deliver her a finished truth. It leaves her open, at the crossing, still seeking, which is the only truth it holds does not fail.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that wants the novel to end by handing Vera a settled answer, the truth she set out to find delivered at last, and the reading that takes the drowning of the paradise and the three deaths for simple defeat, the seeker’s quest ending in loss. Neither the delivered answer nor the final defeat is what the ending offers.

The footing is that the sealed world does not survive the flood, Catherine’s opium paradise swept away entire, because what is sealed always fails; that what remains after the seal is gone is the small ordinary remainder, the worn string of pearls, the glass at the tide line, The Sacred Is Here enduring where the grand construction could not; that Vera carries grief and desire together, unresolved and unsealed, mourning three deaths and still in love with an older love; and that the book leaves her on the crossing, still open, having become the Witness, still seeking rather than having found. The sealed drowns, the ordinary remains, and the seeker ends open. Vera stands at the crossing in mourning and in love, carrying her pearls, and the book leaves her there, which is where it has said the only truth that does not fail is kept.

For Reflection

When a sealed world one has depended on is swept away, what small ordinary remainder is left to carry?

How does one hold grief that will not close and desire that will not die, both at once, without sealing either?

If the seeker ends still seeking, open, at the crossing rather than in possession of a final answer, has the quest failed, or arrived?
