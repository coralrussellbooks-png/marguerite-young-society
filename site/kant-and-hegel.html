Chapter 8

  • Representation is a philosophical theory that argues that the mind perceives only mental images (representations) of the external world, not the world itself. It's opposed to realism, which holds that we directly grasp objects as they are in the world. In his philosophy, Kant distinguishes between intuitions (immediate, sensory representations) and concepts (general, mediate representations). He also distinguishes between appearances (things as they appear to us) and things-in-themselves (the world as it is independently of our perception).

Chapter 14

  • The meaninglessness of time in Kant’s antinomies is in philosophy. An antinomy is a real or apparent contradiction between two conclusions, both of which seem justified. It is a term used in logic and epistemology, particularly in the philosophy of Immanuel Kant. Kant's antinomies are four: two "mathematical" and two "dynamical". They are connected with (1) the limitation of the universe in respect of space and time (whether the universe has a beginning or is infinite), (2) the theory that the whole consists of indivisible atoms (whereas, in fact, none such exist), (3) the problem of free will in relation to universal causality (free will vs determinism), and (4) the existence of a necessary being. The first two antinomies are dubbed "mathematical" antinomies, presumably because in each case we are concerned with the relation between what are alleged to be sensible objects (either the world itself, or objects in it) and space and time. The second two are dubbed "dynamical" antinomies, presumably because the proponents of the thesis are not committing themselves solely to claims about spatio-temporal objects.

Chapter 22

  • George Berkeley was an Anglo-Irish philosopher, writer, and clergyman who is regarded as the founder of “immaterialism”, a philosophical theory he developed which was later referred to as “subjective idealism” by others. He was an influence on Kant and Hume. Berkeley, California and the University of California, Berkeley are named after him.

Chapter 45

  • “Shall love be infinitely postponed? If infinitely postponed, then it is love.” may refer to Hegel. To deny something is to determine it, and to determine something is to negate it. Spinoza’s principle is “every determination is negation”. Hegel takes this one step further by suggesting that negation itself is a process of creation, and is even the essence of positive being. By denying a certain trait or characteristic, one is implicitly acknowledging the possibility of its opposite or the existence of a different reality. For Hegel, this power of the negative, or the act of negation, is what drives progress and development. Denying a particular concept allows for the possibility of a new one to emerge, incorporating both the original concept and its negation. This is often explained through the concept of the negation of the negation, where a denial of a denial leads to a new synthesis that transcends both the original thesis and the antithesis. From an existentialist perspective denying a predefined purpose or essence allows for the possibility of individuals to create their own meaning and existence. The line speaks to love as an infinite emotional state, one that transcends immediate gratification and endures through time, absence, and even death, resonating with the novel's larger themes of longing, loss, and the elusive nature of human connection.

Chapter 48

  • In Hegel's philosophy, negation is not simply a denial or opposition, but a dynamic and constructive force driving the development of concepts and reality. It's a key aspect of his dialectical method, where a concept (thesis) generates its opposite (antithesis), and the resolution of this opposition (synthesis) leads to a more advanced understanding. This process of negation, or "negativity," is essential for overcoming limitations and contradictions in thought and experience. Negation in the chapter functions to unsettle any notion of a stable, knowable reality or self. It reflects the novel's preoccupation with the interplay between presence and absence, being and non-being, and the limits of human understanding in the face of cosmic and existential mysteries. This persistent negation challenges the reader to confront the impermanence and ambiguity at the heart of the human condition as portrayed in the narrative.

Chapter 49

  • Conceptualism - Conceptualism is a position that is meshed between realism and nominalism. Conceptualists believe that universals can indeed be real, but only existing as concepts within the mind. Conceptualists argue that the "concept" of universals are not mere "inventions but are reflections of similarities among particular things themselves." For example, the concept of 'man' ultimately reflects a similarity amongst Socrates and Kant.

Chapter 49

  • Peter Abelard was a medieval French scholastic philosopher, leading logician, theologian, teacher, musician, composer, and poet. In philosophy he is celebrated for his proposed logical solution to the problem of universals via nominalism and conceptualism and his pioneering of intent in ethics. Often referred to as the "Descartes of the twelfth century", he is considered a forerunner of Rousseau, Kant, and Spinoza. He is sometimes credited as a chief forerunner of modern empiricism. In Catholic theology, he is best known for his development of the concept of limbo, and his introduction of the moral influence theory of atonement. He is considered (alongside Augustine) to be the most significant forerunner of the modern self-reflective autobiographer. He paved the way and set the tone for later epistolary novels and celebrity tell-alls with his publicly distributed letter, The History of My Calamities and public correspondence. In history and popular culture he is best known for his passionate, tragic love affair and intense philosophical exchange with his brilliant student and eventual wife, Héloïse d'Argenteuil. After they secretly married and went to different convents Heloise’s guardian had Abelard ambushed and castrated. Roscellinus and others made fun of him. He did father a son before that event.
