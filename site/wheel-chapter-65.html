Chapter 65

On Chapter 65 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of The Wheel and the Spokes.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Research notes: Chapter 65 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 65

Characters: John Knox MacIntosh · Lorena · Mercy MacIntosh · Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Titus Bonebreaker

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

"Yet one revelation seemed to lead to another — and Miss MacIntosh, little by little, seemed to take on many moods and many dimensions such as, in the previously well-ordered days of almost meaningless years, I had hardly guessed."

Miss MacIntosh is not one person. She is simultaneously:

  • The stern, practical nurse of the "previously well-ordered days"
  • The bald, wounded woman revealed in September's dying light
  • The young hopeful in black gloves walking Chicago's rain-soaked streets
  • The pin-setter in the bowling alley, staggering after surgery
  • The soprano singing Love Lifted Me to the blind
  • The would-be bride who was never a bride
  • The woman who walked into the ocean

None of these selves cancels the others. They are simultaneous and irresolvable, layered rather than sequential. The narrator's growing awareness of Miss MacIntosh's multiplicity is itself the chapter's central drama, not any external event, but the collapse of the illusion that a person is singular and legible.

The narrator explicitly names this: Miss MacIntosh becomes "a storm center of mirages", "perhaps being nothing in herself" while simultaneously being "a mirror to the dreams of others." The plural self is not a psychological curiosity here; it is the ontological condition of every person in the novel, made visible through Miss MacIntosh's stripped-bare literalness.

Mr. Bonebreaker, too, is plural in ways he cannot acknowledge: the failed royalty claimant, the Gospel salesman, the shoe-store clerk, the man who sleeps with his umbrella, the man who cracked heads in saloons, the man who ran from a bald woman into the hailstones. He is constituted by his contradictions: eating eggs while refusing meat, preaching the end of time while consulting a punctual watch, proposing marriage while perpetually deferring it. He cannot hold his own multiplicity, and this failure is precisely what destroys him as a moral actor in Miss MacIntosh's life.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

This pillar runs through the chapter as its deepest structural principle.

"Perhaps she would be a mirror held up to a mirror when all images faded, when there was neither moon nor sun nor star nor cloud."

Young does not offer us a "real" Miss MacIntosh beneath the wigs. The wigs are not concealment of a true self; they are themselves constitutive. The black wig worn in memory of Lorena. The red wig worn in memory of Richard. The ashen wig worn at Niagara Falls in the attempt to remake herself entirely. Each wig is a constructed reality, and each is as real as any other. The bald head beneath is not more true; it is simply another layer, equally constructed, equally contingent.

The chapter's most philosophically charged moment on this point is the Niagara Falls passage:

"She changed from the dark to the fair... she was the changed person, wearing an ashen wig, for it was her decision to put the past entirely behind her."

This is not self-deception presented as failure. Young presents it with a kind of solemn respect. The construction of a new self through a new wig is not less real than the bald self it covers. The line between illusion and reality is, as the Pillar states, itself a construction.

Mr. Bonebreaker's entire existence dramatizes Pillar 2 from the other direction. He builds an elaborate reality (the imminent end of the world, his claim to the Swedish throne, Miss MacIntosh as a beautiful woman with flowing hair) and inhabits it with total conviction. His reality is more coherent than most, precisely because he has constructed it so completely. When the construction fails (the moment Miss MacIntosh removes her wig), he cannot integrate the new information. He runs. The cave he built was carefully lit, but it was still a cave, and he could not survive a larger one.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

"My poor darling. When she was young and hopeful, she had scrupulously mended those black gloves for a sad occasion which she could not foresee."

This is the chapter's emotional center, and it is a perfect enactment of grief as climate rather than event. The grief does not arrive with Mr. Bonebreaker's flight. It was already present in the loss of the breast, in the childhood in What Cheer, in the brother Richard lost among the head-hunters. It accumulates across the chapter in layers: the pink envelope burned in the fireplace, the black gloves mended with white thread, the watch worn over the missing breast.

The chapter's most devastating grief-as-weather passage:

"Those black gloves she mended so patiently, with such great, ominous care, awkward but graceful, white thread zigzagging on black, were those very black gloves she must have worn when, with her head shining, bald as a lost planet, with her hands crossed over two major wounds, the breast, the side, she walked into the roaring ocean on a cold September morning."

The reader knows the end before the story arrives there. The grief is proleptic; it reaches backward from the ocean into every scene of mending, every patient domestic act. The gloves she mends for a future she cannot foresee are the gloves she wears to her death. This is grief as permanent weather in its most precise form: the loss was always already happening, and the honest life, Miss MacIntosh's life, did not pretend otherwise, even when it could not name what it knew.

The chapter refuses the stages of grief entirely. Miss MacIntosh does not move from wound to healing to acceptance. She moves from wound to work to ocean. The grief does not resolve. It accumulates until it becomes the sea itself.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator (young, naive, hopeful by her own admission) is positioned throughout as witness:

"I felt as if it were I who was far away from reality now, not she, and might I never return to reality if this was what I found in recompense for all that had been lost."

The narrator's function in this chapter is not to intervene, explain, or resolve. She bends over Miss MacIntosh's bald head and sees prisms and flames. She listens to the history of Mr. Bonebreaker without flinching. She observes the gradual dissolution of Miss MacIntosh's person (the increasing informality, the wig removed, the breast exposed) without rushing toward conclusion.

"It seemed to me that all people might love her if they saw her as I saw her now in her essential loneliness and mystery stripped bare of all pretense."

This is the witness's credo. The narrator does not try to save Miss MacIntosh. She does not argue against the ocean. She stays in the room, attends fully, and refuses to simplify. The chapter's extraordinary length, its refusal to compress or summarize, is itself the act of witnessing. Young's prose enacts Pillar 4 formally: the refusal to rush toward resolution is built into the syntax, which spirals and recurses rather than proceeding.

The narrator's admission that she "scarcely listened to these sad prophecies of mortal woe" is not a failure of witnessing; it is its honest acknowledgment. The witness is not omniscient. She is present, imperfect, and continuing.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The chapter is structured recursively. Miss MacIntosh's history is not told linearly but returned to, circled, approached from different angles. The Chicago story is begun, interrupted, resumed. The Mr. Bonebreaker narrative loops back to What Cheer, forward to Buffalo, back to Chicago, forward to Niagara Falls, back to the blind musician, forward to the ocean.

"She had made her plans. As Mrs. Titus Bonebreaker, she had planned, she would be able to hold up her head among the women of What Cheer."

The plan is built knowing it will not be completed. Miss MacIntosh buys the mutilated crockery for the mission. She finds the abandoned poultry store. She imagines the hens and the rooster. She builds anyway. The building is never finished, Mr. Bonebreaker runs, but the building was not therefore meaningless. The crockery was purchased. The plans were real. The provisional construction is the only construction available, and Miss MacIntosh, for all her practicality, understands this.

The watch given as an engagement seal, worn for decades over the missing breast, is the most precise emblem of Pillar 5 in the chapter. It is the artifact of an unfinished building, carried forward into a life that continued being built anyway:

"Which for many, many years she had worn on her dress over the breastbone where the breast was missing — as if to equalize the unequal body."

She returns to the beginning with what she learned. The watch keeps time over the wound. The building continues.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Mr. Bonebreaker's marriage is "continually postponed", and this postponement is presented not as his failure of commitment but as a structural feature of his entire relationship to reality. He cannot complete because completion would require confronting what is actually there. His theology of the world's imminent end is itself a theology of incompletion elevated to cosmic principle: the world will end before anything needs to be finished.

Miss MacIntosh's life, by contrast, embodies incompletion as integrity. She never finishes the mission. She never marries. She never resolves her relationship to Mr. Bonebreaker, to her baldness, to her lost breast. But she mends the gloves. She darns the black cotton gloves. She sews on the seed pearl button. These are ongoing sentences, not concluded ones.

"God forgive me for this present vanity... and yet may I not hope what every woman hopes? There is surely a man somewhere in every woman's life."

This is incompletion at its most ethically precise. She sews on the button. She hopes. She knows the hope is probably futile. She sews on the button anyway. Unfinished Is Honest does not demand resolution; it demands the ongoing act, which is itself the integrity.

The chapter's own form embodies Pillar 6. It does not conclude. Miss MacIntosh walks into the ocean, but the prose does not treat this as resolution:

"The sun and the moon shone in the same pearly sky when she walked into the ocean. There was no direct light but only a diffusion of blowing light... At last, however, she was given to something."

"At last, however, she was given to something." This is not the language of completion. It is the language of a process that continues in a different register. The unfinished life does not become finished by ending.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Miss MacIntosh is the chapter's central evidence for this Pillar, and she is designed to be.

She is bald in a world where hair signifies femininity, desirability, and social legibility. She has one breast in a world that counts women by their bodies. She is unmarried by circumstance rather than choice. She is from What Cheer, Iowa: a place whose very name is ironic, whose horizons are "limited", where "seeing was believing" and Mr. Bonebreaker would have to be seen to be believed.

"She was born with some deficiencies, and she had acquired others, being mortal."

This sentence is devastating in its precision. The deficiencies are both congenital and accumulated. She is marginal by birth and by experience. And it is precisely this marginality that makes her the evidence: the place where the constructed nature of beauty, femininity, normalcy, and salvation becomes most visible.

Mr. Bonebreaker's Biblical quotations about baldness are the chapter's darkest irony: "Instead of well-set hair, there would be a baldness". He preaches baldness as apocalyptic punishment to a woman who is already bald, without knowing it. The marginal person is present in the center of the discourse that defines her as marginal, and the discourse cannot see her.

The blind musician passage extends this:

"She was born without her hair as they without their eyes, that she was a type of beauty which was vanquished before time began."

"A type of beauty which was vanquished before time began." This is Pillar 7 at its most philosophically acute. The marginal is not the exception to beauty; it is the evidence of beauty's constructed and violent nature. Miss MacIntosh's baldness is not a deviation from a norm. It is the norm's exposure.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The chapter's temporal structure is explicitly sedimentary. The past does not recede. It presses upward through everything.

The watch worn over the missing breast for "many, many years" is sedimentary time made wearable. The black umbrella: plucked by Mr. Bonebreaker from a dead man, left behind in his flight, carried by Miss MacIntosh for the rest of her life, turned inside out on the beach at her death: is an object that accumulates time in layers: the dead man's time, Mr. Bonebreaker's time, Miss MacIntosh's time, the ocean's time.

"Old church bells rang in memory, after all, like bells under water. She heard them."

This is Pillar 8 in its most lyrical form. The What Cheer childhood does not recede when Miss MacIntosh stands in the rain in Chicago listening to Mr. Bonebreaker. It rises. The bells ring from below. The past is beneath her, still pressing.

The chapter's narrative structure enacts this formally. The narrator moves between Miss MacIntosh's present dying and her past Chicago, not as flashback but as simultaneous layers. The bald woman mending gloves by the hurricane lamp and the young woman in the black wig staggering after surgery are not separated by time in the prose; they press against each other, the past beneath the present, still active.

"Perhaps the doctor knew. She had already gone back to her work in the bowling alley where, every evening, it was her duty to set ninepins up as the balls knocked against them and caused them to fall."

The ninepins fall and are set up again. This is sedimentary time as labor: the past is always falling, and the work is always setting it back up. The bowling alley is not merely a job. It is a cosmological image of how time works in this chapter.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Young's prose is the most direct enactment of Pillar 9 in the chapter. The language does not report reality; it constructs it in the act of speaking, and it names its own constructedness.

Mr. Bonebreaker speaks almost entirely in Biblical quotation, and the chapter is acutely aware of what this means:

"He spoke words more than necessary, as if he were in love with the sound of his rushing voice."

His language does not describe the world; it replaces it. He cannot see Miss MacIntosh's face because his language has already constructed a face for her: "her hair was as a flock of wild goats... her neck was a tower of ivory... her eyelids were the eyelids of morning". The Biblical language shapes what can be seen. It makes Miss MacIntosh invisible by making her too visible in the wrong register.

The narrator's language, by contrast, is explicitly self-aware of its own location:

"Perhaps I was confused now by my dreams of her even before she should leave me."

The narrator names her own constructedness as witness. She does not speak from nowhere. She speaks from the position of a young, naive, hopeful girl who is already dreaming the person she is trying to see. This is honest language in the Pillar 9 sense: it names its own location rather than pretending to neutrality.

The chapter's most formally interesting moment of language-as-construction is the Chamber of Commerce pamphlets mixed in with the Bible literature:

"See Chicago and live — for he had mixed up with his Bible literature some pamphlets of the Chamber of Commerce, and the messages were not the same."

They are not the same, but they are both constructions, both languages that shape what can be seen, both speaking from somewhere in particular. The juxtaposition is comic and devastating simultaneously. Language that promises salvation and language that promises new skyscrapers are both, in Young's hands, constructions that cannot deliver their goods.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter is entirely immanent. It lives in the ordinary objects, the domestic acts, the grey and salt-stained particulars of Miss MacIntosh's existence.

"She hemmed an old grey skirt, patched an old coat with many-colored patches, sewed on bone buttons, darned her long-fingered black cotton gloves."

This is the chapter's liturgy. The hemming and patching and darning are sacred acts, not because they point toward a beyond, but because they are fully present in the immanent world, performed with attention and care in the face of approaching death. The sacred here is the act of mending what will not last, of preparing for a winter that will not come, of sewing a seed pearl button onto gloves that will be worn into the ocean.

Miss MacIntosh's baldness is immanent transcendence made literal:

"Sometimes I saw a prism or a flame reflected there or a flame which seemed to gleam under her bald dome and sometimes the reflection of an eye gleaming as if at the world's top."

The sacred is visible in the bald head, not beyond it, not above it, but in it, as light in a physical surface. The narrator bends over the dome and sees the world reflected there. This is the sacred as depth within the immanent, not height above it.

Mr. Bonebreaker's religion, by contrast, is the chapter's counter-example: a transcendence that points entirely outward and upward, toward heavenly crowns and ivory gates and angels on cherubim. His God is always somewhere else, in the beginning, at the end, in the sky over Chicago. He cannot see the sacred in Miss MacIntosh's face because he is looking in the wrong direction.

The ocean into which Miss MacIntosh walks is the chapter's final image of immanent transcendence:

"At last, however, she was given to something."

Not to heaven. Not to God. To the ocean, which is here, which is immanent, which is the same water that ran under the frozen lake in Chicago, the same water that fell as rain around Mr. Bonebreaker's umbrella, the same water that is the first and last element of this world. The transcendence is entirely within the immanent. She is given to something that was always already here.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is the Pillar that the chapter's final pages most directly enact, and it is where the analysis must end.

"I felt as if it were I who was far away from reality now, not she, and might I never return to reality if this was what I found in recompense for all that had been lost, this more beautiful than life or death, this which was death in the heart of life or life in the heart of death."

The narrator is constituted by Miss MacIntosh. She is where Miss MacIntosh's life crosses into the life of the next generation. She carries the watch, the umbrella, the history of Mr. Bonebreaker, the image of the bald head with its prisms and flames. She is the ground on which Miss MacIntosh's life continues to press upward.

The chapter's most explicit statement of Pillar 11:

"Her death might seem more difficult to her than her life had been, certainly... and perhaps in some ways she had already done so as I scarcely realized then, being young, naive, hopeful, and hopeful most when there was no reason for hope."

The narrator will carry Miss MacIntosh forward. The obligation is not chosen; it is structural. Miss MacIntosh deposited herself in the narrator's sediment before the narrator understood what was being deposited.

And Miss MacIntosh herself is a crossing point in the same sense. She carries What Cheer, Iowa, and John Knox MacIntosh, and Mercy MacIntosh who knew how to cut the umbilical cord in the snow, and the bald generations of dour Scots, and Mr. Bonebreaker's watch with his grandmother's snow-colored hair in the back compartment. She is constituted by what was deposited before she arrived. She carries the ancestors in her sediment. She walks into the ocean carrying all of it.

"The sun and the moon shone in the same pearly sky when she walked into the ocean. There was no direct light but only a diffusion of blowing light. There was no division between the turbulent water and the wild, wet sky."

No division. This is the Crossing Point as image: the place where the boundaries dissolve, where the self that was deposited and the self that will deposit meet in a single undivided light. The ancestors are in the sediment. The carrying continues. Miss MacIntosh walks into the water that was always already her element, and the narrator remains on the shore, carrying what was given.
