Part Three: Terre Haute, 1880

Part of a reading of Harp Song for a Radical (1999), Marguerite Young’s life of Eugene Victor Debs.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 25, 2026

The real history that connects Debs to Hannah’s cause

Cousin Hannah belongs to a tradition. The society’s essay “Married to the Movement” places her in a line of warrior women that runs from the Amazons through Boudicca and Joan of Arc to Emmeline Pankhurst, calling her “all of these condensed into a single Boston feminist.” Her chapters are full of suffrage battles, and in the novel’s account she carried the cause to the far corners of the earth.

Behind every fictional suffragette stands the real one Americans knew best, and Susan B. Anthony appears by name in Harp Song for a Radical. The real history behind that appearance brings Debs and Hannah’s cause into the same room.

As a young man in Terre Haute, Debs helped found a lecture society, the Occidental Literary Club, and served as its president. The club brought national speakers to town: Robert Ingersoll, the freethinking orator; Wendell Phillips, the old abolitionist who had taken up the cause of labor; and the poet James Whitcomb Riley. Then Debs proposed Anthony, and the club refused to sponsor her. That refusal brought him face to face with the cause of women’s rights and with the scorn its leaders met wherever they went.

Debs went ahead anyway. In a reminiscence published in 1909, he wrote: “I had of course read of Susan B. Anthony and from the ridicule and contempt with which she was treated I concluded that she must be a strong advocate of, and doing effective work for, the rights of her sex.” With Ida Husted Harper, who would later become Anthony’s biographer, he arranged her meetings himself. He met her train. In 1912 he remembered that Harper, he, and two or three others “were about the only people in Terre Haute who believed that woman was a human being.”

This is the scene a reader of Young’s novel can hold against Hannah’s chapters. Hannah is the suffragist on the march, met with jeers in foreign cities. Debs is the young man on the platform waiting for her train, in a town that had closed its doors.

Young did not leave this scene to the historians. Harp Song for a Radical gives Anthony several pages, set among the lecturers the Occidental Literary Club brought to Terre Haute: Robert Ingersoll, Wendell Phillips, and the woman the club would not sponsor. In Young’s telling, much of the town shunned Anthony, and Debs welcomed her and escorted her through the streets in public. Ida Husted Harper, who wrote the Woman’s Department of the firemen’s magazine Debs edited, stands beside him. Young also notes that Debs supported woman suffrage from the start, well before most labor leaders.

So the line from Cousin Hannah to Eugene Debs is not only something a reader can draw. Young drew it herself. She put a suffragette at the exact center of her novel, and then, in her history, she walked the most famous suffragist in America through an Indiana town that had turned its back, with a young Debs at her side. The novel gives the crusade from inside the crusader’s armor. The biography gives it from the platform where the rare ally waited for her train.
