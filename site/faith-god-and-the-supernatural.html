Faith, God, and the Supernatural

Part of To All My Darlings, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Definition from research

Chapter 3 · Faith and Doubt

  • While Miss MacIntosh acknowledges God, she does not believe in extravagant religious fantasies or promises of an afterlife.
  • She sees life itself as a sufficient test and believes in accepting one’s lot without illusions.

Chapter 7 · Mystery and Suspicion

  • The deaths of Josh and his brother Rudolph are shrouded in mystery and accusations of murder, highlighting themes of betrayal and concealed violence.
  • The locked door and fire incident suggest murder but remain unproven, emphasizing distrust and ambiguity.

Chapter 8 · Isolation and Mystery

  • The setting (a spectral village at night, broken fences, a flooded yard with refuse, and a mysterious woman who hides from strangers) creates a sense of isolation and secrets.
  • The village itself appears ghost-like, with empty streets, wooden geese, and flickering lights, symbolizing a place suspended between life and death.

Chapter 12 · The Limits of Human Understanding and Divine Mystery

  • Vera reflects on the limitations of human knowledge, emphasizing that humans are not omniscient and should not try to tamper with God’s mysteries.
  • God is portrayed as remorseful over creation’s imperfections, but humans are advised to focus on their own lives rather than cosmic mysteries.

Chapter 12

  • “filled with the greatest remorse”: “And it repented the Lord that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him at his heart” (Genesis 6:6).

Chapter 19 · Faith and Morality

  • Her Christian beliefs, criticism of gambling, and chained Bible contrast with the ominous presence of the cards and other symbols of fate, indicating internal conflicts or tensions between belief and fate.

Chapter 20 · Spiritual and Existential Questions

  • The narrative moves toward existential and spiritual reflection, pondering God’s nature, the problem of creation, and the place of suffering and imperfection in the divine order.
  • Vera likens the “baldness” and loss to a profound experience, possibly greater than mere mortal wisdom or aging, invoking biblical and mythological imagery.
  • The chapter closes with a meditation on life failing before one’s eyes and the idea of seeing something no one “should see and live to relate.”

Chapter 21 · The Devil and Deception

  • The narrative includes reflections on evil’s many forms, warning against being deceived by charming appearances (“The Devil does not always wear horns”).
  • The Devil is described metaphorically as “God’s ape,” emphasizing the complexity of good and evil and the danger of superficial judgments.

Chapter 25

  • “who did not believe in Heaven except as a state of mind”: The concept of heaven “as a state of mind” originates from interpretations of Jesus’ teachings, particularly the phrase “the kingdom of God is within you” (Luke 17:21), which suggests that a state of peace, love and spiritual fulfillment can be achieved here on Earth through one’s mindset and actions, rather than being solely a place one goes to after death; this interpretation is often associated with mystical or spiritual traditions that emphasize inner consciousness and personal transformation. It captures a key existential and metaphysical perspective in the novel, emphasizing the internal, often ambiguous nature of spiritual experience over fixed external realities.
  • “his existence which she denied”: Proving existence through denial refers to a philosophical concept where the act of actively denying something’s existence can inadvertently reveal its presence, often used in arguments where the very act of trying to refute something implies its reality, particularly in discussions about abstract concepts like God or the nature of consciousness. In his Proslogion (1078), Anselm answers “the fool” who “hath said in his heart, There is no God” (Psalm 14:1): to deny God, the fool must first understand “that than which nothing greater can be conceived,” and such a being, Anselm argued, must exist in reality as well as in the mind. This concept can be misused to manipulate arguments by implying that simply denying a claim equates to its truth, which is a logical fallacy.

Chapter 25 · Faith and Doubt

  • Mr. Spitzer lacks faith in an afterlife, which contributes to his hesitant living.
  • His brother’s suicide suggests a belief in an afterlife or at least a finality that Mr. Spitzer cannot embrace.
  • The tension between hope and despair permeates the narrative.

Chapter 29 · The Mystery of Origin and Existence

  • The chapter speculates on the origins of butterflies and existence itself.
  • Questions arise about whether butterflies predate time and space or came from cosmic or otherworldly realms.
  • This reflects broader metaphysical inquiry into creation, existence, and the nature of the divine.

Chapter 29 · The Role of God and the Divine

  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates the nature of God, presenting a multifaceted and paradoxical image of divinity.
  • God is both light and darkness, creator and destroyer, and elusive beyond human comprehension.
  • The divine is intertwined with the symbolism of butterflies and the cosmic order.

Chapter 29 · Reflections on God and Creation

  • The multifaceted nature of God and the mystery of creation, mirrored in the symbolism of butterflies.

Chapter 30 · The Cosmic and the Mystical

  • Frequent cosmic imagery: stars, planets, moons, constellations, and celestial events.
  • Catherine’s dialogue with cosmic elements (stars, waves, tides) reflects a mystical quest for meaning beyond the earthly.

Chapter 44 · Mystery and the Human Heart

  • The suffragette is depicted as a repository of secrets and confidences, receiving the hidden sorrows and loves of others even as her own heart remains obscure.
  • The novel questions whether anyone can truly understand love, life, or ambition, suggesting these remain inscrutable mysteries.
  • The idea that knowing without experiencing, knowing love without loving, or death without dying, might be a deeper form of understanding is proposed.

Chapter 45 · Mysticism and Symbolism

  • The chapter is rich with symbolic imagery: mountains, clouds, snow, stars, fireflies, sea shells, the moon, and butterflies.
  • These symbols often blur the boundary between reality and hallucination, reflecting Mr. Spitzer’s and Cousin Hannah’s fractured consciousness.
  • The narrative suggests the possibility of existence beyond physical death, through memory, spirit, or cosmic forces.

Chapter 49 · The Cosmic and the Mystical

  • The cosmos, stars, and celestial imagery permeate the text, emphasizing vastness, mystery, and the unknowable.
  • Mr. Spitzer imagines his existence woven into universal patterns, yet feels lost and disoriented within them.
  • The narrative contemplates the idea of multiple dimensions, alternate realities, and the eternal recurrence of life and death.

Chapter 65 · Faith, Salvation, and Doubt

  • The chapter explores the tension between religious fervor, spiritual hope, and personal doubt.
  • Mr. Bonebreaker’s apocalyptic preaching contrasts with Miss MacIntosh’s pragmatic and skeptical attitude.
  • Her rejection of his God and his ultimate flight from her when confronted with her full reality illustrates this tension.

Chapter 69 · The Supernatural vs. Reality

  • The narrative blurs lines between reality and the supernatural with ghostly figures, haunted rooms, and symbolic presences.
  • The landlord’s tall tales and Mr. Weed’s shadow complicate the story’s realism.

Chapter 72 · Mystery and the Unseen

  • The unborn child, the stillborn babies, and the hidden layers of Esther’s life symbolize the mysteries of existence and creation.
  • References to shadows, ghosts, and invisible presences underscore this motif.

Chapter 74 · Mystery and Ambiguity

  • The narrative blurs reality and memory. Esther’s story is full of mysteries: the paternity of her children, the nature of her pregnancies, and the actions of the detective.
  • The repeated motif of “stillborn” children and the detective’s ambiguous role deepen this theme.

Chapter 75 · The Body and the Supernatural

  • Esther’s body is a site of suffering and mystery; her miscarriages are described in surreal terms (e.g., “mud baby,” “marble statue,” “ectoplasm”).
  • The dead babies return as ghostly figures, sometimes walking, sometimes lying in boats, symbolizing unresolved grief and the supernatural intrusion into her life.

Chapter 77 · Mystery and the Unknown

  • The nature of Esther’s pregnancies, the deaths, and the detective’s role create a pervasive sense of mystery.
  • The chapter explores the limits of understanding and the unknowable facets of human experience.

Chapter 80 · Life, Death, and the Supernatural

  • The narrative blurs boundaries between life and death, as Esther communicates with spirits and imagines an "other world" parallel to this one.
  • Her miscarriages and stillborn children are depicted as ghostly presences and delicate entities like moths, feathers, and snowflakes.
  • Walter exists both as a living child and a spectral presence haunting her conscience.
