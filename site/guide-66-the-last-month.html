Chapter 66: The Last Month

On Chapter 66 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of A Guide to Reading Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Research notes: Chapter 66

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Titus Bonebreaker · Old Friday · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Faith, God, and the Supernatural

Symbols: Clothing, Hats, and Skirts · Clouds and Storms

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Exploration and the Poles · Gambling and Playing Cards · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · Shakespeare · Suicide in History and Literature

We are in the final weeks. Miss MacIntosh is still alive, still talking, but everything she says now carries the weight of what Vera knows, looking back: that this is the last month they will have together, that the stories are being told because there won't be another chance to tell them. The chapter opens with Miss MacIntosh dressed for a long voyage she insists they are not going on, and it ends with her clothes strewn on the beach and her red wig torn in the sand.

Chapter 66 is the month, and the disappearance, and everything between.

What actually happens

Miss MacIntosh teaches Vera during a storm (geography, history, the heart, biology, socialism, finance) questions she fires out whether or not Vera can answer them, which Vera usually cannot. She tells Vera about her imaginary dealings on the Stock Exchange, which she calls "a palace of evil" she visits to level the rich. She tells her about the Arctic Explorer she met there. She tells her about her removed ovaries, her physical amputations, her rival love. She prepares Vera, in her eccentric and relentless way, for a world she is about to enter alone.

And then, one cold September morning, she walks into the ocean.

Miss MacIntosh in a storm

The image Young gives us at the chapter's opening is one of her great portraits: Miss MacIntosh in her whaler's hat and sea boots, black umbrella billowing, teaching Vera lessons by hurricane lamp while a storm batters the windows. She is dressed for a voyage she claims is not happening. She is prepared for an ocean she says she is not entering.

She asks questions she doesn't wait for answers to. She lectures on socialism while walking in the surf with her skirts pinned up. She decries the unequal distribution of wealth, the rich throwing dollar bills out of windows while the poor don't catch them, and she goes to the Stock Exchange to make imaginary money, she says, to level them all so they should be poor together. She invests in anthracite and tin but refuses to invest in zippers because in What Cheer, Iowa, zippers are against the will of God.

She is, as she has always been, magnificent and impossible and entirely herself.

The Arctic Explorer

At the Stock Exchange, that palace of evil she visits with her imaginary portfolio, Miss MacIntosh meets a wooden-legged explorer from Denmark, born near Elsinore Castle (yes, Hamlet's castle, and yes, Young knows exactly what she is doing with that detail). He has swept his broom, as he says, through all the great squares of the world: Trafalgar, the Kremlin, St. Mark's. He has seen things in the Arctic that have stripped him of whatever ordinary life he might have had.

He asks Miss MacIntosh, repeatedly, to come with him to the North Pole. He wants to leave everything behind (the Stock Exchange, the cities, all of it) and go north into the silence and the ice, where, he says, there is nothing unknown because nothing is known, and therefore nothing to fear.

She is drawn to him. They have “something in common—their differences from others,” their amputations (his leg, her breast and baldness) and their shared “sense of having killed something young and helpless” in themselves somewhere along the way. He reminds her of her father, who also lost a leg. Young does not make this explicit as psychology; she offers it plainly, as one of the facts of Miss MacIntosh's interior life.

But she doesn't go north with him. She has Vera to attend to. She has her imaginary stocks. She has her socialism and her umbrella and her What Cheer prejudices against zippers. She stays.

"That old religion"

Miss MacIntosh reflects on Mr. Bonebreaker in this chapter with the clarity of retrospect: he belonged to "that old religion where women must kneel before men." She couldn't kneel. She was bald and had no breast and had nothing of what that religion required of women: no seductive hair to wash a man's feet with, no performed femininity, no willingness to submit. She rejected this not defiantly but practically: it simply was not available to her, and she was not sorry.

Young makes here one of the novel's most direct feminist arguments, and she makes it through Miss MacIntosh's characteristic combination of practicality and dark humor. The "old religion" is patriarchy (the system that entitles men to women's submission, bodies, and labor) and Miss MacIntosh has spent her whole life outside it, not by choice exactly, but by condition. Her baldness, her missing breast, her removed ovaries made her unintelligible to the system. And so she went her own way.

Her hopes for the world

Among the things Miss MacIntosh tells Vera she hopes for before she dies: an end to the British Empire, an end to colonialism, the King of England losing his crown, the Tong wars ending, China the Sleeping Dragon awakening, the world reformed so that by the time Vera grows up there will be no rich people. She teaches Vera "the beauty of deprivation." She hopes for simple humanity.

These are not small hopes. They are the hopes of a woman who has lived entirely outside privilege and power, who has seen what wealth does and what empire does and what patriarchy does, and who has concluded that the structure needs to come down. She has no illusions that it will. But she teaches Vera to see it clearly, because seeing clearly is what she has always done.

The amputations she carries

In this chapter we learn the full accounting of what Miss MacIntosh's body has lost: her hair, her breast, her ovaries. She carries these losses the way she carries everything, without sentimentality, with full acknowledgment. She mentions "a rival love": suggesting her romantic life was more complicated than even Chapter 65's story of Mr. Bonebreaker conveyed. She does not elaborate. She moves on.

What she says instead is this: her death, when it comes, will be a natural death. Not a suicide; she is not like the gamblers on the Stock Exchange, she says, frenzied by losses, jumping from high windows after the crash of 1929. She will just go. Quietly, without forwarding address, into the sea.

The clothes on the beach

Young gives us the disappearance with devastating simplicity. The clothes strewn on the beach. The red wig torn in the sand. The waves returning with everything but her. Old Friday, the dog, barking by the sea: still barking, afterward, as if she might be “a moving shadow, a shadow of a black umbrella moving over water and sand—as if perhaps she had never gone or had returned.”

Vera asks: had the dog “not known the denudation and the dream, the reality and the illusion, how each must live by some pretense?” Old Friday barks at the waves. The waves come back swollen with wind, “like sheep with a thousand bells.” She is not in them.

She walked in. She did not come back. That is all.

What Vera understood, long years after

This chapter is told in retrospect: Vera looking back from long years after the disappearance, seeing the lessons in a new light. As a child, she took Miss MacIntosh's eccentricities for granted. The whaler's hat during arithmetic. The umbrella in the surf. The imaginary stocks. She absorbed all of it without fully understanding it, as children do: taking on every color except her own, she says, because she had no color yet to claim.

Now she understands. The lessons were not orthodox. The questions were not about answers. The month of stories was a bequest: Miss MacIntosh giving away everything she knew before she went into the water. She was making her will, the way Mr. Spitzer made his will with his music: leaving what she had to someone who might receive it, even if reception was uncertain.

What to hold onto

Old Friday, at the edge of the sea, barking. The red wig in the sand. The waves coming back without her. A dog who perceives something the living cannot quite see (a moving shadow, a black umbrella's shadow over water) and who refuses to stop barking at it.

She kept the rain out for as long as she could. Then she walked into the rain.
