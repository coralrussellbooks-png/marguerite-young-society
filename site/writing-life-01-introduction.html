Radical Immanence Across Marguerite Young's Writing Life

Introduction

Radical Immanence Across Marguerite Young's Writing Life · Chapter One: Not One Self →

Marguerite Young said she spent her entire career writing about one thing her entire life. She understood something philosophy has been circling for centuries without quite saying: the self is not something a person finds. It is something a person keeps surviving. The philosophy she was developing carries across Young’s childhood poems, gained a method in her graduate years, was argued outright in her verse, turned toward history and community in her prose, and was still changing in the unfinished book she left at her death. This study follows that philosophy. Through years of studying her work I called it Radical Immanence before I knew the history of the term. Where she fits historically with the term and the philosophy around it has been covered on Substack here. This book asks whether Young can fairly be credited with developing it through her writing.

Young was filed under the wrong category. Not maliciously, not carelessly, but wrongly in a way that has cost us something. We call her a novelist, an eccentric, a maximalist, a writer’s writer, a forgotten genius waiting to be rediscovered. All of that is true and none of it is the point. The point is that Young built a philosophical system, and she built it in poetry, fiction, and history because she was honest enough to know that the system could not survive in any other form. A treatise on the impossibility of stable selfhood written in stable expository prose would contradict itself before the second paragraph.

Philosophy, as it has been practiced in the Western tradition, depends on formal commitments that Young’s system cannot make without self-betrayal. It depends on the resolvable argument, the claim that can be stated, supported, and concluded. It depends on the stable authorial voice, the consistent “I” who stands behind the claim. And it depends on the assumption that reality yields to systematic description, that if the right method is applied rigorously enough, the truth will hold still long enough to be captured. Young refuses all three. Resolution is the lie her system is built to expose. A stable voice performing a theory of plural selfhood undermines the theory. And reality, for Young, is precisely the kind of thing that keeps becoming something else while it is being described.

Why the Tradition Missed Her

The Western philosophical tradition handles thinkers who do not fit its categories in three ways. It absorbs them, keeping the parts that translate into acceptable philosophical language and calling the rest style. It files them, assigning the work to a discipline that can contain it without being challenged by it: literature, mysticism, women’s writing, experimental fiction. Or it ignores them, which is easier than it sounds when Young’s central work is a 1,198 pages long novel that refuses to summarize. Young got all three.

She fits the profile of the missed thinker with uncomfortable precision. She was a woman. Her work was long in a way that read as excess rather than ambition, because excess and ambition are distributed differently by gender in every critical tradition we have. Its texture was emotional, full of grief and longing and interior weather, and emotional texture has historically moved work out of the philosophy section regardless of the rigor underneath. And it refused the payoff.

Philosophy advances through contestation, through claims that can be refuted and refined. Young’s novel does not say the self is plural. It enacts plurality in a way that makes the reader experience the argument rather than evaluate it. That is a more honest move for this particular argument, but it is not one the tradition knows how to answer. This is not Young’s failure. It is the tradition’s limitation.

There is also the question of timing. Young’s novel appeared in 1965, one year before Foucault’s Les Mots et les Choses was published in French and two years before Derrida’s Of Grammatology. The conversation best equipped to receive her, the poststructuralist dismantling of the unified subject and the attention to how form carries argument, was only beginning in Europe and had not yet reached American intellectual culture with any force. She was not ahead of her time in the romantic sense. She was genuinely prior to the moment that would have known what to do with her. By the time that moment arrived, she had been filed, and filed things do not get reconsidered easily. Again this is covered in depth on the Substack here.

The Name and the Test

Radical Immanence is a later name. Young never used it, and she never set out her ideas as a system. Steven Shaviro comes closest when he calls her vision “in no way transcendent or otherworldly,” a celebration of something “available to us, at every moment, in ordinary life.” To read Young’s novel alone is to see the philosophy at its fullest. To credit Young with developing it requires more: a philosophy that is developed, rather than merely present, should show a history, with early forms, later refinements, turns, and returns.

The evidence comes from the whole of Young’s record: interviews and remembered conversations, the published criticism, the essays, stories, and reviews in "Inviting the Muses", and her books "Miss MacIntosh, My Darling", "Angel in the Forest", "Harp Song for a Radical", and her "Collected Poems". Each source has been weighed on its own against the eleven pillars of Radical Immanence and then placed in order of composition and publication.

The work falls into seven phases

Phase One covers the childhood and early poems, from 1908 to 1934, where the philosophy appears as feeling.

Phase Two covers the Chicago years, the graduate essays, and Prismatic Ground, from 1935 to 1937, where it gains a method in William James, a founding act of witness beside Minna Weissenbach, and its first prose statements.

Phase Three covers the philosophical poems of 1938 to 1945, where Young argues the pillars most directly.

Phase Four covers her book "Angel in the Forest" and “Complex Life and Complex Letters,” from 1945 to 1946, where the philosophy turns toward history and community.

Phase Five covers "Miss MacIntosh, My Darling", written over about eighteen years and published in 1965, where the philosophy is lived by characters and reaches its fullest form.

Phase Six covers the occasional prose, the interviews, and Young’s readings of her own work, where she both confirms the philosophy and speaks against parts of it.

Phase Seven covers "Harp Song for a Radical", written in her last decades and published in 1999, where the philosophy turns fully to labor, power, and memory.

Some cautions run through every phase

Writing dates and publication dates often differ. Most of the essays in "Inviting the Muses" are undated, and the interviews come mostly after 1965. The appendix of drafts in "The Collected Poems" holds work Young never chose to publish. "Harp Song for a Radical" was arranged by its editors after her death. Where dates are uncertain, work is placed by period rather than by year.

The following has eleven chapters that cover each pillar of Radical Immanence with analysis of the seven phases of Young’s work. Each phase takes the eleven pillars in turn, gives the evidence that supports each and, where the record holds it, the evidence that complicates it. Complications are given equal weight. A philosophy credited to a writer should be found in what she wrote, including the places where she argued against it. That, too, is faithful to her. A system built on the refusal of false wholeness should not be presented as more finished than it was.

Below is a summary of the context around each phase and what the phase adds to the whole.

Philosophers get named for their positions.

Young’s position is Radical Immanence.

Phase One, Childhood and the Early Poems (1908 to 1934)

Context

Marguerite Young was born in Indiana on August 26, 1908. The philosophy begins before any published work. In her family's telling, she was the reincarnation of her dead cousin Harry, whom her grandmother believed "had been born again in me," and in her own later account "the child becomes a vehicle for the grandmother." She said her first poem, written at five, was about loss, and she offered a poem written at six as proof that the whole of her work lay in her from childhood. Near eighty, she could still recite from memory "I Heard a Bird," written at nine, and dictated it for her collected poems.

Her first published poems appeared in college literary magazines from 1927 to 1930, and "Jonathan's House" won first prize in the Butler Literary Contest in 1929. From 1930 to 1934 she published in Poetry: "Recurring," "Ballad-Loving Woman," "Lot's Wife," and "A Girl's Song" in 1930, "Biography" in 1932, and "Minnows and a Monster" and "Woodlands Far Removed" in 1934. Several of these were later collected in Prismatic Ground (1937), but they were written and first published in this phase. The forms are conventional: sonnets, ballads, triolets, refrain songs. The subjects are local: old women sweeping leaves, a farmer with postcards, a peddler woman with a wagon, a widow in a stone house. Here the philosophy shows up as feeling, not argument.

What This Phase Contributes

Phase One gives the philosophy its emotional ground. Grief that does not end, the self both merged and alone, the fragile thing built anyway, and God found in the world are all present by the time Young was twenty-six. Her later claim that "everything is prophetic in writing" holds for these poems. What is missing is argument, politics, and a sacred that is free of theism. Those come later.

Phase Two, The Chicago Years, Prismatic Ground, and the Graduate Essays (1935 to 1937)

Context

Young took her MA in early modern English literature at the University of Chicago in 1936. These are the years in which she sat with Minna Weissenbach. By her graduate years she was reading William James, whose Psychology she studied "as if it were a Bible." Her academic essays come from her graduate study, though the record does not give their exact dates: "Coventry Patmore's Theory of Knowledge" and "The Theistic Mysticism of Young's Night Thoughts." Her poems appeared steadily: eight in Poetry in September 1935, others in Comment in 1935 and 1936, five more in Poetry in March 1937, and "Daniel" in The New Republic in August 1937. Prismatic Ground, her first book, came out in 1937.

The poems of this phase are denser and stranger than the early ones. Angels fall into clover, a man turns into an orchard, frogs watch a girl in the dark. This is the phase in which the sacred moves decisively into the world.

What This Phase Contributes

Phase Two gives the philosophy its method, its founding experience, and its first prose statements. James supplies the plural self and the wild data. Minna supplies the witness. The graduate essays supply three sentences that state Pillars 2, 9, and 10 almost outright. The poems move the sacred from God-in-nature to sainthood-as-rootedness and heaven-as-clover. The complications also take shape: witnessing that comes too late, testimony that fails, and the racial stereotypes that would run through Young's later work.

Phase Three, The Philosophical Poems (1938 to 1945)

Context

These are the war years, and the years in which Young argued her philosophy most directly. She published in the leading literary journals: "Ventriloquist: The Coffee Hour" in Poetry (1940); "Death by Rarity" and "The Long War" in The Kenyon Review (1940); "The Funeral," "Snow in the Mountains," "Slow Motion," "The Clinic," and "At the Cinema" in New Directions (1941); "Guide to New Harmony, Indiana" in The Saturday Review (1941); "A Crystal Principle" and "Moderate Fable" in The Kenyon Review (1942); others in The Southern Review, Accent, and American Prefaces. Moderate Fable (1944), dedicated to the memory of Minna Weissenbach, was named poetry book of the year by the National Academy of Arts and Letters, according to her editors. The late poems appeared in 1945: "Of Deed, Love, Death" in The Kenyon Review, "Proem to Reality" in Partisan Review, and "The Contravalid" and "Elegy" in Accent. The unpublished drafts in the Beinecke papers come from the same years.

At the same time Young was writing "Angel in the Forest", first as lyric ballads. "Guide to New Harmony, Indiana" (1941) survives from that version. The poems argue with Berkeley, Descartes, Aquinas, Heraclitus, Calvin, Plato, and Pythagoras. The vocabulary turns technical: "negative magnitude," "null class," "the if-then relation," "schemata," "specious present." The war comes in as parachutists, airmen, submarines, and walrus herds.

What This Phase Contributes

Phase Three is the philosophy argued. Every pillar gets either a formal statement or an exact image within these eight years, and several get their clearest statement anywhere in Young's work: the flaw, the contravalid, the wound of absence, God as creation's flaw. The war turns witness and grief toward the state, the laboratory, and extinction. The phase also holds the strongest counterarguments: the past as nothing, the self's division as terror, and the soul as exile from elsewhere. In 1945 Young left the lyric, and the arguments passed into prose.

Phase Four, "Angel in the Forest" and “Complex Life and Complex Letters” (1945 to 1946)

Context

"Angel in the Forest" began as about sixty lyric ballads, became "a long pseudo-Miltonic blank verse poem," and became prose only when Young found verse "inadequate for the inclusion of facts and figures." She said she "stumbled into prose almost reluctantly." The book is a history of two utopias at New Harmony, Indiana, the Rappites and the Owenites, set beside the town in 1940. It was published in 1945 and praised by Mark Van Doren and Robert Penn Warren. What first drew Young to New Harmony, by her own account, was "a kind of group of Steinbeck characters living in tents at the outskirts," the stragglers "who sat at the gates of both utopias and never joined either one," and a band of wandering Ozark coal miners. In 1945 she was also, by her own account, a pacifist. "Complex Life and Complex Letters" (1946) followed, an argument about the nature of reality in the form of a literary manifesto.

The change of form is a change in the philosophy. The ideas argued in the poems move into history, community, and economics.

What This Phase Contributes

Phase Four turns the philosophy outward. Pillars 2, 4, 5, 8, and 9 gain a social and political dimension: who keeps the shared illusion, who witnesses and for whom, who builds and who is sacrificed, whose bones lie under whose graves, who controls the words. The tone is drier and more comic than the poems. The book holds both sides of every question, utopia and doubt, perfection and flaw, and the one sure thing it reaches is the miller's thumbprint.

Phase Five, "Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" (written from the mid 1940s, published 1965)

Context

Young began the novel soon after "Angel in the Forest" and worked on it for about eighteen years. It was published in 1965, nearly twelve hundred pages long. Her accounts of its making bear on the philosophy. She said she "knew the last scene the day I started," that she was "controlled by intellectual ideas" and not automatic writing, and that she listened "until I heard a kind of inner music." The book "took on its cosmic magnitude" only when she rewrote Mr. Spitzer so that he does not know whether he is himself or his dead twin. She folded the unwritable flour-miller story into every manuscript. She built the book from monologues, "one character at a time," and refused the walk-on butler, since every minor figure must move "in an orbit of his own dream." In these years she also wrote to President Kennedy about atomic testing. The novel draws the strongest critical readings of her philosophy, including Petrolle's "magical metaphysic" and Shaviro's "pluralistic ontology."

The novel holds the philosophy at full strength. What does the novel add to the work that came before it? For each pillar the question is what changed over eighteen years of composition, which pillars first become fully lived here.

What the Novel Adds

Phase Five is the synthesis. The philosophy argued in the poems and set in history in "Angel in the Forest" is lived by characters across 1,198 pages. Measured against the earlier work, the novel adds four things. It turns the pillars from statement into character, since every minor figure moves “in an orbit of his own dream.” It makes the keeper of the shared fiction a beloved person, so that discovery and grief arrive together. It brings race to the center of witness and memory: the white detective, the lynching rope, and a narrator who names her own failure to see.

Phase Six, The Occasional Prose, Interviews, and Self-Interpretation (1940s to 1995)

Context

This phase is defined less by dates than by kind. "Inviting the Muses" (1994) gathers essays, stories, and reviews written across decades, and the record does not date most of them. The interviews, with Studs Terkel, Susan Neville, LaVerne Harrell Clark, Charles Ruas, and others, come mostly after the novel appeared in 1965. Young taught for decades, at the New School, Fordham, and elsewhere, and told students to read James's Psychology as a writer's central text. In 1989 she wrote the introduction to her collected poems. In this phase Young explains her philosophy in her own voice, and also speaks against parts of it.

What This Phase Contributes

Phase Six is Young interpreting herself. She confirms the pillars in her own sentences and extends Pillars Seven and Nine to deafness and life without language. It is also the phase of her clearest dissents: the steady self, the wish for permanence, the voice from nowhere, the full vocabulary of angels and resurrection. Her habit of holding opposite positions at once is most visible here, in her own voice.

Phase Seven, "Harp Song for a Radical" (written in her last decades, published 1999)

Context

After the novel, Young turned to Eugene V. Debs. In 1989 she described "the presently forthcoming volumes of the Debs trilogy," along with a biography of James Whitcomb Riley and novellas. She wrote that Dostoyevsky and Tolstoy influenced "all my mature work and particularly that of the present," and recalled that Debs spoke of "the Lazarus flowers of Socialism." Ruas reports that she saw the Vietnam protests as "a modern-day recapitulation" of Debs's issues, and that she wanted "tremendously big archetypal figures" because history "is directed by the individual." She called the work a "transcendental socialism." She was writing the Pullman strike chapter when she fell ill. Her drafts "became mixed together" during a building renovation, and she died on November 17, 1995. Charles Ruas prepared the text, and "Harp Song for a Radical" appeared in 1999. Its prose is hers, and its arrangement is partly the editors'.

What This Phase Contributes

Phase Seven gives the philosophy its political and historical body. The illusion becomes capitalism, the witness becomes the record-keeper of the working dead, the past becomes Sand Creek and Ludlow, the word becomes "red," and the sacred becomes the spark in the dead souls of labor. The crossing point gets its strongest image, and transmission finally succeeds. The complications are great-man history, narrowed sympathy for some women, and an incompletion that Young did not choose. The book's last words fit Debs and fit her too. The elegiac sense continues "perhaps long afterward. As will be seen."
