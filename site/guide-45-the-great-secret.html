Chapter 45: The Great Secret

Research notes: Chapter 45

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Esther Longtree · Gertrude Hathaway Tidings · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Love

Symbols: Roses and Flowers

Philosophy: Kant and Hegel · Marguerite Young on Her Craft · Nietzsche

Allusions: American Religious Movements · Music and Composers · Women's Suffrage

The bus is still moving. Vera is still on it. The novel's wheel keeps turning. But you are in new territory now, and Chapter 45 opens it with one of the volume's central revelations hanging just out of reach, the way all of Young's revelations hang: present, half-stated, trembling on the edge of being said.

Mr. Spitzer has a secret. He has had it since the deathbed, since the papers, the letters, the unintelligible languages, the evidence of the double life. He has vowed not to tell it until long after Cousin Hannah is gone. Chapter 45 keeps that vow while circling the secret so closely that its shape becomes visible even through the silence.

The shape is this: the great public captain of suffrage, the woman who had no time for love and devoted everything to the cause, was haunted her entire life by a love she never fully had. And the question the chapter turns over, slowly and from every angle, is whether the cause and the grief were separate things, or whether they were the same thing, the grief finding the only form available to it, the love becoming the movement because it could not become the person.

What actually happens in Chapter 45

We remain in the long aftermath of Cousin Hannah's death, still in Mr. Spitzer's consciousness, still sorting through the papers and the memories and the last words. The deathbed has ended but it hasn't ended; it echoes forward into this chapter the way all of Cousin Hannah's chapters have echoed, each one unable to contain her completely, each one requiring another.

Mr. Spitzer contemplates what he calls her double life, perhaps even a triple life. The public figure and the private person were so thoroughly separated that he, who was her lawyer and her witness, cannot reconcile them into a single coherent portrait. He knows what she did. He is beginning to understand what she felt. The two do not map onto each other cleanly.

Cousin Hannah's dying words continue to surface: the cry for her lost love, the accusation of betrayal (of herself, by herself, the false modesty), the question (was I always falling?) that sounds like despair but might be the most honest accounting she ever gave of what it felt like to reach as high as she reached. “I aspired to greater heights than most, so I fell a greater distance.”

Mr. Spitzer reflects on fame and oblivion. He considers what will be written on her grave. Twelve miles, he thinks: a distance that is both literal and symbolic, a measure of how far she traveled and how far she still was from where she meant to arrive. He meditates on pigeons as symbols of memory and the soul, on the way public memory reduces even the greatest lives to monuments, losing the secret heart.

If one knew all hearts, one should know none

This is Mr. Spitzer's formulation, and it is one of the chapter's philosophical centers: the person who has heard every confession, who has been the keeper of everyone's secrets, who knows the hidden sorrow and the private love of every person she encountered, that person, paradoxically, knows no one. Because knowledge at that scale becomes abstraction. The individual disappears into the pattern.

Cousin Hannah was the confidante of many. She received whispered loves and private griefs and kept them. She knew more about human feeling than most people accumulate in a lifetime. And she herself, behind all that knowing, remained unknown: to Mr. Spitzer, to Catherine, possibly to herself.

Young is working through a genuine epistemological problem here: what is the relationship between breadth of knowledge and depth of experience? Can you understand love by witnessing it in a thousand people, or does understanding require the particular, the singular, the one? Cousin Hannah had the breadth. What she may have lacked was the particular singular love that would have made everything else legible from the inside.

Love infinitely postponed

“Shall love be infinitely postponed? If infinitely postponed, then it is love.”

This is one of Young's most compressed and exact formulations, and it arrives in Chapter 45 as a kind of philosophical coda to everything that has happened in the deathbed chapters. The idea, drawn from Hegel's logic of negation, is that the very act of deferral defines the thing deferred. A love that is kept waiting, kept just out of reach, kept perpetually on the verge of being claimed: that love acquires a particular intensity from the waiting. It becomes absolute precisely because it was never made conditional on reality.

Catherine's love for Peron, kept alive by his silence and her interpretation of it. Cousin Hannah's love, killed by false modesty, surviving its own death as a wound that never closed. Mr. Spitzer's love, if he has one, conducted entirely in the second remove. The novel is full of loves that were infinitely postponed. And Young asks, through Mr. Spitzer's meditation: what if that is what love actually is? What if the loves that got lived out, that arrived and were received and settled into daily life: what if those became something else, something valuable but no longer love in the absolute sense? What if love requires the infinite postponement to remain itself?

The novel does not endorse this as a prescription for how to live. But it takes it seriously as a description of what happens to certain kinds of people: people like Catherine, like Cousin Hannah, like everyone in this household who loved most fiercely what they could not have.

He was become his brother

To All My Darlings pauses over a line Young drops almost in passing: he was become his brother.

This is potentially the novel's largest bomb, buried in subordinate syntax. Mr. Spitzer (Joachim Spitzer, the surviving twin, the lawyer, the cautious and conservative keeper of dignity) may not be Joachim at all. The line hints that he is Peron. That the twin who survived is not the one we were told survived. That the man Catherine refused to love, the man who spent decades in this household waiting to be seen as a unique individual, was the very man she thought was dead, and she never knew it, and he could not tell her, and so he waited and was not seen and became, over the years, his brother, which is to say became no one.

Young will not confirm this explicitly. She is not that kind of novelist. But the possibility, once planted, reshapes everything that came before. Every time Mr. Spitzer faded into the background of Catherine's dreams, every time she looked past him toward the dead, if he is Peron, she was looking past him toward himself. The grief and the love were in the same room the whole time, wearing each other's clothes.

Susan B. Anthony and the model behind the captain

Chapter 45 surfaces what To All My Darlings makes explicit: Young may have modeled Cousin Hannah in part on Susan B. Anthony and her sister Hannah.

The parallels are striking. Anthony devoted her life to suffrage with the totality Cousin Hannah displays. She never married: journalists asked her repeatedly why, and her answers shifted depending on the day: the men she wanted she could not have; she never found the man necessary to her happiness; she could not give up her freedom to become a man's housekeeper. Anthony's religion followed a trajectory similar to Hannah's: Quaker roots, Unitarian drift, eventual agnosticism, the refusal of a God made happy by submission. Work and worship are one with me, Anthony said. I can not imagine a God of the universe made happy by my getting down on my knees.

When Anthony's sister Hannah was dying, she asked Susan to speak about the great beyond. Anthony later wrote that she could not dash her faith with my doubts, nor could I pretend a faith I had not; so I was silent in the dread presence of death. Mr. Spitzer at Cousin Hannah's deathbed, keeping the great secret, being silent in the dread presence of death: the echo is exact. The silence is the same silence. Young knew her Anthony, and she built her captain from that knowledge.

Anthony predicted, in an 1877 speech, an epoch of single women: that women who would not accept marriage with subjugation, and men who would not proffer it without, would have no alternative but to live without it. Cousin Hannah is one of those women. She lived without it. She died calling for the thing she lived without. The epoch Anthony predicted, and the cost it exacted on the women who inhabited it first, is what Young is elegizing in these chapters.

She knew her Carthage

Young's phrase, she knew her Carthage, is dense with meaning and deserves unpacking.

Carthage was founded, in legend, by Dido: the queen who built a city from nothing, who led her people across the sea, who created something magnificent and enduring. In Virgil's Aeneid, Dido also loved Aeneas, the Trojan hero who stayed in Carthage and then left because the gods required him to found Rome. She died for love of him, by her own hand, after he sailed away. The city she built survived her. Her love did not.

To say Cousin Hannah knew her Carthage is to say she knew what it was to build something great and lose what mattered most. She built the suffrage movement with the same energy Dido built her city: relentlessly, magnificently, against every resistance. And she lost, somewhere along the way, the love that might have sustained her privately. She knew the shape of that loss. She had lived inside it her whole life.

The dwarf with the rose of consciousness

“Who is that dwarf, a turret on his head, a rose at his collar? It is the rose of consciousness.”

Young's image here draws on Nietzsche's Zarathustra, where the dwarf (the spirit of gravity, of self-doubt, of conventional thought) is the obstacle that must be overcome before the highest truths can be faced. Zarathustra confronts the dwarf at the gateway moment of eternal recurrence: the place where all time meets, where past and future coil around the present instant. The dwarf represents everything that makes a person shrink from the full size of their life.

But Young gives the dwarf the rose of consciousness. This is not Nietzsche's dwarf exactly; it is Young's revision of him. In her version, even the spirit of gravity, even the self-doubt and the limitation, carries the rose: carries awareness, carries the capacity to feel and perceive, carries the knowledge of what is being missed. The dwarf knows what the hero knows. He simply cannot act on it. This is Mr. Spitzer. This is the man who has been watching from the second remove, who has all the knowledge and none of the mobility, who carries the rose and cannot place it anywhere it might bloom.

What to hold onto

Chapter 45 is a chapter of aftermath and accumulation: the settling of everything the deathbed chapters stirred up, and the first hints of what Volume 2 will do with it. The secret Mr. Spitzer holds is the chapter's center of gravity, and you do not need to know it to feel its weight. You feel it in every passage where he circles and does not land, every reflection where he approaches and retreats.

What to carry forward: love infinitely postponed as the novel's governing paradox. The possibility that Mr. Spitzer is Peron: that the living man and the dead man have been the same man all along, and Catherine never saw it. The Susan B. Anthony parallel, which roots Cousin Hannah in a specific and real historical tradition of women who gave everything to a cause and died without the private life they secretly wanted. And the rose of consciousness on the dwarf's collar: the awareness that cannot act, that watches and knows and cannot change what it sees.

The bus is still moving. Volume 2 has just begun.
