Chapter 6

  • “You think that beauty is as beauty does”: The proverb says a person’s true beauty is in their character and actions, not their physical appearance. The older proverb is “handsome is as handsome does” (1500s). The sentiment is found in the writings of Geoffrey Chaucer in “The Wife of Bath’s Tale” (c. 1387) from The Canterbury Tales. Chaucer’s lines suggest that the most virtuous person, regardless of physical appearance, is the most beautiful. The Greek philosophers Plato and Aristotle linked beauty to virtue, order, and symmetry. This perspective influenced the development of the proverb, suggesting that inner qualities are just as important as outward appearance in determining beauty.

Chapter 8

  • “that dream within the dream”: This may refer to Edgar Allan Poe’s poem “A Dream within a Dream” (1849), which says, “All that we see or seem / Is but a dream within a dream.” This dream within a dream is a recurring theme throughout the book. The idea that life is a dream goes back to Plato, Aristotle, and Descartes. The first stanza ends with that statement, and the last stanza turns it into a question: “Is all that we see or seem / But a dream within a dream?” This symbolizes the recursive, elusive quality of existence in the novel: a reality constantly refracted through multiple dreamlike states that challenge the notion of a fixed, singular truth.

Chapter 9

  • “Early to bed and early to rise makes a man healthy, wealthy and wise”: The proverb praises rising before most others, early and consistently, so as to accomplish more in the day. This method has been recommended since antiquity. The philosopher Aristotle said a phrase very similar to the one Miss MacIntosh used. Within the context of religious observances, spiritual writers have called this practice the heroic minute, referring to the sacrifice which this entails. Benjamin Franklin printed the exact phrase Miss MacIntosh used in Poor Richard’s Almanack (1735). It is a saying that is viewed as a commonsensical proverb.

Chapter 14

  • “the music of the spheres, the silent music”: Boethius was a Roman senator, consul, magister officiorum, polymath, historian, and philosopher of the Early Middle Ages. He sought to reconcile the teachings of Plato and Aristotle with Christian theology. He broke logic into three parts: that which defines, that which divides, and that which deduces. There are three types of arguments: those of necessity, of ready believability, and sophistry. In De Musica there are three classifications: Musica mundana, Musica humana, Musica instrumentalis. In Dante’s Divine Comedy, the spirit of Boethius is pointed out by Saint Thomas Aquinas and is mentioned further in the poem. In his book De Institutione Musica (Fundamentals of Music) his drawings appear striking and may be an inspiration for the organization of Young’s novel. Musica universalis or music of the spheres or harmony of the spheres is a Greek philosophical concept founded by Pythagoras. This metaphysical principle stated that mathematical relationships could produce ‘tones’ of energy that could produce, among other things, sounds. Pythagoras proposed that the celestial bodies all have their own unique hum based on their orbital revolution. This idea influenced many scholars until the end of the Renaissance. Johannes Kepler, a German astronomer from the 1600s wrote extensively on the subject. Later astronomy set it aside. Boethius wrote The Consolation of Philosophy in prison awaiting execution, with its Wheel of Fortune.
  • “her peacocks”: Aristotle referred to the peacock’s vain reputation. The Roman poet Ennius, following the Pythagorean teaching of rebirth, dreamed that Homer’s soul had passed into a peacock and then into himself. The peacock symbolizes the dazzling but ephemeral nature of beauty and social display, the multiplicity and fragmentation of perception, and the tension between illusion and reality that the characters navigate.

Chapter 15

  • “one swallow does not make a summer”: Aristotle uses the saying in the Nicomachean Ethics (1.7) to discuss happiness and eudaimonia (living well or flourishing). He argues that a brief period of happiness or a single good deed doesn’t make a person truly happy or blessed. Happiness, for Aristotle, is a state of being that develops over a lifetime through virtuous activity.

Chapter 23

  • “What was so rare a thing as common sense”: Thomas Paine’s polemical pamphlet Common Sense (1776) has been described as the most influential political pamphlet of the 18th century, affecting both the American and French revolutions. Today, the concept of common sense, and how it should best be used, remains linked to many of the most perennial topics in epistemology and ethics, with special focus often directed at the philosophy of the modern social sciences.

Chapter 29

  • The philosophical exploration of happiness and sorrow are intertwined, with sorrow often seen as a necessary component for experiencing the depth of joy and vice versa rather than as simple opposites. It can be viewed in a number of ways: a dialectical relationship (yin and yang-complementary forces where one cannot exist without the other); the contrast highlights the value of positive experiences; a catalyst for growth, introspection, and a deeper understanding of life’s complexities. Philosophical perspectives are found in Buddhism, Christianity, Hinduism, Stoicism, Utilitarianism, Hedonism, Life Satisfaction Theory. Plato and Aristotle’s eudaimonia where flourishing and well-being are achieved by living a virtuous life, not simply experiencing happiness. Nirvana is a state of liberation from suffering and the cycle of rebirth. Equanimity is the ability to remain calm and balanced in the face of both pleasure and pain. The philosophical exploration of happiness and sorrow is presented through the reflections of Mr. Spitzer, who contemplates the ambiguous and intertwined nature of these emotions. The chapter discusses how happiness and sorrow seldom occur in pure, disassociated states; rather, happiness is often tinged with sorrow due to the fear of its temporality, and sorrow is tinged with happiness from the knowledge that it may endure. Mr. Spitzer observes the delicate distinctions between butterflies and moths, using them as metaphors for the subtle and elusive qualities of emotions like happiness and sorrow. Butterflies, associated with daytime and vibrant colors, contrast with moths, which fly by night and evoke muted and exaggerated perceptions. This imagery reflects the nuanced interplay of light and darkness, joy and pain, in human experience. The chapter touches on the fleeting and ephemeral nature of life and emotions, highlighting that moments of happiness are brief and often overshadowed by sorrow. Mr. Spitzer’s meditations suggest that both happiness and sorrow are transient, yet deeply interconnected aspects of existence that shape the human condition. It philosophically portrays happiness and sorrow not as opposites but as closely related states, each containing elements of the other, and both integral to the complexity of life’s experiences. This exploration is woven into the natural and symbolic imagery of butterflies, moths, and the rhythms of day and night, emphasizing the fluid and often paradoxical nature of these emotions.

Chapter 29

  • The soul is the purported immaterial aspect or essence of a living being. It is typically believed to be immortal and to exist apart from the material world. The three main theories that describe the relationship between the soul and the body are interactionism, parallelism, and epiphenomenalism. Anthropologists and psychologists have found that most humans are naturally inclined to believe in the existence of the soul and that they have interculturally distinguished between souls and bodies. In philosophy, the soul has been the central area of interest since ancient times. Socrates envisioned the soul to possess a rational faculty, its practice being man's most godlike activity. Plato believed the soul to be the person's real self, an immaterial and immortal dweller of our lives that continues and thinks even after death. Aristotle sketched out the soul as the "first actuality" of a naturally organized body: form and matter arrangement allowing natural beings to aspire to full actualization. The soul holds profound significance as a complex and elusive entity intertwined with themes of identity, mortality, and the metaphysical boundaries between life and death. Vera, through Mr. Spitzer’s reflections, presents the soul not as a singular, fixed entity but as something multifaceted and possibly plural. The soul is described as comprising body, soul, and spirit, each with distinct but interconnected roles, and these components may exist simultaneously or sequentially, blurring the lines between life and death, self and other. This ambiguity reflects the novel’s broader theme of fragmented identity and the elusive nature of selfhood. The soul is portrayed as engaging in a continuous interplay with life and death. Mr. Spitzer contemplates how the soul might live beyond death, or how life and death might be inseparable, with moments of existence overlapping in ways that defy linear time. The soul’s journey is likened to traversing between worlds, sometimes indistinguishable from shadows, clouds, or reflections, emphasizing the thin veil between existence and non-existence. The soul is connected to natural and cosmic imagery (clouds, stars, moths, butterflies, and sea creatures) suggesting that it is part of a larger, universal process of transformation and transmutation. This imagery conveys the soul’s ephemeral and yet eternal qualities, linking human experience to the rhythms of nature and the cosmos. Mr. Spitzer’s meditations reveal the soul as a source of both wonder and confusion. He acknowledges his limited understanding of the soul, its origins, and its destiny. The soul’s elusive nature challenges certainty and invites contemplation about the limits of human knowledge, the nature of existence, and the possibility of immortality or reincarnation. The chapter discusses the relationship between soul and memory, suggesting that memory might be a manifestation or extension of the soul. Memory, like the soul, is imperfect and fragmentary, and the dead may live on through memories or echoes rather than physical presence. This concept supports the novel’s recurring theme of the persistence of identity beyond physical life. The soul is significant as a symbol and concept embodying the novel’s exploration of identity, mortality, and the metaphysical uncertainties of human existence. It is neither wholly present nor absent but exists in a liminal space between being and non-being, connected intimately to the natural and cosmic order, and enveloped in mystery and ambiguity.

Chapter 29

  • Aristotle's bipartite psychology divides the soul into two main parts: the rational soul and the non-rational soul. The rational soul is responsible for reason, intellect, and higher thinking, while the non-rational soul encompasses emotions, appetites, and bodily functions. Aristotle further subdivided the rational soul into two parts: the calculating and the scientific. He also divided the non-rational soul into vegetative and appetitive. Young uses Aristotle's bipartite psychology as a framework for Mr. Spitzer's existential reflections on his divided self, the interplay of body and soul, and the blurred boundaries between life and death. The chapter emphasizes the complexity and ambiguity inherent in human nature and identity, highlighting the philosophical tension between physical mortality and spiritual continuity.

Chapter 37

  • The philosophy of reversals consists of various philosophical ideas and concepts related to the notion of something being turned upside down or reversed. It explores the nature of reversals in different domains: time, causality, values, societal structures. It examines how these reversals impact our understanding of reality, ethics, and meaning. Reversals in literature and art are used to create suspense, irony, and thematic complexity. It can be used to challenge expectations, expose hidden truths, and engage audiences on a deeper level. Aristotle’s reversal and recognition, Baudrillard’s Principle of Reversal examines how oppositions and differences can be overturned, leading to new forms of understanding and meaning. The philosophy of reversals is a prominent theme that permeates the narrative, revealing a worldview where binaries dissolve and opposites interpenetrate, creating a complex and unstable reality. This chapter explores the idea that opposites, such as night and day, sun and moon, beginning and end are not separate entities but are deeply intertwined and often indistinguishable. The narrator, Vera, reflects on how "his night was his day, and his day was his night," and "his dawn was his twilight, and his moon was his sun, and his sun was his moon, and his beginning was his end, and his end was his beginning". These statements encapsulate the core of the reversal philosophy: the collapsing of distinctions that we usually take for granted. The narrative also conveys a sense that reality itself is fluid and mutable, with physical and metaphysical elements constantly changing places or merging. For example, "there should be these somersaultings as when the ceiling was the mirroring floor, trees of enormous crystal growing with their roots in the clouded sky, their branches under the earth". This imagery suggests a world where spatial and conceptual norms are inverted, challenging linear and stable perceptions of existence. The chapter touches on the paradoxical nature of life and death, love and its absence. Vera notes that "Love might wear the face of death, the grinning skull, as death might wear the face of love", emphasizing that what seem like opposites are reflections or transformations of each other. This complicates the understanding of identity and existence, as individuals may be "other than we seemed," with two forms "blended into one which was amorphous, being neither". The philosophy of reversals extends to the cosmic scale, where even celestial bodies and natural laws are subject to change and ambiguity. The narrative questions the fixity of time and space, suggesting that "there should be no harmony, no silent music like the music of the spheres" in the world beyond, because "nothing should be simple there because nothing was simple here". This reflects a skepticism of absolute order or finality in the universe. It presents a vision of a world and perhaps a universe where dualities are illusory, and existence is marked by continuous transformation and contradiction. This philosophy challenges conventional binaries and invites readers to consider a reality that is simultaneously unified and divided, stable and unstable, known and unknowable. This philosophy is central to the novel's exploration of existence, identity, and perception, illustrating a world where certainty dissolves and the dreamlike nature of reality is revealed.

Chapter 49

  • Problem of Universals - The problem of universals is an ancient question from metaphysics that has inspired a range of philosophical topics and disputes. "Should the properties an object has in common with other objects, such as color and shape, be considered to exist beyond those objects? And if a property exists separately from objects, what is the nature of that existence?” The problem of universals relates to various inquiries closely related to metaphysics, logic, and epistemology, as far back as Plato and Aristotle, in efforts to define the mental connections a human makes when they understand a property such as shape or color to be the same in nonidentical objects. Universals are qualities or relations found in two or more entities. As an example, if all cup holders are circular in some way, circularity may be considered a universal property of cup holders. Further, if two daughters can be considered female offspring of Frank, the qualities of being female, offspring, and of Frank, are universal properties of the two daughters. Many properties can be universal: being human, red, male or female, liquid or solid, big or small, etc. Philosophers agree that human beings can talk and think about universals, but disagree on whether universals exist in reality beyond mere thought and speech.

  • William James - Though James certainly agreed with Peirce and against Berkeley that general ideas exist as a psychological fact, he was a nominalist in his ontology: “From every point of view, the overwhelming and portentous character ascribed to universal conceptions is surprising. Why, from Plato and Aristotle, philosophers should have vied with each other in scorn of the knowledge of the particular and in adoration of that of the general, is hard to understand, seeing that the more adorable knowledge ought to be that of the more adorable things and that the things of worth are all concretes and singulars. The only value of universal characters is that they help us, by reasoning, to know new truths about individual things.” William James, The Principles of Psychology (1890). There are at least three ways in which a realist might try to answer James' challenge of explaining the reason why universal conceptions are more lofty than those of particulars: the moral–political answer, the mathematical–scientific answer, and the anti-paradoxical answer. Each has contemporary or near-contemporary advocates.

Chapter 51

  • Boethius was a Roman senator, consul, magister officiorum, polymath, historian, and philosopher of the Early Middle Ages. He was a central figure in the translation of the Greek classics into Latin, a precursor to the Scholastic movement, and, along with Cassiodorus, one of the two leading Christian scholars of the 6th century. The local cult of Boethius in the Diocese of Pavia was sanctioned by the Sacred Congregation of Rites in 1883, confirming the diocese's custom of honoring him on the 23rd of October. Boethius was born in Rome a few years after the forced abdication of the last Western Roman emperor, Romulus Augustulus. A member of the Anicii family, he was orphaned following the family's sudden decline and was raised by Quintus Aurelius Memmius Symmachus, a later consul. After mastering both Latin and Greek in his youth, Boethius rose to prominence as a statesman during the Ostrogothic Kingdom, becoming a senator by age 25, a consul by age 33, and later chosen as a personal advisor to Theodoric the Great. In seeking to reconcile the teachings of Plato and Aristotle with Christian theology, Boethius sought to translate the entirety of the Greek classics for Western scholars. He published numerous transcriptions and commentaries of the works of Nicomachus, Porphyry, and Cicero, among others, and wrote extensively on matters concerning music, mathematics, and theology. His Arithmetica, geometria, et musica became the classical quadrivium or the basis of the liberal arts curriculum in the Middle Ages. This grouping emphasizes the interconnectedness of these disciplines, with arithmetic and geometry as foundational, and music was understood as a manifestation of mathematical principles. These disciplines were seen as pathways to understanding the underlying mathematical structure of the universe and were considered essential for a well-rounded education.

Chapter 51

  • Though his translations were unfinished following an untimely death, it is largely due to them that the works of Aristotle survived into the Renaissance. Despite his successes as a senior official, Boethius became deeply unpopular among other members of the Ostrogothic court for denouncing the extensive corruption prevalent among other members of government. After publicly defending fellow consul Caecina Albinus from charges of conspiracy, he was imprisoned by Theodoric around the year 523. While jailed Boethius wrote On the Consolation of Philosophy, a philosophical treatise on fortune, death, and other issues which became one of the most influential and widely reproduced works of the Early Middle Ages. He was tortured and executed in 524, becoming a martyr in the Christian faith by tradition.

The Latin phrase can be rendered in English as "from the perspective of the eternal". More loosely, it is commonly used to refer to an objective (or theoretically possible alternative) point of view. In the history of philosophy, this way of proceeding may be contrasted with Aristotle's. Aristotle's methodological differentiations in his "philosophy of human affairs" and his natural philosophy are grounded in the distinction between what is "better known to us" and things "better known in themselves", or what is "first for us" and what is "first by nature", a distinction that is deliberately discarded by Spinoza, and also by other modern philosophers.
