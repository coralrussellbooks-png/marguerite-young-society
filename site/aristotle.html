Definition from research

Chapter 6

  • “You think that beauty is as beauty does”: The proverb says a person’s true beauty is in their character and actions, not their physical appearance. The older proverb is “handsome is as handsome does” (1500s). The sentiment is found in the writings of Geoffrey Chaucer in “The Wife of Bath’s Tale” (c. 1387) from The Canterbury Tales. Chaucer’s lines suggest that the most virtuous person, regardless of physical appearance, is the most beautiful. The Greek philosophers Plato and Aristotle linked beauty to virtue, order, and symmetry. This perspective influenced the development of the proverb, suggesting that inner qualities are just as important as outward appearance in determining beauty.

Chapter 8

  • “that dream within the dream”: This may refer to Edgar Allan Poe’s poem “A Dream within a Dream” (1849), which says, “All that we see or seem / Is but a dream within a dream.” This dream within a dream is a recurring theme throughout the book. The idea that life is a dream goes back to Plato, Aristotle, and Descartes. The first stanza ends with that statement, and the last stanza turns it into a question: “Is all that we see or seem / But a dream within a dream?” This symbolizes the recursive, elusive quality of existence in the novel: a reality constantly refracted through multiple dreamlike states that challenge the notion of a fixed, singular truth.

Chapter 9

  • “Early to bed and early to rise makes a man healthy, wealthy and wise”: The proverb praises rising before most others, early and consistently, so as to accomplish more in the day. This method has been recommended since antiquity. The philosopher Aristotle said a phrase very similar to the one Miss MacIntosh used. Within the context of religious observances, spiritual writers have called this practice the heroic minute, referring to the sacrifice which this entails. Benjamin Franklin printed the exact phrase Miss MacIntosh used in Poor Richard’s Almanack (1735). It is a saying that is viewed as a commonsensical proverb.

Chapter 13

  • “not even the creaking of starlight”: Aristotle reports the Pythagorean answer: the spheres do make music, and people do not hear it because they have heard it since birth (On the Heavens 2.9). Lorenzo tells Jessica, “Such harmony is in immortal souls; / But whilst this muddy vesture of decay / Doth grossly close it in, we cannot hear it” (The Merchant of Venice 5.1).

Chapter 14

  • “the music of the spheres, the silent music”: Boethius was a Roman senator, consul, magister officiorum, polymath, historian, and philosopher of the Early Middle Ages. He sought to reconcile the teachings of Plato and Aristotle with Christian theology. He broke logic into three parts: that which defines, that which divides, and that which deduces. There are three types of arguments: those of necessity, of ready believability, and sophistry. In De Musica there are three classifications: Musica mundana, Musica humana, Musica instrumentalis. In Dante’s Divine Comedy, the spirit of Boethius is pointed out by Saint Thomas Aquinas and is mentioned further in the poem. In his book De Institutione Musica (Fundamentals of Music) his drawings appear striking and may be an inspiration for the organization of Young’s novel. Musica universalis or music of the spheres or harmony of the spheres is a Greek philosophical concept founded by Pythagoras. This metaphysical principle stated that mathematical relationships could produce ‘tones’ of energy that could produce, among other things, sounds. Pythagoras proposed that the celestial bodies all have their own unique hum based on their orbital revolution. This idea influenced many scholars until the end of the Renaissance. Johannes Kepler, a German astronomer from the 1600s wrote extensively on the subject. Later astronomy set it aside. Boethius wrote The Consolation of Philosophy in prison awaiting execution, with its Wheel of Fortune.
  • “her peacocks”: Aristotle referred to the peacock’s vain reputation. The Roman poet Ennius, following the Pythagorean teaching of rebirth, dreamed that Homer’s soul had passed into a peacock and then into himself. The peacock symbolizes the dazzling but ephemeral nature of beauty and social display, the multiplicity and fragmentation of perception, and the tension between illusion and reality that the characters navigate.

Chapter 15

  • “one swallow does not make a summer”: Aristotle uses the saying in the Nicomachean Ethics (1.7) to discuss happiness and eudaimonia (living well or flourishing). He argues that a brief period of happiness or a single good deed doesn’t make a person truly happy or blessed. Happiness, for Aristotle, is a state of being that develops over a lifetime through virtuous activity.

Chapter 21

  • “evil has many forms, and the good has only one”: Aristotle writes that “it is possible to fail in many ways… while to succeed is possible only in one way,” and quotes the line “men are good in but one way, but bad in many” (Nicomachean Ethics 2.6).

Chapter 23

  • “What was so rare a thing as common sense”: Thomas Paine’s polemical pamphlet Common Sense (1776) has been described as the most influential political pamphlet of the 18th century, affecting both the American and French revolutions. Today, the concept of common sense, and how it should best be used, remains linked to many of the most perennial topics in epistemology and ethics, with special focus often directed at the philosophy of the modern social sciences.
  • “the middle brow, the middle way”: “Middlebrow” was new in Young’s day. Russell Lynes’s “Highbrow, Lowbrow, Middlebrow” ran in Harper’s in 1949. The middle way is Aristotle’s mean, virtue as the middle between two extremes (Nicomachean Ethics 2.6).

Chapter 29

  • “between the ecstasy of happiness and that of sorrow”: In philosophy, happiness and sorrow are intertwined, with sorrow often seen as a necessary component for experiencing the depth of joy and vice versa rather than as simple opposites. It can be viewed in a number of ways: a dialectical relationship (yin and yang-complementary forces where one cannot exist without the other); the contrast highlights the value of positive experiences; a catalyst for growth, introspection, and a deeper understanding of life’s complexities. Philosophical perspectives are found in Buddhism, Christianity, Hinduism, Stoicism, Utilitarianism, Hedonism, Life Satisfaction Theory. Plato and Aristotle’s eudaimonia where flourishing and well-being are achieved by living a virtuous life, not simply experiencing happiness. Nirvana is a state of liberation from suffering and the cycle of rebirth. Equanimity is the ability to remain calm and balanced in the face of both pleasure and pain. The philosophical exploration of happiness and sorrow is presented through the reflections of Mr. Spitzer, who contemplates the ambiguous and intertwined nature of these emotions. The chapter discusses how happiness and sorrow seldom occur in pure, disassociated states; rather, happiness is often tinged with sorrow due to the fear of its temporality, and sorrow is tinged with happiness from the knowledge that it may endure. Mr. Spitzer observes the delicate distinctions between butterflies and moths, using them as metaphors for the subtle and elusive qualities of emotions like happiness and sorrow. Butterflies, associated with daytime and vibrant colors, contrast with moths, which fly by night and evoke muted and exaggerated perceptions. This imagery reflects the nuanced interplay of light and darkness, joy and pain, in human experience. The chapter touches on the fleeting and ephemeral nature of life and emotions, highlighting that moments of happiness are brief and often overshadowed by sorrow. Mr. Spitzer’s meditations suggest that both happiness and sorrow are transient, yet deeply interconnected aspects of existence that shape the human condition. It philosophically portrays happiness and sorrow not as opposites but as closely related states, each containing elements of the other, and both integral to the complexity of life’s experiences. This exploration is woven into the natural and symbolic imagery of butterflies, moths, and the rhythms of day and night, emphasizing the fluid and often paradoxical nature of these emotions.
  • “the bipart soul”: Aristotle’s bipartite psychology divides the soul into two main parts: the rational soul and the non-rational soul. The rational soul is responsible for reason, intellect, and higher thinking, while the non-rational soul encompasses emotions, appetites, and bodily functions. Aristotle further subdivided the rational soul into two parts: the calculating and the scientific. He also divided the non-rational soul into vegetative and appetitive. Young uses Aristotle’s bipartite psychology as a framework for Mr. Spitzer’s existential reflections on his divided self, the interplay of body and soul, and the blurred boundaries between life and death. The chapter emphasizes the complexity and ambiguity inherent in human nature and identity, highlighting the philosophical tension between physical mortality and spiritual continuity.

Chapter 37

  • “or there should be these reversals”: The philosophy of reversals consists of various philosophical ideas and concepts related to the notion of something being turned upside down or reversed. It explores the nature of reversals in different domains: time, causality, values, societal structures. It examines how these reversals impact our understanding of reality, ethics, and meaning. Reversals in literature and art are used to create suspense, irony, and thematic complexity. It can be used to challenge expectations, expose hidden truths, and engage audiences on a deeper level. Aristotle’s reversal and recognition. Baudrillard’s Principle of Reversal examines how oppositions and differences can be overturned, leading to new forms of understanding and meaning (Jean Baudrillard, Symbolic Exchange and Death, 1976). The philosophy of reversals is a prominent theme that permeates the narrative, revealing a worldview where binaries dissolve and opposites interpenetrate, creating a complex and unstable reality. This chapter explores the idea that opposites, such as night and day, sun and moon, beginning and end are not separate entities but are deeply intertwined and often indistinguishable. Vera’s “his night was his day” passage is in Chapter 61.
  • “this mistaken earth which was perhaps its counter-earth”: The Pythagorean Philolaus set a counter-earth, the Antichthon, beyond the Earth, always hidden from it, to bring the heavenly bodies to ten (Aristotle, On the Heavens 2.13).
  • “as man became woman with her heaving breasts”: Gender as a social construct refers to the socially created roles, behaviors, expressions, and identities associated with being a woman, man, boy, or girl. It’s not inherently determined by biology, but rather shaped by cultural norms, societal expectations, and historical contexts. This means gender is not fixed and can vary across cultures and time. In short, it is not a biological reality: it is socially created, varies across cultures, is dynamic and fluid, and is distinct from biological sex. Philosophical thought on gender goes back to ancient Greece, where Pythagoras’s table of opposites set male against female (Aristotle, Metaphysics 1.5) and Plato argued that women could be guardians (Republic 5). Later came John Locke, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Judith Sargent Murray (“On the Equality of the Sexes,” 1790), and Mary Wollstonecraft (A Vindication of the Rights of Woman, 1792). The chapter and novel present gender as a fluid and changeable construct rather than a rigid, innate fact, using metaphorical and dreamlike language to question fixed dichotomies between male and female, self and other, life and death.

Chapter 38

  • “the beautiful music of the spheres”: Musica Universalis (music of the spheres) concept incorporates the metaphysical principle that mathematical relationships express qualities or “tones” of energy that manifests in numbers, visual angles, shapes and sounds which are all connected within a pattern of proportion. This has been explored by Pythagoras, Plato, Aristotle (he rejected the idea), Boethius developed three categories of music (musica mundana, musica humana, and musica quae in quibusdam constituta est instrumentis), Kepler expounded the idea in Harmonices Mundi (1619). Boethius’s writings were later illustrated, and Young may have taken inspiration from these illustrations for the novel.
  • “Life contained death as a cloud contained a passing star”: Zeno’s paradoxes: the finite can contain the infinite. Aristotle reports Zeno’s argument “that that which is in locomotion must arrive at the half-way stage before it arrives at the goal” (Aristotle, Physics 6.9, trans. R. P. Hardie and R. K. Gaye, 1930). The chapter conveys a sense of confusion about the continuity of life and death and the elusive nature of reality. The characters reflect on the idea of being between two worlds, the living and the dead, and the uncertainty of identity and existence, which echoes the philosophical challenge posed by Zeno’s paradoxes regarding infinite divisibility and the nature of motion. This discussion illustrates the novel’s broader themes of ambiguity, the interplay between reality and illusion, and the complexity of human consciousness and experience.

Chapter 40

  • “who should know when the dream ended”: In philosophy there is the question of dreams by Zhuangzi, Plato, and Aristotle. Descartes asked and tried to answer the following question “How can I know that I am not now dreaming?” which seems to be the same question Catherine is preoccupied with. Descartes’ answer was that we cannot trust our senses in waking life without invoking a benevolent God who would not deceive us. Catherine takes this one step further at the end of Chapter 39 and asks, “What if God is dreaming?” In psychology the question “Is Life a Dream?” is answered with the coherence of the waking world does give evidence that it is not just a figment of our imagination. However, the objects that we perceive as permanent are in reality through the science of quantum physics mostly made up of empty space. In a sense people do live in a dream world that is created by the mind. When people are awake, their minds conform their dreaming to a reliable set of patterns, which they assume to be determined by a reality that exists independently of their experience of it, though they have no way of knowing that reality except through the complex ways in which it affects their dream. There is also reality that is experienced and affected by psychedelic drugs and other altered states of consciousness. The chapter weaves together the philosophical inquiries of Zhuangzi, Plato, Aristotle, and Descartes on dreams to underscore the complexity and mystery of human consciousness and the elusive quest for truth amid illusions.
  • “a golden mean between extremes”: Aristotle placed virtue at the mean between two extremes, as courage lies between cowardice and rashness (Nicomachean Ethics 2.6).
  • “All was water”: Thales, the first Greek philosopher, held that all things are water (Aristotle, Metaphysics 1.3).

Chapter 41

  • “Aristotle had said that the ear was the doorway to the soul”: Aristotle was a Greek philosopher. The phrase “doorway to the soul” is not found in Aristotle. The nearest is his judgment that sight serves the needs of life, “but for developing intelligence, and in its indirect consequences, hearing takes the precedence,” for “rational discourse is a cause of instruction in virtue of its being audible” (Aristotle, On Sense and the Sensible 1, trans. J. I. Beare, 1908).

Chapter 42

  • “this sublunar world”: For Aristotle, all change and decay belong to the world below the moon, while the heavens above it are unchanging (On the Heavens 1).

Chapter 49

  • “one of whom no unitary statement could be made”: The problem of universals in philosophy concerns the nature and existence of universals, qualities or properties that can be shared by multiple particular things, and how they relate to particulars. In this chapter, there are multiple reflections on identity, existence, perception, and the nature of reality that offer a profound literary meditation of this “Problem.”

Chapter 51

  • Boethius (about 477-524) was a Roman senator, consul, and philosopher who sought to translate Plato and Aristotle into Latin; largely through his translations the works of Aristotle survived. His works on arithmetic, geometry, and music, with astronomy, formed the quadrivium, the core of the medieval liberal arts, in which music was the mathematical study of harmony, ratio, and proportion rather than performance. Imprisoned by Theodoric for defending a fellow consul against charges of conspiracy, he wrote On the Consolation of Philosophy, a treatise on fortune and death, before he was tortured and executed in 524. (Wikipedia)

Chapter 55

  • “the muted murmur of the paper nautilus”: The paper nautilus, or argonaut, is an octopus whose female makes a thin, paper-white shell to carry her eggs; Aristotle and Pliny believed she raised two arms as sails and sailed the sea in it.

Chapter 61

  • Mr. Spitzer thought Aristotle said that hedgehogs always stand with their backs to the sea. Aristotle: History of Animals IX says they look out of their holes in the direction of the wind.
