Chapter 63

On Chapter 63 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Characters: Miss MacIntosh

The Questions

Miss MacIntosh is “the Rock of Ages in a land where all things crumbled.” Is that solidity real, or the one construction dressed to look like bedrock?

She is “the one person never dreaming where all might dream.” Can a person truly not dream, or only refuse to admit the dream?

She holds “that there was a framework in which all must live.” Is a framework a discovery, or a thing she has built and sealed?

Why does the book keep returning to the plainest figure it has already shown to be made?

The novel comes back to Miss MacIntosh, and it comes back to her surface, restating the claim the reader has by now seen behind. She is “the Rock of Ages in a land where all things crumbled,” “the acme of common sense,” “the surefooted Pilgrim,” “the one person never dreaming where all might dream.” This is her blueprint in its full self-presentation, the solid rock against a crumbling world. The difference now is the reader, who has watched the wig lifted and the plurality show and the plainness itemized as props. The same solid surface is offered again, and it can no longer be taken as bedrock, because the book has shown what it is made of. The rock is a construction restating its own solidity.

Her claim to be “the one person never dreaming” is the blueprint’s central boast, and it is the one the book most distrusts. To be the person who does not dream, in a book where dreaming is the ordinary condition and the individual who believes himself sealed and single is the one illusion, is not to have escaped the dream but to have sealed against admitting it. Miss MacIntosh does not fail to dream; she refuses the dream, walls it out, and calls the walling common sense. Her not-dreaming is the most elaborate construction in her, a dream of not-dreaming, the sealed self insisting it alone stands outside the invention everyone lives in.

The framework she trusts is the finished blueprint named outright. She holds “that there was a framework in which all must live,” a fixed structure given rather than built, binding on everyone. This is exactly the frozen blueprint the book teaches its reader to distrust, presented as Miss MacIntosh’s deepest conviction: not that we build frameworks and keep them open, but that there is one framework, discovered and mandatory, in which all must live. She has mistaken her construction for the structure of the world, and the mistaking is the whole of what the book examines in her, the sealed frame taken for the given ground.

So the return is not repetition but transparency. The book brings the reader back to Miss MacIntosh’s surface precisely so that the reader can see through it, can hear “the Rock of Ages” and “never dreaming” and “a framework in which all must live” as the self-presentation of a sealed construction rather than as description of a solid fact. She is genuinely magnificent in her solidity, and the solidity is made. The chapter loves her and does not believe her, and it trusts the reader, by now, to do the same, to grant the rock its grandeur and to know it for a rock she carved.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Miss MacIntosh’s solidity for the bedrock the book finally rests on, and the reading that takes her claim never to dream at face value, as if she alone stood outside the invention. Neither the bedrock nor the true non-dreamer is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that Miss MacIntosh’s solidity is a construction restating its own solidity, no longer available as bedrock to a reader who has seen behind it; that her claim to be “the one person never dreaming” is not escape from the dream but a sealing against admitting it, a dream of not-dreaming; and that her trust in “a framework in which all must live” is the finished blueprint mistaken for the given structure of the world. The rock is carved, the non-dreamer dreams, and the framework is built. The book returns to her surface so the reader can see through it, and the chapter grants her grandeur without granting her the bedrock she claims.

For Reflection

What in one’s own life is presented, even to oneself, as solid bedrock that is actually a carved construction?

Is claiming not to dream a way of escaping the dream, or of sealing against admitting it?

When one holds that there is one framework in which all must live, is that a discovery or a seal?
