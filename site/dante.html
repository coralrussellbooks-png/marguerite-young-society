Chapter 8

  • Beatrice is a name derived from the French name Béatrice, which came from the Latin Beatrix, which means "she who makes happy". The popularity of the name spread because of Dante Alighieri's poetry about the Florentine woman Beatrice Portinari. Dante presents Beatrice as being worthy of speaking for God, making her a holy individual. Beatrice "Bice" di Folco Portinari was an Italian woman who has been commonly identified as the principal inspiration for Dante Alighieri's Vita Nuova, and is also identified with the Beatrice who acts as his guide in the last book of his narrative poem the Divine Comedy, Paradiso, and during the conclusion of the preceding Purgatorio. Within the Comedy, Beatrice is symbolic of divine grace and theology.

Chapter 11

  • Roland was a military leader under Charlemagne who died in the battle at Roncevaux. His life was turned into an epic tale, The Song of Roland which is a highly romanticized account of the Battle of Roncevaux Pass and Roland's death. In Dante’s Divine Comedy, Roland is named Orlando and in the Heaven of Mars together with others who fought for the faith.

Chapter 14

  • Boethius was a Roman senator, consul, magister officiorum, polymath, historian, and philosopher of the Early Middle Ages. He sought to reconcile the teachings of Plato and Aristotle with Christian theology. He broke logic into three parts: that which defines, that which divides, and that which deduces. There are three types of arguments: those of necessity, of ready believability, and sophistry. In De Musica there are three classifications: Musica mundana, Musica humana, Musica instrumentalis. He said “all reality is musical” In Dante’s Divine Comedy, the spirit of Boethius is pointed out by Saint Thomas Aquinas and is mentioned further in the poem. In his book De Institutione Musica (Fundamentals of Music) his drawings appear striking and may be an inspiration for the organization of Young’s novel. Musica universalis or music of the spheres or harmony of the spheres is a Greek philosophical concept founded by Pythagoras. This metaphysical principle stated that mathematical relationships could produce ‘tones’ of energy that could produce, among other things, sounds. Pythagoras proposed that the celestial bodies all have their own unique hum based on their orbital revolution. This idea influenced many scholars until the end of the Renaissance. Johannes Kepler, a German astronomer from the 1600s wrote extensively on the subject. It has since been debunked.

Chapter 38

  • “seven deathless eternities” may refer to Dante’s Divine Comedy where there are seven sins in Purgatory, seven heavens (which is also found in Judaism, Islam, and Hinduism), and seven virtues (which originated from Paganism). In Hinduism there are seven living gods. It can be understood as representing the eternal, unchanging spiritual realms or dimensions, where the soul and spirit exist beyond physical mortality and temporal constraints. It highlights the novel's recurring theme of the interplay between mortal life, death, and the possibility of immortal spiritual existence.

Chapter 54

  • Inferno is the first part of Italian writer Dante Alighieri's 14th-century narrative poem The Divine Comedy, followed by Purgatorio and Paradiso. The Inferno describes the journey of a fictionalized version of Dante himself through Hell, guided by the ancient Roman poet Virgil. In the poem, Hell is depicted as nine concentric circles of torment located within the Earth; it is the "realm [...] of those who have rejected spiritual values by yielding to bestial appetites or violence, or by perverting their human intellect to fraud or malice against their fellowmen". As an allegory, the Divine Comedy represents the journey of the soul toward God, with the Inferno describing the recognition and rejection of sin.

Chapter 54

  • Paradiso is the third and final part of Dante's Divine Comedy, following the Inferno and the Purgatorio. It is an allegory telling of Dante's journey through Heaven, guided by Beatrice, who symbolizes theology. In the poem, Paradise is depicted as a series of concentric spheres surrounding the Earth, consisting of the Moon, Mercury, Venus, the Sun, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, the Fixed Stars, the Primum Mobile and finally, the Empyrean. It was written in the early 14th century. Allegorically, the poem represents the soul's ascent to God.

Chapter 71

  • Dante's "wooing" of Beatrice was not a traditional courtship but an idealized love he expressed through poetry, famously documented in his book Vita Nuova. He fell in love with her at a very young age, and although he encountered her only a couple of times, she became his lifelong muse, representing a spiritual and intellectual ideal. The term "courtly love" was applied to their relationship, a concept involving an idealized and often unattainable love that inspires poetry and virtue.
