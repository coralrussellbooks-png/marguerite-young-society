Definition from research

Chapter 2

  • Dante Alighieri was an Italian poet who wrote the Divine Comedy.

Chapter 8

  • “that same old one-eyed Beatrice”: Beatrice is a name derived from the French name Béatrice, which came from the Latin Beatrix, which means “she who makes happy.” The popularity of the name spread because of Dante Alighieri’s poetry about the Florentine woman Beatrice Portinari. Dante presents Beatrice as being worthy of speaking for God, making her a holy individual. Beatrice “Bice” di Folco Portinari was an Italian woman who has been commonly identified as the principal inspiration for Dante Alighieri’s Vita Nuova, and is also identified with the Beatrice who acts as his guide in the last book of his narrative poem the Divine Comedy, Paradiso, and during the conclusion of the preceding Purgatorio. Within the Comedy, Beatrice is symbolic of divine grace and theology. Young’s Beatrice, a one-eyed washerwoman called a whore, is the opposite of Dante’s guide to heaven. Yet she too lives where the road divides, the graveyard on one side and the village of “promise through eternity” on the other.

Chapter 11

  • “Roland who was killed at Roncevalles”: Roland was a military leader under Charlemagne who died at the Battle of Roncevaux Pass in 778, when Basques ambushed Charlemagne’s rear guard. The Song of Roland (c. 1100) turns them into Saracens and has Roland blow his horn too late. In Dante’s Divine Comedy, Roland is named Orlando and placed in the Heaven of Mars with others who fought for the faith (Paradiso 18).

Chapter 14

  • “the music of the spheres, the silent music”: Boethius was a Roman senator, consul, magister officiorum, polymath, historian, and philosopher of the Early Middle Ages. He sought to reconcile the teachings of Plato and Aristotle with Christian theology. He broke logic into three parts: that which defines, that which divides, and that which deduces. There are three types of arguments: those of necessity, of ready believability, and sophistry. In De Musica there are three classifications: Musica mundana, Musica humana, Musica instrumentalis. In Dante’s Divine Comedy, the spirit of Boethius is pointed out by Saint Thomas Aquinas and is mentioned further in the poem. In his book De Institutione Musica (Fundamentals of Music) his drawings appear striking and may be an inspiration for the organization of Young’s novel. Musica universalis or music of the spheres or harmony of the spheres is a Greek philosophical concept founded by Pythagoras. This metaphysical principle stated that mathematical relationships could produce ‘tones’ of energy that could produce, among other things, sounds. Pythagoras proposed that the celestial bodies all have their own unique hum based on their orbital revolution. This idea influenced many scholars until the end of the Renaissance. Johannes Kepler, a German astronomer from the 1600s wrote extensively on the subject. Later astronomy set it aside. Boethius wrote The Consolation of Philosophy in prison awaiting execution, with its Wheel of Fortune.

Chapter 17

  • “as if I were entombed in fields of everlasting ice”: At the bottom of Dante’s Hell, the traitors are frozen in the ice of Cocytus (Inferno 32-34). Vera is about to feel betrayed.

Chapter 20

  • “a babe cast into purgatory”: Catholic tradition placed unbaptized infants not in purgatory but in limbo, at the edge of Hell, where Dante puts the virtuous unbaptized (Inferno 4).

Chapter 23

  • “where the dead souls wandered like flames”: This may recall Dante’s flames, each wrapping a soul, among them Ulysses (Inferno 26).

Chapter 26

  • “wandering through the intricate streets of a dark city in perhaps the underworld”: Dis, the walled city of Hell in Dante’s Inferno (cantos 8-9).
  • “in order to reach the upper world, one must first travel through the lower world”: Mr. Spitzer’s belief that people need to travel through the lower world to reach the upper world mirrors The Pilgrim’s Progress (1678) and/or Dante’s writings. Purgatory has seven terraces for the seven deadly sins, and there are seven virtues. Hell has nine circles and Heaven has nine spheres: the Moon, Mercury, Venus, Sun, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, the fixed stars, and the primum mobile. According to Dante the cosmos depends on God, who exists in the Empyrean, an immaterial heaven beyond space and time. The Empyrean is made up of God’s love and light, and is home to angels, the blessed, and the souls of the faithful. Interesting note that the woman with the Siamese cats believes she was the reincarnation of Cleopatra and Dante meets Cleopatra in the circle of Hell called Lust (“Cleopatràs lussurïosa,” Inferno 5.63). Virgil wrote “Facilis descensus Averno,” “the descent to Avernus is easy… but to retrace one’s steps and climb back to the upper air, that is the task, that is the labor” (Aeneid 6.126-129), and Heraclitus, “The way up and the way down are one and the same.”

Chapter 26 · The Upper and Lower Worlds

  • Mr. Spitzer’s lower world of “the thief who had stolen bright jewels” and “the painted street woman leering under a lamp” must be crossed to reach the upper, a journey “endless like some of those journeys taken by my mother.”

Chapter 29

  • “the last infernal circle of this Divine Comedy”: Divine Comedy (c. 1308-21) is a long Italian narrative poem by Dante Alighieri. It depicts the afterlife according to the Western Church medieval world view of the time. Like The Pilgrim’s Progress (1678) it is a journey from sin to heaven.

Chapter 30

  • “where Childe Roland had blown his horn before a dark tower”: Childe Roland is a character in a poem by Robert Browning. The character Roland in the poem makes a journey to the Dark Tower much like the journey of Pilgrim’s Progress and the Divine Comedy. The name Child Roland comes from the Shakespeare play King Lear Act 3 Scene 4 Lines 195-197. Browning’s poem is “Childe Roland to the Dark Tower Came” (1855). The horn also recalls Roland of The Song of Roland, who blew his horn Oliphant at Roncevaux (778).

Chapter 38

  • “seven deathless eternities” may refer to Dante’s Divine Comedy where there are seven sins in Purgatory, seven heavens (which is also found in Judaism, Islam, and Hinduism), and seven virtues (which originated from Paganism). In Hinduism there are seven living gods. It can be understood as representing the eternal, unchanging spiritual realms or dimensions, where the soul and spirit exist beyond physical mortality and temporal constraints. It highlights the novel's recurring theme of the interplay between mortal life, death, and the possibility of immortal spiritual existence.

Chapter 40

  • Dante or Durante di Alighiero degli Alighieri was a Florentine poet, writer and philosopher.

Chapter 54

  • Inferno is the first part of Italian writer Dante Alighieri's 14th-century narrative poem The Divine Comedy, followed by Purgatorio and Paradiso. The Inferno describes the journey of a fictionalized version of Dante himself through Hell, guided by the ancient Roman poet Virgil. In the poem, Hell is depicted as nine concentric circles of torment located within the Earth; it is the "realm [...] of those who have rejected spiritual values by yielding to bestial appetites or violence, or by perverting their human intellect to fraud or malice against their fellowmen". As an allegory, the Divine Comedy represents the journey of the soul toward God, with the Inferno describing the recognition and rejection of sin.

Chapter 54

  • Paradiso is the third and final part of Dante's Divine Comedy, following the Inferno and the Purgatorio. It is an allegory telling of Dante's journey through Heaven, guided by Beatrice, who symbolizes theology. In the poem, Paradise is depicted as a series of concentric spheres surrounding the Earth, consisting of the Moon, Mercury, Venus, the Sun, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, the Fixed Stars, the Primum Mobile and finally, the Empyrean. It was written in the early 14th century. Allegorically, the poem represents the soul's ascent to God.

Chapter 71

  • Dante's "wooing" of Beatrice was not a traditional courtship but an idealized love he expressed through poetry, famously documented in his book Vita Nuova. He fell in love with her at a very young age, and although he encountered her only a couple of times, she became his lifelong muse, representing a spiritual and intellectual ideal. The term "courtly love" was applied to their relationship, a concept involving an idealized and often unattainable love that inspires poetry and virtue.
