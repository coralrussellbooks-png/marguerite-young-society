Chapter 8: The Village at the End of the Road

Research notes: Chapter 8

Characters: Beatrice Fitzgerald · Homer Capehorn · Jacqueline White · Justice O’Leary · Madge Capehorn · Miss MacIntosh · Moses Hunnecker · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Death and Mortality

Symbols: The Bus and the Journey · Twins and Doubles

Philosophy: Augustine and Christian Theology · Descartes and Dualism · Marguerite Young on Her Craft · Plato and Platonism

Allusions: Dante · Edgar Allan Poe · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest

The bus is almost there.

After seven chapters of driving through an American Midwest that has grown increasingly strange with flooded roads, spectral doctors, the stories of Madge and the Tidings family and all the small-town dead we are nearly at the destination. Chapter 8 is the chapter of arrival, or almost-arrival. And Young, being Young, makes the approach as layered and strange as everything that came before it.

The storm intensifies. The road becomes a river. And the bus stops, briefly, at a crossroads that turns out to be one of the novel’s great symbolic intersections.

What actually happens in Chapter 8

The bus drives through a violent cloudburst: thunder, a broken windshield wiper, rain coming down like a deluge, the road nearly indistinguishable from the flooded fields around it. Vera reflects that it feels like the beginning of creation, or perhaps a farewell to the earth as she has known it.

The bus stops at a fork in the road near a graveyard hill. Old tombstones line the slope, their names worn faint. One branch of the fork leads down toward a small village. Moses describes that village as a kind of dreamland: a place where the children dance, where the young do “not grow old, where the old” never die. A land of promise through eternity.

The other side of the fork is Beatrice Fitzgerald’s house, a decrepit tin structure in a flooded yard full of mud, refuse, and wreckage. Moses stops here to change horses. And Madge and Homer argue about who Beatrice is and what she deserves.

Madge calls her the ugliest old woman for miles. A “common prostitute.” Someone who puts out her lights so no one will see her. Homer offers a different picture: a woman who works harder than anyone, who pulls a little red sled over snow with her face turning blue, who lost an eye when a boy shot her with a bow and arrow as a child, whose son, fathered, it’s rumored, by Old Doc, who never claimed him, joined the Navy and travels the world. Homer calls her “royal,” because names beginning with “Fitz” are said to trace back to royal bastard lines in England.

Moses delivers a long, dark, funny monologue about death and medical science, about how Old Doc couldn’t help Jacqueline White, about how no doctor can, about how there is no hell, no heaven, no cure, just the tide going out when it goes. And then the bus rolls into the village at night.

What Vera sees is spectral: upright houses like coffins, a pale moon like a skull, great spaces yawning between the buildings. She wonders if it is real. If any of it is. Whether she is inside a dream, or a dream within a dream.

The fork in the road

Young does not make you work hard to find the symbolism here, she places it plainly, and trusts you to feel its weight.

On one side: the graveyard, the tombstones with their fading names, the dead who have already arrived at their destination. On the other side: the village described as a land of eternal promise, where no one ages, no one dies, the children dance forever.

And right there at the junction, neither one nor the other: Beatrice Fitzgerald’s ruined house.

Beatrice is not dead, but she is not exactly thriving. She exists at the threshold between the graveyard and the village, between what the community has decided she is and what she actually is, between the two versions of her that Madge and Homer offer. She is the novel’s crossroads figure, and she is treated accordingly: as an embarrassment, a mystery, a rumor, and a person who nonetheless pulls a red sled through the snow to do other people’s laundry.

Beatrice Fitzgerald, and what the two versions of her mean

Madge’s version: an ugly old woman, socially disgraced, a prostitute, someone who hides from the world by putting out her lights.

Homer’s version: a woman of possible royal lineage, a tireless worker, someone who bore a son nobody would claim, who lost an eye to a childhood injury and never made a fuss about it, who has endured quietly in a flooded tin house while the rest of the town talked about her.

Both versions are being offered by people who know her only through gossip. Neither Madge nor Homer is her. Young never gives Beatrice a voice in this chapter and she exists entirely through what other people say about her, which is its own kind of statement.

The name Beatrice comes from the Latin for “she who makes happy” and is most famous from Dante’s Divine Comedy, where Beatrice Portinari is Dante’s guide through Paradise, a figure of divine grace, a woman so pure she was thought worthy to speak for God. Young’s Beatrice is the opposite in every external way: old, poor, scorned, living in a flooded yard. And yet Homer calls her royal. And yet she works harder than anyone. And yet her son, out at sea, still remembers her.

Young is doing what she does throughout this novel: placing the grandest possible resonance inside the smallest, most overlooked life. Beatrice Fitzgerald in her tin house is Dante’s Beatrice, reduced and enduring. The guide through paradise, standing at the crossroads, putting out her lights so nobody will see her.

Moses on death, and why it matters

Moses’s monologue in Chapter 8 is one of his longest and most philosophically direct. He is, essentially, delivering a sermon on mortality as one that strips away every comfort. No heaven. No hell. No doctor who can stop the tide when it turns. Jacqueline White is dying and the best doctor in the world couldn’t help her.

This is bleak, and Young means it to be. But notice what Moses is doing underneath the bleakness: he is grieving. His rant about Old Doc and the futility of medicine is not the speech of a man who doesn’t care. It is the speech of a man who cares enormously and has found no way to act on it. He cannot fix Jacqueline. He cannot fix Old Doc. He cannot fix Beatrice’s flooded yard. The rage is helplessness wearing a different face.

This is Moses’s consistent register throughout the novel: a man who sees everything clearly, grieves everything quietly, and expresses it all as fury.

Arriving at the village

The chapter ends with Vera’s description of the village at night, and it is one of the most carefully composed passages in these early chapters. The houses stand like coffins. The moon is pale and dented like a skull. Great spaces yawn between the buildings.

This is not welcoming. This is not the land of promise Moses described. Or perhaps it is and this is what eternal life looks like when you arrive: spectral, silent, emptied of everything ordinary.

Vera asks whether it is real or a dream. Whether the journey has been a dream within a dream, a phrase Young borrows from Edgar Allan Poe, who borrowed it from the long philosophical tradition going back to Plato and Descartes, asking whether what we perceive is ever the world itself or only our representation of it.

You have been on this bus for eight chapters. You may have started asking yourself the same question.

What to watch for as you keep reading

Beatrice’s son. He joined the Navy and travels the world: Singapore, Borneo, “the China Sea, the Rock of Gibraltar.” He is out there somewhere, seeing everything, the child of a woman nobody respects and a man who never claimed him. Young will not forget him. Watch for the places he travels through, they become a kind of atlas of the world beyond this narrow midwestern road.

The name Longtree appears among the tombstones on the graveyard hill. Hold onto it. It is the last name of a character who will appear much later in the novel. Young plants names in passing throughout these early chapters, not to confuse you, but because she is building a world that is already fully inhabited before the novel begins. The graveyard names are not decorative. They are a record.

“A dream within a dream.” This phrase, lifted from Poe, will echo through the rest of the novel. Young is building a question here that has no clean answer: at what point does the story we tell ourselves about reality become the only reality we have? When does the dream replace the thing it dreamed of? Vera has been asking this question about Miss MacIntosh since Chapter 1. The village ahead is going to press it further.

Light and darkness. Beatrice puts out her lights so people won’t see her. The village arrives at night, described as spectral and empty. Moses drives through a cloudburst with a broken windshield wiper. Light in this chapter is failing, intermittent, unreliable and characters’ relationships to it tell you something about their relationship to being seen.

What to hold onto

Chapter 8 can feel like a chapter of arrivals and thresholds, the fork in the road, the graveyard on one side, the dreamland village on the other, Beatrice at the crossroads, the bus rolling finally into a place that looks like death dressed up as a town.

If you feel the strangeness of it pressing in, that is exactly right. This is the edge of something. The bus has arrived, or almost arrived. But arrival in this novel does not mean resolution. It means the next question.

What Vera is searching for, Miss MacIntosh, or the truth about Miss MacIntosh, or the truth about herself, is still ahead. The village is spectral and silent. But she is still on the bus. She has not stopped looking.

Neither should you.
