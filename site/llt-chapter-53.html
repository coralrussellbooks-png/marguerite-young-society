Chapter 53

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: Not One Self · Unfinished Is Honest · Language Shapes What's Possible

The Questions

Mr. Spitzer finds “no unity such as he had desired, no integration of these lost thematic segments.” Is the failure to unify a defeat, or the truth of a self?

His great work is “his unfinished song.” Why does the book give its musician a song he never completes?

His notes carry “many signs like his S which concealed other signs.” What is a sign that hides more signs beneath it?

He leads “his future existence, one already outside the realm where he was.” Where is a self that lives ahead of itself?

This chapter states Not One Self as an unfinished search rather than a settled fact. Mr. Spitzer has “sought for it” and found “no unity such as he had desired, no integration of these lost thematic segments and contrapuntal instincts wandering through a storm of years.” He wanted to be one, tried to gather the wandering themes of himself into a single composition, and could not. The book elsewhere affirms the many selves as the ordinary condition; here it shows the man who wants them integrated and is refused, and the refusal is not presented as his failure but as the honest result. The segments stay lost and disconnected because that is what a self is, and the desire for unity is the thing that goes unmet.

The unfinished song is Unfinished Is Honest made his whole life’s work. Mr. Spitzer is “this lost musician with his unfinished song,” an opus meant for “crescendos of unbelievable climax” and never brought to them, only “poor notes” and “faintest gropings.” The book has praised the unfinished as the truthful shape and dreaded the completed as the dead; it gives its composer, fittingly, a song he will never finish, because a finished song would be a sealed thing, and the elegy of a life kept open cannot arrive at its own last chord. His incompletion is not the tragedy of the chapter. It is the honesty of it.

His notation carries the plank at the level of the mark itself. His score bears “many signs like his S which concealed other signs,” the initial that stands for him hiding further signs beneath it. This is Language Shapes What's Possible at its densest: the sign does not point cleanly at one meaning but layers over others, the way the noun does not fix one identity but covers a shifting plurality. His very name-mark, the S, is not a label but a lid, and under it are more marks, more selves, more meanings, none of them final. To read him is to lift sign after sign and find sign beneath.

And he lives ahead of himself. “Already in this temporal life expiring with each moment, he led his future existence, one already outside the realm where he was, one in that space where he would never be.” The self is not even located in its own present; it is out ahead, in a realm it will never reach, so that Mr. Spitzer is displaced from himself in time as he is displaced in unity and in sign. The unintegrated segments, the unfinished song, the sign concealing signs, and the self living ahead of itself are one condition seen four ways: a self that is many, unfinished, layered, and never quite where it is.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that treats Mr. Spitzer’s failure to find unity as a defeat to be pitied, and the reading that treats his unfinished song as a promise the book means him eventually to keep. Neither the pitiable failure nor the deferred completion is what the chapter offers.

The footing is that the desire for a unified self goes honestly unmet, the segments staying lost because that is what a self is; that the unfinished song is Unfinished Is Honest made a life’s work, a finished song being a sealed and therefore dead thing; that the sign conceals further signs, so that even his name-mark is a lid over a plurality rather than a label on one meaning; and that the self lives ahead of itself, never quite where it is. The self is many, unfinished, layered, and displaced, and none of that is a flaw to correct. Mr. Spitzer’s song stays unfinished, and the chapter does not promise its last chord.

For Reflection

If the desire to be one integrated self goes unmet, is that a failure, or the truth being refused a lie?

Why might the unfinished work be truer than the completed one it was meant to become?

What sign that one uses for oneself is really a lid over further, unresolved signs?
