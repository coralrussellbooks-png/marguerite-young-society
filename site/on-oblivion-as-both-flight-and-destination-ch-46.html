On oblivion as both flight and destination.

Young's observation that great people often value a moment of oblivion more than lasting glory, that oblivion is paradoxically both what they flee and what they seek, is one of the chapter's most psychologically exact formulations. The person who has given everything to a cause, who has been entirely public and entirely purposeful, sometimes longs for the relief of not being remembered, not being called upon, not being the instrument of anything. The suffragette who was ashamed of her mountain-climbing accomplishments is a person who has lived so fully in service of recognition for the cause that the private wish for invisibility has never had a moment to breathe. Young sees this. She has always seen it in Cousin Hannah: the great captain who secretly wanted the skirt, the freedom fighter who secretly wanted peace.
