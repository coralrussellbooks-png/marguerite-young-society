Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 21

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

The bald head becomes an image the chapter turns over slowly, a "moon sunken beyond a watery horizon," "the secret pearl," the thing under "the palpable disguise of the average man." This is the loss of the feminine beauty tied to hair, and it is the body as the place the self comes apart raised to a general law: the baldness is not Miss MacIntosh's private wound but the truth under the disguise every person wears, the face left when the covering is gone. Vera means to hide it so Miss MacIntosh can stay "a usual being in the light," the tender concealment from the last chapter carried forward, the mask kept on for love.

Miss MacIntosh, "incapable of grand or feeble impersonations or plain hypocrisies," is nonetheless the one who says that "evil has many forms, and the good has only one and has no other face." The line is her plain morality, and it is the book's arithmetic of the self, the many faces the novel keeps finding set against the single face it almost never does. Her warning to "not be easily deceived by fine feathers which make fine birds" is the same thought worn as country wisdom.

Vera's pity settles on the body itself, "the stalk of a breast which had been cut away so early," "the scar," "no eyebrows, no eyelashes," and the chapter honors the "denudement of the substance" rather than flinching from it. When the ruined party dress is thrown out for good, "There'll be no new one," the ornamental self goes with it, the filmy floating disguise discarded so the plain, unmasked person can stand where it stood.
