How the People Actually Govern

A Companion Document to the Constitution

The Philosophical Foundation

The vote is the most basic mechanism by which people exercise power over the decisions that govern their lives. Every restriction on who can vote, every system that makes some votes count more than others, every barrier between the person and the ballot is a decision about whose voice the political system will hear. Those decisions have never been neutral. They have consistently reflected the interests of the people already in power and excluded the people those in power wanted to keep out.

This constitution starts from a different premise: the person who is subject to the laws has a stake in who makes them. That principle, applied honestly, produces a voting system that is radically more inclusive than the one currently in place. This document describes what that system looks like and how to build it.

Who Votes

The Franchise at Sixteen

The voting age under this constitution is sixteen. The person who is sixteen pays taxes, is subject to criminal law, can be tried as an adult, drives on public roads, and will live inside the consequences of decisions being made today for sixty or seventy years. They have a stake in those decisions now. The argument that sixteen-year-olds are not mature enough to vote applies with equal force to many adults who vote without challenge, and it has never been applied consistently. It is applied selectively to exclude a population that is young, less conservative on average than older voters, and less likely to support the interests of the people who set voting ages.

The research on youth voting is consistent: young people who vote in their first eligible election are significantly more likely to become lifelong voters. Civic participation rooted in schools and communities, where young people are already engaged, produces durable democratic habits. The vote at sixteen is full and equal: no parental consent, no junior franchise, no limitation by office or type of election.

Incarcerated People

Incarcerated people retain full voting rights under this constitution. The person subject to the criminal legal system has the strongest possible argument for a voice in who controls it. The disenfranchisement of incarcerated people is not a penological principle with a documented rationale. It is a political choice with a documented history: felony disenfranchisement laws were expanded dramatically after the Civil War as a mechanism for suppressing Black political participation after the Fifteenth Amendment prohibited explicit racial exclusion from voting. The racial composition of the incarcerated population, which this constitution's criminal justice companion document addresses directly, means that felony disenfranchisement continues to suppress Black political participation today.

Voting while incarcerated is a logistical question, not a philosophical one. Ballots are delivered to correctional facilities. People vote by mail or in designated voting areas within the facility. The facility is obligated to facilitate participation, not obstruct it.

People Under Guardianship

People under guardianship retain full voting rights. The obligation of guardianship is to support the person's participation in their own life, not to extinguish it. The assumption that a person under guardianship lacks the capacity to vote is not supported by evidence and is inconsistent with the disability rights framework this constitution adopts throughout. Cognitive difference does not disqualify a person from having preferences about who governs them. The support needed to exercise the vote is provided as part of the universal design requirements for the voting system.

Residents Subject to the Laws

Voting rights extend to all residents subject to the laws of this nation, not citizens alone. The person who pays taxes here, sends children to schools here, works here, and is subject to arrest under laws made here has a stake in who makes those laws. The demand that people be governed without voice is the original grievance of this nation's founding. It has never been fully resolved for the people who live here without citizenship. It is resolved here.

The implementation requires coordination between the voter registration system and the immigration system to ensure that participation is genuinely accessible to non-citizen residents and that the exercise of voting rights does not create immigration consequences. These are solvable administrative problems. They are not arguments against the principle.

How Voting Works

Automatic Registration

Every person eligible to vote is automatically registered at sixteen, or upon establishing residency if they arrive after sixteen. Registration is not a barrier to be cleared before voting. It is an administrative record maintained by the government as a condition of its obligation to facilitate participation. The current system, in which registration is the individual's responsibility and failure to register in time forecloses participation, is a filter that falls most heavily on people who move frequently, people who have had prior involvement with the criminal legal system, and people with less experience navigating bureaucratic processes. None of these are legitimate reasons to exclude someone from voting.

Registration records are updated automatically when a person moves, using address information from tax records, postal records, and other government databases, with a notification process that allows the person to verify and correct their registration. No person loses their registration without notice and an opportunity to correct the record before the next election.

Election Day

Election Day is a national holiday. The argument that people can vote before or after work ignores the reality of how many people work: the person working two jobs, the person whose employer does not provide paid time off, the person whose shift starts before polls open and ends after they close, the person whose childcare situation does not accommodate an unplanned stop at a polling place. Making Election Day a holiday does not solve all of these problems. It signals that the society considers participation important enough to protect time for it.

Early voting is available for a minimum of two weeks before Election Day, including weekends. Mail voting is available to every voter without requiring a reason. The option to vote early or by mail does not reduce Election Day options: polling places remain open and fully staffed on Election Day for people who prefer to vote in person.

Polling Place Access

Polling places are located and staffed to ensure that no person faces an unreasonable burden in accessing them. The closure of polling places in low-income and minority communities, documented extensively in the years following the Supreme Court's 2013 decision in Shelby County v. Holder, which gutted the preclearance requirement of the Voting Rights Act, is a constitutional violation under the equal protection provision. The number of polling places, their hours, their physical accessibility, and their staffing are subject to federal standards that cannot be reduced without federal review.

Polling places are physically accessible to people with disabilities. This means accessible entrances, accessible voting equipment, and staff trained to assist voters who need assistance. The voter who needs assistance may bring a person of their own choosing to assist them, with the only restriction being that the assistant may not be their employer or union representative.

Voting Technology

Voting technology is required to produce a paper record that the voter can verify before their ballot is cast and that can be audited after the election. Paperless electronic voting systems, which provide no independent verification of the vote recorded, are prohibited. The paper record is the authoritative record in any recount or audit.

The security of voting technology is a federal responsibility. The systems used to cast and count votes are subject to federal security standards, regular independent security audits, and mandatory reporting of any breach or attempted breach. Election administration is carried out by nonpartisan officials whose employment is not contingent on the electoral outcomes they administer.

Multilingual Access

Voting materials, ballot instructions, and assistance are available in every language spoken by a significant portion of the voting population in a given jurisdiction. The definition of significant is set at a level that ensures genuine access rather than technical compliance. The person who speaks a language other than English as their primary language is not a lesser citizen and does not receive lesser access to the democratic process.

How Elections Are Structured

Ranked Choice Voting

Elections for all federal offices use ranked choice voting. Voters rank candidates in order of preference. If no candidate receives a majority of first-choice votes, the candidate with the fewest votes is eliminated and their voters' second choices are distributed to the remaining candidates. The process continues until one candidate has a majority. The winner of a ranked choice election has majority support, which the winner of a plurality election in a multi-candidate race often does not.

Ranked choice voting reduces the spoiler effect that has structured American electoral politics for generations: the dynamic in which voting for a candidate who is not one of the two major party candidates is treated as throwing your vote away. When voters can rank their preferences, they can express support for candidates outside the two major parties without forfeiting their influence on the outcome between the major party candidates. The result is a more accurate representation of what the electorate actually wants.

Multi-member districts, in which more than one representative is elected from a given geographic area through ranked choice voting, produce representation proportional to the distribution of political preferences in the electorate. A district that is sixty percent one party and forty percent another elects representatives from both parties rather than producing a winner-take-all outcome that leaves forty percent of voters without representation. This is not a partisan advantage for either major party. It is the honest representation of a politically diverse electorate.

No Gerrymandering

District maps are drawn by independent commissions with members drawn equally from all parties and from citizens who belong to none. The process is public, with all meetings open, all data available, and all draft maps subject to public comment before adoption. The criteria for drawing districts are specified in advance and applied consistently: geographic compactness, preservation of existing political subdivisions where possible, compliance with the Voting Rights Act, and proportional representation of the population. Maximizing the electoral advantage of any party is explicitly prohibited as a criterion.

The history of gerrymandering in the United States is the history of the party in power drawing maps designed to keep itself in power. Both major parties have done this wherever they had the opportunity. The result is a House of Representatives in which the majority of seats are safe for one party or the other before a single vote is cast, which reduces electoral competition, reduces accountability, and produces representatives whose primary challenge comes from primary voters rather than the general electorate. Independent redistricting does not produce perfect maps. It produces maps drawn without the explicit goal of partisan advantage, which is a substantial improvement.

The Electoral College

The Electoral College is abolished. The President is elected by national popular vote, every vote weighted equally regardless of where it is cast. The current system, in which the votes of people in a small number of competitive states determine the outcome of the presidential election while the votes of people in non-competitive states are effectively irrelevant to the outcome, is inconsistent with the principle that the people are the government. A person's vote for President should count equally whether they live in Wyoming or California, in a safe state or a swing state.

The National Popular Vote Interstate Compact, through which states agree to award their electoral votes to the winner of the national popular vote, has demonstrated that the transition to a national popular vote can be accomplished through democratic means. This constitution completes that transition by eliminating the Electoral College entirely.

Primary Elections

Primary elections determine who appears on the general election ballot. The current system, in which primaries are often decided by a small and unrepresentative fraction of the electorate, produces general election candidates who reflect the preferences of primary voters rather than the broader electorate. Open primaries, in which all registered voters regardless of party affiliation may participate, produce candidates with broader appeal and reduce the dynamic in which the most ideologically extreme candidate in a safe district wins the primary and therefore the general election without ever needing to appeal to the full electorate.

The specific design of primary systems is left to the states within the requirement that primaries be open to all registered voters and conducted in a manner consistent with the equal protection and voting rights provisions of this constitution.

Campaign Finance

The Problem

The current campaign finance system allows the concentration of wealth to translate directly into political influence. The Supreme Court's 2010 decision in Citizens United v. FEC held that corporations and other organizations have a First Amendment right to spend unlimited amounts on political speech. The result has been the transformation of American elections into contests in which the candidates and causes with the most money have a substantial structural advantage over those with less, regardless of the merits of their positions or the breadth of their support.

The argument that money is speech, and therefore that limiting campaign spending violates the First Amendment, produces a system in which the people with the most money have the most speech and the people with the least money have the least. This is not a robust conception of free expression. It is the conversion of economic inequality into political inequality through the mechanism of campaign finance.

The Solution

Campaign contributions are capped at a level that allows broad participation without allowing any individual or organization to dominate the financing of a campaign. The cap is set at a level accessible to a substantial portion of the electorate, not only to the affluent. Contributions above the cap are prohibited regardless of the form they take: direct contributions, independent expenditures, and contributions to organizations that spend on elections are all subject to the same cap.

All contributions above a minimal threshold are disclosed in real time in a publicly accessible database. The voter who wants to know who is funding a candidate can find out before the election, not months afterward in a disclosure filing. The organization that spends money on elections without disclosing its donors is not exercising free speech. It is exercising anonymous influence, which is a different thing.

Public financing is available to candidates who demonstrate a threshold of grassroots support through small donations from a minimum number of constituents. The publicly financed candidate receives matching funds that allow them to compete without being dependent on large donors. The system is designed to make small donors more valuable to candidates than large ones, which changes the incentive structure of fundraising in ways that reduce the influence of concentrated wealth.

Elected officials may not receive income from private sources during their term. The official who holds a financial stake in an industry they regulate is not making decisions in the public interest. The transparency provisions require disclosure of all financial interests. The conflict of interest provisions require recusal from decisions affecting those interests.

Direct Democracy

The Initiative Process

Any proposed law or constitutional amendment may be placed before the full voting public by petition. A petition signed by two percent of registered voters triggers a national referendum. A majority vote enacts the measure into law with the same force as any act of Congress. Constitutional amendments by initiative require sixty percent approval.

The initiative process is a check on a legislature that has stopped representing the people who elected it. It is not a replacement for representative democracy. It is the mechanism by which the people can act when their representatives will not. The history of the initiative process at the state level shows both its value and its risks: it has been used to expand civil rights and to restrict them, to invest in public goods and to defund them. The constitutional rights provisions of Article VII constrain what any initiative can accomplish: the people may not vote away another group's fundamental rights.

The initiative process requires that proposed measures be written in plain language accessible to the people who will vote on them, that the fiscal impact be independently assessed and disclosed, and that the full text be available to every voter before the election. The ballot measure that is written to obscure its effects is a manipulation of the democratic process, not an exercise of it.

The Referendum

Congress must submit any law that significantly restructures voting rights, civil rights protections, or the powers of government itself to a public referendum before it takes effect. The determination of what constitutes a significant restructuring is made by an independent body insulated from the political interests of the Congress whose legislation it reviews. The standard is set to capture genuine structural changes rather than routine legislation, so that the referendum requirement does not become a tool for obstructing normal legislative function.

The popular referendum, by which the people may challenge a law passed by Congress, is triggered by a petition of three percent of registered voters within two years of the law's passage. If the public rejects the law, it is repealed. This is the check the original constitution never provided: the people may undo what their representatives do.

The Recall

Any elected federal official may be removed from office before their term ends by a recall petition signed by ten percent of voters in their district or state, followed by a recall election. The replacement is elected simultaneously on the same ballot so that no office is vacant and no appointment fills a democratic seat. The President may be recalled by petition of fifteen percent of registered voters.

The recall is not a mechanism for relitigating close elections or for harassing officials whose policies are unpopular with a minority. The petition threshold is set high enough to ensure that a recall represents genuine widespread dissatisfaction rather than organized minority opposition. The recall is the ultimate expression of the principle that elected officials serve at the pleasure of the people who elected them.

Protecting Elections from Manipulation

Disinformation

Coordinated disinformation campaigns in referendum contests and elections are treated as election fraud under this constitution. The distinction between protected political speech and election fraud is the distinction between the genuine expression of views, however mistaken, and the coordinated industrial production and distribution of false information for the purpose of corrupting democratic processes.

The enforcement of this distinction requires an independent electoral commission with genuine authority, adequate resources, and structural independence from the political parties whose conduct it regulates. The commission is not structured to deadlock along partisan lines. It has a clear mandate, clear authority, and real enforcement capacity.

Foreign Interference

Foreign governments and foreign nationals are prohibited from spending money on American elections or coordinating with American political campaigns. The prohibition applies to direct contributions, to independent expenditures, and to the provision of any thing of value to a campaign or to an organization that spends on elections. The enforcement of this prohibition is a matter of national security as well as electoral integrity and is treated with the seriousness that designation implies.

Voter Suppression

Any action by a government official or private person that is designed to prevent eligible voters from voting is a federal crime. The documented tactics of voter suppression, the closure of polling places in minority communities, the purging of voter rolls without adequate notice, the distribution of false information about polling locations and dates, the intimidation of voters at polling places, are all federal crimes subject to federal prosecution regardless of whether the state where they occur chooses to prosecute them.

The Voting Rights Act of 1965 is restored and strengthened. The preclearance requirement, gutted by the Supreme Court in 2013, is reinstated with a coverage formula that reflects current patterns of voter suppression rather than historical ones. Jurisdictions with documented patterns of voter suppression are required to obtain federal approval before making changes to their voting laws and procedures.

Civic Education

The vote is only as meaningful as the voter's ability to use it with genuine understanding of what they are deciding. Civic education is a constitutional obligation, described in detail in the education companion document. This document adds the specific electoral dimension: every person who reaches voting age has received education in how elections work, how to register, how to research candidates and ballot measures, how ranked choice voting works, and how the initiative and referendum processes work. The education is not partisan. It does not tell people how to vote. It tells them how voting works and why it matters.

The civic education obligation extends beyond schools. The government is obligated to provide voter information in accessible formats to every registered voter before every election: the candidates, their positions, the ballot measures, the fiscal impact assessments, the plain-language summaries. The voter who cannot access this information because of language, disability, or lack of internet access receives it through whatever channel reaches them. No voter is left without the information they need to participate.

The Transition

The transition from the current electoral system to the one this document describes requires changes at the federal level, the state level, and the local level simultaneously. Some changes, the abolition of the Electoral College and the establishment of ranked choice voting for federal elections, require federal action. Others, the redesign of primary systems and the implementation of automatic registration, require state action within federal standards. All of them require the political will to redesign a system that the people currently in power won under, which is the hardest kind of reform to achieve.

The transition is supported by federal funding for the administrative changes states are required to make, by technical assistance for election administrators implementing new systems, and by a timeline that allows for the testing and refinement of new processes before they are used in high-stakes elections.
