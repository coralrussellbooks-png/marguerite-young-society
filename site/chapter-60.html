To All My Darlings

Chapter 60

On Chapter 60 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 9, 2026

← Chapter 59 · Home · Chapter 61 →

Summary

Mr. Spitzer wonders whether he is really a musician or only a gambler like Peron, who read nothing but the sporting page, never went to a concert, and could never have imagined silence. A gambler would know how to call moose, and Mr. Spitzer remembers practicing moose calls on an empty city street at dusk until a great purple moose answered him through the fog; ever since, he has only whispered. A gambler would know duck calls too, and once, absent-minded in a windy avenue, Mr. Spitzer must have made one, for a flight of wild ducks came down and rushed past him, or through him, while he stood still like a lighthouse. He decides they were mandarin ducks blown far off course, exotic pilgrims to this shore. Their honking follows him for days or years, and he concludes that he need not write his elegy after all: the wild duck’s horn is his elegy.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a profound reflection on whether the protagonist, Mr. Spitzer, is truly a musician or merely a gambler who has deluded himself into believing in a lost musical career. This internal conflict is central where he is haunted by the “germinal music in his head,” yet increasingly obsessed with gambling. The imagery of cards (ace of clubs, spots on dice) and horse races evokes a chaotic, chance-driven world contrasted with the ordered art of music. The passage poses the question of identity and authenticity and whether his essence is musical or rooted in worldly, mundane obsessions like gambling. This sets the tone for the chapter’s exploration of perception and self-delusion.
  • The chapter imagines the gambler Mr. Spitzer might have been, who is really Peron: a man who read only the sporting page, ignored international, financial, and high society news, and never attended a concert in his life. Measuring himself against that man, Mr. Spitzer tests whether his own music is real. The gambler’s ignorance of “the value of a sand dollar or a Boston dollar or an English pound sterling” sets the world of the track against the world of money and high culture.
  • There is a symbolic and surreal moment where Mr. Spitzer practices moose calls in the city at dusk, only to hear the answering call of an actual moose which is an extraordinary, almost hallucinatory experience. The moose’s antlers are described as “looming like tree boughs through the blowing purple fog,” blending natural imagery with urban and cosmic elements (traffic lights, the Pleiades). This encounter blurs the boundary between reality and imagination, the natural and the supernatural, highlighting Mr. Spitzer’s liminal state between sanity and hallucination, music and noise, life and death.
  • The climax of the chapter is the vision of the wild ducks flying overhead: exotic “beautiful Asiatics” with crested heads and golden eyes, described in luminous, almost otherworldly colors. Mr. Spitzer perceives their honking calls as a “wild sonata,” a natural music that becomes his elegy. The ducks’ flight is metaphorically linked to transcendence, migration, and perhaps the soul’s journey. He imagines himself metamorphosed into wings, a silent observer caught between earth and sky. The passage conveys a sense of awe, mystery, and the dissolution of boundaries between self and nature, music and silence, sound and chaos.
  • Near the end, the chapter suggests that Mr. Spitzer’s elegy (the music he cannot write or remember) may be embodied in the calls of the wild ducks. This is a powerful metaphor for artistic creation as something wild, natural, and perhaps uncontrollable, rather than composed or intellectual. The elegy is not just a lament but an acceptance of the wild, untamed forces that accompany him “through the long reaches of the silence.”

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • Mr. Spitzer wonders whether he “had merely deceived himself by his pretensions to a lost musical career.” Why does he test himself against the gambler Peron?
  • “Sound shared the silence, but his dead brother could not have imagined the silence.” What does silence mean to Mr. Spitzer that it could not mean to Peron?
  • Young calls music “a path dividing sound from chaos.” How does the chapter’s own long first sentence find its way through chaos?

Characters

  • Peron never read “the obituary columns listing himself.” What does it mean to imagine a dead man who does not know he is dead?
  • After the moose answers, Mr. Spitzer “had only whispered for fear the great purple moose might be anywhere and answer.” What does this comic episode show about him?
  • Mr. Spitzer stands “like the burning of the lighthouse on the distant rock.” Who in his life has been the lighthouse, and who the birds?

Connections

  • The “great flutist who had caused the avalanche” returns from Chapters 58 and 59. Why does Young keep calling him back?
  • Mr. Spitzer hears “the starter’s music” again. How do horse racing and music twine together through the Spitzer chapters?
  • In Chapter 59 Mr. Spitzer could not finish his elegy; here “his elegy was this wild duck’s horn.” What has changed?

Reflection

  • The birds “have mistaken the lighthouse for the light, the reflection of a star.” What lights do people mistake for stars?
  • The wings roar “not so much past him as through him.” What would it mean for music to pass through a person rather than past?
  • Mr. Spitzer rebuilds the ducks from a few gleams, “even as a dead man might build the world.” How much of any memory is rebuilt this way?

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • Torn between his identity as a musician and as a gambler, Mr. Spitzer experiences profound self-doubt and alienation.
  • His focus on gambling and his distracted thoughts reveal a fragmented psyche, obsessed with trivial and profound matters simultaneously.
  • Despite his urban surroundings, he has a deep affinity for natural sounds, which evoke powerful emotional and spiritual responses.
  • Memories of his dead twin brother and lost musical compositions haunt him, reflecting a sense of unfinished business and loss.
  • Mr. Spitzer embodies the human struggle to find meaning through art, memory, and connection, even when confronted with mortality and oblivion.

Peron Spitzer

  • Peron appears as the gambler Mr. Spitzer measures himself against: a man who read only the sporting page, never attended a concert in his life, and could not have imagined silence.
  • He would have known how to call moose and wild ducks, and would have recognized the ducks’ horns “even if he had been in his grave.”

Research: Themes

Identity and Self-Perception

See: Identity and the Self

  • Mr. Spitzer is uncertain about whether he is truly a musician or merely a gambler who has deceived himself.
  • His self-questioning reveals a deep internal conflict and a search for meaning in his life.
  • The duality of musician versus gambler serves as a metaphor for the struggle between art and chance, order and chaos.

Music and Sound as Metaphor

See: Music, Sound, and Silence

  • Music is portrayed as a transcendent, intellectual force that shapes reality and the imagination.
  • The “music of the world” and the “music of the track” symbolize different aspects of life, artistic creativity versus raw, primal existence.
  • Mr. Spitzer reflects on musical composition, the clef, bar, and rhythm as pathways dividing sound from chaos, highlighting sound as a spiritual and intellectual phenomenon.

Memory, Imagination, and the Unconscious

See: Dreams and Imagination · Memory and the Past

  • The text weaves memories, hallucinations, and dreams, blurring boundaries between past and present, real and imagined.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s recollections of his dead brother and the imagined music he never wrote suggest themes of loss, regret, and the elusive nature of artistic creation.

Nature and the Wild

See: Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos

  • Calls of moose and wild ducks serve as recurring motifs, connecting Mr. Spitzer to the natural and primal world.
  • These animal calls symbolize communication beyond human language, evoking mystery and transcendence.
  • The vivid description of the wild ducks conveys a mystical beauty and the theme of otherworldly visitation.

Silence and Sound

See: Music, Sound, and Silence

  • Silence is portrayed as unimaginable to the dead brother, emphasizing the importance of sound as life and presence.
  • Sound and silence share a complex relationship, where sound moves “like the human spirit through heavens of imagined silence,” suggesting spiritual continuity beyond death.

Death and Transcendence

See: Death and Mortality

  • References to death, such as “the sleep of the dead,” “bones in an old bone-box,” and the leveling effect of death, permeate the text.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s experiences of hearing moose calls and wild duck honkings border on the supernatural, indicating a liminal state between life and death.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

The Ace of Clubs / Playing Cards

See: Cards, Games, and Chance

  • Symbolize chance, fate, and the gamble of life.
  • The falling ace and the spots on cards evoke the randomness and structure of existence.

The All Black Harpsichord and Instruments

See: Music and Instruments

  • Represent lost artistic potential and the melancholic beauty of forgotten music.
  • The “all black keys” and “viola da gamba” suggest a somber, elegiac tone.

Moose Calls and Antlers

See: Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos

  • Symbolize primal communication and a connection to a wild, untamed world.
  • The moose’s antlers “looming like tree boughs” create a haunting, majestic image of nature’s power.

Wild Ducks

See: Birds

  • Represent migration, passage, and the ephemeral beauty of life.
  • Their “crested heads and veiled gold eyes” suggest exoticism and a mystical presence.
  • Their flight and honking are likened to a “wild sonata,” connecting nature with music.

Traffic and Cityscape

See: The Bus and the Journey

  • The contrast between natural sounds and the urban environment highlights the tension between civilization and nature.
  • The “old cars” and “traffic streams” serve as a backdrop to the surreal, almost supernatural events.

Stars and Heavens

See: Moon and Stars

  • Stars are symbolic of eternity, guidance, and mystery.
  • The merging of all stars into “one great star” suggests unity and transcendence beyond earthly existence.

Research: Resources

  • “the pedal note prolonged and crying above a choir”: A pedal note, or pedal point, is a long-held note, usually in the bass, sounding on while the harmonies above it change; on the organ it is played with the feet. See Music and Instruments.
  • “the ace of clubs falling like a swallow from his sleeve”: A card sharp hides an ace up his sleeve, the source of the phrase for a secret advantage. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “seeing the cubistic dream player melt upon the trembling air”: The phrase refers metaphorically to Mr. Spitzer’s fragmented and multifaceted perception of himself and his life. The term “cubistic” evokes the art movement Cubism, where objects are depicted from multiple viewpoints simultaneously, resulting in fragmented and abstracted forms. The “cubistic dream player” suggests a person whose identity and experiences are fractured, seen from various angles that do not form a coherent whole, much like a cubist painting. Mr. Spitzer feels that he is composed of many disjointed parts or “selves,” and his memories and perceptions are scattered and overlapping, not fitting together into a unified reality. This fragmentation reflects his inner confusion and the blending of dream and reality that permeates the novel. This image captures Mr. Spitzer’s sense of dislocation and the complexity of his existence, as he struggles with his identity, the loss of his brother Peron, and the elusive nature of truth and memory. It is a symbolic way to portray the multiplicity and ambiguity of Mr. Spitzer’s consciousness and life experience, emphasizing the novel’s themes of fractured identity and the interplay of illusion and reality. See On the cubistic dream player.
    • The Cubist art movement, pioneered by Pablo Picasso and Georges Braque in the early 20th century, revolutionized art by rejecting traditional single-point perspective and instead depicting subjects from multiple viewpoints simultaneously. This approach resulted in paintings and sculptures that appear fragmented into abstract, geometric forms, emphasizing the flat, two-dimensional surface of the canvas. Notable women Cubist artists are:
    • Marie Laurencin: A French painter who met Georges Braque as a fellow student at the Académie Humbert and developed a distinct style with soft, pastel colors and graceful, flowing figures. Her work offered a lyrical and feminine counterpoint to the more traditional Cubist aesthetic.
    • María Blanchard: A Spanish painter who moved to Paris and became a significant figure in the Cubist movement. Despite being an important painter, she was often overlooked in historical accounts of the era, which were dominated by male artists.
    • Sonia Delaunay: While also known for her work in Orphism, Delaunay’s style was heavily influenced by Cubism and she was a key figure in the avant-garde.
    • Natalia Goncharova: A Russian avant-garde artist who, along with her partner Mikhail Larionov, was a founder of the Rayonist movement. Her work engaged with many modernist styles, including Cubism.
    • Lyubov Popova: A leading figure of the Russian avant-garde, Popova’s work moved through various phases of modernism, including Cubo-Futurism.
    • Aleksandra Exter: Studied art in Kiev and became a prominent avant-garde artist whose work became increasingly radical, mixing Cubist influences with other modernist ideas.
    • Mainie (Mary Harriet) Jellett (1897-1944): An Irish painter who studied with André Lhote and Albert Gleizes in Paris; her Decoration (1923) was among the first abstract paintings shown in Ireland.
    • The central idea of Cubism was to show objects as the mind comprehends them in their totality, rather than from a single, fixed viewpoint. This involved breaking down and reassembling objects into an abstract form to suggest their three-dimensional structure and their movement through time and space. Artists reduced figures and objects to fundamental geometric shapes such as cubes, spheres, and cones. This fragmentation of forms challenged traditional naturalistic representation. Cubists rejected the illusion of depth created by traditional perspective, instead highlighting the flatness of the canvas. Background and foreground often merge in their compositions. Cubism was significantly influenced by the late work of Post-Impressionist painter Paul Cézanne, who simplified nature into geometric forms, and by the abstract, stylized forms of African tribal masks and Iberian sculpture.
    • Compare Paul Cézanne’s Card Players (1890s) and Pablo Picasso’s Card Player (winter 1913-14, Museum of Modern Art), in which the player and his cards break into flat, overlapping planes. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “the wild fool, the solitary player playing on and on”: The joker, the fool of the deck, is often played wild; a card game for one is solitaire, or patience. See the joker in euchre in the Chapter 59 resources. See Cards, Games, and Chance.
  • “the starter’s music awakening him after so many years of sleep”: See the “Call to the Post” in the Chapter 59 resources.
  • “the value of a sand dollar”: A sand dollar is a flat, round sea urchin whose bleached skeleton washes up on beaches like a coin.
  • “golden shekels, talents, florins, drachmas”: The talent was the largest weight of money in the ancient world; in the parable, the servant who buried his one talent in the earth lost it (Matthew 25:14-30, KJV). The florin was first struck in gold in Florence in 1252. See The Bible.
  • “the writings of Boston music critics”: Boston’s critics were famous for severity: John Sullivan Dwight ran Dwight’s Journal of Music (1852-1881), and Philip Hale wrote the Boston Symphony’s program notes from 1901 to 1933.
  • “had never sat in a diamond horseshoe”: See the Diamond Horseshoe in the Chapter 48 resources.
  • “dresses of bone lace or bird-cages”: Bone lace is bobbin lace, worked on a pillow with bobbins once carved from bone. A bird-cage was the hooped steel crinoline of the 1850s and 1860s. See Clothing, Hats, and Skirts.
  • “whether they carry black umbrellas or white umbrellas”: See The Umbrella.
  • “that death which levels all”: “Sceptre and crown / Must tumble down, / And in the dust be equal made / With the poor crooked scythe and spade” (James Shirley, “Death the Leveller,” from The Contention of Ajax and Ulysses, 1659). See Horace’s pale Death in the Chapter 59 resources. See Death and Mortality.
  • “some poor old tramp who slept under the El”: The El is an elevated railway. New York’s Sixth Avenue El came down in 1939 and its Third Avenue El in 1955-56; Boston’s Washington Street Elevated ran from 1901 to 1987, a date after 1964.
  • “whistle like Mozart in the silent hours”: See Mozart in the Chapter 55 resources. At fourteen Mozart heard Allegri’s Miserere in the Sistine Chapel and wrote it out from memory (1770). See Music and Composers.
  • “Why should he think he was an authority on the harpsichord?”: The harpsichord plucks its strings with quills rather than striking them with hammers like the piano. See Music and Instruments.
  • “He would have known more about calling moose”: The chapter explores Mr. Spitzer’s deep existential and artistic struggles through metaphoric natural imagery and philosophical reflection. The moose call symbolizes the raw, natural world’s immediacy, contrasting with Spitzer’s intellectualized, elusive music, reflecting the novel’s broader themes of reality versus illusion, life versus death, and the search for meaning in a shifting, uncertain world. The passage highlights the intertwined fates of the twin brothers, the limits of perception, and the profound loneliness and ambiguity of human existence.
    • Hunters called moose with a horn of rolled birch bark. In Thoreau’s “Chesuncook” (The Maine Woods, 1864), the Penobscot guide Joe Aitteon “imitated the call of the moose,—ugh-ugh-ugh, or oo-oo-oo-oo.” See American Writers.
    • A bull moose’s antlers can spread five or six feet, wider than Young’s “four-foot spread.”
  • “causing the northern light beams to rustle”: People of the far North have long said the aurora rustles or crackles. Finnish researchers led by Unto Laine recorded such sounds in 2012 (after 1964). See Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos.
  • “the dim clusters of the Pleiades”: The Pleiades, the Seven Sisters, are a star cluster in Taurus; “Canst thou bind the sweet influences of Pleiades, or loose the bands of Orion?” (Job 38:31, KJV). See Astronomy and Greek Mythology.
  • “sound being an intellectual thing”: Compare William Blake: “For a Tear is an Intellectual Thing” (“The Grey Monk,” c. 1803, and Jerusalem). See Romantic Poets.
  • “the great gold-eyed snow owl”: The snowy owl of the Arctic has yellow eyes; in some winters snowy owls come south in numbers to fields, shores, and airports. See Birds.
  • “old owl car bumping down the dusk”: An owl car was a streetcar that ran through the night; late-night transit is still called owl service.
  • “passing the horse fountains”: Between 1902 and 1915 Hermon Lee Ensign’s National Humane Alliance gave about 125 granite watering troughs to American cities, for horses, dogs, cats, and people. See Horses and Carriages.
  • “calling the wild ducks out of the clouds”: The different references to ducks are multifaceted symbols that evoke loss, innocence, mourning, and the liminal state between life and death. They enrich the emotional landscape, highlighting suffering and the fragile nature of existence amidst the novel’s broader themes of decay, memory, and transcendence. See Birds.
  • “that great flutist who had caused the avalanche”: The “great flutist who had caused the avalanche” may refer to the god Kokopelli. Kokopelli is the humpbacked flute player of the rock art of the American Southwest, a bringer of fertility and rain. See On Kokopelli. See also Amphion in the Chapter 58 resources.
  • “the all black harpsichord with the all black keys”: Many old harpsichords reverse the piano’s colors, with black natural keys and white sharps; Young’s is black throughout. See Chopin’s “Black Key” Étude in the Chapter 58 resources. See Music and Instruments.
  • “the viola da gamba”: The viola da gamba, or viol, is the “viol for the leg,” held between the knees and bowed underhand. It has frets and usually six strings. The viols are a separate family from the violins; they flourished in the Renaissance and Baroque and were revived in the twentieth century. See On the viola da gamba. See Music and Instruments.
  • “lute with an angel’s head as screw”: Old viols and lutes often end in a carved head in place of a scroll, a lion, a woman, or an angel.
    • A “lute with an angel’s head” is not a specific type of musical instrument. Angels, cherubs, and putti playing the lute are also a common motif in Renaissance and Baroque art.
  • “tortoise-shell violin”: In the Homeric Hymn to Hermes, the newborn god makes the first lyre from a tortoise shell, stretching strings of sheep gut across it. See Greek Mythology and Music and Instruments.
  • “the polysyllabic horns of the wild ducks”: Young’s “polysyllabic horns” are the ducks’ calls of many syllables, which hunters imitate with wooden duck calls. See Birds.
  • “like an unfinished fugue running through his mind”: A “fugue” has two main meanings: a complex musical composition where a theme is repeated and interwoven by multiple voices, and a temporary mental state of amnesia and wandering, often associated with dissociative disorders. Musically, it involves a single theme being introduced by one voice and then imitated by others in a structured, imitative style. Psychologically, a dissociative fugue is a condition where an individual forgets their past and may travel, only to have no memory of the period later. Bach’s Art of Fugue breaks off in its last fugue; he died in 1750 before finishing it. See On fugue. See Music and Composers.
  • “for he had forgotten many fugues”: Lethe is both a river in the Greek underworld and the personification of forgetfulness and oblivion. Souls who drank from the River Lethe would forget their past lives, and this concept of forgetfulness is used in a broader, metaphorical sense as well. See On the River Lethe. See Greek Mythology.
  • “many antiphonies”: “Antiphonies” are a type of responsive singing or music between two groups, creating a “call and response” effect. The term can also refer to a verse sung before and after a psalm or hymn, often used in religious services. See On antiphonies. See Music and Composers.
  • “many winding horns”: “Winding horns” are a type of wind instrument, historically made from animal horns or metal, used for signaling over long distances in activities like hunting, battle, and ceremonies. Also known as blowing horns or war horns, they produce a single note, and different meanings are conveyed through rhythms and speed. See Music and Instruments.
  • “the old Locomobile, circa 1912”: Locomobile, circa 1912 may refer to the Locomobile Company of America which was one of the earliest manufacturers of cars before the assembly line.
    • The Locomobile Company of America made steam cars from 1899, then luxury gasoline cars; its racer “Old 16” won the Vanderbilt Cup in 1908.
  • “skipped a beat like a trip hammer”: A trip hammer is a heavy power hammer lifted by a cam and dropped again and again, used in forges and mills.
  • “almost angelic wings”: Compare the cherubim of Solomon’s temple: “the wing of the one touched the one wall, and the wing of the other cherub touched the other wall; and their wings touched one another in the midst of the house” (1 Kings 6:27, KJV). See Angels and Archangels and The Bible.
  • “a flight of mandarins driven by non-adventitious winds far off their usual course”: The mandarin duck of East Asia is the most brilliantly colored of ducks, the drake crested and with orange “sails” on his back. In China a pair of mandarin ducks stands for faithful married love. See Birds.
  • “the dappled light cast by a dying dolphin”: See Byron’s dying dolphin in the Chapter 56 resources. See Romantic Poets.
  • “those poor birds of passage who, with their eyes blinded by the light”: Lighthouse keepers reported hundreds of migrating birds killed against the lantern in a single foggy night; the light draws night migrants, above all in fog. See Birds and Lamps, Candles, and Light.
  • “exotic Pilgrims to this shore”: The “exotic Pilgrims” are the mandarin ducks, the “beautiful Asiatics” Mr. Spitzer pictures as pilgrims arriving at a distant shore. These “exotic Pilgrims” are portrayed as radiant, colorful figures with gold-tipped wings and delicate features, drifting and soaring through great clouds and mist. They symbolize travelers or visitors from faraway, mysterious lands, whose presence is both enchanting and otherworldly. The phrase captures a sense of wonder and the uncanny, highlighting the contrast between familiar reality and a dreamlike vision filled with beauty and mystery. These pilgrims evoke a feeling of journeying beyond ordinary experience, aligning with the novel’s recurring themes of transition between worlds, dreams, and the mystical or spiritual journey. The description blends natural imagery with poetic and symbolic language to suggest a realm beyond the mundane.
    • The capital P recalls the Pilgrims who landed at Plymouth in 1620, and the chapter’s Boston dollars and Boston critics point toward New England.
  • “his elegy was this wild duck’s horn”: Compare Henrik Ibsen’s The Wild Duck (1884), whose wounded duck, kept in a garret, comes to stand for lives sustained by illusion.
