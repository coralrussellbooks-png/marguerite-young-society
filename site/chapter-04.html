To All My Darlings

Chapter 4

Summary

For years after the disappearance, Vera grieves by the shore. Her hair is cropped and her body heavy, and she drifts in sleep and dreaming. She tries to write a book, lights fires on the beach, and questions fishermen and sailors. Catherine decides her daughter is losing her mind and offers the only cure she knows: opium and a dreamless paradise of eternal youth. Vera refuses, and years later travels west by train from Grand Central Station and then by bus, stopping short of Miss MacIntosh’s Iowa in Indiana. She recalls a young woman who jumped from the Brooklyn Bridge on her way to be married, clutching the suitcase that held her wedding veil. On the train, a fat, soft-faced lady warns her that the Middle West is desolate, a place of old people who are deaf because they do not wish to hear.

Key passages

From the research

  • Vera begins by describing a long period dominated by the dream of Miss MacIntosh, a lost and beloved figure whose disappearance profoundly affected her. She is trapped in a somnolent, vacant state, disconnected from reality, with her mother interpreting this as a loss of sanity. This passage highlights themes of grief, memory, and the struggle to hold onto reality after loss. The dream is both a refuge and a prison, reflecting Vera’s internal conflict.
  • Catherine’s response to Vera’s mental state is a complex act of love and resignation. She accepts Vera’s “death in the midst of my life,” urging her to remain still and give up on living fully, which symbolizes a surrender to despair and the avoidance of life’s harsh realities. This acceptance also suggests a generational transmission of defeat and illness, emphasizing the themes of mortality, mental illness, and the desire for oblivion.
  • Vera recounts her mother Catherine’s promises of eternal youth and beauty, describing a fantastical vision filled with precious jewels, supernatural servants, and luxurious attire. These images contrast sharply with Vera’s own physical decline and sorrow, underscoring the tension between illusion and harsh reality. The mention of the “opium paradise” and “sleeping wakefulness” evokes a state of escapism, where illusion replaces the world.
  • Despite years of despair, Vera has not given up searching for Miss MacIntosh, symbolizing a search for meaning, connection, and a normal life. The dream of a simple, domestic, and ordinary life with knitting needles in the sand, building a house of driftwood, and lighting a fire on the beach stands in stark contrast to her actual existence. This passage expresses the human longing for stability, belonging, and the “other America” of routine and community.
  • Vera recalls Miss MacIntosh’s descriptions of Iowa, a place of cold, ordinary, and finite life, filled with hardships and community ties. This reflection points to the inescapability of origin and the harsh realities that contrast with dreams of escape. The cold imagery of frozen ears, frozen horses, and frozen smoke symbolizes emotional and spiritual freezing, a life stunted by environment and circumstance.
  • A fat, soft-faced lady warns Vera about the desolation awaiting her destination. She describes it as a place “out of this world,” devoid of social life, communication, human contact, and filled with decay and death. This forewarning encapsulates themes of isolation, the death of community, and the futility of Vera’s quest. It also reflects the fear of entering a place where life is “chilling,” “numbing,” and where the “golden bowl was broken.”
  • The chapter recounts the tragic end of a young woman who jumped from the Brooklyn Bridge clutching her suitcase, her veil packed inside, disappearing into the dark waters. This vivid and haunting image captures the despair and finality of fate, especially those of the lower classes, and reinforces themes of loss, hopelessness, and the elusive nature of identity and love.
  • Toward the end, Vera expresses a yearning for a life filled with ordinary joys like family picnics, blossoming orchards, laughter of children, and a natural death after a long life. This vision contrasts with the dark hallucinations and self-deception that have plagued her. It symbolizes hope, renewal, and the possibility of healing from eternal mourning.
  • Vera questions whether she has made the “wrong choice” or if there is no real choice at all, reflecting on the many “turnings” and “windings” of her journey. This existential doubt touches on themes of destiny, free will, and the search for meaning amid confusion and despair.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • Catherine offers Vera “my death in the midst of my life” and promises that “The years should bring me youth.” Why does she think stillness is the kindest gift she can give?
  • Vera dreams “of nothing but a shoemaker’s awl, an old tramp’s shoe which needed mending, a hammer, a nail.” Why does a rich girl dream of tools?
  • Vera tries to write a book but cannot compete with “four snails crawling across the open pages,” whose “writing was more beautiful than mine.” Why does she leave the book by the tide?
  • Miss MacIntosh’s Iowa is all frozen, down to the farmer brought ashore with his hands frozen to the reins. Then Vera says, “But this was not Iowa.” Whose Iowa is it?

Characters

  • Catherine’s offer is called “my mother’s greatest act of love, the only sign that she had a child.” How can a gift of oblivion be love?
  • The dead girl accepts the proposals of men she has never seen and describes herself differently to each. How is she like Vera, who sets out for a life she has read about in seed catalogues?
  • The fat lady on the train calls the Middle West “the jumping-off place, the end of the world.” Is she a false prophet, or does she know something Vera does not?

Connections

  • Vera calls herself “a Lazarus of these awakening joys.” Who or what does she hope will call her out of the tomb?
  • The chapter promises “the silver, the golden, never the leaden bells.” How do bells mark the stages of a life here and in the chapters before?
  • Miss MacIntosh “had given her life to prove that dreams were false,” yet Vera now lives by dreaming of her. How does the chapter turn the nursemaid herself into a dream?

Reflection

  • Vera says she came “because only of the unknown, because only of that which I had reached for, life, the unreachable.” Can a person set out for a place they have only imagined?
  • The old people are deaf because “they did not wish to hear,” their ears filled with “the roarings of the world inside.” What do people stop hearing when grief or age fills them?
  • “Time died when my love died.” How does grief change a person’s sense of time?

Research: Characters

Vera Cartwheel

  • Deeply affected by Miss MacIntosh’s disappearance or death.
  • Battles between despair, illusions, and the desire for a meaningful life.
  • Struggles with her mother Catherine’s perception of her sanity.
  • Embodies themes of mourning, hope, and transformation.

Miss MacIntosh

  • The lost love, symbolizing a “normal life” and idealized domesticity.
  • Her disappearance is mysterious and traumatic.
  • Associated with innocence and unattainable dreams.

Catherine Cartwheel

  • Acts as both caretaker and enforcer of oblivion.
  • Seeks to protect Vera by encouraging a withdrawal from life.
  • Represents acceptance of death in life and resignation.

Fat, soft-faced lady on the train

  • Provides a warning about the desolation of Vera’s destination.
  • Symbolizes the voice of experience and harsh reality.
  • Contrasts with Vera’s hope and romanticism.

Research: Themes

Loss and Mourning

See: Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • Vera is consumed by the loss of Miss MacIntosh.
  • Grief manifests as a somnolent, almost catatonic state, with Vera’s body described as “slothful and immense” and her mind “employed by nothing but the dream” of normalcy.
  • The loss is so profound that time seems to stop and Vera experiences a “long sleep of empty years.”

Reality vs. Illusion

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Catherine believes Vera is losing her sanity and attempts to have Vera “stilled and put away” through opium or drug-induced sleep, offering her an “opium paradise” as an escape from reality.
  • Catherine creates a vision of immortal beauty and oblivion for her daughter, likening her eventual state to an “embalmed corpse” or a nymph in a fairy grotto as a metaphor for eternal stasis and escape from pain.
  • Vera contrasts this illusion with her own experience, becoming “the creature of my own sorrow,” a “fat, stupid, indolent girl” whose sorrow isolates her from life.

Search for Meaning and Life

See: The Search for Meaning

  • Despite despair, Vera clings to memories and symbols of Miss MacIntosh, such as the ivory knitting needles found in the sand, and lights a fire on the beach to signal her lost love.
  • Vera undertakes a physical quest, talking to fishermen and sailors, symbolizing a search for connection and understanding in the vastness of the sea and life.
  • Ultimately, Vera seeks to reconcile with life, imagining a “charmed life” in a familiar, ordinary world of nature, community, and simple joys, such as family picnics, wildflowers, and children’s laughter.

The Burden of the Past and Hope for Renewal

See: Memory and the Past

  • Vera acknowledges the haunting presence of past memories and illusions but expresses hope for a new life free from these obsessions.
  • She envisions a future where the “dead past would be buried,” and she would awaken to the “blessed events” of life, including natural death after a long life filled with love and community.
  • The chapter returns again and again to the need to turn away from the insubstantial past toward the reality of life, even though it is fraught with disappointment and loss.

Further notes from the text

  • Catherine’s offer to Vera comes with a whole philosophy of not living: it is better to think of walking than to walk, better to think of dancing than to dance, and Vera should lie still as “the companion corpse of her eternal illness,” with an immortal beauty as her reward.
  • Years after the disappearance Vera finds Miss MacIntosh’s ivory knitting needles in the sand. She searches by sailboat and questions fishermen drying their nets, lobstermen red as the lobsters they trap, and codfish fishermen back from the Grand Banks.
  • Vera names herself formally here: daughter of Catherine Helena Cartwheel, née Snowden, “and of a father unknown, perhaps of many fathers.” She explains her own name, Vera, as standing for truth, and imagines the Midwest as a place where everyone will greet her by it.
  • Before she reaches Iowa she imagines it frozen: birds dying on crystal branches, smoke frozen in the chimneys, a farmer whose wagon went through the ice of a lake and who came ashore in spring with his hands frozen to the reins and his four horses rising through the foam. Then she admits this was not Iowa at all.
  • The bride who jumps from the Brooklyn Bridge had lost her father at three and was traveling a thousand miles to marry a stranger with a waxed mustache. She fell clutching her straw suitcase with her crushed veil inside. The waxed mustache links her to Vera’s dream bridegroom in Chapter 10.
  • The fat lady on the train adds folk consolations to her warnings: there are as many fish in the sea as were ever caught, and “there was a second husband for everybody.”

Research: Resources

  • “For those who died in the midst of life would never die”: The burial service of the Book of Common Prayer says, “In the midst of life we are in death,” from the medieval Latin chant Media vita in morte sumus. Catherine turns it around: Vera must accept “my death in the midst of my life.”
  • “She twirled her white umbrella in her swan boat”: The swan boat in the Boston Public Garden is real and has been in service since 1877. See Swan boats (Ch 4).
  • “Sometimes the sound of the sea was faded to a single cicada’s cry”: Periodical cicadas spend 13 or 17 years underground and then emerge by the millions in the same spring, filling the woods of the Middle West with their shrill song.
  • “not even if I walked on stilts like the stilt birds”: The black-necked stilt is a slender shorebird of marshes and salt pans whose very long pink legs let it wade deeper than other birds. See Birds.
  • “a poor invalid sleeping in a charity ward or under the roofless heavens”: Catherine repeats a fear from Chapter 2, where “Sometimes she thought she was a poor patient sleeping in a charity ward.” See the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “the inexhaustible jewels of Golconda”: Golconda, in southern India, was the market for the diamond fields that produced the Koh-i-Noor and the Hope diamond, and its name came to mean any source of endless wealth.
  • “filagreed coral reefs, sapphire pavements”: In Exodus 24:10 the elders of Israel see the God of Israel, “and there was under his feet as it were a paved work of a sapphire stone, and as it were the body of heaven in his clearness.” See The Bible.
  • “like a nymph in a mountain cave . . . the fairy grotto where the lone traveller is lured”: In Keats’s “La Belle Dame sans Merci” (1819), a knight is lured by a fairy lady to “her elfin grot” and wakes alone on a cold hillside. See Romantic Poets.
  • “the opium paradise which should bring the power of wakeful sleep”: Thomas De Quincey’s Confessions of an English Opium-Eater (1821) described the dreams and torments of opium, and Charles Baudelaire’s Artificial Paradises (1860) took up the same theme. See Opium.
  • “I should be the greatest debutante of any season”: A debutante was a young woman formally presented to society, usually at a ball at the start of the winter season, to show that she was ready for marriage. Her “coming-out party” was the great event of a rich girl’s youth.
  • “white satin slippers with paper soles such as are worn by the dead who have not far to travel”: Undertakers sold burial slippers with soles of cardboard or paper, made only to be seen in the coffin.
  • “or perhaps a thousand canaries blown here by the storm and singing in the candle flames”: In Chapter 2 Catherine’s canary Marco Polo flew out through an open transom into a thunderstorm. See the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “the routine of Monday following Sunday, Tuesday following Monday, the washings on the lines . . . baking day”: The old household week ran by a fixed round of chores, washing on Monday, ironing on Tuesday, mending on Wednesday, and baking later in the week, as in the nursery rhyme, “They that wash on Monday / Have all the week to dry.”
  • “knitting for the mission at some far Arctic post”: Church women across America knitted mittens, sweaters, and hoods for missions such as the Grenfell Mission of Labrador and Newfoundland, founded by the doctor Wilfred Grenfell in the 1890s.
  • “I did not dream I lived in marble halls, for I lived in marble halls”: The line turns the song “I Dreamt I Dwelt in Marble Halls” from Michael Balfe’s opera The Bohemian Girl (1843), in which a lost noble girl raised by Gypsies dreams of the riches she was born to.
  • “the red-faced lobster-trappers red as the lobsters they trapped”: Live lobsters are a dark greenish brown. They turn bright red only when cooked, as the heat frees the red pigment that a protein in the shell had kept hidden.
  • “codfish fishermen returning from the Great Banks”: The Grand Banks off Newfoundland were for centuries the richest cod grounds in the world. In Young’s day the schooners of Gloucester and Boston still sailed to the Banks for cod, and many never came home. Overfishing later collapsed the cod, and Canada closed the fishery in 1992.
  • “to sailors of the deep, to all who were wayfarers of the sea”: Psalm 107:23-24 reads, “They that go down to the sea in ships, that do business in great waters; These see the works of the Lord, and his wonders in the deep.”
  • “the night shrouded the night of the soul”: The phrase recalls “the dark night of the soul,” from the poem of the sixteenth-century Spanish mystic Saint John of the Cross, the soul’s passage through darkness and desolation toward God.
  • “that was why she wore ear muffs”: Earmuffs were invented in 1873 by Chester Greenwood of Farmington, Maine, a fifteen-year-old whose ears froze while he skated. Miss MacIntosh wore hers against Iowa winters so cold that “the children’s ears were frozen.”
  • “when the great boomers were booming”: The ice of a frozen lake booms and groans as it cracks and shifts with the changing temperature, especially in spring as it begins to break up.
  • “the promised land of which I had read in catalogues of wild flowers and seed catalogues”: The Promised Land is the land God promised Israel, “a land flowing with milk and honey” (Exodus 3:8). Seed catalogues such as W. Atlee Burpee’s, begun in 1876, brought pictures of gardens and orchards to farm families across the country. See Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest.
  • “There would be cherry and apple and peach and persimmon trees”: The wild persimmon grows across southern Indiana, where its fruit, sweet only when fully ripe, often after the first frost, is made into persimmon pudding. Mitchell, Indiana, has held a Persimmon Festival since 1946.
  • “old rail fences broken down by the wild mares”: Split-rail fences, built of logs split lengthwise, zigzagged across the frontier Midwest. Abraham Lincoln grew up in southern Indiana from 1816 to 1830, and his campaign of 1860 sold him as “the rail-splitter.”
  • “All people would greet me by my name, Vera, that which stands for truth”: Vera means “true” in Latin and “faith” in Russian. See the Chapter 1 resources.
  • “I would be a Lazarus of these awakening joys”: Lazarus of Bethany, brother of Martha and Mary, was raised from the dead by Jesus after being dead for four days. This miracle is a central event in the Gospel of John (John 11). The story of Lazarus’s raising highlights the close relationship between Jesus and his friends, as well as Jesus’s power over death. The miracle was a powerful sign of Jesus’s divine authority and his identity as the Messiah as well as a symbol of the promise of eternal life through faith in Jesus. Lazarus in this context represents Vera’s yearning for a new beginning, a restored life beyond the illusions and failures of the past, where she can experience reality as it is lived by ordinary people, with the natural rhythms of life and death, love, and community. See Lazarus of Bethany (Ch 4).
  • “births and weddings and wedding anniversaries, natural death at the end of a long life, the silver, the golden”: Silver and golden weddings mark the twenty-fifth and fiftieth anniversaries of a marriage, a custom that began in Germany, where a wife was given a wreath of silver or of gold.
  • “the silver, the golden, never the leaden bells”: Bells in this chapter symbolize the presence of death and the funeral rites permeating the house, the relentless passage of time and the cycle of life and death, a link between the material and spiritual worlds, calling attention to the unseen and the spectral, the deep, underlying melancholy that shadows the characters’ lives and the house itself. Edgar Allan Poe’s poem “The Bells” (1849) moves through the same stages of life, from silver sleigh bells and golden wedding bells to brazen alarm bells and the iron bells of the funeral. See Bells (Ch 4) and Edgar Allan Poe.
  • “the burning brimstone butterfly and the pebble moth”: The brimstone butterfly is named for its sulfur-yellow wings, and some think the butter-colored brimstone gave the word butterfly its name. The pebble prominent is a grey-brown moth of willows and poplars. See Butterflies and Moths.
  • “a ribbon to the youngest baby, a medal to the oldest citizen”: County fairs and the Indiana State Fair, held since 1852, gave ribbons for babies, livestock, pies, and quilts, and the small-town celebration honored its oldest citizen.
  • “from the insubstantial fabric of old days, old nights”: The phrase returns from Chapter 3, where Miss MacIntosh “dismissed the insubstantial fabric of the dream,” an echo of Prospero in The Tempest. See the Chapter 3 resources.
  • “because of a dead girl’s love letters strewn on the floor of a room like a cell”: The dead girl first appears in Chapter 1, where Vera lists her among the reasons for her journey. Here her story is told in full. See the Chapter 1 resources.
  • “each accepting the proposal of a man she had never seen”: Matrimonial newspapers and lonely hearts clubs printed letters from men and women seeking marriage, often farmers in remote country, and many couples married after corresponding without ever having met.
  • “marriage, the union of body with soul, children, chickens, a little farm with a vine-covered gate”: The reality for women at that time was they had to be married or they were nothing. There were no other prospects. Marriage and a family was the most important event in a woman’s life and a wedding was every girl’s dream until very recently in the United States.
  • “waxen brides in their wedding gowns with their long trains and veils”: The white wedding gown became the fashion after Queen Victoria wore white satin at her wedding in 1840. The dead girl has “no father who would give her away,” the old custom in which the bride passes from her father to her husband.
  • “who wore a waxed mustache, an old-fashioned handlebar”: The stranger’s handlebar matches the one in “her portrait at the age of three in the arms of her father who had worn an old-fashioned handlebar.” She is going a thousand miles to marry a man who looks like the father she lost.
  • “her mother who had been an Irish wash woman”: Irish immigrant women, many of them fleeing the famine of the 1840s, made up a large share of the laundresses and domestic servants in nineteenth-century American cities.
  • “under a shower of rice and old shoes”: Guests threw rice at a wedding couple as a wish for fertility and plenty, and old shoes were thrown after them or tied to their carriage for luck.
  • “for Grand Central Station”: The Chapter 2 resources tell the history of Grand Central. See Grand Central Station (Ch 4).
  • “she had jumped from Brooklyn Bridge”: The first man known to jump from the Brooklyn Bridge, Robert Odlum, died doing it in 1885, two years after the bridge opened, and many have taken their lives there since. Hart Crane, whose long poem The Bridge celebrates it, drowned himself in 1932 by leaping from a ship in the Gulf of Mexico. See The Brooklyn Bridge (Ch 4) and Suicide in History and Literature.
  • “she would go to Boise or Reno, where she had gone to get her divorces”: In 1931 Nevada cut the residency required for a divorce to six weeks, and Idaho soon matched it. Reno became the divorce capital of America, and its “divorce ranches” housed women waiting out their weeks. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “there were just as many fishes in the sea as ever were caught”: The old proverb, “There are plenty more fish in the sea,” is the usual consolation for a lost love.
  • “there were bats in every broken belfry”: To have bats in the belfry, an American expression from about 1900, is to be crazy, the bats flitting about in an empty head.
  • “there were funeral wreaths hanging on all the doors”: A wreath or a band of black crepe hung on the front door announced a death in the house and asked passersby for quiet. See Weddings and Funerals.
  • “The voice of the turtle-dove was not heard in all that land, for the golden bowl was broken and the wheel broken at the cistern”: The lady reverses the Song of Solomon, “the voice of the turtle is heard in our land” (2:12), and quotes Ecclesiastes 12:6, “or the golden bowl be broken, or the pitcher be broken at the fountain, or the wheel broken at the cistern.” That chapter of Ecclesiastes describes old age and failing senses, “and all the daughters of musick shall be brought low,” which fits the deaf old people. Henry James took the title of his novel The Golden Bowl (1904) from it. See Ecclesiastes.
  • “Nobody knew what time it was, for all the clocks were stopped”: An old custom stopped the clocks of a house at the moment of a death. In Dickens’s Great Expectations, Miss Havisham stopped every clock at twenty minutes to nine, the moment she was jilted on her wedding day. See Clocks and Watches.
  • “carrying old-fashioned ear trumpets which they did not turn on”: Before electric hearing aids, the hard of hearing held horn-shaped ear trumpets to their ears to gather sound. Beethoven used them as his deafness grew. See Silence and Deafness.
  • “their ears were mere empty shells filled with no roarings of the outside world”: The image recalls the Chapter 3 entry on the roar heard in a shell, the sound of the room caught inside. Here the shells are empty. See the Chapter 3 resources.
  • “they had taken quinine to cure their malaria”: Quinine is an alkaloid, a naturally occurring compound, extracted from the bark of the cinchona tree. Quinine was historically and remains an important medication for treating malaria. Quinine’s bitter taste makes it a component of tonic water and some other beverages. Historically, this usage originated from mixing medicinal quinine with soda and sugar to mask its bitterness for malaria prevention in tropical regions. Quinine here is both a literal reference to the antimalarial drug and a symbolic element illustrating unintended suffering and the complex nature of healing and illness in the novel’s landscape. Large doses of quinine cause cinchonism, whose symptoms include ringing in the ears and hearing loss, so the cure really could have deafened them. See Quinine (Ch 4).
  • “caused by the festerings in the river bottoms, the marsh butterflies”: Malaria, called the ague, plagued the river bottoms of nineteenth-century Indiana and Illinois. People blamed the bad air of the marshes until Ronald Ross showed in 1897 that mosquitoes carry the disease. The Chapter 3 resources explain malaria’s name, mal aria, “bad air.”

Vocabulary

Awl
A small pointed tool for piercing leather, used by shoemakers.
Belfry
A bell tower, or the room in a tower where the bells hang.
Brackish
Of water: slightly salty, as where a river meets the sea.
Brimstone
Sulfur. The brimstone butterfly is named for its sulfur-yellow wings.
Cat’s eye
A gemstone, such as chrysoberyl, with a band of light across it like the slit of a cat’s eye.
Cistern
A tank for storing water, often underground.
Crow’s feet
The small wrinkles at the outer corners of the eyes.
Debutante
A young woman making her formal entrance into society.
Diadem
A jeweled crown or headband worn as a sign of royalty.
Ear trumpet
A horn-shaped tube held to the ear to help the hard of hearing.
Ermine
The white winter fur of the stoat, used to trim the robes of kings and nobles.
Ethereal
Light, airy, and delicate, as if not of this world; heavenly.
Festering
Rotting and oozing; growing more corrupt.
Filigree
Delicate ornamental work of fine twisted wire. Young writes “filagreed.”
Fire opal
A red, orange, or yellow opal with flashes of color.
Fishmonger
A seller of fish.
Foolhardy
Recklessly bold.
Freighter
A ship that carries cargo.
Handlebar
A mustache with long ends curled upward like the handlebars of a bicycle.
Hawking
Hunting with trained hawks or falcons.
Hectagonal
Young’s word, probably a slip for hexagonal (six-sided) or heptagonal (seven-sided). Quartz crystals grow in six-sided columns.
Hoar-frost
White frost formed as feathery ice crystals.
Husbanded
Kept carefully; conserved.
Ice floe
A sheet of floating ice.
Imperceptibly
So slowly or slightly as to go unnoticed.
Indolent
Lazy; avoiding effort.
Lapis lazuli
A deep-blue stone, ground by painters to make the pigment ultramarine.
Matrimony
Marriage.
Moonstone
A milky, pearly-blue gemstone.
Née
Born; used before a woman’s maiden name. Young writes “nee.”
Negligee
A woman’s light, flowing dressing gown.
Noxious
Harmful or poisonous.
Oblivion
The state of being forgotten, or of being unaware of everything.
Owlishly
Like an owl; staring wide-eyed and solemn.
Prism
A clear piece of glass with flat sides that splits light into the colors of the rainbow.
Scudding
Moving fast before the wind.
Slumberous
Sleepy; inducing sleep.
Somnolent
Sleepy; causing sleep.
Spar
A pole used as a ship’s mast, boom, or yard. Spar is also the name for certain shiny, easily split crystalline minerals, such as Iceland spar.
Squall
A sudden, violent gust of wind, often with rain or snow.
Tallow
Animal fat used for candles and soap. Tallow-colored is a dull, yellowish white.
Tributary
A stream or river that flows into a larger one.
Turtle-dove
A small wild dove. The “turtle” of the Bible’s “voice of the turtle” is this bird.
Undulating
Rising and falling in waves.
Vapid
Dull and empty of life or interest.
Vaunted
Boasted of; praised highly.
Wayfarer
A traveler, especially on foot.
