  • “If you did not know Theobald, how will you know her?”: The Ship of Theseus, also known as Theseus’s Paradox, is a paradox and a common thought experiment about whether an object is the same object after having all of its original components replaced over time, typically one after the other. In Greek mythology, Theseus, the mythical king of the city of Athens, rescued the children of Athens from King Minos after slaying the minotaur and then escaped onto a ship going to Delos. Each year, the Athenians would commemorate this by taking the ship on a pilgrimage to Delos to honor Apollo. A question was raised by ancient philosophers: If no pieces of the original made up the current ship, was it still the Ship of Theseus? Furthermore, if it was no longer the same, when had it ceased existing as the original ship? In contemporary philosophy, the thought experiment has applications to the philosophical study of identity over time. Within the contemporary philosophy of mind, it has inspired a variety of proposed solutions and concepts regarding the persistence of personal identity. The Ship of Theseus metaphor in the chapter underscores the novel’s ongoing exploration of the tension between permanence and change, illusion and reality, and how people understand themselves and the world around them in a mutable existence.

  • Weaving plays a significant role in the mythologies of various cultures, often symbolizing creation, destiny, and even the weaving of life itself. In many stories, weaving is attributed to deities or individuals who control fate or have supernatural abilities. For example, the Greek Moirae (Fates) weave the tapestry of human lives, and the Norse goddess Frigg is associated with weaving and Seidr magic, which involves weaving destiny. Greek mythology: Athena, Arachne, Moirae (Fates), Penelope, Philomela. Norse mythology: Frigg, The Norns. Other mythologies: Neith, Laima, Paivatar and Kuutar, Spider Woman, Arianrhod. Weaving has symbolic meanings: creation, fate, communication, transformation, supernatural abilities. Weaving carries deep symbolic significance related to creation, life, consciousness, and fate in the chapter. It explores metaphors of weaving as a cosmic and existential process, intertwining themes of life and death, illusion and reality, and the complex interrelations of souls and identities. Weaving is portrayed as a divine or cosmic act, with references to a "spindle of creative flame" that sleeps in eternal darkness, weaving bodies, souls, and dreams. This suggests that life and identity are constructed like a tapestry or garment, woven from countless threads representing experiences, memories, and spiritual elements. The chapter implies that individual identity is not singular but composed of many intertwined selves, memories, and possibilities. Weaving here symbolizes the complexity and multiplicity of being, with "two souls" or "many souls" merging and diverging in an intricate pattern, reflecting the fragmented and overlapping nature of consciousness and existence. The act of weaving also relates to the tension between illusion and reality. The woven fabric can be seen as a metaphor for the illusions that make up human experience and the "fabric of self-deception" that both sustains and confines individuals. This weaving of illusions is necessary but also a source of existential uncertainty. The chapter suggests that life and death, beginnings and endings, are not linear but cyclical and intertwined like threads in a weave. Death is not simply an end but part of the ongoing process of creation and dissolution, much like the continuous interlacing of threads in fabric. The metaphor also points to human limitations in understanding the full "pattern" or "design" of life. The weaving is often hidden, complex, and beyond full human comprehension, alluding to the mysterious forces (possibly divine) that govern existence and the unknowability of ultimate truths. It is a rich, multifaceted symbol that encapsulates the novel's exploration of existence as a complex, interwoven pattern of life, death, illusion, and identity, where boundaries between these concepts blur and human understanding is limited. This symbolism aligns with many other images in the novel related to threads, webs, and patterns, emphasizing the intricate, often elusive nature of reality and self.

  • The entire scene is dreamlike, blurring the line between reality and illusion. Mr. Spitzer finds himself entangled in wedding gowns, which transform into seas, sails, and shrouds. This reflects a questioning of what is real and what is merely a construct of the mind. The passage suggests that the material world might be less substantial than the dreams and ideas that inhabit it. This aligns with metaphysical idealism, which posits that reality is fundamentally mental or spiritual. Mr. Spitzer's inability to discern whether there is one moon or many mirrored moons, or even no moon at all, highlights the limitations of human knowledge. He is trapped within his own "hypothesis," which he cannot prove. This underscores the theme of uncertainty and the difficulty of attaining objective truth. The census-taker reference is interesting because census-takers are about collecting data to create an idea of a population, Mr. Spitzer fears the census-taker because he fears the idea of ultimate knowledge. The passage touches on existential themes of meaninglessness and the search for purpose. The old maid's rejection of marriage and her call to "divest themselves of excess luggage" can be interpreted as a rejection of societal norms and a pursuit of individual freedom. However, her obsession with wedding gowns suggests an underlying longing for connection and meaning, even in the face of her conscious rejection of it. Mr. Spitzer's role as a "dead man coming to mourn for her" reinforces the sense of existential angst and the confrontation with mortality. The imagery of carousels of brides where there is no bride, and a spiral staircase finding through clouds where constellations sleep, underscores the absurdity of existence. Mr. Spitzer is caught in a world of contradictions and paradoxes, where meaning is elusive and reason fails to provide answers. The reference to a world of mirrored moons and the difficulty in knowing which is real is reminiscent of Plato's Allegory of the Cave. The world we perceive may be a mere reflection of a higher, more authentic reality. The wedding gowns themselves are rich in symbolism. They represent not only marriage but also societal expectations, lost dreams, and the weight of the past. The fact that Mr. Spitzer is "trapped" by them suggests that he is burdened by these forces, unable to break free from the constraints of tradition and memory. Mr. Spitzer likens himself to Charon's ferry boat. Charon is a figure in Greek mythology who carries souls of the newly deceased across the river Styx that divided the world of the living from the world of the dead.

The struggle to escape from sexual pursuit is one of the most popular motifs of Greek mythology: Persephone, Callisto, Daphne, Europa, Io, etc. Greek mythology depicts women as vulnerable to assault, able to escape only through death, or metamorphosis like Daphne who becomes a laurel tree. The consequence of fleeing sexual violence is another type of force on the body, the loss of the human form where women who survive sexual violence become pregnant, and when gods as the rapists, produce a hero child. The Chicago detective is described as raping Esther because he thinks she’s dead. Chione in Greek mythology was said to be so beautiful that she was the object of a thousand men's desire. Hermes used magic to make Chione to fall into a deep sleep and proceeded to rape her. Since Young has said Esther is the mother of us all and Mr. Spitzer as the father of us all, these could be archetypes for Adam and Eve and possibly Lilith. In On the Origin of World, part of the Nag Hammadi Library, Eve is described as being raped by archangels and bears children. In the Kabbalist myth Eve is raped by Lilith, Adam’s first wife, in the guise of Samael, Lilith’s demon lover or Satan. The Kabbalists continued the story to include Eve, through her menses, to have defiled Adam which then lead to Lilith being able to rape Adam, become impregnated and birth Lilin or demons by Adam.
