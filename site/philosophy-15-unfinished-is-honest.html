Chapter 15. Unfinished Is Honest

Part of The Philosophy in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Previous · Philosophy of MMMD Home · Next →

See also: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Philosophy: Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres

Chapters discussed: 21, 51

Definition from research

The unfinished is not failure. It is honesty. Anyone who claims to hold the complete and final answer is usually selling something.

Where the previous principle concerns how to build, this one concerns how to judge. The novel consistently treats claims of completeness with suspicion and treats incompletion with tenderness.

Mr. Spitzer’s unfinished music is the central image. An old piano mender tells him “that there had always been a missing keyboard. There had always been a string missing in the harp of God” (ch. 51). The creation itself is incomplete, and Mr. Spitzer wonders whether the missing string is his.

The opposite figure appears in the sixty-second chapter: the conductor. Mr. Spitzer gives his sympathies “not to the conductor,” and “not to the mad fellow imagining he could conduct all the music of all the singing spheres.” The music of the spheres was the Pythagorean dream, the idea that the whole cosmos sounds one perfect harmony. Pythagoras was a guest in Catherine’s house in the ninth chapter. Here his dream returns as the fantasy of a despot. Whoever imagines he can conduct all the music is, in the novel’s word, mad.

Even Miss MacIntosh, the realist, is described as “this unfinished thing, tentative and unbelievable” (ch. 21), and Vera loves her not in spite of this but because of it. Incompletion is not a flaw to be hidden. It is what a real person looks like in the morning light.
