The Protestant Ethic

Part of Intertextuality in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Previous · Intertextuality in MMMD Home · Next →

Characters: Miss MacIntosh

Allusions: Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest

Definition from research

Category: accidental. Weber is never cited, and the word "Protestant" appears only twice, in passing. The connection belongs to readers.

Max Weber's The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1905) argued that Protestant faith, with its stress on hard work, thrift, and plain living as signs of grace, helped shape the modern economic world.

Readers reach for Weber because Miss MacIntosh fits his description so closely. She was "always, therefore, very industrious, even in the hottest days, always bustling back and forth, enthusiastic" (chapter 3). For her, "The path of virtue was rugged and hard and girt about by rocks having no foothold" (chapter 3). "If there was any heaven, it should be bare and plain and unfurnished" (chapter 3). In her hometown of What Cheer, "God was worshiped in a building of plain walls such as befitted God or out of doors where there were no walls." She believed that God "had left many things uncreated," and that "We should do very well to take care of each new day as it came" (chapter 3).

Her plain ethic has a social side Weber would not predict. "The mill workers were striking, and she would like to picket for them if only there was time" (chapter 22). The fit with Weber is real, and a reader who brings him to the novel will find much to discuss. But the connection is the reader's, not Young's, and the volume labels it that way.
