Characters: Moses Hunnecker

“Good-night and good-morning”: There is an Arabic phrase for the “Good-night and good-morning”; see the Chapter 42 resources. “The lover and the loved” may refer to Rumi or Sufism that also have similar sayings. seems to mirror mystical interpretations from Sufism and Christian Theology and signifies the ultimate unity and interconnectedness of love, the person experiencing love, and the object of that love. In Sufism, the phrase highlights the journey of the soul towards union with the divine. The lover seeks to merge with the Beloved and in that ultimate union the distinction between the two dissolves. In Christian Theology the Father can be seen as the Lover, the Son as the Beloved, and the Holy Spirit as the love that binds them: “Behold, then, there are three things: he that loves, and that which is loved, and love” (Augustine, On the Trinity 8.10.14, trans. Arthur West Haddan, 1873). See Augustine and Christian Theology. Earlier in Chapter 42.

"God rest you merry, gentlemen" quotes the old English carol of comfort and joy.

"Moses in his mollusk boat" reimagines the infant Moses among the reeds, his basket become a shell, a vulnerable child set adrift to be saved.

“through which he had lived, moved, gathered weight”: “For in him we live, and move, and have our being” (Acts 17:28). See The Bible.

“neither to stay nor to depart”: “To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven: A time to be born, and a time to die” (Ecclesiastes 3:1-2). See Ecclesiastes.

“there was no Savior upon these waters”: When Peter sinks on the waters he cries, “Lord, save me. And immediately Jesus stretched forth his hand, and caught him” (Matthew 14:30-31). It underscores the stark reality that sometimes there is no rescue, no reprieve, no saving grace, only the merciless, indifferent “waters” that engulf all. See The Bible.

“the king with many crowns”: “His eyes were as a flame of fire, and on his head were many crowns” (Revelation 19:12). See The Bible.

“when all which had been lost should be found”: “For this thy brother was dead, and is alive again; and was lost, and is found” (Luke 15:32), the parable of the Prodigal Son. See The Bible.

“void and without shape upon the waters of the darkness”: “And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters” (Genesis 1:2). See Genesis. Earlier in Chapters 1, 12, 15, 20 and 37.

“Should the reprobate be pure as snow”: “Though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow” (Isaiah 1:18), and “If I wash myself with snow water, and make my hands never so clean; yet shalt thou plunge me in the ditch” (Job 9:30-31). See the ace of spades in the Chapter 42 resources. See The Bible, The Book of Job, and Gambling and Playing Cards. Earlier in Chapter 42.

“crumbled into dust and ashes”: “Behold now, I have taken upon me to speak unto the Lord, which am but dust and ashes” (Genesis 18:27). See Genesis.

“Death was the Orient whether it was heaven or earth”: Orient comes from the Latin oriens, rising, for the east where the sun rises. Christian churches and graves were long laid out facing east, toward the rising sun and the hoped-for resurrection. For Mr. Spitzer death is that east, heaven and earth at once, where Hannah goes, “where men wore skirts.”

“Better that her cruel lord should never have plowed this stony land”: “Some fell upon stony places, where they had not much earth” (Matthew 13:5). See The Bible.
