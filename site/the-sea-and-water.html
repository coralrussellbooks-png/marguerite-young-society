Definition from research

Chapter 2 · The Sea and Water Imagery

  • Catherine is often associated with water, tides, and drowning.
  • Symbolizes her immersion in dreams, unconsciousness, and the fluidity of existence.

Chapter 3 · Sea and Surf

  • Represent the uncontrollable forces of nature and time.
  • The beach is the boundary between worlds and the place where Miss MacIntosh’s disappearance occurs.

Chapter 8 · The Broken Windshield Wiper and Flooded Road

  • Symbolize vulnerability and the overwhelming forces of nature or fate that the characters cannot control.

Chapter 8 · The Old Tin House and Flooded Yard

  • The dilapidated house and refuse-strewn yard symbolize decay, neglect, and the harsh realities hidden behind appearances.

Chapter 9 · Sea and Water Imagery

  • The sea, waves, tides, fog, and shore recur repeatedly as symbols of life’s uncertainty, danger, and transition.
  • The sea represents the unknown, the subconscious, and the boundary between life and death.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s identity as a “land-locked sailor” who “could not swim an inch” but who constantly talks in nautical terms symbolizes a life caught between stability and the wild, uncontrollable forces of nature.

Chapter 10

  • “Porpoises shall eat the drowned sailor. The waters of this world shall close over his head”: The preacher gives the bride and groom a sailor’s burial at sea. Milton’s “Lycidas” (1638) mourns a friend drowned in the Irish Sea, and Ariel sings of a drowned father in The Tempest: “Full fathom five thy father lies.” The line foreshadows Miss MacIntosh lost in the waves.

Chapter 11

  • “his intimate name, Ocean or Water”: Elizabeth called him “Water.” Spenser named him “the Shepherd of the Ocean” in Colin Clouts Come Home Againe (1595), and Raleigh wrote The Ocean’s Love to Cynthia, with the Queen as the moon who rules the tides.

Chapter 14 · Imagery of Water, Snow, and Night

  • Recurring references to water, foaming seas, snowflakes, and darkness evoke a liminal, otherworldly atmosphere.
  • These natural elements symbolize change, decay, and the boundary between life and death.

Chapter 15 · The Sea

  • Symbolizes life’s vastness, mystery, and power.
  • The “black, whirling waters” and “primal memories” suggest subconscious depths and timeless forces.
  • The sea’s roar and the waves breaking evoke both danger and excitement.

Chapter 15

  • “my body gleaming with finny phosphorescence”: Late summer seas glow with bioluminescent plankton when the water is stirred.

Chapter 16

  • “the great mother-of-pearl mollusk which might have contained the form of Venus”: Botticelli’s The Birth of Venus (c. 1485) shows the goddess rising from the sea on a scallop shell.
  • “the shell which was the great turbaned Turk, the phaeton, the winding stairway”: These are real shells. The turban shell is one, and the wentletrap takes its name from the Dutch for “winding staircase.”
  • “I should be the umbrella medusa rising to the surface”: The jellyfish’s bell is called its umbrella, and its free-swimming stage is the medusa.

Chapter 16 · The House as a Tomb and Water Castle

  • The grand house is described with imagery of tombs, water, and underwater life, creating a sense of mystery and timelessness. The references to the phoenix and musical tomb further emphasize themes of death and rebirth.

Chapter 18 · The Sea, Waves, and Tides

  • Evoke transience, the unconscious, and the relentless movement of life and death.

Chapter 19

  • “a clump of blackened seaweed hanging on a nail on the door”: “much like some other wig.” Miss MacIntosh will be lost to the sea.

Chapter 21 · Sea and Light

  • The sea light covering the scene suggests mystery, change, and the vastness of life’s experience.

Chapter 22

  • “the sea is full of wrinkles, though it has neither age nor grey hairs”: “Time writes no wrinkle on thine azure brow: / Such as creation’s dawn beheld, thou rollest now” (Byron, Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage IV, 1818).

Chapter 22 · Memory and Forgetfulness

  • The sea, a recurring motif, is described as both full of “wrinkles” and yet possibly without memory. This paradox symbolizes the blurred lines between remembering and forgetting, life and oblivion.

Chapter 22 · The Sea

  • Represents memory, oblivion, and the passage of time. It is both a life source and a graveyard, swallowing ships and memories alike.

Chapter 27

  • “the great breakers coming in with their foaming manes and tails”: Poseidon was god of the sea and of horses, and breaking waves are still called “white horses.”

Chapter 30 · Ships and Seas

  • Metaphors for passage between life and death, reality and dream.

Chapter 36 · Water and the Sea

  • Recurring images of water, surf, tides, and waves symbolize fluidity, change, and the unconscious.
  • Water is associated with identity shifts and the impermanence of life.

Chapter 37 · Sea and Waves

  • The sea is a motif for transformation, fluidity, and the unconscious.

Chapter 39 · Sea and Shore

  • The meeting of sea and land as a metaphor for the interface between life and death, reality and dream.

Chapter 42 · Lost Ships and Stars

  • Ships represent journeys, lost hopes, and the passage of souls. Stars symbolize guidance, memory, and the unreachable. Mr. Spitzer’s cataloging of lost ships parallels the attempt to map lost lives and loves.

Chapter 43 · Skirt of Clouds and Waters

  • Symbolizes beauty, identity, and the suffragette’s fading presence.

Chapter 45 · Sea Shell/Sea

  • Memory, the call of the unknown, the eternal sea of life and death.

Chapter 47 · Stars, Moons, and Waves

  • Cosmic cycles, impermanence, and spiritual journeying (throughout).

Chapter 49 · Water and Ice

  • Recurring images of frozen waters, ice domes, and watery graves symbolize stasis, death, and the frozen moment between life and death.
  • Water also represents fluidity, dream states, and the passage between worlds.

Chapter 50 · Water and Tides

  • Represent flux, the movement between life and death, progress and retreat, echoing the ebb and flow of existence.

Chapter 54 · The Sea and Water Imagery

  • The sea symbolizes the unconscious, the fluidity of reality, and the passage between life and death. Underwater fountains, sea’s burning gold, and submerged cities evoke a dreamlike state and loss.

Chapter 56 · Clouds, Sea, and Tides

  • Recurring natural motifs representing impermanence, change, and the fluid boundary between life and death.

Chapter 61 · Sea Shells

  • Symbolize journeys, the passage of time, and the blending of past, present, and future.

Chapter 62 · Water and Rivers

  • Metaphors for the flow of life, time, and reality. The river transforms from fire to ice, representing change and the cyclical nature of existence.

Chapter 62 · Dream of the River of Fire and Ice

  • Reflects the shifting states of reality and consciousness, the tension between light and darkness, life and death.

Chapter 64 · Sea and Water Imagery

  • Symbolize life’s constant movement, change, and the inevitability of death.

Chapter 65 · Niagara Falls and Honeymooners’ Hotel

  • Represents the irony of Miss MacIntosh’s solitude amidst joy and new beginnings for others.

Chapter 66 · The Sea and Waves

  • Symbolize the passage of time, death, and the subconscious.

Chapter 75 · The River and Water

  • Associated with cleansing, oblivion, and the passage of time; the river carries away dead babies, symbolizing attempts to escape grief.

Chapter 79 · Interpersonal Relationships

  • The coldness between Esther’s parents contrasts with the strange intimacy involving the other woman.
  • Esther’s relationship with her parents is fraught with misunderstanding and blame.

Chapter 81 · The Storm at Sea

  • A metaphor for chaos, destruction, and the fragility of life.

Chapter 82 · The Sea

  • Represents overwhelming change, the passage of time, and the erasure of the past. It is both nurturing and destructive.
