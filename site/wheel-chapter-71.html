Research notes: Chapter 71 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 71

Characters: Tabitha

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The narrator is never singular. She wears her dead mother's clothes, "the black velvet which had been her abandoned shroud", her mother's lace collar, her mother's pearls. She is not dressed as herself. She is dressed as a sediment of persons. The mother's voice continues inside her mind as an active presence, not a memory: "Those real pearls, my sleeping mother had always said, could not be lost or stolen or sold". The mother is simultaneously dead, sleeping, and speaking.

The doctor, Dr. O'Leary, is perhaps the chapter's most explicit figure of the plural self. He is simultaneously a Yale-trained physician, a man in second childhood, a grieving brother calling for Sarah, a deliverer of imaginary babies, and a naked figure wandering a barren countryside. He cannot be resolved into one person. When the narrator stands in his room, she notes that even his clothes hang there without him: the self and its social costume are separated, the costume remaining while the person has vanished into some other version of himself.

Even the landlady, whose speech has collapsed into a private linguistic system, "she rid the elephants," "her husbane," "clombed the stars", is a plural self in the most radical sense: a person whose interior language no longer maps onto shared language, who therefore exists in a kind of sovereign but unreachable interiority. She is simultaneously present and inaccessible.

The widow on the stairs is split literally down the face: "the high red lighted only one cheek — the other was ashen". The self, here, is visually incomplete, half-constructed, half-absent.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter systematically refuses to let us know what is real. Was the hangman there at all: "Had I dreamed the hangman?". Is Sarah real? The widow insists she is not: "there was no Sarah, none at all". But the narrator heard her. The doctor's patients are described as imaginary, yet their shoes are on the floor. The pregnant man is described as "a real, pregnant man": the insistence on the word real is itself suspicious, a construction trying to assert its own status.

The narrator's mother entertained a thief who, when he left, could not be certain whether he had "dreamed all those great, crystal-lighted rooms". The house may have been a dream. The thief may have been a dream. The Revolution he was going to finance, which could not be named with an exact sum or exact date, is the dream of all dreams: a transformation that cannot be located in time or money.

Most crucially: "The grey light came from the mind if not from the sky". The chapter does not decide. It offers both possibilities with equal weight. The cave is everywhere. The larger cave is more carefully lit.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The entire chapter is saturated with unresolved, accumulating grief. The hangman's grief is the most explicit: his wife Marie hanged herself from a sleety peach tree while pregnant, and he had known where she was going and had searched instead for his hanging clothes and his Jew's harp. His grief is not guilt resolved into acceptance. It is guilt compounded, by habit, by the mandrake children springing from the gallows, by his own children playing at hanging when he is away. His grief has become his entire cosmology.

The narrator's grief for her mother is not past tense. The mother's pearls, clothes, voice, and stories are present tense throughout. The grief is the climate of the narration; it is the atmosphere in which all observation occurs.

The landlady's grief for her dead dog is folded into grief for her fallen-away husband, which is folded into grief for the priest who no longer comes, which is folded into the grief of the small dancer whose castaway clothes Tabitha now wears. These griefs do not form a sequence. They exist simultaneously, layered, each one pressing on the others.

The widow upstairs grieves a husband who "died before he ever came to bed": grief for what never happened, for the unlived life, which is perhaps the most irresolvable grief of all.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator's primary activity in this chapter is witnessing. She does not fix anything. She does not conclude anything. She moves through the hotel (the hangman's story, the doctor's room, the widow's rage, the landlady's speech) and she looks. She reads the books on the shelf. She studies the mateless shoes. She reads the calendar that stopped twenty years ago. She notes the cracked mirror, the thermometer she crushes underfoot, the wet sponge her hand brushes against.

The witnessing is not passive. It is ethically active. When the widow delivers her antisemitic, racist tirade, the narrator does not argue, does not fix, does not editorialize at length. She records it. The refusal to simplify, to either endorse or dismiss, is the act. The reader is left holding the ugliness without a frame that neutralizes it.

The chapter ends with Tabitha, the mute chambermaid, washing one stone in the pale spring air. The narrator witnesses this without interpretation. Tabitha looks up and says "Good-morning", and the chapter ends. No conclusion. No meaning assigned. The witness stays in the room, and the room is enough.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The narrator is explicitly on a quest; she has come "to pick wild flowers, the first white flowers of spring", she has come "to find" something, she has traveled far. But the quest keeps folding back on itself. She enters the doctor's room looking for something and finds only evidence of other searches. The doctor's room is itself a recursive construction: prescription blanks, old magazines, instruments for birth, books from every era, a calendar stopped at a different time.

The mother's story of the pearls is the chapter's most explicit image of recursive construction: every time the string breaks and pearls are lost, the faithful James returns more pearls than were lost, "The more often she lost the pearls...the more pearls there were". The building is never finished. Loss generates more material. You return with more than you started with, though the original string is broken.

The hangman's recursive construction is darker: he has hanged men all his life, perfecting a craft, only to find the craft "unperfected", the mandrake children rising from the gallows, the habit of preparation persisting even as his wife hanged herself. He builds the same structure over and over, and it keeps producing what he did not intend.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter's formal structure enacts this pillar directly. Sentences run for half a page. Parenthetical clauses open and never quite close. The landlady's speech is perhaps the most radical example: her syntax has become genuinely incompletable, not from stupidity but from a kind of excess of reality pressing through damaged language. She is not failing to complete her sentences. She is being honest about a world that does not complete.

The doctor's life is structurally incomplete. His diploma hangs on the wall. His instruments are laid out. His practice continues, but his patients are imaginary, his telephone does not exist, his calendar stopped twenty years ago. He is a physician who cannot complete the act of medicine. And yet the narrator does not judge this as failure. She asks: "how many of us, if we expressed our inner lives, would not express disorder?".

The quest for the white flower, the stated reason for the journey, is never completed in this chapter. The flower is described as "far away, the landscape of a dream which should not be dreamed in the Wabash country". The incompletion is the honest report.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Every character in this chapter is marginal. The hangman is professionally marginal: society's necessary executioner who must be kept at a distance from society. The mad doctor delivers imaginary babies in a barren countryside. The pregnant man, "a real, pregnant man", violates the categorical boundary of biological sex. Tabitha is mute, described as "too crazy to be on relief", wearing another woman's castaway clothes, performing labor that is noticed only in its absence.

The landlady's aphasia places her at the margin of language itself; she exists in the gap between private meaning and shared communication. She is not outside the community; she runs the hotel. But she runs it from a position of linguistic marginality that makes her simultaneously central and unreachable.

The narrator herself is marginal: a woman traveling alone, accused of being a "tramp girl" by the widow, wearing a dead woman's clothes, carrying pearls that are worth no more than fakes in the current market. She belongs nowhere in this landscape. Her marginality is precisely what allows her to witness it all.

The widow's bigotry is, under this pillar, particularly revealing: her rage against Jews, Catholics, Black people, Roosevelt, her insistence on categorical purity, is the reaction to the ontology of the marginal. She senses that the categories are constructed and responds with violence against that perception, doubling down on the lines. Her hysteria is the evidence that the lines are not holding.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This is perhaps the pillar most thoroughly embodied in the chapter's texture. The doctor's room is a geological cross-section of time: Mary Pickford on a calendar from twenty years ago, books from every era, instruments from different periods of medicine, shoes from "every era and every land". Time has not passed in this room. It has accumulated.

The narrator's mother's estate (the sea-blackened house with gold towers, the rose garden, the marble statues) is described as gone and simultaneously present: "though the world renamed and the house was gone" it enters the hotel room, "included" in the space of sleeplessness and memory. The house does not recede. It presses up through the floor of the present.

The hangman's wife Marie is cut down, dead, but her children play at hanging in the orchard, and the hangman fears to return home not because of Marie but because of what her children might be doing now: the past's action continuing in the present through the bodies of the living.

The dogs' graveyard in the hotel backyard, wooden crosses made from missing fence palings, is a perfect image of sedimentary time: the dead are beneath the yard, the fence is incomplete because of them, the living space is shaped by the accumulated dead.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter is acutely self-aware about language as construction. The Jew's harp is noted to be "not Jewish": the name constructs an association that the object does not bear. The hangman plays it before every hanging: an instrument named for an othered group, used in the ritual of state killing. The chapter notices this without belaboring it.

The landlady's speech is the chapter's most extended meditation on language as construction. Her private language, "clombed," "husbane," "agone," "axed," "combed", is not random. It is a consistent system, internally coherent, shaped by her particular history and body. It constructs a reality that is partially shared and partially sovereign. When she says "there was martial happiness", she may mean marital, or she may mean something more precise than either word captures.

The narrator notes that the mother's pearls "in the present market, would bring no more than cultivated pearls". The word real has lost its market value. The language of authenticity has been devalued by the language of the market. What counts as real is determined by what counts as economically real.

The widow's language constructs a world in which racial and religious categories are ontologically fixed, "the dirty Jew," "the Negroes," "mulattoes", a world in which the constructed nature of categories is violently refused. Her language does not describe the world. It attempts to enforce it.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The chapter's sacred moments are all embedded in the most degraded ordinary. The hangman's Jew's harp music before a hanging: music as the thin membrane between the ordinary and the transcendent, played in the moment before death. The mother's pearls that multiply when lost: an infinity discovered not in heaven but in arithmetic, in the faithful servant's hands, in the broken string.

The doctor's room, for all its squalor, contains "lurid illustrations" that "fluttered into a golden light": diseased kidneys and enlarged hearts illuminated as if sacred. The instruments of birth laid out on the dresser (forceps, bellows, wooden arm) are described with a reverence usually reserved for altar objects.

Tabitha washing one stone in the pale spring air; this is the chapter's final and most concentrated image of immanent transcendence. A mute woman, called crazy, wearing dead people's clothes, performing the most menial labor, washing one round stone that caught the sunlight. The sunlight on the stone. The attention given to one stone. The sacred is here, in this, not elsewhere.

The narrator's quest for the white flower, "the first white flower of spring, the last white winter flower", is a quest for immanent transcendence: not a flower that symbolizes something beyond itself, but a flower that is the crossing point of winter and spring, death and life, in its own ordinary flowering.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The narrator is explicitly constituted by what was deposited before she arrived. She wears her mother's clothes, her mother's pearls, her mother's stories. She carries the memory of a house that no longer exists. She is traveling through Indiana looking for something her mother's world contained: a white flower, a beauty "such as I had seen once and lost". She is not a self moving through time. She is the place where her mother's time and her own time cross.

The hangman is a crossing point of the state's violence and his own domestic grief: the professional habit of preparing for a hanging crossed with the knowledge that his wife was hanging herself in the orchard. He could not separate the two. The preparation ritual crossed both events simultaneously.

The doctor is the crossing point of Yale medicine and rural Indiana madness, of the living and the imaginary, of the competent young man in the photograph and the old man screaming for his dead sister. His room is the physical space where these times cross: the diploma above the pillow where two heads have left their indentations.

Tabitha is perhaps the most structurally significant crossing point: she is the only character who is described as genuinely present in the chapter's final image. She is mute; she cannot construct language. She wears the clothes of a dead dancer. She tends the mad doctor. She washes one stone. She is the ground on which all the chapter's accumulated grief, plurality, and incompletion finally rests. When she says "Good-morning", the chapter's last words, it is not a greeting. It is an acknowledgment. The crossing continues.
