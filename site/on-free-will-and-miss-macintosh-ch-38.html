On free will, and Miss MacIntosh.

The chapter's meditation on human will circles back to Miss MacIntosh: absent as always, but still exerting gravity. The novel suggests that Miss MacIntosh's will was not about imposing control but about perseverance within limits: doing the work in front of you, attending to what is real, refusing the fantasies that make limitation bearable by making it invisible. Mind your own knitting. Young takes this seriously as a moral position. It is not glamorous. It is not the Redcrosse Knight or the suffrage captain or the seven-stringed harp. It is a woman with a schedule and a rubber coat, choosing reality every morning. The novel believes, somewhere in its depths, that this choice is its own kind of heroism.

On 'Babylon has fallen.' Revelation 18 describes the fall of Babylon (the great city, the center of worldly power and excess) as a cataclysm that arrives suddenly and leaves only silence and smoke. Young uses this as a shorthand for the collapse of any order that had seemed permanent. Cousin Hannah's world (the suffrage campaigns, the shared history with Catherine, the iron keys and jeweled swords) has fallen into the past in the same way. What seemed like the whole world is now rubble. The characters standing in the aftermath are trying to understand what the city even was, now that it is gone.
