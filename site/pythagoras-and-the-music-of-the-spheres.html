Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres

Part of To All My Darlings, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).

Definition from research

Chapter 5

  • “the elegiac music of the spheres or with those heavenly harmonies no mortal ears could hear”: Pythagoras taught that the planets make music as they move, a harmony too constant for human ears to notice.

Chapter 9

  • “Pythagoras who had a golden thigh”: Pythagoras was an ancient Greek philosopher. It is believed his teachings were metempsychosis, that every soul is immortal and after death enters another body, and musica universalis where the planets move according to mathematical equations and create an inaudible symphony of music. Legend says he showed a golden thigh at Olympia as a sign of his divinity.

Chapter 13

  • “not even the creaking of starlight”: Aristotle reports the Pythagorean answer: the spheres do make music, and people do not hear it because they have heard it since birth (On the Heavens 2.9). Lorenzo tells Jessica, “Such harmony is in immortal souls; / But whilst this muddy vesture of decay / Doth grossly close it in, we cannot hear it” (The Merchant of Venice 5.1).

Chapter 14

  • “the music of the spheres, the silent music”: Boethius was a Roman senator, consul, magister officiorum, polymath, historian, and philosopher of the Early Middle Ages. He sought to reconcile the teachings of Plato and Aristotle with Christian theology. He broke logic into three parts: that which defines, that which divides, and that which deduces. There are three types of arguments: those of necessity, of ready believability, and sophistry. In De Musica there are three classifications: Musica mundana, Musica humana, Musica instrumentalis. In Dante’s Divine Comedy, the spirit of Boethius is pointed out by Saint Thomas Aquinas and is mentioned further in the poem. In his book De Institutione Musica (Fundamentals of Music) his drawings appear striking and may be an inspiration for the organization of Young’s novel. Musica universalis or music of the spheres or harmony of the spheres is a Greek philosophical concept founded by Pythagoras. This metaphysical principle stated that mathematical relationships could produce ‘tones’ of energy that could produce, among other things, sounds. Pythagoras proposed that the celestial bodies all have their own unique hum based on their orbital revolution. This idea influenced many scholars until the end of the Renaissance. Johannes Kepler, a German astronomer from the 1600s wrote extensively on the subject. Later astronomy set it aside. Boethius wrote The Consolation of Philosophy in prison awaiting execution, with its Wheel of Fortune.
  • The teaching most securely identified with Pythagoras is the “transmigration of souls” or metempsychosis, which holds that every soul is immortal and, upon death, enters into a new body. He may have also devised the doctrine of musica universalis, which holds that the planets move according to mathematical equations and thus resonate to produce an inaudible symphony of music.
  • “her peacocks”: Aristotle referred to the peacock’s vain reputation. The Roman poet Ennius, following the Pythagorean teaching of rebirth, dreamed that Homer’s soul had passed into a peacock and then into himself. The peacock symbolizes the dazzling but ephemeral nature of beauty and social display, the multiplicity and fragmentation of perception, and the tension between illusion and reality that the characters navigate.
  • “the golden cock perched at the foot of her bed, that it was really Pythagoras shedding his golden feathers”: In Lucian’s dialogue “The Dream, or the Cock,” a cobbler’s rooster reveals that it is Pythagoras reborn. Pythagoras was said to have a golden thigh. Rimsky-Korsakov’s opera The Golden Cockerel (1909), from Pushkin’s tale (1834), has a golden cock that warns a tsar.

Chapter 25

  • “circles within circles, orbs within orbs, many spheres of influence”: The Pythagoreans held that the planets, turning in their spheres, make a music of the heavens that human ears cannot hear.

Chapter 26

  • “Musica Universalis” is a metaphysical principle by Boethius that uses mathematical relationships to express ‘tones’ that could be heard by the soul. The "Musica Universalis" here symbolizes the harmonious and eternal cosmic order that underlies the apparent chaos of mortal life and death. It is depicted as a sublime, transcendent music that governs the movements of the stars and planets, reflecting a divine harmony beyond human perception. Mr. Spitzer, a semi-retired lawyer and composer of "silent music," embodies this principle as someone who is attuned to the "music of the spheres" or the eternal, silent harmonies that exist beyond earthly sounds. His compositions, unheard by living ears and played only for the dead or in the celestial realm, metaphorically represent this cosmic music. The chapter emphasizes the contrast between earthly, temporal music and this higher, immutable harmony, suggesting that the tangible world is but a fragment or shadow of a greater metaphysical reality. Catherine's opium dreams expand this theme by blurring the boundaries between the real and the illusory, the living and the dead, reflecting the ineffable nature of the universal music that ties all existence together. The "Musica Universalis" is not just an abstract concept but a living presence in her visions with images of angels harping, celestial choirs, and cosmic rhythms permeating her consciousness. The chapter suggests that this music is intertwined with suffering and mortality (the "music of the perfect silence beyond the chance of the brittle and falling stars") highlighting a metaphysical view where life, death, and cosmic order are inseparable parts of a greater whole. The "Musica Universalis" is used to articulate a metaphysical principle that all existence, from the smallest earthly details to the vast cosmos, is bound by a harmonious, eternal music. This music transcends the limitations of physical reality and human understanding, offering a vision of unity amid fragmentation and a cosmic order beneath the apparent chaos of life and death.

Chapter 29

  • Immortality within the framework of mortality is a philosophical exploration of extending life, potentially indefinitely, while acknowledging the inherent limitations of our biological existence. Collective immortality suggests that while individuals are mortal, their legacy and impact on others can create a form of enduring existence. Some argue that immortality could strip life of its meaning and urgency, while others believe it could offer an opportunity for greater exploration and purpose. Exploring the concept of death and its implications for our understanding of life and existence is central to the discussion of immortality. Mortality can also be a source of meaning and value, as it can make experiences more precious and urgent. Immortality is explored as a concept deeply intertwined with mortality rather than separate or opposed to it. Mr. Spitzer reflects on the ambiguous boundary between life and death, the transient nature of existence, and the paradoxes of human identity and experience. The chapter discusses butterflies and moths as symbols at the boundary between life and death, day and night, tangible and intangible. Butterflies, which live briefly and beautifully, represent the fleeting moments of life that nonetheless embody a form of immortality through their delicate and recurring patterns. Moths, often nocturnal and mysterious, suggest the hidden, unconscious aspects of existence. The imagery of metamorphosis, from caterpillar to chrysalis to butterfly, serves as a metaphor for transformation beyond physical life, hinting at a form of life that transcends mortality. Mr. Spitzer, as a character caught between life and death (being the surviving twin of a deceased brother), embodies this tension. His reflections reveal that mortality and immortality are not simply opposites but exist in a dynamic relationship where life contains elements of death and death contains elements of life. He perceives that the "dead did not return except in our thoughts," suggesting that immortality may reside in memory, legacy, or a spiritual continuation beyond physical life. The chapter also touches on the idea that time and individuality are illusions or constructs, and that existence may be cyclical or layered with overlapping realities. The "immortal invalid" and the "deathless dead" are motifs illustrating beings who exist in this liminal space, neither fully alive nor fully dead. Immortality within the framework of mortality signifies the persistence of being beyond physical death, not as an escape from mortality but as a transformation and continuation in memory, spirit, or cosmic cycles. It highlights the paradox that while human life is ephemeral and marked by loss and change, there is a form of enduring existence that transcends bodily death, embodied in symbols like the butterfly, the moth, and the music of the spheres that Mr. Spitzer contemplates. This nuanced view challenges simple dichotomies, emphasizing that mortality and immortality are deeply connected, and immortality is found within the very experience of mortal life and death rather than beyond it.

Chapter 37

  • The philosophy of reversals consists of various philosophical ideas and concepts related to the notion of something being turned upside down or reversed. It explores the nature of reversals in different domains: time, causality, values, societal structures. It examines how these reversals impact our understanding of reality, ethics, and meaning. Reversals in literature and art are used to create suspense, irony, and thematic complexity. It can be used to challenge expectations, expose hidden truths, and engage audiences on a deeper level. Aristotle’s reversal and recognition, Baudrillard’s Principle of Reversal examines how oppositions and differences can be overturned, leading to new forms of understanding and meaning. The philosophy of reversals is a prominent theme that permeates the narrative, revealing a worldview where binaries dissolve and opposites interpenetrate, creating a complex and unstable reality. This chapter explores the idea that opposites, such as night and day, sun and moon, beginning and end are not separate entities but are deeply intertwined and often indistinguishable. The narrator, Vera, reflects on how "his night was his day, and his day was his night," and "his dawn was his twilight, and his moon was his sun, and his sun was his moon, and his beginning was his end, and his end was his beginning". These statements encapsulate the core of the reversal philosophy: the collapsing of distinctions that we usually take for granted. The narrative also conveys a sense that reality itself is fluid and mutable, with physical and metaphysical elements constantly changing places or merging. For example, "there should be these somersaultings as when the ceiling was the mirroring floor, trees of enormous crystal growing with their roots in the clouded sky, their branches under the earth". This imagery suggests a world where spatial and conceptual norms are inverted, challenging linear and stable perceptions of existence. The chapter touches on the paradoxical nature of life and death, love and its absence. Vera notes that "Love might wear the face of death, the grinning skull, as death might wear the face of love", emphasizing that what seem like opposites are reflections or transformations of each other. This complicates the understanding of identity and existence, as individuals may be "other than we seemed," with two forms "blended into one which was amorphous, being neither". The philosophy of reversals extends to the cosmic scale, where even celestial bodies and natural laws are subject to change and ambiguity. The narrative questions the fixity of time and space, suggesting that "there should be no harmony, no silent music like the music of the spheres" in the world beyond, because "nothing should be simple there because nothing was simple here". This reflects a skepticism of absolute order or finality in the universe. It presents a vision of a world and perhaps a universe where dualities are illusory, and existence is marked by continuous transformation and contradiction. This philosophy challenges conventional binaries and invites readers to consider a reality that is simultaneously unified and divided, stable and unstable, known and unknowable. This philosophy is central to the novel's exploration of existence, identity, and perception, illustrating a world where certainty dissolves and the dreamlike nature of reality is revealed.

Chapter 37

  • Gender as a social construct, refers to the socially created roles, behaviors, expressions, and identities associated with being a woman, man, boy, or girl. It’s not inherently determined by biology, but rather shaped by cultural norms, societal expectations, and historical contexts. This means gender is not fixed and can vary across cultures and time. Not a biological reality, socially created, varies across cultures, dynamic and fluid, individual identity, distinct from biological sex attributes. The earliest recorded philosophical thought on gender emerged in ancient Greece with Plato and Aristotle, also Mary Wollstonecraft, Judith Sargent Murray, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, John Locke, Pythagoras. The chapter and novel presents gender as a fluid and changeable construct rather than a rigid, innate fact, using metaphorical and dreamlike language to question fixed dichotomies between male and female, self and other, life and death.

Chapter 38

  • Musica Universalis (music of the spheres) concept incorporates the metaphysical principle that mathematical relationships express qualities or “tones” of energy that manifests in numbers, visual angles, shapes and sounds which are all connected within a pattern of proportion. This has been explored by Pythagoras, Plato, Aristotle (he rejected the idea), Boethius developed three categories of music (musica mundana, musica humana, and musica quae in quibusdam constituta est instrumentis), Keppler expounded on the idea and cherry picked data to support his claims. They have since been refuted. Boethius developed imagery to go with his writings and I believe Young took inspiration from these illustrations for the novel. Musica universalis serves as a profound metaphor for the cosmic order, the spiritual unity beyond earthly fragmentation, and the haunting, silent music that connects life, death, memory, and the infinite. It highlights the novel’s exploration of metaphysical questions about existence, identity, and the search for meaning beyond the limits of human perception and experience.

Chapter 55

  • “twin President Garfields” James Abram Garfield (November 19, 1831 – September 19, 1881) was the 20th president of the United States, serving from March 1881 until his death in September that year after being shot two months earlier. A preacher, lawyer, and Civil War general, Garfield served nine terms in the United States House of Representatives and is the only sitting member of the House to be elected president. Before his candidacy for the presidency, he had been elected to the U.S. Senate by the Ohio General Assembly, a position he declined when he became president-elect. Garfield was born into poverty in a log cabin and grew up in northeastern Ohio. After graduating from Williams College in 1856, he studied law and became an attorney. He was a preacher in the Stone– Campbell Movement and president of the Western Reserve Eclectic Institute, affiliated with the Disciples. Garfield was elected as a Republican member of the Ohio State Senate in 1859, serving until 1861. He opposed Confederate secession, was a major general in the Union Army during the American Civil War, and fought in the battles of Middle Creek, Shiloh, and Chickamauga. He was elected to Congress in 1862 to represent Ohio's 19th district. Throughout his congressional service, he firmly supported the gold standard and gained a reputation as a skilled orator. He initially agreed with Radical Republican views on Reconstruction but later favored a Moderate Republican–aligned approach to civil rights enforcement for freedmen. Garfield's aptitude for mathematics extended to his own proof of the Pythagorean theorem, which he published in 1876. At the 1880 Republican National Convention, delegates chose Garfield, who had not sought the White House, as a compromise presidential nominee on the 36th ballot. In the 1880 presidential election, he conducted a low-key front porch campaign and narrowly defeated the Democratic nominee, Winfield Scott Hancock. Garfield's accomplishments as president included his assertion of presidential authority against senatorial courtesy in executive appointments, a purge of corruption in the Post Office, and his appointment of a Supreme Court justice. He advocated for agricultural technology, an educated electorate, and civil rights for African Americans. He also proposed substantial civil service reforms, which were passed by Congress in 1883 as the Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act and signed into law by his successor, Chester A. Arthur. Garfield was a member of the intraparty "Half-Breed" faction who used the powers of the presidency to defy the powerful "Stalwart" Senator Roscoe Conkling from New York. He did this by appointing Blaine faction leader William H. Robertson to the lucrative post of Collector of the Port of New York. The ensuing political battle resulted in Robertson's confirmation and the resignations of Conkling and Thomas C. Platt from the Senate. On July 2, 1881, Charles J. Guiteau, a disappointed and delusional office seeker, shot Garfield at the Baltimore and Potomac Railroad Station in Washington. The wound was not immediately fatal, but an infection caused by his doctors' unsanitary methods in treating the wound killed Garfield on September 19. Due to his brief tenure in office, historians tend to rank Garfield as a below-average president or omit him entirely from rankings, though he has earned praise for anti-corruption and pro-civil rights stances.

Chapter 70

  • "There is no return but the return of what is different, I suppose, and that by establishing relationships where none exist, we merely deceive ourselves into believing that life as a whole has a hidden, constant meaning like the unheard music of the spheres." The passage reflects a philosophical meditation on the unavoidability of change, the human compulsion to find or impose meaning, and the possibility that such meaning is not inherent but constructed. It challenges the belief in a fixed cosmic order and suggests that what we perceive as meaningful patterns may be self-deceptions, resonating with existentialist skepticism and the impermanence stressed in ancient and modern philosophies.

Chapter 70

  • “All a’s are b’s, it read—all b’s are c’s—and therefore, dear heart, all a’s are c’s. You know me.” The equation is the Pythagorean theorem by the Greek philosopher Pythagoras and is a fundamental relation in Euclidean geometry among the three sides of a right triangle. The theorem has been proven numerous times by many different methods. The Pythagorean theorem has attracted interest outside mathematics as a symbol of math being hard to understand, mystique, or intellectual power; popular references in literature, plays, musicals, songs, stamps, and cartoons abound.
