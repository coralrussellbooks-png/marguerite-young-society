Definition from research

Chapter 5

  • “the elegiac music of the spheres or with those heavenly harmonies no mortal ears could hear”: Pythagoras taught that the planets make music as they move, a harmony too constant for human ears to notice.

Chapter 9

  • “Pythagoras who had a golden thigh”: Pythagoras was an ancient Greek philosopher. It is believed his teachings were metempsychosis, that every soul is immortal and after death enters another body, and musica universalis where the planets move according to mathematical equations and create an inaudible symphony of music. Legend says he showed a golden thigh at Olympia as a sign of his divinity.

Chapter 13

  • “not even the creaking of starlight”: Aristotle reports the Pythagorean answer: the spheres do make music, and people do not hear it because they have heard it since birth (On the Heavens 2.9). Lorenzo tells Jessica, “Such harmony is in immortal souls; / But whilst this muddy vesture of decay / Doth grossly close it in, we cannot hear it” (The Merchant of Venice 5.1).

Chapter 14

  • “the music of the spheres, the silent music”: Boethius was a Roman senator, consul, magister officiorum, polymath, historian, and philosopher of the Early Middle Ages. He sought to reconcile the teachings of Plato and Aristotle with Christian theology. He broke logic into three parts: that which defines, that which divides, and that which deduces. There are three types of arguments: those of necessity, of ready believability, and sophistry. In De Musica there are three classifications: Musica mundana, Musica humana, Musica instrumentalis. In Dante’s Divine Comedy, the spirit of Boethius is pointed out by Saint Thomas Aquinas and is mentioned further in the poem. In his book De Institutione Musica (Fundamentals of Music) his drawings appear striking and may be an inspiration for the organization of Young’s novel. Musica universalis or music of the spheres or harmony of the spheres is a Greek philosophical concept founded by Pythagoras. This metaphysical principle stated that mathematical relationships could produce ‘tones’ of energy that could produce, among other things, sounds. Pythagoras proposed that the celestial bodies all have their own unique hum based on their orbital revolution. This idea influenced many scholars until the end of the Renaissance. Johannes Kepler, a German astronomer from the 1600s wrote extensively on the subject. (Wikipedia) Later astronomy set it aside. Boethius wrote The Consolation of Philosophy in prison awaiting execution, with its Wheel of Fortune.
  • The teaching most securely identified with Pythagoras is the “transmigration of souls” or metempsychosis, which holds that every soul is immortal and, upon death, enters into a new body. He may have also devised the doctrine of musica universalis, which holds that the planets move according to mathematical equations and thus resonate to produce an inaudible symphony of music. (Wikipedia)
  • “her peacocks”: Aristotle referred to the peacock’s vain reputation. The Roman poet Ennius, following the Pythagorean teaching of rebirth, dreamed that Homer’s soul had passed into a peacock and then into himself.
  • “the golden cock perched at the foot of her bed, that it was really Pythagoras shedding his golden feathers”: In Lucian’s dialogue “The Dream, or the Cock,” a cobbler’s rooster reveals that it is Pythagoras reborn. Pythagoras was said to have a golden thigh. Rimsky-Korsakov’s opera The Golden Cockerel (1909), from Pushkin’s tale (1834), has a golden cock that warns a tsar.

Chapter 25

  • “circles within circles, orbs within orbs, many spheres of influence”: The Pythagoreans held that the planets, turning in their spheres, make a music of the heavens that human ears cannot hear.

Chapter 26

  • “the heavenly harmonies forever fixed above the floods of time, dark time, those unmoving golden spheres”: “Musica universalis,” the music of the spheres: Boethius, in De institutione musica (c. 500), divided music into musica mundana (the music of the spheres), humana, and instrumentalis. The idea goes back to Pythagoras. Mr. Spitzer imagines “the silent music which requires neither instruments nor voices.” Mr. Spitzer, a semi-retired lawyer and composer of “silent music,” is attuned to the music of the spheres, the eternal, silent harmonies beyond earthly sounds.

Chapter 27

  • “the Pythagorean harmonies, the muffled planetary voices, the music of the silent spheres”: The music of the spheres.

Chapter 29

  • “when the heavenly harmonies were established on a surer foundation”: The music of the spheres.

Chapter 36

  • “He had thought he was listening to the music of the spheres”: The music of the spheres, or musica universalis, is the metaphysical principle that mathematical relationships express qualities or “tones” of energy.

Chapter 37

  • “this mistaken earth which was perhaps its counter-earth”: The Pythagorean Philolaus set a counter-earth, the Antichthon, beyond the Earth, always hidden from it, to bring the heavenly bodies to ten (Aristotle, On the Heavens 2.13).

Chapter 38

  • “the beautiful music of the spheres”: Musica Universalis (music of the spheres) concept incorporates the metaphysical principle that mathematical relationships express qualities or “tones” of energy that manifests in numbers, visual angles, shapes and sounds which are all connected within a pattern of proportion. This has been explored by Pythagoras, Plato, Aristotle (he rejected the idea), Boethius developed three categories of music (musica mundana, musica humana, and musica quae in quibusdam constituta est instrumentis), Kepler expounded the idea in Harmonices Mundi (1619). Boethius’s writings were later illustrated, and Young may have taken inspiration from these illustrations for the novel.

Chapter 38 · Music of the Spheres

  • Mr. Spitzer’s cosmic music imagery symbolizes the ineffable and eternal aspects of existence beyond human comprehension.

Chapter 48

  • “the unheard music of this waning planet”: The music of the spheres, the harmony Pythagoras heard in the turning planets, is music no living ear can hear. “Heard melodies are sweet, but those unheard / Are sweeter” (John Keats, “Ode on a Grecian Urn,” 1819).

Chapter 50

  • “threads wrapped around a spindle”: In Plato’s myth of Er the heavens turn on the Spindle of Necessity, and “on the upper surface of each circle is a siren, who goes round with them, hymning a single tone or note. The eight together form one harmony” (Republic 10, 617b, trans. Benjamin Jowett, 1871).

Chapter 53

  • “the music of the spheres”: Pythagoras held that the planets make a music by their motion, too constant for human ears to notice; in the Myth of Er at the end of Plato’s Republic a siren on each sphere sings one note, and together they make one harmony.

Chapter 55

  • “such a thing as a non-aural music”: Pythagoras taught that the planets make a music by their motion that human ears cannot hear. Boethius divided music into three kinds, the music of the world, the music of the human being, and the music of instruments, and only the last can be heard (Boethius, De institutione musica 1.2, about 510).

Chapter 59

  • “this planet singing as it died”: In the Pythagorean music of the spheres, each planet sounds a note as it moves.

Chapter 70

  • “the unheard music of the spheres”: The passage reflects a philosophical meditation on the unavoidability of change, the human compulsion to find or impose meaning, and the possibility that such meaning is not inherent but constructed. It challenges the belief in a fixed cosmic order and suggests that what we perceive as meaningful patterns may be self-deceptions, resonating with existentialist skepticism and the impermanence stressed in ancient and modern philosophies. Pythagoras taught that the planets sound a harmony too perfect for human ears. Compare Keats, in the ode Vera has already echoed: “Heard melodies are sweet, but those unheard / Are sweeter.”
