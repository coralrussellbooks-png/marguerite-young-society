Harp Song for a Radical

Chapter 7. A Boy in a Breaking Nation

← Chapter 6 · The Unfinished Harp: Reading Harp Song for a Radical · Chapter 8 →

Dred Scott, John Brown, the Lincoln funeral train, and Young's portrait of Mary Todd Lincoln

The chapters covering Debs's childhood (pages 198 to 262) contain surprisingly little of Debs. Young surrounds the boy with the nation's great quarrels and lets him stand at the edge of the picture: a small figure spinning a top, walking to Sunday school, reading his schoolbook. The foreground belongs to Dred Scott, John Brown, Abraham Lincoln, Andrew Johnson, and, at greatest length, Lincoln's widow.

The method is the same one Young used in Part One. The boy is shaped by events he barely understands, and the book shows those events so that a reader can feel their weight on him.

Two burials

Young introduces Dred Scott through his graves. Debs was "not quite three years old" when Scott was first buried, and older when Scott's body was dug up and moved (202). The Supreme Court decision that bore Scott's name had declared that a Black man could claim no protection from the Constitution. Young describes the chief justice reading it for three hours in a voice so faint that many words were lost, in a courtroom where "justice was blind and deaf and dumb even more than if it had been a stone" (204).

John Brown enters the same way, through a child's memory. On the day Brown was hanged in December 1859, four-year-old Eugene was spinning a top (203). He grew up hearing his father repeat that for Victor Hugo, "John Brown had been a greater man than George Washington" (204). Decades later, Young notes, admirers would call Debs "the second John Brown, the second Abe Lincoln, and the first Debs there ever was" (210).

This is The Past Doesn't Go Away at the scale of a single childhood. The boy did not choose his heroes from a list. They were pressed into him by his father's talk at the grocery counter, before he could read.

The schoolbook that left things out

Young is sharp about what the boy was taught. McGuffey's readers, the standard schoolbooks of the era, taught children that "Socrates died like a philosopher but that Jesus Christ died like a god" (221). They taught property rights through little moral tales. They said nothing about the Fugitive Slave Act, Dred Scott, or John Brown, even in the middle of a war over slavery. In McGuffey's world, "The struggles for freedom in the McGuffey's Acropolis were always of the remote past" (221).

This is Language Shapes What's Possible applied to a child's education. A schoolbook that puts every struggle for freedom in ancient Greece or Rome teaches, without saying so, that freedom is already won. Young notes that the boy's religious life ran on different lines. His Catholic mother and freethinking father let the children choose their own church or none, and "Debs's church would always be, it has been said of him, a church without walls" (219).

The joke as a safety valve

Young's Lincoln is a joker. "Jokes had provided a safety valve through which the steam could escape and without which old Abe would have blown up like a riverboat or a train" (213). For Lincoln, she writes, the joke was "the language of the skeptic brain transmuted by the faithful heart" (213). It is the same double vision she found in Debs and Twain, humor carrying grief rather than denying it.

When Lincoln's body went home to Springfield by train in 1865, Young describes towns so draped in mourning that there were "rivers of black crepe streaming across this continent" (216). The ten-year-old in Terre Haute was living in a country that was grieving along its railroad lines.

The black widow

The longest portrait in this stretch belongs to Mary Todd Lincoln, and it is one of the most compassionate in the book. Young follows her through widowhood: the debts, the long fight for a pension from a Congress that had hated her husband, the humiliation of William Herndon's lectures claiming Lincoln had never loved her, the deaths of her sons. In 1875, at her son Robert's request, a Chicago court declared her insane, and she was sent to a private asylum in Batavia, Illinois. A later court declared her sane. Young notes bitterly that some people would believe the first verdict "but not the reversal which had declared that she was sane" (256).

Young does not sanctify her. She says plainly that Mrs. Lincoln's "social consciousness" had shriveled "to so thin a thread that she could see little or no connection between her struggles and those of the sufferings of the blacks and the reds" (249). This is Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act at work. Young stays in the room with a difficult, grieving, sometimes selfish woman without either excusing or condemning her.

Then comes the thread that ties the portrait to Debs. Robert Todd Lincoln, the son who had his mother committed, would become "the future most important cog of the Pullman empire and lawyer for George Mortimer Pullman" (254). He later served as president of the Pullman Company. Mrs. Lincoln wrote to him in fury that he had "injured himself, not her" (255). Two decades later, Debs would lead the great strike against Pullman.

This is the Crossing Point, reached by an unexpected road. The same family that gave the country its emancipator would give Pullman his lawyer. Young does not draw the moral. She lets the dates carry it.

What the lens shows

These chapters are about inheritance. Not One Self appears in a boy formed at once by a Catholic mother, a freethinking father, a Protestant Sunday school, and a war. Grief Doesn't End runs through the Lincoln household. Reality Is Constructed shows in a schoolbook that erased the living struggle for freedom and a court that could erase a woman's sanity at her son's request.

Checking the record

  • The Dred Scott decision. Young places it "the very day after" Buchanan's inauguration (198). Buchanan took office on March 4, 1857. The decision came on March 6, two days later.

  • Scott's second burial. Young places Debs at "about nine years old" and the nation in the middle of the war when Scott's body was moved (202). The reburial took place in 1867, after the war, when Debs was eleven.

  • The Lincoln pension and trial. These are accurate. Congress passed the widow's pension in July 1870. The Chicago insanity hearing took place in May 1875, and it was reversed in June 1876. Robert Todd Lincoln did become Pullman's lawyer and, after Pullman's death, president of the company.
