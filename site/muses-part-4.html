Inviting the Muses, Part 4

← Part 3 · Inviting the Muses: A Reading, Part by Part · Part 5 →

Part 4 covers "The Doll People" in full and the first half of "The Old Sweetheart," which breaks off mid-story as Riley falls for a woman he has never met.

"The Doll People" (Mademoiselle, 1950)

On its surface this is a magazine feature about American dolls. Underneath, it is Young's argument that small, overlooked objects carry a nation's history better than official records. Johnny Gruelle, a political cartoonist, is said to have made a truer "commentary on American life" with Raggedy Ann than with any cartoon, because "everything that happens in the great world is reflected in the little."

The rag doll is her hero: "the oldest doll in America, the original folk doll," sewn from scraps in a scrap bag and "far from perfect, for she was the doll of a pragmatic people who were far from perfect themselves." Her face might be cut from old underwear, her features painted in pokeberry juice, and sometimes, "in the rush of creative events, her nose was forgotten." That detail is Young's kind of comedy, and it also restates her aesthetic from "Complex Life": the imperfect, improvised thing is the honest one.

She reads the doll trade as cultural history. American doll bodies were long fitted with European heads, "a commentary on the intellectual scene, much of our culture also coming from abroad." Only when submarine warfare cut off German doll heads in 1917 did Gruelle revive Raggedy Ann, a doll whose head, "though simple, was her own." There are good anecdotes: pirates who board a ship for gold and find only breakable doll heads; Edward and Wallis dolls made the same size "so that they should not seem conspicuous"; quintuplet dolls sold at birth and at six months to create "the illusion of growth"; Grace Putnam Storey, who modeled a doll on a newborn at a Salvation Army shelter and had to market it "against the obstacles of masculine resistance." One line records "old mammy dolls with nut-brown faces" appearing in the postwar South, and Young passes over the image without comment, the same kind of unexamined period note seen in the Midwest essay.

The essay's second half moves from dolls to Indiana's literature of childhood. Raggedy Ann is linked to Riley's "Little Orphant Annie" and the Raggedy Man, and here Young makes a sharp point about nostalgia. The rag doll represents "a past which may never have been what we think it was," and her colors are "scraps of an illusion." The proof is the real Orphant Annie, Mary Alice Smith. Riley, not knowing where she was, "killed her off in the grand old nineteenth-century manner, just with a stroke of his pen," giving her a death of a broken heart. She turned up after his death, alive on an Indiana farm with the husband who had not died in the war, too busy "shooing the chickens off the porch" to know she had become a legend. It is the plainest statement yet of a theme running through the collection: the story told about a person and the actual person rarely match, and the actual person usually has other things to do.

The ending turns darker. Young brings in Capote and McCullers's The Member of the Wedding, "where John Henry, the child like a little old man, a child who soon will die, plays with a doll." The last line is "And thus we see the impermanence of people, the permanence of things, even of dolls." Set against "My Grandmother's Foot," where the parents love "inanimate objects" as "substitutes for reality," the thought has turned over. Here objects are not a substitute for life but what survives it.

"The Old Sweetheart" (first half)

Riley has now appeared in every part of the book: Old James's poetic rival, the children's poet who "drank to forget the children," the creator of Orphant Annie. This essay finally gives him full attention, and it reads like a chapter from a biography in progress. It is framed as a detective question, "Who was the old sweetheart of James Whitcomb Riley?", and Young warns at once that the search "must take us into the night of the soul." Riley, she notes, asked the same question in his poem.

She builds the portrait from the inside out. His mother wrote poems in the household ledger, believed in fairies, and had heard Swedenborgian ideas from her friend Johnny Appleseed. Her death, when he was not quite twenty-one, drew "something like a lens" over his eyes. Outwardly he was a clown and mimic; his violin was his sweetheart. He later bought back his boyhood home and imagined it furnished, and when Greenfield restored it as a memorial, his poems were the most accurate guide, down to "the pheasant-feather fan that was used to sweep away the ashes." Life was reconstructed from art, which is the reverse of what realist critics expect.

Young names his method directly as a "Proustian dedication to the remembrance of things past," and sees marriage as its enemy: "Should not his wife be rivaled by that greatest love, the past?" That puts Riley alongside Old James, another Indianapolis man preferring memory to the present, though Young treats Riley with far more tenderness.

The candidates follow one at a time, each tied to a keepsake in "the old hair trunk": Nell, who believed in his gifts; Adda, with hair "as black as the midnight shadows," one of the girls passing west on the National Road; and a school-teaching poetess who wanted marriage and a roof, and who could not wait for a poor, sometimes drunk poet. Young gives her a fair hearing. "Who would pay for the firelight?" is a real question, not a sneer. The comedy of his luck is kept, too: a father at the door growling that his daughter "hain't goin' with you. She's got the measles." The famous poem itself, she says, was written in the margins of Blackstone while he studied law, and his poems were stuffed in his hat, his cuffs, the soles of his shoes, falling out of his umbrella "like a flurry of snowflakes."

Part 4 stops just as the strangest candidate arrives: a young woman in a remote forest, introduced by a wandering friend, McClanahan, whom she loves hopelessly. Riley never met her. What he fell for was a tintype of her hand, "satin-cuffed, holding between its long fingers a folded Chinese fan." After the grandmother's foot, the baby's hand in the shoe box, and Moore's bluebird foot, the collection's fascination with the detached body part comes back once more, now as the object of a love that exists only in letters.

Across part 4

Both essays are about the gap between the made thing and the living one. The rag doll and the old sweetheart are both assembled from scraps, and both stand in for a past that may never have existed as remembered. Young is drawn to that longing, and she writes it with real affection, but she keeps setting the real person beside the legend: Mary Alice Smith churning butter, the schoolteacher asking who will pay for the firelight. The permanence she grants to things always comes with a reminder that people moved on.
