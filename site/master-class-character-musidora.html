Musidora: The One They Looked At and Did Not Know

Part of Teaching a Writing Masterclass, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Mrs. Tidings · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Mrs. Hathaway →

Spoiler note: This portrait is written for readers who have finished the novel. It discusses the character’s whole story, including how it ends.

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Gertrude Hathaway Tidings · Musidora Tidings · Peron Spitzer

The town builds whole lives out of nothing, a husband no one saw, a murder no one witnessed, and it cannot recognize the one woman standing in plain sight. Musidora is the daughter of Mrs. Tidings and the sister of Gertrude, and she is the character the gossip of the town circles without ever seeing her.

What the town knows of her is all disgust and distance. She "grew up with the water on her brain." She pushes "a battered baby carriage" from house to house to pick rags, and no one knows why, and she throws the rags into the river so the water might carry them away. She works "off and on at the Tavern," "crawling on all fours," "cleaning the slop out," for "scraps of the landlord's bread." She is barefoot, "those big, bare feet with the blackened soles," "marble-colored eyes," "mottled skin," "grey, ragged curls, water running down her legs." She is "always a baby inside, always another outside." And she "never speaks." When someone wonders why, the answer the book allows is the plainest one: "Maybe she had nothing to say."

Ask the wrong question and Musidora is grotesque, a bit of squalor at the edge of the tavern talk. Ask the right one and she is the fourth claim of Radical Immanence carried past where the clam-digger left it. The truth is not at the shining center but at the margin, in the person nobody watches, and Musidora is the margin of the margin, a mute rag-picker even the other marginal figures step over.

Her own family cannot place her. She begs at Gertrude's and at her mother's, and the two of them will not speak to each other, and she "does not seem to know them," and they "do not act as if they ever saw her before." This is the book's deepest wound worn by its lowest figure. Everyone here loves a composite of the lost and cannot see the living person in the room. Catherine grieves Peron and pours his tea without knowing him. The mother rides behind a coachman whose face never turns. Musidora is that failure of recognition made total and made mutual, a woman unknown to the two people who made her and raised her, standing in their doorways with her hand out, a stranger to her own blood.

She is Esther Longtree's sister in kind. Both are the town's perpetually pregnant woman, "always a baby inside, always another outside," bearing what will not stop coming and mourned by no one. But where Esther is given a whole book of interior, her losses named and counted, Musidora is given nothing from the inside at all. She is Esther seen only from the street, the same everlasting grief with the door to it locked, a silence the town fills with revulsion because it will not fill it with attention. And her silence is not empty. Like the clam-digger, whose closed mouth is the shape his knowing takes, Musidora's wordlessness may be the last dignity she has. "Maybe she's sane," the gossip admits, twice, unable to decide, and the doubt is the point. The town needs her to be an idiot because an idiot can be dismissed, and it cannot quite manage the dismissal, because something in the silent woman crawling on the floor will not reduce all the way to nothing.

The turn for a writer is the hardest one to practice, because Musidora is the person a writer is most tempted to skip. The town invents a husband for Mrs. Tidings and a murder for Mrs. Hathaway and a whole biography for the mother's absent chauffeur, and it does all that inventing while a real woman gathers rags in the actual street and is seen by no one. That is the writer's ordinary sin, to lavish invention on the vivid and the absent while the present, unglamorous, silent person goes unwritten. Young plants Musidora at the very bottom of the book to catch the reader in exactly that reflex, to let the gossip skate over her and the reader skate with it, and then to let the plainest sentence in the passage stop the skating: "Maybe she had nothing to say." The lesson is that the figure beneath even the town's tireless inventing, the one so overlooked that no one troubles to make up a story worthy of her, is the one who most needs the writer to stay. To witness Musidora is to write the person the story was built to walk past, and to refuse, for once, to walk past her.
