Chapter 13: Farewell to the Last Certitude

← Chapter 12 · Guide Home · Chapter 14 →

Research notes: Chapter 13

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Music, Sound, and Silence

Symbols: Horses and Carriages · Silence and Deafness

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Angels and Archangels · Deafness and Sign Language · Fairy Tales and Folklore · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · Romantic Poets

We have been building toward this chapter for twelve chapters. Young has been placing the pieces with great care: the plain alarm clock, the stopped clocks, the garden of the blind, the portrait of Miss MacIntosh’s philosophy, the sentence at the end of Chapter 12 that told you something enormous was coming.

Chapter 13 is where it arrives.

Something happens to Vera on the night of her fourteenth birthday that changes how she sees Miss MacIntosh, and therefore how she sees everything. Young tells us this has happened before she shows us what happened. That choice is deliberate and important, and we’ll come back to it.

What actually happens in Chapter 13

Vera wanders through the estate’s gardens on the night of her fourteenth birthday. She moves through what she now calls the garden of the deaf: a different garden from the blind one in Chapter 12, or perhaps the same garden understood differently, a place where sound has died. She sees chessmen moving on a chessboard of light and shadow, life-sized, played by invisible hands. She sees marble statues, mist-flowers that fly away like moths when she reaches for them, gargoyles spouting water, cherubim made of barnacles.

And somewhere in this night, she sees something about Miss MacIntosh that she will never unsee.

Young does not show us the moment directly. Not yet. What she gives us instead is its aftermath: the knowledge that Vera now carries, the understanding that Miss MacIntosh (the one solid, ordinary, unambiguous person in the entire novel) was not what she appeared to be.

The chapter then moves into a long, extraordinary conversation between Catherine and Mr. Spitzer about the garden of the deaf: a garden where, Catherine insists, the ear is dead, where no sound reaches, where Mr. Spitzer’s music can only be silence because no voice carries and no one can hear another speaking farewell.

The rider over Lake Constance

Before anything else, pause at the passage near the chapter’s opening. It is one of the most important in the entire novel, and Young places it here for a reason.

A traveler on horseback crosses Lake Constance in winter, in a blizzard, at night. He cannot see anything, not the landscape, not his reflection, not where land ends and lake begins. He rides the forty-six miles in blinding snow, thinking he is crossing solid ground. When he reaches the other shore and looks back and sees what he has actually crossed (a lake that was never completely frozen, that no one had ever ridden across and survived) he drops dead from “the shock of the after-knowledge.”

He was safe as long as he didn’t know. The knowing killed him.

This is what Vera says her ignorance about Miss MacIntosh was: the thing that kept her alive and sane. And this is what she is about to lose. She has reached the other shore. She is looking back. She is about to see where she has been.

What Vera now knows, without telling us yet

Young builds the chapter around an absence at its center. We know that Vera saw something. We know it changed everything. We know that after this night she will never again see Miss MacIntosh as she knew her. But Young withholds the specific revelation for just a little longer.

This is not coyness. It is the structure enacting the experience. Vera’s knowledge arrived the same way: she saw something she wasn’t prepared for, and then she had to live inside the new reality while the old one dissolved around her. Young makes the reader inhabit that same disorientation. You know something happened. You are not yet sure what. You are on the shore, looking back at the frozen lake, not yet able to name what you crossed.

What we are given instead of the revelation itself is the cascade of farewells that opens the chapter. Farewell to Miss MacIntosh. Farewell to the last certitude. Farewell to the old moralities and the proverbial wisdom and the rote questions and answers. Farewell to What Cheer and its cherry blossoms and peach orchards. Farewell to the “fabric of self-deception we two persons had lived by, and farewell to the past and its illusion, and farewell to the reason, the purpose, the goal, the future.”

All of it. Everything that Miss MacIntosh represented is dismantled in a single page, before Young has told us why.

The garden of the deaf

The chapter’s second half is a sustained, heartbreaking conversation between Catherine and Mr. Spitzer about a garden where, according to Catherine, the ear is dead.

She is not speaking metaphorically, or rather she is, but she means it literally too. In this garden (part of the vast estate, companion to the garden of the blind from Chapter 12) sound has ceased. One can see the bird but not hear the song. One can see a lover’s face but not hear his voice speaking farewell. The ear that is dead, when Mr. Spitzer asks whose ear she means, is his: his dead brother’s ear, buried somewhere without ceremony, silent in the ground.

Mr. Spitzer pushes back, carefully. He points out that his brother was not buried on this estate. He also admits, in his precise, lawyerly way, that in his right ear there is “always a subdued roaring, a noise” his brother’s physician told him does not come from this world, and that he is learning “not to turn his head every time he” hears a traffic horn that isn’t there.

Catherine is not persuaded. She keeps talking, louder and louder, even perhaps after Mr. Spitzer has quietly left, still arguing with the sense of him that remains in the room. The sound is fled from the universe. His music can only be silence. “One should see the bird.” “One should not hear the song.”

For Mr. Spitzer, whose life’s work is music he never plays and never finishes, this is a more devastating verdict than Catherine probably realizes she is delivering.

What to watch for as you keep reading

Light spoilers: This section hints at what happens later in the novel. Skip it if you want to meet the story fresh.

Chapter 14 will give you what Chapter 13 withheld. Young has prepared you as carefully as she can. The revelation about Miss MacIntosh is coming, and when it arrives it will be both shocking and, in retrospect, inevitable: all the signs were there.

Notice, going forward, how the novel’s language around Miss MacIntosh shifts. The plain, practical, unambiguous woman remains present in Vera’s memory, but she is now always seen from the other shore. Everything she said and did carries a different weight. The thimble engraved Juliet, 1899. The needles and pins bristling from her shirtwaist. The one face, not two. All of it will mean something different now.

The garden of the deaf joins the garden of the blind as one of the novel’s organizing spaces. Young is building a landscape of absent senses: a world that can be touched but not seen, heard but not touched, seen but not heard. Pay attention to which sense is missing in each passage. It tells you something about what kind of knowledge is available in that moment.
