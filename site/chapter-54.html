Summary

From his years with Catherine, Mr. Spitzer knows the underworld of dreams. He wanders a Boston whose buildings keep rearranging themselves. He helps derelicts and lost heirs, and is troubled by an old accusation that he stole a rajah's jewel. He prefers glass jewels to real ones. The chapter ends with his elegy, written on scraps of paper, blowing away down a dark street while he stands weeping and stamping his cane.

Key passages

From the research

  • At the beginning, Mr. Spitzer is described as knowing the "subterranean world—of the vast unconscious like a dream no one had dreamed," filled with fantastical images such as golden spires sunken under the sea, underwater fountains, narrow queens with wooden faces, and old heroes nurtured on mares’ milk. This passage establishes the surreal, dreamlike atmosphere that pervades the chapter. It symbolizes the unconscious mind’s vastness and mystery, reflecting Mr. Spitzer’s psychological state and his passive role in understanding things that transcend rational comprehension.
  • Mr. Spitzer hears a "sound effects man" who can imitate everything from trains, roosters, barking dogs, to the wind and ice creaking. This character, though unseen by Mr. Spitzer, fills the environment with chaotic noises that disturb the silence Mr. Spitzer cherishes but also evokes an unwilling envy in him. The sound effects man embodies the chaotic, uncontrollable external world that contrasts with Mr. Spitzer’s desire for silence and order. It also metaphorically represents the intrusion of madness, memory, and noise into Mr. Spitzer’s internal world after his brother’s death.
  • Throughout the chapter, Mr. Spitzer wrestles with his identity, often confusing himself with his brother Peron, who has died. He searches for him among the living and the dead, blurring the lines between himself and his brother, reality and dream, living and dead. This passage explores themes of duality, identity crisis, and grief. The indistinguishability of the brothers and the shifting realities emphasize the fragile nature of selfhood and the impact of loss.
  • Mr. Spitzer wanders through a city resembling Boston but constantly changing, with streets moving, buildings appearing and disappearing, and landmarks like the Flatiron Building or the Fishback Building misplaced. The city becomes a metaphor for Mr. Spitzer’s mental landscape: uncertain, unstable, and haunted by memory and illusion. This shifting environment reflects his inner confusion and his search for meaning and connection.
  • Mr. Spitzer meets various figures (derelicts, disguised persons, forgotten heirs, and those who have lost their identity or status) such as the old cancer specialist who does not know his own greatness, or the old woman who had been Lady Macbeth but now wanders as a man. These encounters highlight themes of loss, forgotten greatness, and the fragility of identity. They also underline Mr. Spitzer’s role as a mediator attempting to restore lost connections or identities, reflecting his own struggle with self and memory.
  • Mr. Spitzer is agitated and fearful over a missing jewel from a rajah’s crown, an incident he is somehow implicated in but does not remember clearly. This episode symbolizes the elusive nature of truth and guilt, and the burden of unremembered or misunderstood events. The jewel itself can be seen as a metaphor for something precious lost or perhaps time, identity, or reality.
  • Mr. Spitzer seeks out "lost heirs," "forgotten legacies," and "persons who had forgotten their bank accounts," trying to restore what has been lost. He helps the poor and forgotten, buying clothes and necessities for those in need. This portrays Mr. Spitzer as a figure of compassion and redemption, despite his own suffering and confusion. It also reflects the broader theme of searching for and reclaiming lost fragments of identity and memory.
  • Mr. Spitzer is described as a composer who carries his musical notes on scraps of paper, composing an elegy that is fragmented and incomplete, with motifs of mistake and truth intertwined. The music represents life’s ephemeral and fragmented nature, the interplay of creation and error, memory and loss. Mr. Spitzer’s music is his means of making sense of his existence and grief, even as it remains unresolved.
  • The chapter repeatedly returns to Mr. Spitzer’s grief for his dead brother and his own fractured existence, caught between life and death, reality and dream, certainty and doubt. The imagery of snow, fog, shadows, and shifting streets reinforce this pervasive melancholy and ambiguity. This is the emotional core of the chapter, emphasizing the universal human experience of loss and the difficulty of finding meaning in a world that is constantly changing and often incomprehensible.
  • The chapter closes with Mr. Spitzer, alone, his elegy scattered like music fluttering away in the wind, a figure both present and absent, caught in a liminal space beyond human knowledge. This ending encapsulates the chapter’s meditation on mortality, artistic creation, and the transience of life and memory. Mr. Spitzer’s elegy symbolizes his enduring yet fading presence in a world indifferent to individual existence.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer (Joachim Spitzer)

  • The protagonist, an aging, reflective man struggling with his brother’s death and his own sense of self. He is a man of order and logic but is overwhelmed by grief and uncertainty, experiencing shifting realities and identities. Mr. Spitzer is profoundly lonely and disoriented, caught between madness and sanity.
  • He experiences shifting identities with Peron, unsure of who he is or whether he is alive or dead.
  • His grief is pervasive, touching on universal themes of loss and the search for meaning.
  • He is meticulous but uncertain, often doubting his perceptions and memories.
  • Despite his despair, he displays moments of kindness and creative impulse, especially through his music.

Peron Spitzer

  • Mr. Spitzer’s deceased brother, described as wild, gregarious, and reckless, almost the opposite of Joachim. Peron’s life and death haunt Joachim, and his presence is felt as a contrasting force driving Joachim’s reflections and wanderings.

Sound Effects Man

  • A mysterious figure associated with Peron, capable of producing chaotic, mimicry-like sounds symbolizing the noise and madness of life. He represents the opposite of Spitzer’s silence and order.

  • Mr. Spitzer oscillates between his own identity and that of his brother, sometimes confused about who he truly is. The chapter explores the thin line between sanity and madness, with Joachim embodying order and Peron chaos, yet both are reflections of each other.

See: Dreams and Imagination · Death and Mortality

  • The narrative blurs the boundaries between the living and the dead. Mr. Spitzer’s experience suggests he might be dead or in a liminal state ("he was dead"), emphasizing the ambiguity of existence and the unconscious mind's role in understanding life.

Memory, Time, and Reality

See: Illusion and Reality · Memory and the Past · Time

  • Time is fluid and non-linear. Newspapers are dated years ago but read as current; streets and cities shift; memories and experiences overlap. This challenges the concept of objective reality and highlights the fragility of memory.

Isolation and Grief

See: Isolation and Connection · Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • Joachim’s profound loneliness is expressed through his endless wandering and failed attempts to find connection or meaning after Peron’s death. His grief touches on universal sorrow, described as a "precious grief" that illuminates him despite its pain.

The Search for Meaning

See: The Search for Meaning

  • Mr. Spitzer’s journey is a metaphorical search for self-understanding, love, and redemption, yet it is fraught with failures, illusions, and ambiguities.

Music and Creation

See: Music, Sound, and Silence

  • Music symbolizes order, creativity, and the attempt to impose meaning. Mr. Spitzer is described as a composer of life’s elegy, writing fragments of music on his clothing, representing his fragmented perception and desperate attempt to create coherence.

  • A phonograph, later called a gramophone, and since the 1940s a record player, or more recently a turntable, is a device for the mechanical and analogue reproduction of sound. The sound vibration waveforms are recorded as corresponding physical deviations of a helical or spiral groove engraved, etched, incised, or impressed into the surface of a rotating cylinder or disc, called a record. To recreate the sound, the surface is similarly rotated while a playback stylus traces the groove and is therefore vibrated by it, faintly reproducing the recorded sound. In early acoustic phonographs, the stylus vibrated a diaphragm that produced sound waves coupled to the open air through a flaring horn, or directly to the listener's ears through stethoscope-type earphones. The phonograph was invented in 1877 by Thomas Edison; its use would rise the following year. Alexander Graham Bell's Volta Laboratory made several improvements in the 1880s and introduced the graphophone, including the use of wax-coated cardboard cylinders and a cutting stylus that moved from side to side in a zigzag groove around the record. In the 1890s, Emile Berliner initiated the transition from phonograph cylinders to flat discs with a spiral groove running from the periphery to near the center, coining the term gramophone for disc record players, which is predominantly used in many languages. Later improvements through the years included modifications to the turntable and its drive system, stylus, pickup system, and the sound and equalization systems. The disc phonograph record was the dominant commercial audio distribution format throughout most of the 20th century, and phonographs became the first example of home audio that people owned and used at their residences. In the 1960s, the use of 8-track cartridges and cassette tapes were introduced as alternatives. By the late 1980s, phonograph use had declined sharply due to the popularity of cassettes and the rise of the compact disc. However, records have undergone a revival since the late 2000s.

  • "Indian massacre" The phrase eventually became commonly used to also describe mass killings of American Indians. Killings described as "massacres" often had an element of indiscriminate targeting, barbarism, or genocidal intent. According to historian Jeffrey Ostler, "Any discussion of genocide must, of course, eventually consider the so-called Indian Wars, the term commonly used for U.S. Army campaigns to subjugate Indian nations of the American West beginning in the 1860s. In an older historiography, key events in this history were narrated as battles. It is now more common for scholars to refer to these events as massacres. This is especially so of a Colorado territorial militia's slaughter of Cheyennes at Sand Creek (1864) and the army's slaughter of Shoshones at Bear River (1863), Blackfeet on the Marias River (1870), and Lakotas at Wounded Knee (1890). Some scholars have begun referring to these events as “genocidal massacres,” defined as the annihilation of a portion of a larger group, sometimes to provide a lesson to the larger group." In The Wild Frontier: Atrocities during the American-Indian War from Jamestown Colony to Wounded Knee, lawyer William M. Osborn compiled a list of alleged and actual atrocities in what would eventually become the continental United States, from first contact in 1511 until 1890. His parameters for inclusion included the intentional and indiscriminate murder, torture, or mutilation of civilians, the wounded, and prisoners. His list included 7,193 people who died from atrocities perpetrated by those of European descent, and 9,156 people who died from atrocities perpetrated by Native Americans. In An American Genocide, The United States and the California Catastrophe, 1846–1873, historian Benjamin Madley recorded the numbers of killings of California Indians between 1846 and 1873.   • “Wall Street broker” The history of Wall Street brokers traces the evolution of securities trading from informal outdoor dealings to the high-tech, regulated global financial markets of today. The profession has shifted from being a specialized service for the wealthy to a digitized industry, leading traditional brokers to focus on financial advising rather than trade execution. In the late 1700s, merchants and speculators met on Wall Street to trade U.S. government bonds and shares of early banks. Business was initially conducted informally, often taking place under a buttonwood tree outside 68 Wall Street or in nearby coffeehouses. To formalize their business and edge out the auctioneers who had previously dominated securities trading, 24 brokers signed the Buttonwood Agreement in 1792. This established a fixed commission rate and required them to give preference to each other in negotiations. This agreement became the foundation of what would later be the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE). As the market grew, trading split between the formal NYSE and an informal "curb" market for riskier stocks. For much of the 19th century, brokers who traded stocks for small, new, or speculative companies operated in the streets of lower Manhattan. These "curbstone brokers" specialized in securities for emerging industries like railroads, mining, and oil. The Open Board of Stock Brokers, founded in 1864 by former curb brokers, competed with the NYSE before merging with it in 1869. In 1921, the curb market moved indoors and eventually became the American Stock Exchange (AMEX). The introduction of the stock ticker in 1867 brought real-time stock prices to brokerage offices, a vast improvement over pneumatic tubes and runners. The "Roaring Twenties" and the crash of 1929 saw an explosion in brokerage activity and a dramatic increase in public participation in the market. Leaders of firms like Merrill Lynch began encouraging middle-class Americans to invest in common stocks, fueling market growth. Brokers aggressively promoted speculative investments and margin trading (borrowing money to buy stocks) to boost their commissions. This pushed stock prices to unsustainable levels. The stock market crash of 1929, triggered by panic selling after prices began to fall, led to widespread broker bankruptcies and financial ruin for many clients. In the aftermath, government regulation was introduced to prevent such events in the future. After the crash, the industry faced greater oversight and eventually underwent a technological transformation. The Securities Exchange Act of 1934 created the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) to regulate the securities industry and protect investors.

Historically, carbuncles were valued for their beauty and were believed to possess magical and healing properties, with mentions in ancient texts and prominent use in Victorian jewelry.
  • “Tower of London and the ravens” The ravens of the Tower of London are a group of at least six captive ravens (nine in 2021) resident at the Tower of London. Their presence is traditionally believed to protect the Crown and the Tower; a belief holds that "if the Tower of London ravens are lost or fly away, the Crown will fall and Britain with it." Some historians, including the Tower's official historian, believe the "Tower's raven mythology is likely to be a Victorian flight of fantasy". The earliest known reference to captive ravens at the Tower is an illustration from 1883.

  • “the Ritz” The Ritz hotels, now a symbol of luxury, originated from the vision of Swiss hotelier César Ritz in the late 19th century. He partnered with chef Auguste Escoffier to create the first Ritz hotel in Paris in 1898, followed by the Ritz in London. These hotels redefined luxury hospitality, emphasizing personalized service and elegant accommodations, including en-suite bathrooms and telephones in every room, which were revolutionary at the time. In 1911, Albert Keller acquired the rights to franchise the Ritz name in North America, leading to the first Ritz-Carlton in New York City. The Ritz-Carlton brand, born from this expansion, continued to build upon Ritz's original vision of refined elegance and impeccable service, establishing hotels in various locations.
  • “Catherine the Great” Catherine the Great was the German-born Empress of Russia from 1762 to 1796, known for expanding Russia's territory, westernizing the country through Enlightenment ideals, championing the arts and education, and reorganizing the Russian legal code. She ascended to the throne after staging a coup against her husband, Peter III, and reigned for 34 years, a period often considered Russia's Golden Age. Her reign significantly increased Russia's influence in Europe and saw major developments in culture, science, and trade. Born Sophie of Anhalt-Zerbst in Prussia, she married into the Russian royal family and converted to Orthodox Christianity to become Ekaterina (Catherine). She seized power in 1762 by orchestrating a military coup that overthrew her unpopular husband, Peter III, shortly after he became emperor. Catherine embraced the ideas of the European Enlightenment, corresponding with thinkers like Voltaire and promoting a more "enlightened" vision for Russia. Under her rule, Russia gained significant new territories, including Crimea, and played a key role in the partitions of Poland, substantially increasing Russia's landmass. Catherine was a significant patron of the arts and learning, fostering a cultural renaissance and establishing educational and scientific institutions. She also started the art collection that formed the basis of the Hermitage Museum. She initiated reforms to the Russian legal system and reorganized the country's administration, with changes that lasted until the Russian Revolution. Catherine is remembered as one of Russia's greatest rulers, a figure of national pride for many Russians, whose reign solidified Russia's position as a major European power.
  • “soap coupons” B.T. Babbitt, a soap manufacturer, initiated a promotion where customers could send in 25 wrappers from his Sweet Home Laundry Soap to receive a set of color lithographs. This encouraged customers to buy and collect more bars of soap, establishing a connection between purchasing a product and receiving a premium, which is a precursor to modern couponing. Coupons for various products, including food and personal care items, began to appear in newspapers and magazines. During the 1930s, the coupon saw a surge in popularity as people looked for ways to save money.
  • “a mare clausum” is a term used in international law to refer to a sea, ocean or other navigable body of water under the jurisdiction of a state that is closed or not accessible to other states. Mare clausum is an exception to mare liberum (Latin for "free sea"), meaning a sea that is open to navigation to ships of all nations. In the generally accepted principle of international waters, oceans, seas, and waters outside national jurisdiction are open to navigation by all and referred to as "high seas" or mare liberum. Portugal and Spain defended a Mare clausum policy during the Age of Discovery. This was soon challenged by other European nations.
  • “though he had stolen only the jewel of time” Foreshadowing of who Mr. Spitzer really is and the theft of “the great rajah’s jewel”. The jewel itself may refer to The Moonstone by Wilkie Collins in 1868. It is considered the first modern detective novel, and established many of the ground rules of the genre. A major theme of the novel is the intrusion of imperialism on everyday English life. Lynn Pykett argues that "the main narrative of The Moonstone concerns the disruption of the tranquility and order of genteel English life by a colonial legacy." The events of the plot are set into motion by Colonel John Herncastle's unlawful theft of the Moonstone in India, and, in leaving the diamond to Rachel Verinder in his will, he is perpetuating his crime. It is only when the diamond is returned to its rightful owners that the 'curse' can be lifted. Pykett also comments that the contradictory forces of feminine passion and silence provide the conflict of the novel, Sergeant "Cuff is defeated by the silence of women (Rachel and Rosanna), by feminine reticence (Lady Verinder), and the failure of individual women to conform to dominant stereotypes of femininity".
  • “mockingbird psychology” It is a metaphorical expression rather than a straightforward psychological term. Throughout the novel, there is a deep exploration of identity, illusion, reflection, and the blurred boundaries between reality and dreams. The "mockingbird" is a bird known for mimicking the songs of other birds, often symbolizing imitation, lack of originality, or an identity that is constructed from external voices rather than authentic selfhood. In this context, "mockingbird psychology" likely refers to a psychological state or pattern wherein a person’s identity, thoughts, or emotions are shaped by imitation or mimicry of others rather than originating from an authentic center. It suggests a kind of fragmented or dependent consciousness that reflects and echoes external stimuli or influences without a stable, original self. This fits with the novel’s frequent motifs of doubles, shadows, reflections, and spectral presences, as with Mr. Spitzer’s reflections on his own duality and the suffragette’s elusive identity. Characters often seem caught in a liminal state between self and other, reality and dream, original and copy. It can be understood as the psychological condition of being a reflective echo or mimic, lacking a fixed, authentic self, resonating with the novel’s larger meditation on identity, memory, and the elusive nature of reality.
  • The mockingbird's history in the US involves its initial decline due to the caged bird trade in the 19th century, its subsequent expansion northward after the trade ceased, and its cultural symbolism as a fighter for its home, its use in literature, and its adoption as state bird in several Southern states. English colonists brought the tradition of caging songbirds to America, and the Northern Mockingbird's skill at mimicry made it a highly prized pet, leading to heavy trapping and population decline in the 19th century. Despite its diminishing numbers, it became a prominent symbol of the American South, celebrated in music and literature. After the cessation of the caged bird trade, the Northern Mockingbird population recovered and expanded its range northwards. The Northern Mockingbird is the official state bird of Arkansas, Florida, Mississippi, Tennessee, and Texas. Texas, in 1927, officially adopted it as the state bird due to its "fighter for the protection of his home" nature. In Hopi and other Pueblo tribes, the mockingbird is credited with teaching people to speak and serves as a diplomat in the Native American culture.
  • “Ivory soap flakes” In 1840, the J.B. Williams Company in Glastonbury, Connecticut, manufactured soap under the name Ivorine. Williams decided to focus on its shaving soap and sold Ivorine to Procter & Gamble, which later developed Ivory. In 1879, James Norris Gamble, son of Procter & Gamble co-founder James Gamble and a trained chemist, developed an inexpensive white soap. The name Ivory was created by Harley Procter, the other founder's son, who was inspired by the quote "[a]ll thy garments smell of myrrh and aloes and cassia out of the ivory palaces", from Psalm 45 of the Bible. In September 1879, Procter & Gamble trademarked "Ivory" as the name of its new soap product. As Ivory is one of P&G's oldest products it is sometimes called "Ivory Towers", and its factory and research center in St. Bernard, Ohio, is named "Ivorydale". Ivory's first slogan, "It Floats!", was introduced in 1891. The product's other well-known slogan, "99+44⁄100% Pure", which was in use by 1895, was based on the results of an analysis by an independent laboratory that Harley Procter hired to demonstrate that Ivory was purer than the castile soap available at the time. The original Ivory bar soap was whipped with air in its production and floated in water, although P&G discontinued this version of the soap in 2023, and the new version no longer floats. According to an apocryphal story, later discounted by the company, a worker accidentally left the mixing machine on too long, and the company chose to sell the "ruined" batch because the added air did not change the basic ingredients of the soap. When appreciative letters about the new, floating soap inundated the company, P&G ordered the extended mix time as a standard procedure. However, company records indicate that the design of Ivory did not come about by accident. In 2004, over 100 years later, the P&G company archivist Ed Rider found documentation that revealed that James N. Gamble, who was a chemist, had discovered how to make the soap float and noted the result in his writings.
  • “American soap flake king” may refer to Procter & Gamble (P&G), founded by William Procter and James Gamble. They were a pivotal force in the American soap industry and its transition to soap flakes. While British manufacturer Lever Brothers were the first to produce flakes commercially, P&G popularized the product in the U.S., particularly with its Ivory soap brand. The American Civil War provided a major boost to P&G's fortunes when the company secured contracts to supply the Union Army with soap and candles. After the war, returning soldiers created nationwide demand for P&G products. In the 1910s, P&G created Ivory Flakes, a shredded form of their popular Ivory soap. Unlike the harsh laundry soap of the era, the mild, pure Ivory Flakes were marketed for washing delicate items like baby clothes, silks, and nylons. The product's success cemented P&G's dominance in the market.
  • “Gold Dust Scouring Twins” were fictional, racially stereotypical black children who served as mascots for Fairbanks' Gold Dust washing powder from the 1890s until 1960. Created by the N.K. Fairbanks Company, the twins, "Goldie" and "Dusty," were depicted doing household chores and promoting the slogan "Let the Gold Dust Twins do your Work". They were a highly successful, albeit controversial, marketing tool, appearing on packaging, in print, and eventually on a popular radio show, becoming as famous as other racially insensitive brands.
  • “majolica” is a type of tin-glazed pottery characterized by its thick white background glaze, on which colorful images are painted before firing to create a durable, vibrant, and often glossy finish. Originating in the Middle East and becoming famous in Italy during the Renaissance, the term "majolica" can also refer to colorful, naturalistic 19th-century earthenware, which was produced in England. The technique involves applying a tin oxide glaze to low-fired earthenware, then painting decorative designs onto the glaze before a second firing, which fuses the colors into the surface.
