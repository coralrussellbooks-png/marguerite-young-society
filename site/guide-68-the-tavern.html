Chapter 68: The Tavern

Research notes: Chapter 68

Characters: Homer Capehorn · Madge Capehorn · Marie Weed · Miss MacIntosh · Moses Hunnecker · Mr. Weed · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Death and Mortality

Symbols: Cards, Games, and Chance · The Bus and the Journey · The Sea and Water

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Angels and Archangels · Fairy Tales and Folklore · Gambling and Playing Cards · Lynching and Racial Violence · Romantic Poets · Suicide in History and Literature

Chapter 68 is the chapter where the bus stops.

After all those miles of grey midwestern mist, the endless circling of Vera’s mind, the somnambulant passengers and Moses Hunnecker’s whiskey-fueled navigation through fog, the bus finally arrives. Vera, Madge, and Homer disembark. And then, quietly, without ceremony, Madge and Homer walk away and disappear from the novel entirely. You may have spent sixty-seven chapters with these people. They are simply gone now.

What Vera walks into instead is the Tavern: a decaying, mildewed hotel built in 1827 by what its sign calls “God’s servants,” now given over to shadows and thin walls that seem to hear everything. And at the bar, she finds Mr. Weed.

Mr. Weed is a professional hangman. A Christian one. And he would very much like to tell you about his work.

What Actually Happens

Moses parks the bus badly. Vera watches Madge and Homer leave the novel without looking back. She waits to check in at the Tavern while, at the bar, the Tavern’s landlord and Mr. Weed, a large man in a wide-brimmed Western hat, are deep in conversation.

Mr. Weed is a hangman by profession and a family man by inclination. He has two wives: a first one, Marie, who hanged herself from a peach tree in their orchard because she feared another pregnancy, and a second one, Julia, who has the mind of a child and sometimes wears Marie’s old clothes. He has children who play at hanging themselves. He carries a Parker fountain pen given to him by a condemned man, a Jew’s harp he plays on the shortcut home through the orchard, and a black poker chip he found in his coat pocket one day, in the suit he wears to hangings and to church, and has no idea how it got there.

He sees himself as a humanitarian. He makes death painless. He is, in his own estimation, more merciful than the alternatives.

The landlord is skeptical. He has a “Remember the Marne” tattoo and a hotel that, he says, sinks every night. He would rather talk about knots, not hangman’s knots, but lovers’ knots, the marital kind that hold until Gabriel blows his horn. He thinks Mr. Weed doesn’t even know how to tie his own bow-tie properly.

Vera listens to all of this. That is what she does in this chapter. She listens.

The People You’re Meeting

Mr. Weed, the Christian Hangman

He is one of Young’s great grotesque figures: a man whose profession is death and whose self-image is one of mercy. He is a good father, he says. He attends church. He is a vegetarian who dreams of cooking live pigs. He is haunted by the memory of cutting Marie down from the peach tree “a split second too late.” He tried to hang a man in Kentucky “for sleeping with a white woman,” and the rope was cut too soon, and the man survived and was seen months later pushing a baby buggy through town, which frightened everyone. This is the kind of efficiency problem that troubles Mr. Weed. Not the injustice of the hanging. The incompleteness of it.

He is not a monster. That is what makes him so disturbing. He is ordinary. He is integrated into American society, into its churches and its courtrooms and its sense of civic duty. He is the man who makes it all painless, and the rest of the people are, as the novel puts it, “silent accepters of death.”

His three symbolic objects tell you everything you need to know about him: the pen that was a dead man’s last gift, the harp that can’t quite drown out the darkness, and the poker chip that appeared from nowhere and connects to nothing. Clues that lead nowhere. Evidence that explains nothing.

Marie Weed, the First Wife

She is only present through her absence, through the hangman’s fractured memories, through the peach tree in the orchard, through Julia wearing her old clothes. She hanged herself because she could not bear another pregnancy. She was afraid of her own body, of what it might produce against her will. Young is quietly, devastatingly precise about this: the Comstock Laws were still in effect, women had essentially no legal right to contraception, and a woman’s body under those conditions was not entirely her own. Marie’s suicide is individual and it is also historical.

Her maiden name was Oliver, and Mr. Weed notes that “the Olivers were known for hanging” themselves. Young has chosen this carefully: Oliver Cromwell was posthumously hanged; Andrew Oliver was hanged in effigy; Oliver Moore was lynched in North Carolina in 1930. The name carries the history of bodies suspended from ropes, official and unofficial, sanctioned and not.

Moses, the Bus Driver

Moses reappears briefly here, struggling to park the bus, anxious about getting home to his old mother. His mother, he says, thinks he is her bridegroom. He keeps traffic rules, metaphorically, even in her bridal chamber; he maintains distance, refuses to enter her delusion fully, remains tethered to her by guilt and obligation rather than love. He won’t cut his hair until the Democrats stop running things. He made that promise to her. He dreams of escape. He goes home instead.

Moses has led his passengers through the grey wilderness. He never reaches the promised land himself. That’s always been his job.

The Landlord

A WWI veteran, a man who has heard everything and trusts none of it, who runs a hotel that sinks every night and somehow surfaces every morning. He grounds the scene. He is the voice of the mundane world pushing back against Mr. Weed’s philosophical self-justifications. His counter-argument is the marital knot: love, he implies, is the only knot that matters, and Mr. Weed, for all his expertise with rope, doesn’t understand it at all.

The Farewell to Madge and Homer

Don’t let this pass you by. For sixty-seven chapters, Madge Capehorn has been sleeping her resistant, unhappy sleep on the bus: pregnant, possibly with someone else’s child, returning to a life she didn’t choose. Homer has been sleeping his innocent, placid sleep beside her. They have been one of the novel’s first quiet jokes: the woman who knows too much, awake and suffering; the man who knows nothing, peacefully asleep.

And now they simply walk away. No resolution. No reckoning. They disappear into their lives the way people in novels almost never do but people in reality always do: mid-story, without explanation, while you’re still watching. This is Young telling you something about the structure of attention. Vera’s attention, and yours, has been so focused on the question of Miss MacIntosh that the rest of the world continues, and ends, at the edges of that focus. We never find out what happens to Madge. That is the point.

The Tavern

Built in 1827 by God’s servants, now given over to mildewed brick and claw-like vines and walls so thin they seem to hear everything. The sign out front reads “GENtLE INCLINe—BRIDE AHEAD.” It is supposed to say “Bridge Ahead.” This is the first thing Vera notices.

A gentle incline. A bride. Ahead. The Tavern is a crossroads between life and death: the kind of place you stay when you are between one thing and another, when you have left what you had and haven’t yet arrived at what comes next. It is filled with human-faced wasps and shadows that don’t lift. Its interior, Vera notes, surpasses her imagination. The place exceeds expectation in the wrong direction. It is, in short, America: built with faith, decayed into commerce, full of secrets, haunted by history, and somehow still operating.

The Black Poker Chip

This is one of Young’s great images of futile evidence. A black poker chip found near the head of a murdered woman wearing rose-colored sunglasses. A clue. Except it leads “nowhere, nowhere at all, like a” businessman’s address book “found in the hollow of a nearby oak tree.” It is simply confusing.

And then Mr. Weed reaches into his coat pocket and finds a black poker chip there. He has no idea how it got in. He doesn’t gamble. It is the suit he wears to hangings and to church. This is what the novel does with truth and evidence: accumulates them without resolving them. The chip is real. The chip is meaningless. Both things are true at once. Reality, as Vera reflects in this chapter, is whatever is experienced: “even the unreal things were real.” The chip is in his pocket. That is all we know. That is enough.

The Central Paradox: Mercy and the Rope

Mr. Weed sees himself as a missionary of painless death. He is there to make the terrible thing as humane as possible. He is, in his estimation, the most merciful man in the room.

Young does not let this go unchallenged, but she also doesn’t refute it cheaply. Mr. Weed is not wrong that the alternative: a botched hanging, a man cut down too soon, surviving his own execution to push a baby buggy through a town that cannot look at him: is worse. He is not wrong that the people who hire him are comfortable outsourcing their discomfort. He is not wrong that he is the only one who has to live with what happens in that room.

He is entirely wrong that this makes him good. The chapter holds both things. It is the novel’s way with American mythology: the story we tell ourselves about mercy, about justice, about painless efficiency, about the man who does the difficult work so the rest of us don’t have to. We make him respectable. We put him in a church pew. We let him carry a Parker pen and a Jew’s harp, and we don’t look too hard at the suit he wears to both.

What to Hold Onto

The misread sign: “GENtLE INCLINe—BRIDE AHEAD.” You are on a gentle incline now, moving toward something. Whether it is a bride or a bridge is not yet clear.

The black poker chip in the pocket of the suit worn to hangings and to church. Evidence that goes nowhere. Keep it.

Marie Weed “hanging from the peach tree in the” orchard, her husband arriving a split second too late. He plays the Jew’s harp on the shortcut home through that same orchard. He still takes the shortcut.

And Vera, at the edge of all this, watching. Listening. Adding these people to the great interior catalogue of her mind. She cannot save any of them. She can only bear witness.

Miss MacIntosh believed in facts. In the cold honest surface of things. Vera has been traveling toward that belief for sixty-eight chapters now, and what she keeps finding instead is this: Mr. Weed’s poker chip. A clue that leads nowhere. The truth that keeps arriving in the wrong suit.
