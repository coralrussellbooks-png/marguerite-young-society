Chapter 4

On Chapter 4 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Woman in Young's Magnum Opus.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · The Stone Deaf Man · Vera Cartwheel

Grief is the engine of this chapter, and it is grief of the permanent kind. Vera has been haunted for years, declining, keeping a fire lit on the shore as a signal to a woman who does not come. The mourning does not lessen and does not resolve into anything, which is the pillar exactly: not an event with an end but a standing condition. Around this, Catherine decrees that Vera must accept a "death in the midst of life," and the phrase is not, at bottom, about the silencing of a daughter. It is the dissolving boundary between life and death handed down as an instruction, a living death Vera is told to inhabit.

The dead girl's letters carry the chapter's sharpest figure. She had accepted marriage proposals from a series of interchangeable strangers, then jumped from the Brooklyn Bridge with her suitcase and veil. The interchangeable men are commodification and are also the illusion of the individual seen from its bleakest side: the strangers are not distinct, they blur into one another, and the girl's death is one more open threshold. She is a double of Vera, a version of the path not taken, plural selfhood reaching across two women who are versions of each other.

The deaf old people at the chapter's edge are not a footnote about society refusing to hear women. They are the first appearance of a motif the book returns to for the rest of its length, invisibility and erasure through the cutting-off of a sense, the world going silent, which comes back in the garden of the deaf and the stone deaf man Vera comes to love.
