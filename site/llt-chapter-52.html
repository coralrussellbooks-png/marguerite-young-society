Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 52

← Chapter 51 · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 53 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh

Pillars: Grief Doesn't End

The Questions

Mr. Spitzer remembers Cousin Hannah “like a traveller reconstructing in his imagination a land he had never visited.” Can one remember what one never saw?

Did Cousin Hannah lose a great love, “or had she never been given that which was her loss”? What is it to grieve something one never had?

“If she was not his mother, she was perhaps his father.” Why will the relation not settle into one name?

He “remembered what he remembered not.” What kind of memory is that?

Memory here builds what it never received. Mr. Spitzer, long after Cousin Hannah’s death, “remembered what he remembered not, even like a traveller reconstructing in his imagination a land he had never visited.” This is memory as construction pushed to its limit. He does not misremember a real past; he assembles, out of need and sympathy, a past he never witnessed, a country he never entered, and the assembly is his memory of her. The book has shown memory building the impossible before, the visit that could not have happened, the dead kept alive by imagining them. Here it names the method plainly: to remember can be to construct a land one never saw, and the construction is no less real for it.

The grief the chapter reaches for is grief without a possessed object. Mr. Spitzer wonders what Cousin Hannah “had lost so long ago… or had she never been given that which was her loss,” whether the thing she mourned was ever hers to lose, like “a sea shell… born without a mantle.” This is Grief Doesn't End at a strange frontier: grief not for what was had and taken but for what was never had at all, the ache of an absence that was always absent. The book has insisted that loss cannot be sealed out; here it finds a loss that predates having, a missing thing that was never present to go missing, and grieves it anyway.

And the relation will not hold one name. “If she was not his mother, she was perhaps his father,” Cousin Hannah becoming “the father of his amputated loves.” The categories that fix people into kin, mother, father, the settled grammar of relation, come loose the way the nouns came loose at the dream-wedding and at Miss MacIntosh’s unsealing. Mr. Spitzer cannot say what Cousin Hannah was to him because the words that would say it are built, and the built words will not sit still on the shifting thing they try to name. She is mother and father and neither, a relation felt exactly and namable in no fixed term.

So the chapter is memory doing without its supposed materials. It remembers the unvisited, grieves the never-had, and names the unnamable relation, and in each case the thing memory works on was never solidly there to begin with. This is not a failure of Mr. Spitzer’s recollection but the truth the book keeps finding under recollection: the past is not retrieved but made, and it can be made even out of what was never possessed, so that a man may carry a remembered country, and a grief, and a relation, built from an absence that was always absent.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that treats Mr. Spitzer’s memory of the unvisited as mere confusion to be corrected, and the reading that treats grief for the never-had as a mistake, a mourning of nothing. Neither the error nor the empty grief is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that memory constructs rather than retrieves, so that one can remember a land never visited and the memory is real; that grief can attach to what was never possessed, a loss that predates having and is felt none the less; and that the names for relation are built and will not settle, so Cousin Hannah is mother and father and neither. Memory makes its object, grief needs no prior possession, and the kin-words do not hold. Mr. Spitzer carries a remembered country he never entered and a grief for what was never his, and the chapter lets both stand as truer than any corrected record.

For Reflection

Is there a past one carries that one built rather than witnessed, and is it any less one’s own?

Can one grieve something one never actually had, and is that grief false?

What relation in one’s life refuses to settle into a single available name?
