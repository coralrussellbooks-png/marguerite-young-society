Chapter 23

On Chapter 23 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of The Wheel and the Spokes.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 22 · Wheel and Spokes Home · Chapter 24 →

Research notes: Chapter 23 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 23

Characters: Miss MacIntosh

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

Miss MacIntosh presents herself as singular, consistent, reliable: "always so forthright and sensible". But the narrator's doubt accumulates:

"Miss MacIntosh, always so forthright and sensible and charged with energy as I still half-way supposed, though perhaps with a vague doubt arising in my mind..."

The self Miss MacIntosh performs (practical, common-sensical, anti-romantic) is revealed as one layer over others. She is simultaneously:

  • The pragmatist who distrusts illusion
  • The cosmic visionary who speaks of Baffin Bay and the first starfish
  • The person concealing "something missing from the essential human"
  • The unconscious dreamer whose conscience "kept guard, a lonely vigil"

These are not reconciled. They are simultaneous. The chapter does not resolve them. That irresolution is Miss MacIntosh.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Miss MacIntosh's extended skepticism is the chapter's most explicit philosophical content:

"History lies, and geography is uncertain, unstable as water or a sick lady's dreams, and we know not who or what we are in these modern times or which star we inhabit."

This is not mere cynicism. It is a genuine ontological claim: the coordinates by which we locate ourselves (historical, geographical, personal) are constructions. Caesar may never have existed. The Rubicon may be fiction. The chapter offers no corrective to this. The narrator does not reassure us that Caesar was real. The larger cave, more carefully lit, is all there is.

Crucially, Miss MacIntosh believes she is the exception, the champion of common sense against illusion, while the narrator quietly observes that her common sense is itself a construction covering something she does not wish to reveal. The ironist and the ironized occupy the same sentence.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The chapter's seasonal arc enacts this pillar structurally. In August sunlight, Miss MacIntosh speaks of winter:

Ah, "we must get ready for winter’s blast. It’s the cold hand of death for those who are not ready."

The narrator responds with a meditation on the absence of transition, "was there to be no season of transition, no gradual change, no autumn", but then corrects herself: winter has its own colors, dimmed but present. The bronze of beach grass. The pale iridescence of the seagull's eye. The luminous opal of the winter sea through fog. Grief, here, is not the absence of color. It is the narrowed spectrum. It is permanent, it is the climate, but it is not colorless. The honest life does not pretend the colors are gone. It names them accurately: dimmed.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator's position throughout is one of sustained, non-intervening attention. She does not correct Miss MacIntosh. She does not comfort her. She does not conclude. She watches:

"The sunlight dashed against her face."

This sentence recurs. It is not metaphor. It is the act of witnessing made literal: light striking a face, a person being seen in full illumination. The narrator stays in the room. She sees the doubt forming in herself without resolving it. She sees Miss MacIntosh's concealment without exposing it. Attention is the practice. The refusal to simplify is the act.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The chapter's structure is itself recursive. Miss MacIntosh returns again and again to electric lights, to Benjamin Franklin, to the same complaints, circling back:

"Electric lights, electric lights, indeed!’ she muttered. ‘Electric trains, electric irons! Benjamin Franklin, my foot."

"Electric lights, electric lights, indeed, electric trains! Stuff and nonsense..."

"Electric lights, electric lights, indeed, what nonsense!"

This is not mere repetition. Each return adds a layer: marriage, privacy, divorce, the North star, God's original darkness. The building is never finished. The complaint is never resolved. The recursion is the content. The argument advances by circling, not by arriving.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter does not conclude. It does not resolve Miss MacIntosh's character, her concealment, the narrator's doubt, the question of winter, the question of marriage, the question of what is real. It ends mid-argument, mid-season, mid-revelation. This is not aesthetic failure. It is the chapter's most honest gesture. The demand for resolution (for Miss MacIntosh to be explained, for the doubt to be settled, for the season to change) would be its own violence against the experience being recorded. The ongoing sentence is the integrity. The chapter is, formally, the ethics it describes.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Miss MacIntosh is, structurally, the marginal figure: the servant, the nurse, the practical woman without ornament or jewel, dressed for "just that life which she lived here on this rough shore, for no other life". She wears:

"the old grey baseball sweater with its ravelling sleeve, the stout shoes which surely covered no angelic feet"

The description is loving and devastating simultaneously. She is not the romantic heroine. She is not the dreamer. She is the edge-figure, the one who cannot be easily categorized, and it is precisely in her marginality that the constructed nature of the world becomes visible. She is the evidence that the categories (practical/dreamer, real/illusory, common/cosmic) do not hold.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The chapter's rhetoric is almost entirely composed of accumulated historical sediment. Miss MacIntosh does not speak of the present. She speaks of:

  • Emperor Constantine
  • Caesar crossing the Rubicon
  • Benjamin Franklin and his kite
  • President Garfield
  • Helen of Troy and Ulysses
  • Penelope weaving her web
  • Thomas Edison
  • The first day of creation

These are not digressions. They are the substance of her consciousness. The past does not recede for Miss MacIntosh; it presses upward through every breakfast conversation. Constantine disturbed time; Franklin disturbed darkness; Edison disturbed privacy. Each historical figure is a geological layer still actively shaping the present moment at the breakfast table by the sea.

The narrator makes this explicit:

"the first starfish upon the first beach of the first evening, the first cuttlefish, the first lobster, the first fiddler crab, the first moon drawing the first tide!"

The first tide is still drawing. The sediment is still pressing.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Miss MacIntosh's monologue is obsessively self-aware about the unreliability of language and record, yet she speaks in an unbroken torrent. The contradiction is the point. Her language:

  • Cites authorities she dismisses ("Ignorance of the law is no excuse," she said without conviction )
  • Invokes Biblical cadences ("Am I my brother's keeper?" ) while undercutting scripture
  • Uses the rhetoric of common sense to advance positions that are anything but common

The narrator observes that Miss MacIntosh was "always reading aloud, at the breakfast table, the news of yesterday": yesterday's news, spoken today, as if it were current. Language here is explicitly temporal displacement. The newspaper is already wrong by the time it is read. The language that pretends to speak of now speaks from somewhere already past.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The chapter's most philosophically concentrated moment arrives near its close, when the narrator lists what Miss MacIntosh's cosmic evocations actually conceal and reveal simultaneously:

"the first starfish upon the first beach of the first evening, the first cuttlefish, the first lobster, the first fiddler crab, the first moon drawing the first tide"

These are not symbols pointing outward to a transcendent beyond. They are first things: the sacred located in the most specific, most ordinary, most biological particulars. A fiddler crab. A lobster. Not angels. Not heaven. The sacred is here, in the first morning, which is also this morning, at the breakfast table, with the newspaper and the glass of water and the house fly buzzing around Miss MacIntosh's head.

The chapter ends with Miss MacIntosh asking:

"Why should light,’ she asked, ‘be given to a man who is in misery and whose way is darkness and whom God hath hedged in and though the blind may see? A blind man saw me, certainly, and without electric lights. Electric lights, fiddlesticks! The stars have lighted the heavens from pole to pole."

The sacred light she accepts is not Franklin's, not manufactured, not artificial, not purchased. It is the immanent light already present in the darkness God made. The transcendence she gestures toward is not elsewhere. It is the original darkness, the first night, the stars that were always already there.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The narrator occupies the structural position described by this pillar most precisely. She is constituted by Miss MacIntosh's deposited speech, years of breakfast monologue have formed the ground she stands on, while simultaneously constituting the record of Miss MacIntosh that will persist. The chapter is the crossing point itself:

"It occurred to me, in the sunlight at the breakfast table, that first morning of what seemed, in some inscrutable way, our new creation which bore already its sense of loss and death..."

"Our new creation which bore already its sense of loss"; this is the crossing point articulated with precision. The new moment arrives already carrying its own ending. The narrator is where this time crosses itself.
