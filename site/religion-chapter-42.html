Chapter 42

On Chapter 42 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Christianity and Religion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 24, 2026

Characters: Cousin Hannah

“Should the lost locate the lost?”: “For the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost” (Luke 19:10). See The Bible.

“bringing with it the fullness of days”: “So Job died, being old and full of days” (Job 42:17). See The Book of Job.

“the great lamp-lighter going his rounds”: God and light have been closely linked in the Bible. Lamps are lit in many religions around the world, as at Diwali, the Hindu festival of lights, and at Hanukkah. Maria Susanna Cummins’s The Lamplighter (1854) was a bestseller. It is about an orphan that is taken in by a lamplighter and raised with love and taught virtue and religious faith. See The Bible. Its success provoked Hawthorne’s complaint that “America is now wholly given over to a d——d mob of scribbling women” (Nathaniel Hawthorne, letter to William D. Ticknor, January 19, 1855). See American Writers.

“through the pearly gates”: “And the twelve gates were twelve pearls” (Revelation 21:21). See Revelation and the Apocalypse. Earlier in Chapter 3.

“No one net could ever drag in all the stars”: “The kingdom of heaven is like unto a net, that was cast into the sea, and gathered of every kind” (Matthew 13:47). See The Bible.

“Forever would a lost psyche wander down the wind”: While death is inevitable there are different forms of immortality, covered above, and St. Augustine’s perspective where he emphasized the eternal significance of the “hour of death” and the importance of being prepared for it since one has been promised forgiveness but not necessarily the time: “No one has promised you that you are going to be alive tomorrow” (Augustine, Sermon 40.5, trans. Edmund Hill, 1990). See Augustine and Christian Theology.

“she was Hamid in the East”: One of the Beautiful names of Allah, al Hamid means the praiseworthy, laudable one, the object of all praise. Hannah, from the Hebrew for grace, is the mother of Samuel in 1 Samuel 1. See The Bible.

“struck fear and trembling”: “Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling” (Philippians 2:12), the source of Kierkegaard’s title Fear and Trembling (1843). See The Bible.

“each who dies must die twice”: “And death and hell were cast into the lake of fire. This is the second death” (Revelation 20:14). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.

“the difference between right and left”: Nineveh held “more than sixscore thousand persons that cannot discern between their right hand and their left hand” (Jonah 4:11). See The Bible.

“Where, he wondered, was the great tent-maker now?”: Omar Khayyam’s name means tentmaker. See the Chapter 43 resources. Paul, too, was a tentmaker: “for by their occupation they were tentmakers” (Acts 18:3). See The Bible.

“like a winding shroud”: In Islamic burial practices the body is wrapped in a plain, usually white shroud.

“with their faces covered like Ahaseuris”: Ahaseuris or Ahasureus was a king and the husband of Esther in the Bible. In Esther it is Haman’s face that is covered as he is condemned: “As the word went out of the king’s mouth, they covered Haman’s face” (Esther 7:8). See the Ahasuerus entry in the Chapter 40 resources. See The Bible. Earlier in Chapter 40.

“might there be resurrection and eternal life”: “I am the resurrection, and the life” (John 11:25). See The Bible.

“the ideal immortality from his point of view”: There are different views on immortality and what that looks like. Many beliefs and religions focus on the individual’s soul or spirit after death. In Buddhism there is a state of no-self (Anatta) where there is no permanent, unchanging self or essence, including the individual. There may be some kind of continuation after death but it is not a separate, conscious existence. Non-personal immortality is where philosophers and thinkers believe there may be a collective consciousness with or without an individual component. Symbolic immortality is where immortality lies in one’s legacy, impact, and memory is what is remembered after death. This forms a sort of immortality through history. See Buddhism. Mr. Spitzer’s ideal is the resurrection of the body of the Apostles’ Creed: “It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body” (1 Corinthians 15:44). See The Bible.

“waiting for the spirit to return”: “I will return into my house from whence I came out; and when he is come, he findeth it empty, swept, and garnished” (Matthew 12:44). See The Bible.

Death "came not with loud armies but came like a thief in the night" gathers the gospel warnings that the end comes unlooked-for (1 Thessalonians 5, Matthew 24), quiet and sudden.

"heaven opened like a door, she being that ivory door" makes Cousin Hannah the gate of heaven itself, a Peter holding the keys of the kingdom.

She trails "clouds of glory" and rises "into Christian paradise beyond these fields of asphodel," passing into the afterlife.

“where was now her armor of virtue”: See “the whole armour of God” (Ephesians 6:11) in the Chapter 20 resources. See The Bible. Earlier in Chapter 20.

"should not all be as brides going to the bridegroom" makes every woman in death a bride of Christ, drawing on the marriage of the Lamb and the parable of the ten virgins.

“the void calling to the void”: “Deep calleth unto deep” (Psalm 42:7). See the Chapter 1 resources. See The Bible. Earlier in Chapter 1.

“Oh, is there no pavilion to hide me from the storm?”: “For in the time of trouble he shall hide me in his pavilion” (Psalm 27:5), and “thou shalt keep them secretly in a pavilion from the strife of tongues” (Psalm 31:20). See The Bible.
