Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 75

← Chapter 74 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 76 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Esther Longtree

Grief opens this chapter as a boundless force beyond control, and the dissolving boundary between life and death runs through it. Esther cannot tell whether the thing she carries is motherhood or "a benign tumor," and the ambiguity is the book's refusal to let the female body be simply read, a pregnancy that hovers between creation and disease, life and its absence. The dead child that haunts her, at once beautiful and terrifying, is the boundary held open around a being neither fully born nor fully gone, the stillborn kept present the way the book keeps all its dead.

A fisherman witnesses her ordeal and cannot be sure what he has seen, and his uncertainty is the world's disbelief of a woman's account, the erasure of her lived reality even by the one who watches it happen. The stillborn thrown into the river is the invisibility of female reproductive loss made literal, a child disposed of into moving water and forgotten, the failure of the record to keep what a woman's body produced and lost, and the normalization of these repeated losses is the town's indifference hardening into Esther's own resignation.

The dead child in her visions is bound to her father, the policeman who patrolled the graveyard, and the intimation of incest folds patriarchal authority into the origin of her suffering, the same man who policed other couples implicated in her own violation. Her dreams of motherhood shade into fear, the marble statue, the uncertainty about what is real, and the confusion is real and dream as one country worked through a woman's reproductive life, the visions as true to her as anything the daylight admits.

At night she crawls and bites and nurses her own fist, feeds an invisible child, and the isolation is the emotional labor of a motherhood no one supports, a woman mothering where the world has left her alone. Yet she keeps her hope against every judgment, refuses to be controlled by the men's narratives, and questions the reproductive justice that would blame her, and her contentment in a hostile world is a quiet reclamation of joy. The chapter closes on a looming war and a society indifferent to the crimes already committed against women, the personal loss and the coming public one set side by side, and Esther enduring at the center of both.
