Chapter 25

On Chapter 25 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Woman in Young's Magnum Opus.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Mr. Spitzer visits Catherine as a dead man visiting a woman who is more alive dead than he is, and the chapter belongs to his unmaking. He is "divided," "at loss," his mind gone watery, his memories vague, and he suffers a run of "little accidents," at one point walking straight through a glass wall. Others have begun to declare him dead while he stands physically before them. This is male disorientation set against female control, and it is the dissolving boundary between life and death working on the book's central man, Spitzer thinning toward the "living death" the chapter names, plural selfhood in a person who can no longer be counted as present or absent, living or gone.

Catherine, delicate and yet a "bulwark," holds the authority here because she is more at home in the dissolution than he is. She accuses him of "falsehood" and "subtle theft," she transmutes the objects and identities of her "phantasmagoric bedroom" at will, and her command of the dream is real and dream as one country ruled from the bed. Her power is not a woman winning out over a man. It is a woman who has stopped resisting the thing that is happening to both of them, while he still flinches from it.

His dead brother Peron stands behind the chapter, the one who chose his death outright, with faith in an afterlife, against Spitzer's slow undeciding drift. The twin iron beds and golden cradles set birth beside death in the same furniture, the cradle and the grave in one room, and the two brothers, the decisive dead one and the undead divided one, are the first full sounding of the doubling the book will carry to its end.
