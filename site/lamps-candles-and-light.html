Lamps, Candles, and Light

Part of To All My Darlings, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 30, 2026

Definition from research

Chapter 7 · Violin and Lantern

  • Old Doc’s violin (sometimes broken) and smoky lantern symbolize fading life, memory, and guidance in darkness.

Chapter 8 · Light and Darkness

  • The dimming and raising of lights in the house reflect attempts to hide or reveal truth, beauty, and identity.
  • Madge’s insistence on keeping the light on contrasts with Beatrice’s hiding in darkness.

Chapter 9 · Light and Darkness

  • The interplay of light and darkness appears in the descriptions of the house and Vera’s inner world.
  • The house is described as cold and resembling a funeral parlor, emphasizing decay and death.
  • Vera’s dreams and secret thoughts are contrasted with the harsh reality imposed by Miss MacIntosh, symbolizing the tension between imagination and discipline.

Chapter 12 · Moonlight and Mist

  • The pregnant moonlight and mist create an atmosphere of mystery, illumination, and ambiguity.

Chapter 17 · Moonlight and Darkness

  • The moonlight creates a cold, distant atmosphere.
  • The interplay of light and shadow symbolizes the tension between knowledge and ignorance, reality and illusion.

Chapter 20 · Light and Dawn

  • Dawn is both “diamond” and “many-prismed,” a metaphor for clarity, revelation, but also a harsh revealer of truth.
  • The “Aurora Borealis” in her sparsely furnished bedroom suggests a surreal, otherworldly illumination, the “first experience of light” upon uncreated things.

Chapter 21 · Sea and Light

  • The sea light covering the scene suggests mystery, change, and the vastness of life’s experience.

Chapter 23 · Electric Lights

  • Symbolize modernity’s intrusion into natural human rhythms and privacy.

Chapter 23 · Darkness and Light

  • Represent privacy, natural order, sleep, and the divine versus exposure, artificiality, and disturbance.

Chapter 23 · Stars and Natural Light

  • Symbolize the eternal, natural order, and the divine presence.

Chapter 28 · The Candle Flames and Venetian Mirrors

  • Reflect the delicate, transient nature of perception and memory.

Chapter 29 · Light and Darkness

  • Represent knowledge and ignorance, life and death, existence and the unknown.
  • Moths are drawn to light, symbolizing the soul’s yearning for truth or transcendence.

Chapter 29 · On Butterfly and Moth Distinctions and Flight Times (Day versus Night)

  • The ambiguous boundary and symbolic contrast between butterflies and moths, day and night, happiness and sorrow.

Chapter 32 · The Symbolism of Light and Darkness

  • Cousin Hannah is described as “standing in a beam of sudden light,” a figure of revelation and defiance.
  • Yet the chapter also has Mr. Spitzer’s “dark cape,” the ladies’ “already shadowed snow-white faces,” and Catherine’s “clouded looks,” symbolizing mystery, obscurity, and the unknown. This interplay of light and dark reflects the duality of the characters and the ambiguous outcomes of Cousin Hannah’s battles.

Chapter 35 · Light and Darkness

  • Light (stars, jewel reflections, candles) contrasts with darkness (shadows, fog, cosmic darkness).
  • Cousin Hannah is connected with shining stars and blazing eyes, symbolizing her enduring spirit.
  • Darkness often surrounds Mr. Spitzer and Catherine’s fragmented self.

Chapter 39 · Stars, Moons, and Light

  • Celestial imagery emphasizes timelessness, otherworldliness, and Catherine’s ethereal state.
  • Light and darkness interplay to suggest knowledge, madness, and mystery.

Chapter 43 · Firefly Light / Star

  • Symbolizes hope, guidance, and the fragile spark of life amid darkness.

Chapter 44 · The Whirlwind and Darkness

  • Represent confusion, chaos, and the unknowability of the human heart.

Chapter 46 · Fog and Darkness

  • Fog represents uncertainty, loss, and the blurring of reality and memory. The drifting fog and shadows evoke the liminal space between life and death and the difficulty of grasping truth.

Chapter 50 · Fireflies and Light

  • Recurring symbols of the soul or consciousness, fragile and transient, yet illuminating the darkness.

Chapter 51 · The Baton / Lamp-lighter’s Pole

  • The baton he lifts symbolizes control and orchestration, but he doubts whether he is a conductor or merely a lamp-lighter or oarsman, indicating his ambivalence about his role in the universe.

Chapter 51 · The Star / Moon / Light

  • Light and celestial bodies symbolize knowledge, life, and death. Mr. Spitzer is associated with a “supernumerary star,” a star beyond the normal count, symbolizing his unique, marginal existence.

Chapter 55 · The Candle Flames and Snuffers

  • Represent life and death, consciousness and oblivion; the snuffer putting out stars is a metaphor for fading life.

Chapter 62 · The Old Oil Lamp with Bull’s-Eye

  • Represents the faint, fragile light of consciousness and creativity amid darkness.

Chapter 63 · Electric Lights vs. Natural Darkness

  • Electric lights symbolize modernity, artificiality, and the loss of privacy and natural rhythms.
  • Miss MacIntosh longs for the natural darkness, the "night of six months," and the simple light of the moon or candle, reflecting a desire for authenticity and peace.

Chapter 66 · The Hurricane Lamp

  • Represents fragile knowledge and guidance amid darkness and storms.

Chapter 68 · Shadows and Darkness

  • Represent the unknown, death, and the persistent presence of the past.

Chapter 75 · The Moonlight and Starlight

  • Juxtaposed images representing the mysterious, supernatural, and elusive nature of Esther’s experience.

Chapter 77 · Shadows and Darkness

  • Recur throughout as metaphors for hidden truths, guilt, and fear.
  • Esther’s shadow and the detective’s shadow symbolize identity and the unknown.

Chapter 82 · Fog and Moonlight

  • Indicate ambiguity, the boundary between clarity and obscurity, life and death, dream and reality.
