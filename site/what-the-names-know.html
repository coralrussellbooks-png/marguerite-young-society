Definition from research

The Novel's Onomastic Architecture and the Wheel of Fortune

This section distinguishes between two categories of evidence: names that Young glosses explicitly within the novel, and names whose meanings operate through etymology and structural implication. Both categories are real. They make different orders of claim, and the scholarship is strongest when it honors that difference.

Part One: Names Young Glosses Within the Novel

Young was not subtle about names when she wanted to make sure the reader understood them. She glossed five names directly within the novel: sometimes through a character's own statement, sometimes through narrative explanation, sometimes through dialogue. These five are the bedrock of the onomastic argument. Everything else in this section is built on top of them.

Vera: Truth

Young states this directly in Vera's own voice. The passage is unambiguous:

All people would greet me by my name, Vera, that which stands for truth and not merely the fickle promise or dead substitute for that life which was never to be realized.

Vera tells the reader what her name means. This is not interpretive. The novel announces it. Vera is truth. She is the unborn passenger, the fifth, the one who is in the process of becoming what her name already is. The 1,198 pages are truth arriving at itself, the name finally inhabited by the person who carries it.

The qualification Young adds is equally important: not merely the fickle promise. Truth is not what Vera has been living. She has been living inside an illusion, her mother's opium world, the fiction of Miss MacIntosh as a stable center. Her name is the destination she is traveling toward, not the condition she starts from. Vera is truth on the way to being true.

Moses Hunnecker: The Lawgiver Who Cannot Enter

Moses names himself in his own monologue: You know me, Moses Hunnecker, the bus-driver. The self-naming is deliberate and emphatic. He says his name as an announcement of identity, as if reminding himself who he is while driving drunk through a dead country at night.

The novel does not gloss Moses the way it glosses Vera. It does not say Moses means the lawgiver who leads the people to the edge of the promised land and cannot enter. It does not need to. The biblical Moses is part of the English-reading tradition in a way that makes the gloss unnecessary. What the novel does instead is build the Moses pattern into every element of the character: the uncut hair as Nazarite vow, Indiana identified explicitly as the promised land, the journey that circles back rather than arriving, the driver who will be going home while his passengers go on without him.

The infant Moses sleeping in his little boat among the reeds appears later in the novel in Mr. Spitzer's chapters, in a completely different context. Young uses the Moses image twice, at different scales, in different registers: the bus-driver who names himself Moses, and the hidden infant in the bulrushes who will grow into the lawgiver. Both ends of the same life are present in the novel. Neither reaches the promised land.

Dr. Justice O'Leary: The Name He Announces Frequently

The novel is explicit about this name's weight: Dr. Justice O'Leary, for so he called himself, announcing frequently not only his name but the fact that he was the best medical practitioner this country ever saw. Announcing frequently is the key phrase. Justice O'Leary insists on his name in the way that people insist on titles they fear are being forgotten. He practices justice only in his hallucinated practice. He delivers babies no one else can see. He is justice as it survives in the imagination when the world has stopped recognizing it.

The combination of titles is specific: Doctor and Justice. Both are titles of authority, one medical and one legal. He holds both and neither functions. In a novel where Mr. Spitzer practices law in courts paved with the moonstones of lost cases and Moses drives a bus that may be going nowhere, Justice O'Leary is the figure who names the novel's attitude toward institutional authority most directly: it continues to announce itself long after it has ceased to operate.

Falada: The Speaking Dead Head

Mr. Spitzer explains Falada's name within the novel. This is the most elaborate name-gloss in the novel, running to several sentences:

Falada, Mr. Spitzer had later ascertained, was the name of the horse in an old Arabic fairy tale which had its parallels in many countries, in many ages, Falada being that horse whose head was cut off and nailed above the city gates and whose head, after its death, had spoken to the people. This horse's head had spoken oracles and parables to the people passing through the gates. He had spoken secrets and perhaps was speaking now.

Falada speaks after death. Falada's head, nailed above the city gates, delivers oracles to those who pass through. This is what Vera rides toward Miss MacIntosh's room in Chapter 13. She is riding a horse whose name means the dead oracle, the voice that speaks after the body has been destroyed.

And “perhaps was speaking now” is Young's own addition to the folk tale's meaning. The horse may still be speaking. The oracle may be active. What Vera learns in Chapter 13, what Falada carries her toward, may itself be an oracle: the revelation of Miss MacIntosh's secret is the speaking of the dead truth, the thing that was always there and could only be said after innocence was destroyed.

Beatrice Fitzgerald: Royal by the Bar Sinister

Homer glosses this name in dialogue. He does not know he is doing so; he is simply defending Beatrice against Madge's contempt. But the gloss is explicit:

They say she's royal, that there's blue blood in her. That's because her name is Fitzgerald. Everything that begins with Fitz is royal in this country. Fitzwater, Fitzsimmon, Fitzhugh, Fitzpatrick, Fitzgerald. That was because of some woman that was never married to some old king but had a child by him. It's the bar sinister. It's the bastard line.

Beatrice is Dante's guide through Paradise. Beatrice Fitzgerald is royal by the bastard line: she carries the blood of heaven but was never legitimized by it. She lives in a flooded tin house. She is nearly blind and losing the other eye. She is the guide who cannot see, the heavenly mediator who has been stripped of her standing by the circumstances of her birth.

This is Young's consistent method with classical allusion: she does not simply reference, she revises. Dante's Beatrice leads the poet upward through the spheres of Heaven. Young's Beatrice pulls a child's sled over the snow in the coldest winter, and the snowflakes fall into her eyes, and a boy shot her with a bow and arrow. The guide through Paradise is here, royal by the bastard line, nearly blind, washing other people's clothes.

Part Two: Names Whose Meanings Operate Through Constellation

The following names are not glossed within the novel. Young does not explain them the way she explains Vera or Falada or Fitzgerald. What she does instead is build characters whose actions, conditions, and relationships rhyme with what their names carry etymologically and historically. The evidence is cumulative and architectural rather than direct. It is a different order of claim from Part One, and should be read as such.

Catherine Helena Cartwheel, nee Snowden

Three names working simultaneously, confirmed by the novel's full form of the character's name: Mrs. Catherine Cartwheel, nee Snowden, widow of Jock.

Catherine derives from the Greek Aikaterina, associated in the hagiographic tradition with Saint Catherine of Alexandria, whose martyrdom instrument was the breaking wheel: the Catherine wheel. Catherine Cartwheel is the woman broken by the wheel who produced, by that breaking, the cart-wheel that is Vera. The surname is not an incidental decoration. The daughter of the woman named for the breaking wheel is named Cartwheel. The breaking becomes the vehicle. The instrument of torture becomes the instrument of motion.

Helena means light in Greek: torch, bright one. Catherine's middle name is Helena, the light she carries even in her opium bed. She is Cartwheel's light, however dimmed by the drugs that sustain her.

Snowden is what Catherine was before she became a Cartwheel. Snow suggests both whiteness and blankness: the original state before the wheel turned. Catherine was born into the blank, the undifferentiated, and married into the wheel. She has been turning ever since, unable to stop, her opium dreams the only thing that slows the rotation.

Jock is Scottish for Jack, itself a diminutive of John: the fashionable dog fancier, the father unknown. The father of truth is a nickname for a Gospel name, fashionable, kept dogs, and is unknown. Truth's father is everywhere and nowhere, a nickname for John.

Esther Longtree

Esther derives from the Persian word for star, or possibly from the Hebrew word for hidden or concealed. Both meanings apply simultaneously to Esther Longtree. She is the star that the novel barely sees: she is described in proximity to the evening star and the morning star in the novel, though without explicit etymology. She is also profoundly hidden: her pregnancies are invisible to everyone who should see them, her grief is carried in silence, her stillborn children are known only to her.

Longtree is the name that carries the most direct structural weight. A tree that is too long, that outlasts its season, that keeps bearing what cannot be born. Esther Longtree is perpetually pregnant and perpetually losing her children. She is the tree whose fruit never reaches the ground as living children. She bears and bears and bears. The tree is long: it has been doing this for a long time and will continue.

Young told interviewers that Esther is the mother of us all. The mother of us all is named for the hidden star and the long-bearing tree. She carries what cannot be born and does not stop.

Joachim and Peron Spitzer

Joachim is a Hebrew name meaning God will establish or God has established. In the Christian tradition, Joachim is the name of the father of the Virgin Mary: the man who, with his wife Anna, produced the vessel for the incarnation. Joachim Spitzer is the twin who survives, who practices silent music and imaginary law, who waits and witnesses and establishes nothing that the world recognizes. He is the father of what will not be born into public acknowledgment, the establisher of a music that will not be played, a justice that will not be administered.

Peron is the twin who dies early, the gambler, the worldly one who loved only the old and ugly and found them beautiful. The name Peron carries associations of traveler and pilgrim in several traditions. He is the one who went ahead, who took the journey that Joachim could not, who died into knowledge of the darkness that the living twin could only imagine. In the novel's final revelation Joachim admits to Catherine that he has been Peron all along, that it was Joachim who died. The names have been performing a shell game throughout the novel: the establisher and the pilgrim have been trading places, and neither can be finally located.

Spitzer means pointed one or sharp one in German. A Spitzer is a pointed thing: an arrow, a pencil sharpened to its tip. The twins are pointed at something they never reach. The name names the vector without naming the destination.

The Cartwheel and the Wheel of Fortune

The novel uses the phrase wheel of fortune once, in Mr. Spitzer's meditation: “perhaps the smallest things were indeterminate, that only they escaped this wheel of fortune or these sealed orders of that fate under which man had sailed and which was determined long ago and was inscrutable, that perhaps only some delicate rainbow-colored butterfly gleaming through the mist or far ripple of water streaked by moonlight or snowflake like a web of lace laid upon the mouth of the dead could escape this universal cloud.” It uses "She was turning on this turning wheel. She could not stop. She could not cease. She was dancing." of Madge in the bus section. It describes Mr. Spitzer and his twin as "turning like the wavering spokes of a wheel through light as through darkness"

Cartwheels appear in the novel's imagery twice as shapes among snowflakes: cartwheels, earth-borne asters, stars, flowers, and elsewhere whirling multiform snowflakes, snowflakes like roses, cartwheels, moths, stars. The cartwheel appears in lists of shapes that the mind perceives in the world's patterning: not as the family name being referenced but as a shape the universe makes.

What this constellation suggests is that Young chose the family name Cartwheel with awareness of both the Wheel of Fortune and the cartwheel as a vernacular shape of turning. The Cartwheels are the family that turns. Catherine turns on her opium bed. Vera turns on the turning wheel of the bus journey. The wheel of fortune turns over them. Their name names the condition they cannot escape.

Boethius's Wheel of Fortune, which the existing Deep Architecture chapters have addressed in connection with the novel's structure, turns without regard for human merit or desire. What goes up comes down. What was high is brought low. Catherine Cartwheel was once high: a fashionable woman with a great house by the sea. She is now horizontal in her bed, unable to rise, turned by the wheel to the lowest point. Her daughter is searching for the stability that the wheel keeps denying. Their name is the wheel.

The Architecture of the Naming

Young named her characters with the same intentionality she brought to every other level of the novel's construction. The evidence falls into two distinct orders: the names she glossed directly, which are bedrock, and the names whose meanings operate through etymology and structural resonance, which are the constellations built on that bedrock.

What connects both orders is a consistent principle: every name in this novel is working. No name is neutral. The question for each is whether Young is telling the reader what the name means, or whether she is trusting the reader to follow the meaning through the character's life and find out what the name already knew about its bearer.

Vera is truth: Young says so. Catherine is the breaking wheel: Young builds a woman broken by the wheel who cannot stop turning and named her child Cartwheel. Falada speaks after death: Young has Mr. Spitzer explain this directly. Justice administers no justice: Young has Justice O'Leary announce his name frequently in a world that has stopped needing what he offers. Moses will not enter the promised land: Young has Moses Hunnecker drive the bus toward Indiana and name himself in his own monologue. Fitzgerald is royal by the bastard line: Young has Homer say so in dialogue.

These are not coincidences. In a novel that Young spent eighteen years building sentence by sentence, with nearly four thousand question marks, with over eight hundred uses of the word shadow, with the word dead appearing more than sixteen hundred times: in that novel, the names know exactly what they are doing.

The wheel turns. The Cartwheels turn with it. The spokes hold their distances from the absent center. Moses drives. Truth is being born.
