Chapter Eleven: The Sacred Is Here

Pillar: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here · Across every book

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Moses Hunnecker · Vera Cartwheel

The tenth pillar is the most delicate of the five extensions and the one that required the most time to name accurately.

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling by Marguerite Young is suffused with a quality that can only be called sacred: a sense that the ordinary world, the Indiana plains, the Grey Goose bus driven by Moses Hunnecker, the yellow canary in Catherine's room, the wig on the dresser, is charged with a significance that exceeds ordinary description. But the significance does not point beyond the world. It points deeper into it. Young offers a way of experiencing the world as sacred that requires no transcendence, no beyond, no god outside the becoming. The sacred is immanent or it is nothing. This is the tenth pillar, and it is, in some ways, the one that holds all the others together.

Immanence is the philosophical term for what is present within rather than transcendent beyond. It is the opposite of the move that most philosophical and religious systems make when they encounter the difficulty of existence: the move upward, outward, beyond, toward the foundation that does not shift, the ground that holds still, the truth that exists outside the flux and can therefore anchor it. Young refuses that move at every level of her system. There is no transcendent ground in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling by Marguerite Young. There is no outside to which Vera can appeal, no stable reality beneath Catherine's opium world, no solid Miss MacIntosh underneath the wig. Everything that exists, exists here, in the immanent world, in the middle of the becoming, in the room with the contradiction.

Radical is the modifier that specifies how far the immanence goes. A moderate immanence might acknowledge that most of what we know is available within experience while leaving open the possibility of some transcendent anchor. Young's immanence is not moderate. It goes all the way down. The immanence is the whole of it. What is here is what there is, and what is here is plural, constructed, grief-saturated, and alive in ways that the search for transcendence has always prevented us from fully seeing.

This is not nihilism. The refusal of transcendence is not the refusal of meaning. It is the insistence that meaning is made here, in the immanent world, by plural selves building provisional structures with the full knowledge that the structures are provisional. That is a harder and more honest account of meaning than any transcendent guarantee could provide. Young understood this. She built 1,198 pages of demonstration of it.

Robin Wall Kimmerer's work on reciprocity and the intelligence of the living world enters the conversation around the tenth pillar. Kimmerer's insistence that the world is not a collection of objects but a community of subjects, that attention to the non-human is not sentiment but epistemology, rhymes with Young's own practice of giving the Indiana landscape the same density and interiority she gives her human characters. The yellow canary in Catherine's room is not a symbol. It is a subject, a living presence whose significance is immanent in its being there, in its smallness and its song and its survival in the room where Catherine drifts between waking and dreaming. The Indiana plains are not a backdrop. They are a participant in the novel's world, carrying the same weight of accumulated time and grief as any human character.

The tenth pillar is where the system becomes, if the word is not too large, spiritual, but spiritual in a way that requires no god outside the becoming, no transcendent ground beneath the flux. The sacred is the ordinary world attended to with the full quality of attention the witness brings. The grey goose bus is sacred. The wig on the back of the crab is sacred. The permanent grief is sacred. The plural self in all its constructed, immanent, unresolved reality is sacred.

That is the tenth pillar. That is radical immanence. That is what the title names.
