The Infinite Detour: Tristram Shandy and the Shape of Miss MacIntosh, My Darling

Movement Five: The Traditions the Novel Absorbs

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Titus Bonebreaker · Vera Cartwheel

Symbols: The Bus and the Journey

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Pillars: Pillar 6 (Unfinished Is Honest) · Pillar 11 (The Crossing Point)

Chapters discussed: 1, 5, 55, 65, 82

Laurence Sterne was an English country parson who, in his forties, began a comic novel that made him famous. The Life and Opinions of Tristram Shandy, Gentleman came out in nine small volumes between 1759 and 1767. It is supposed to be the story of Tristram's life. It hardly gets there. Tristram starts with the night he was begotten: “I was begot in the night betwixt the first Sunday and the first Monday in the month of March, in the year of our Lord one thousand seven hundred and eighteen.” He is not born until the third volume. Every time the story sets off, a side road opens, and Tristram takes it.

He knew exactly what he was doing. “Digressions, incontestably, are the sunshine;—they are the life, the soul of reading,” he says. “In a word, my work is digressive, and it is progressive too,—and at the same time.” In the fourth volume he does the sum. He is a year older than when he started writing, and he has got “no farther than to my first day’s life.” At this rate he can never catch up with himself.

Marguerite Young loved this book. When Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs asked her about influences, she named Tristram Shandy among the books that formed her, with Saint Augustine's City of God, Gogol's Dead Souls, Dickens, and Poe. When they called her experimental, she answered: “I don’t really consider Sterne, Joyce, and Proust experimental either because the tradition of their writing goes back a long way. Traditional. The Grand Tradition. Clear back to Don Quixote” (Review of Contemporary Fiction, Fall 1989). In 1983 she told the American Audio Prose Library that she felt nearer to Sterne and Joyce than to Henry James, because she saw “a more chaotic world.” Talking with Studs Terkel in 1965, she named Sterne, with Proust and Joyce, when she explained her long “dragnet sentence.” And in a review from the 1940s, collected in Inviting the Muses (1994), she placed Sterne among the writers who work from “the anarchic principle that reality is given to us only through our sensations,” for whom “the best of reality is an opinion.” That phrase sits close to Sterne's own motto. Tristram Shandy opens with a line from the Greek teacher Epictetus: people are troubled not by things but by their opinions about things. Hence the title, the Life and Opinions.

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is built the same way. It sets out on a journey. Vera rides a Grey Goose bus through the fog, looking for the nursemaid she lost (chapter 1). Then the road keeps opening sideways. The book goes back into the house of her childhood, into Catherine's opium visions, into Mr. Spitzer's long walks and his unfinished music, into Cousin Hannah's travels. The story of Miss MacIntosh's own past, the woman the book is named for, waits until chapter 65. Like Tristram's book, this one is digressive and progressive at the same time. It moves toward its end by going around.

Young even gives the method to a character. The bus-driver, as he sees it in chapter 5, keeps “taking the wrong roads because the right road would have been too direct, too short a way, taking this infinite detour through nowhere.” That could stand as a description of the whole novel.

Young never names Sterne in the novel, but his images come into it in her own words. Sterne's most famous scene comes in the sixth volume. Kind Uncle Toby swears an oath out of pity for a dying soldier, and the recording angel, writing it down in heaven, “dropp’d a tear upon the word, and blotted it out for ever.” The recording angel keeps turning up in Young's book. The bus-driver gets “no credit in the book of human life, none by the recording angel” (chapter 5). Mr. Spitzer, who can never make his own accounts come out right, asks, “could not the recording angel have made, also, a grand mistake?” (chapter 55). Sterne's angel blots out a sin with a tear. Young's angel might simply get the sums wrong.

A second saying of Sterne's arrives by way of Mr. Bonebreaker. After he flees from Miss MacIntosh, she picks up the Bible tracts he dropped and reads, “God loves the shorn lamb” (chapter 65). Sterne made the saying famous in A Sentimental Journey (1768): God tempers the wind to the shorn lamb. Young gives it to the woman in the book who has no hair.

The last echo may be the deepest. Tristram Shandy begins on the night of a begetting, with Tristram wishing his parents “had minded what they were about when they begot me.” Miss MacIntosh, My Darling ends on that same night seen from the other side. Vera, carrying a child, says, “I do not know the night in which I conceived as I do not know the night in which I was conceived” (chapter 82). Sterne's book starts at the moment of conception and hardly gets past it. Young's book reaches that moment only at the end, and stops there.

The Radical Immanence Reading

In the language of Radical Immanence, the infinite detour is Pillar 6 (Unfinished Is Honest): a life told honestly cannot be told in a straight line, and a book that admits this has not failed. Vera's last thought, about two nights she cannot know, is Pillar 11 (The Crossing Point). She stands between the night that made her and the night that made her child. The Thousand Nights follows the same method in Scheherazade, whose tangents keep her alive. The Masterclass chapter on the novel's symbolism traces what Young took from Sterne and from Fielding.
