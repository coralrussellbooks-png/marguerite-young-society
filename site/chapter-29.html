Summary

Mr. Spitzer speaks at length about butterflies. He compares the difficulty of telling butterfly from moth to telling brother from brother, and happiness from sorrow. He names species by the hundreds and dwells on the butterfly as psyche, the soul. He describes Peron's funeral, crowded with gamblers and merry mourners, and the one person who saw his grief, an old lamplighter weeping on a street corner. He wonders whether a sock darned again and again until no original thread remains is still the same sock. The chapter ends with him as a dark atheist who knows more of the life beyond the grave than of this one.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens by discussing the difficulty of distinguishing butterflies from moths, creatures that exist on a boundary line, symbolic of the blurred lines between happiness and sorrow, day and night, life and death.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s observations on butterfly and moth behavior are presented as metaphors for life’s uncertainties.
  • The mystery of night-flying butterflies and oceanic migrations suggests the theme of wandering souls or psyches, with butterflies representing spiritual voyagers seeking reunion with immortal love.
  • The transformation of the caterpillar into the chrysalis and then the butterfly is described with deep symbolism: death and rebirth, the chrysalis as a shroud or mummy, and the butterfly as an epitome of immortality within mortality.
  • The enduring and recurring memories of butterflies in Mr. Spitzer’s mind serves as a metaphor for memory, identity, and the persistence of the soul beyond physical death.
  • The chapter elaborates on the spectacular colors of butterflies, which are illusions created by light on scales, not pigmentation, paralleling the illusory nature of life and perception.
  • The idea that male butterflies emit perfumes to attract scentless females introduces a sensual, almost spiritual dimension to the butterfly’s existence, with Mr. Spitzer even imagining he can smell these perfumes as extensions of his own thoughts or memories.
  • Mr. Spitzer reflects on the unknowability of life beyond death, the multiplicity of gods and spirits, and the possibility that butterflies may have originated beyond Earth, highlighting the vast unknown and the limits of human reason.
  • The metaphor of the “bipart soul” and the questioning of selfhood and identity are central to the chapter’s existential inquiries. Who really owns a soul, and can one be alive and dead simultaneously?
  • The death of Mr. Spitzer’s brother Peron and the impact of that loss loomed large. The chapter contrasts Peron’s early death and wild life with Joachim’s (Mr. Spitzer’s) long survival marked by grief, memory, and the pursuit of meaning through butterflies.
  • The funeral scene with its bizarre, lively crowd reflects the complexities of social death and memory.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s mental state and his ongoing internal struggle with grief and identity are woven through the reflections on butterflies and memory.
  • Despite his age and diminished faculties, Mr. Spitzer retains and even deepens his knowledge of butterflies, from common species to exotic and mythical varieties, associating them with gods, myths, and celestial bodies.
  • The chapter gives a sense of the butterfly as a cosmic and spiritual symbol, embodying life’s fragility and transcendence.
  • The analogy of Mr. Spitzer’s patched socks and tattered coat illustrates the human condition of decay and endurance, the fragility of identity, and the process of continual repair and reinvention.
  • The regal butterflies, despite their vulnerability, symbolize dignity and persistence amid mortality.
