Chapter 29

On Chapter 29 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Summary

Mr. Spitzer speaks at length about butterflies. He compares the difficulty of telling butterfly from moth to telling brother from brother, and happiness from sorrow. He names species by the hundreds and dwells on the butterfly as psyche, the soul. He gives God many names, from the True Vine to the King of Light “opposed… by the necessary King of Darkness,” and counts the gods who visit Catherine’s bedroom. He describes Peron’s funeral, crowded with gamblers and merry mourners, and the one person who saw his grief, an old lamplighter weeping on a street corner. He wonders whether a sock darned again and again until no original thread remains is still the same sock. The chapter ends with him as a dark atheist who knows more of the life beyond the grave than of this one.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens by discussing the difficulty of distinguishing butterflies from moths, creatures that exist on a boundary line, symbolic of the blurred lines between happiness and sorrow, day and night, life and death.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s observations on butterfly and moth behavior are presented as metaphors for life’s uncertainties.
  • The mystery of night-flying butterflies and oceanic migrations suggests the theme of wandering souls or psyches, with butterflies representing spiritual voyagers seeking reunion with immortal love.
  • The transformation of the caterpillar into the chrysalis and then the butterfly is described with deep symbolism: death and rebirth, the chrysalis as a shroud or mummy, and the butterfly as an epitome of immortality within mortality.
  • The enduring and recurring memories of butterflies in Mr. Spitzer’s mind serve as a metaphor for memory, identity, and the persistence of the soul beyond physical death.
  • The chapter elaborates on the spectacular colors of butterflies, which are illusions created by light on scales, not pigmentation, paralleling the illusory nature of life and perception.
  • The idea that male butterflies emit perfumes to attract scentless females introduces a sensual, almost spiritual dimension to the butterfly’s existence, with Mr. Spitzer even imagining he can smell these perfumes as extensions of his own thoughts or memories.
  • Mr. Spitzer reflects on the unknowability of life beyond death, the multiplicity of gods and spirits, and the possibility that butterflies may have originated beyond Earth, highlighting the vast unknown and the limits of human reason.
  • The metaphor of the “bipart soul” and the questioning of selfhood and identity are central to the chapter’s existential inquiries. Who really owns a soul, and can one be alive and dead simultaneously?
  • The death of Mr. Spitzer’s brother Peron and the impact of that loss looms large. The chapter contrasts Peron’s early death and wild life with Joachim’s (Mr. Spitzer’s) long survival marked by grief, memory, and the pursuit of meaning through butterflies.
  • The funeral scene with its bizarre, lively crowd reflects the complexities of social death and memory.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s mental state and his ongoing internal struggle with grief and identity are woven through the reflections on butterflies and memory.
  • Despite his age and diminished faculties, Mr. Spitzer retains and even deepens his knowledge of butterflies, from common species to exotic and mythical varieties, associating them with gods, myths, and celestial bodies.
  • The chapter gives a sense of the butterfly as a cosmic and spiritual symbol, embodying life’s fragility and transcendence.
  • The analogy of Mr. Spitzer’s patched socks and tattered coat illustrates the human condition of decay and endurance, the fragility of identity, and the process of continual repair and reinvention.
  • The regal butterflies, despite their vulnerability, symbolize dignity and persistence amid mortality.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “Just as it might at times be difficult to distinguish between brother and brother.” Why does the chapter begin with butterflies and moths?
  • “Yet butterflies were colorless.” What does that discovery mean to a man who doubts his own reality?
  • Are the darned socks “new socks or old”?

Characters

  • Joachim played Peron’s role to break off Peron’s affairs. What did it cost him to be taken for his brother?
  • At the funeral, only an old lamplighter saw his grief. Why a lamplighter?
  • “This dark atheist, this believer in nothing” says no one can “speak with greater authority than he of the life beyond the grave.” How can both be true?

Connections

Research: Characters

Joachim Spitzer (Peron’s twin brother, who allegedly lived)

  • An old, reflective lawyer and butterfly collector.
  • He embodies melancholy, wisdom, and the quest for understanding life through the metaphor of butterflies.
  • His thoughts are clouded by grief and age, yet his butterfly knowledge grows even in mental decline.
  • His grief after his brother’s death is profound and almost hidden, and it shapes his worldview.
  • He played “Peron’s role” as his brother’s “reluctant go-between,” breaking off Peron’s affairs by seeming to be him.
  • At the funeral he seemed serene, “strange as the phoenix,” but he was “utterly devastated,” and only an old lamplighter saw his tears.
  • He calls himself “this dark atheist, this believer in nothing.”

Peron Spitzer (Joachim’s twin brother, allegedly dead by suicide)

  • A mysterious, volatile figure who lived a fragmented life.
  • His death profoundly impacts Mr. Spitzer, whose mourning colors his worldview.
  • Peron’s character contrasts with Mr. Spitzer’s logical nature; he is seen as a greater mystery.
  • His funeral drew “the merriest mourners who ever were”: gamblers, vaudeville stars, mountebanks, and “ruined gamblers.”

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • She believes herself “both living and dead, this animated and bejeweled corpse,” and imagines the migratory beds of the famous dead drifting to her.
  • Imagined alongside Mr. Spitzer’s butterfly reflections, creating a shared atmosphere of mystery.

  • “as the plume of Raphael”: The plume (feather) of Raphael may refer to the archangel. See Angels and Archangels.
  • “How out of the heart of darkness”: Heart of Darkness (1899) is a novella by Polish-English novelist Joseph Conrad. The novel is widely regarded as a critique of European colonial rule in Africa, while also examining the themes of power dynamics and morality. The “heart of darkness” symbolizes the profound and often painful interior reality that characters must confront, a place of mystery, suffering, and the unknown depths of the human condition.
  • “old Beau Brummells who had risen from their graves”: The dandies of Chapter 24. See the Chapter 24 resources.
  • “merry andrews and fat aldermen”: A merry-andrew was a clown at a fair, and a domino a hooded masquerade cloak.
  • “strange as the phoenix, the perfumed bird of Arabia arising from its ashes”: Herodotus describes the phoenix that comes from Arabia every five hundred years (Histories 2.73). See Greek Mythology.
  • “an old lamp-lighter”: Lamplighter is a person employed to light and maintain cable or gas street lights. Very few exist today.
  • “like Jacob with his ladder of dreams”: Jacob’s Ladder is a ladder leading to heaven that was featured in a dream the biblical Patriarch Jacob had during his flight from his brother Esau in the Book of Genesis 28. The significance of the dream has been debated, but most interpretations agree that it identified Jacob with the obligations and inheritance of the people chosen by God, as understood in Abrahamic religions. It is significant as a symbol of transcendence and the complex interplay between different realms of existence, reflecting the novel’s broader themes of spiritual searching, change, and the quest for understanding beyond the physical world. See Genesis.
  • “the similitude of death, its twin brother”: Sleep and Death are twins in Hesiod (Chapter 25), and sleep is “death’s counterfeit” in Macbeth 2.3. See Twins and Doubles and Shakespeare.
  • “the powdery pollen gold freighting the bright wings of transcendental butterflies”: Transcendental butterflies may refer to the Butterfly curve which is a transcendental plane curve discovered by Temple H. Fay (“The Butterfly Curve,” American Mathematical Monthly 96, no. 5, May 1989, 442-443).
  • “the veined leaf green, the milky midnight blue”: The veined leaf green is a butterfly found in Europe and Asia.
    • The milky midnight blue is found in tropical Mexico, Central and South America.
  • “the old Diomedes with planets and half moons”: Diomedes (Deudorix) is a butterfly found in Africa. See Homer’s Epics.
    • Diomedes is a prominent figure in Greek mythology. He is known as a brave and skilled warrior who participated in the Trojan War and is renowned for his exploits in Homer’s Iliad.
  • “the ethereal tortoise-shell”: Tortoise-shell butterflies are found in Europe, Asia, and North America.
  • “the white-veined blacks, the black-veined marble whites”: The white-veined blacks are a common butterfly found in northwest Africa and Asia.
    • The black-veined marble white may be the Black-veined White (Aporia crataegi), a large butterfly of Europe that died out in Britain in the 1920s.
  • “butterflies as big as the wailing lapwing”: Lapwing is a ground nesting bird found in Europe. See Astronomy.
    • Tropic of Cancer (Northern Tropic) is the most northerly circle of latitude on Earth.
    • The Tropic of Capricorn (Southern Tropic) is the circle of latitude closest to the equator.
    • Aurora Borealis is the northern lights.
    • Aurora Australis is the southern lights.
  • “the emerald, the amethyst, the coxcomb red”: The emerald (Papilio palinurus) butterfly is found in Southeast Asia.
    • The amethyst may refer to the Purple emperor butterfly found in Europe and Asia.
    • The “coxcomb red” most likely refers to the Coxcomb Prominent moth (Ptilodon capucina). This moth is named for the white tuft of scales on its head, which resembles a coxcomb or the crest of a rooster. It’s a fairly common moth in Britain and is known for its camouflaged appearance when at rest, resembling a dead leaf. The moth’s caterpillar is green with two red projections and has a unique defense mechanism of curling its head back when alarmed.
  • “the red admiral of the light-wing fleet”: The red admiral is a butterfly found on five continents.
  • “the old butterfly geographers drawing their maps of this world”: The Piri Reis map (1513), drawn by an Ottoman admiral from older sources.
  • “the uniform of the checkered skipper”: The checkered skipper is a butterfly found in the US.
    • The silver-spotted skipper is a butterfly found in North America.
    • The grizzled and dingy skippers are butterflies found in Europe and Asia.
    • The greylings is a butterfly found in Europe.
    • Adonis blues butterflies are found in Europe and Asia.
    • Wood white butterflies are found in Europe.
  • “Io on one wing and Io on the other wing as if they knew they had two souls”: Io was one of the mortal lovers of Zeus in Greek mythology. See Greek Mythology and Twins and Doubles.
  • “the cabbage-haunting butterflies”: Cabbage white butterflies are found in Europe and North America. See the Chapter 28 resources.
    • The painted lady is a butterfly found on every continent except Antarctica and South America.
    • Swallowtail butterflies are found mostly in tropical regions but are found on every continent except Antarctica.
  • “crowding through his mind in empty hours”: Young is describing a hyperfixation where a person is completely engrossed by something, to the point where it becomes all-consuming and disrupts day-to-day functioning. It gets hard to focus on anything other than the object of hyperfixation. With hyperfixation, passion can turn into obsession. The object of interest takes up so much time, it comes at the cost of personal wellbeing, relationships, and responsibilities. It can even be a symptom of an underlying mental health condition. (Jackie Menjivar, “What Is Hyperfixation, and How Can You Manage It?” I Don’t Mind, March 8, 2022)
  • “the harmless hobby of knitting fine socks”: Knitting’s history traces back to the Middle East and North Africa, with evidence suggesting its origins around the 10th century. It spread to Europe via trade routes and became a widespread craft.
  • “to ask himself the philosopher’s question”: Mr. Spitzer has applied the philosophical question of the Ship of Theseus to his socks. Ship of Theseus is a thought experiment that questions if an object that has had all of its components replaced remains fundamentally the same object. There have been three proposed solutions: there is no identity over time, four-dimensionalism, and according to cognitive science it is an example of extreme externalism - the assumption that what is true in our minds is true in the world. In philosophy of mind, the ship is replaced by a person, whose identity over time is called into question. The Ship of Theseus metaphor and the image of Mr. Spitzer’s socks together articulate the tension between change and sameness, the fragility of identity, and the inevitable passage of time that leaves physical and existential marks on the individual. They highlight the novel’s preoccupation with the mutability of self and the paradox of persistence through transformation. The puzzle is from Plutarch, Life of Theseus 23.
  • “old monarchs with their orange-brown wings”: King butterfly is another name for a Monarch butterfly.
    • Queen butterflies are found in North and South America.
    • Cardinal butterflies are found in Europe and Asia.
  • “He swore that their chant would be almost Gregorian”: Gregorian chant is a form of monophonic, unaccompanied sacred song in Latin of the Roman Catholic Church. See Music and Composers.
  • “this dark atheist, this believer in nothing”: Mr. Spitzer considers himself a dark atheist. A negative atheist is someone who does not believe in the existence of deities but does not explicitly assert that there are none. Mr. Spitzer calling himself a “dark atheist” signifies his profound struggle with the mysteries of existence, death, and faith. It captures his skeptical yet deeply reflective nature, his confrontation with loss and absence, and his place within the novel’s broader themes of illusion, reality, and the search for meaning amid chaos.
