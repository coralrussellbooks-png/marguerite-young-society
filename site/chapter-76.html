To All My Darlings

Chapter 76

On Chapter 76 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 10, 2026

← Chapter 75 · Home · Chapter 77 →

Content note: The Esther Longtree chapters (72 to 81) deal with stillbirth and pregnancy loss, rape, infanticide, racist violence including lynching, and cruelty to children. This chapter deals with infant death and abortion; Esther says she once tried to kill herself, and tells of a girl made pregnant by the police in a jail.

Summary

Esther wonders whether her successful conceptions were her worst mistakes. She tells of the Chicago detective, who wears disguises and solves every mystery but his own, above all the “quiet, quiet mysteries” where no bodies are missed; he writes that he will track her to her grave if she does not produce his living children. He is still hunting the “bleeding rapist” whose handiwork he found in Esther, and now she says he “had never touched her.” Her stillborn children hover and applaud whenever she lies with a salesman, and a small brown hand closes her womb. She would rather face trial for mass infanticide than for one murder, says she once tried to kill herself, and confesses that in her mother’s womb she murdered her twin by kicking it out too early. Cupid has missed her, though her heart is broken by experience. Fired from teaching school on false charges, she knits and unravels a black hood for a child to be born white as snow, and she is tired of all the men and all the fathers.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, Esther contemplates her past life, realizing that her “successful conceptions” might have been her worst mistakes: her “unfulfilled abortions” and “many births.” This passage reveals her conflicted feelings about motherhood, conception, and the pain intertwined with creation and loss. She is puzzled by her “last triumphant pregnancy” and the “diffused radiance” she feels despite her troubles. This sets the tone for her psychological and emotional state throughout the chapter.
  • The Chicago detective is portrayed as a relentless figure, always uncovering “murders, murders everywhere,” yet unable to solve his own mysteries. His pursuit of crimes, both “quiet” and “loud,” symbolizes an unyielding quest for truth amid chaos and silence. He represents external pressure on Esther, threatening exposure for her alleged crimes concerning his children. This passage also highlights his obsession and the burden of his past cases, setting up the tension between him and Esther.
  • The Chicago detective’s fascination with “quiet mysteries” or murders “where no bodies were missed” parallels Esther’s own experience with stillborn children and miscarriages. The notion that murder and loss can be silent, unrecorded, and unseen reflects Esther’s hidden suffering and the societal invisibility of her pain. This metaphor deepens the theme of unseen tragedies and the complexity of guilt and accusation.
  • A poignant passage details how Esther perceives her numerous stillborn children as “robed, bloodless,” hovering between life and death, sometimes applauding her “act of intercourse.” This vivid imagery captures her maternal longing, guilt, and the haunting presence of loss that accompanies her sexual encounters. It also emphasizes her isolation and the burden of motherhood without fulfillment.
  • Esther’s thoughts on being accused, tried, and perhaps imprisoned reveal her resignation and complex acceptance of her circumstances. She contemplates the “mass infanticide” she might be charged with and paradoxically finds a form of comfort in the vastness of her “cosmic” evidence: her body, nature, and memories. This passage underscores her mixture of defiance, despair, and hope.
  • Towards the end, Esther wonders why Cupid, the god of love, has “missed her,” even though her heart is broken. This small but profound question highlights her loneliness and the pain of being unloved despite her capacity to love. It also connects to the broader themes of emotional abandonment and longing.
  • The closing passages describe Esther’s daily life bound by routine yet wild and free in spirit, marked by visions of birth and death, presences and evidence everywhere she goes. Despite her hardships and the looming threat of trial, she holds onto a flicker of hope and purpose, knitting and unraveling a black hood symbolizing night and birth. This ending encapsulates her resilience and the cyclical nature of her existence.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “There were always two ways of cruelty.” What are the two?
  • “She was always knitting and unravelling the black hood for one who would be born as white as melting snow.” Why black, and why unravel it?
  • “Her children were snowflakes drifting in eternity, were leaves, were tears.” What do the three images share?

Characters

  • Now Esther says the detective “had never touched her” and “was no more than a mistaken father.” How does this square with Chapter 74?
  • The detective hunts “the bleeding rapist” and finds “his handiwork, Esther Longtree.” Whose handiwork?
  • “She had tried to kill herself.” Why does Esther say it so quietly, between other thoughts?

Connections

  • Esther “had murdered her twin by kicking it out early.” How does this echo the Spitzer twins?
  • Like Penelope at her loom, Esther is “knitting and unravelling.” What is she waiting for?
  • “Who had not killed cock robin among the fallen autumn leaves?” How has Esther’s answer changed since Chapter 72?

Reflection

  • “There were some little boys who were murderers, too, and killed their mothers, but it was only because their mothers had already killed them.” Is that fair to the mothers?
  • “Her heart had been broken by experience.” Can experience break a heart as love does?
  • “She was guilty of some kind of crime.” Why does Esther feel guilty even where she is innocent?

Research: Characters

Esther Longtree

  • A complex woman, described as pregnant and burdened with past conceptions that were either miscarriages or stillbirths.
  • She is reflective, brooding on her life’s mistakes and her uncertain relations with the detective and the many men (salesmen) she encounters.
  • Esther is maternal yet haunted, caught between hope and despair, and struggles with her identity and the accusations she faces.

The Chicago Detective

  • An enigmatic figure who has solved countless murders, both loud and quiet, often using disguises and undercover tactics.
  • He is relentless and invasive, unable to avoid uncovering dark secrets wherever he goes.
  • His interest in Esther is personal, as he believes she has his children.
  • He is still hunting “the bleeding rapist” and says he found “his handiwork, Esther Longtree with her clothes torn and her mouth foaming”; yet in Chapter 74 Esther says the detective raped her, and here that he “had never touched her.”
  • His presence represents judgment, law, and the relentless pursuit of truth.

Research: Themes

Motherhood and Infanticide

See: Motherhood, Pregnancy, and Birth

  • Esther’s experience with pregnancy is fraught with loss, as she has many stillborn or miscarried children, which the detective calls “quiet mysteries.”
  • The concept of murder is extended metaphorically to include unborn or unconceived children, highlighting complex feelings of guilt and responsibility.

Memory and Guilt

See: Memory and the Past · Guilt, Innocence, and Justice

  • Esther is haunted by memories of past events, including her twin’s death in utero, her own repeated losses, and her interactions with men.
  • These memories induce a sense of guilt, self-accusation, and uncertainty about truth and justice.

Justice and Investigation

See: Guilt, Innocence, and Justice

  • The detective’s role symbolizes the pursuit of truth and justice, but also the intrusion and exposure of private pain.
  • His tireless search uncovers “quiet murders” that society ignores, reflecting on the nature of crime and detection beyond the obvious.

Isolation and Alienation

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Esther’s internal world is lonely and bleak. She feels persecuted by both the detective and her memories.
  • Her physical environment (a messy bedroom, empty cradle) mirrors her emotional and psychological state.

The Feminine and the Unseen

See: Women, Gender, and Marriage

  • The narrative explores the unseen aspects of womanhood (miscarriage, stillbirth, and the emotional landscape tied to pregnancy and motherhood) often ignored or silenced in society.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

  • Pregnancy and Birth: Symbolizes creation, life, and hope, but in Esther’s case, it also represents loss, failure, and mystery. Her “last triumphant pregnancy” is tinged with both anticipation and dread.
  • Stillborn Children / Silent Murders: Represent the quiet tragedies that go unnoticed. They haunt Esther and serve as metaphors for lost potential and hidden guilt.
  • The Detective’s Disguises: His changing appearance symbolizes the fluidity of identity, the hidden truths beneath surfaces, and the persistent nature of truth-seeking.
  • Nature (Leaves, Autumn, Flowers, Moths): Recurring natural imagery symbolizes cycles of life and death, decay and renewal, and Esther’s connection to a cosmic order beyond human understanding.
  • The Empty Cradle and Wedding Rings: The empty cradle signifies lost children and unfulfilled motherhood, while wedding rings on Esther’s nipples but not her fingers suggest a distorted or incomplete domestic/marital identity.

Research: Resources

  • “Poor Esther Longtree, who might be killed by little Johnny-jump-up”: Esther Longtree emerges in this chapter as a deeply tragic and complex figure, embodying themes of fertility, loss, sorrow, and marginalization. Her character is portrayed with a rich, symbolic density that reveals much about her internal struggles and her relationship to the world around her. Esther is described as physically large, often pregnant or swollen, with features like a long nose and crossed eyes, evoking a somewhat grotesque yet poignant image. She is burdened by repeated pregnancies, many of which end in stillbirths or miscarriages. This physical condition mirrors her emotional state: she is exhausted, confused, and isolated, caught in a cycle of hope and despair. Her body and mind are both “clouded” and “indistinct,” reflecting her fragmented sense of self and history. Esther’s experience of pregnancy and motherhood is fraught with sorrow and complexity. She carries numerous “stillborn children,” described almost as ghostly presences hovering around her, watching her with innocent smiles and applause, yet also causing her pain and distraction during intercourse. These spectral children symbolize her lost potential for motherhood and the deep trauma of repeated loss. She does not reject these children but claims them as hers, showing a profound maternal attachment despite the pain they bring. However, this attachment is intertwined with guilt and confusion, as she contemplates the nature of her past conceptions and births. Esther is entangled with a Chicago detective who is aggressively pursuing proof of his paternity of her children. This detective symbolizes the harsh, intrusive forces of society, law, and judgment. Esther fears his investigations, seeing them as threats that could expose her to legal and social punishment. Yet at the same time, she contemplates the possibility of the trial with a strange blend of hope and resignation, almost welcoming the exposure as a form of acknowledgment of her suffering and existence. This ambivalence highlights her isolation and the precariousness of her position as a woman burdened by reproductive tragedy and societal scrutiny. The chapter frequently returns to motifs of birth and death, conception and miscarriage, seen both literally and metaphorically in Esther’s story. She is described as knitting and unravelling a black hood, symbolizing the night and mystery surrounding her existence. Her pregnancies are “clouded,” and her children are often invisible or spectral, linking her personal tragedy to broader cosmic or existential mysteries. Esther’s life is likened to an “empty coffin on the water,” merging images of life and death, presence and absence. This symbolism situates her story within larger themes of loss, fate, and the unknowable forces shaping human experience. Despite her suffering, Esther exhibits a kind of wildness and defiance. She runs, she laughs, she knits, she endures. She is “wilder than people thought,” living a life bounded by routine but also marked by moments of freedom and rebellion. Her laughter in the face of the detective’s threats and the looming trial shows a resilience and complexity that defy simple victimhood. Esther’s narrative is one of survival amid despair, of a woman caught between social condemnation and her own intricate inner world. Esther’s experience is deeply intertwined with natural imagery: autumn leaves, winter bees, snowflakes, mists, rivers, and stars. These elements emphasize the cosmic scale of her suffering and the universality of her predicament. Her children are likened to “snowflakes drifting in eternity” and “robed” children and “old cherubim,” linking personal loss to the eternal cycles of life and death. This connection to nature and the cosmos reinforces the mystical and tragic dimensions of her character. Esther Longtree is a richly drawn figure whose story encapsulates themes of loss, fertility, maternal anguish, societal judgment, and existential mystery. She is at once vulnerable and resilient, tragic and wild, caught in a liminal space between life and death, hope and despair. The chapter paints her not merely as a victim but as a complex woman grappling with profound personal and cosmic questions, embodying the novel’s broader exploration of human suffering and endurance. See Esther Longtree.
    • Esther Longtree symbolizes a deeply tragic and complex history of fertility, loss, and maternal suffering that can be read as a metaphor for the burdens borne by many women across American history, particularly those marginalized or caught in cycles of neglect and violence. Her character embodies the pain of countless stillbirths, miscarriages, and unfulfilled pregnancies, reflecting a history of loss not only of children but of potential, hope, and future generations. Esther’s experience of repeated pregnancies resulting in stillborn or unconceived children, her reflections on the silent, “quiet mysteries” of death without visible bodies, and her haunted sense of guilt and accusation evoke a history of women’s reproductive struggles often ignored or misunderstood in society. She is portrayed as carrying the weight of many unborn or lost children “snowflakes drifting in eternity,” “leaves,” “tears” symbolizing not only personal loss but perhaps collective losses related to the harsh realities of life for women who have been abandoned, mistreated, or rendered invisible by legal and social systems. Esther’s sense of persecution, threatened by a detective’s pursuit to uncover her “children,” parallels the historical scrutiny and moral judgment women have faced regarding their reproductive lives, particularly those who have suffered trauma or have been entangled in social and legal conflicts about motherhood and fertility. Her uncertainty, her tangled memories, and the spectral presence of her many lost children evoke a history marked by silence, stigmatization, and the often invisible consequences of violence, both physical and societal, against women. Esther Longtree symbolizes the historical narrative of reproductive suffering and the silenced tragedies of many American women, embodying themes of loss, survival, and the complex interplay between personal history and broader social forces in the United States. Her story reflects a history of pain and resilience, maternal sacrifice, and the haunting presence of what is lost or denied in American society.
  • “her pupa, her Little Tom Thumb”: Little Tom Thumb may refer to Tom Thumb, a character in English folklore. The History of Tom Thumb was published in 1621 and was the first fairy tale printed in English. Tom Thumb may have been a real person born around 1519, as there is a grave purporting to be his. The grave measures just 16 inches in length. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “to bring life into this sad, mistaken world”: This is a deeply personal, philosophical, and biological question. Despite widespread anxiety about environmental issues, political instability, and suffering, many women continue to choose motherhood. Many believe that creating new life is an act of defiance against despair, bringing love, joy, and potential for positive change into the world. They may believe they can raise children who will contribute to fixing the world’s mistakes, acting as agents of change. Humans are biologically programmed to reproduce, with hormonal and physiological influences often reinforcing the desire to have children. This desire is frequently described as a powerful, instinctual urge. Having children is often seen as a way to find purpose, share love, and create a deeper, lasting connection with a partner. Women are recognized for their immense resilience and capacity to act as “backbones of communities,” even in difficult circumstances, by nurturing the next generation. Some argue that while the world contains suffering, it also holds immense beauty and joy, and that experiencing life, including its challenges, is worthwhile. Despite the trend toward smaller families, significant social, religious, and familial pressures still exist, urging women to conform to the traditional expectation of having children. For many, the desire to experience motherhood and the unique bond with a child outweighs the perceived risks of the world. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.

    • The Chicago detective may refer to a French detective from the 1880s, Robert Ledru, who was famous for solving crimes until a murder he was helping to investigate led to proof that he was, in fact, the murderer. Through an experiment conducted by the police it was determined that he suffered from stress and while sleepwalking, if he had access to a weapon, would shoot and kill a person.
  • “There were always two ways of cruelty”: The idea that “Nothing could ever seem to happen by itself in one cruel, conclusive way” implies that events, especially traumatic or violent ones, are not simple or linear. Psychologically, this aligns with systemic views of behavior, where one action is often a response to another, and causes are multifaceted. The recognition of “two ways of cruelty” acknowledges that hurt and harm are often reciprocated or that one form of cruelty begets another, reflecting the complexity of human interactions and the difficulty of assigning singular blame.
  • “there were some little boys who were murderers, too”: The belief that “some little boys who were murderers, too, and killed their mothers, but it was only because their mothers had already killed them, in quick ways and slow ways” suggests a psychological interpretation of violence as a symptom or consequence of earlier trauma, specifically maternal harm or neglect. This resonates with psychoanalytic and developmental psychology concepts about how early childhood experiences, especially those involving caregivers, profoundly shape emotional regulation, attachment, and later behavior. Children who experience harm, whether physical, emotional, or symbolic (such as neglect or abandonment), may internalize and later reenact these patterns, sometimes violently. The phrase “mothers had already killed them, in quick ways and slow ways” can be interpreted both literally and metaphorically. Psychologically, “killing” here may represent not only physical harm but also emotional or psychological damage such as: rejection, neglect, or emotional unavailability. This “kills” parts of the child’s spirit, identity, or sense of safety. This damage can manifest later in destructive behaviors, including violence towards others, even those closest to the child. The passage underscores the difficulty in attributing simple blame in situations of cruelty and violence. It suggests a cycle where victims may become perpetrators, and cruelty can be both cause and effect. This aligns with psychological understandings of the “cycle of abuse,” where patterns of trauma perpetuate across generations or within family dynamics, making it challenging to identify clear origins or solutions. The sentence carries an existential weight, reflecting on the ambiguity and complexity of human suffering. It challenges the reader to see cruelty not as a straightforward moral failing but as part of a tangled web of pain and reaction. Psychologically, this can be related to the concept of moral injury or the struggle to reconcile acts of harm within relationships that are also sources of love and care. See Freud and Jung.
    • Historical context: Mothers are often blamed for their children’s lives due to historical gender roles that place the burden of child-rearing primarily on them, a phenomenon called mother blaming. This can be seen in societal beliefs, professional fields like medicine and social work, and media, where mothers are assumed to be the cause of their children’s problems, from developmental issues like autism to behavioral problems. This practice overlooks external factors and other contributors to a child’s life and well-being, leading to guilt, shame, and a failure to address underlying issues appropriately. Blaming mothers for their children’s problems, despite gender inequality and lack of support, is a long-standing issue rooted in misogyny and societal expectations. Unequal gender roles assign mothers the bulk of childcare and emotional labor, creating a standard of perfection that is impossible to meet and leaves them vulnerable to blame.

  • “Some new crime would break, loud and noisy”: “Some new crime would break, loud and noisy, and the quiet mysteries would remain quiet forever if that was so.” The U.S. news cycle evolved from a slow, partisan, and highly local system in the early 18th century to a fast-paced, nationalized, and professionalized industry dominated by newspaper chains, radio, and the emergence of network television by the early 1960s. Early Colonial to Early 19th Century: Early journalism focused heavily on international news, which was often weeks or months old by the time it arrived by ship. Newspapers in the 18th and early 19th centuries were often subsidized by political parties or factions, acting as propaganda tools rather than objective information sources. Initially, newspapers were expensive and tailored to political and mercantile elites. Mid-19th Century: The launch of The Sun in 1833 revolutionized news by making it affordable (one cent) and focused on human-interest stories, crime, and gossip, targeting a mass audience. The invention of the telegraph and high-speed steam-powered printing presses in the 1840s enabled news to travel instantly, facilitating the growth of national news stories. The mid-19th century saw the rise of the Associated Press (AP), which allowed for the standardization of national news reporting. Late 19th to Early 20th Century: Publishers like Joseph Pulitzer and William Randolph Hearst engaged in sensationalized, scandalous, and often inaccurate reporting to boost circulation. In response to yellow journalism, the early 20th century saw a shift toward, and demand for, objective, “informed” journalism, exemplified by the New York Times under Adolph Ochs. By 1900, massive newspaper chains, such as the Hearst organization, dominated the landscape. 1920s to 1950s: In the 1920s and 1930s, radio introduced the first “instant” news updates, with major expansion during World War II. The Radio Act of 1927 and the Communications Act of 1934 (creating the FCC) brought standards and licensing to the airwaves. By the 1950s, daily television news began to displace radio and newsreels as the dominant medium, with the “Big Three” networks (NBC, CBS, ABC) dominating the airwaves. The news cycle was defined by the daily morning or evening newspaper and the nightly 15-to-30 minute evening news broadcast (e.g., CBS Evening News with Walter Cronkite). Audiences relied on a few trusted figures to interpret major events, such as the Cold War, the Civil Rights movement, and the Kennedy assassination. The 1960 Kennedy-Nixon debate, televised to 70 million viewers, highlighted the shift toward image-driven, nationalized media, setting the stage for the intense, image-focused coverage of the mid-1960s.

  • “to other little salesmen”: The salesmen characters in the novel may symbolically reflect various aspects of capitalism in the US through their behaviors, roles, and the social contexts they inhabit.
    • The salesmen appear repeatedly throughout the novel in different guises, selling a wide range of goods, from everyday objects like brooms, bird cages, and toys, to more abstract commodities like stocks and bonds. Their omnipresence symbolizes the reach of capitalist commerce into every facet of life, even into the most personal and intimate spaces of the characters. They reflect the diversity and saturation of market interactions in American life.
    • The salesmen often embody the pressures, anxieties, and instabilities caused by the capitalist system. Many salesmen in the novel are depicted as transient, struggling, or lost figures, highlighting the precariousness and alienation experienced by those caught in the capitalist economy. They symbolize the vulnerability of individuals within a capitalist society.
    • The salesmen also represent the commodification of human relations and the alienation inherent in capitalism. They sell not only material goods but also intangible things like securities and futures, connecting to themes of speculation and the illusion of wealth. This commodification extends to human relationships, as seen in the metaphor of the salesmen interacting with stillborn children or lost souls, symbolizing how capitalism can reduce human value to market transactions and speculative interests.
    • The novel hints at exploitation and inequality through the salesmen’s interactions and the socio-economic backdrop. The salesmen, as agents of this system, are both participants in and victims of such exploitation.
    • The salesmen’s activities also symbolize the illusory and unstable nature of capitalist wealth. The novel repeatedly emphasizes the ephemeral quality of money and possessions: stocks that may be worthless, jewels that may be fake, and transient fortunes. The salesmen’s persistent presence amidst loss, deception, and uncertainty reflects capitalism’s inherent volatility and the elusiveness of material success.
    • The salesmen embody human desires fueled by capitalism: desire for wealth, security, social status, and belonging. However, these desires are often unfulfilled or lead to suffering, as seen in the characters’ personal tragedies and the broader social critiques embedded in the narrative. The salesmen’s relentless selling and the characters’ interactions with them suggest the endless cycle of desire and consumption characteristic of capitalist societies.
    • The salesmen in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling symbolize capitalism in the US by representing its ubiquity, its impact on individuals, the commodification of human relations, capitalist exploitation and inequality, the illusory nature of wealth, and the insatiable human desires it engenders. They serve as embodiments of the economic forces shaping the characters’ lives and the social environment of the novel, reflecting both the promise and the perils of capitalist society.

    • Most simply, the “enlarged birth ring” is the birth canal stretched by many births. It may also refer to a Bandl’s ring, a rare and dangerous complication of obstructed labor that appears as a constriction at the junction between the upper and lower segments of the uterus. In contrast, a normal birth involves the cervix (a ring of muscle at the base of the uterus) dilating to allow a baby to pass through. While the cervix normally enlarges and opens during a vaginal birth, a Bandl’s ring is an abnormal, pathologic ring of thickened muscle that obstructs the birth process.
    • The labia minora are the inner folds of skin of the female external genitalia, located between the labia majora and surrounding the clitoris and vaginal and urethral openings. They vary significantly in size, shape, and color, and their functions include a sexual one, as they engorge with blood during sexual arousal. The appearance of the labia minora can change throughout a person’s life due to factors like genetics, hormones, aging, and weight fluctuations.
    • The labia majora are the two outer, fleshy folds of skin that form the “lips” of the vulva, protecting the other external genitalia. They are located on the mons pubis and extend down to the perineum, and contain sweat and sebaceous glands. These folds enclose and protect the vaginal and urethral openings and can change in appearance due to factors like aging, childbirth, and hormonal shifts.

  • “She was always knitting and unravelling the black hood”: Penelope, waiting twenty years for Odysseus, wove a shroud for his father by day and unravelled it by night to put off her suitors (Odyssey, Book 2). See Homer’s Epics and Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “the membrane like a shimmering bridal veil hanging from barren trees”: See the caul in the Chapter 74 resources.
  • “by Cupid, the blind god of love”: In classical mythology, Cupid is the god of desire, erotic love, attraction and affection. “And therefore is wing’d Cupid painted blind” (A Midsummer Night’s Dream, 1.1). See Greek Mythology, Love, and Shakespeare.
  • “drifting like a cloud with a silver lining”: “Was I deceiv’d, or did a sable cloud / Turn forth her silver lining on the night?” (Milton, Comus, 1634).
  • “She had tried to kill herself”: Esther says it once, in passing, and goes straight on to Cock Robin. See Esther Longtree.
  • “who had not killed cock robin”: Who Killed Cock Robin is an English nursery rhyme. The earliest record of the rhyme is in Tommy Thumb’s Pretty Song Book. A number of theories have been advanced to explain the meaning of the rhyme: a mythological event, such as the death of the god Balder from Norse mythology, or the ritual sacrifice of a king figure; a parody of the death of King William II, who was killed by an arrow while hunting, and who was known as William Rufus, which means red; the fall of Robert Walpole’s government in 1742, since Robin is a diminutive form of Robert and the first printing is close to the time of the events mentioned. Some folklore readings make the rhyme a seasonal myth, with the red-breasted robin as Lugh, the Irish god of light, skill, and kingship, killed at the turn of the year by winter. In Celtic studies Lugh is often paired with Brân the Blessed, the Welsh giant-king whose name means “Raven” and who was “crowned king over this Island” (Britannica). See the Chapter 72 resources. See Fairy Tales and Folklore and Birds.
  • “What twin accused his twin whom he had murdered”: This seems to refer to Joachim and Peron Spitzer and here Esther is saying that Peron killed Joachim and took his identity. See Joachim Spitzer, Peron Spitzer, and Twins and Doubles.
    • One set of twins in mythology where one is divine and the other mortal are Castor and Pollux, known as the Dioscuri. The pair are an example of heteropaternal superfecundation. The Dioscuri were regarded as helpers of humankind and held to be patrons of travelers and of sailors in particular, who invoked them to seek favorable winds. Their role as horsemen and boxers also led to them being regarded as the patrons of athletes and athletic contests. They characteristically intervened at the moment of crisis, aiding those who honored or trusted them. See superfecundation in the Chapter 73 resources.
    • The dioscuri had two twin sisters: Helen of Troy and Clytemnestra.
  • “had murdered her twin by kicking it out early”: Physicians have long known that one twin may die early in pregnancy and be shed or reabsorbed. With ultrasound in the 1970s this “vanishing twin” proved far more common than anyone had guessed. See Twins and Doubles and Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery.
  • “swimming around Cape Horn”: Cape Horn is the southernmost headland of southern Chile and marks where the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans meet.
    • See Homer Capehorn.
  • “or maybe he had got to Capricorn”: Capricorn is the tenth astrological sign in the zodiac originating from the constellation of Capricornus, the horned goat. The Tropic of Capricorn, the southern line of the sun’s farthest reach, takes its name from the sign. See Astrology, Alchemy, and Esoterica and Astronomy.
  • “the school trustees who would now be the jury had not fired her on false charges”: Esther once taught school. Rural school boards hired and fired teachers at will, and a woman teacher could be dismissed for marrying, for paint and powder, or for gossip. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
