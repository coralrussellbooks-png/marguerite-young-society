Chapter 79

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Esther Longtree

Pillars: Not One Self · The Margins Reveal the Truth

The Questions

Esther is “illusive… cloud-like, drifting… the weak and gentle,” though “from the outward view” she seems “firm, muscular, wind-burned.” Which Esther is the real one?

The settled world sees a “dumb” and “mulish” waitress. What does the outward view miss?

She kicks “against anyone who might try to harness her.” What is she refusing to be harnessed into?

Why does the book insist on the drifting cloud inside the mulish body?

The chapter turns on the gap between the Esther others see and the Esther she is. “Esther Longtree was illusive—yes, she was—cloud-like, drifting, herself the weak and gentle even though, from the outward view, she might seem otherwise—so firm, muscular, wind-burned.” Inwardly she is a drifting cloud, gentle and illusive; outwardly she is a coarse, muscular, mulish waitress leaning on a counter. This is Not One Self in a single body, the two Esthers utterly unlike, and the chapter’s point is that the outward one, the one the settled world sees and names, is not the real one, or not the only one. Under the mule is a cloud, and the cloud is as true as the mule.

The outward view misses everything that matters. The world sees Esther as “dumb” and “mulish even in her looks,” her lips “hanging like red cherries,” her skin “coarse and dark-stained,” and stops there, taking the coarse surface for the whole. But the chapter has just told the reader what the surface hides: the illusive, drifting, gentle inner life invisible to anyone who reads only the looks. This is The Margins Reveal the Truth turned inward on the one at the edge: the marginal figure is not only positioned to see the center’s seams, she is herself mis-seen by the center, flattened into “dumb” and “mulish” by an outward view that cannot reach the cloud drifting inside the wind-burned body. The edge has an inner life the center never suspects.

She kicks against being harnessed, and the kicking is her refusal of the seal. “Kicking, kicking against anyone who might try to harness her, wanting to be beautiful and graceful as a white horse running in the moonlight,” Esther will not be fitted into harness, will not be broken to the settled world’s use or its names. To harness her would be to fix her as the mule the outward view takes her for, to seal her into the coarse surface and deny the drifting cloud. Her mulishness, the very thing the world holds against her, is in this light her refusal to be sealed, her kicking insistence that she is not only what she is taken for, not to be harnessed into anyone’s fixed idea of her.

The book insists on the cloud inside the mule because that is the truth of the plural self at the edge. It would be easy to let Esther be the dumb mulish waitress the town sees, or, sentimentally, to make her secretly beautiful; the chapter does neither. It holds both at once, the coarse wind-burned body and the illusive drifting cloud, the mule and the white horse, neither cancelling the other. Esther is genuinely both, seen as one and being the other, and the book refuses to resolve her into either the surface the world reads or the inner life the world misses. She is the plural self the book has described, lived by the figure the settled world was surest it had sized up in a glance.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes the outward Esther, the dumb mulish waitress, for the whole of her, and the reading that would sentimentally replace that surface with a secretly beautiful true self. Neither the coarse surface alone nor the pretty inner truth alone is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that Esther is a plural self in one body, an illusive drifting cloud within a coarse muscular form, neither the real one at the other’s expense; that the settled world’s outward view flattens her into “dumb” and “mulish” and misses the inner life entirely, so the one at the edge is not only clear-sighted but mis-seen; and that her kicking against being harnessed is her refusal to be sealed into the surface the world takes for her whole self. The mule and the cloud are both true, and the surface the world is surest of is the one that misses the most. Esther kicks against every harness, and the chapter holds her double without resolving her.

For Reflection

Which is the real person, the one the world sees at a glance or the one drifting invisibly inside, and does the question have a single answer?

What inner life does the confident outward view of a coarse or plain or dumb-seeming person entirely miss?

What harness is one kicking against, refusing to be sealed into someone else’s fixed idea of who one is?
