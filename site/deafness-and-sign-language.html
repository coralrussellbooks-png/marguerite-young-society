Deafness and Sign Language

Part of To All My Darlings, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated November 11, 2026

Home

In the research: Chapter 1, Chapter 9, Chapter 13, Chapter 29, Chapter 49, Chapter 51, Chapter 61, Chapter 81, Chapter 82

Definition from research

Chapter 1

  • “because of a deaf musician” This figure introduces the theme of communication and isolation. A deaf musician cannot hear the music he creates or the world around him, symbolizing a disconnect or failure of connection, similar to the unanswered questions Vera faces.

Chapter 9

  • “A blind violinist and a deaf dancer”: There are many blind, deaf, and mute people in the book. This could be a play on the Three Wise Monkeys. The Three Wise Monkeys are a Japanese folk representation of the proverb “see no evil, hear no evil, speak no evil.” They symbolize prudence and purity, and are often seen as a call for discretion or willful ignorance. The three monkeys are Mizaru (see no evil), Kikazaru (hear no evil), and Iwazaru (speak no evil). The concept originates in Japan, with statues of the monkeys found in temples and shrines. The proverb “see no evil, hear no evil, speak no evil” is often interpreted as a way to avoid negative experiences or situations by choosing to ignore them. In Japan, the monkeys are seen as attendants of Koshin, the God of Roads, and their message is used to teach prudence and purity. In the West, the proverb is often used sarcastically to criticize those who deliberately ignore wrongdoing. The idea of avoiding what is contrary to propriety is rooted in Confucius’s teachings. Some representations include a fourth monkey, Sezaru, who “does no evil.” One misses the train by seeing it but not hearing its whistle, the other by hearing but not seeing.

Chapter 13

  • “not even the creaking of starlight”: Aristotle reports the Pythagorean answer: the spheres do make music, and people do not hear it because they have heard it since birth (On the Heavens 2.9). Lorenzo tells Jessica, “Such harmony is in immortal souls; / But whilst this muddy vesture of decay / Doth grossly close it in, we cannot hear it” (The Merchant of Venice 5.1).
  • “a subdued roaring, a noise which came from the head only”: Tinnitus, a ringing or roaring in the ears with no outside source. Beethoven described it in the Heiligenstadt Testament (1802).

Chapter 29

  • “It was his own personal theory that a butterfly could hear”: Science has found membranes on some butterfly wings that allow it to hear. The hearing organ on the wings of some butterflies is called Vogel’s organ, after Richard Vogel, who described it in 1912. His own “dead ear” is from Chapter 25.

Chapter 49

  • “Their yesterday was a pointing backward over the shoulder”: Young seems to use actual ASL sign language gestures but some seem to have changed over time. In American Sign Language time runs along the body: the past is signed behind, over the shoulder, and the future ahead.

Chapter 51

  • “Seldom did he meet with anyone who did not suffer from some form of complex illusion beyond necessity”: Most people Mr. Spitzer meets suffer “some form of complex illusion beyond necessity”: the old woman who believes she is a bird cage, the piano-tuner declaring foreheads “hollow.” Blindness and deafness become metaphors for the limits of perception: the blind portrait painter, the blind war veteran in the galleries of Europe, the deaf music critics who hear every error without hearing a note, judgment resting on something other than the senses. “One great dream informing all dreamers and all dreams” recalls a collective unconscious or universal mind, or Plato’s Form of the Good, the source all strive toward behind the shadows of sense. Happiness is fleeting as a snowflake; “three minutes of happiness seemed perhaps all there might be,” and “the greatest happiness might come through the sorrow of waiting, of postponement.” The passage holds the absurdity of existence and, through Mr. Spitzer’s work for the dead, the responsibility of making sense of their lives.
  • “the most famous music critics were the deaf”: Beethoven was nearly deaf when his Ninth Symphony was first played in 1824, and he had to be turned around to see the applause.

Chapter 61

  • “many signs which Mr. Spitzer had not understood”: American Sign Language grew from the school that Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet and the deaf French teacher Laurent Clerc opened in Hartford, Connecticut, in 1817.
  • “hearing with their eyes, their skin, their hair”: Studies of deaf adults find heightened attention to the edges of the visual field and to touch (Daphne Bavelier and others, “Do deaf individuals see better?” 2006, after 1964).
  • “a silent attendant to a Turk”: The Ottoman sultans kept mutes, the dilsiz, at the Topkapı Palace; they spoke among themselves in signs and sometimes served as executioners. See the Kafes in the Chapter 42 resources.
  • “a Spanish deaf mute lip-reader in Boston”: Boston was a center of oralism, which taught deaf children to read lips and speak and drove out sign language. Alexander Graham Bell taught his Visible Speech at the Boston School for Deaf Mutes in 1871, and the Milan congress of 1880 voted to ban signing in deaf schools.
  • “he was born deaf but had been made mute”: Most deaf people have working voices. The old word “deaf-mute” came from the fact that a child born deaf did not learn speech by ear, and many Deaf people today reject the term.

Chapter 81

  • Ludwig van Beethoven was a German composer and pianist. One of the most revered figures in the history of Western music, his works rank among the most performed of the classical music repertoire and span the transition from the Classical to the Romantic era. Beethoven’s early period, during which he forged his craft, is typically considered to have lasted until 1802. From 1802 to around 1812, his middle period showed an individual development from the styles of Joseph Haydn and Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart, and is sometimes characterised as heroic. During this time, Beethoven began to grow increasingly deaf. In his late period, from 1812 to 1827, he extended his innovations in musical form and expression.

Chapter 82

  • “Little boidies don’t have no tits”: The stone-deaf man was Young’s friend Howard Mitcham, a deaf painter in Greenwich Village. “He used to love to sing this, little boydies don’t have no tits. Little boydies don’t have no umbilical cords,” and “he knew that I was putting him in” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983). For a magazine article on the deaf she interviewed deaf leaders in New York and heard the Lord’s Prayer in sign language at St. Mark’s (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).
