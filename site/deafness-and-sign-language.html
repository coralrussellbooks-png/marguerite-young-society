Chapter 9

  • There are many blind, deaf, and mute people in the book. This could be a play on the Three Wise Monkeys. The Three Wise Monkeys are a Japanese folk representation of the proverb "see no evil, hear no evil, speak no evil." They symbolize prudence and purity, and are often seen as a call for discretion or willful ignorance. The three monkeys are Mizaru (see no evil), Kikazaru (hear no evil), and Iwazaru (speak no evil). The concept originates in Japan, with statues of the monkeys found in temples and shrines. The proverb "see no evil, hear no evil, speak no evil" is often interpreted as a way to avoid negative experiences or situations by choosing to ignore them. In Japan, the monkeys are seen as attendants of Koshin, the God of Roads, and their message is used to teach prudence and purity. In the West, the proverb is often used sarcastically to criticize those who deliberately ignore wrongdoing. The idea of avoiding what is contrary to propriety is rooted in Confucius's teachings. Some representations include a fourth monkey, Sezaru, who "does no evil".

Chapter 29

  • Nietzsche’s frog is a way to metaphorically understand perspective and belief. Nietzsche believed that all knowledge and values are perspectival, meaning they are shaped by our individual experiences and values. This is reflected in the idea of a "frog's perspective," which can be understood as a belief system that is constantly changing and shifting. The term "frog's perspective" can also be associated with the idea of narrow-mindedness or being unable to see the bigger picture. Another metaphorical expression, "swallowing the frog" can refer to confronting unpleasant realities or difficult emotions in order to grow or achieve a higher level of understanding. This aligns with Nietzsche's emphasis on confronting difficult truths and embracing the "unpleasantness" of life. The frog can also symbolize a sense of "being in the muck," referring to the lower aspects of oneself that need to be overcome in order to reach a higher level of awareness. Frogs appear symbolically connected to themes of life, death, transformation, and communication beyond ordinary human experience. Mr. Spitzer encounters a mute man who uses a frog as a musical instrument, training it to croak and even mimic human speech. This mute and his frog symbolize profound voicelessness and the struggle to communicate in a world that is largely silent or deaf to certain truths. The frog’s croaking becomes an emblem of fragile, ephemeral voice amid silence, reflecting the broader motifs of mortality, loss, and elusive knowledge that permeate the novel. The frog also represents vulnerability and the liminal state between life and death. Its death mourned by Mr. Spitzer as a loss greater than many others. The mute’s deep grief over the frog's death underscores a shared human sorrow for lost expression and connection, heightening the novel’s meditation on mortality and the persistence of memory or spirit beyond physical life. Nietzsche’s frog metaphor, often interpreted as symbolizing awakening or transformation, where the frog must endure discomfort to emerge anew, parallels the frog’s role in the novel as a creature caught between states (mute yet singing, alive yet dying). The frog’s croaking in the mute’s mouth, its death, and burial evoke themes of metamorphosis, suffering, and the fragile boundary between existence and oblivion, which are central to Nietzschean philosophy of overcoming and transformation. It relates symbolically to liminality, transformation, and the struggle for voice and meaning amid silence and death. This aligns conceptually with Nietzsche’s frog as a figure of existential change and awakening, though Young’s usage is more focused on fragility, loss, and the ineffable aspects of human experience. The frog in the novel is a potent symbol of voicelessness, transformation, and mortality, echoing themes akin to Nietzsche’s idea of the frog as an emblem of awakening and existential transition.

Chapter 51

  • “Seldom did he meet with anyone who did not suffer from some form of complex illusion beyond necessity” The paragraph begins by stating that most people suffer from "some form of complex illusion beyond necessity," suggesting a fundamental questioning of what constitutes reality. The examples of the old woman believing she's a birdcage and the piano tuner declaring foreheads "hollow" highlight the subjective and often distorted nature of individual perceptions. The recurring motif of blindness serves as a powerful metaphor for the limitations of human perception. The blind portrait painter raises questions about the nature of art and representation: Can true beauty or understanding be captured without sight? The reference to a blind war veteran visiting art galleries with a seeing-eye dog further emphasizes the limitations and interpretations of reality. The idea of deaf music critics "hearing" errors without actually hearing the music is paradoxical. It suggests that judgment and understanding can be based on something other than direct sensory experience, perhaps on intellectual analysis, intuition, or an awareness of structure and form. This challenges the conventional notion of sensory experience as the primary source of knowledge. Mr. Spitzer reflects that there might be "only one great dream informing all dreamers and all dreams." This concept aligns with philosophical ideas about a collective unconscious or a universal mind, where individual experiences are interconnected and share a common source. This also questions the nature of reality as an individual or shared experience. The paragraph questions whether time even matters when considering happiness. Is happiness something that exists outside of time, or is it bound by its constraints? The narrative suggests happiness is fleeting, like a snowflake. Time is further explored through the contrast between Mr. Spitzer and his brother. Mr. Spitzer's life has been so long that he can't remember certain things, while his brother's life was cut short. This contrast highlights the different ways in which time can be experienced and how it shapes our understanding of life and death. The observation that "three minutes of happiness seemed perhaps all there might be or less than there had been" in most lives suggests a pessimistic view of human existence. However, it also implies that happiness is a rare and precious commodity, to be cherished when it is found. The paragraph concludes by stating that "the greatest happiness might come through the sorrow of waiting, of postponement." This paradoxical statement suggests that the anticipation of happiness, or even the acceptance of sorrow, can be a source of fulfillment in itself. It also hints at the idea that happiness and sorrow are intertwined, and that one cannot exist without the other. The paragraph also touches on Plato's Theory of Forms. Plato believed that the world we perceive is merely a shadow of a higher realm of perfect Forms. The blind individuals, the deaf critics, and the deluded people Mr. Spitzer encounters highlight the imperfect and distorted nature of our sensory experiences. The idea that all dreamers share one great dream can be seen as a reflection of Plato's concept of the Form of the Good. This Form is the ultimate source of truth and knowledge, and it is what all individuals are striving to reach. The shared dream, therefore, could be interpreted as a connection to this Form. The paragraph reflects existentialist themes, particularly the absurdity of existence and the search for meaning in a meaningless world. The characters' illusions, their distorted perceptions, and the fleeting nature of happiness all contribute to a sense of the inherent absurdity of human life. Despite the seeming meaninglessness of existence, the paragraph also implies a sense of individual responsibility. Mr. Spitzer, as a lawyer dealing with the affairs of the dead, is tasked with making sense of their lives and legacies. This responsibility highlights the importance of individual choices and actions, even in the face of an indifferent universe.

Chapter 81

  • Ludwig van Beethoven was a German composer and pianist. One of the most revered figures in the history of Western music, his works rank among the most performed of the classical music repertoire and span the transition from the Classical to the Romantic era. Beethoven's early period, during which he forged his craft, is typically considered to have lasted until 1802. From 1802 to around 1812, his middle period showed an individual development from the styles of Joseph Haydn and Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart, and is sometimes characterised as heroic. During this time, Beethoven began to grow increasingly deaf. In his late period, from 1812 to 1827, he extended his innovations in musical form and expression.

Chapter 82

  • The final chapter marks a significant shift in Vera's narrative. After recounting the stories of Esther Longtree, Miss MacIntosh, Mr. Spitzer and her mother, Vera turns her attention to her own experiences and a newfound love with a stone deaf man. This signals a move towards personal redemption and finding meaning amidst the chaos and illusions that permeate the novel.

Chapter 82

  • Vera's relationship with the stone deaf man highlights the theme of communication beyond conventional means. Since he cannot hear, their connection relies on visual cues, touch, and unspoken understanding. This echoes the novel's broader exploration of how people connect despite their individual limitations and the illusory nature of reality.

Chapter 82

  • The stone deaf man's perspective offers a unique insight into the world of silence. He claims that all the waters of creation had smashed against his ears, leaving him stone deaf. He is bombarded by loud noises in his mind, but it is a silent world. Silence allows Vera and her lover to connect on a deeper level, transcending the superficiality of spoken words. The silence also seems to be a way for him to be closer to death.

Chapter 82

  • The stone deaf man's experiences also emphasize the importance of imagination. Having lost his hearing at fourteen, he lives in a world where sounds are memories or creations of his mind. This reinforces the novel's recurring motif of the power of imagination to shape reality and find beauty in the face of adversity.

Chapter 82

  • The relationship between Vera and the stone deaf man shows an acceptance for one another. He accepts that she can hear and she accepts that he cannot. This can be compared to the acceptance that Miss MacIntosh has for death and Vera has for her mother's opium addiction.

Chapter 82

Chapter 82

Chapter 82
