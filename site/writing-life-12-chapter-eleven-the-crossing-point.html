Chapter Eleven: The Crossing Point

← Chapter Ten: The Sacred Is Here · Radical Immanence Across Marguerite Young's Writing Life · Conclusion →

Pillar: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point · Across every book

Characters: Esther Longtree · Miss MacIntosh

Every pillar stands vertical. Every pillar holds a horizontal load. The architecture of the ten pillars is already, in its form, a crossing point: the vertical supports bearing the horizontal weight of what the system carries. What has not yet been named is the intersection itself. The place where the two axes meet. The place where you are already standing.

That place is the eleventh pillar. It is not added to the ten. It is found inside them, at the center of the architecture, where the weight crosses.

It has been there the whole time.

Every one of the ten pillars describes the self on a lateral axis. The plural self spreads across multiple registers simultaneously. The sedimentary time presses down in layers, accumulating horizontally beneath the present moment. The permanent grief moves through the atmospheric conditions of a life, spreading rather than resolving. Even the recursive construction, which might seem to involve a vertical dimension, is described as a return to the beginning on the same level, a circling back rather than a descent or an ascent.

The horizontal axis is the axis of accumulation. It is the axis on which you can see the full spread of what a self contains: the multiple registers of the plural self, the layered sediment of time, the wide atmospheric spread of grief. It is the axis that the ten pillars map with extraordinary precision.

What the horizontal axis cannot see, by definition, is the vertical.

The vertical axis is the axis of transmission. It is the line that runs through the crossing point from what was given to what will be required. It is the line that connects the ancestors in the sediment to the not-yet-born in the not-yet-formed ground. It is the line that makes obligation possible, not as a chosen commitment but as a structural fact: you are constituted by what was deposited before you arrived, and you are constituting what will be deposited after you leave. The vertical axis is not something you can opt into or out of. It is the dimension you are already inside.

The ten pillars describe what you are. The eleventh pillar describes what you are inside of.

To be a carrier is not to be a vehicle. A vehicle is a neutral medium. The thing carried passes through it unchanged. What you carry, you change, and what changes you, you carry forward transformed. The transmission is not neutral. It is not a pipeline. It is a process of sedimentation, and you are both the sediment and the thing being deposited, both the weight pressing down and the layer being formed.

There is a word in geology for what happens when material is carried by a moving medium, water or wind or ice, and deposited somewhere else when the medium slows. The word is alluvial. Alluvial material is material that has been carried and deposited. It is not where it started. It is not the medium that carried it. It is the record of a transmission: the evidence that something moved, was held, was released, came to rest in a new configuration.

The self is alluvial.

This is not a metaphor, or it is not only a metaphor. It is a description of the actual process by which selves are constituted. You are made of what was carried to you and deposited in you by the moving medium of time and relation. The language you think in was carried to you by people who are dead. The categories through which you organize experience were carried to you by philosophical and cultural traditions that stretch back further than any individual life. The specific grief you carry was deposited in you by specific losses that were themselves deposited by earlier losses, the grief of your parents and their parents, the grief of the people who stood on the ground before you stood on it, all of it alluvial, all of it carried and deposited, none of it where it started, all of it still itself in a new configuration.

The transmission model is not a longer road. It is a different geometry entirely.

On the transmission model, the past is not behind you. It is beneath you, in the sediment, pressing up through the soles of your feet. The future is not ahead of you. It is above you, in the not-yet-formed layers that will be pressed down by the weight of what you deposit. And the present is not a moving edge. It is a crossing point: the place where the vertical line of transmission intersects the horizontal spread of the living moment, the place where what has been deposited meets what is being deposited, the place where the alluvial self stands in the middle of the carrying.

There is a specific experience that the eleventh pillar is trying to name, and it is an experience that most people have had and very few people have a philosophical vocabulary for.

It happens at the moment of becoming a parent, or at the moment of losing one. It happens at the moment of standing at a grave or at a birth or at the bedside of someone who is dying. It happens sometimes in the middle of an ordinary afternoon when something, a smell or a quality of light or the weight of a silence, suddenly makes visible the line running through you from before you were born to after you will be gone. It is the experience of the rotation: the moment when the horizontal axis, the lateral spread of the present moment and its accumulations, recedes briefly, and the vertical line becomes palpable.

The philosophical vocabulary for this experience has largely been the vocabulary of the sublime: the overwhelming, the transcendent, the experience that exceeds the capacity of ordinary consciousness to process. But the sublime is the wrong category. The sublime is a response to scale, to the experience of being dwarfed by something larger than yourself. What the rotation produces is not the experience of being dwarfed. It is the experience of being located: the specific, precise, almost physical sensation of knowing where you are in the transmission, of feeling the weight of what is beneath you and the weight of what is above you, of being the crossing point.

The crossing point is not large. It is exact. It is the most precisely located thing there is: the intersection of this specific accumulation with this specific line of transmission, here, now, in this body, in this moment. The rotation does not produce awe at scale. It produces something more like recognition: the sensation of finally seeing the structure you have always been inside.

Grief is one of the most reliable routes to this recognition. The third pillar of Radical Immanence establishes that grief is permanent weather, the atmospheric condition of a world where everything you love is always already changing. The eleventh pillar adds the vertical dimension: grief is also the experience of the transmission line becoming palpable. When you lose someone who was part of your constitution, someone whose deposit is in your sediment, someone who was carrying part of what you carry, you feel the vertical line. You feel the weight that was being held by another person and is now being held by you alone. You feel the transmission.

This is why grief is not only weather. It is also a form of geological education. It teaches you, in a way that nothing else teaches you with the same precision, what you are made of and what you are carrying. It teaches you the crossing point by making the vertical line temporarily visible in a way it is not usually visible through the horizontal spread of ordinary accumulation.

The turn is not comfortable. The rotation that moves you from the horizontal axis to the vertical is the same rotation that the figure at the bottom of the Wheel of Fortune is experiencing: the stripping away of the lateral accumulation, the exposure of the vertical line, the discovery that the crossing point was always there and that the horizontal spread was always resting on it.

But the turn is not only loss. It is also expansion, of a specific kind that is easy to miss because it does not look like expansion from the horizontal axis. From the horizontal axis, the rotation looks like reduction: you lose the lateral spread, the accumulation, the wide extension of the self in its social and relational world. From the crossing point, the rotation reveals that you were always inside something larger than the lateral spread. You were always on the transmission line. You were always constituted by what was deposited before you arrived and constituting what will be deposited after you leave. The expansion is not into a larger space. It is into a deeper understanding of the space you were already in.

Here is not only here. Here is also the sediment beneath here, and the not-yet-formed ground above here, and the transmission line running through here from the ancestors to the not-yet-born, and the crossing point where all of that meets in this body, in this moment, in this specific configuration of alluvial material that is what the self actually is.

Young knew about the vertical. Young's sentences are crossing points. They are the places where the horizontal spread of the plural self meets the vertical line of transmission, where the accumulation of the present moment is intersected by the weight of what came before and the weight of what will come after. The sentences that circle back and refuse to finish are sentences that are trying to hold both axes at once, to stay in the horizontal spread of the present moment while feeling the pull of the vertical line.

The crossing point does not turn. The wheel turns around it. The discovery is not that fortune is unstable. The discovery is that the crossing point is stable. Not stable in the sense of permanent or unchanging, but stable in the sense of being the axis rather than the rim: the thing that holds while everything else rotates. The wheel is not a description of the instability of fortune. It is a description of the rotation between two axes.

Before writing, before organized religion, before the wheel and the plow and the organized political state, human beings were making a specific mark on the surfaces available to them. It appears in the cave paintings of the Upper Paleolithic. It appears in the earliest known writing systems. It appears in the Neolithic petroglyphs of every inhabited continent. It appears in the cosmological systems of cultures that had no contact with each other and no shared tradition of any kind.

It is the simplest possible mark that contains two dimensions. It is two lines crossing.

Western Christianity made the cross into a specific theological symbol, and that specificity has made it difficult for secular philosophy to think clearly about what the cross was before it was a theological symbol, and what it continues to be underneath the theology. But the cross is older than Christianity. It is older than any of the religious traditions that have claimed it. It is the mark that human beings make when they are trying to represent the structure they are inside: two axes, their intersection, the crossing point.

The cross is a symbol of the crossing point: the structure of existence that becomes visible when you stop moving along one axis long enough to feel the other. Every religious tradition that has made the cross central has been responding to this recognition, though most have translated it into the language of transcendence: the vertical arm points upward to God, to heaven, to the beyond. The eleventh pillar reads the vertical arm differently. It points downward as much as upward. It points into the sediment as much as into the sky. It is the transmission line, not the transcendence line, and the distinction illuminates the philosophical difference.

The fourth pillar of Radical Immanence establishes the witness as the central ethical stance of the system: the one who stays in the room with the contradiction and does not flinch and does not conclude. The witness maintains the quality of attention that the thing in front of them deserves, the attention that does not look away when the looking becomes painful and does not rush toward resolution when the contradiction becomes uncomfortable.

The eleventh pillar extends this into the vertical dimension.

The intergenerational witness is the one who stays in the room with the transmission and does not flinch and does not conclude. The room is larger than any single life can contain. The thing in front of the intergenerational witness is not only the present contradiction but the full vertical span of what was given and what will be required, the full weight of the sediment beneath and the not-yet-formed ground above.

What does it mean to witness the ancestors? Not to honor them in the sense of performing reverence for what they did. Not to judge them in the sense of applying present moral standards to past actions. To witness them: to read the sediment they deposited with the full attention it deserves, to not flatten what they carried into a more useful shape, to acknowledge the weight of what was given without pretending it was lighter or heavier than it was.

The honest witnessing of ancestors is one of the most politically demanding acts available. It requires acknowledging what was deposited in the sediment that the present stands on, including the deposits of violence and extraction and exclusion that the present political arrangement has been built on top of and has largely preferred not to read. The sediment is not only the deposit of wisdom and beauty and genuine human achievement. It is also the deposit of the long con: the centuries of systematic exclusion, the theft of land and labor, the construction of hierarchies that were natural in the sense of being deposited so deep in the sediment that they feel like bedrock rather than alluvium.

The intergenerational witness reads the sediment honestly. It does not require that the reading produce guilt as a permanent condition. It requires that the reading produce acknowledgment: the honest recognition that the present is built on the accumulated past, that the past is not behind us but beneath us, and that building justly requires attending to what is in the substrate rather than pretending the ground is neutral.

The hardest philosophical problem the eleventh pillar creates is also the most important one: how do you ground intergenerational obligation without transcendence?

The eleventh pillar cannot use any of these grounds. It is committed to radical immanence: the sacred without the beyond, the ethical without the transcendent anchor, the obligation that is real without being grounded in anything outside the world it operates in.

The ground the eleventh pillar offers is structural rather than moral. The obligation to future generations does not arise from a moral principle that transcends the present moment. It arises from the fact of being a crossing point: from being constituted by what was deposited before you arrived, from being a carrier of what will constitute the ground that comes after. The obligation is not chosen. It is constitutional. It is in the structure of what you are.

The crossing point is not outside the obligation. The crossing point is constituted by the transmission that generates the obligation. You cannot step outside the obligation any more than you can step outside the sediment you are standing on. The obligation is not something that applies to you from outside. It is something you are made of.

This does not mean the obligation is unlimited. The crossing point is finite. The life that stands at the crossing point is sixty, seventy, eighty or ninety years against the age of what surrounds it. The obligation is not to carry everything. It is to carry honestly: to acknowledge the weight, to not flatten it into a more useful shape, to pass it forward with the full recognition of what it is and where it came from.

The crossing point does not resolve the tension between the finite self and the infinite transmission. It holds the tension. That is what crossing points do. They hold the intersection of what cannot be reconciled into a single axis, the horizontal and the vertical, the finite and the geological, the present moment and coming generations. The obligation is to hold that intersection honestly, without pretending it is lighter than it is, without pretending the ground is neutral, without pretending you are not already inside the transmission.

Here is enough. The tenth pillar of Radical Immanence establishes this: the sacred without the beyond, the ordinary world charged with a significance that points not outward to a transcendent ground but deeper into the immanent one. Here is enough because here holds all of it, the plural self and the constructed real and the permanent grief and the witness and the recursive construction and the incompletion and the marginal and the sedimentary time and the honest language and the immanent sacred.

Here is also enormous. The eleventh pillar adds this: here is not only the lateral spread of the present moment, the horizontal accumulation of the ten pillars. Here is also the vertical line of the transmission, the crossing point where the ancestors in the sediment meet the not-yet-born in the not-yet-formed ground. Here is the intersection of everything that was given and everything that will be required. Here is the crossing point.

Here is enough. Here is also enormous. Here is the crossing point.

That is the eleventh pillar. That is what Young's sentence was always reaching toward. That is what the wheel was always turning around. That is what the cross was always marking.

Phase One, Childhood and the Early Poems (1908 to 1934)

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

"Minnows and a Monster" (1934) is the emblem. The speaker casts "delicate meshes" for minnows on a stream and catches "this enigma gleaming," which draws "the boatman under / The unfurled dark! I know not; / I sink with him in wonder." A small, located act opens onto something vast. Young read this poem as her own fate in 1989: the fisherman who "went out with a net to catch minnows and caught a whale."

Complication: the poem ends in wonder, which is close to the awe the pillar sets aside, rather than recognition.

Phase Two, The Chicago Years, Prismatic Ground, and the Graduate Essays (1935 to 1937)

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The evidence is thin. "Awakening" (1937) has a body "Bearing the mark of many whips" that "Was changed in a single night." The nightly reading to Minna is the closest biographical case: one exact act, repeated, becoming a passage into another world.

Phase Three, The Philosophical Poems (1938 to 1945)

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

"Slow Motion" (1941) holds the instant "with a fabulous precision." "The Funeral" (1941) gives recognition rather than awe: the farmer tethers his horse "as he had always done," "it all seemed simple, ample, and profound," and then "earth was turning on another course. / I felt its turn." "The Contravalid" names "One heartbeat separating each life and total death." "That Cloud" names the "hard jewel of consciousness," and a draft finds "a public destiny / Even in the most delicate event." Transmission appears in "Death Bed of Beauty": Beauty dies, and her sister's children are "Beauty's, her children, / Her unburied eyes." "Wild Yam Fruit" gives the located sign without its line: "Having no vine I saw, it fell like snow."

Complication: the scale is cosmic: Uranus, Saturn, Sirius, Aldebaran, and "Earth is the loneliest voyager."

Phase Four, "Angel in the Forest" and “Complex Life and Complex Letters” (1945 to 1946)

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The book ends on the miller's parable. No two millers make the same flour "even though they be father and son." The flour "bears the impression of the miller's thumb and the miller's soul," a difference "so slight that nobody can name it, nobody can catch hold of it. That is life." The located sign of a body and its failed passage down a line become the book's last philosophical statement.

Phase Five, "Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" (written from the mid 1940s, published 1965)

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Esther says: "I do not know the night in which I conceived as I do not know the night in which I was conceived." The most exact moment of transmission is unlocatable. Miss MacIntosh asks: "If you have seen me every day and do not know me now, how will you know me in the future?" That is recognition of what was always there. When she removes her wig, life delivers "a cold surprise."

Complication: Mr. Spitzer's single note becomes "two notes or one and its echo." Shaviro calls the book "an inverted circle whose circumference is everywhere, and whose center is nowhere."

In the novel it is present. Its strongest image waits for Young’s last book.

Phase Six, The Occasional Prose, Interviews, and Self-Interpretation (1940s to 1995)

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The flour miller is "the thing I never could write," "the idea of identity and passage of the soul." The violinist plays one true note after twenty years: "you hit reality once and die." To Terkel: "you frighten me by being so absolutely right." Her method was exact: one small street error in all of Boston, two weeks on a sentence. In 1989: art "exists not in objective time alone but in the subjectivity of time," and she was "still in the process of awakening."

Complication: she wanted "a universal or cosmic statement," a book "endlessly expanding like a universe."

Phase Seven, "Harp Song for a Radical" (written in her last decades, published 1999)

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Dostoyevsky kneels hooded in the snow, thinking "I might perhaps have five minutes more to live," at "the evermoving boundary line between sleeping and dreaming." Young ties the moment to Debs "recording the deaths." Transmission works here, unlike with the miller's son: the dream "never really dies as some other dreamer must take over the dream." Debs learns French poetry from his father at three, and his names come from Sue and Hugo.
