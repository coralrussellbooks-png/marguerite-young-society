Chapter 24: After All, How Else Should They Keep Their Jobs?

← Chapter 23 · Guide Home · Chapter 25 →

Research notes: Chapter 24

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos

Symbols: The Sea and Water

Philosophy: Augustine and Christian Theology · Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Ancient Egypt · Genesis · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · Napoleon and Wellington · Shakespeare · Women's Suffrage

Chapter 24 marks a shift. After nine chapters in the intense, close world of the birthday night and its aftermath, Young pulls back the camera. We are still at the breakfast table, still in the morning after, but now Vera’s mind moves away from Miss MacIntosh and out into the house, into the servants’ quarters, into Catherine’s bedroom, into the vast social machinery of imaginary guests and disconnected telephone lines.

Think of this chapter as a wide establishing shot after a long series of close-ups. It shows you the full scale of the world Vera has been living in. And it ends with Vera asking, quietly and without an answer, who on earth she is supposed to turn to.

What actually happens in Chapter 24

Vera’s mind, sitting at the breakfast table with Miss MacIntosh, moves through the household. She thinks about the servants, who have absorbed Catherine’s dream world so completely that they argue about whether it is Roman in the morning and Greek in the afternoon, polish shoes left out by imaginary guests, wait on Wellington, believe they live in forests of cuckoo clocks, drive Hannibal’s elephants over the Alps.

She thinks about Catherine: the horizontal lady who will “die in the midst of her dreams,” who discusses her own obsequies endlessly with absent social secretaries, who calls Athens on a telephone connected only to the servants’ quarters and gets Rome, who has been trying for years to reach Cousin Hannah on a number that was disconnected long ago.

She thinks about Mr. Spitzer, who is coming again in the evening, “filled with his usual euphemistic certainties” about a Heaven that will unite us all, and utterly uncertain about this earth.

The chapter ends with Vera’s question: to whom should she turn, asking the personal questions that burn in her “mind, questions as to the secret purpose of life,” love, marriage, reproduction, death? Her imagination wanders “like a dark and formless cloud, seeking” a certitude it might never have.

The servants’ sea change

Young has mentioned the servants throughout the novel, but Chapter 24 gives us our fullest portrait of what Catherine’s household does to ordinary people over time. “After an hour or a year or many years in this employment,” even the most sensible servants undergo what Young calls a sea change. They reach for door knobs that are not door knobs. They open doors that are not doors. They “stagger to and fro chasing illusions like butterflies.”

And yet (and Young gives this its own line, which stops you) after all, “how else should they keep their jobs?”

The servants have adapted. They have entered into Catherine’s spirit because that is the only way to function in this household. The one who polishes imaginary guests’ shoes, the valet who waits on Wellington, the housemaid who speaks only to herself and is several fine ladies simultaneously; these are not mad people. They are people who have found the only workable response to an impossible situation: go along, elaborate, make yourself useful to the dream.

Young is generous about this. She does not mock the servants. She presents their adaptation as logical, even wise. The alternative (refusing the dream, insisting on the real) is Miss MacIntosh’s approach, and we know what that cost her. The servants chose differently. Neither choice is free.

Catherine’s telephone

The image of Catherine as an entire telephone exchange, calling dead numbers and reaching wrong parties, is one of the novel’s great comic inventions and also one of its most moving.

She calls Athens and gets Rome. She calls the Bishop of Hippo and he has just stepped out. She calls the Emperor Hadrian and he has gone into his garden. King Canute is on a long voyage. The Lowells and the Saltonstalls never seem to be home. Cousin Hannah’s number was disconnected long ago. Uncle Ronald is dead. Uncle Bernard died in Moscow carrying a message to the czar.

Her telephone, it turns out, is connected only to the servants’ quarters. She never guesses this. She thinks she is placing transatlantic calls, “calls ship to shore, star to star, these calls from the living to the dead.” She is leaving messages with the second butler who believes he is the Great Emir.

The gap between what Catherine believes she is doing and what she is actually doing is the gap the novel is organized around. She is in her room. She is talking to the servants. She believes she is in communication with the dead, the famous, the cosmic. Young does not decide which of these is more true. The Bishop of Hippo always just stepped out. He may be just around the corner.

Cousin Hannah

This is the first time Cousin Hannah’s name appears in the novel, and Young drops it so quietly that you might miss it. Catherine has been trying to reach her on a telephone number that was disconnected long ago. Why had Cousin Hannah never called?

Cousin Hannah is one of the novel’s major figures: the Boston feminist, the balloonist, the mountain climber, the militant suffragette known through the seraglios of the East as Al Hamad. She will have her own chapters later, and she is one of Young’s most extraordinary characters. But she arrives here first as an absence: a disconnected number, a name Catherine reaches for and cannot find.

Notice it. She will arrive in full later. For now she is a name in a list of unreachable people, and that is where Young wants her to begin: among the dead and the missing and the ones who have sailed away.

To whom should I have turned?

The chapter’s final question is the one that closes the entire Miss MacIntosh sequence. Vera has spent nine chapters living through the revelation and its aftermath, learning something enormous about the one person she trusted to be solid and ordinary and real. And now, at the breakfast table in the morning sunlight, she asks: who is left?

Not Catherine, who places “her faith only in the unreal.” Not the servants, who have adapted to the dream. Not Mr. Spitzer, who is so unsure of himself that he cannot remember his own memories as well as strangers’, whose evasions exceed even his own understanding. And not, anymore, Miss MacIntosh in the way Vera once understood her, though she is right there, “coarse-grained and ruddy, her red hair gleaming like another light.”

Vera’s imagination wanders “like a dark and formless cloud, seeking” a certitude it might never have. Young leaves her there, wandering. The breakfast is over. The novel is about to move on. Vera will keep wandering (across cities, across years, across eight hundred more pages) looking for Miss MacIntosh in What Cheer, Iowa, and finding, instead, the whole of America.

What to watch for as you keep reading

The novel is about to open dramatically. Chapter 25 and beyond will introduce new characters and new storylines, and you will sometimes feel as if you have lost the thread entirely. You have not. Young is building what Anaïs Nin described as spokes of a wheel: every digression, every new character, every detour leads back to the same center, which is the question Vera cannot stop asking: what is real, what is illusion, and what do we do when the only person who taught us the difference turns out to have been something other than we thought?

Catherine’s phrase for Mr. Spitzer, “a roundhouse of the trains which run no more,” is one of the novel’s great compressed portraits of a character. Mr. Spitzer as a building designed to house trains that no longer run, a structure whose purpose has been overtaken by time, whose tracks are crossed in eternal darkness, whose signal lights show red where they should show green. It is tender and precise and entirely in Catherine’s voice.

The gardener who believes he is the spouse to Potiphar is a detail worth holding. In the book of Genesis, Potiphar is the Egyptian captain whose wife falsely accuses Joseph of assault: a story about betrayal, false witness, and the person who tells the wrong story about another. Young places one of the servants inside that story without comment. In this household, everyone is inside a story. The question is always which one.
