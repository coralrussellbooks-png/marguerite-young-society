Movement I. Diminished Heaven: The Sacred in the Small

← The Poems in Movements · Exploring Young's Poetry · Movement II. Dubious Ground: The Constructed and Uncertain R →

Characters: Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer

The oldest promise is that the meaning of a life waits somewhere above the life, in a heaven, a hereafter, a reward for the patient. These poems refuse that promise and put the sacred back where the body actually stands, in the grass and the weather and the smallest living things. This is the movement that shows the vision was native to Young rather than arrived at, since the refusal is already complete in the earliest work. "Worship," written young, will not bow in "church" or "chapel" and finds the divine only "In stars and flowers and shining eyes," and "I Heard a Bird," written at nine, gives its whole heart to one small creature and its nest. From there the principle holds for the next forty years. "Sky of Grasses" shrinks heaven to the sound of a seed pod, "Fall of Man" lets the angels down into a field of clover, "Wild Orchard" names the human being "the errant vegetable," and her very last poem, "Elegy," gives the vision the closing word: "our God is not the God of Weight / But ourselves." The grief in these poems is real, since bringing heaven down to earth means giving up the consolation of a beyond. What is gained is the world itself, held as holy. This is the ground Angel in the Forest, watching a promised paradise sink back into Indiana soil.

"Worship"

The poem refuses God in "church" and "chapel" and finds the divine instead "In stars and flowers and shining eyes, / In dewdrops and in pearls." This is The Sacred Is Here stated as a young person's creed, immanence chosen over the institution before Young could have named it that. It belongs at the head of the movement as proof that she located the holy in the actual world from the beginning.

"Twilight"

The mind's "intrepid eagle" would soar "Toward the sun's red streaming" against oblivion, but "the eagle turns, he veers" and "This lesser dark descends." The upward reach toward transcendence fails and the poem settles back into the actual dark. The refused ascent is The Sacred Is Here's discipline, keeping the self on the ground the eagle could not leave.

"I Heard a Bird"

Written at nine, and already the vision is here in miniature: a bird sings of its own small life, and "the nest was put into a museum," kept but perhaps already taken. The poem gives its whole attention to one ordinary creature and what remains of it, which is The Sacred Is Here before Young had any theory for it. That a child reached for exactly this subject is the first evidence that the ground under her work was there from the start.

"Street Fires"

Old women sweep and burn autumn leaves so "That Beauty may die, they light their fires," an ordinary chore made a small rite of loss. The Sacred Is Here here is the ceremony hidden inside a domestic task, beauty consumed and consecrated in one motion. Meaning is in the raking and the smoke, not above them.

"Sonnet"

A woman buys "thin painted china when she needed bread," prizing the beautiful and useless though the world "laughed at loving this so old." The poem holds that such things are worth more than the practical, which is The Sacred Is Here as a way of valuing, and it edges toward The Margins Reveal the Truth in the mockery she draws for it.

"Brave Youth"

A vain young fowl is plucked and eaten, "reduced to food / For hunger," youth served up as ordinary meat. The humbling is the reverence, as in "Wild Orchard": the strutting human is one more mortal creature, and the poem plucks his feathers "with apology."

"Eclipse"

When darkness falls on the sun, "The lizard and my spotted soul / Kept their counsel well, / Remembering the sun." The speaker's soul is kin to the lizard, the ordinary creature, both keeping faith with the plain light rather than any beyond. Immanence is a loyalty to the sun that is actually there.

"Proud Lady"

"She was vain / Of curious things, / Not her bright hair / Nor ruby rings. / Of orioles / And sky and cloud / Of such she was / Absurdly proud." It is the seed of "Proudest Lady" from Prismatic Ground, the same pride in the unownable, reworked years later. Set side by side, the two show The Sacred Is Here as a lifelong habit rather than a late idea, and pairing them makes the point without a word of commentary.

"Sky of Grasses."

A body passes as lightly as dew, and heaven shrinks until it is no bigger than a seed pod's sound. This is The Sacred Is Here at its purest: the holy is not overhead but underfoot, in the grass and the glow-worms that see the dying woman off. It is the exact move Angel in the Forest makes across 435 pages, watching a promised heaven come to rest in ordinary Indiana ground.

"Fall of Man."

The angels do not crash. They come down softly as sparrows that dropped from frozen nests, the celestial folding quietly into the earthly. The Sacred Is Here works here as a descent that is also a homecoming, the divine consenting to be ground-level, and what theology calls a catastrophe the poem makes a settling.

"All Saints' Day."

After death the human essence does not rise. It returns, the "fibrous hearts" unspun into leaves on a wild cherry branch. Sainthood, in this poem, is what the body becomes when it rejoins the orchard. The sacred is the compost, not the cloud.

"Wild Orchard."

Calling a man "the errant vegetable" strips the species of its claim to stand above the growing world. The humbling is the reverence: The Sacred Is Here insists that a human is one more temporary green thing, mortal and rooted, and that this is not a demotion but the truth.

"Wild Deer."

A garden pulls the wild deer away from "the village streets and laughter," making a small cultivated plot into a meeting place between the tame and the untamed. The sacred gathers at the edge of an ordinary garden and needs no altar. Meaning is local, and it grazes.

"Strange Prophecy."

Only bees keep company with "the portals of the dead," small workers tending a threshold the living avoid. Whatever survives death is located not in a heaven but in the hum of insects at the grave. It is the seed-pod scale of "Sky of Grasses" turned toward what lingers.

"Rosary in Discord."

The poem opens in prayer and then lets the sacred tone break against discord, refusing the clean devotional resolution. What is holy here has to be said inside noise and disorder, not above them. The Sacred Is Here keeps its reverence and gives up its calm.

"Paradise Is Still Lost."

Angels are "obscured in gaslight faces," the divine dimmed and cheapened by the Club Ha-Ha and the Dime. Paradise stays lost because it was always sought in the wrong direction, upward and elsewhere, when the only ground available is here. The title rhymes on purpose with Angel in the Forest's chapter "Paradise Was Lost," where two real utopias sink back into the soil that outlasts them.

"The Angels."

The poem hunts for where angels might still live, finds only remote atolls and the names of vanishing birds, and concludes that "death is the flight of angel and golden plover." The sacred has retreated into the last wild habitats and the fact of extinction. Heaven is a bird sanctuary, and it is going.

"Noah's Ark."

The ark becomes body and soul at once, "a prison of purple wood" riding a drowned world, carrying life across catastrophe toward a ground it cannot yet see. The poem holds The Sacred Is Here and the long reach of the Crossing Point together, creation as a cargo handed forward through the flood. It is one of the poems that will not sit in a single movement, and that doubling is the point rather than a flaw.

"Winter Scene."

The earth leans "toward the one invisible, the prince of space," and the poem lets that pull be "disproved," keeping belief and the plain cold world in tension. The mystical is entertained and then set back down in the snow. Reverence survives here only by staying honest about what cannot be confirmed.

"Awakening."

After crying and "the mark of many whips," the sufferer is covered by "the spider's web of summer," a shroud that heals instead of traps. Nature does the work a heaven is supposed to do, taking the pain into itself. The Sacred Is Here is the world's own capacity to close over a wound.

"Spring."

Birth, growth, and the pressure of death share one season, life arriving already carrying its end. The poem finds the sacred in the ordinary turning of the year rather than in any promise beyond it. In the novel this same current runs through Esther Longtree, forever pregnant and forever losing, the season that cannot complete itself.

"Proudest Lady."

Her vanity is not in "jewelled rings" but in "bright orioles" and "April's opalescent cloud," pride taken in things no one can own. Attention to the small and the passing becomes a kind of devotion. To treasure the overlooked is The Sacred Is Here practiced as character.

"Advertisement."

Refusing "shillings in purses of silk," the poem prizes a rabbit's paw and a crow's call instead, wealth reckoned in charged, useless, living things. Value is pulled back down from the market to the field. What matters was never for sale.

"Country Scene."

An "innocent cow" stands in "bitter salt" and "fields but of ice," the ordinary animal set against a hard, barren world. The poem refuses to prettify the earth and still holds it as the only place meaning lives. The Sacred Is Here includes the cold.

"Lenten Feast."

The "subtle spiritual nourishment" here is made of "pigeon shadows" and song sparrows, a communion taken from the passing natural world. The sacrament is birds. In the novel Mr. Spitzer mourns Martha, the last passenger pigeon, and the poem's feast is lit by the same grief, the holy and the vanishing bound together.

"A Girl's Song."

April stands for renewal and for how quickly renewal passes, youth and beauty already leaving as they arrive. The poem keeps its reverence for the fleeting without wishing it permanent. To love the transient as transient is The Sacred Is Here's discipline.

"To the Strangest Lady."

The gift is a catalog of the discarded: "a freckled rock," "mouse's fur," "snake skins drying in the grass." The poem hands the marginal and the imperfect to its strange addressee as if they were treasures, which under The Sacred Is Here they are. Nothing is too small or too spoiled to be holy.

"Experiment in Azure."

A "feathered breast" and a "moon-washed sea" hold warmth and cold together, life nurtured inside a shifting, fragile element. The poem finds tenderness in the ordinary precariousness of a living thing. Care is sacred precisely because it is uncertain.

"Song."

Body and soul keep returning into each other in a cycle with no exit and no final rest, existence renewing and spending itself. The reverence is for the turning itself, not for any escape from it. Meaning is the loop, held here rather than beyond.

"Elegy"

Her last published poem gathers the vision and gives it the closing word: "our God is not the God of Weight / But ourselves," reality "wild and on the wing," and the final line, "An apple toward the sky is always falling." God is relocated into the self and the world, weight and apple and all, with nothing kept in a beyond. The Sacred Is Here has the last word in the last poem, and the title and its "reproach of distance" keep Grief Doesn't End folded inside it.
