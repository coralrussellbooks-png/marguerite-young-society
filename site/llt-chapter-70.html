Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Mrs. Hogden · Vera Cartwheel

Pillars: Grief Doesn't End

The Questions

A faceless Mrs. Hogden, “though I never saw her,” fills Vera’s dreams. What is a phantom one never sees but cannot escape?

Mrs. Hogden is “a substitute for the true subject of my dreams… Miss MacIntosh.” Why does the mind build a stand-in for the one it has lost?

The phantom is not ethereal but “fat, corporeal, moon-eyed.” What kind of ghost is more solid than the living?

Next door “someone had triumphed, and someone had been subdued.” What does the unseen struggle add to the night?

The chapter turns on a phantom who stands in for the lost. Vera is pursued through her dreams by “a faceless woman, a Mrs. Hogden who, though I never saw her, implored my attention,” and the chapter names what Mrs. Hogden is: “a substitute for the true subject of my dreams, on whom all dreams hinged”, Miss MacIntosh. The mind, having lost its true subject, builds a stand-in, a substitute figure to carry the attention the lost one can no longer receive. This is the construction of a person out of need, the same building the book has shown throughout, now performed as substitution: Vera cannot dream Miss MacIntosh directly, so she dreams a Mrs. Hogden in her place, a made figure standing where the lost one stood.

The substitution is grief’s own architecture. Miss MacIntosh is the subject on whom “all dreams hinged,” the lost center of Vera’s attention, and Mrs. Hogden is what the dreaming mind supplies when it cannot reach that center. This is Grief Doesn't End building rather than resolving: the loss does not close, and the mind does not heal it, but it does construct, throwing up a substitute to hold the place of the missing subject, a phantom to receive the attention that has nowhere to go. The book has shown grief walling itself in, outrunning itself, becoming a man’s material; here it shows grief manufacturing a stand-in, dreaming a substitute because the true subject cannot be dreamed.

And the phantom is more solid than a phantom should be. “If one is pursued by a phantom, a creature of the mind only, then at least the phantom should be ethereal,” but Mrs. Hogden is “fat, corporeal, moon-eyed, not beautiful, waddling in and out of my dreams.” The substitute has more body than the lost original, the made figure more corporeal than the real one it replaces. This is the strange density of the construction: the thing built to stand in for the lost can be more present, more physical, more insistent than what it replaces, so that Vera’s manufactured Mrs. Hogden waddles solidly through the dreams where the true, longed-for Miss MacIntosh will not appear.

The unseen struggle next door sets the frame. “Someone had triumphed, and someone had been subdued in the room next to mine,” a drama of victory and defeat conducted out of sight, known only by its aftermath and its silence. The night is full of what cannot be seen and must be constructed, the struggle next door, the faceless Mrs. Hogden, the substitute for the lost subject, all of them built from sound and absence and need. Vera lies in a world she cannot see and can only make, dreaming a corporeal substitute for a woman she has lost, listening to a triumph and a subduing she will never witness, and the chapter holds her there, among the phantoms her grief and the dark construct.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Mrs. Hogden for a random dream-figure, meaningless night-noise, and the reading that takes the substitution for a healing, the mind moving on from Miss MacIntosh to someone new. Neither the random figure nor the healing is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that the mind builds a substitute for the subject it has lost, dreaming a stand-in because the true subject cannot be dreamed directly, Mrs. Hogden constructed to hold the place of Miss MacIntosh; that this substitution is grief’s architecture, the loss not resolved but manufactured around, a phantom made to receive the attention that has nowhere to go; and that the built substitute can be more corporeal than the lost original, denser and more present than what it replaces. Grief builds substitutes, and the substitute can outweigh the real. Vera dreams a solid Mrs. Hogden for want of the lost Miss MacIntosh, and the chapter leaves her among the phantoms the dark and her grief construct.

For Reflection

Has the mind ever built a substitute, a stand-in figure, for someone it could not reach or had lost?

Why might a manufactured substitute feel more solid and present than the real subject it replaces?

What does grief construct when it cannot heal the loss it carries?
