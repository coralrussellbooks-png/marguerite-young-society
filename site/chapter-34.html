Summary

Catherine recalls being married both in white and in black, with a dog reciting the ceremony in Latin and a St. Bernard giving her the last sacrament. Then she remembers Hannah's presence in her room: iron keys at her belt, a metallic energy, sharp wit, contempt for mortal men, and total indifference to Mr. Spitzer. In Hannah's company Catherine felt both flirtatious and guilty, sensing a love she could not name. Hannah always predicted that Joachim would fail Catherine at the crucial moment, as Peron had by his suicide.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a surreal and haunting depiction of Catherine’s marriage, described as both a wedding and a funeral, veiled in white and black, with multiple conflicting versions of what happened. This passage highlights Catherine’s uncertain, fragmented memory and symbolizes the blending of beginnings and endings, life and death, love and loss. It evokes the mysterious, almost mythical quality of her experience and sets the tone for the chapter’s exploration of elusive reality.
  • Cousin Hannah is portrayed as a powerful, almost otherworldly woman. She challenges Catherine’s fantasies and illusions about love and men, urging her to face the harsh realities of life without illusions. This passage contrasts Cousin Hannah’s stark realism with Catherine’s dreamy, romantic visions and introduces the theme of female independence and existential struggle.
  • The golden cock, which causes Cousin Hannah to dance and emit “showers of metallic sparks,” is a striking symbol associated with awakening and revelation. This passage links to themes of awakening from dreams or illusions and the harsh truths of reality. The cock’s crowing also connects to cultural and geographical references, such as Moroccan courtyards and veiled women, adding layers of meaning about visibility, secrecy, and awakening.
  • Catherine’s desire to learn arithmetic and geometry so she can understand who "the geometer" is (whether sane or insane) symbolizes her quest for order, meaning, and clarity in a chaotic and mysterious world. This passage encapsulates the chapter’s central tension between rationality and imagination, reality and dream, and reflects Catherine’s struggle to make sense of her life and the illusions that surround it.
  • The chapter contains a profound meditation on human existence, describing man as “a nine days’ wonder,” “a falling star,” “a broken sea shell,” emphasizing transience, fragility, and the illusory nature of life. This philosophical reflection deepens the chapter’s existential themes and Catherine’s skepticism about men, love, and reality.
  • Throughout the chapter, Catherine both resists and admires Cousin Hannah, who tries to provoke her and break down her defenses. Catherine’s flirtation, guilt, and fascination highlight the interplay of imagination, memory, and identity. Cousin Hannah’s relentless energy and refusal to submit contrast with Catherine’s vulnerability and dreams, illustrating different responses to life’s hardships.
  • The final passages consider Cousin Hannah’s distrust of men, her “perpendicular” strength compared to Catherine’s “horizontal” invalidity, and Catherine’s wavering faith both in men and women. The chapter ends on a note of ambiguity, with references to death, sorrow, and the slow passage of time, underscoring the unresolved tensions and mysteries that permeate Catherine’s life.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Catherine

  • A complex character caught between dreams and reality, longing for love but resigned to solitude.
  • Her memories and fantasies reveal her inner turmoil and sense of loss.
  • She is reflective, romantic, and somewhat nostalgic, haunted by unanswered questions about love and life.

Cousin Hannah

  • A formidable, almost mythical figure symbolizing strength and independence.
  • She challenges Catherine’s illusions and urges her to face reality.
  • Known for her fiery spirit, sharp wit, and relentless energy, she is both a source of inspiration and tension.
  • Her distrust of men and disdain for conventional womanhood provide a counterpoint to Catherine’s experiences.

Research: Themes

Marriage and Loss

See: Grief, Loss, and Mourning · Women, Gender, and Marriage

  • Catherine’s reflections on her marriage are fragmented and surreal. She imagines multiple versions of her wedding (married in white, black, at a funeral, by a dog, or not at all) signifying confusion, loss, and the elusive nature of commitment and love.
  • Marriage is portrayed as a metaphorical death of the soul, supported by Cousin Hannah’s cynical view that marriage leads to sacrifice and the wasting of a woman’s talents.

Illusion vs. Reality

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Catherine struggles to distinguish between dreams, memories, and reality. Her imagination creates a world where events happen simultaneously or not at all, where identities and places blur into one another.
  • The mirrors, illusions, and dreams symbolize the instability of perception and the human desire for clarity and understanding.

Identity and Loneliness

See: Identity and the Self · Isolation and Connection

  • Catherine feels isolated, spouseless, and robbed of love, reflecting a deep sense of loneliness and alienation.
  • Cousin Hannah represents a contrasting figure of strength and defiance, yet also one marked by solitude and an almost otherworldly existence.

Death and Time

See: Death and Mortality · Time

  • Death is a recurring motif, intertwined with marriage, dreams, and memory. Catherine contemplates the inevitability of death and the fading of beauty and life’s passions.
  • Time is fluid and fragmented, with references to sleep, awakening, and timelessness, emphasizing Catherine’s disoriented state.

Female Strength and Defiance

See: Women, Gender, and Marriage · Fear and Courage

  • Cousin Hannah embodies resilience, independence, and courage. She is portrayed as a warrior-like figure, unyielding and untamed, contrasting with Catherine’s vulnerability.
  • Cousin Hannah’s disdain for men and her distrust of Catherine’s whimsical nature highlight different approaches to womanhood and survival.

Research: Resources

  • “the king was a dog, that it was a dog who had worn the crown and diamond cross.” The story of King Halga’s death and the dog’s succession is a tale from medieval Scandinavian history, specifically the Chronicon Lethrense and Annales Lundenses, where after Halga’s death, the Swedish King Eadgils forced the Danes to accept a dog as their king before the rightful heir, Rolf Krage, could take the throne. This was a symbolic act of power and control. The phrase conveys a complex symbolic idea that challenges traditional notions of power and identity, using the image of a dog, an animal often associated with loyalty but also with baseness, as the bearer of royal and sacred emblems.
  • Subplot of the Irish housemaid who went mad polishing a mirror. See resources for the history of the “domestic service question” in the US in the 1800s. The Irish housemaid subplot serves to deepen the novel’s exploration of the blurred lines between sanity and insanity, presence and absence, life and death. Through her muteness, prayers, and spectral existence, she symbolizes the pervasive sense of spiritual and emotional isolation that permeates the household, reflecting the novel’s melancholic and haunting atmosphere.

In the book the Dialectic of Sex a quote from Carrie Chapman Catt estimates that: “to get the word ‘male’ out of this country 52 years of pauseless campaign… During that time they were forced to conduct 56 campaigns of referenda to male voters, 480 campaigns to get legislatures to submit suffrage amendments to voters, 47 campaigns to get state constitutional conventions to write woman suffrage into state constitutions, 277 campaigns to get state party conventions to include woman suffrage planks, 30 campaigns to get presidential party conventions to adopt woman suffrage planks in party platforms and 19 successive campaigns with 19 successive Congresses. Thus defeat was so frequent, and victory so rare - and then achieved by such bare margins - that even to read about the struggle for suffrage is exhausting, let alone to have lived through it and fought for it.” In light of this Cousin Hannah’s relentless energy is seen as not disproportionate to the women’s suffrage movement. Cousin Hannah’s attitude to marriage and men (at least the outward appearance) would reflect the fundamental struggle. Again from The Dialectic of Sex “To make both women and children totally independent would be to eliminate not just the patriarchal nuclear family, but the biological family itself.”
