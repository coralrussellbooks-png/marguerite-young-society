To All My Darlings

Chapter 34

On Chapter 34 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 6, 2026

← Chapter 33 · Home · Chapter 35 →

Summary

Catherine remembers her wedding many ways: in white and in black, in a cathedral during a royal funeral, at home with only the black coachman as witness, as an elopement with the robber bridegroom pursued by Cossack horsemen. Most often she was married by a St. Bernard dog who spoke church Latin, gave her the Viaticum, and foretold that her husband would be lost between two frozen peaks. Cousin Hannah told her that marriage was “the death of a woman’s soul” and that no man had ever loved her. Catherine remembers Hannah’s visits: the iron keys jangling at her belt, her wit and metallic energy, her weird dance whenever a cock crowed, her contempt for Joachim. In her company Catherine felt “both very flirtatious and very guilty, trying to resist this man who was no man.” She longs to learn arithmetic and geometry so that she might know whether the great geometer of the universe “was sane or insane.” Hannah always predicted that Joachim would fail Catherine at the crucial moment, as Peron had by his apparent suicide.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a surreal and haunting depiction of Catherine’s marriage, described as both a wedding and a funeral, veiled in white and black, with multiple conflicting versions of what happened. This passage highlights Catherine’s uncertain, fragmented memory and symbolizes the blending of beginnings and endings, life and death, love and loss. It evokes the mysterious, almost mythical quality of her experience and sets the tone for the chapter’s exploration of elusive reality.
  • Cousin Hannah is portrayed as a powerful, almost otherworldly woman. She challenges Catherine’s fantasies and illusions about love and men, urging her to face the harsh realities of life without illusions. This passage contrasts Cousin Hannah’s stark realism with Catherine’s dreamy, romantic visions and introduces the theme of female independence and existential struggle.
  • The crowing of a cock, which causes Cousin Hannah to dance and emit “showers of metallic sparks,” is a striking symbol associated with awakening and revelation. This passage links to themes of awakening from dreams or illusions and the harsh truths of reality. The cock’s crowing also connects to cultural and geographical references, such as Moroccan courtyards and veiled women, adding layers of meaning about visibility, secrecy, and awakening.
  • Catherine’s desire to learn arithmetic and geometry so she can understand who “the geometer” is (whether sane or insane) symbolizes her quest for order, meaning, and clarity in a chaotic and mysterious world. This passage encapsulates the chapter’s central tension between rationality and imagination, reality and dream, and reflects Catherine’s struggle to make sense of her life and the illusions that surround it.
  • The chapter contains a profound meditation on human existence, describing man as “a nine days’ wonder,” “a falling star,” “the broken sea shell,” emphasizing transience, fragility, and the illusory nature of life. This philosophical reflection deepens the chapter’s existential themes and Catherine’s skepticism about men, love, and reality.
  • Throughout the chapter, Catherine both resists and admires Cousin Hannah, who tries to provoke her and break down her defenses. Catherine’s flirtation, guilt, and fascination highlight the interplay of imagination, memory, and identity. Cousin Hannah’s relentless energy and refusal to submit contrast with Catherine’s vulnerability and dreams, illustrating different responses to life’s hardships.
  • The final passages consider Cousin Hannah’s distrust of men, her “perpendicular” strength compared to Catherine’s “horizontal” invalidity, and Catherine’s wavering faith both in men and women. The chapter ends on Cousin Hannah’s belief that Joachim “would cruelly disappoint my mother at the last, crucial moment,” as Peron had.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • The dog gave her the Viaticum, “though whether the provisions for a journey or the last rites given to one when dying, she could not say.” Why might they be the same?
  • “Nothing had happened at all… or everything had happened simultaneously like one image repeated in many mirrors.” Which is stranger?
  • Hannah cried the loudest “perhaps because she knew the silence of the unanswering heart.” What is she besieging?

Characters

  • Hannah asks what man “was worth a woman’s secret life.” How would Catherine answer?
  • Catherine felt “both very flirtatious and very guilty, trying to resist this man who was no man.” Who is she resisting?
  • Hannah believes Joachim “would cruelly disappoint my mother at the last, crucial moment.” Is she fair to him?

Connections

  • The St. Bernard foretells that her husband “would fall between two frozen mountain peaks.” How does this echo Jock’s disappearance in Chapter 33 and the St. Bernard of Chapter 31?
  • The cock that makes Hannah dance is also “the golden cock crowing in the twilight of my mother’s opium dreams” of Chapter 14. What does each announce?
  • Catherine “eloped with the robber bridegroom,” just as Hannah tried to carry her away in Chapter 32. Who is the robber?

Reflection

  • Can a wedding and a funeral fall on the same day of a life?
  • “Nothing lived except through imagination, and nothing died except through imagination.” Do you agree?
  • What would you need to learn to know whether the world’s designer is sane?

Research: Characters

Catherine

  • A complex character caught between dreams and reality, longing for love but resigned to solitude.
  • Her memories and fantasies reveal her inner turmoil and sense of loss.
  • She is reflective, romantic, and somewhat nostalgic, haunted by unanswered questions about love and life.
  • She was married most often by “a St. Bernard dog, an Alpine retriever who had come to her in a prophetic dream.”
  • She yearns for “a teacher of arithmetic” so that she might know “who the geometer was.”
  • When Cousin Hannah came, she would say that she “had gone to Pisa to see the Leaning Tower.”

Cousin Hannah

  • A formidable, almost mythical figure symbolizing strength and independence.
  • She challenges Catherine’s illusions and urges her to face reality.
  • Known for her fiery spirit, sharp wit, and relentless energy, she is both a source of inspiration and tension.
  • Her distrust of men and disdain for conventional womanhood provide a counterpoint to Catherine’s experiences.
  • She “had always worn around her belt numerous iron keys which had jangled, giving off dissonant music.”
  • At the sound of a cock crowing she danced “a weird dance, giving off showers of metallic sparks.”
  • “Her contempt for old Joachim had remained necessarily boundless, like her indifference.”
  • “It was said that she was jealous of Mt. Blanc.”

Joachim Spitzer

  • He is “the elephant with the angelic wings, the failing memory.”
  • Cousin Hannah believed he “would cruelly disappoint my mother at the last, crucial moment.”

Peron Spitzer

  • He “apparently committed suicide, taking a coward’s way out.”

The Black Coachman

  • At one of her weddings there was “no eye witness but the black coachman, now dead.”

Jock Cartwheel

  • “It was my unknown father who had died.”

Mr. Chandelier

  • Cousin Hannah seemed as strange “as Mr. Chandelier or a seven-branched crystal candelabra glittering in a far heaven.”

Research: Themes

Marriage and Loss

See: Grief, Loss, and Mourning · Women, Gender, and Marriage

  • Catherine’s reflections on her marriage are fragmented and surreal. She imagines multiple versions of her wedding (married in white, black, at a funeral, by a dog, or not at all) signifying confusion, loss, and the elusive nature of commitment and love.
  • Marriage is portrayed as a metaphorical death of the soul, supported by Cousin Hannah’s cynical view that marriage leads to sacrifice and the wasting of a woman’s talents.

Illusion versus Reality

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Catherine struggles to distinguish between dreams, memories, and reality. Her imagination creates a world where events happen simultaneously or not at all, where identities and places blur into one another.
  • The mirrors, illusions, and dreams symbolize the instability of perception and the human desire for clarity and understanding.

Identity and Loneliness

See: Identity and the Self · Isolation and Connection

  • Catherine feels isolated, spouseless, and robbed of love, reflecting a deep sense of loneliness and alienation.
  • Cousin Hannah represents a contrasting figure of strength and defiance, yet also one marked by solitude and an almost otherworldly existence.

Death and Time

See: Death and Mortality · Time

  • Death is a recurring motif, intertwined with marriage, dreams, and memory. Catherine contemplates the inevitability of death and the fading of beauty and life’s passions.
  • Time is fluid and fragmented, with references to sleep, awakening, and timelessness, emphasizing Catherine’s disoriented state.

Female Strength and Defiance

See: Women, Gender, and Marriage · Fear and Courage

  • Cousin Hannah embodies resilience, independence, and courage. She is portrayed as a warrior-like figure, unyielding and untamed, contrasting with Catherine’s vulnerability.
  • Cousin Hannah’s disdain for men and her distrust of Catherine’s whimsical nature highlight different approaches to womanhood and survival.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

White and Black in Marriage

See: Weddings and Funerals

  • White symbolizes innocence and first marriage; black, “as befitted one who should be married twice,” signifies death, mourning, and a second marriage.

The Funeral and the King of France

See: Weddings and Funerals

  • Represents grandeur, loss, and the blending of significant life events into one confusing memory.

The St. Bernard Dog

See: Dogs

  • A prophetic figure in Catherine’s dreams, symbolizing guidance, loss, and perhaps spiritual rites.

Mirrors and Reflections

See: Mirrors and Reflections

  • Symbolize self-perception, illusion, and the fragmented nature of identity.

Mountains and Avalanches

See: Mountains

  • Represent obstacles, permanence, and sudden destruction in life and relationships.

The Cock Crowing

See: Birds

  • Symbolizes awakening, sovereignty, and perhaps the harsh reality that disrupts illusions.

Iron Keys

See: Keys, Doors, and Locks

  • Worn by Cousin Hannah, they symbolize access, control, and the discordant music of life’s complexities.

Research: Resources

  • “cherubim riding on the backs of bottle-nosed dolphins”: Cupids riding dolphins are a favorite of Renaissance art, as in Raphael’s Triumph of Galatea (1512).
  • “it was the king of France who had died”: At the death of a French king the herald cried, “Le roi est mort, vive le roi!” (“The king is dead, long live the king!”), first in 1422.
  • “that the king was a dog, that it was a dog who had worn the crown and diamond cross”: The story of King Halga’s death and the dog’s succession is a tale from medieval Scandinavian history, specifically the Chronicon Lethrense and Annales Lundenses, where after Halga’s death, the Swedish King Eadgils forced the Danes to accept a dog as their king before the rightful heir, Rolf Krage, could take the throne. This was a symbolic act of power and control. The phrase conveys a complex symbolic idea that challenges traditional notions of power and identity, using the image of a dog, an animal often associated with loyalty but also with baseness, as the bearer of royal and sacred emblems.
  • “gilded Louis Quatorze chairs”: Louis Quatorze is the architectural and decorative style of Louis XIV or French Classicism. Louis XIV reigned 1643-1715.
  • “she had been married by a St. Bernard dog, an Alpine retriever”: The hospice dogs of the Great St. Bernard Pass. See the Chapter 31 resources and Dogs.
  • “or a glacier would move over him during the long night”: The guides lost in the Hamel party’s avalanche on Mont Blanc in 1820 came out at the foot of the Bossons Glacier in 1861, forty-one years later. See Mountains.
  • “This dog had given her the Viaticum”: Viaticum is used in the Catholic Church for the Eucharist (Holy Communion) for a person who is dying as part of the Last Rites. Viaticum is Latin for provisions for a journey, as the text says.
  • “Caesar’s message to the Gauls”: Commentaries on the Gallic War were written by Julius Caesar as his account of the war. It was used in Latin instruction because of its simple and direct prose. It opens, “All Gaul is divided into three parts.”
  • “Pyrenees or at a ski resort covered by an avalanche of stars”: The Pyrenees are a mountain range bordering France and Spain.
  • “Besides, Mr. Alexander Pope was holding her hand”: Alexander Pope was a poet and satirist in 18th century England. “Nothing so true as what you once let fall, / ‘Most Women have no Characters at all’” (Epistle to a Lady, 1735).
  • “She had worn her archery veil. Many golden arrows had sought her heart”: In Ovid, Cupid’s golden arrow kindles love and his leaden arrow puts it to flight (Metamorphoses 1). See Greek Mythology.
  • “had eloped with the robber bridegroom”: The Robber Bridegroom is a novella by Eudora Welty which used the Cupid and Psyche myth. See the Chapter 32 resources and Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “with horse-head clouds racing along the wind”: The “luminous mares’-tails” before it are high cirrus clouds. The weather proverb runs, “Mackerel sky and mares’ tails make tall ships carry low sails.”
  • “it had been a Gretna Green wedding”: Gretna Green is known for “runaway marriages” since it was the first village over the Scottish border. England had strict marriage laws in the 1700s, under Lord Hardwicke’s Marriage Act of 1753, for couples under twenty-one years old whereas Scotland did not. Scottish law allowed for irregular marriages and almost anyone had the authority to conduct a marriage. Blacksmiths were known as “anvil priests.”
  • “furies unnameable”: The Furies, or Erinyes, pursued wrongdoers. The Greeks feared to name them and called them the Eumenides, “the Kindly Ones.” See Greek Mythology.
  • “screeching harpies, half woman and half bird”: The Harpies, “snatchers,” carried off the souls of the dead, and in the Aeneid they foul the Trojans’ feast (3). See Greek Mythology.
  • “Was Cousin Hannah the first horseman? Was she the second”: The Four Horsemen of Revelation 6. See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “Was not marriage, as that great suffrage captain had always said, the death of a woman’s soul”: Psychology has researched the ways marriage changes husband and wives. In previous chapters, the differences for women in marriage have been discussed. See the Chapter 23 resources and Women, Gender, and Marriage.
    • When Lucy Stone married Henry Blackwell in 1855, they read a protest against laws that gave the husband “custody of the wife’s person.”
  • “My mother’s beauty was already fading”: In 2017 the Pew Research Center found that, among those who see differences, 61 percent of men said it was biology that determined what things men and women excel at in the workplace, while 65 percent of women said it was due to societal expectations (Pew Research Center, “Americans are divided on whether differences between men and women are rooted in biology or societal expectations,” December 5, 2017). One of those expectations is that women are valued for their physical beauty and nurturing. In July 2022, at the Turning Point USA Student Action Summit in Tampa, Representative Matt Gaetz asked why “the women with the least likelihood of getting pregnant” were “the ones most worried about having abortions” and said, “Nobody wants to impregnate you if you look like a thumb.” That May he had called abortion-rights protesters “over-educated, under-loved millennials” (Matt Gaetz, Twitter, May 4, 2022). See Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery.
  • “It had left its little claw foot”: “Til crowes feet be growe under youre yë” (Chaucer, Troilus and Criseyde 2.403).
  • “Men die of many reasons before they die of love”: “Men have died from time to time, and worms have eaten them, but not for love” (Rosalind, As You Like It 4.1). See Shakespeare.
  • “this cynical suffrage captain”: Through Cousin Hannah, Marguerite Young is not speaking about the futility of suffrage even though it was a long fought battle that has not been fully documented. In The Dialectic of Sex, Firestone quotes Carrie Chapman Catt: “To get the word ‘male’ in effect out of the Constitution cost the women of the country fifty-two years of pauseless campaign… During that time they were forced to conduct 56 campaigns of referenda to male voters, 480 campaigns to get legislatures to submit suffrage amendments to voters, 47 campaigns to get state constitutional conventions to write woman suffrage into state constitutions, 277 campaigns to get state party conventions to include woman suffrage planks, 30 campaigns to get presidential party conventions to adopt woman suffrage planks in party platforms and 19 successive campaigns with 19 successive Congresses” (Carrie Chapman Catt and Nettie Rogers Shuler, Woman Suffrage and Politics, 1923, as quoted in Shulamith Firestone, The Dialectic of Sex, 1970). Firestone adds, “Thus defeat was so frequent, and victory so rare—and then achieved by such bare margins—that even to read about the struggle for suffrage is exhausting, let alone to have lived through it and fought for it.” In light of this Cousin Hannah’s relentless energy is seen as not disproportionate to the women’s suffrage movement. Cousin Hannah’s attitude to marriage and men (at least the outward appearance) would reflect the fundamental struggle. Again from The Dialectic of Sex, “To make both women and children totally independent would be to eliminate not just the patriarchal nuclear family, but the biological family itself” (Shulamith Firestone, The Dialectic of Sex, 1970). See Women’s Suffrage.
  • “seas of fire, seas of ice, seas of glass”: “A sea of glass mingled with fire” (Revelation 15:2). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “beetling cliffs”: “The dreadful summit of the cliff / That beetles o’er his base into the sea” (Hamlet 1.4). See Shakespeare.
  • “never a charivari for a lonely bride”: Charivari is a mock parade where the object was for the crowd to make as much noise as possible. It was a mock serenade of pots, pans, and horns for newlyweds, especially a mismatched couple or a remarried widow.
  • “land where no bird sang”: “The sedge has withered from the lake, / And no birds sing” (Keats, “La Belle Dame sans Merci,” 1819). See Romantic Poets.
  • “as ineffectual as the poorest gallows birds with their chains gibbering”: “The sheeted dead / Did squeak and gibber in the Roman streets” (Hamlet 1.1). See Shakespeare.
  • “she had gone to Pisa to see the Leaning Tower”: The bell tower of Pisa Cathedral began to lean on its soft ground in 1178, while it was being built.
  • “Who was worth the candle’s end”: The candle proverb. See the Chapter 31 resources.
  • “that great Crusader was not a man’s woman”: There is an uncomfortable trope about women/feminists that Young uses against Cousin Hannah. She was so vehement against men when she was so ugly that men didn’t find her attractive and so there was nothing for her to defend against. It keeps coming up even to the present day. Young admitted that “there was a tendency to be satirical about her,” but she used the Alpine story of the lost skirt to explain Hannah, and in the trousseau trunks of her real source found that “she had a dream too” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See the Chapter 30 resources, the Chapter 31 resources, and Marguerite Young on Her Craft.
  • “this mortal stage, my mother’s bedroom”: “All the world’s a stage” (As You Like It 2.7). See Shakespeare.
  • “a parable in unknown tongues”: “He that speaketh in an unknown tongue speaketh not unto men, but unto God” (1 Corinthians 14:2). See The Bible.
  • “lord of the morning, lord of all he surveys”: “I am monarch of all I survey” (William Cowper, “Verses Supposed to Be Written by Alexander Selkirk,” 1782).
  • “numerous iron keys which had jangled”: The chatelaine, a chain of keys at the belt, was the badge of the mistress of a house and of the housekeeper.
  • “By her splendid elan vital”: Henri Bergson’s élan vital, the vital impulse driving life and evolution (Creative Evolution, 1907).
  • “she was jealous of Mt. Blanc”: The named mountain ranges are not mere settings but profound symbols of the human condition’s difficult but necessary journey through adversity, change, and the pursuit of higher understanding in the novel. See Mountains.
    • Mont Blanc is the highest mountain in the Alps and Western Europe.
    • The Jungfrau is one of the main summits of the Bernese Alps. Together with the Eiger and Mönch, which Young spells “Moentsch,” as she writes “Eigar” for Eiger, the Jungfrau forms a massive wall of mountains overlooking the Bernese Oberland and the Swiss Plateau, one of the most distinctive sights of the Swiss Alps.
    • The Matterhorn is a mountain of the Alps, straddling the main watershed and border between Switzerland and Italy. Edward Whymper’s party made the first ascent in 1865, and four of the seven died on the way down.
    • Monte Rosa is a mountain massif in the eastern part of the Pennine Alps. It is between Italy and Switzerland.
  • “a seven-branched crystal candelabra”: The menorah of the Tabernacle had seven branches (Exodus 25:31-37). See Judaism.
  • “the golden cock crowing in the twilight of my mother’s opium dreams”: Weissenbach’s golden cockerel. See the Chapter 14 resources.
  • “That cock which… an Irish housemaid swore had also awakened her”: Subplot of the Irish housemaid who went mad polishing a mirror. Irish women were the largest group of domestic servants in American cities in the late 1800s, and “the servant question” filled the magazines. The Irish housemaid subplot serves to deepen the novel’s exploration of the blurred lines between sanity and insanity, presence and absence, life and death. Through her muteness, prayers, and spectral existence, she symbolizes the pervasive sense of spiritual and emotional isolation that permeates the household, reflecting the novel’s melancholic and haunting atmosphere.
  • “Cousin Hannah had exceeded these human dimensions by many dazzling cubits”: “Which of you by taking thought can add one cubit unto his stature?” (Matthew 6:27). See The Bible.
  • “What was man, this poor amalgam crumbling at a touch”: The chapter’s thoughts about man fly in the face of the patrilineal succession where men serve as heads of almost every important social, cultural, and political institution and their name is passed down through children. These things are thought to pass on a legacy of power for men and their male heirs. In Genesis God creates divisions between light and dark, water and earth, male and female and he puts man to oversee it all and keep it in order. See Genesis.
  • “a nine days’ wonder”: A nine days’ wonder means something or someone that creates a short-lived sensation. See the Chapter 29 resources.
  • “a teacher of arithmetic who would teach her to add”: Galileo wrote that the book of nature “is written in the language of mathematics” (The Assayer, 1623).
  • “with his golden-footed compass moving across a clouded sky”: “He took the golden compasses, prepared / In God’s eternal store, to circumscribe / This universe” (Milton, Paradise Lost 7.225-227). Blake drew it as The Ancient of Days (1794), and Plutarch reports Plato’s saying that “God always geometrizes.” See Milton.
  • “these great concentric circles and eccentric circles and half moons and elliptical paths”: Ptolemy explained the planets with circles, eccentrics, and epicycles. Kepler showed in 1609 that their orbits are ellipses. See Astronomy.
  • “like a Houdini fooled by his own tricks”: Harry Houdini was a Hungarian-born American escape artist, illusionist, stunt performer noted for his escape acts. In the 1920s he spent his last years exposing fraudulent spiritualist mediums, and he died in 1926.
  • “the inner light and the outer darkness”: “Cast out into outer darkness” (Matthew 8:12). George Fox’s Quakers spoke of the Inner Light, God’s light in every soul. See The Bible.
  • “like the other side of the moon, was never turned toward this world”: The Moon keeps one face toward the Earth. Its far side was first photographed by the Soviet Luna 3 in 1959. See Astronomy.
