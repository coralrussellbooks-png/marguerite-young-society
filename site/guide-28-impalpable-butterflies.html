Chapter 28: Impalpable Butterflies

← Chapter 27 · Guide Home · Chapter 29 →

Research notes: Chapter 28

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Weed · Peron Spitzer

Symbols: Butterflies and Moths

Philosophy: Aristotle · Marguerite Young on Her Craft · Stoicism

Allusions: Deafness and Sign Language · Exploration and the Poles · Greek Mythology · The History of Opium

Chapter 28 is the last chapter of the extended Spitzer sequence and one of the most beautiful in the novel. It does something Young has been saving: it gives Mr. Spitzer a passion. Not the silent music, not the legal errands, not the faithful evening visits. Something more intimate and more surprising: butterflies.

This is a short chapter, and the butterflies matter.

What actually happens in Chapter 28

The chapter opens with Mr. Spitzer acknowledging that he may be, even in his own awareness, nothing more than one of Catherine’s hallucinations: a bearded Russian bishop, an Antarctic mountain range in a lace cape, “the headless horseman fording a brook.” His life, except for these enchanted evenings, has “been a dark and empty plain.” It is only here, still denied and turned away, that he is renewed.

Young then gives us the thing Mr. Spitzer has given up: his butterfly collection. For years before Peron’s death, he had collected “beautiful butterflies of every coloration and kind.” Brimstone-colored ones burning like torpid flames. Bejeweled ones precious to him beyond jewels or mosaic emperors. He had maintained “a large correspondence with other meditative butterfly hunters.” He had watched the “birth of a butterfly, the first tremblings of the feeble, dew-lit wings as they unfolded”) and its death. He had observed that if one were called away at the crucial moment, one might never know whether one had seen a beginning or an end.

He has not added to his collection in many years. Many things came “to an end on the day of Peron’s devastating funeral, including, of course, his own capacious heart, the living heart, his way of life or most of it.” But sometimes, “when his memory of his brother” grows dim, he still remembers “the phenomenal world of the butterfly.” Seas of twinkling yellow flowers. “Clouds of yellow butterflies like flowers floating in the pale morning air.” The butterfly on the flower, identical to the flower, twin to twin, until the flower flew away and the winged petals remained.

Mr. Spitzer and his butterflies

The butterfly collection is not incidental. In European literary and philosophical tradition, the butterfly, the psyche in Greek, has been the symbol of the soul since antiquity. Mr. Spitzer, who collects souls for a living in the legal sense (the lost heirs, the disputed estates, the dead people’s properties) collected actual butterfly souls in glass cases with the same meditative scrupulousness. It was his version of his silent music: an attempt to pin down something ephemeral, to hold the beautiful and fleeting still long enough to study it.

The key observation is what the butterflies showed him: that if you “watched at only a certain moment, if one were suddenly called away in the midst of these proceedings, one should never know whether it was death or birth, beginning or end.” “The manifestations of birth and death were the same in this realm.” This is what first caused him to “embark upon his studies of impalpable butterflies.”

Mr. Spitzer began collecting butterflies because he could not tell whether they were dying or being born. He has spent his entire life unable to tell whether Peron’s fall was an ending or a kind of beginning. Whether his own continued existence is a life or a prolonged dying. Whether the moment he is living is the first trembling of the wings or the last. The butterfly collection was his attempt to learn to distinguish these things. He never quite learned. He gave it up when Peron died.

The contrast with Peron

Young uses this chapter to draw the sharpest contrast yet between the two brothers, and she does it through their relationship to time.

Peron cared for the actual, the present, the immediate. “The wastrel wheels of chance, the games of loaded dice, the” swift horses, the present hour that sufficed. He had “no patience to wait for the caterpillar to become the butterfly.” “He had given no thought to a future life.” The present moment was enough, or so it had seemed. Young’s qualification is quiet but exact: or so it had seemed. We know now that Peron removed his watch before jumping. Whatever he thought about the future, he thought about it enough to care for the timepiece.

“Mr. Spitzer had thought only of the impossible future.” He watched the caterpillar. He timed the butterfly’s birth. He composed music for voiceless choirs and deaf listeners. He carried “an out-dated passport for a journey he had not taken.” He is the man who waits, who watches, who outlives the moment he was prepared for.

And yet, Young plants this with precision, it is Mr. Spitzer who still occasionally remembers “the phenomenal world of the butterfly.” The living heart may have stopped on the day of Peron’s funeral. But the memory “of yellow butterflies like flowers floating in the pale morning air” is still there, when he allows himself to find it.

Why Catherine needs him

The chapter opens with a passage about why Mr. Spitzer keeps coming, and Young gives it more weight here than she has before. He finds in Catherine’s house “his only sense of his unbroken continuity in life.” No chair has changed its place for years. There are “these ethereal gleamings of gold and silver,” these intricately woven bellropes, “these candle flames reflected in dark Venetian mirrors.” The constancy of her unchanging world is, paradoxically, what makes him feel continuous.

Everything outside her room shifts and disorients him: glass walls, wrong cities, revolving doors, the dead man’s watch ticking on his wrist. In here, the same candles, the same mirrors, the same denial and the same welcome. He is renewed by her obtuse faith that he will always return. She denies his reality and she counts on it simultaneously. Both are necessary to him.

And he still remembers her as she was in his youth. He comes each evening to see a woman who has not moved in decades, a woman who is preserved by opium and darkness and the unchanged arrangement of her furniture. For a man whose own life stopped when his brother died, she is the only continuity he has.

What to watch for as you keep reading

After Chapter 28, the novel leaves the breakfast table and the coastal house and begins its great expansion into the wider world. You will meet Esther Longtree, Cousin Hannah, Titus Bonebreaker, the hangman Mr. Weed, the featherweight champion, many others. Young has spent twenty-eight chapters building you a set of lenses: Miss MacIntosh’s insistence on the real, Catherine’s faith in the unreal, Mr. Spitzer’s inability to tell the difference. Every new character will be refracted through these lenses.

The butterfly as a symbol of the soul, the psyche, will keep reappearing throughout the novel. Young uses it the way she uses most of her symbols: repeatedly, lightly, from different angles. When you see a butterfly in the chapters ahead, it carries the weight of what Mr. Spitzer learned watching them: that at a certain moment, you cannot tell birth from death, beginning from ending, the first trembling from the last.

Young uses the phrase the living heart to mark exactly what Peron’s death took from Mr. Spitzer: not his life, which continues, but the living part of his heart, its capacity for passion and pursuit. The butterfly collection was the living heart’s expression. The silent music, the legal errands, the faithful evening visits; these are what the surviving heart can still manage. It is not nothing. But it is not the living heart.
