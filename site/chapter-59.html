To All My Darlings

Chapter 59

On Chapter 59 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 12, 2026

← Chapter 58 · Home · Chapter 60 →

Content note: This chapter is a long meditation on suicide, which Mr. Spitzer wonders might be “the most creative act.”

Summary

Mr. Spitzer wanders the foggy city tossing coins to street musicians, never sure who is poor or rich, living or dead. One old horn-blower he pitied for years turns out to play in a great symphony orchestra and to be richer than he is. Another, a pale cavalier in white silks outside the old Opera House, turns out to be an usher waiting for his cue; Mr. Spitzer’s gold coin lands in his horn, and soon the whole street is tossing coins. He hears bells, horns, and the cries of migratory birds, and writes his silent music on his shirt cuff where Peron once kept his horse scores. He wonders whether Peron’s suicide was a creative act, a reach for life beyond the grave. He grows convinced that the skeleton inside him is not his own, that body, soul, and spirit may have been exchanged, and that he might even be Peron. What he fears most is a third body that is neither brother. At last he suspects that one fact would explain everything: he is already dead.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with vivid imagery of “forlorn adventures” of sad or mad musicians, old torch singers, derelict piano players, horn-blowers, and beggars mistaken for kings or vice versa. Mr. Spitzer’s indiscriminate generosity, tossing coins to street musicians, reveals his sympathy and confusion about appearance versus reality, life and death, wealth and poverty. This motif of mistaken identity and blurred boundaries continues throughout the chapter.
  • A particularly striking passage describes an old horn-blower who, despite begging on the streets and playing glassy jazz, also belongs to a prestigious symphony orchestra. This paradox highlights the theme of duality and deception or appearances that often mask a deeper reality. It also underscores Mr. Spitzer’s ongoing dilemma in discerning the true nature of those he helps.
  • Mr. Spitzer is “deafened... by bells ringing,” including those “muffled sounds of bells under water,” evoking images of drowned cathedrals and lost worlds. The ringing bells symbolize haunting memories and the border between life and death. Here music becomes a metaphor for existence itself: fleeting, ephemeral, and intertwined with silence and emptiness.
  • Contrary to his brother, who feared darkness, Mr. Spitzer embraces it as his “only light” and “illumination.” The darkness reveals invisible gleamings of fish fins, stars, and iridescences which symbolize insight into the hidden or unconscious realms beyond ordinary perception. This passage conveys a mystical acceptance of death and the unknown.
  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates his brother’s suicide, seeing it paradoxically as a creative act or a desire to live beyond life’s limits and to seek an immortal love. This complex meditation challenges conventional views on death, suggesting death might be a transformative passage rather than mere cessation.
  • One of the most important and haunting passages deals with Mr. Spitzer’s conviction that his body is not truly his and that his skeleton and physical form have been replaced in “this substitution,” inhabited by an alien presence or a different self. This reflects themes of alienation, fragmentation of identity, and the instability of the self. He feels like a “shadow trembling in the air” and a man who “wore a borrowed mask,” underscoring his existential dislocation.
  • Mr. Spitzer muses on the nature of the individual as composed of body (illusion), soul (all-informing), and spirit (temporary as the wind). He speculates that transformations between these components may occur without awareness, further complicating the notion of a stable self. This passage deepens the philosophical inquiry into mortality and identity.
  • Throughout the chapter, Mr. Spitzer reflects that there is “no dividing line, no distinction between life and death,” that birth and death are like a “wild sea without a shore.” This motif emphasizes the fluidity and interpenetration of existence and non-existence, challenging binary oppositions and inviting a more nuanced understanding of human experience.
  • Mr. Spitzer is “afraid of everything” and paradoxically “would be most afraid if he had no fear.” He fears loss of identity, silence, thresholds, and even his own shadow. This pervasive anxiety captures his profound existential uncertainty and the human struggle to face the unknown.
  • Mr. Spitzer returns repeatedly to the images of cloud, tree, and moon as symbols of impermanence, transformation, and liminality. He recognizes that he is a “temporary conformation of cloud and tree and moon,” enduring only briefly before dissolving into change. This metaphor encapsulates the chapter’s meditation on transience and the elusive nature of self.
  • The chapter closes with Mr. Spitzer’s tentative acceptance that he is already dead, though he experiences this through “circumlocutions and way-stations,” not straightforwardly. The acknowledgment of death as a present reality, rather than a future event, frames the entire chapter as an exploration of the liminal existence between life and death, presence and absence.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • Mr. Spitzer finds “the darkness his only light.” What can he see by night that he cannot see by day?
  • “He knew only that in order to be what one was one must be what one was not.” How does this sentence work as a rule for the whole chapter?
  • Mr. Spitzer insists that “this skeleton was not his skeleton.” What does the long list of bones that are not his do to a reader?

Characters

  • Mr. Spitzer insults “the usher who played the horn to announce the dying king.” Why does Young keep showing him mistaking beggars for kings and kings for beggars?
  • “His brother had feared life because it was brief. Mr. Spitzer had feared life because it was long.” How do the twins mirror each other?
  • Mr. Spitzer calls himself “a psychological beggar wearing a coat of patchwork.” Is this self-pity, self-knowledge, or both?

Connections

  • Mr. Spitzer has “salt water in his veins instead of blood.” Where else does the sea run through the novel’s characters?
  • Mr. Spitzer brought the King of Cracow “the rocking chair, though too late.” How does this carry on the King of Cracow’s story from Chapter 54?
  • Mr. Spitzer thinks that he “might be his brother, and Peron might be Joachim.” How does this chapter prepare for later questions about which brother is alive?

Reflection

  • “That which was known should cause less fear than that which was unknown.” Does knowing a fear make it smaller?
  • “And if chance, it was not music.” Can art come from chance?
  • Mr. Spitzer asks, “were we not all members of one another.” What is owed to the strangers who brush past in the street?

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • A melancholic, introspective figure who is both a lawyer and a musician.
  • Haunted by memories of his brother Peron and his own existential plight.
  • Portrayed as a man caught between life and death, reality and illusion.
  • Exhibits deep sympathy for poor musicians, often giving coins indiscriminately.
  • Suffers from sensory impairments and a profound sense of alienation from his own body and identity.
  • Experiences a metaphysical substitution of his skeleton and body, questioning his own existence.
  • His thoughts reflect a complex understanding of mortality and immortality, and he embraces darkness as a form of illumination.

Peron Spitzer (Joachim’s twin brother)

  • A contrasting figure who feared life because it was brief.
  • His death deeply affects Mr. Spitzer and acts as a catalyst for many of Spitzer’s reflections.
  • Represents the opposite approach to life compared to Mr. Spitzer’s fear of life’s length.

The Old Horn-Blower / Street Musicians

  • Symbolize the blurred lines between poverty and wealth, life and death.
  • One horn-blower leads a double life: a street musician and a member of a classical orchestra.
  • Represents the multiplicity of identity and the deceptive appearances of social roles.
  • Another horn-player, a skull-faced cavalier in white silks outside the old Opera House, is only an usher waiting to announce the dying king. Mr. Spitzer’s gold coin falls into his horn, the horn blasts, every traffic horn answers, and the whole street starts tossing coins.

Research: Themes

Life and Death as Interwoven States

See: Death and Mortality

  • Mr. Spitzer is caught between life and death, uncertain of his true state.
  • The narrative suggests that birth and death are part of a continuous, shoreless sea.
  • Death is not a finality but a transformation. Suicide may even be a creative act, like sexual love begetting another person, a desire to live beyond the grave.

Identity and Alienation

See: Identity and the Self · Isolation and Connection

  • Mr. Spitzer questions his own identity, feeling alienated from his body and self.
  • The metaphor of a stolen or substituted skeleton underscores his existential dislocation.
  • This raises philosophical questions about what constitutes the self (body, soul, or spirit) and the instability of these concepts.

Music as Metaphor

See: Music, Sound, and Silence

  • Music recurs as a symbol of life’s ephemeral beauty and the fleeting nature of existence.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s compositions, based on the patterns of migratory birds, and the cue-ball musician’s symphony for billiard balls represent the unpredictability and chance in life.
  • The music of the dead and the living intertwine, suggesting a continuum beyond mortality.

Illusion and Reality

See: Illusion and Reality · Eyes and Blindness · Fog and Mist

  • The blurred perceptions of Mr. Spitzer, including his dim eyesight and confusion about colors and sounds, symbolize the difficulty of discerning reality.
  • The chapter explores illusions (revolving doors, glass walls, and shifting clouds) as metaphors for life’s uncertainties and transformations.

Time and Memory

See: Memory and the Past · Time

  • Time is depicted as fluid and unreliable; Mr. Spitzer experiences memories of vanished streets, people, and moments.
  • His experience of time is cyclical and elusive, with the past, present, and future intermingling.
  • The idea that memories and sensations can persist beyond death is central to his reflections.

Fear and Courage

See: Fear and Courage · Shadows

  • Fear dominates Mr. Spitzer’s existence: fear of death, loss of identity, silence, and change.
  • Yet, he also embraces darkness as illumination and finds a strange peace in failure and uncertainty.

On Suicide and Immortality

See: Suicide in History and Literature

  • Suicide is contemplated as a potentially creative act, a desire to transcend mortality rather than a mere escape.
  • The relationship between fear of death and the will to live is explored deeply.

On the Self and Its Components

See: Identity and the Self · Descartes and Dualism

  • Mr. Spitzer distinguishes between body (illusion), soul (all-informing), and spirit (temporary), reflecting a tripartite view of human existence.
  • The instability of these components leads to questions about the permanence and reality of the self.

On Memory and Loss

See: Grief, Loss, and Mourning · Memory and the Past

  • Memory holds both joy and pain; past pleasures become present pains.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s awareness of his own fading perceptions underscores the themes of loss and impermanence.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Bells and Horns

See: Music and Instruments · Silence and Deafness

  • Bells represent the passage of time and change ringing without repetition, symbolizing life’s unpredictability.
  • Horns, especially the old horn-blower, signify calls or signals between life and death, reality and illusion.

Cloud, Tree, Moon

See: Moon and Stars · Clouds and Storms

  • Recurring images representing the transient, mutable, and interconnected nature of existence.
  • Clouds suggest the ephemeral and formless; trees denote rootedness yet vulnerability; the moon symbolizes mystery and change.

Skeleton and Bones

See: Identity and the Self · Twins and Doubles

  • The alien skeleton inhabiting Mr. Spitzer’s body symbolizes the uncertainty of identity and the fracturing of the self.
  • His bones creaking and speaking imply the body’s betrayal or the intrusion of something foreign within him.
  • This is also foreshadowing of his true identity.

Birds and Migratory Patterns

See: Birds

  • Migratory birds symbolize movement between worlds, the passage of souls, and the patterns of life and death.
  • The cries of birds are likened to music and the fleeting nature of human emotion.

Music and Notes

See: Music and Instruments

  • Musical notes written on Mr. Spitzer’s shirt cuff are his elegy and memory, fragments of his lost self.
  • The metaphor of music as elusive and ephemeral highlights the fragility of life and consciousness.

  • “old torch singers singing under the city arc lamps”: A torch song is a lament for a love that is not returned; to “carry a torch” is to go on loving someone who does not love back. Helen Morgan, Libby Holman, and Ruth Etting were famous torch singers of the 1920s and 1930s. See Music and Composers.
  • “the red rose of the sun or the white rose of the moon”: Compare the Temple Garden scene of Henry VI, Part 1, where the lords of York pluck white roses and the lords of Lancaster red, the start of the Wars of the Roses (William Shakespeare, 2.4). See Shakespeare and Roses and Flowers.
  • “played the starter music at the shining glow-worm tracks”: At American racetracks a bugler sounds the “Call to the Post” to bring the horses onto the track. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “the coin for Charon”: See coins for the dead and Charon’s fare in the Chapter 52 resources. See Greek Mythology.
  • “his long white Steinkirk”: See the Steinkirk scarf in the Chapter 56 resources. See Clothing, Hats, and Skirts.
  • “the glittering diamond horseshoe”: See the Diamond Horseshoe of the old Metropolitan Opera House in the Chapter 48 resources.
  • “Mr. Spitzer’s silent music de profundis”: De profundis is Latin for from the depths. “Out of the depths have I cried unto thee, O LORD” (Psalm 130:1, KJV). Oscar Wilde took the phrase for the long letter he wrote in Reading Gaol in 1897, published as De Profundis in 1905. See On De profundis and The Bible.
  • “the theft of the great rajah’s crown”: See the carbuncle from the rajah’s crown in the Chapter 55 resources.
  • “the old bell-ringer’s change ringing”: Young defines change ringing in her own aside. Fabian Stedman’s Tintinnalogia (1668, with Richard Duckworth) and Campanalogia (1677) set out the art, and Stedman’s method is still rung. See change ringing in the Chapter 44 resources. See Music and Instruments.
  • “the bells of the drowned cathedrals”: In Breton legend the city of Ys was drowned for its sins, and on calm mornings its cathedral rises from the sea with its bells tolling. Claude Debussy set the legend in “La cathédrale engloutie” (“The Sunken Cathedral,” Préludes, Book 1, 1910). See Music and Composers.
  • “the bells of the bucentaurs”: The Bucentaur (Bucintoro) was the golden state barge of the Doges of Venice. Every Ascension Day the Doge was rowed out in it for the Marriage of the Sea and dropped a gold ring into the Adriatic. The last Bucentaur was burned by Napoleon’s troops in 1798.
    • Bucentaure was an 86-gun ship in the French Navy. It was Admiral Villeneuve’s flagship at Trafalgar (1805).
  • “that land where no explorer had ever set his flag”: Robert Peary claimed the North Pole in 1909, a claim still disputed, and Roald Amundsen’s party planted the Norwegian flag at the South Pole on December 14, 1911. See Exploration and the Poles.
  • “the sign marking the beginning or the end of a repeat”: Young defines the segno, the sign in a score that marks where a repeat begins or ends; “dal segno” (D.S.) means “from the sign.” See Music and Composers.
  • “a celestial equator making the day and night everywhere of equal length”: The celestial equator is Earth’s equator projected onto the sky. When the sun crosses it at the spring and autumn equinoxes, day and night are nearly equal everywhere on Earth. See Astronomy.
  • “The darkness was his illumination”: Compare Henry Vaughan: “There is in God (some say) / A deep, but dazzling darkness” (“The Night,” 1650). See Light and Darkness.
  • “whether she was a beautiful lady white as snow or some old hag”: Compare the loathly lady of Geoffrey Chaucer’s “Wife of Bath’s Tale” (The Canterbury Tales, 1390s). An old hag offers her husband a wife foul and faithful or fair and perhaps unfaithful; when he leaves the choice to her, she becomes both fair and true.
  • “like seeds falling from another star”: See panspermia in the Chapter 29 resources.
  • “this undiscovered shore”: Compare Hamlet’s “undiscover’d country from whose bourn / No traveller returns” (William Shakespeare, Hamlet, 3.1). See Shakespeare.
    • Mr. Spitzer invites comparison with Hamlet. Both are haunted by the dead, Hamlet by his father and Mr. Spitzer by his brother: “Beautiful memories of memories thronged through his mind, as many as the dead.” Both doubt their own perceptions; Mr. Spitzer suspects a substitution, a “temporary tenant” in his own flesh. He fears becoming “an echo lost under a domeless sky,” as Hamlet dies into “the rest is silence” (5.2), and he is “porous as a cloud,” a “psychological beggar wearing a coat of patchwork,” as estranged as Hamlet at Elsinore. Both meditate on the “undiscovered country,” and both are held back by fear and doubt. Mr. Spitzer is a modern Hamlet.
  • “his escape from this chrysalis”: The Greek word psyche means both soul and butterfly, and a butterfly leaving its chrysalis was an ancient image of the soul leaving the body. See Butterflies and Moths.
  • “that which has a local habitation and a name”: The poet “gives to airy nothing / A local habitation and a name” (William Shakespeare, A Midsummer Night’s Dream, 5.1). See the Chapter 9 resources and Shakespeare.
  • “as a ghost may cause the hairs to raise up on one’s hand”: “Then a spirit passed before my face; the hair of my flesh stood up” (Job 4:15, KJV). See The Book of Job.
  • “He knew only that in order to be what one was one must be what one was not”: To be himself, Mr. Spitzer, the living brother who carries the dead one, must also be his brother, his shadow, his opposite: identity holds what it is not. See On being what one was not and Identity and the Self.
  • “the records of euchre”: Euchre is a trick-taking card game commonly played in English speaking countries. The joker was added to the American deck around the 1860s as the top trump in euchre, the “best bower.” See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “the barkings of red-eyed dogs accompanying dead souls to Hades”: In northern English folklore the Gabriel Hounds, or Gabble Ratchets, were a spectral pack heard yelping overhead at night, and their cry foretold a death; the sound was probably wild geese in passage. See Birds, Dogs, and Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “dwarves with their feet on backwards”: Pliny the Elder describes the Abarimon, a people of India whose feet are turned backward, yet who run with great speed (Natural History, Book 7).
  • “so many dying swans to sing again”: Anna Pavlova made The Dying Swan, which Michel Fokine set for her in 1905 to Saint-Saëns’s “The Swan,” her signature dance; she is said to have asked for her swan costume as she died in 1931. See the swan song in the Chapter 53 resources.
  • “the old King of Cracow to whom, as he had been unable to restore his throne, he had brought the rocking chair”: Symbolically he is estranged from his origins or realities. It explores displacement and fragmentation of self. See Krakus, king of Cracow, in the Chapter 54 resources.
  • “the revolving castles of the dead, the glass castles, the spiral castles”: In the Welsh poem Preiddeu Annwfn (The Spoils of Annwn, Book of Taliesin, early fourteenth century), Arthur sails with three shiploads of men to the fortresses of the otherworld, among them a Glass Fortress, and only seven come back. Caer Sidi, the otherworld fortress of another Taliesin poem, has been translated “Revolving Castle.” See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “What was more pitiful than man?”: When the immortal horses of Achilles weep for Patroclus, Zeus pities them: “for of all creatures that live and move upon the earth there is none so pitiable as he is” (Homer, Iliad, Book 17, trans. Samuel Butler, 1898). See Homer’s Epics.
  • “he had been caught in a revolving door”: See the revolving door and the escalator in the Chapter 57 resources. Dee Horton and Lew Hewitt of Corpus Christi, Texas, developed the automatic sliding door in the mid-1950s because swinging doors fought the coastal wind; their first commercial doors came in 1960. See Keys, Doors, and Locks.
  • “this planet singing as it died”: In the Pythagorean music of the spheres, each planet sounds a note as it moves. See Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres.
  • “that great flute-player who had caused the roaring avalanche”: See Amphion in the Chapter 58 resources. See Greek Mythology.
  • “that old cue-ball musician who was writing a symphony for musical billiard balls”: Composers have long played with chance. The Musikalisches Würfelspiel, a dice game for composing waltzes, was published under Mozart’s name in 1792, and John Cage composed his Music of Changes (1951) by tossing coins according to the I Ching. Mr. Spitzer’s verdict: “And if chance, it was not music.” See Music and Composers and Fate and Chance.
  • “The ivory balls would not roll twice the same”: Heraclitus taught that everything flows; Plato reports his saying that no one can step twice into the same river (Cratylus 402a). See Heraclitus and Flux.
  • “Chance might be the overwhelming factor of the universe”: Mr. Spitzer sees the human wish for order meet a universe of chance. His brother the gambler embraced chance; he, cautious, tries to manage or deny it. Chance runs from the dice table to the birth and death of stars, and into memory, identity, and the music of a life, whose notes and silences fall unpredictably. Attempts at total order fail, and he accepts chance with melancholy, as a destroyer and as the condition of life itself. See Fate and Chance.
    • Billiard balls struck at random make a music never exactly repeated: “The music was like the wind shaking the crystal prisms, like the wind blowing the surf, the spectral foam patterns never twice the same as they drifted and broke, the song of the fluttering moth.” Yet cycles suggest an order under chance: “And why should he be caught in revolving doors? Why should he suffer twice, thrice, four times?” Chance reaches identity itself, for “every man and every woman might have suffered a substitution like his own,” and even suicide might be “the most creative act, even like sexual love begetting another person.” Music, the most fleeting of things, holds chance and order together: “Might not a musician fear music as he feared death, dying into his music.”
  • “a sea of black umbrellas billowing like hearts”: See The Umbrella.
  • “should one walk seven times around all existence”: The Battle of Jericho in biblical tradition involved the walls of Jericho falling after the sounding of trumpets and a ritual march around the city which is an event symbolizing the collapse of barriers and the onset of a new order. “And the seventh day ye shall compass the city seven times, and the priests shall blow with the trumpets” (Joshua 6:4, KJV). See On the Battle of Jericho and The Bible.
  • “Death was equal on all sides”: Horace: “Pallida Mors aequo pulsat pede pauperum tabernas / regumque turris,” pale Death knocks with an equal foot at the huts of the poor and the towers of kings (Odes 1.4.13-14). See Death and Mortality.
  • “One cell was the begetter of all cells”: Rudolf Virchow’s maxim “Omnis cellula e cellula,” every cell from a cell (1855), became a founding rule of biology.
  • “He was afraid of becoming an echo”: See Echo in the Chapter 58 resources. See Greek Mythology.
  • “there was salt water in his veins instead of blood”: “whirling darkness when earth itself and all the waters of earth rang like this dark bell” evokes a profound cosmic and natural imagery. The “whirling darkness” suggests a tumultuous, almost apocalyptic atmosphere as an environment charged with overwhelming forces, possibly reflecting internal turmoil or existential dread. The earth and all its waters ringing “like this dark bell” personifies the planet and its oceans as resonating with a somber, mournful sound. See On salt water in the veins.
    • “for he was his father’s son,” grounds this cosmic imagery in lineage and inherited identity. It asserts the inevitability of descent and connection to one’s roots, implying that Mr. Spitzer carries within him not just genetic inheritance but also the weight of ancestral memory and fate.
    • “and there was salt water in his veins instead of blood, and there was the taste of salt upon his lips” deepens this connection to nature, especially the sea. Salt water, symbolizing the ocean, evokes themes of fluidity, vastness, and the primordial source of life. It suggests that Mr. Spitzer’s very being is intertwined with the sea as his life force is not typical blood but something elemental and ancient, linking him to the natural world’s endless cycles of life and death. The “taste of salt upon his lips” evokes sensory immediacy, emphasizing a visceral, intimate connection to the oceanic element, and perhaps a lingering presence of sorrow or loss, as salt is also associated with tears.
  • “an old blind sheep dog barking with thin, wild cries all night long”: See The Old Blind Sheep Dog.
  • “Should he not be the leper or the beggar or the blind man”: The leper of Leviticus must “cry, Unclean, unclean” (Leviticus 13:45, KJV); medieval lepers carried a bell or clapper to warn others away. See The Bible.
  • “Out of a cloud they came, and into a cloud they returned”: “For dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return” (Genesis 3:19, KJV). See Genesis.
  • “the gorgons in the mirrors”: Perseus cut off the Gorgon Medusa’s head by looking only at her reflection in his polished shield, for to meet her eyes was to turn to stone (Ovid, Metamorphoses, Book 4). See Greek Mythology and Mirrors and Reflections.
  • “the Sphinx faces in the streets”: The Sphinx of Thebes killed all who failed her riddle: what walks on four feet in the morning, two at noon, and three in the evening? Oedipus answered, man. See Greek Mythology.
  • “hearing the kingfisher’s cries”: In Ovid, Ceyx drowns at sea and his wife Alcyone finds his body washed ashore; the gods turn both into kingfishers, and the calm “halcyon days” are the days when the sea is still for their nesting (Metamorphoses, Book 11). See Greek Mythology and Birds.
  • “there was a missing rib”: “And the rib, which the LORD God had taken from man, made he a woman” (Genesis 2:22, KJV). Adam, too, was a sleeper: “the LORD God caused a deep sleep to fall upon Adam, and he slept” (Genesis 2:21, KJV). See Genesis.
  • “water to water and dust to dust”: The burial service of the Book of Common Prayer commits the body “earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust.”
  • “ossifrage, the breaking of his bones”: Ossifrage means bone-breaker, from the Latin os, bone, and frangere, to break. It is the old name of the bearded vulture, which drops bones on rocks to split them for the marrow; the King James Bible lists “the ossifrage” among the unclean birds (Leviticus 11:13). See Birds and The Bible.
  • “roaring like the cockles of his heart”: The “cockles of the heart,” its innermost depths, is often traced to the Latin cochleae cordis, the ventricles of the heart, or to the heart-shaped cockle shell. See The Sea and Water.
  • “cuneiform, atlas”: The atlas is the first bone of the neck, named for the Titan Atlas because it holds up the globe of the skull as he holds up the heavens. The cuneiform bones of the foot are named, like cuneiform writing, for their wedge shape (Latin cuneus). See Greek Mythology.
  • “his tragic sense had been the greatest joy”: Compare Miguel de Unamuno’s The Tragic Sense of Life (Del sentimiento trágico de la vida, 1913), which finds the root of human feeling in a hunger for immortality that reason cannot satisfy. See Existentialism.
  • “these tri-stranded beings comprising the individual”: “And I pray God your whole spirit and soul and body be preserved blameless unto the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ” (1 Thessalonians 5:23, KJV). Christian writers who divide the person into body, soul, and spirit cite this verse. See The Bible.
  • “He was disturbed by galaxies colliding”: In the early 1950s Walter Baade and Rudolph Minkowski identified the strong radio source Cygnus A with what they took to be two galaxies in collision. Later work found another cause for its radio noise, but colliding galaxies are real. See Astronomy.
  • “He was no trinitarian”: The Trinity in Christianity is the belief that there is one God who exists eternally as three distinct persons: the Father, the Son (Jesus Christ), and the Holy Spirit. These three persons are not separate gods but are one divine essence, each fully God, who share a single being and nature. While the word “trinity” doesn’t appear in the Bible, the doctrine is based on scriptural revelations about God’s nature and actions. See On the Trinitarians and The Bible.
    • Trinitarians were a Holy Order founded in 1198 and dedicated to raising funds for the ransom of Christians held by Muslims during the crusades and piracy along the Mediterranean coast. They also performed charitable acts in the areas where the orders were established and were dedicated to studying the mystery of the Holy Trinity.
  • “his soul had departed upon the wings of the morning”: “If I take the wings of the morning, and dwell in the uttermost parts of the sea” (Psalm 139:9, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “That which Mr. Spitzer feared with his most overwhelming fear was probably a third body”: The three-body problem in classical physics and celestial mechanics involves predicting the motion of three gravitationally interacting bodies. Unlike the two-body problem, which has a precise, predictable solution, the three-body problem is famously complex and chaotic: small changes in initial conditions can lead to wildly different outcomes, and there is no general closed-form solution. The system is characterized by instability, unpredictability, and intricate, often non-repeating orbits. Mr. Spitzer wonders if he is “one man in two worlds or two men in one world,” and fears a third body, neither himself nor his brother, out of the “unholy trinity of body, soul, spirit.” Like three bodies in orbit, his selves have no stable solution: he is “like a cloud continually changing,” and “When the definition was correct, the thing defined should be incorrect.” There is no “algebra of the human spirit,” and “were we not all members of one another,” as bodies pull on one another in the sky. See On the three-body problem.
    • Newton solved the motion of two bodies; for three, no general formula exists. In 1887 King Oscar II of Sweden offered a prize for the problem, and Henri Poincaré’s prize memoir (1890) showed how wildly the paths of three bodies can wander, the beginning of chaos theory. See Time, Physics, and Chaos Theory.
  • “were we not all members of one another”: “For we are members one of another” (Ephesians 4:25, KJV); “So we, being many, are one body in Christ, and every one members one of another” (Romans 12:5, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “was not the moth married to the star which had extinguished it”: See Shelley’s “desire of the moth for the star” in the Chapter 57 resources. See Romantic Poets and Butterflies and Moths.
  • “the idea of a central and coordinating self was probably”: Compare David Hume, for whom the self is “nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable rapidity, and are in a perpetual flux and movement” (A Treatise of Human Nature, 1.4.6, 1739). See Empiricism (Locke, Berkeley, Hume) and Identity and the Self.
  • “had already paid the black coachman for his long journey through the night”: Mr. Spitzer’s coin for Charon becomes the black coachman’s fare. See The Black Coachman.
