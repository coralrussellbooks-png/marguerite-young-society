Chapter 10. Not One Self

← Previous · Philosophy of MMMD Home · Next →

See also: Pillar 1 Not One Self

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Esther Longtree · Eustace · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Identity and the Self

Chapters discussed: 1, 30, 47, 63, 72

Definition from research

A person is a crowd, not a single fixed identity. The many selves are not a malfunction to correct. They are the normal condition of being alive.

The novel announces this principle before it states any idea. The first philosophical passage begins with “a double-headed man” by the side of the road, a man carrying a child on his shoulders (ch. 1). From there, the doubling spreads through every major character. Mr. Spitzer cannot tell whether he is himself or Peron, and he wonders whether he “lived on both sides, died on both sides” (ch. 47). Cousin Hannah could be “both herself and a gentleman” (ch. 30). Miss MacIntosh tells Vera, “My elder brothers called me George,” and wishes she had been “an honest woman or an honest man” (ch. 63). Catherine’s Eustace “might be simultaneously many persons” (ch. 30). Even Esther Longtree says she has the “feeling of not being born myself yet” (ch. 72).

The trials showed what happens when a person tries to be one thing. Miss MacIntosh insisted on being plain and simple and had to hide half of herself under a wig. Mr. Spitzer tried to be the careful brother and found he could not say which brother he was. The plural self is what remains when the demand for a single identity breaks.

The principle is also a kind of mercy. When Vera, in the twenty-first chapter, finally sees her nursemaid without disguise, she finds “no identity but the individual stripped bare of individuality and vain pretence.” The individual is still there. What is gone is the pretence that the individual is one simple thing.
