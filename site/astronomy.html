Chapter 14

  • “the music of the spheres, the silent music”: Boethius was a Roman senator, consul, magister officiorum, polymath, historian, and philosopher of the Early Middle Ages. He sought to reconcile the teachings of Plato and Aristotle with Christian theology. He broke logic into three parts: that which defines, that which divides, and that which deduces. There are three types of arguments: those of necessity, of ready believability, and sophistry. In De Musica there are three classifications: Musica mundana, Musica humana, Musica instrumentalis. In Dante’s Divine Comedy, the spirit of Boethius is pointed out by Saint Thomas Aquinas and is mentioned further in the poem. In his book De Institutione Musica (Fundamentals of Music) his drawings appear striking and may be an inspiration for the organization of Young’s novel. Musica universalis or music of the spheres or harmony of the spheres is a Greek philosophical concept founded by Pythagoras. This metaphysical principle stated that mathematical relationships could produce ‘tones’ of energy that could produce, among other things, sounds. Pythagoras proposed that the celestial bodies all have their own unique hum based on their orbital revolution. This idea influenced many scholars until the end of the Renaissance. Johannes Kepler, a German astronomer from the 1600s wrote extensively on the subject. Later astronomy set it aside. Boethius wrote The Consolation of Philosophy in prison awaiting execution, with its Wheel of Fortune.
  • The teaching most securely identified with Pythagoras is the “transmigration of souls” or metempsychosis, which holds that every soul is immortal and, upon death, enters into a new body. He may have also devised the doctrine of musica universalis, which holds that the planets move according to mathematical equations and thus resonate to produce an inaudible symphony of music.
  • “the twelve moons shining over Jupiter”: Galileo found four of Jupiter’s moons in 1610. The twelfth was found in 1951, so twelve was the count when Young wrote.
  • “Copernicus, the author of that great celestial revolution”: Nicolaus Copernicus proposed the sun was stationary and the center of the universe, not the earth. His findings triggered the Copernican Revolution. His book was De revolutionibus (1543). Freud named Copernicus, with Darwin and psychoanalysis, as one of three blows to human pride (1917).

Chapter 22

  • “The longer the orbit, the longer the planetary year”: Kepler’s third law (Harmonices Mundi, 1619) is that the square of a planet’s orbital period is directly proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis of its orbit, meaning a larger orbit leads to a longer orbital period.
  • “two stars in the Great Bear, the lines which joined them pointing nearly toward the North star”: The Pointers, Dubhe and Merak, at the bowl’s edge of the Big Dipper.

Chapter 26

  • “Musica Universalis” is a metaphysical principle by Boethius that uses mathematical relationships to express ‘tones’ that could be heard by the soul. The "Musica Universalis" here symbolizes the harmonious and eternal cosmic order that underlies the apparent chaos of mortal life and death. It is depicted as a sublime, transcendent music that governs the movements of the stars and planets, reflecting a divine harmony beyond human perception. Mr. Spitzer, a semi-retired lawyer and composer of "silent music," embodies this principle as someone who is attuned to the "music of the spheres" or the eternal, silent harmonies that exist beyond earthly sounds. His compositions, unheard by living ears and played only for the dead or in the celestial realm, metaphorically represent this cosmic music. The chapter emphasizes the contrast between earthly, temporal music and this higher, immutable harmony, suggesting that the tangible world is but a fragment or shadow of a greater metaphysical reality. Catherine's opium dreams expand this theme by blurring the boundaries between the real and the illusory, the living and the dead, reflecting the ineffable nature of the universal music that ties all existence together. The "Musica Universalis" is not just an abstract concept but a living presence in her visions with images of angels harping, celestial choirs, and cosmic rhythms permeating her consciousness. The chapter suggests that this music is intertwined with suffering and mortality (the "music of the perfect silence beyond the chance of the brittle and falling stars") highlighting a metaphysical view where life, death, and cosmic order are inseparable parts of a greater whole. The "Musica Universalis" is used to articulate a metaphysical principle that all existence, from the smallest earthly details to the vast cosmos, is bound by a harmonious, eternal music. This music transcends the limitations of physical reality and human understanding, offering a vision of unity amid fragmentation and a cosmic order beneath the apparent chaos of life and death.

Chapter 52

  • “His faith came at that moment which should have most justified his lack of faith” This paragraph uses the figure of Mr. Spitzer, a lawyer and musician, to explore the fragile boundary between faith and doubt, reality and illusion, life and death. His faith, unexpectedly strengthened by loss, parallels the fate of his music: unheard, incomplete, and destined to fade. The woman’s life and identity are similarly elusive, caught in endless cycles and transformations like the horses on a carousel or the turns of a planetary wheel. The paragraph reflects on how truth often comes too late, and how identity, art, and existence are marked by impermanence and mystery.

Chapter 52

  • The entire scene is dreamlike, blurring the line between reality and illusion. Mr. Spitzer finds himself entangled in wedding gowns, which transform into seas, sails, and shrouds. This reflects a questioning of what is real and what is merely a construct of the mind. The passage suggests that the material world might be less substantial than the dreams and ideas that inhabit it. This aligns with metaphysical idealism, which posits that reality is fundamentally mental or spiritual. Mr. Spitzer's inability to discern whether there is one moon or many mirrored moons, or even no moon at all, highlights the limitations of human knowledge. He is trapped within his own "hypothesis," which he cannot prove. This underscores the theme of uncertainty and the difficulty of attaining objective truth. The census-taker reference is interesting because census-takers are about collecting data to create an idea of a population, Mr. Spitzer fears the census-taker because he fears the idea of ultimate knowledge. The passage touches on existential themes of meaninglessness and the search for purpose. The old maid's rejection of marriage and her call to "divest themselves of excess luggage" can be interpreted as a rejection of societal norms and a pursuit of individual freedom. However, her obsession with wedding gowns suggests an underlying longing for connection and meaning, even in the face of her conscious rejection of it. Mr. Spitzer's role as a "dead man coming to mourn for her" reinforces the sense of existential angst and the confrontation with mortality. The imagery of carousels of brides where there is no bride, and a spiral staircase finding through clouds where constellations sleep, underscores the absurdity of existence. Mr. Spitzer is caught in a world of contradictions and paradoxes, where meaning is elusive and reason fails to provide answers. The reference to a world of mirrored moons and the difficulty in knowing which is real is reminiscent of Plato's Allegory of the Cave. The world we perceive may be a mere reflection of a higher, more authentic reality. The wedding gowns themselves are rich in symbolism. They represent not only marriage but also societal expectations, lost dreams, and the weight of the past. The fact that Mr. Spitzer is "trapped" by them suggests that he is burdened by these forces, unable to break free from the constraints of tradition and memory. Mr. Spitzer likens himself to Charon's ferry boat. Charon is a figure in Greek mythology who carries souls of the newly deceased across the river Styx that divided the world of the living from the world of the dead.

Chapter 54

  • “Indian maiden Pocahontas” Pocahontas also known as Matoaka and Rebecca Rolfe was a Native American woman belonging to the Powhatan people, notable for her association with the colonial settlement at Jamestown, Virginia. She was the daughter of Wahunsenacawh, the paramount chief of a network of tributary tribes in the Tsenacommacah (known in English as the Powhatan Confederacy), encompassing the Tidewater region of what is today the U.S. state of Virginia. Pocahontas was captured and held for ransom by English colonists during hostilities in 1613. During her captivity, she was encouraged to convert to Christianity and was baptized under the name Rebecca. She married the tobacco planter John Rolfe in April 1614 at the age of about 17 or 18, and she bore their son, Thomas Rolfe, in January 1615. In 1616, the Rolfes travelled to London, where Pocahontas was presented to English society as an example of the "civilized savage" in hopes of stimulating investment in Jamestown. On this trip, she may have met Squanto, a Patuxet man from New England. Pocahontas became a celebrity, was elegantly fêted, and attended a masque at Whitehall Palace. In 1617, the Rolfes intended to sail for Virginia, but Pocahontas died at Gravesend, Kent, England, of unknown causes, aged 20 or 21. She was buried in St George's Church, Gravesend; her grave's exact location is unknown because the church was rebuilt after being destroyed by a fire. Numerous places, landmarks, and products in the United States have been named after Pocahontas. Her story has been romanticized over the years, many aspects of which are fictional. Many of the stories told about her by the English explorer John Smith have been contested by her documented descendants. She is a subject of art, literature, and film. Many famous people have claimed to be among her descendants, including members of the First Families of Virginia, First Lady Edith Wilson, American actor Glenn Strange, and astronomer Percival Lowell.

Chapter 62

Its bright blue stars and visibility in the winter night sky have made it a significant feature in cultures around the world for centuries.

Chapter 62

  • Uranus - Uranus is the seventh planet from the Sun, a tilted "ice giant" with at least 28 moons and faint rings. It is a cold, blue-green planet primarily composed of water, ammonia, and methane, and is known for its extreme axial tilt which causes long periods of continuous daylight and darkness at its poles. In mythology, Uranus was the Greek god of the sky.

Chapter 62

It is one of the smallest and most easily recognized constellations in the southern night sky.

Chapter 63

  • This is a profound and poetic meditation by Miss MacIntosh, reflecting on the harsh realities of life, death, and the human condition. Through vivid imagery and metaphor, she expresses a sense of existential weariness and resignation, while also touching on themes of identity, loss, and the illusions we maintain. Miss MacIntosh begins by describing a place or state that feels like a "No Man’s Land," symbolizing isolation, emptiness, or a barren emotional or spiritual landscape. The physical sensations she describes water pouring from her skin, her nose lengthening, eyes watering which reflect intense anguish or transformation. She speaks of being "killed" repeatedly, not just physically but emotionally and spiritually, mourning not herself but others, which suggests deep empathy and sorrow for collective human suffering. She reflects on her body's frailty (her baldness, scars, missing breast), and the metaphor of being a skeleton wandering through endless waters. This imagery conveys both a literal and metaphorical stripping away of illusions and defenses, revealing vulnerability and the inevitability of death. The passage of "invisible assassin" and "one’s own heart which has no other enemy" suggests internal conflict, self-doubt, or self-inflicted suffering. It highlights the idea that much of human pain comes from within, from our own hearts or minds. Miss MacIntosh discusses the loss of her hair and wig, symbols of vanity and identity, revealing her head, "naked of all its illusion of planetary glory" is a metaphor for shedding false pride or external appearances. She acknowledges the "vain, shallow pretense" of life that fools might see through, exposing the emptiness behind social facades. She references the Spitzer brothers and their conflicting choices, suggesting that life’s paths are often confusing, contradictory, and beyond full control. The phrase "Time moves us when we move not" captures the inexorable passage of time regardless of human action. Miss MacIntosh feels "rooked" or cheated by God, lamenting the failure of divine promises or plans. She portrays herself as "God’s poorest and lowest," stripped of glory and dignity. This expresses a profound spiritual crisis or disillusionment. Moving from the personal to the social, she recounts the history of the umbrella as a symbol of equality: once a royal privilege, now a common man’s protection. The umbrella represents practical innovation that democratizes protection and privacy, disrupting old hierarchies (e.g., coachmen, nobility). This illustrates her practical, grounded view of life and society amidst the existential turmoil. At the end, Miss MacIntosh calls for "celestial navigation," urging a broader perspective and wisdom to navigate life’s complexities rather than being bogged down in fragmented, superficial knowledge or emotional despair. This suggests a desire to find meaning or direction despite the chaos. This passage reveals Miss MacIntosh’s complex character: a woman deeply aware of suffering, mortality, and the illusions of life, yet also practical, historically informed, and resolute. She embraces her imperfections and losses, including her baldness and physical scars, as part of her identity. She challenges Vera (the narrator) to see beyond appearances and illusions to the stark truths beneath, while also acknowledging the need to carry on with life’s "mournful business" despite the sorrow and emptiness. This passage encapsulates the novel’s themes of death, identity, illusion versus reality, and the struggle to live meaningfully in a world that often seems indifferent or hostile.

  • The most famous historical figure called the "Black King" (or Prince) is Edward of Woodstock, the eldest son of King Edward III of England, known as the Black Prince, though the exact reason for the nickname remains debated. An English prince, famous for his military prowess during the Hundred Years' War, who never became king himself but was heir apparent. His black armor or black shield are potential origins for the name. The chess scenario symbolizes a situation of inevitable defeat or entrapment, reflecting Mrs. Hogden's own existential and mental state as she navigates her disordered life and surroundings. Mrs. Hogden’s emotional response to this chess game (she sits and cries when the Black King is inextricably checkmated) suggests a deep identification with the helplessness and inevitability embodied by the Black King’s plight. Chapter 71

  • The Cow Who Jumped over the Moon is an English nursery rhyme. There are numerous theories about the origin of the rhyme. James Orchard Halliwell suggested that it was a corruption of ancient Greek, probably advanced as a result of a deliberate hoax. It was connected with Hathor worship. It refers to various constellations (Taurus, Canis Minor, etc.). It describes the Flight from Egypt (the Exodus) or the founding myth of the Israelites. It depicts Elizabeth, Lady Katherine Grey, and her relationships with the earls of Hertford and Leicester. It deals with anti-clerical feeling over injunctions by Catholic priests for harder work. It describes Catherine of Aragon or Catherine I of Russia, the wife of Peter the Great or Canton de Fidèle, a supposed governor of Calais and the game of cat, a popular English pup game. This profusion of unsupported explanations was satirized by J. R. R. Tolkien in his fictional explanations of "The Man in the Moon Stayed Up Too Late". Most scholarly commentators consider these to be unproven and state that the verse is probably meant to be simply nonsense.

Chapter 71

  • An astrolabe is an ancient astronomical instrument, a handheld star chart and analog computer used by astronomers, navigators, and astrologers to tell time, determine latitude, find celestial positions, and even survey, serving functions similar to modern GPS but relying on stars and the horizon. Essentially a metal disc with intricate rotating parts and markings for constellations, it helps users calculate the time of day, predict sunrise/sunset, and locate celestial bodies by matching observations to the device's model of the sky.
