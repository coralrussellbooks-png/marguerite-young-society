7. Invented Time

← Previous · Intertextuality in MMMD Home · Next →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Titus Bonebreaker · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Time

Symbols: Clocks and Watches

Allusions: Judaism · The Bible

Definition from research

A surprising number of the novel's references concern calendars, and nearly all of them treat time as something people built.

Miss MacIntosh's longest grudge is against the Emperor Constantine. She blames him because "he went over to the pagan Church of Rome and got us all off on the wrong day of the week, resting on Sunday which was the day God had not rested" (chapter 22). She returns to the grievance again and again (chapters 23, 63, 64, 66). The source seems to be her old suitor, the Gospel salesman Mr. Bonebreaker. He preached that "the old creation which was wrecked by the Emperor Constantine shall not be restored," and that in the new creation "there will be no time as in our present sense, no memory" (chapter 65).

Other calendars surround hers. Vera learns the Hebrew months, "Nisan which is March, Jyar which is April" (chapter 20). Catherine watches for "the groundhogs who emerged on Candlemas Day to see if winter was over" (chapter 41). The mute's frog dies on Maundy Thursday (chapter 61). Each calendar is local, made, and different from the others.

The strategy matters because the novel's own handling of time is so loose. Clocks stop at different hours, and years pass in a sentence. By filling the book with competing calendars and one emperor who changed the week, Young shows time as an agreement people keep, which a single man was once able to alter. Miss MacIntosh's anger at Constantine is comic, but it rests on a real perception: someone built the calendar, and so it could have been built otherwise. That is the "The Past Doesn't Go Away" of the radical immanence notes, which warns against calling any arrangement inevitable and so hiding the people who made it. Miss MacIntosh names the builder.
