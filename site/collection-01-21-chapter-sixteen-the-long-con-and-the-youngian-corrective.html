Chapter Sixteen: The Long Con and the Youngian Corrective

← Chapter Fifteen: The Geological We · Radical Immanence Collection · Part Six: The Third Act →

The long con is the name for what happens when the language of universal ideals is used to construct a system that systematically excludes most of the people those ideals claim to cover. It is the gap between the promise and the mechanism, between We the People and the three-fifths clause, between liberty and the slave trade, between justice and the property qualification. The long con is not incidental to the American project. It is structural. It was built in, on purpose, by people who understood exactly what they were doing and chose the language of universalism to cover the machinery of exclusion.

Young's philosophy is the most rigorous available corrective to the long con, not because it offers a counter-ideology but because it dismantles the epistemological conditions that make the long con possible.

The long con depends on three things that Young's system refuses.

It depends on the unified self: the citizen who can be sorted, who has a single legible identity that the system can process, who is either inside the We or outside it.

Young's plural self cannot be sorted this way. The person who is simultaneously inside and outside the categories the system uses is not an anomaly to be managed. They are the condition. The sorting system is the anomaly.

The long con depends on the naturalized real: the construction of exclusion as the way things simply are, the natural order, the obvious truth that certain people are less capable or less deserving or less fully human.

Young's insistence that all reality is constructed removes the ground from under this naturalization. If the real is always already constructed, then the natural order is always already a political choice, and the political choice can be challenged and remade.

The long con depends on the processed grief: the demand that the people most damaged by the exclusion perform recovery, demonstrate resilience, show that they have moved through their grief and arrived at a functional participation in the system that produced their loss.

Young's permanent weather refuses this demand. The grief does not resolve. The losses are permanent and cumulative and the demand that they be performed as resolved is its own violence.

The Youngian corrective is not a new ideology to replace the long con. It is a philosophical ground from which the long con can be seen clearly for what it is, its mechanisms named, its epistemological dependencies identified, and its alternatives genuinely imagined. Not as utopia. As honest construction: the provisional structure that knows what it is and builds that knowledge into its design.

Young herself stated the political stakes of her philosophy of illusion directly: if you understand hallucination and illusion, you don't blindly follow any leader. You must know if the person is sane or insane, over the abyss. The novel does not have a social conscience in the programmatic sense, but the book does. The epistemology is the politics. The second pillar is the corrective.
