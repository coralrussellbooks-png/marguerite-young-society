Chapter 16

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Falada · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Pillars: Not One Self · Language Shapes What's Possible

The Questions

Vera “did not know what [she] thought until [she] heard [herself] speaking the words.” Does language report thought, or build it?

As a “headless rider” with Falada for her “head,” where exactly is Vera’s self?

Is Vera’s imagined ride a sealed escape like Catherine’s dream, or something that opens rather than closes?

She ends by seeking out Miss MacIntosh, “one who was real, one who was plain.” What is she grounding after the flight?

The chapter states Language Shapes What's Possible almost as a confession. “I often did not know what I thought until I heard myself speaking the words.” The word does not come after the thought to report it; the word makes the thought, brings it into being in the saying. Vera tells the horse Falada secrets “which even I had never known until I spoke them,” and the sequence is the claim: speaking is not expression of an inner content already there but the building of it. Grammar does not carry a world already made. It makes one.

Her self, in the ride, is not sealed in one body. She is a “headless rider,” and Falada is “my head, my intelligence,” so that the thinking part of her is out ahead of her on four legs. This is Not One Self spatialized, a self distributed across rider and horse, refusing to sit in a single bounded place. It is comic and dreamlike, but it is exact: Vera cannot be located in one skin any more than Mr. Spitzer can be sealed off from his brother.

The question the chapter raises is whether Vera’s imaginative world is a sealed one like her mother’s. The surreal house, the mosaic emperors, the funeral gondola in the fountain, the phoenix, could be another opium paradise. But Vera’s imagining works the other way. It discovers rather than encloses; she learns what she thinks and who she is by riding out into the story, not by shutting herself into it. Her invention is open, generative, a way of finding the world rather than sealing the self against it.

And she ends by riding to Miss MacIntosh, “one who was real, one who was plain.” After the flight, the plain fact. This is not a retreat from imagination into common sense but the pairing the book keeps reaching for: the imaginative construction and the ordinary held together, the ride and the plain woman at the end of it. Vera does not have to choose the seal of pure fantasy or the seal of pure fact. She moves between the open ride and the ground, and the movement is healthy.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that makes Vera’s imaginative flight the same thing as Catherine’s sealed dream, and the reading that makes her return to Miss MacIntosh a defeat of imagination by common sense. It is not collapsing the two women’s inventions into one, nor choosing plainness over wonder.

The footing is that language builds thought and self, so speaking is making, not reporting; the self rides distributed, unsealed, its head out ahead of it; and imagination can be open rather than enclosing, a way of finding the world instead of walling it out. Vera’s movement between the open ride and the ground is the pairing the book trusts, neither seal chosen. The word makes the thought, and imagination can open as easily as it can enclose.

For Reflection

What has one only understood after hearing oneself say it?

If the thinking part of a person can ride out ahead of the body, where is the self?

What is the difference between an imagination that opens the world and one that seals the self against it?
