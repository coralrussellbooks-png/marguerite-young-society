Pillar: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest · Across every book

Or, Why the Perfect Ending is a Trap

We live in a culture that is absolutely obsessed with the finished product. We want the clean resolution, the tied-up loose ends, and the satisfying final chapter. We treat an unfinished project like a failure, and an unresolved argument makes us deeply uncomfortable. From corporate milestones to our personal lives, there is a massive amount of pressure to cross the finish line, stamp a project as done, and move on. We celebrate the completed memoir, the solved problem, the person who has moved on from their trauma. We are trained to believe that a life well-lived is one where the chapters resolve properly, where closure is achieved, where you can finally say: I am finished with that.

But Marguerite Young steps in with a radical reality check. She argues that demanding everything be perfectly wrapped up and completed is actually a form of violence against our real, day-to-day human experience. It is a violence we commit against ourselves, against others, and against the work itself.

The Masterpiece of the Unfinished

Young's philosophy accepts the unfinished as the natural, honest state of things. Why? Because a philosophy that requires everything to be finished is a philosophy that can only describe a world that does not actually exist. The world does not resolve. Our inner selves do not stop changing. Our grief does not just vanish once we have processed it properly. Our work remains forever provisional. Our communities remain forever under construction. Our understanding deepens and then shifts again.

To insist that life must be finished is to insist that we must lie about this. It is to force ourselves and others to pretend that closure exists when it does not, that final answers are possible when they are not, that we can ever truly be done with anything that matters.

Young draws this insight from observing something crucial about human making. When we look at the work that lasts, the work that continues to matter across generations, it often carries the mark of incompletion. It does not pretend to be final. It does not close the door on further thinking. It leaves itself open to interpretation, revision, and deepening.

To help us understand this, consider Walter Benjamin, the German philosopher who spent years gathering thousands of notes, fragments, and reflections for a massive study on modern culture and the development of capitalism. The work was called The Arcades Project. Benjamin was obsessed with the great shopping arcades of Paris, those magnificent covered passageways that had just come into being during the nineteenth century. He wanted to understand what they meant: how they revealed the logic of modern desire, how they transformed urban space, how they embodied the fantasies and alienations of a new kind of human world.

Benjamin died in 1940, on the run from the Nazis, before he could assemble all those raw materials into a finished book. He left behind thousands of notes: observations, quotations from other texts, photographs, reflections, fragments of analysis. For decades, readers approached this work as an unfortunate incompleteness. If only Benjamin had lived, the thinking went, he would have organized all this material into a proper book, with a clear argument and a systematic structure. Then we would truly understand what he was trying to say.

But when you actually read the Arcades Project as Benjamin left it, you realize something startling: its incompleteness is its greatest strength. If Benjamin had forced all those raw, chaotic pieces of history into a neat, polished, systematic textbook, he would have ruined the entire point. The fragments needed to stay fragments to be honest to real life. The quotations needed to accumulate without being resolved into a single argument. The incompleteness was not a failure. It was total integrity.

The form of the Arcades Project mirrors its content. It is a book about capitalism, about the way modern systems promise coherence and resolution while actually delivering fragmentation and endless desire. To write about fragmentation while pretending to coherence would be a lie. To write about endless accumulation while organizing everything into a tidy conclusion would be dishonest. Instead, Benjamin's incompleteness says something that no finished argument could: this is what it actually feels like to live in this world. This is how knowledge actually accumulates: in fragments, in layers, in unresolved observation. This is what honesty looks like.

When we finally encounter the Arcades Project as it actually exists, we have to do something harder than reading a finished book. We have to engage with the thinking itself. We have to let the fragments work on us. We have to sit with the tensions and contradictions. We have to resist the urge to resolve everything into a single, clean meaning. And in doing that, we become part of the work's ongoing process. We become collaborators in its incompletion. The book does not tell us what to think; it invites us to think alongside it, to find our own connections, to build our own understanding.

This is what Young means by the ethics of incompletion. It is not laziness. It is not a failure to finish. It is, instead, a commitment to honesty about the nature of knowledge, human relationships, and the work itself. It is refusing to compress lived experience into a false finality.

Busting the Political Long Con

So, how does celebrating the unfinished protect us from authoritarian control?

In daily life, the demand for a perfectly finished product can feel like standard social pressure. But at a political scale, Young warns us that this demand is exactly what produces the long con: the confidence scheme that authoritarian movements depend on to maintain their power.

Authoritarian movements and deceptive leaders pull off this con by claiming they have the absolute, perfectly completed answers to all of society's problems. They present rigid platforms, unshakeable positions, and flawless policy statements to project total authority. They want you to believe their system is entirely finished, entirely thought through, and therefore beyond question. A demagogue does not say: here is my preliminary thinking on this problem, and I will revise it as I learn more. That would invite scrutiny and revision. Instead, the authoritarian says: I have the perfect solution. It is complete. It is unshakeable. You simply have to trust me and follow it.

This finished, perfect, unquestionable system is the entire basis of totalitarian control. It leaves no room for adaptation. It admits no error. It demands absolute compliance because to question any part of it would be to question the whole thing, and the whole thing is, by definition, perfect.

When we embrace the ethics of incompletion, we dismantle this trap.

We unmask the false promise: We recognize that any political leader pretending to have a flawless, permanent fix is running a manipulation tactic. No human being has finished answers to complex problems. Anyone claiming they do is lying, and the lie is the whole con.

We keep power adaptable: A policy that claims to be finished is incredibly dangerous because it cannot be revised or corrected when it starts causing real-world harm. It is frozen in place. It becomes a weapon. An institution built on the principle that it is forever unfinished, forever open to revision, forever willing to ask if it is causing harm, remains responsive to the actual needs of actual people.

We treat rules as active conversations: Instead of viewing founding political documents as sacred, untouchable monuments, we see them for what they truly are: ongoing, living arguments that must change as we learn more about what works and what does not. The original architects of a system are not prophets. They are human beings with limited perspective, shaped by the particular moment they lived in. Their work deserves respect, but not veneration. It deserves revision.

By building the unfinished directly into our mindsets and our institutions, we protect our communities from becoming frozen into tools of tyranny. We ensure that power remains transparent, accountable, and open to revision. We create a structure where every person in the system has the responsibility and the right to say: this is not working, we need to change it. And the system can actually respond and change, rather than demanding that reality itself conform to the finished blueprint.

The Cost of Closure: The Archive Wars

To understand what it actually costs to demand closure, it helps to look at how archives work as a site of political struggle. The historical archive is never complete. It is always fragmentary. It is always shaped by what was preserved and what was destroyed, by whose voices were recorded and whose were silenced. The official history that gets written in textbooks is actually a curated selection from a much messier, more contradictory body of material.

What happens when a regime demands that history be finished, organized into a clean narrative, resolved into a single official story? What gets left out? Whose experiences become untellable because they do not fit the narrative arc?

The answer is always the same: the people who were already on the margins. The women whose labor built a movement. The artists whose visions did not serve the new state. The dissidents who wanted something different. The complex, contradictory human beings who could not be reduced to a single role in the official story. All of them have to be compressed into a finished narrative, or erased from it entirely.

What protects us from this erasure is the refusal to finish the archive. It is the commitment to preserving the fragments, the letters that do not fit, the photographs that complicate the official record, the voices that were not supposed to survive. An archive that stubbornly remains incomplete, that resists easy narration, that forces readers to sit with contradiction and ambiguity, is an archive that cannot be used for propaganda. It cannot be turned into a finished justification for power.

The fragments remain difficult. They remain unglamorous. They remain hard to turn into an inspiring national story. But they remain true. And in remaining true, they preserve the possibility of a more honest future: one that is not built on the lie of a finished past, but on the honest weight of everything that actually happened.

The Border That Would Not Close: Gloria Anzaldúa

To understand what the ethics of incompletion actually demands of a person, consider Gloria Anzaldúa, who spent her life refusing to let the wound close on command.

Anzaldúa grew up on the Texas-Mexico border, in a family that had worked that land for generations before a line was drawn across it and half of what they knew became a foreign country. She grew up between languages, between cultures, between the identity her community expected of her and the desires and visions she could not suppress. She was Chicana and queer and a mystic and a scholar and a poet, and none of those things resolved neatly into the others. The border was not just geography. It was the permanent condition of her inner life.

When she wrote Borderlands/La Frontera: The New Mestiza in 1987, she made a formal choice that most editors and academics found uncomfortable: she refused to finish it. Not in the sense that she ran out of time, but in the sense that the book's form enacts its argument. It moves between Spanish and English without translation, trusting the reader to sit with what they cannot fully parse. It moves between scholarly essay and poetry and memoir and myth without signaling which register is primary. It circles back. It contradicts itself. It leaves threads hanging. It refuses to arrive at a single, clean thesis that a reader can extract, summarize, and shelve.

This was not a failure of craft. Anzaldúa was a meticulous writer who revised obsessively. The incompletion was the point. She was writing about a wound, and she understood that a wound that closes too quickly does not heal. It festers. The honest thing, the only ethical thing, was to leave the border open on the page the way it remained open in her body. To force a tidy conclusion onto that material would have been to lie about what the border actually does to a person. It would have been to perform recovery she had not achieved and could not honestly claim.

She called the consciousness that lives in this unresolved space the new mestiza consciousness, a way of thinking that holds contradiction without demanding resolution. It is not comfortable. It is not efficient. It does not produce clean arguments or settled identities. But it is honest, and in its honesty it is more durable than any finished system. A person who has learned to live in the borderland, to tolerate the open wound, to work from the unresolved center, is a person who cannot be captured by any ideology that promises to finally sort them out.

Authoritarian systems cannot tolerate the open border. They need the wound to close, the identity to settle, the edges to be declared and defended. A person who insists on living in the unresolved space between categories is a person the sorting machine cannot process. Anzaldúa's refusal to finish, her insistence on leaving the book and the self permanently open to revision, was not a personal limitation. It was a structural refusal of the demand for completion that power depends on.

She died in 2004 with several projects unfinished. The scholars who came after her found the fragments and the drafts and the notes. Some tried to assemble them into finished arguments. Others understood that the fragments were already doing their work exactly as they were, the same way Benjamin's arcades kept speaking through their incompletion. The unfinished work was not waiting to be completed. It was complete in its incompletion. It was telling the truth about what it cost to live at the border, and the truth required the open form.

The Practice

Find one thing you are currently working on or worrying about that feels frustratingly incomplete. It could be a messy creative draft, a house project that never quite gets finished, or a complicated relationship dynamic that never quite resolves. It could be your understanding of a historical event, or your relationship to a loss that just refuses to fully heal.

Instead of stressing over how to force a quick ending, take a deep breath and give yourself permission to leave it unfinished for a moment. Tell yourself: this messiness is not a failure; it is honesty. Notice how much breathing room opens up when you stop demanding an immediate arrival.

Also notice the language you use when you talk about unfinished things in your life. Do you call them failures? Do you apologize for them? Do you rush to fix them before anyone else notices? What if you reframed the incompletion as integrity instead? I am still working on this. This is more complex than I can resolve right now, and that is okay. I do not have the answer yet, and I am learning as I go.

When you hear a political figure claim to have all the answers, all the finished solutions, the perfect plan, pause. Remember Benjamin's fragments. Remember that anyone claiming to have finished the work is lying. The only honest politics is one that admits: we are building this together, we will get it wrong, we will need to revise, and that is where our actual power lives.
