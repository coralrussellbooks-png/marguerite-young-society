Chapter 45

  • “Shall love be infinitely postponed? If infinitely postponed, then it is love.” may refer to Hegel. To deny something is to determine it, and to determine something is to negate it. Spinoza’s principle is “every determination is negation”. Hegel takes this one step further by suggesting that negation itself is a process of creation, and is even the essence of positive being. By denying a certain trait or characteristic, one is implicitly acknowledging the possibility of its opposite or the existence of a different reality. For Hegel, this power of the negative, or the act of negation, is what drives progress and development. Denying a particular concept allows for the possibility of a new one to emerge, incorporating both the original concept and its negation. This is often explained through the concept of the negation of the negation, where a denial of a denial leads to a new synthesis that transcends both the original thesis and the antithesis. From an existentialist perspective denying a predefined purpose or essence allows for the possibility of individuals to create their own meaning and existence. The line speaks to love as an infinite emotional state, one that transcends immediate gratification and endures through time, absence, and even death, resonating with the novel's larger themes of longing, loss, and the elusive nature of human connection.

Chapter 47

  • “he was agonized by attributing consciousness to everything” - may refer to panpsychism which suggests that a mind, or mind-like aspect, is a fundamental and widespread characteristic of reality. It says that the subjective experience associated with human consciousness exists, in some form, throughout the universe, even in its most basic elements. It has its roots in ancient Greek philosophy with Thales and Plato and later with Spinoza, Leibniz, and Schopenhauer. It reflects Mr. Spitzer's profound and troubling awareness of the pervasive presence of consciousness or life-like qualities in all things around him. This means that Mr. Spitzer experiences a kind of existential distress or mental anguish because he perceives consciousness, intention, or awareness not just in people but extending to inanimate objects, natural phenomena, and abstract concepts. He senses life, emotion, or meaning in things that are traditionally considered lifeless, such as clouds, stars, waves, or even shadows and this blurs the line between what is truly alive and what is not. This overwhelming and almost mystical perception causes him suffering because it challenges ordinary reality and makes his own sense of self and understanding of the world unstable and confusing. This agonizing attribution is part of Mr. Spitzer's broader struggle with reality, illusion, memory, and identity. It underlines his sensitivity to the ambiguities and mysteries of existence, where the boundaries between life and death, the living and the non-living, and reality and dream are not distinct. His mind is burdened by this expansive and unsettling sense of interconnectedness and animation of all things, which he cannot easily reconcile or control. The phrase captures Mr. Spitzer's deep philosophical and emotional turmoil as he contemplates a universe filled with consciousness beyond human life, contributing to the novel’s themes of illusion, perception, and the elusive nature of reality.

Chapter 49

  • Peter Abelard was a medieval French scholastic philosopher, leading logician, theologian, teacher, musician, composer, and poet. In philosophy he is celebrated for his proposed logical solution to the problem of universals via nominalism and conceptualism and his pioneering of intent in ethics. Often referred to as the "Descartes of the twelfth century", he is considered a forerunner of Rousseau, Kant, and Spinoza. He is sometimes credited as a chief forerunner of modern empiricism. In Catholic theology, he is best known for his development of the concept of limbo, and his introduction of the moral influence theory of atonement. He is considered (alongside Augustine) to be the most significant forerunner of the modern self-reflective autobiographer. He paved the way and set the tone for later epistolary novels and celebrity tell-alls with his publicly distributed letter, The History of My Calamities and public correspondence. In history and popular culture he is best known for his passionate, tragic love affair and intense philosophical exchange with his brilliant student and eventual wife, Héloïse d'Argenteuil. After they secretly married and went to different convents Heloise’s guardian had Abelard ambushed and castrated. Roscellinus and others made fun of him. He did father a son before that event.

Chapter 55

  • “sub specie aeternitatis, under the aspect of eternity, in its essential or universal form” This reflects a philosophical viewpoint regarding the nature of existence and identity beyond the limitations of time and individuality. This Latin phrase, traditionally used by philosophers like Spinoza, means to consider something "under the aspect of eternity," i.e., from a timeless, eternal perspective rather than a temporal or limited one. In the context of the chapter, it suggests looking at the self or existence not just as a particular individual bound by time, but as part of a universal, timeless whole. Mr. Spitzer is reflecting on his own identity and existence, recognizing that he is more than the sum of his temporal experiences. He contemplates that he may have been "retailored" or transformed in ways that transcend ordinary perception, and that his "essential or universal form" is something beyond his individual, physical self. The passage conveys his awareness of the fluidity and multiplicity of identity and existence, moving beyond the confines of linear time and fixed boundaries. It means considering oneself or one’s being from the perspective of eternity, seeing beyond the limited, changing self to a more fundamental, universal essence that is not confined by time or space.

Chapter 55

  • It is an honorific expression denoting what is considered to be universally and eternally true, without any reference to or dependence upon temporal facets of reality. The Latin phrase can be rendered in English as "from the perspective of the eternal". More loosely, it is commonly used to refer to an objective (or theoretically possible alternative) point of view. Spinoza's "eternal" perspective is reflected in his Ethics (Part V, Prop. XXIII, Scholium), where he applies Euclid's method (with the use of geometry) to philosophical inquiry, starting with God and nature, before moving to human emotions and the human intellect to reach an understanding of moral philosophy. By proceeding sub specie aeternitatis, Spinoza sought to arrive at an ethical theory that is as precise as Euclid's Elements. In the history of philosophy, this way of proceeding may be contrasted with Aristotle's. Aristotle's methodological differentiations in his "philosophy of human affairs" and his natural philosophy are grounded in the distinction between what is "better known to us" and things "better known in themselves", or what is "first for us" and what is "first by nature", a distinction that is deliberately discarded by Spinoza, and also by other modern philosophers.
