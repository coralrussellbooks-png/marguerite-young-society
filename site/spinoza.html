Definition from research

Chapter 24

  • “Spinoza and Shakespeare and Mrs. Sydington were already in the green”: Spinoza was a Dutch philosopher, author of the Ethics (1677). Shakespeare was a famous British playwright.

Chapter 45

  • “Shall love be infinitely postponed?”: may refer to Hegel. To deny something is to determine it, and to determine something is to negate it. Spinoza’s principle is “every determination is negation” (Spinoza, letter 50 to Jarig Jelles, 1674). Hegel takes this one step further by suggesting that negation itself is a process of creation, and is even the essence of positive being. By denying a certain trait or characteristic, one is implicitly acknowledging the possibility of its opposite or the existence of a different reality. For Hegel, this power of the negative, or the act of negation, is what drives progress and development. Denying a particular concept allows for the possibility of a new one to emerge, incorporating both the original concept and its negation. This is often explained through the concept of the negation of the negation, where a denial of a denial leads to a new synthesis that transcends both the original thesis and the antithesis. From an existentialist perspective denying a predefined purpose or essence allows for the possibility of individuals to create their own meaning and existence. The line speaks to love as an infinite emotional state, one that transcends immediate gratification and endures through time, absence, and even death, resonating with the novel’s larger themes of longing, loss, and the elusive nature of human connection.
  • “Perhaps body and soul were one.”: Spinoza held that mind and body are one and the same thing, conceived now under the attribute of thought, now under the attribute of extension (Ethics 2, proposition 21, scholium).

Chapter 47

  • “give up his assumption that all things which existed should be tangible”: Peron and Joachim as materialism and immaterialism. In philosophy, materialism says that everything that exists is fundamentally physical, composed of matter and energy. All phenomena, including consciousness, are ultimately reducible to physical processes. They point to the success of science in explaining the physical world and that consciousness can be explained by brain activity. Physicalism is similar in that everything can be explained by physics. Immaterialism believes there is an existence of entities or properties that are not physical, such as minds, consciousness, or spiritual realms. Non-physical entities or properties exist alongside or instead of the material world. Idealists believe that only minds and their perceptions are real, while the material world is a construction of the mind. Peron symbolizes the external, material world with its pleasures, follies, and impermanence, while Joachim represents the internal, immaterial realm of thought, memory, and spiritual seeking. Their contrast embodies the novel’s exploration of human existence as oscillating between these poles, neither fully attainable nor entirely separable, but always entwined in the complex dance of life and death. See Spinoza’s mind and body as one in the Chapter 45 resources.
  • “he was agonized by attributing consciousness to everything”: This may refer to panpsychism, the view that mind, or something like mind, is a fundamental and widespread feature of reality, present in some form even in its most basic elements. It has roots in ancient Greek philosophy, in Thales, who held that “all things are full of gods” (Aristotle, On the Soul 1.5), and in Plato, and later in Spinoza, Leibniz, and Schopenhauer. Mr. Spitzer suffers because he senses consciousness not only in people but in clouds, stars, waves, palings, and weathervanes, so that the line between the living and the lifeless blurs and his own sense of self grows unstable. It belongs to the novel’s larger struggle with reality, illusion, memory, and identity.

Chapter 49

  • Peter Abelard, the twelfth-century philosopher who argued the problem of universals against his teachers Roscellinus and William of Champeaux, is in the Chapter 52 resources, with Héloïse and their letters.

Chapter 55

  • “sub specie aeternitatis”: Sub specie aeternitatis is Latin for under the aspect of eternity or that which is universally and eternally true.
