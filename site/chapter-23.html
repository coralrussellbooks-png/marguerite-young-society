To All My Darlings

Chapter 23

On Chapter 23 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 22 · Home · Chapter 24 →

Summary

The breakfast continues. Miss MacIntosh denounces vain man, the Emperor Constantine, and Caesar, declares that history lies, and professes her faith in common sense and the middle way. Then she settles on Benjamin Franklin, whose experiments with lightning led to electric light and the end of privacy. Darkness, she argues, once protected marriage and the poor and the ugly. Vera realizes what the tirade is for: a woman who was just seen without her wig is arguing that no one should have to be seen. She talks of igloos and ice floes, of winter’s blast, of anything but herself. The chapter ends with Miss MacIntosh quoting Job and crying that the stars have always lit the heavens.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a profound meditation on the futility and vanity of human aspirations, as expressed through Miss MacIntosh’s reflections on Emperor Constantine and the “Slough of Despond.” The metaphor of life as a marshland filled with brambles and weeds sets a somber tone about the human condition and the closing of the heart from within, emphasizing internal barriers to understanding or connection.
  • This passage questions history and knowledge, asserting that “history lies, and geography is uncertain,” underscoring the unreliability of accepted truths and the elusive nature of identity in modern times.
  • Miss MacIntosh expresses a strong belief in common sense as the “middle brow,” the middle way, and the average life, asserting it as a rare and valuable quality. This passage reveals an internal tension between embracing ordinary life and the cosmic or remote issues that also preoccupy her.
  • The comparison of dwarves dreaming they are giants and giants dreaming they are dwarves illustrates the relativity of perception and ambition, hinting at the complexity of human identity.
  • One of the most extended and important passages is the critique of electric lights, attributed ironically to Benjamin Franklin, who paved “the way for electric lights” and thereby disrupted natural rhythms of day and night. Miss MacIntosh laments how electric illumination invades privacy, disturbs sleep, and ruins traditional human experiences such as marriage by exposing flaws that darkness once concealed.
  • This passage deeply mourns the loss of darkness, which once provided refuge and mystery, and contrasts it with the artificial brightness that keeps us “up all night like Roman soldiers,” contributing to social ills like divorce and loneliness.
  • The nostalgia for “bayberry candles” and natural starlight versus intrusive modern electric lights highlights a tension between technological progress and preservation of human dignity and natural order.
  • Miss MacIntosh reflects on the role of darkness in protecting the sacredness and mystery of marriage, allowing spouses to be “modest” and sheltered from immediate judgment. The electric light, by exposing all, undermines this protective veil, leading to disillusionment and social problems.
  • This meditation extends to a broader social commentary on how modern illumination disrupts traditional human relationships and perceptions.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s practical, unadorned manner of dress and demeanor symbolize her serious approach to life, rejecting frivolity, dreams, and illusions. She lives fully in the present reality, “for no other life,” and is skeptical of sentimental or metaphysical distractions.
  • This passage highlights her grounded nature and possibly her resistance or inability to engage with deeper or more cosmic concerns that transcend ordinary experience.
  • Vera reflects on how Miss MacIntosh’s frequent evocations of cosmic phenomena (creation, tides, stars) serve to mask her own human limitations and inner absences. These grand themes conceal what she does not wish to reveal about herself.
  • This passage suggests a tension between the vastness of universal mysteries and the intimate realities of human life.
  • The chapter ends with a meditation on the coming of winter, the changing of seasons, and the narrowing spectrum of colors in the natural world, symbolizing transition, loss, and endurance. The imagery of crystalline snow, dimmed colors, and the “luminous colors of the winter sea” evokes both harshness and beauty in nature’s cycles.
  • This passage metaphorically connects the natural world’s rhythms to human experience, emphasizing acceptance and preparation for inevitable change.
  • The concluding words question why light should be given to those in misery and darkness, affirming the enduring value of natural starlight and rejecting artificial electric light. Miss MacIntosh’s exclamation, “Electric lights, fiddlesticks! The stars have lighted the heavens from pole to pole,” reaffirms a preference for natural order over human tampering.
  • “These were dreams, impersonal and vast, and these did cover, as is said in gambling circles, her hand, for they did conceal her own limitations, the fact that there was something missing from the essential human, something she did not want me to see.” This is where Vera sees what the talk is for.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “The heart, my dear, was locked from the inside.” Whose heart?
  • “How should I know what is true unless I know what is false?” How does Miss MacIntosh answer her own question?
  • Why does she blame Franklin and Edison, of all people, this morning?

Characters

  • “She would say that she snored, not that she loved.” What is Vera beginning to understand?
  • “Of herself, of herself alone, what had she ever said that was personal and sad and revelatory?” What has she said instead?
  • She speaks of winter “now in the August sunlight.” Why skip the autumn?

Connections

  • Chapter 19’s door was “barred from the inside.” What else is locked from the inside here?
  • She blamed Constantine for confusing the days (Chapter 22) and now blames Franklin for confusing the nights. What is she defending?
  • “A man had married a man, and a woman had married a woman, and no one had been the wiser.” How does that line speak to the night of Chapters 17 and 18?

Reflection

  • Is there a right to be unseen?
  • Does darkness protect love, or hide it?
  • What do we talk about so that we don’t have to talk about ourselves?

Research: Characters

Miss MacIntosh

  • Miss MacIntosh is portrayed as fiercely grounded in what she calls “common sense,” valuing the “middle brow,” the “middle way,” and the “average life,” a disposition she prides herself on as her “greatest love,” even if others despise it.
  • This common sense coexists with a pervasive skepticism toward accepted truths such as history, geography, and law, which she dismisses as unstable, uncertain, or outright falsehoods.
  • She challenges grand narratives, like that of Caesar crossing the Rubicon or the veracity of historical figures, suggesting that time has neither accumulated nor taught wisdom.
  • This skeptical realism reveals a character who questions the foundations of knowledge and human pretensions but does so without despair; rather, she seems to accept human limitations with a certain weary humor and irony.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s discourse centers on her criticism of modern inventions, especially electric lights. She associates electricity, notably Benjamin Franklin’s experiments, as a disruptive force that has destroyed natural rhythms, privacy, and the quietness of night. She laments electric lights for erasing darkness, which she sees as essential for sleep, privacy, and even mystery in human relationships, particularly marriage. Electric lights, she argues, expose flaws and imperfections that were once safely hidden in darkness, thereby damaging marriages and social harmony.
  • Her detailed critique extends to “electric trains,” “electric irons,” “fluorescent lamps,” and “two-way lighting systems” as unnatural intrusions into life’s natural order.
  • This stance encapsulates her resistance to tampering with “old, damaged creation” and her preference for simpler, older ways such as “bayberry candles,” “snuffers,” and natural starlight. Her view is that the modern world’s brightness is a form of extravagant excess that impoverishes human experience and dignity.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s practical nature is emphasized by descriptions of her appearance and habits. She dresses plainly, in worn, practical clothing: a grey baseball sweater with a ravelling sleeve, stout shoes, and a patched salt-and-pepper tweed coat.
  • She carries her “rigging” everywhere, including an old black umbrella likened to a sail and a whale-boned ice-breaker corset, symbolizing her preparedness and no-nonsense approach to life on a “rough shore.”
  • She rejects frivolities such as filmy dresses or adornments, even on significant occasions like weddings or funerals, signaling her seriousness and focus on the immediate, tangible world rather than on dreams or illusions.
  • Despite her talk of cosmic and remote issues like glaciers, submerged continents, and the first days of creation, Miss MacIntosh reveals little of her personal emotions or vulnerabilities.
  • She claims her consciousness ceases during sleep and that she cannot remember dreams, not even her own, suggesting a detachment from inner emotional life or a guardedness.
  • Her conscience “kept guard” as a “lonely vigil,” indicating solitude and perhaps a self-imposed emotional isolation.
  • She evades personal disclosures and remains impassive, almost austere, in her demeanor.
  • This emotional reserve contrasts with the vastness of the cosmic themes she invokes, highlighting a tension between the grand and the personal.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s reflections often shift from the mundane to the cosmic and from daily life and societal critiques to the “first starfish upon the first beach” and “the first moon drawing the first tide.” This oscillation suggests that she perceives human life as part of a vast, impersonal natural order that transcends immediate existence.
  • She uses cosmic imagery to mask what she does not want to reveal: her fears, losses, or inner life.
  • This duality makes her a somewhat enigmatic figure, connecting the earthly and the universal while remaining personally elusive.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s thoughts about the coming winter, the colorlessness of the world under snow and ice, and the need to prepare with warm clothing and supplies reveal a keen awareness of mortality and the cycles of life.
  • Winter symbolizes death or hardship, and her sober preparation indicates acceptance rather than fear. Yet, she also acknowledges the subtle beauty of winter’s muted colors, reflecting a nuanced appreciation of life’s phases and transitions.
  • Her concern with time’s passing, the loss inherent in change, and the persistence of “dimmed colors” in hardship underscore a mature, grounded philosophy.
  • Throughout her discourse, Miss MacIntosh repeatedly condemns human vanity, illusions of grandeur, and false hopes.
  • She refers to “vain man” in multiple historical and contemporary contexts, from Emperor Constantine and Caesar to Benjamin Franklin, pointing out the futility of human pretensions to immortality or mastery.
  • She warns against illusions, falsehoods that may mask other truths, and the human tendency to stumble blindly in the “broad daylight which God has lighted.”
  • Her repeated invocation of vanity and illusion underscores a theme of human limitation and the importance of humility and realism.

Vera Cartwheel

  • Notes that Miss MacIntosh never speaks personally or reveals sadness or revelation about herself.
  • Senses that the grand themes Miss MacIntosh talks about serve to conceal her own limitations and personal absence.
  • Wonders and questions Miss MacIntosh’s answers.
  • Realizes for the first time that Miss MacIntosh has not shared any personal history with her.
  • Questions Miss MacIntosh’s insistence on learning about the past and “common sense” and that it might all be false, imaginary, and not real.

Research: Themes

Illusion and Reality

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Miss MacIntosh opens with a critique of “vain man” and the illusions that humans harbor, such as the belief in immortality or the certainty of history.
  • She questions the reliability of history and geography, suggesting that what we accept as truth may be unstable or false.
  • The idea that truth cannot be known without understanding falsehood is emphasized.

Common Sense and the Middle Way

See: Order, Chaos, and Discipline

  • She values common sense and the “middle brow,” the average life, as a guiding principle.
  • Despite her philosophical musings on vast, cosmic issues, she prides herself on practicality and sensibility.

Critique of Modern Technology: Electric Lights

See: Light and Darkness

  • A significant portion of the chapter is devoted to a critical view of electric lighting and its consequences.
  • Miss MacIntosh blames Benjamin Franklin for pioneering electricity, which has disrupted natural cycles of day and night.
  • Electric lights are portrayed as a destroyer of privacy, natural rhythms (sleep and work), and even marital harmony.
  • She laments the loss of darkness, which she sees as essential for personal privacy and the natural order.
  • The chapter contrasts the artificial glare of electric lights with the natural, gentle light of stars and candles used by ancestors.

Historical and Cultural Reflections

See: Memory and the Past · Society, Class, and Race

  • References to historical figures such as Emperor Constantine, Benjamin Franklin, and President Garfield serve as symbols of human folly or misguided progress.
  • The chapter also evokes images from ancient times and myths (e.g., Helen of Troy, Ulysses) to illustrate timeless human experiences affected by modern changes.

Human Relationships and Privacy

See: Isolation and Connection

  • The darkness once allowed spouses to be ignorant of each other’s full appearance or flaws, preserving marital bliss.
  • Electric lighting exposes all imperfections, potentially leading to divorces and the breakdown of relationships.
  • This theme extends to a broader loss of innocence and the protective veil of ignorance.

Nature and the Cosmic Order

See: Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos · Order, Chaos, and Discipline

  • The novel references natural phenomena such as glaciers, tides, northern stars, and the changing seasons.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s talk about ice floes, winter preparations, and the Arctic environment symbolizes the fragility and impermanence of existence.
  • There is a tension between the cosmic, the remote, and the immediate, everyday life.

Mortality and the Passage of Time

See: Death and Mortality · Time

  • The inevitability of winter and death is anticipated, reflecting on the cycles of life.
  • Despite the harshness of winter, there is beauty in its muted colors and quiet persistence.
  • The chapter ends with a meditation on light and darkness as metaphors for life, suffering, and divine presence.

Research: Resources

  • “a Slough of Despond this life is”: “The slough of Despond,” where Christian sinks under the weight of his sins in Bunyan’s The Pilgrim’s Progress (1678). Slough of Despond is an area in the book Pilgrim’s Progress that Miss MacIntosh reads more than the Bible. See the Chapter 3 resources.
  • “The heart, my dear, was locked from the inside”: Madge and Homer argued about Josh being locked in the closet and dying at the beginning of the book (Chapter 7). Old Josh, the barkeeper, died in a coat closet that Madge insisted “was locked from the outside.” Miss MacIntosh’s heart is locked from the inside. See Madge Capehorn, Homer Capehorn, and the Chapter 7 resources.
  • “great Caesar crossing the Rubicon”: The phrase “crossing the Rubicon” is an idiom that means “passing a point of no return.” Its meaning comes from an allusion to the crossing of the river Rubicon from the north by Julius Caesar in early January 49 BC. It symbolizes a critical turning point or a decisive act that cannot be undone.
  • “Who knows that he crossed the Rubicon or that there was a Caesar?”: Richard Whately’s satire Historic Doubts Relative to Napoleon Buonaparte (1819) argued, with the same logic, that Napoleon could not be proved to have existed.
  • “Ignorance of the law is no excuse”: The Roman maxim “ignorantia juris non excusat.”
  • “We make mountains of molehills”: In print by 1548 (Nicholas Udall). See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “We stumble in the broad daylight which God has lighted”: “We stumble at noonday as in the night; we are in desolate places as dead men” (Isaiah 59:10), and “God said, Let there be light” (Genesis 1:3). It encapsulates a moment or idea of transcendence, an acknowledgment that even in the bleakest moments of human experience or the “darkness” of suffering and death there is a possibility of divine light or spiritual awakening. It is a poetic way of expressing hope, mystery, and the complex interplay of life and death that Vera contemplates. See The Bible.
  • “these falsehoods which, though false, may be the face of another truth”: Even though something is demonstrably false, it can still hold a certain level of truth or reveal a deeper underlying reality, often by acting as a metaphor or symbolic representation of a complex idea, even if the literal details are inaccurate. This concept is often explored in art and literature where fictional narratives can convey profound truths about human nature or society.
  • “Poor mortals all and blind as bats in a cave”: This may echo Plato’s prisoners of the cave, who take shadows for the real (Republic 7). See Plato and Platonism.
  • “Deliver not my soul to a turtle or a crab”: “O deliver not the soul of thy turtledove unto the multitude of the wicked” (Psalm 74:19). Young turns the turtledove into a turtle. See The Bible.
  • “Am I my brother’s keeper?”: Genesis 4:9. Miss MacIntosh asks what Mr. Spitzer might. It resonates with his struggle with his sense of duty, guilt, and responsibility for his brother’s legacy and the lives entangled in their shared history. The question also connects to broader existential concerns about individual responsibility in a chaotic and often indifferent world. The novel explores themes of illusion, memory, and the blurred boundaries between life and death, self and other. It asks whether one is truly responsible for the fate, suffering, or salvation of another, particularly a close other like a brother, and by extension, for others in one’s life or society. It highlights the novel’s exploration of moral responsibility, the limits of that responsibility, and the intimate yet often painful ties that bind individuals to one another in a world marked by loss, deception, and uncertain reality. See Genesis and Joachim Spitzer.
  • “One can be an epicure”: Epicurus lived chiefly on bread and water: “Send me a little pot of cheese, that when I like I may fare sumptuously” (Diogenes Laertius 10.11). See Epicureanism.
  • “One can enjoy too much one’s poverty, in fact, and make a great business of it”: Seneca warns against showy poverty, “a rough cloak, unkempt hair, a slovenly beard” (Letters to Lucilius 5). Seneca was a leading Roman Stoic. The school was founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC. Seneca’s works discuss both ethical theory and practical advice, and Seneca stresses that both parts are distinct but interdependent. Seneca regards philosophy as a balm for the wounds of life. The destructive passions, especially anger and grief, must be uprooted, or moderated according to reason. He discusses the relative merits of contemplative life and active life, and he considers it important to confront one’s own mortality and be able to face death. One must be willing to practice poverty and use wealth properly, and he writes about favours, clemency, the importance of friendship, and the need to benefit others. The universe is governed for the best by a rational providence, and this must be reconciled with acceptance of adversity. See Stoicism.
  • “Dwarves may dream that they are giants”: Gulliver is a giant in Lilliput and a dwarf in Brobdingnag (Swift, Gulliver’s Travels, 1726).
  • “What was so rare a thing as common sense”: Thomas Paine’s polemical pamphlet Common Sense (1776) has been described as the most influential political pamphlet of the 18th century, affecting both the American and French revolutions. Today, the concept of common sense, and how it should best be used, remains linked to many of the most perennial topics in epistemology and ethics, with special focus often directed at the philosophy of the modern social sciences. See Aristotle, Empiricism (Locke, Berkeley, Hume), Descartes and Dualism, and William James and Pragmatism.
    • The philosophy of common sense is a set of beliefs that challenge skepticism and subjective idealism. It’s based on the idea that there is a basic ability to perceive and judge that most people share. Aristotle first used the term, for the faculty that joins the five senses (On the Soul, Book 3). It was also followed by John Locke, Thomas Reid, and G. E. Moore. Basic tenets are sensations are more than just ideas or subjective impressions; beliefs are based on common sense and reason; people of all levels of education can use common sense; starting off on the wrong path can lead to absurdities.
    • Nous is a concept from classical philosophy, sometimes equated to intellect or intelligence, for the faculty of the human mind necessary for understanding what is true or real.
    • Thomas Reid wrote common sense works as such: If all men observe an item and believe the same qualities about that item, then the knowledge of that item is universally true. It is common knowledge, which without explanation is held true by other people; so, what is universally seen is universally believed. Charles Sanders Peirce later wrote (“Some Consequences of Four Incapacities,” 1868), “The real, then, is that which, sooner or later, information and reasoning would finally result in, and which is therefore independent of the vagaries of me and you. Thus, the very origin of the conception of reality shows that this conception essentially involves the notion of a community, without definite limits, and capable of a definite increase of knowledge.” The combination of the same ideas, of a thing, by multiple people, is what confirms the reality of an object. Reid also believes that the philosophers of his time exaggerated what is truly real. Where most philosophers believe that what we see is not fully what that thing is, for example, Descartes, Reid counters this argument simply by stating that “such a hypothesis is no more likely to be true than the common-sensical belief that the world is much the way we perceive it to be.” Reid’s own book is An Inquiry into the Human Mind on the Principles of Common Sense (1764).
  • “the middle brow, the middle way”: “Middlebrow” was new in Young’s day. Russell Lynes’s “Highbrow, Lowbrow, Middlebrow” ran in Harper’s in 1949. The middle way is Aristotle’s mean, virtue as the middle between two extremes (Nicomachean Ethics 2.6). See Aristotle.
  • “Vain man, Benjamin Franklin!”: Benjamin Franklin’s philosophy was practical, optimistic, and focused on self-improvement. He believed that a republic could only succeed if its people were virtuous and had the right temper. His ideas included: practicality, through hard work and self-discipline and improving their character through effort and self-control; optimism, as a supporter of the Enlightenment and the power of reason and scientific discovery people could improve their lives through education, community service, and virtuous living; self-improvement, people could improve their character through effort and self-control and that people should strive to live virtuous and purposeful lives; political philosophy, the importance of freedom of the press, religion, education, pragmatism, and the republic would flourish if the freedoms were secured by the US Constitution combined with wisdom and virtue. His lightning rod (1752) drew the heavens’ fire down a wire into the ground. See American Writers.
  • “old President Garfield had seen well enough without them”: Garfield is mentioned in the context of a “time” when people saw well enough without electric lights, implying that even a prominent historical figure like Garfield did not require such artificial illumination to live effectively or happily. The White House was wired for electricity in 1891, under Benjamin Harrison, ten years after Garfield died of an assassin’s bullet.
  • “Even Skid Row”: The name comes from Seattle’s Skid Road (1850s), where logs were skidded down to the mill. It came to mean the poor quarter of any city.
  • “About the only advantage is midnight baseball”: The first major league night game was played in Cincinnati on May 24, 1935. President Roosevelt pressed a button in Washington to turn on the lights.
  • “it was he who had written that early to bed makes us wise”: “Early to bed and early to rise, makes a man healthy, wealthy, and wise” (Poor Richard, 1735), as in Chapter 15. See American Writers and the Chapter 15 resources.
    • While Benjamin Franklin is often mistakenly credited with inventing daylight saving time, he actually penned a satirical essay in 1784 suggesting Parisians wake up earlier to save on candles, not proposing a change to clocks. In 1784, while serving as an American envoy in Paris, Franklin wrote a satirical essay titled “An Economical Project” published in the Journal de Paris. The essay suggested that Parisians could save money on candles and lamp oil by waking up earlier and making the most of the natural daylight. Franklin’s proposal was about changing sleep schedules, not changing the time on clocks, which is what modern daylight saving time involves. Because of this essay, Franklin is often mistakenly credited with inventing daylight saving time, but he was only suggesting a change in sleep habits, not the time itself. The modern concept of daylight saving time, which involves changing clocks, originated later, with some attributing the idea to George Hudson, an entomologist from New Zealand, in 1895. The concept of daylight saving time gained traction during World War I as a way to conserve energy, and it was formally adopted in the US in 1918.
  • “had always brought gold in their mouths”: The German proverb “Morgenstunde hat Gold im Munde,” “the morning hour has gold in its mouth.” See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “Bayberry candles were good enough for our fathers to see by”: The bayberry custom is to burn a candle on Christmas Eve or New Year’s Eve to bring good luck, wealth, and health. It is said to have originated in Colonial America. “A bayberry candle burned to the socket brings food to the larder and gold to the pocket.” See the Chapter 15 resources.
  • “Was Helen of Troy ever disturbed by electric lights”: Helen of Troy in Greek mythology was said to be the most beautiful woman in the world. “Was this the face that launched a thousand ships?” (Marlowe, Doctor Faustus, 1604). Young makes it “caused a thousand ships to sail.” See Greek Mythology.
  • “Ulysses, if ever he had seen Penelope under electric lights”: Ulysses is the Roman name for Odysseus, a hero in ancient Greek literature. Penelope is the wife of Ulysses and is an example of a faithful wife because she waits for her husband to return and tricks suitors who wish to marry her. Penelope wove a shroud by day and unwove it by night to put off her suitors (Odyssey 2). See Homer’s Epics.
  • “of marriage, the sacred union”: Women’s rights in the US were restricted during marriage: married women were legally subordinate to their husbands; they could not own property, keep their own wages, or enter into contracts; they had no rights to their children. Under coverture, “the husband and wife are one person in law” (Blackstone, 1765). New York’s Married Women’s Property Act came in 1848. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “an ugly woman was safe in her husband’s arms”: Only women are called ugly, because the feminine beauty ideal in every country holds that beauty is a woman’s most important asset. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “all these dreadful fluorescent lamps”: Fluorescent lamps went on sale in 1938 and were shown at the New York World’s Fair of 1939, which helps date this breakfast.
  • “What business had he flying a kite”: Franklin’s kite experiment of June 1752 showed that lightning is electricity. See American Writers.
  • “to meddle and tamper with creation the way God left it last Saturday”: Miss MacIntosh’s creation week of Chapter 22, when God “had stopped” on Saturday. See the Chapter 22 resources.
  • “The wisest men are never married”: Most philosophers were in fact childless and/or never married. They did not have progeny or like Descartes, had an infant that died. To the current day philosophers are 75% men and 25% women.
    • There are women philosophers who have made significant contributions to the field: Hypatia, Mary Wollstonecraft, Simone de Beauvoir, Hannah Arendt, and G.E.M. Anscombe. They represent a wide range of eras and philosophical fields, from ancient Greece to modern philosophy, covering topics like politics, ethics, and metaphysics.
  • “A fine thing, she would say to Thomas Edison”: In the US there was a serious debate about which to use, AC or DC current, and that may be what is referred to in the novel. The war of the currents was a series of events surrounding the introduction of competing electric power transmission systems in the late 1880s and early 1890s. It grew out of two lighting systems developed in the late 1870s and early 1880s: arc lamp street lighting running on high-voltage alternating current (AC), and large-scale low-voltage direct current (DC) indoor incandescent lighting being marketed by Thomas Edison’s company. In 1886, the Edison system was faced with new competition: an alternating current system initially introduced by George Westinghouse’s company that used transformers to step down from a high voltage so AC could be used for indoor lighting. Using high voltage allowed an AC system to transmit power over longer distances from more efficient large central generating stations. As the use of AC spread rapidly with other companies deploying their own systems, the Edison Electric Light Company claimed in early 1888 that high voltages used in an alternating current system were hazardous, and that the design was inferior to, and infringed on the patents behind, their direct current system. In the spring of 1888, a media furor arose over electrical fatalities caused by pole-mounted high-voltage AC lines, attributed to the greed and callousness of the arc lighting companies that operated them. In June of that year Harold P. Brown, a New York electrical engineer, claimed the AC-based lighting companies were putting the public at risk using high-voltage systems installed in a slipshod manner. Brown also claimed that alternating current was more dangerous than direct current and tried to prove this by publicly killing animals with both currents, with technical assistance from Edison Electric. The Edison company and Brown colluded further in their parallel goals to limit the use of AC with attempts to push through legislation to severely limit AC installations and voltages. Both also colluded with Westinghouse’s chief AC rival, the Thomson-Houston Electric Company, to make sure the first electric chair was powered by a Westinghouse AC generator. By the early 1890s, the war was winding down. Further deaths caused by AC lines in New York City forced electric companies to fix safety problems. Thomas Edison no longer controlled Edison Electric, and subsidiary companies were beginning to add AC to the systems they were building. Mergers reduced competition between companies, including the merger of Edison Electric with their largest competitor, Thomson-Houston, forming General Electric in 1892. Edison Electric’s merger with their chief alternating current rival brought an end to the war of the currents and created a new company that now controlled three quarters of the US electrical business. Westinghouse won the bid to supply electrical power for the World’s Columbian Exposition in 1893 and won the major part of the contract to build Niagara Falls hydroelectric project later that year (partially splitting the contract with General Electric) (Wikipedia, “War of the currents”).
  • “keeping us up all night like Roman soldiers”: The Roman army divided the night into four watches.
  • “a man had married a man, and a woman had married a woman, and no one had been the wiser”: Womanless marriages were staged as community events and fundraisers. It is a “most unique and attractive entertainment,” the Houston Post reported on May 27, 1917. “Some of the most prominent men in the city will be in it. It is filled with fun from beginning to end and every city or town in which it has been given there have been record breaking audiences, even standing room being at a premium.” In nineteenth-century New England, two women living together as a couple were said to be in a “Boston marriage.” See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “is Baffin Bay?”: Baffin Bay is located between Baffin Island and the west coast of Greenland. It is named for William Baffin, who charted it in 1616. See Exploration and the Poles.
  • “It’s always well”: An igloo (Inuktitut iglu, “house”) is built of snow blocks. See Exploration and the Poles and Snow, Ice, and Winter.
  • “a new broom sweeps clean”: John Heywood’s proverb (1546). See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “fourteen tall white candles”: The candles of Vera’s fourteenth birthday. See the Chapter 9 resources.
  • “where the dead souls wandered like flames”: This may recall Dante’s flames, each wrapping a soul, among them Ulysses (Inferno 26). See Dante.
  • “the latitude and longitude in which may be located the Austral Islands, ice-locked Baffin Bay or the Magnetic Pole”: The Austral Islands are in French Polynesia. James Clark Ross reached the North Magnetic Pole in 1831. See Exploration and the Poles.
  • “dancing like mad dervishes”: The Mevlevi, the whirling dervishes of Turkey, pray by turning.
  • “in the August sunlight”: A clue to the time of these events: August.
  • “no Indian summer like the Indian summer of the human soul”: “Indian summer,” a warm spell after the first frost, is an American phrase, in print by 1778.
  • “no time for the migrations of passenger birds”: The passenger pigeon, once counted in billions, died out with Martha, the last of them, in 1914.
  • “winter wore its coat of many colors”: Joseph’s “coat of many colours” (Genesis 37:3). See Genesis.
  • “be given to a man who is in misery and whose way is darkness and whom God hath hedged in”: “Wherefore is light given to him that is in misery” and “Why is light given to a man whose way is hid, and whom God hath hedged in?” (Job 3:20, 23). See The Book of Job.
    • The last paragraph may refer to the time Miss MacIntosh worked in a house for the blind.

Vocabulary

Aberration
A departure from what is normal or expected.
Astral
Having to do with the stars.
Bayberry
A shrub of the Atlantic coast whose waxy berries were boiled to make fragrant candles.
Corset
A close-fitting undergarment, stiffened with whalebone or steel and laced tight, worn to shape the waist.
Crystalline
Clear and sparkling, like crystal.
Cuttlefish
A sea creature related to the squid that can change color and squirt ink.
Dervish
A member of a Sufi order known for whirling in worship.
Drove
A herd or flock driven or moving together.
Epicure
One who takes refined pleasure in food and drink.
Festering
Rotting and oozing; growing more corrupt.
Fluorescent
Giving off light through a glowing gas in a tube.
Fuliginous
Sooty; dusky.
Glow-worm
A beetle whose wingless female and larvae give off a greenish light.
Ice floe
A sheet of floating ice.
Igloo
An Inuit house built of snow blocks.
Inscrutable
Impossible to understand or interpret.
Iridescence
A rainbow shimmer of colors that change with the angle of view.
Lugubrious
Mournful; gloomy.
Mauve
A pale purple.
Middlebrow
Of conventional, middling taste.
Monsoon
A seasonal wind in South Asia that brings months of heavy rain.
Mossback
An old-fashioned person; literally, an old turtle with moss on its back.
Oblivion
The state of being forgotten, or of being unaware of everything.
Phantasmagoric
Like a phantasmagoria, a shifting series of dreamlike or nightmarish images. A phantasmagoria was an eighteenth-century magic-lantern ghost show.
Precipitous
Very steep; headlong.
Rubicon
A small river in northern Italy. Julius Caesar crossed it with his army in 49 BC, starting a civil war, so “crossing the Rubicon” means passing a point of no return. See Crossing the Rubicon (Ch 23).
Salt-and-pepper
Of cloth or hair: flecked with black and white.
Snuffers
A scissor-like tool for trimming or putting out a candle.
Spruce
An evergreen tree of northern forests.
Tarpaulin
A heavy waterproof cloth for covering things.
Transcended
Went beyond.
Tweed
A rough woolen cloth, often of mixed colors, first woven in Scotland.
Vainglorious
Excessively proud and boastful.
Vigil
A period of staying awake to keep watch, especially at the bedside of someone sick or dying.
Whalebone
Baleen, the flexible plates from the mouths of certain whales, once used to stiffen corsets.
