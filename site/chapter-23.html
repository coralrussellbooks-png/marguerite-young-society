Summary

The breakfast continues. Miss MacIntosh denounces vain man, the Emperor Constantine, and Caesar, declares that history lies, and professes her faith in common sense and the middle way. Then she settles on Benjamin Franklin, whose experiments with lightning led to electric light and the end of privacy. Darkness, she argues, once protected marriage and the poor and the ugly. Vera realizes what the tirade is for: a woman who was just seen without her wig is arguing that no one should have to be seen. The chapter ends with Miss MacIntosh quoting Job and crying that the stars have always lit the heavens.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a profound meditation on the futility and vanity of human aspirations, as expressed through the character’s reflections on Emperor Constantine and the "Slough of Despond." The metaphor of life as a marshland filled with brambles and weeds sets a somber tone about the human condition and the closing of the heart from within, emphasizing internal barriers to understanding or connection.
  • This passage questions history and knowledge, asserting that "history lies, and geography is uncertain," underscoring the unreliability of accepted truths and the elusive nature of identity in modern times.
  • The character expresses a strong belief in common sense as the "middle brow," the middle way, and the average life, asserting it as a rare and valuable quality. This passage reveals an internal tension between embracing ordinary life and the cosmic or remote issues that also preoccupy her.
  • The comparison of dwarves dreaming they are giants and giants dreaming they are dwarves illustrates the relativity of perception and ambition, hinting at the complexity of human identity.
  • One of the most extended and important passages is the critique of electric lights, attributed ironically to Benjamin Franklin, who paved "the way for electric lights" and thereby disrupted natural rhythms of day and night. The character laments how electric illumination invades privacy, disturbs sleep, and ruins traditional human experiences such as marriage by exposing flaws that darkness once concealed.
  • This passage deeply mourns the loss of darkness, which once provided refuge and mystery, and contrasts it with the artificial brightness that "keeping us up all night like Roman soldiers," contributing to social ills like divorce and loneliness.
  • The nostalgia for "bayberry candles" and natural starlight versus intrusive modern electric lights highlights a tension between technological progress and preservation of human dignity and natural order.
  • The character reflects on the role of darkness in protecting the sacredness and mystery of marriage, allowing spouses to be "modest" and sheltered from immediate judgment. The electric light, by exposing all, undermines this protective veil, leading to disillusionment and social problems.
  • This meditation extends to a broader social commentary on how modern illumination disrupts traditional human relationships and perceptions.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s practical, unadorned manner of dress and demeanor symbolize her serious approach to life, rejecting frivolity, dreams, and illusions. She lives fully in the present reality, "for no other life," and is skeptical of sentimental or metaphysical distractions.
  • This passage highlights her grounded nature and possibly her resistance or inability to engage with deeper or more cosmic concerns that transcend ordinary experience.
  • Vera reflects on how Miss MacIntosh’s frequent evocations of cosmic phenomena (creation, tides, stars) serve to mask her own human limitations and inner absences. These grand themes conceal what she does not wish to reveal about herself.
  • This passage suggests a tension between the vastness of universal mysteries and the intimate realities of human life.
  • The chapter ends with a meditation on the coming of winter, the changing of seasons, and the narrowing spectrum of colors in the natural world, symbolizing transition, loss, and endurance. The imagery of crystalline snow, dimmed colors, and the "luminous colors of the winter sea" evokes both harshness and beauty in nature’s cycles.
  • This passage metaphorically connects the natural world’s rhythms to human experience, emphasizing acceptance and preparation for inevitable change.
  • The concluding words question why light should be given to those in misery and darkness, affirming the enduring value of natural starlight and rejecting artificial electric light. The character’s exclamation, "Electric lights, fiddlesticks! The stars have lighted the heavens from pole to pole," reaffirms a preference for natural order over human tampering.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Miss MacIntosh

  • Miss MacIntosh is portrayed as fiercely grounded in what she calls "common sense," valuing the "middle brow," the "middle way," and the "average life", a disposition she prides herself on as her "greatest love," even if others despise it.
  • This common sense coexists with a pervasive skepticism toward accepted truths such as history, geography, and law, which she dismisses as unstable, uncertain, or outright falsehoods.
  • She challenges grand narratives, like that of Caesar crossing the Rubicon or the veracity of historical figures, suggesting that time has neither accumulated nor taught wisdom.
  • This skeptical realism reveals a character who questions the foundations of knowledge and human pretensions but does so without despair; rather, she seems to accept human limitations with a certain weary humor and irony.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s discourse centers on her criticism of modern inventions, especially electric lights. She associates electricity, notably Benjamin Franklin’s experiments, as a disruptive force that has destroyed natural rhythms, privacy, and the quietness of night. She laments electric lights for erasing darkness, which she sees as essential for sleep, privacy, and even mystery in human relationships, particularly marriage. Electric lights, she argues, expose flaws and imperfections that were once safely hidden in darkness, thereby damaging marriages and social harmony.
  • Her detailed critique extends to "electric trains," "electric irons," "fluorescent lamps," and "two-way lighting systems" as unnatural intrusions into life’s natural order.
  • This stance encapsulates her resistance to tampering with "old, damaged creation" and her preference for simpler, older ways such as "bayberry candles," "snuffers," and natural starlight. Her view is that the modern world’s brightness is a form of extravagant excess that impoverishes human experience and dignity.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s practical nature is emphasized by descriptions of her appearance and habits. She dresses plainly, in worn, practical clothing: a grey baseball sweater with a ravelling sleeve, stout shoes, and a patched salt-and-pepper tweed coat.
  • She carries her "rigging" everywhere, including an old black umbrella likened to a sail and a whale-boned ice-breaker corset, symbolizing her preparedness and no-nonsense approach to life on a “rough shore.”
  • She rejects frivolities such as filmy dresses or adornments, even on significant occasions like weddings or funerals, signaling her seriousness and focus on the immediate, tangible world rather than on dreams or illusions.
  • Despite her talk of cosmic and remote issues like glaciers, submerged continents, and the first days of creation, Miss MacIntosh reveals little of her personal emotions or vulnerabilities.
  • She claims her consciousness ceases during sleep and that she cannot remember dreams, not even her own, suggesting a detachment from inner emotional life or a guardedness.
  • Her conscience "kept guard" as a "lonely vigil," indicating solitude and perhaps a self-imposed emotional isolation.
  • She evades personal disclosures and remains impassive, almost austere, in her demeanor.
  • This emotional reserve contrasts with the vastness of the cosmic themes she invokes, highlighting a tension between the grand and the personal.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s reflections often shift from the mundane to the cosmic and from daily life and societal critiques to the “first starfish upon the first beach” and “the first moon drawing the first tide.” This oscillation suggests that she perceives human life as part of a vast, impersonal natural order that transcends immediate existence.
  • She uses cosmic imagery to mask what she does not want to reveal: her fears, losses, or inner life.
  • This duality makes her a somewhat enigmatic figure, connecting the earthly and the universal while remaining personally elusive.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s thoughts about the coming winter, the colorlessness of the world under snow and ice, and the need to prepare with warm clothing and supplies reveal a keen awareness of mortality and the cycles of life.
  • Winter symbolizes death or hardship, and her sober preparation indicates acceptance rather than fear. Yet, she also acknowledges the subtle beauty of winter’s muted colors, reflecting a nuanced appreciation of life’s phases and transitions.
  • Her concern with time’s passing, the loss inherent in change, and the persistence of “dimmed colors” in hardship underscore a mature, grounded philosophy.
  • Throughout her discourse, Miss MacIntosh repeatedly condemns human vanity, illusions of grandeur, and false hopes.
  • She refers to "vain man" in multiple historical and contemporary contexts, from Emperor Constantine and Caesar to Benjamin Franklin, pointing out the futility of human pretensions to immortality or mastery.
  • She warns against illusions, falsehoods that may mask other truths, and the human tendency to stumble blindly in the "broad daylight which God has lighted."
  • Her repeated invocation of vanity and illusion underscores a theme of human limitation and the importance of humility and realism.

  • Thomas Reid wrote common sense works as such: If all men observe an item and believe the same qualities about that item, then the knowledge of that item is universally true. It is common knowledge, which without explanation is held true by other people; so, what is universally seen is universally believed. "The real, then, is that which, sooner or later, information and reasoning would finally result in, and which is therefore independent of the vagaries of me and you. Thus, the very origin of the conception of reality shows that this conception essentially involves the notion of a community, without definite limits, and capable of a definite increase of knowledge". The combination of the same ideas, of a thing, by multiple people, is what confirms the reality of an object. Reid also believes that the philosophers of his time exaggerated what is truly real. Where most philosophers believe that what we see is not fully what that thing is, for example, Descartes, Reid counters this argument simply by stating that "such a hypothesis is no more likely to be true than the common-sensical belief that the world is much the way we perceive it to be".   • War of the Currents - in the US there was a serious debate about which to use, AC or DC current, and that may be what is referred to in the novel The war of the currents was a series of events surrounding the introduction of competing electric power transmission systems in the late 1880s and early 1890s. It grew out of two lighting systems developed in the late 1870s and early 1880s: arc lamp street lighting running on high-voltage alternating current (AC), and large-scale low-voltage direct current (DC) indoor incandescent lighting being marketed by Thomas Edison's company. In 1886, the Edison system was faced with new competition: an alternating current system initially introduced by George Westinghouse's company that used transformers to step down from a high voltage so AC could be used for indoor lighting. Using high voltage allowed an AC system to transmit power over longer distances from more efficient large central generating stations. As the use of AC spread rapidly with other companies deploying their own systems, the Edison Electric Light Company claimed in early 1888 that high voltages used in an alternating current system were hazardous, and that the design was inferior to, and infringed on the patents behind, their direct current system. In the spring of 1888, a media furor arose over electrical fatalities caused by pole-mounted high-voltage AC lines, attributed to the greed and callousness of the arc lighting companies that operated them. In June of that year Harold P. Brown, a New York electrical engineer, claimed the AC-based lighting companies were putting the public at risk using high-voltage systems installed in a slipshod manner. Brown also claimed that alternating current was more dangerous than direct current and tried to prove this by publicly killing animals with both currents, with technical assistance from Edison Electric. The Edison company and Brown colluded further in their parallel goals to limit the use of AC with attempts to push through legislation to severely limit AC installations and voltages. Both also colluded with Westinghouse's chief AC rival, the Thomson-Houston Electric Company, to make sure the first electric chair was powered by a Westinghouse AC generator. By the early 1890s, the war was winding down. Further deaths caused by AC lines in New York City forced electric companies to fix safety problems. Thomas Edison no longer controlled Edison Electric, and subsidiary companies were beginning to add AC to the systems they were building. Mergers reduced competition between companies, including the merger of Edison Electric with their largest competitor, Thomson-Houston, forming General Electric in 1892. Edison Electric's merger with their chief alternating current rival brought an end to the war of the currents and created a new company that now controlled three quarters of the US electrical business. Westinghouse won the bid to supply electrical power for the World's Columbian Exposition in 1893 and won the major part of the contract to build Niagara Falls hydroelectric project later that year (partially splitting the contract with General Electric). DC commercial power distribution systems declined rapidly in numbers throughout the 20th century; the last DC utility in New York City was shut down in 2007.

  • While Benjamin Franklin is often mistakenly credited with inventing daylight saving time, he actually penned a satirical essay in 1784 suggesting Parisians wake up earlier to save on candles, not proposing a change to clocks. In 1784, while serving as an American envoy in Paris, Franklin wrote a satirical essay titled "An Economical Project" published in the Journal de Paris. The essay suggested that Parisians could save money on candles and lamp oil by waking up earlier and making the most of the natural daylight. Franklin's proposal was about changing sleep schedules, not changing the time on clocks, which is what modern daylight saving time involves. Because of this essay, Franklin is often mistakenly credited with inventing daylight saving time, but he was only suggesting a change in sleep habits, not the time itself. The modern concept of daylight saving time, which involves changing clocks, originated later, with some attributing the idea to George Hudson, an entomologist from New Zealand, in 1895. The concept of daylight saving time gained traction during World War I as a way to conserve energy, and it was formally adopted in the US in 1918.
