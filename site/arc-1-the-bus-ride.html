Arc 1: The Bus Ride (Chapters 1–8)

Story Arcs Home · The Birthday Night and Its Aftermath →

Spoiler note: This page covers Chapters 1 to 8. If you are reading along, read it after you finish Chapter 8.

Overview

Vera rides the Grey Goose bus through the Midwest mist toward What Cheer, Iowa. Between stretches on the bus, her memory supplies the two women who shaped her: her opium-dreaming mother, Catherine, and the plain nursemaid Miss MacIntosh, whose disappearance set her traveling. Then the bus takes over: Moses at the wheel, Madge and Homer and their hometown dead, flooded roads, and arrival in a village at night.

Central figures: Vera Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Madge Capehorn · Moses Hunnecker · Catherine Cartwheel · Homer Capehorn

Chapter by chapter

Chapter 1

Vera Cartwheel rides a Grey Goose bus through the mist of the American Midwest. The driver, Moses Hunnecker, has long bushy hair. He whistles, drinks openly from a whiskey bottle, calls on God and the angels, and drives erratically. Vera imagines a wife and children waiting for him at home, then wonders whether she has invented them. The only other passengers are a sleeping couple, Madge and Homer Capehorn. Madge is pregnant and seems to resent it, and Homer sleeps in placid innocence. Along the roadside Vera sees a moving van piled with furniture and a double-headed man staring into nothing. These sights lead her to reflect that individuality is an illusion and every soul is joined to every other. She explains why she is traveling. She is searching for Miss MacIntosh, the plain, red-haired nursemaid from What Cheer, Iowa, who disappeared or died. She was the one solid presence in Vera's childhood, in a house by the New England sea ruled by her mother's opium dreams.

Chapter 2

Vera describes her mother, Catherine. She is an opium addict who has lain in bed for years among jewels and phantoms and treats her hallucinations as more real than the world. The chapter traces how she came to this. There was a brief marriage to Jock Cartwheel, and a love triangle with twin brothers: Catherine loved Peron Spitzer, who did not love her, while his brother Joachim loved Catherine without return. Joachim, Mr. Spitzer, is the family lawyer and visits her bedside every evening. An Egyptian sailor arrives looking for the dead Peron and mistakes Joachim for him, and Catherine gives the sailor elaborate directions through her labyrinthine house. There is also the chauffeur, James, with whom she once tried to run away. Whenever her canary dies, Mr. Spitzer secretly replaces it. The chapter ends when a canary escapes into a thunderstorm. Catherine calmly accepts that she must now live forever and dismisses its distant twittering as another dream.

Chapter 3

After Catherine took to her bed, Mr. Spitzer hired Miss Georgia MacIntosh of What Cheer, Iowa, to raise Vera. Vera paints her in full: marcelled red hair, a broken nose, a black umbrella, a tight corset, sensible shoes, morning swims in any weather, strict schedules, and a deep suspicion of anything imaginary. She is everything Catherine is not, the practical Middle West in one person. Vera recalls how Miss MacIntosh dealt with an Australian bushman living in the household who was obsessed with her head, and she mentions the nursemaid's brother. Then Vera returns to the loss. Miss MacIntosh's clothes were found on the beach and her purse held two pennies. Her body was never found, and Vera blames herself. Mr. Spitzer always insists that she was a good and dependable woman who never wavered.

Chapter 4

For years after the disappearance, Vera grieves by the shore. Her hair is cropped and her body heavy, and she drifts in sleep and dreaming. She tries to write a book, lights fires on the beach, and questions fishermen and sailors. Catherine decides her daughter is losing her mind and offers the only cure she knows: opium and a dreamless paradise of eternal youth. Vera refuses and leaves for What Cheer by way of Grand Central Station. She recalls a young woman who jumped from the Brooklyn Bridge in her wedding veil on her way to be married. On the train, a fat, soft-faced lady warns her that the Middle West is desolate, a place of old people who are deaf because they do not wish to hear.

Chapter 5

Night falls on the bus. The roads are flooded and there are no stars or signposts. The bus waits at a Pennsylvania Railroad crossing while Madge moans in her sleep, accusing every man but her husband. Moses sings, drinks, and holds forth. He refuses ever to cut his hair. He rails against the Democrats and the New Deal, though he never has time to vote. He lives with his old mother, has never married, and talks constantly of pregnant women and of old Doc O'Leary, who once delivered babies and has now lost his mind. Birth and death fill his talk. The chapter leaves open whether Moses is driving the bus or only dreaming that he drives it.

Chapter 6

The bus passes broken gates, fallen pillars, and a derelict windmill in a flooded field. Madge wakes and her story emerges. She is five months pregnant, older than Homer, and worked as an operator in a pottery town. She married Homer to give her child a father, though the real father is a man named Adam. Homer, half asleep, dreams of his high school sweetheart, Jacqueline White, who is dying of tuberculosis. Madge accuses him of loving her still, and he denies it. Death runs through both families. Madge's mother died giving birth to her. Years later her father mistook Madge for her dead mother, ran into a winter storm, and drowned. Homer's father killed himself when he learned his wife was pregnant. Madge expects to die in childbirth. The chapter ends with her painting her lips in a darkened mirror and finding she has painted two mouths.

Chapter 7

Through rain and flood, Moses spots the wheelless frame of old Doc's Model T by the road. He imagines the doctor tending patients who are ghosts or were never born. He mocks the old man and is moved by him, but does not stop. Inside the bus, Madge tells Homer the dark stories of their hometown. Josh and Rudolph Hathaway were brothers, and both are dead. Gertrude, who married first one and then the other, is accused by Madge of killing them both, in a murky story of a locked door and a fire. Musidora, Gertrude's simple-minded sister, was married off to an old man and has too many children, who play in the graveyard. Homer wants to think the best of everyone. Madge brings the talk back to Jacqueline, and Homer says he is hungry.

Chapter 8

The bus pushes through a cloudburst with a broken windshield wiper, the road barely distinguishable from the flooded fields. It stops at a fork near a hill of old tombstones, beside Beatrice Fitzgerald's tin house in a yard of mud and wreckage. Madge calls Beatrice a common prostitute. Homer defends her: a hard worker who lost an eye to a boy's arrow, whose son, rumored to be old Doc's, joined the Navy. Moses delivers a long, dark meditation on death and medicine, insisting that no doctor could have saved Jacqueline. The bus enters a village at night. Its houses look like coffins under a skull-like moon, and Vera wonders whether she has arrived in a land of eternal night or a dream within a dream.
