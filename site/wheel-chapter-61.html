Chapter 61: Radical Immanence in the World of Mr. Spitzer: A Philosophical Analysis

On Chapter 61 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of The Wheel and the Spokes.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Research notes: Chapter 61 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 61

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Preface: The Chapter as Philosophical Object

This chapter from what appears to be Marguerite Young's Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is not merely a narrative about an eccentric mourner and a deaf-mute burying a frog. It is a sustained philosophical performance: a prose enactment of precisely the conditions described by the Eleven Pillars of Radical Immanence. The chapter does not illustrate these ideas from outside; it is them, structurally and linguistically. To analyze it through this philosophy is to discover that the philosophy was already living inside the prose, unnamed.

Pillar 1 Not One Self; You are not one person

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

Mr. Spitzer is constitutionally multiple. His night is his day, his day his night, his beginning his end, his end his beginning. This is not poetic paradox deployed for ornament. It is the ontological condition of his selfhood. He cannot be located at a single coordinate.

He is simultaneously:

  • A mourner at a frog's funeral
  • A lawyer who has presented his last case and cannot remember the decision
  • A musician whose music no one has heard
  • A brother to a dead twin, himself arguably already dead
  • A dreamer who may be dreaming this entire episode

The chapter makes the plurality explicit when it describes him as uncertain whether he is "the creator or the creation which should perilously endure". This is not a rhetorical question. It is the genuine condition of a self that cannot stabilize into singularity. He forgets "from moment to mortal moment the music he had immortalized": meaning that even his own interiority is not continuous, not retrievable, not one.

Most strikingly, his dead brother Peron haunts the chapter as a kind of shadow-self, a second person who was always already part of him. The twin structure is described as "two born at a birth or so closely related that they were the same in death". Mr. Spitzer is the living brother who remained: "even like the body without the soul or the soul without the body". He is literally half a self, the remainder of a plural entity that death has not resolved but only redistributed.

Even his physical presence is described as a "mere facade of a dead musician, pointing his dead fingers to which a starfish clung". The facade is not a mask over a real self. The facade is the self. There is no interior solid person behind the flowing cape and the rustling musical manuscripts. He is surfaces, accumulations, echoes.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed: Reality is what we make

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter is saturated with the deliberate confusion of reflection and original, image and thing. Mr. Spitzer consistently cannot distinguish between the real and its shadow:

"perhaps all were reflections, and there was no other image"

This is not described as a failure of perception or a symptom of his grief. It is offered as a genuine epistemological position. The chapter asks whether there is any image that is not a reflection of a reflection. The answer it gives is: probably not.

The meadow where the frog is buried is described with extraordinary hallucinatory precision (hundreds of named plants, birds, streams, insects) and yet the whole landscape has a dreamlike quality, an excess of detail that paradoxically makes it less real rather than more. This is the logic of the constructed real: the more elaborately you build it, the more visible the building becomes.

The cave metaphor from the Eleven Pillars appears almost literally:

"hallucinatory spires painted upon the fog, hallucinatory carriages and carriage wheels"

The city is explicitly hallucinatory. Its spires are painted; that is, constructed representations, not solid architecture. And yet Mr. Spitzer lives in this city, navigates it, mourns in it. The hallucinatory is not contrasted with the real. It is the real, more carefully lit.

The frog itself, the instrument of the mute's voice, is the chapter's central figure for constructed reality. The frog speaks the Lord's Prayer, asks for a light, gives racing tips, requests coins. Each of these is a real communication that is simultaneously an illusion: a frog's croak shaped by labial and laryngeal manipulation into the appearance of human speech. The chapter never resolves whether this is miracle or mechanism. It holds both.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End: The climate of a world where everything is always already changing

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

This pillar is perhaps the most completely enacted of all eleven. Grief in this chapter is not a response to a specific loss. It is the atmospheric condition of existence.

Mr. Spitzer mourns:

  • The frog
  • His dead brother Peron
  • His own youth
  • The brevity of life generally
  • The mute's voicelessness
  • His own unheard music
  • The death of all things, frogs and men alike

These griefs do not compete with each other or take turns. They are simultaneous, layered, climatological. The chapter says explicitly:

"Such was mortality that all must die, of course, both frogs and men, and all must go through death's great metamorphosis"

But this is not consolation. The universality of death does not diminish the grief; it expands it to encompass everything. Mr. Spitzer is described as one who "if only a frog had died in all the world, if no one else had ever died...would still have been the universal mourner". He is constitutionally a mourner. Grief is not his reaction to events. It is his mode of being.

The chapter also refuses the stages of grief entirely. There is no movement toward acceptance, no arc of healing. The frog died "long ago under the blind moon", yet the mourning is as present as ever, as immediate as the rain. The past does not recede. It accumulates.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act: Stay in the room. See fully. Refuse to simplify.

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Mr. Spitzer is the chapter's witness figure, but he is a witness of a very particular and philosophically precise kind. He does not interpret what he sees. He does not resolve it. He attends to it with an almost unbearable thoroughness.

The catalogue of plants at the frog's burial ground is the most extreme example. The list: milkwort, cockscomb, moonwort, Dutchman's pipes, Dutchman's breeches, jack-in-the-pulpit, coneflowers, fringed orchis, hedge hyssop, nightshade, heliotrope, cattails, oxlip, bladder campion, frog lilies, snakeroot, crowfoot, couch grass, crab grass, pectoral grass, mouse-ear, snakemouth, yellow marsh mallows, large periwinkle, pennyroyal, marsh flags, star grass, starflowers: goes on for a full paragraph. This is not decoration. This is the practice of witness. Every plant is named. Nothing is generalized into "wildflowers" or "vegetation." The world is attended to in its full particularity.

The same witnessing is applied to the mute's sign language at the graveside, where Mr. Spitzer watches and catalogs every gesture: sign of the cross, sign of man, signs of North and South and East and West, sign of the hour hand, sign of the minute hand, sign of the quick and the dead, sign of prayer, sign of meeting, sign of parting, sign of flight, without claiming to fully understand them. He witnesses without concluding. He sees without resolving. The ethical weight of this witnessing is made explicit when Mr. Spitzer accompanies the mute on the long journey to the burial ground, not because he is obligated to, not because he knows the mute well, but because:

he "had been ruled by his sympathies which had made him offer himself as mourner in the first place. For who else would express the mute’s grief?"

The Witness does not fix. The Witness stays. Mr. Spitzer stays, even when his Congress gaiter breaks and drags, even when his trousers are splattered with mud, even when he is slightly irritated by the long route. He stays because attention is the practice.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary; You build anyway, knowing the building is provisional

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Mr. Spitzer builds music. He builds it constantly, compulsively, on every available surface:

"his starched white shirt front or the white cuff of his sleeve or his black-bordered handkerchief...or his crumpled calling cards or marble table tops in public restaurants or paper lace napkins...or crumbling city walls or the sands of the sea before the tides came in"

The building is explicitly provisional. The tide comes in. The napkins crumple. The calling cards are lost. He scatters his manuscripts "all over Boston and sometimes Philadelphia and sometimes Trenton, Camden, Asbury Park", forgotten by him and noticed only by janitors and street-cleaners and beachcombers.

Yet he continues. He builds not despite the provisionality but within it. The chapter describes him composing an elegy for the mute while the mute is still alive, "feeling quite disloyal, as if he anticipated an event which might never take place." He builds the memorial before the death. He begins again before the ending. This is recursive construction in its purest form: the return to the beginning with what was learned, the building that is never finished.

The chapter also performs recursive construction formally, in its prose. Sentences return to their beginnings, clauses loop back through earlier clauses, images recur in slightly altered form. The opening paradox, "his night was his day, and his day was his night", is itself a recursive structure, a sentence that builds and then returns to its starting point transformed.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest: The unfinished is not failure

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter is built around incompletions. Mr. Spitzer's music is never performed. His elegy for the frog is improvised, half-croaked, lost in the wind. His legal career has ended with a case whose decision he cannot remember. His twin brother is dead, leaving the self permanently incomplete.

The chapter explicitly philosophizes this incompletion:

"Half should always be omitted, Mr. Spitzer thought — perhaps less than half, perhaps more than half. As to which there should be, he was never quite clear"

This is not resignation. It is a principled position about the nature of honest expression. The demand for completion (the concluded sentence, the resolved grief, the finished symphony) is itself named as a kind of violence, or at least as a falsification. The ongoing sentence is the honest one.

The mute's sign language enacts this formally. His signs are "signs which Mr. Spitzer had not understood", signs that exceed interpretation, signs for which there is no grammar. The communication is real, something is being expressed, but it is never completed into meaning. The ethical response is not to demand completion but to witness the expression in its incompleteness.

Even Mr. Spitzer's eulogy for the frog trails off:

"Indeed, Mr. Spitzer must have said much else which he had forgotten now"

The elegy is incomplete. The forgetting is not a failure. It is the condition of all elegies, all memorials, all attempts to hold what is already dissolving.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth: The edges of categories are where the constructed nature of reality becomes most visible

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

The chapter is populated almost exclusively by marginal figures, and each one is marginal in a way that illuminates the constructed nature of the categories they fall outside:

The frog-musician: Not human, not merely animal. A frog trained to speak human language, to ask for a match, to give racing tips, to recite the Lord's Prayer. The frog occupies the margin between animal and human, between nature and culture, between the sacred (Lord's Prayer) and the mundane (a racing tip). At this margin, the categories themselves become visible as constructions.

The mute: Neither fully in the world of sound nor fully outside it. He communicates through signs, through a frog's voice, through the trembling of his throat cords. He is marginal to language, and his marginality reveals that language is not a natural fact but a constructed system from which some are structurally excluded.

Mr. Spitzer himself: A musician whose music is never heard, a lawyer who no longer practices, a living man who is arguably already dead, a twin who is half of a self. He occupies the margin between life and death, between sound and silence, between existence and non-existence. The chapter describes him as "a mere facade of a dead musician": a person at the margin of personhood.

The frog's funeral: A funeral for an animal, conducted with the full seriousness of human burial ritual, silver spoon, cigar box coffin with silver foil and black cross, a red rose dropped into the grave, a eulogy. The ceremony occupies the margin between the sacred and the absurd, and at that margin reveals that all funeral ceremony is constructed, that the distinction between a meaningful death and a meaningless one is a human imposition.

The chapter makes this explicit in Mr. Spitzer's meditation on burial rites (Etruscans, Eskimos, Indians, Egyptians) which demonstrates that every culture constructs its own version of the sacred passage, and that none has priority.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away: The past accumulates beneath us, still pressing up

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This pillar is enacted in the chapter's very prose texture. The past does not recede in this chapter. It presses up through every paragraph.

Mr. Spitzer's dead brother is present throughout, not as a memory that is visited and then set aside, but as a continuous pressure on the present. When he hears the bull frog croaking "too deep, too deep", he immediately thinks of his brother, and the chapter spirals into a meditation on whether "too, to, two" (adverb, preposition, or noun) and whether the two brothers were "the same in death, going together". The brother is not behind him. He is beneath him, pressing up.

The chapter also figures sedimentary time through its geological and oceanic imagery. Sea shells are described as having traveled from the Arctic to the Antarctic, from China beaches to Atlantic shores, "sometimes pulverized by friction of waves and wind before they reached their journey's end". The sea shell carries its entire journey in its form. It is made of its accumulated passage.

Mr. Spitzer himself is described in these terms. His coat lining is "lined with music, the great symphony", his hat stuffed with musical movements and notes. He is literally a sedimentary figure, layered with accumulated composition, carrying his entire history in his person.

The chapter also figures this through the Etruscan burial meditation, where the dead are described as sleeping in their houses with mirrors in their hands and stone ferrets at their feet and "submarine memories painted on the walls." The dead carry their memories into death. The past does not stop when the person stops. It continues, deposited, pressing up.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible: Honest language names its own location

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter is acutely conscious of the constructedness of language. This consciousness is most concentrated in the meditation on the bull frog's croaking:

"For it would make a great difference in the meaning, Mr. Spitzer believed, as to whether it was too, to, two — adverb or preposition or noun"

This is not wordplay. It is a genuine philosophical claim: that the same sound carries entirely different meanings depending on which grammatical construction you impose on it, and that there is no neutral, pre-linguistic reality that determines which construction is correct. Language shapes what can be seen.

The chapter extends this to the mute's sign language, which is described as:

"silence being filled with ambiguities like the sign language of God Who created heaven and earth, these hieroglyphic birds and beasts and flowers and men walking on stilts"

God's creation is itself a sign language: a constructed system of meaning that is filled with ambiguities. The natural world is not a transparent reality but a text, and like all texts, it is subject to misreading, over-reading, under-reading.

The chapter also demonstrates that language excludes as much as it includes. The mute is excluded from the English-speaking world, but the chapter imagines this exclusion multiplied:

"what more pitiful than to be a Spanish deaf mute lip-reader in Boston or a Persian deaf mute in this grey city of the moving lips"

The person who cannot access the dominant language is not simply inconvenienced. They are, in a real sense, unable to exist in the social world that language constructs. The mute's silence is not merely personal. It is structural.

Mr. Spitzer's own musical language participates in this construction. His scores are described as "sometimes unintelligible even to him"; he has constructed a language that even he cannot fully read. The language has exceeded its author.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here: The sacred lives inside the ordinary world

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The chapter is saturated with the sacred, but the sacred never points outward to a beyond. It points inward, deeper into the immanent.

The frog's funeral is the chapter's central sacred event, and it is conducted with complete seriousness: silver spoon, cigar box coffin, red rose, eulogy, the mute's elaborate sign-language prayers. The sacred is not diminished by being applied to a frog. The frog is not a symbol of something larger. The frog is the thing. The sacred lives in the frog's actual death, the mute's actual grief, the muddy walk through actual marshland.

The chapter's most explicit statement of immanent transcendence comes in Mr. Spitzer's eulogy:

"for thou shalt be a man, little frog, in the next world — and thou shalt have thy musical memories, thy rain music like the water gurgling in the water pipes"

Even the promised afterlife is described in terms of immanent experience: rain music, water gurgling in pipes. The transcendent is imagined as a continuation of the immanent, not an escape from it.

The stars throughout the chapter are described not as a transcendent beyond but as part of the same fabric as the earth: "stars like flying sparks or fireflies reflecting on whirling pools", meadows "moored not upon this earth but were islands floating above his head", "the long-stemmed lilies of light, trumpet lilies, silent trumpets gleaming through the clouds". The sacred is not up there, separate. It is woven into the same cloth as the fog, the mud, the broken Congress gaiter.

The mute's sign language at the graveside is the chapter's most sustained sacred ritual, and it is performed in a muddy meadow, by a man with spotted skin and frog's eyes, using a silver spoon borrowed from his breast pocket. The sacred does not require a church or a priest. It requires only full attention to the actual.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point; You are where time crosses itself

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is perhaps the deepest pillar, and the chapter enacts it most completely in its treatment of Mr. Spitzer's relationship to his dead brother, to the mute, and to time itself.

Mr. Spitzer is explicitly described as a crossing point. He is the living twin, the remainder, the one who stayed: "the living brother who had remained, even like the body without the soul or the soul without the body". He is constituted by what was deposited before him (his brother's existence, his own musical inheritance, the long history of burial rites from Etruscans to Eskimos) and he is constituting the ground others will stand on (his scattered musical manuscripts, his witness to the mute's grief, the elegy he composes for the still-living mute).

The chapter makes the structural nature of this obligation explicit:

"He was the hub, the central part of the wheel — but his brother was the blurred, the singing spokes of that great wheel turning with its periphery lost among the clouds"

Mr. Spitzer is where time crosses itself. He is the hub through which past and future pass. He did not choose this position. It is structural.

The sea shell meditation is the chapter's most extended figure for the Crossing Point. The sea shell carries within its form the entire history of its journey: every sea it crossed, every shore it touched, every current that shaped it. It is constituted by what was deposited before it arrived at this shore, and it will constitute the shore it rests on, its calcium eventually becoming part of the sand. The obligation is not chosen. It is structural. The ancestors are in the sediment.

Mr. Spitzer's musical manuscripts scattered across multiple cities are his version of the sea shell's journey: deposits left in the sediment of the world, to be found by janitors and beachcombers and street-sweepers, constituting a ground that the not-yet-born will stand on without knowing it. The carrying continues, even in forgetting, even in loss.
