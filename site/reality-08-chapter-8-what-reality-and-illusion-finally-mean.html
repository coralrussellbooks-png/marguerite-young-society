Reality and Illusion in MMMD

Chapter 8: What Reality and Illusion Finally Mean

← Chapter 7: Narrative, Time, and Memory · Exploring Reality and Illusion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling · A Note on the Text →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · The Stone Deaf Man · Vera Cartwheel

This book began with a claim: illusion is primary. Most of what passes for solid ground is built, not found, and kept standing by repetition. Seeing the construction is the first step out from under its spell. Each chapter has tested that claim against a different part of Young’s novel. This last chapter asks what the tests add up to, and what the novel’s ending finally says about reality and illusion.

What the Chapters Found

In perception, Chapter 3 found no character who sees without a lens. What separates Young’s dreamers from her realists is not contact with the real but candor about the lens. In identity, Chapter 4 found selves that are crowds, split and substituted and assembled from other people. The characters who suffer most are the ones who insist on being one. In mortality, Chapter 5 found deaths no one sees, reaching the living only through what the dead leave behind, and an afterlife the living carry themselves. In ethics, Chapter 6 found goodness built by habit and held provisionally, like a basting stitch. In form, Chapter 7 found a style that makes the reader live through the thesis rather than merely hear it.

Together these findings describe a world in which everything is made: sight, selves, deaths, virtues, and time itself. The question the novel leaves is not whether to accept this, since no one in the book gets to refuse it. The question is how to live once it is known.

Fleeing Illusion into Illusion

Vera states the problem at the moment of the unmasking: “What shall we do when, fleeing from illusion, we are confronted by illusion? When falling from illusion, we fall into illusion?” This is the book’s hardest sentence, because it closes the obvious exit. There is no floor of plain fact under the dreaming. Every step out of one illusion lands in another.

Young’s answer is not despair, and it is not Catherine’s surrender either. It unfolds slowly, through the ending, and it has three parts.

The House Goes into the Sea

The first part is that the constructions fall. In the final chapter Vera tells what she could not say aloud: “that my mother was dead, that the opium paradise had crumbled, that the sea had swept over the dark house with its golden turrets and towers.” Catherine, “after many years of sleeping, arose from her bed and walked, not quite sleeping, awake when she died.” Mr. Spitzer learned at last “which brother he truly was the dead.” Miss MacIntosh’s “bald head” lies “upon the waves.” Vera is “in mourning for three deaths.”

The great edifice of illusion that Vera spent her life fleeing is simply gone, swept away “after the storm,” leaving “the chandeliers strewn for miles at the sea’s edge.” Yet its collapse does not reveal a plain reality beneath. Vera finds herself “facing that reality which had been a dream and which had crumbled, perhaps into reality, perhaps into a dream if nothing was real.” The ending refuses to say which. What it insists on is that the old construction is over and a new life must be built.

A Sense Missing

The second part is an admission. Brooding on the deaf, whose world lacks one whole channel of the real, Vera thinks: “Perhaps there is always a sense missing, that sense which would make the world real.” This is the closest the novel comes to a theory of its own. The world never becomes fully real to anyone, because every perceiver lacks something, a sense, an angle, or a light. Catherine lacked the waking world. Miss MacIntosh lacked the dream. Mr. Spitzer lacked the present. The missing sense is not a defect to be cured. It is the condition of seeing at all.

The man Vera finally loves embodies this. He is a stone deaf man who “heard with his eyes,” whose “ears were in his finger-tips,” and who became “the artist of his life.” He lives openly with a sense missing. He does not pretend to hear, and he does not treat his silence as a tragedy that cancels everything else. When Vera spells her name on his hand in the dark, she has found a way of meeting the world that works through the gap rather than denying it.

Dream On

The third part is a blessing, and Young gives it to a minor figure. When the deaf man was a boy and white girls turned away from him, an old Black woman down the road told him: “Dream on dream on for life is imaginary, and this be an imaginary world. And the silence had its tongues, and love came before words were or ever will be.”

That is the thesis of this book, spoken plainly by someone on the edge of the novel’s world. Life is imaginary, not in the sense that nothing matters, but in the sense that everything is made, and the making is where love lives. It is fitting that the sentence comes from a figure the novel’s own detective would have overlooked, someone at the margins who sees the categories most clearly. The framework calls this The Margins Reveal the Truth. Young puts her deepest line in exactly that place.

The Pearls

The novel ends in a field at night. Vera and the deaf man lie under the trees, and a child is conceived: “our child will be our ear,” he whispers. Vera does “not know the night in which I conceived as I do not know the night in which I was conceived.” The string of pearls she wore, “the only property which was left to me” from her mother’s house, is lost in the grass: “never would I find my pearls in the morning light, never even look for them.” They plan a wedding and invite “all the wild flowers and all the birds,” “the old mule that climbed over the fence,” and Esther Longtree, “the mother of the stillborn,” who “loved us all and all things hurt or silent or broken or dead or living.”

The pearls matter. They are the last piece of Catherine’s inheritance and the novel’s recurring image of beauty grown around an injury, like the pearl in Mr. Spitzer’s eye. Vera lets them go and does not search. After a lifetime spent searching for Miss MacIntosh, for truth, and for harmony, her last act in the novel is to stop looking for what she has lost, and to build something new in the dark with a man who cannot hear her, for a child who will be their ear.

What Reality and Illusion Mean

Here, finally, is what the novel teaches, set against the pillar with which this book began.

Illusion is primary. There is no bedrock beneath the constructions, and anyone who goes looking for it comes back holding another construction. Young accepts this without flinching, and she makes the reader accept it too, sentence by sentence.

But the fact that everything is made does not make everything equal. The novel draws its moral lines inside the made world, not between the made and the real. Some constructions admit they are constructions and some do not. Some are held lightly and revised, and some are defended at any cost. Some shelter others, and some, like the detective’s categories, crush whoever falls between their lines. Catherine’s dream and Miss MacIntosh’s common sense are both illusions. The difference that matters is how each is held.

Seeing the construction does not free anyone from it. Vera never reaches a world without masks. What seeing does is loosen the grip, so that an illusion can be worn like a basting stitch, firmly enough to hold for now and loosely enough to come out when the true seam is found. That is the freedom the novel offers, and it is small, honest, and enough to live on.

The rest is love, which in this novel is itself a construction: “a system of mutual deception,” Miss MacIntosh called it, and she was not wrong. Yet it is the construction Young honors most. Vera loved a bald woman for her baldness, “their lack would be my love.” She loves a deaf man through the silence. At the end she will raise a child in “an imaginary world,” which is the only kind of world there has ever been. The novel does not ask readers to wake from the dream. It asks them to dream honestly, with others, and to keep building.

Reflection Questions

  1. Vera asks, “What shall we do when, fleeing from illusion, we are confronted by illusion?” After the whole novel, what is the answer?

  2. “Perhaps there is always a sense missing, that sense which would make the world real.” What sense might be missing from one’s own way of seeing?

  3. The novel places its deepest line, “life is imaginary, and this be an imaginary world,” in the mouth of a figure at its edges. Why might wisdom come from the margins?

  4. Vera loses her pearls and never looks for them. What does it take to stop searching for what is lost?

  5. If everything is made, what makes one construction better than another?

  6. Having followed Vera to the end, what illusion would be worth keeping, knowing it is one?
