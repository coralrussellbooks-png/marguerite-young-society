Angel in the Forest

Chapter 1. The Book That Is Not History

Part of a reading of Angel in the Forest (1945), Marguerite Young’s history of the two failed utopias at New Harmony, Indiana.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Angel in the Forest: A Reading in Fourteen Chapters · Chapter 2 →

Marguerite Young / Angel in the Forest, Chapter 1

Marguerite Young arrives in New Harmony, Indiana, in the summer of 1940, and the first thing she does is refuse to stand outside the thing she came to see.

She comes by a creaking ferry that has been running, according to the sign on the iron bell, for fifty years. The hills roll away on both banks. Sandpipers cross back and forth between the two shores, and she notices that they are primitive birds, that they build no nests but only holes in the mudbanks, so that the hills look coated in golden birds. There may be two blind mules riding with her, hauling pale logs of fallen birch. The ferryman never speaks a word. Then she climbs a path that seems to lead nowhere and reaches the town, which is a disappointment. Where there were two utopias, there is now a filling station. Where there was an angel, there is Mr. Babbitt, the ordinary striving American man.

This is how Angel in the Forest begins, and it is worth paying attention to, because nothing here is scene-setting in the usual sense. A conventional historian would clear her throat, place the town on a map, give us the dates, and only then let the past come forward. Young does the opposite. Before a single fact about the Rappites or the Owenites has been laid down, the place is already thick with what happened there. The present is haunted before the history starts. The ivy on the water tower, the swallows in the abandoned belfry, the two dolls under bell glass in a shop window labeled Father and Mother Rapp: all of it is soaked in a failure that has not finished happening. Young does not report on New Harmony from a clean distance. She wades in, and she wants us to understand that there is no clean distance to report from. There is no ferry that carries you to a vantage point above the wreck. There is only the wreck, and you standing in it, adding your own footprints to the ones already there.

That refusal is the entire method she used throughout her work, stated in the first pages. The book calls itself history, and by ordinary measures it is even good history. It has archives, letters, chronology, named and dated people. But it does not behave like history. It behaves like a long philosophical poem that has borrowed the furniture of the archive.

What Van Doren almost said

Mark Van Doren, introducing the book, put it this way: Young is a poet whose prose, the longer you look at it and listen to it, becomes poetry too. He is right, and the compliment is real, but the frame is a little too small. Calling her a poet who writes beautiful prose makes the poetry sound like a finish applied to the surface, a matter of gorgeous sentences laid over an otherwise normal work of local history.

The truth is stranger than that. Young is not decorating history with poetry. She is using the tools of history to do something history is not built to do. Archives, dates, real people: in her hands these are not there to settle what happened. They are there to stage a much larger question, which is how any version of reality gets built in the first place, and why the versions we build keep collapsing. The two communities at New Harmony are her material, but her subject is the construction of the real and its failure. That is not a poem sitting on top of a history. That is a history bent, from the first sentence, into the shape of a philosophical argument.

What she never does

The plainest way to see it is to notice what a normal history of New Harmony would do that Young refuses to do.

A normal history would adjudicate. It would weigh Father Rapp against Robert Owen and tell you which man was closer to right. It would measure outcomes: Rapp's celibate community ended, by its own logic, in heaven, and Owen's community fed, by a longer and stranger route, into the British labor movement. A normal history would assign causes. It would explain why the utopias failed, name the fatal flaw, close the case.

Young does none of this. She accumulates. She catalogs. Read the opening pages and you find her listing the inhabitants of the place across a century as if they were all present at once: an angel, a butterfly catcher, a herb doctor, a celibate breeder of horses, patriarchs, undertakers, the Spirit of Nature, rational man, irrational men. She lists what the two utopias managed to contain inside half a square mile of wilderness: city planning and anarchy, a new calendar and the burning of Rome, a three-hour working day and the exaltation of horses. The lists do not resolve into a thesis. They are not evidence marched toward a verdict. They are the thing itself, the whole teeming contradiction, held open and refused a conclusion.

She does the same with the men. Father Rapp said he saw an angel in Pennsylvania, in the old Harmony, and left the angel's big footprint pressed into a stone. Was the angel there? Was Rapp a madman? Young lets the question float and never lets it come down. Her Rapp is grotesque and, in his own way, great: a tyrant, a mountebank, a man with monstrous ideas about sex, and still someone she will not reduce to a diagnosis. She lets the faith and the folly stand in the same man at the same time, welded so tightly that you cannot pull them apart. That welding is deliberate. It is the argument.

Why the shape is the point

What is being laid out here connects to everything this fourteen-chapter study traces.

The first is the idea that the unfinished is the honest thing.

A history that adjudicates, measures, and assigns causes is telling you, by its very shape, that the matter can be closed, that a final account is available if you are rigorous enough. Young does not believe that, and so she does not let her book pretend it. No verdict is coming. No resolution is being withheld until the last chapter for effect. The book stays open because Young thinks reality stays open, and to seal it off in a tidy conclusion would be a lie about the thing described. The form is not a container for the argument. The form is the argument.

The second thread is that language shapes what can be thought at all.

Young's sentences do not settle. They keep moving, sliding from one image into the next, refusing to let a person or a place harden into a fixed and final noun. New Harmony is a filling station and an angel's footprint and a little planet whirling alone in space, and it is all of these in the space of a paragraph, because that is how the mind actually holds a haunted place. The prose enacts a conviction about reality: that it does not sit still to be named, that the moment you fix it in a stable word you have already lost most of it. This is not a style laid over the philosophy. It is the philosophy, working at the level of the sentence.

The question the book is built around

Early in that first chapter, in the middle of describing what it is like to spend time in the town, Young stops and asks it directly: what is the nature of experience, what dream among dreams is reality?

That is the sentence the entire book hangs from. It is not history's question. History asks what happened and why. Young's question goes underneath that, to whether we can trust the surface of anything at all, whether the past we reconstruct or the present we stand in is any more solid than the dreams the Rappites and the Owenites dreamed on this same ground and lost. She spends over four hundred pages not answering it, and the not-answering is not failure. It is the honest end of an honest method.

Radical Immanence

After reading Young for years I came upon what I believe she accomplished with her writing from her first poem at age 9 until her last work compiled and heavily edited after her death. I believe she developed over her lifetime a complete philosophical system. I call it Radical Immanence. I have written many books dedicated to explaining it, all free and gathered under Radical Immanence.

Here is a short version of it.

Not One Self.

A person is a crowd, not a single fixed identity. The many selves are not a malfunction to correct. They are the normal condition of being alive, and they make a person hard to sort into a clean box.

Reality Is Constructed.

Most of what feels like solid reality is built: money, borders, laws, status. These are agreements held in place by repetition, not facts dug out of the earth. Seeing the construction is the first step out from under its spell.

Grief Doesn't End.

Grief is not a stage to pass through on the way to recovery. It is permanent weather. Refusing to perform a tidy healing is a way of staying truthful about what was lost.

The Witness.

The hardest and most honest stance is to stay in the room with something painful and complicated without rushing to fix it or wrap it up.

Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary.

Since nothing is ever truly final, the honest move is to build anyway, provisionally, and keep revising. The frozen, finished blueprint is the thing to distrust.

Unfinished Is Honest.

The unfinished is not failure. It is honesty. Anyone who claims to hold the complete and final answer is usually selling something.

The Margins Reveal the Truth.

The people who do not fit the categories see the categories most clearly. The margins are where the seams show.

The Past Doesn't Go Away.

The past is not behind us. It is beneath us, pressed into the present like layers of rock. Calling any arrangement “inevitable” hides the people who actually built it.

Language Shapes What's Possible.

Grammar carries a worldview. Nouns freeze things into fixed identities; verbs keep them moving. And any voice that claims to speak “from nowhere,” in pure objectivity, is hiding where it really stands.

The Sacred Is Here.

Meaning is here, in the actual world underfoot, not in a promised beyond. The appeal to a distant beyond, a golden age, a grand destiny, a final arrival, is the oldest tool of anyone who wants to justify sacrifice in the present.

The Crossing Point.

Each person stands where everyone who came before meets everyone who will come after. Every choice packs down the ground the next generations will stand on. Trading their future for a present advantage is the one unforgivable move.
