Chapter 3

On Chapter 3 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Christianity and Religion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 24, 2026

← Previous · Religion in MMMD Home · Next →

Characters: Miss MacIntosh

Philosophy: Augustine and Christian Theology

Allusions: Ancient Egypt · Ecclesiastes · The Bible · The Book of Job

“imaginary nightingales or turrets of ivory”: The Song of Solomon says, “Thy neck is as a tower of ivory” (7:4), and “Tower of Ivory” became a title of the Virgin Mary. By the nineteenth century an ivory tower meant a place of dreamy retreat from the world, which Miss MacIntosh will not allow. The nightingale is the bird of the Romantic poets, as in Keats’s “Ode to a Nightingale.”

The line about a God "Who had God's limitations," who "had left many things uncreated," is the novel's boldest theological stroke. It gives up the all-powerful Creator for a finite one, a God with edges, a creation left open and unfinished. The idea runs against orthodoxy and toward the book's steady questioning of how much of the world was ever truly made or governed.

The problem with God and his attributes of omniscience (God’s complete and perfect knowledge, knowing everything past, present, and future), omnipotence (God’s unlimited power and ability to do anything), omnipresence (God is present everywhere at all times, not limited by space or time), and also added by some omnibenevolence (God has perfect or unlimited goodness) are that they create fallacies: god cannot be omnibenevolent and omnipotent in a universe in which evil exists (i.e., his creation suffers). Can god not prevent evil? Then he is not omnipotent. Does he not want to prevent evil? Then he is not omnibenevolent. If god can prevent evil, and god wants to prevent evil, then evil would not exist. Can god defy the laws of physics (create a rock so heavy even he can’t lift it? destroy himself? create a square circle? make himself not omnipotent?) If god is omniscient, then he has always known every single choice every human has ever and will ever make. Therefore, from his perspective, free will is impossible because there was only ever one choice any person who has ever or will ever exist could make. Any argument made from free will with an omniscient god is logically unsound. Any god that forces his followers to adopt illogical arguments to defend his existence in a universe very clearly explicable by logic is a malicious god, not a benevolent god.

God as a "harness-maker" who "hung her harness with tinkling bells" pictures providence as a craftsman keeping watch, a maker who can always find his creature.

The line that "labor was man's lot upon this earth," set beside a God who "made the furrows on the sea," leans on Ecclesiastes, work as a God-given portion and toil accepted as part of the order of things.

"ours was not the problem of questioning or understanding God's remotest mysteries" states the plain Christian humility that runs through the book: live rightly, and leave what cannot be known alone.

“not venturing to do what the wisest man had failed to do”: When the oracle at Delphi said that no one was wiser than Socrates, Socrates concluded that his only wisdom was knowing that he knew nothing (Plato, Apology). The wisest man in the Bible, Solomon, ends Ecclesiastes with the same humility: “of making many books there is no end.”

The counsel to "mind his own knitting, his own cooking, his own household duties even though he had no house" carries the ethic of plain, honest living, an echo of the call to deny oneself and take up one's cross.

"God's business had never been ours, perhaps had never been God's" holds humility and radical doubt in the same breath, and catches the book's central tension between faith and skepticism whole.

“the mischief caused by those who did not work and yet were restless”: The saying that the devil finds work for idle hands is often thought to be biblical, but it comes from Isaac Watts’s hymn for children “Against Idleness and Mischief” (1715): “For Satan finds some mischief still / For idle hands to do.” Proverbs 16:27 has a similar thought: “An ungodly man diggeth up evil.”

“God, moving in His mysterious ways”: “God Moves in a Mysterious Way” is a Christian hymn written in 1773 by the English poet William Cowper, first titled “Light Shining out of Darkness.” The poem is the likely source for the phrase “God moves in mysterious ways,” although the first line of the poem actually runs “God moves in a mysterious way.” Cowper wrote it at Olney, and it is often said to have been his last hymn, written as he felt a period of deep depression coming on. (Wikipedia)

"Perhaps God's understanding never had been God's" turns even omniscience into a question, another crack in the traditional picture of God.

"The easiest way was the coward's way, the path of least resistance," broad and well paved, set against "the path of the brave," narrow and thorned and lonely, is Matthew 7:13-14, the wide road to ruin and the narrow one to life.

St. Simeon Stylites, the Syrian hermit who lived thirty-seven years atop a pillar, stands for extreme ascetic devotion.

“of fleshly pleasures and fleshpots of luxury, of dancing girls and headless men”: The fleshpots come from Exodus 16:3, where the Israelites in the wilderness long for Egypt, “when we sat by the flesh pots, and when we did eat bread to the full.” The dancing girl and the headless man recall Salome and John the Baptist from Chapter 2. Earlier in Chapter 2.

"There would be no light without the darkness" makes the plain point that contrast is what lets either be seen.

“the light which came from within was the only light by which to steer our way”: The Quakers teach that every person holds the Inner Light, the light of God within, a surer guide than any outward authority. George Fox, who founded the Society of Friends in the 1650s, spoke of “that of God in every one.” See American Religious Movements.

“all those Romish graven images”: Graven images come from the Ten Commandments: “Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image” (Exodus 20:4). Romish is an old, hostile Protestant word for Roman Catholic, and Miss MacIntosh distrusts the statues of saints.

“the whirling dervishes”: The whirling dervishes of the Mevlevi Order, founded by followers of the thirteenth-century poet Rumi in Konya, Turkey, are a Sufi order that practices whirling as a form of dhikr or remembrance of God.

“those sorrows bringing our grey hairs”: Jacob, fearing the loss of his youngest son, says his sons would “bring down my gray hairs with sorrow to the grave” (Genesis 42:38). See Genesis.

“one should not place his faith in earthly things”: Colossians 3:2 reads, “Set your affection on things above, not on things on the earth.”

“pearls of greater price, a pearl as big as her head”: Jesus compares the kingdom of heaven to a merchant who, “when he had found one pearl of great price, went and sold all that he had, and bought it” (Matthew 13:46). See Pearls and Jewels.

“the black-winged angels of mortality falling into the burning sea”: Revelation 8:8 tells how “a great mountain burning with fire was cast into the sea,” and Revelation 12:9 how Satan and his angels were cast out of heaven. See Revelation and the Apocalypse.

“Man was but a breath, a puff of smoke, and quickly gone. Time was short, growing always shorter just like a candle burning in the wind”: The Psalms say, “Man is like to vanity: his days are as a shadow that passeth away” (Psalm 144:4), and James asks, “For what is your life? It is even a vapour, that appeareth for a little time, and then vanisheth away” (James 4:14). Macbeth cries, “Out, out, brief candle!”

"We must not waste our lives in the vain expectation" of what may never come recalls the Parable of the Rich Fool and the futility of hoarding hope against an empty future.

"To kiss the rod was better than not to know self-discipline" urges the acceptance of discipline over the illusion that a person is free and answerable to no one.

“than to imagine we were free and our own architects, building heaven”: In John Milton’s Paradise Lost, Satan, newly fallen into hell, declares, “The mind is its own place, and in itself / Can make a Heaven of Hell, a Hell of Heaven.” The line suggests that one’s inner state can transform the worst outward circumstances. Miss MacIntosh distrusts any such architecture of the mind. See Milton.

“We were the nestless birds”: Jesus says, “Behold the fowls of the air: for they sow not, neither do they reap” (Matthew 6:26), and also, “The foxes have holes, and the birds of the air have nests; but the Son of man hath not where to lay his head” (Matthew 8:20).

“six-winged seraphim with crowns of gold”: In Isaiah’s vision of the throne of God, “each one had six wings; with twain he covered his face, and with twain he covered his feet, and with twain he did fly” (Isaiah 6:2). See Angels and Archangels.

“God was not a harlequin with a shaven head and a masked face and parti-colored tights and a sword of lath”: Harlequin is the acrobatic servant of the Italian commedia dell’arte, in a black half mask and a costume of colored diamonds, carrying a wooden bat or slapstick, the “sword of lath.”

“God was rather an old harness-maker or an old harpooner or a simple carpenter”: In Mark 6:3 the people of Nazareth ask of Jesus, “Is not this the carpenter, the son of Mary?” Miss MacIntosh can imagine God only as a working man.

“we were given our opponent in order that we might struggle with him”: The struggle recalls Jacob, who wrestled through the night with a mysterious stranger and would not let him go until he was blessed (Genesis 32:24-30).

“She believed, with all her simple and aggressive and loudly beating heart, in accepting the plain reality as it was, not in self-indulgence and these corrupting dreams of incorruptibility”: 1 Peter 1:23 speaks of being “born again, not of corruptible seed, but of incorruptible.” The statement emphasizes Miss MacIntosh’s practical, no-nonsense approach to life that values facing facts honestly and living with integrity, rather than indulging in self-delusion or fanciful ideals that deny human frailty and mortality. Philosophical realists hold that the world exists independently of anyone’s mind and can be known as it is. Critics reply that even realists see through their own assumptions and biases. Miss MacIntosh’s realism is closest to the common-sense philosophy of the Scottish thinker Thomas Reid, who held that ordinary people know the world directly through their senses and that common sense is the foundation of all reasoning. American colleges taught his philosophy throughout the nineteenth century. In Chapter 1 she declares, “Common sense is the finest sense.” See Common Sense and American Pragmatism. Earlier in Chapter 1.

“Woman’s heart was folly”: Proverbs 9:13 says, “A foolish woman is clamorous: she is simple, and knoweth nothing.” The line can be interpreted as a recognition of the emotional vulnerability and the sometimes irrational nature of the human heart, especially as experienced through the female characters in the novel, caught between dreams and reality, love and loss.

“This earth was the testing ground even for God, often making His own mistakes which had perhaps been unavoidable as He had wished to leave man free”: Augustine, in On Free Choice of the Will, argued that God gave human beings free will, though they could misuse it, because a world with freedom is better than one without. This free-will defense remains one of the main answers to the problem of evil. See Augustine and Christian Theology.

"the argument as to how many angels might stand on a needle's point" nods to the old satire of medieval hairsplitting, learned debate spent on nothing.

"whether a camel shall enter through the needle's eye or a rich man through the gates of heaven" is Jesus's saying from Matthew 19:24, the hard odds of the rich entering the kingdom.

“these empty husks”: In the parable of the prodigal son, the son who has wasted his inheritance “would fain have filled his belly with the husks that the swine did eat” (Luke 15:16).

Miss MacIntosh prefers Pilgrim's Progress "to even the Word of God." She holds that scripture "should be taken with a grain of salt," open to too many readings and thick with human exaggeration, its very angels "falling, for example, into a dark sea like this sea." Bunyan's allegory of the soul's journey suits her better than doctrine, and her wariness of the Bible as a handled, fallible thing is the book's questioning stance put plainly.

“The sins we committed were worse than those of omission”: Christian teaching distinguishes sins of commission, wrongs done, from sins of omission, good left undone, as in the general confession of the Book of Common Prayer: “We have left undone those things which we ought to have done; And we have done those things which we ought not to have done.” Miss MacIntosh ranks the first as worse, yet elsewhere she speaks of “those things God in His haste had forgotten,” as if God, too, had sins of omission.

“better than all the principalities, all the powers of darkness”: Ephesians 6:12 reads, “For we wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against the rulers of the darkness of this world.”

“nor was he even a good Presbyterian or Free Methodist or Shouting Baptist or Jehovah’s Witness”: Miss MacIntosh lists the plain Protestant churches of the Middle West. The Free Methodist Church was founded in New York in 1860 in protest against pew rents and slavery, and “shouting” describes the loud, ecstatic worship of revival meetings. See American Religious Movements.

“nor did he believe in total immersion, for he believed in sprinkling”: Churches have long divided over baptism. Baptists and many revival churches baptize adults by total immersion, while Catholics, Presbyterians, and Methodists usually baptize by sprinkling or pouring. Anabaptist groups are established in Iowa, including the Amish, the Mennonites, and the Old Order River Brethren, who baptize by immersion. Baptism for especially early Christians was like being buried so that a person could rise to a new life. According to Paul, full immersion under water represents the believer’s death and burial in a watery grave with Christ, and the rising out of the water represents resurrection to a new life (Romans 6:3-4). Drownings during baptism have occasionally happened, and in the 1500s drowning was used as a punishment against the Anabaptists themselves: Felix Manz was drowned in Zurich in 1527, a mocking “third baptism.”

“walls of bones in beautiful designs like lace”: In the Capuchin Crypt beneath the church of Santa Maria della Concezione in Rome, the bones of some 3,700 friars are arranged in arches, rosettes, and chandeliers.

“the dead burying the dead until there were no more to bury”: Jesus tells a would-be follower who first wants to bury his father, “Follow me; and let the dead bury their dead” (Matthew 8:22).

“the primitive Christians of great works and little faith, not of great faith and little works”: The old quarrel between faith and works runs through Christianity. James 2:17 says, “faith, if it hath not works, is dead,” and Martin Luther, who taught salvation by faith alone, called the Epistle of James “an epistle of straw.”

“cherubim, many-faced angels with plumes of gold”: In Ezekiel’s vision the cherubim each have four faces, of a man, a lion, an ox, and an eagle (Ezekiel 1:10).

Daniel, the exile who survived the lions' den for refusing to stop praying, stands for faith kept under pressure and God's protection of the faithful.

St. Augustine, bishop of Hippo, appears with his City of God, which set the heavenly society of the faithful against the earthly City of Man.

“He could not digest the golden steeples and streets of gold”: The heavenly Jerusalem of Revelation has gates of pearl, “and the street of the city was pure gold, as it were transparent glass” (Revelation 21:21). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.

“these gaping cannibals”: Thomas Baker was a Methodist missionary in Fiji and is the only missionary in that country to be killed and eaten.

“to convert the heathen of the Easter Islands”: Easter Island is an island and special territory of Chile in the southeastern Pacific Ocean. It is famous for its nearly 1,000 statues called moai. Its people are the Rapa Nui. Recent research has challenged the old story that an ecological collapse drove the islanders to war and cannibalism.

The Catholic Church had an assimilation policy (as in many other colonized countries) which was used to break the spiritual and cultural identity of Aborigines (and indigenous cultures worldwide) by removing tens of thousands of children from their parents. They apologized to Aborigines for the “stolen generation.” Recently Canada (2008), Pope Francis (2022) and the United States (2024) have also apologized for this practice.

“For herself, she asked nothing”: Saint Teresa Margaret of the Sacred Heart, an eighteenth-century Carmelite nun of Florence, gave this counsel: “Always receive with equal contentment from God’s hand either consolations or sufferings, peace or distress, health or illness. Ask nothing, refuse nothing, but always be ready to do and to suffer anything that comes from His Providence.”

“even as the canker eats the rose, as gold and silver rust”: Shakespeare’s Sonnet 35 says “loathsome canker lives in sweetest bud,” the canker being a worm or blight. James 5:3 warns the rich, “Your gold and silver is cankered; and the rust of them shall be a witness against you,” and Matthew 6:19 speaks of treasures on earth “where moth and rust doth corrupt.”

"What had God done? How should God answer for God's omissions?" presses the hardest question in the book, divine justice weighed against God's silence and what he has left undone.

The line joining "the strength of the unicorn" to a God who would "bring us out of Egypt" layers the wild strength of the re'em (rendered "unicorn" in the King James Version), the Exodus, and the wilderness as a testing ground, deliverance longed for and not yet arrived.

“were we not still in Egypt, the waste-howling wilderness”: Miss MacIntosh’s speech is a weave of scripture. God led Israel out of Egypt, and Deuteronomy 32:10 says he found Jacob “in the waste howling wilderness.” “I was shapen in iniquity” is Psalm 51:5, “the mystery of iniquity” is 2 Thessalonians 2:7, “the joy of the hypocrite but for a moment” is Job 20:5, and “In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread” is Genesis 3:19. See The Bible.

The hope that "iniquity should not be found on our lips" until "the laborer should be paid for the sweat of his brow" voices a longing for an end to sin and a world set right.

"The joys of the hypocrite were but for the moment," set against "the joys of the honest man" that last, draws on Job 20:5, the brief triumph of the wicked against the lasting worth of honest living.

“no man knew, unless it was Mr. Spitzer, the hour of his death”: “Watch therefore, for ye know neither the day nor the hour wherein the Son of man cometh” (Matthew 25:13).
