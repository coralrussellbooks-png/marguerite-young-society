Definition from research

The line about a God "Who had God's limitations," who "had left many things uncreated," is the novel's boldest theological stroke. It gives up the all-powerful Creator for a finite one, a God with edges, a creation left open and unfinished. The idea runs against orthodoxy and toward the book's steady questioning of how much of the world was ever truly made or governed.

The line that "labor was man's lot upon this earth," set beside a God who "made the furrows on the sea," leans on Ecclesiastes, work as a God-given portion and toil accepted as part of the order of things.

God as a "harness-maker" who "hung her harness with tinkling bells" pictures providence as a craftsman keeping watch, a maker who can always find his creature.

"ours was not the problem of questioning God's remotest mysteries" states the plain Christian humility that runs through the book: live rightly, and leave what cannot be known alone.

"Perhaps God's understanding never had been God's" turns even omniscience into a question, another crack in the traditional picture of God.

"whether a camel shall enter through the needle's eye or a rich man through the gates of heaven" is Jesus's saying from Matthew 19:24, the hard odds of the rich entering the kingdom.

"the argument as to how many angels might stand on a needle's point" nods to the old satire of medieval hairsplitting, learned debate spent on nothing.

"There would be no light without the darkness" makes the plain point that contrast is what lets either be seen.

"God's business had never been ours, perhaps had never been God's" holds humility and radical doubt in the same breath, and catches the book's central tension between faith and skepticism whole.

The counsel to "mind his own knitting, his own cooking, his own household duties even though he had no house" carries the ethic of plain, honest living, an echo of the call to deny oneself and take up one's cross.

"To kiss the rod was better than not to know self-discipline" urges the acceptance of discipline over the illusion that a person is free and answerable to no one.

"We must not waste our lives in the vain expectation" of what may never come recalls the Parable of the Rich Fool and the futility of hoarding hope against an empty future.

The line joining "the strength of the unicorn" to a God who would "bring us out of Egypt" layers the wild strength of the re'em (rendered "unicorn" in the King James Version), the Exodus, and the wilderness as a testing ground, deliverance longed for and not yet arrived.

"The joys of the hypocrite were but for the moment," set against "the joys of the honest man" that last, draws on Job 20:5, the brief triumph of the wicked against the lasting worth of honest living.

"What had God done? How should God answer for God's omissions?" presses the hardest question in the book, divine justice weighed against God's silence and what he has left undone.

The hope that "iniquity should not be found on our lips" until "the laborer should be paid for the sweat of his brow" voices a longing for an end to sin and a world set right.

Miss MacIntosh prefers Pilgrim's Progress "to even the Word of God." She holds that scripture "should be taken with a grain of salt," open to too many readings and thick with human exaggeration, its very angels "falling into a dark sea." Bunyan's allegory of the soul's journey suits her better than doctrine, and her wariness of the Bible as a handled, fallible thing is the book's questioning stance put plainly.

"The easiest way was the coward's way, the path of least resistance," broad and well paved, set against "the path of the brave," narrow and thorned and lonely, is Matthew 7:13-14, the wide road to ruin and the narrow one to life.

St. Simeon Stylites, the Syrian hermit who lived thirty-seven years atop a pillar, stands for extreme ascetic devotion.

Daniel, the exile who survived the lions' den for refusing to stop praying, stands for faith kept under pressure and God's protection of the faithful.

St. Augustine, bishop of Hippo, appears with his City of God, which set the heavenly society of the faithful against the earthly City of Man.
