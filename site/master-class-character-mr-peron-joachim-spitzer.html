Mr. Peron/Joachim Spitzer: The Axle the Book Turns On

← The Characters · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Catherine Snowden Cartwheel →

Spoiler note: This portrait is written for readers who have finished the novel. It discusses the character’s whole story, including how it ends.

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

He came to handle the body that was never found. That is how Mr. Spitzer enters the part of this reading that matters most: sideways, on someone else's emergency, doing the paperwork of a disappearance. It is the right way to meet him, because sideways and on someone else's business is how he does everything.

He is an aging, portly gentleman, elegant in an old-fashioned way that does not match the shabbiness he lives in: a high silk hat, a soft cape, an ivory-headed cane, a waxed mustache over a double chin. One ear is dead, so he is forever turning the live side toward whoever speaks. He is a lawyer of a particular kind, tracing lost heirs of lost estates, some of which may never have existed. He is an executor of the dead. He is a composer, though his music is silence, written down and never meant to be played. He is a bachelor who once offered marriage to Catherine, Vera's mother, and was refused, partly because she loved his brother and partly because she could never be certain which brother he was. His twin is dead. He carries two watches, one ticking and one long stopped, and is not always sure which of them is his.

Every item about him is strange, and every item is delivered as a plain fact. That combination, strangeness stated flatly, is the characterization of the man, and it is the first clue to why he matters.

Nothing happens to him. Around him, everything happens. Catherine dreams her life away on opium and mourns her dead. Esther is pregnant for years and never delivers. Miss MacIntosh wears a lifelong disguise and then walks into the sea. And Mr. Spitzer sits at the foot of the bed, consults his watch, is scolded for his slowness, reddens, waits, and goes home. Things are done to him. He does very little back. Sort the characters by dramatic event and he comes in last. He is the still one.

And Young gives him more of the book than anyone else. In a novel crowded with drowning nursemaids and everlasting pregnancies and opium visions, the most pages by far are spent on a quiet lawyer sitting in a chair. This is the disproportion, and the disproportion is the argument. The book is not really about events; events are weather, and they blow across a life and move on. It is an argument about what a person is, and to show what a person is one does not point at the people things happen to. One points at the one who stays, who holds still, through whom all the weather passes. Length, here, is just the book telling a reader where to look.

It would be easy to hear that and decide we have simply found a second hub, another still center to set beside Miss MacIntosh. That is the wrong lesson, and the difference is the whole point. Miss MacIntosh was a hub, just as Marguerite Young said,the thing at the visible center that everyone watches. A hub can be removed; hers came off with her wig, and the wheel kept turning. Mr. Spitzer is not watched. He is looked past. Nobody leans on him; they lean past him, toward the dead, toward the dream, toward the lost estate. He is not the thing at the center everyone sees. He is the thing running through the center that nobody sees. That is an axle, not a hub, and the difference is total. Pull the hub and the wheel keeps turning. Pull the axle and there is no wheel.

The two watches: time reaches him only through the dead, his brother's accurate watch running beside his own broken one. The twin: Peron is noise and appetite and the body, Joachim is silence and restraint and the law, and Young puts them in one womb to make her picture of a single consciousness split against itself. The executor's work: he pays a dead man's debts out of his own torn pockets, keeps faith with the dead, refuses to let their affairs be closed, which is exactly what the novel does on every page and what Vera does as she narrates. The circling prose: he cannot take the shortest line between two points, he makes long navigations around and around a subject, and Young's sentences are built the same way, so that in Mr. Spitzer style and psychology are the same thing. Each facet is the plural self, the person who is not one, drawn from another angle. He is the theme walking around Boston in a cape.

At the very end, carrying the dying Catherine into the garden by the sea, Mr. Spitzer confesses that he is Peron, and that it was Joachim, the lawyer and musician, who died. He says the reason no one has heard his music since Peron's death is that he was Peron all along, that he was never a musician and never a lawyer, that he knew not one note from another and scarcely the plaintiff from the accused, and that the whole Joachim identity was almost a celestial joke. And it comes too late. Catherine has already died, an hour before, at the start of his whispers. She will never know he was not Joachim.

Young does not certify the confession. Her phrase is that all had been deception, even to him. Even to him. The man who spent the longest section of the book unable to say whether he was alive or dead, himself or his brother, ends by naming himself the twin everyone thought was in the grave, and the naming is just one more bet from a gambler who cannot see his own cards. The crossing point does not resolve into a single name. It confesses that it never had one. The plural self does not get solved at the end. It makes its final move.

Mr. Spitzer refuses the processed grief that would let him heal, because Young writes that if his grief left him, stole out like a tenant from an old house, he would no longer be Mr. Spitzer; the grief is not something he has, it is what he is made of. He witnesses the dead without concluding they are gone, provisions them with cheap cheroots while he smokes his own perfumed brand, greets them good-night and good-morning. He builds the unheard music anyway, drowned by his brother's noise through a wall thin as a membrane. Grief that will not end, witnessing without the reward of being witnessed, building without arrival: the Radical Immanence pillars are not illustrated by him one at a time. They live in one threadbare gentleman at once, which is why his section is the longest. He is the crossing point, and a crossing point is where a structure needs the most material to hold.

Young could have told us Spitzer was uncertain of himself. Instead she built the uncertainty into the prose, so that reading him is the closest the novel comes to explaining, from the inside, why it is written the way it is. The circling, the deferral, the clause added to clause and arriving late or not at all, is not a description of his mind. It is his mind. That is the lesson hiding in the length: when style and character become the same thing, you cannot render the man briefly, because brevity would assume a stable subject standing behind the sentence, and he has none. The size of the section is not a flaw to explain away. It is the finger pointing.
