Chapter 18

← Chapter 17 · Home · Chapter 19 →

Content note: This chapter strongly suggests that the young Vera has been sexually assaulted, and it shows an adult frightening a child into silence.

Summary

Through the rest of the night, Miss MacIntosh holds Vera and talks, in two voices: the familiar one of proverbs and geography lessons, and a raw, grieving one that cries for the left breast “already gone,” for her hair, and for “one kiss,” all she ever asked. A red wig hangs on the chair post. She insists nothing happened, that it was all a dream. She covers Vera’s head with a pillow, leaves “the heavy, claw-shaped mark of her hand” upon her forehead “to teach you… charity,” and swears she will kill Vera if she ever tells. By dawn she is quizzing her on Michigan’s industries and the Battle of Shiloh, and promising that all will be as it was. As Vera falls asleep, Miss MacIntosh whispers that she lost her hair in Vera’s service, that she once had a pale gold mane, and that she is not as old as Vera thinks. Vera carries the secret as her sin, and ever after tries to be charitable.

Key passages

From the research

  • Vera confronts “the phantom of change,” a figure that embodies both the fixed and true, yet also the unsettling reality beneath illusion. This passage explores the idea that what we take as reality is often a self-deception, with the “curtain of illusion” stripped away, revealing the naked truth that each person wears a mask, pushing them into “the imperial darkness.” This emphasizes human vulnerability and the elusive nature of identity and reality.
  • Miss MacIntosh, initially a “red-headed, plain nursemaid,” becomes a complex, almost demonic figure: bald, naked, and both terrifying and intimate. The lover who emerges is described as neither man nor woman but an “angelic demon,” symbolizing the secret, hidden aspects within each person, the “other self” that is always present yet unreachable. This passage reveals the interplay of identity, gender, and inner conflict.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s bald, furrowed head “looming above me in the foggy darkness,” with her “false teeth clicking, clicking like old bones or dice in the wind,” represents a haunting presence of mortality and decay. Her voice moans about ephemerality and the transient nature of life, blending imagery of death, loss, and cosmic insignificance. This creates a powerful atmosphere of dread and existential despair.
  • Miss MacIntosh delivers a harsh reflection on mortality, sin, and human frailty: “One leak sinks a ship, and one sin sinks a sinner,” and “God pity us all,” underscoring the inevitability of death and the universal vulnerability of humanity. Her admonitions and proverbs touch on themes of fate, loss, and the futility of resistance, adding a somber philosophical layer to the narrative.
  • Despite the terrifying nocturnal revelations, Miss MacIntosh insists that everything will “be all right” in the morning, that the night’s horrors are but a dream, and life will resume its “fixed schedule.” This tension between nightmare and waking life reflects Vera’s struggle to reconcile the profound encounter with the mundane reality of everyday existence.
  • Baldness here symbolizes a stripping away of pretense and beauty, a harsh exposure to the raw truth beneath appearances. Vera’s painful awareness of Miss MacIntosh’s “essential baldness” represents a loss of innocence and the confrontation with the unvarnished realities of life and human nature.
  • In the closing passage, Miss MacIntosh, holding Vera, reflects on her lost hair as a metaphor for the passage of time and aging. She poses a poignant question about the nature of age itself, “Alas, when shall I grow old?”, suggesting the elusive and paradoxical quality of time and existence.
  • “I have lost my hair in your service.” The nursemaid who threatened to kill Vera if she told turns, in the last lines, into a woman confessing what her service cost her: “When I came here, I had all my own hair, a thick mane of it, pale gold hanging down my back.”

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “Memory sometimes provides the one flower more than ever blossomed. Memory sometimes omits the only flower there really was.” Which does Vera’s memory of this night do?
  • “Perhaps, however, the lack of deception is always another deception.” What deception is left when the wig is off?
  • Miss MacIntosh insists, “Nothing that you think is happening is happening!” Why does she need Vera to believe it?

Characters

  • She marks Vera’s forehead “to teach you… charity.” What lesson does Vera take?
  • “If ever I told what I had seen, she would kill me.” How does the threat change what Vera remembers?
  • “I have lost my hair in your service.” What else has Miss MacIntosh given up?

Connections

  • The “claw-shaped mark” echoes the “claws upon her forehead” Vera imagined in Chapter 10. What has passed from one woman to the other?
  • The quiz questions of Chapter 13 (“Where is North Dakota?”) return here at dawn. What are they for?
  • Vera’s dream made Miss MacIntosh the bridegroom (Chapter 10). How does that dream come back here?

Reflection

  • “Once you see it, you can’t unsee it.” What does a child lose when she learns an adult’s secret?
  • Is a secret kept under threat still one’s own?
  • Can tenderness and harm come from the same person on the same night?

Research: Characters

Vera Cartwheel

  • Questioning reality, memory, and self-identity.
  • Experiences a profound revelation and confrontation with hidden truths.
  • Shows vulnerability, fear, and eventual resolve to accept and understand.
  • She carries “the heavy, claw-shaped mark” of Miss MacIntosh’s hand and resolves to be charitable, “as a retribution for my cruelty.”

Miss MacIntosh

  • Initially perceived as a plain, red-headed nursemaid.
  • Transforms into a bald, frightening figure symbolizing raw truth and loss.
  • Acts as a guide or interlocutor, mixing sternness with intimacy.
  • Represents both nurturing and harsh realities of life, death, and change.
  • Embodies themes of charity, mortality, and the limits of human understanding.
  • Her red wig hangs on the chair post, and she mourns the left breast “already gone.”
  • She threatens to kill Vera if she tells, then says she lost her hair in Vera’s service.

The Phantom Lover / The Secret Man

  • A spectral figure representing the hidden aspects of self.
  • Neither fully man nor woman; embodies the duality of human nature.
  • Symbolizes the unrecognizable parts of identity and love.

The Old Blind Sheep Dog

  • The old blind sheep dog appears only briefly and indirectly. It may function as a potent symbol of failing protection, limited perception, and the human struggle to confront elusive and overwhelming existential truths. Its presence underscores the chapter’s meditation on vulnerability, loss, and the elusive nature of reality and memory.

Research: Themes

The Phantom of Change and Illusion versus Reality

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Vera encounters a “phantom of change” (something fixed, undeniable yet elusive in understanding).
  • Illusion is portrayed paradoxically as “the only reality,” with self-deception being the “only rose” (symbol of beauty and pain).
  • The external world is likened to “a mad man’s dream,” emphasizing the fragility and elusiveness of perceived reality.

Memory and its Limitations

See: Memory and the Past

  • Memory is depicted as incomplete, evolving, and surrounded by the unknown.
  • Vera questions the accuracy of remembered events, hinting that memory may omit or alter essential details.
  • The idea that “the dream is never complete” and that new realities arise from what is missing in memory.

Duality of Human Nature: The Secret Man and Woman

See: Duality and Doubles · Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos

  • The concept that within every woman is a secret man, and within every man, a secret woman.
  • This duality is visualized through the phantom lover who is neither fully man nor woman but an “angelic demon” representing the hidden self.

Fear, Vulnerability, and the Mask of Identity

See: Identity and the Self · Fear and Courage

  • Vera experiences fear and confusion confronting Miss MacIntosh’s bald, naked, and terrifying form, symbolizing raw truth stripped of pretense.
  • The “mask” everyone wears shields them from their true selves, leading to isolation and existential loneliness.

Mortality and the Inevitability of Death

See: Death and Mortality

  • Dialogue and imagery emphasize transience and mortality: “Oh, how ephemeral are we!” and “The brief candle goes out.”
  • The inevitability of death and the futility of resistance are central, with Vera urged to “be silent as the corpse” and accept loss.

Charity and Understanding

See: The Search for Meaning

  • Despite cruelty and misunderstanding, Vera resolves to be charitable towards others, especially towards Miss MacIntosh, recognizing her essential vulnerability and “baldness.”

Return to Normalcy and the Persistence of Routine

See: Order, Chaos, and Discipline

  • Miss MacIntosh insists that the strange night is a dream and that normal life will resume with the morning.
  • The repeated references to daily routines, domestic tasks, and practical lessons reflect the human need to cling to order amid chaos.

Vera reflects on searching for her like searching for a lost star on a distant horizon. 2) the elusive nature of truth and reality, where the tangible world seems like an illusion and the “lost star” represents the truth or reality that continually slips away, much like a star that fades or a dream that is never fully grasped. 3) the fragmentation of identity and memory, as the narrator and other characters grapple with the idea that individual selves are illusions, and that the “lost star” could be a symbol for the self or soul that is divided, missing, or searching for wholeness. 4) A broader spiritual and existential metaphor about the human condition: the search for meaning, connection, and a unifying harmony amid chaos and dislocation. The lost star is a symbol of hope and despair, of an ultimate harmony that might be glimpsed but never fully attained. It is a powerful image that encapsulates the novel’s central motifs of loss, searching, and the tension between illusion and reality. It signifies something precious and luminous that has been lost to the characters and yet continues to influence their inner lives and quests.
  • “Where was Tobias Smollett?”: Tobias Smollett (1721-1771) was a Scottish author known for his picaresque novels. Miss MacIntosh names him in her dawn quiz, among places, battles, and stars.
  • “it was all a dream through which I had lived”: Miss MacIntosh and Vera’s mother Catherine seem to agree that whether a person decides to fill their head with factual knowledge or live in an imaginary world it is all a dream of living and dying. Human beings ultimately go through life not knowing why they are here or not wanting to know. They are described as vertical and horizontal which implies that there is a middle way or intersection between the two that can exist and would be a healthier way of reasoning. Life is both a dream and reality, and human beings oscillate between these states (vertical and horizontal) without full clarity. The healthiest reasoning may lie in embracing this intersection, this balance, which allows for both grounded action and acceptance of mystery. It invites readers to consider the interplay of dream and reality in human life and the possibility of living authentically through a synthesis of the vertical and horizontal ways of being presented between characters at different times in the novel.

The directions of the compass represents infinite possibility, the present/past/future or the direction of your True North. There are two North Poles (magnetic and true). It signifies the challenge of navigating through the complexities of life and consciousness, where certainties dissolve, and one must find or create meaning amid flux and paradox.   • “robbing Peter to pay Paul”: or other versions that have developed over the centuries such as “to borrow from Peter to pay Paul”, and “to unclothe Peter to clothe Paul”, are phrases meaning to take from one person or thing to give to another, especially when it results in the elimination of one debt by incurring another. Within the context of the chapter, it emphasizes the frustration with life’s compromises and the lack of genuine progress or resolution in their difficult circumstances. It conveys the sense of being caught in a cycle of compromises and sacrifices that do not truly improve the situation but merely shift burdens around, reflecting the broader themes of futility, suffering, and the search for meaning in the novel.

In the chapter he is a representation of reason, science, and the human quest for comprehension amidst the mystical and often inscrutable realities that Vera confronts in her dreamlike journey. His figure anchors the narrative’s oscillation between the mystical and the rational, the historical and the personal.
  • “O, temporal O, mores!”: Cicero’s “O tempora, o mores!” (“Oh, the times! Oh, the customs!”), from his speech Against Catiline (63 BC), garbled by Miss MacIntosh.
  • “for he is unworthy to live who lives only for himself and not somewhat in others”: On Christian Liberty by Martin Luther. It expresses a moral and philosophical viewpoint about the nature of a meaningful life. It suggests that a person who lives solely for their own self-interest, without regard or connection to other people, lacks true worth or dignity. This idea aligns with Miss MacIntosh’s practical and no-nonsense worldview, emphasizing selflessness, community, and service to others as fundamental values. She believes that life’s purpose is found in caring for and contributing to the well-being of others (the poor orphans of this world) and that the “fool’s paradise” of self-indulgence and isolation is not the way to live a good or worthy life. The phrase means that a life confined within selfishness and isolation is considered lacking, and true worth comes from empathy, love, and living in connection with other souls. It underscores one of the novel’s recurrent themes about the importance of shared humanity and moral responsibility toward others.
  • “if ever I told what I had seen, she would kill me”: A person who rapes another often threatens their life or the life of someone they care about in order to ensure they will never tell anyone what happened.
  • “a man’s bedroom was a man’s castle”: “A man’s house is his castle” (Sir Edward Coke, 1628). See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “Common sense was the rarest jewel, more precious than rubies”: “For the price of wisdom is above rubies” (Job 28:18). See The Book of Job.
  • “I needed a father to guide me”: Miss MacIntosh believes Vera should have had a father and this might refer to a husband since in earlier marriages a daughter is passed from her father to her husband as her guardian. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “tempus fugit”: Tempus fugit is Latin for “time flies”.
  • “Age is the most difficult thing to achieve”: Life expectancy in 1900 was 47 years and in 1950 rose to 68 years. Neil deGrasse Tyson has said, “The fact that you and I are alive is against stupendous odds. One day we will die, and that’s sad, but there are people who will never die because they were never born. We’re the lucky ones, we get to die” (in conversation with Vikas Shah, Thought Economics). It reflects a profound meditation on the nature of aging beyond mere passage of time or physical growth. It suggests that truly “achieving” age is not simply about getting older in years but involves the complex process of enduring life’s challenges, transformations, and accumulations of experience, suffering, and wisdom. This line is spoken by Miss MacIntosh during a moment when she is discussing the realities of life, death, and change. The phrase implies that aging is a difficult accomplishment because it requires not just survival but also the acceptance of loss, hardship, and the inevitable changes that come with time: both physical and spiritual. Age is portrayed as a kind of achievement or victory, but one that demands courage and resilience. It encompasses the endurance of sorrow, the relinquishing of illusions, and the navigation through life’s uncertainties and pains. Miss MacIntosh, who embodies practical wisdom and fortitude, acknowledges that growing old is not a passive process but an active, often painful engagement with these realities. This idea contrasts with simpler notions of aging as a natural and easy progression; instead, it highlights the complexity and difficulty of truly becoming aged in a meaningful sense: integrating one’s experiences, confronting mortality, and maintaining strength despite decline. It conveys the idea that aging involves profound existential effort and acceptance, far beyond the mere counting of years.
    • Society shapes human health because social factors like income, education, stress levels, access to healthcare, neighborhood environment, social support networks, and cultural norms significantly influence people’s health behaviors, prevailing marketing around lifestyle choices, and overall health outcomes, essentially determining the level of health an individual can achieve within a given community. The World Health Organization calls these the social determinants of health, “the conditions in which people are born, grow, work, live, and age.”
