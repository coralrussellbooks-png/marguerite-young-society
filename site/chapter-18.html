To All My Darlings

Chapter 18

← Chapter 17 · Home · Chapter 19 →

Content note: This chapter strongly suggests that the young Vera has been sexually assaulted, and it shows an adult frightening a child into silence.

Summary

Through the rest of the night, Miss MacIntosh holds Vera and talks, in two voices: the familiar one of proverbs and geography lessons, and a raw, grieving one that cries for the left breast “already gone,” for her hair, and for “one kiss,” all she ever asked. A red wig hangs on the chair post. She insists nothing happened, that it was all a dream. She covers Vera’s head with a pillow, leaves “the heavy, claw-shaped mark of her hand” upon her forehead “to teach you… charity,” and swears she will kill Vera if she ever tells. By dawn she is quizzing her on Michigan’s industries and the Battle of Shiloh, and promising that all will be as it was. As Vera falls asleep, Miss MacIntosh whispers that she lost her hair in Vera’s service, that she once had a pale gold mane, and that she is not as old as Vera thinks. Vera carries the secret as her sin, and ever after tries to be charitable.

Key passages

From the research

  • Vera confronts “the phantom of change,” a figure that embodies both the fixed and true, yet also the unsettling reality beneath illusion. This passage explores the idea that what we take as reality is often a self-deception, with the “curtain of illusion” stripped away, revealing the naked truth that each person wears a mask, pushing them into “the imperial darkness.” This emphasizes human vulnerability and the elusive nature of identity and reality.
  • Miss MacIntosh, initially a “red-headed, plain nursemaid,” becomes a complex, almost demonic figure: bald, naked, and both terrifying and intimate. The lover who emerges is described as neither man nor woman but an “angelic demon,” symbolizing the secret, hidden aspects within each person, the “other self” that is always present yet unreachable. This passage reveals the interplay of identity, gender, and inner conflict.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s bald, furrowed head “looming above me in the foggy darkness,” with her “false teeth clicking, clicking like old bones or dice in the wind,” represents a haunting presence of mortality and decay. Her voice moans about ephemerality and the transient nature of life, blending imagery of death, loss, and cosmic insignificance. This creates a powerful atmosphere of dread and existential despair.
  • Miss MacIntosh delivers a harsh reflection on mortality, sin, and human frailty: “One leak sinks a ship, and one sin sinks a sinner,” and “God pity us all,” underscoring the inevitability of death and the universal vulnerability of humanity. Her admonitions and proverbs touch on themes of fate, loss, and the futility of resistance, adding a somber philosophical layer to the narrative.
  • Despite the terrifying nocturnal revelations, Miss MacIntosh insists that everything will “be all right” in the morning, that the night’s horrors are but a dream, and life will resume its “fixed schedule.” This tension between nightmare and waking life reflects Vera’s struggle to reconcile the profound encounter with the mundane reality of everyday existence.
  • Baldness here symbolizes a stripping away of pretense and beauty, a harsh exposure to the raw truth beneath appearances. Vera’s painful awareness of Miss MacIntosh’s “essential baldness” represents a loss of innocence and the confrontation with the unvarnished realities of life and human nature.
  • In the closing passage, Miss MacIntosh, holding Vera, reflects on her lost hair as a metaphor for the passage of time and aging. She poses a poignant question about the nature of age itself, “Alas, when shall I grow old?”, suggesting the elusive and paradoxical quality of time and existence.
  • “I have lost my hair in your service.” The nursemaid who threatened to kill Vera if she told turns, in the last lines, into a woman confessing what her service cost her: “When I came here, I had all my own hair, a thick mane of it, pale gold hanging down my back.”

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “Memory sometimes provides the one flower more than ever blossomed. Memory sometimes omits the only flower there really was.” Which does Vera’s memory of this night do?
  • “Perhaps, however, the lack of deception is always another deception.” What deception is left when the wig is off?
  • Miss MacIntosh insists, “Nothing that you think is happening is happening!” Why does she need Vera to believe it?

Characters

  • She marks Vera’s forehead “to teach you… charity.” What lesson does Vera take?
  • “If ever I told what I had seen, she would kill me.” How does the threat change what Vera remembers?
  • “I have lost my hair in your service.” What else has Miss MacIntosh given up?

Connections

  • The “claw-shaped mark” echoes the “claws upon her forehead” Vera imagined in Chapter 10. What has passed from one woman to the other?
  • The quiz questions of Chapter 13 (“Where is North Dakota?”) return here at dawn. What are they for?
  • Vera’s dream made Miss MacIntosh the bridegroom (Chapter 10). How does that dream come back here?

Reflection

  • “Once you see it, you can’t unsee it.” What does a child lose when she learns an adult’s secret?
  • Is a secret kept under threat still one’s own?
  • Can tenderness and harm come from the same person on the same night?

Research: Characters

Vera Cartwheel

  • Questioning reality, memory, and self-identity.
  • Experiences a profound revelation and confrontation with hidden truths.
  • Shows vulnerability, fear, and eventual resolve to accept and understand.
  • She carries “the heavy, claw-shaped mark” of Miss MacIntosh’s hand and resolves to be charitable, “as a retribution for my cruelty.”

Miss MacIntosh

  • Initially perceived as a plain, red-headed nursemaid.
  • Transforms into a bald, frightening figure symbolizing raw truth and loss.
  • Acts as a guide or interlocutor, mixing sternness with intimacy.
  • Represents both nurturing and harsh realities of life, death, and change.
  • Embodies themes of charity, mortality, and the limits of human understanding.
  • Her red wig hangs on the chair post, and she mourns the left breast “already gone.”
  • She threatens to kill Vera if she tells, then says she lost her hair in Vera’s service.

The Phantom Lover and the Secret Man

  • A spectral figure representing the hidden aspects of self.
  • Neither fully man nor woman; embodies the duality of human nature.
  • Symbolizes the unrecognizable parts of identity and love.

The Old Blind Sheep Dog

  • The old blind sheep dog appears only briefly and indirectly. It may function as a potent symbol of failing protection, limited perception, and the human struggle to confront elusive and overwhelming existential truths. Its presence underscores the chapter’s meditation on vulnerability, loss, and the elusive nature of reality and memory.
  • Vera hears him barking “perhaps at a spar, perhaps at the last falling star… or some danger greater than any I could have imagined.”

Research: Themes

The Phantom of Change and Illusion versus Reality

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Vera encounters a “phantom of change” (something fixed, undeniable yet elusive in understanding).
  • Illusion is portrayed paradoxically as “the only reality,” with self-deception being the “only rose” (symbol of beauty and pain).
  • The external world is likened to “a mad man’s dream,” emphasizing the fragility and elusiveness of perceived reality.

Memory and its Limitations

See: Memory and the Past

  • Memory is depicted as incomplete, evolving, and surrounded by the unknown.
  • Vera questions the accuracy of remembered events, hinting that memory may omit or alter essential details.
  • The idea that “the dream is never complete” and that new realities arise from what is missing in memory.

Duality of Human Nature: The Secret Man and Woman

See: Duality and Doubles

  • The concept that within every woman is a secret man, and within every man, a secret woman.
  • This duality is visualized through the phantom lover who is neither fully man nor woman but an “angelic demon” representing the hidden self.

Fear, Vulnerability, and the Mask of Identity

See: Identity and the Self · Fear and Courage

  • Vera experiences fear and confusion confronting Miss MacIntosh’s bald, naked, and terrifying form, symbolizing raw truth stripped of pretense.
  • The “mask” everyone wears shields them from their true selves, leading to isolation and existential loneliness.

Mortality and the Inevitability of Death

See: Death and Mortality

  • Dialogue and imagery emphasize transience and mortality: “Oh, how ephemeral are we!” and “The brief candle goes out.”
  • The inevitability of death and the futility of resistance are central, with Vera urged to “be silent as the corpse” and accept loss.

Charity and Understanding

See: The Search for Meaning

  • Despite cruelty and misunderstanding, Vera resolves to be charitable toward others, especially toward Miss MacIntosh, recognizing her essential vulnerability and “baldness.”

Return to Normalcy and the Persistence of Routine

See: Order, Chaos, and Discipline

  • Miss MacIntosh insists that the strange night is a dream and that normal life will resume with the morning.
  • The repeated references to daily routines, domestic tasks, and practical lessons reflect the human need to cling to order amid chaos.

Research: Resources

  • “some angelic demon”: Young may be referring to the Nephilim. In Genesis 6:4, they are born when “the sons of God came in unto the daughters of men.” Later legend made them the children of fallen angels and mortal women, at once angelic and demonic. See Genesis and Angels and Archangels.
  • “That night, the curtain of illusion had been stripped away”: Vera is describing what others in literature have said. Sarah Dessen writes, “You can’t unlearn something, even if you want to. You know what you know” (Along for the Ride, 2009). Arundhati Roy writes, “The trouble is that once you see it, you can’t unsee it” (Power Politics, 2001).
  • “Would that my lover had been a man!”: Vera’s realizations this night seem to be described again as a sexual act, something very intimate and physical. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “such a man as is hidden in the woman”: Jung named the woman within the man the anima, and the man within the woman the animus. See Freud and Jung and Duality and Doubles.
    • Young may also be drawing from the Chinese philosophy of Yin and Yang which is a concept of dualism, describing how obviously opposite or contrary forces may actually be complementary, interconnected, and interdependent in the natural world, and how they may give rise to each other as they interrelate to one another.
  • “one who combs her long gold hair upon the rock”: Heine’s Lorelei (1824), the Rhine siren who combs her golden hair on the rock and draws boatmen to their deaths. Clemens Brentano first told of her in a ballad of 1801. The body “scaled with silver scales” that follows is the mermaid’s. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “Who was that old rapist if not Miss MacIntosh”: Imagery in this chapter hints that either physically, emotionally, or psychologically a rape took place. Vera because of her age and experience does not have the words to fully explain what has happened to her. See Miss MacIntosh.
  • “Oh, how ephemeral are we!”: Ephemeral means something is short-lived or temporary, and it can be used to describe the nature of life and relationships.
  • “Watchman, what of the night? Who goes there?”: “Watchman, what of the night?” (Isaiah 21:11), and the opening of Hamlet, “Who’s there?” See The Bible, Shakespeare, and the Chapter 43 resources.
  • “Dead men tell no tales”: An old proverb, in print by 1664. See Death and Mortality.
  • “wrapped in this house of clay”: “How much less in them that dwell in houses of clay, whose foundation is in the dust” (Job 4:19). See The Book of Job.
  • “Thus did sparrows fall”: “Are not two sparrows sold for a farthing? and one of them shall not fall on the ground without your Father” (Matthew 10:29). It is a poetic way to express the transience of life and the shared fate of all creatures within the vast cycle of existence portrayed in the novel. See The Bible.
  • “The left breast is already gone”: Miss MacIntosh talks about losing her left breast. William Halsted performed his first radical mastectomy in New York in 1882 and described the operation in 1894. There is no clear record of how earlier women survived the operation, or for how long. See Miss MacIntosh, Women, Gender, and Marriage, and the Chapter 17 resources.
  • “Oh, bear the sinner up! Oh, wash our sins away and leave us white as snow!”: “Though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow” (Isaiah 1:18). Compare 1 Peter 2:24, Psalm 38:4, Galatians 6:2, and Lamentations 5:7. It is a plea for cleansing and forgiveness. Just before, “Oh, plunge me in the purple flood” echoes William Cowper’s hymn: “And sinners, plunged beneath that flood, / Lose all their guilty stains” (1772). See The Bible and the Chapter 3 resources.
  • “Oh, where’s What Cheer, Iowa”: Miss MacIntosh’s hometown. See Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest and the Chapter 9 resources.
  • “that hers was that knob where the breast was missing”: In the AMC series Dietland (2018), based on Sarai Walker’s novel, the character Julia (Tamara Tunie) reveals to Plum that she has had a double mastectomy, after a life of wearing “a mask,” as Tunie put it: “the hair, the makeup, the clothes.” See Miss MacIntosh.
  • “This earth is but the poor testing ground”: “The testing ground” of Chapter 15. See the Chapter 15 resources.
  • “and all oxen are our brothers”: “Thou shalt not see thy brother’s ox or his sheep go astray” (Deuteronomy 22:1). The phrase underscores the theme of humility before life’s challenges and the interconnectedness of all beings in their vulnerability and strength. See The Bible.
  • “Only the worm is clothed, and the worm is our little brother”: “I have said to corruption, Thou art my father: to the worm, Thou art my mother, and my sister” (Job 17:14). See The Book of Job.
  • “Where is Issachar”: Jacob’s sons, the tribes of Israel Vera learned to recite in Chapter 9. See The Bible and the Chapter 9 resources.
    • Issachar was a son of Jacob and Leah.
    • Reuben was Jacob’s firstborn son, by Leah. (The Flemish painter is Rubens.)
    • Nabal is in 1 Samuel 25, a story where he insults David and “the Lord smote Nabal, that he died.” His name means “fool” (1 Samuel 25:25), set beside “No fool like an old fool.”
  • “No fool like an old fool”: John Heywood’s proverb (1546). See the Chapter 10 resources.
  • “Out, out, brief candle!”: “Out, out, brief candle! / Life’s but a walking shadow” (Macbeth 5.5). See Shakespeare.
  • “Man was but a shadow and a breath”: “Man is like to vanity: his days are as a shadow that passeth away” (Psalm 144:4). It echoes the novel’s sense that a human life is as insubstantial as a shadow and as brief as a breath. See The Bible and the Chapter 15 resources.
  • “greaves of brass upon her legs, a target of brass between her shoulders, her spear like a weaver’s beam”: Goliath “had greaves of brass upon his legs, and a target of brass between his shoulders. And the staff of his spear was like a weaver’s beam” (1 Samuel 17:6-7). See The Bible.
  • “One leak sinks a ship, and one sin sinks a sinner”: Franklin’s Poor Richard (1745) has “A small leak will sink a great ship,” a warning against neglecting small expenses. Miss MacIntosh turns it from money to sin, where one small fault can sink a soul.
    • John Bunyan is credited with the saying “One leak will sink a ship: and one sin will destroy a sinner.”
  • “when the roll was called up yonder”: “When the Roll Is Called Up Yonder” is a popular Christian hymn that speaks of being ready for the day when God’s chosen ones gather in heaven. The song was written by James Milton Black in 1893 and has been translated into multiple languages and sung by various artists. The song’s lyrics are often interpreted as a reflection on the concept of the Book of Life and the promise of eternal life in heaven. It encourages believers to live a life of faith and service so that they will be among those who are called up yonder. Here the hymn becomes part of Miss MacIntosh’s shipwreck lament, where “God Himself should not know us, and our own parents should not know us.” The phrase underscores the idea that life and death, beginnings and ends, are intertwined and that ultimately, all must face a final accounting beyond earthly existence.
  • “we are sinned against more than we are sinning”: “I am a man / More sinned against than sinning” (King Lear 3.2). See Shakespeare.
  • “Clubs are trumps”: The cards of Chapter 14. See Gambling and Playing Cards and the Chapter 14 resources.
  • “Luck is for the few, but death is for the many”: The phrase is a somber reminder of human mortality and the limits of worldly success, fitting into the novel’s larger themes of illusion, inevitability, and the search for meaning in the face of death.
  • “Never married, never buried. An old man’s love is a young maid’s sorrow”: Both lines are old proverbs, often found in folk traditions and songs, particularly in the context of warnings against marrying old men. This phrase encapsulates the sorrowful and ambiguous nature of love explored in the book, especially the idea that some loves are doomed to remain incomplete, bringing pain and sorrow rather than joy. It also hints at the novel’s recurring motif of lost or ghostly loves that continue to affect the living long after they have faded from ordinary life.
  • “The bald head is soonest shaved”: Miss MacIntosh turns old jokes about baldness on herself, here and in “No grass grows on a busy street.” See Hair, Wig, and Baldness.
  • “a fool’s paradise”: The phrase is Middle English, and the earliest known use is in the Paston Letters of 1462. In Romeo and Juliet, the Nurse warns Romeo not to “lead her into a fool’s paradise” (2.4). Miss MacIntosh tells Vera she was “living in a fool’s paradise, and now you know there is no answer.” See Shakespeare.
  • “Heaven’s nets are woven very wide”: The Tao Te Ching, chapter 73, in one common translation: “Heaven’s net is vast; though its meshes are wide, nothing slips through.” Miss MacIntosh finishes the thought, “but none shall escape them.” Indra’s Net appears in the Atharva Veda, and Huayan Buddhism made it the image of a world where each jewel in an endless net reflects every other. It means that the net of divine grace or judgment is extensive and all-encompassing, offering no easy escape from one’s spiritual destiny. It underscores themes of universality, inevitability, and the encompassing nature of divine authority in the novel. See Taoism and Zhuangzi, Hinduism, and Buddhism.
  • “this life was all a dream”: The idea runs through many works:
    • Pedro Calderón de la Barca’s Life Is a Dream (1636) is a famous play that uses the metaphor of life as a dream to explore themes of free will, fate, and the nature of reality. The play’s central character, Segismundo, is imprisoned in a tower and then released, experiencing both pleasure and suffering before being told it was all a dream. See the Chapter 2 resources.
    • Prospero’s “We are such stuff / As dreams are made on, and our little life / Is rounded with a sleep” (The Tempest 4.1) compares life to a dream.
    • Edgar Allan Poe’s “A Dream Within a Dream” explores the cyclical nature of dreams and the illusion of reality, further reinforcing the idea of life as a dream. See Edgar Allan Poe.
    • Ludwig Wittgenstein wrote in a diary entry of the early 1930s: “We are asleep. Our life is like a dream. But in our better hours we wake up just enough to realize that we are dreaming.” See Wittgenstein.
    • In Indian philosophy, the idea of Maya (illusion) suggests that our world is an illusion or a dream-like state. This signifies the novel’s exploration of existential uncertainty, the fragility of human experience, and the interplay between reality and illusion that permeates Vera’s world. See Hinduism.
  • “her fishnet bag with her Pilgrim’s Progress”: Miss MacIntosh’s Bunyan again. See the Chapter 3 resources.
  • “the last rose of the last summer on earth”: “The Last Rose of Summer” is a poem by the Irish poet Thomas Moore, published in 1813 and written while staying at Jenkinstown Castle in County Kilkenny, Ireland, where he was said to have been inspired by the China rose ‘Old Blush.’ It poignantly represents the final vestiges of youth, vitality, and love, standing on the brink of disappearance, much like the characters grappling with mortality and the fading of dreams. See Roses and Flowers and the Chapter 6 resources.
  • “There is no God”: “The fool hath said in his heart, There is no God” (Psalm 14:1). Diagoras of Melos, a fifth-century BC poet, was called “the Atheist” in antiquity. He was said to have mocked the Eleusinian Mysteries and was condemned at Athens. This phrase marks a moment of profound questioning or a crisis point in the narrative where the comforting presence of God is deeply questioned or felt to be absent, confronting the characters with the mystery of existence and the silence in the face of suffering. See The Bible.
  • “the true love is that which does not alter when it alteration finds”: Shakespeare wrote, “Love is not love / Which alters when it alteration finds… O no! it is an ever-fixed mark / That looks on tempests and is never shaken” (Sonnet 116). Young follows with “The true love wears an old shirt and is familiar.” See Shakespeare and the Chapter 6 resources.
  • “as if I myself had become Falada”: Vera’s pony of Chapter 16, named for the horse in the Grimm tale. See Falada, Horses and Carriages, and the Chapter 16 resources.
  • “the heavy, claw-shaped mark of her hand upon my forehead”: In Chapter 10, Vera imagined “claws upon her forehead like the claws of a lobster.” Now the claw marks Vera. See Miss MacIntosh and the Chapter 10 resources.
  • “I have tried to be charitable”: “And now abideth faith, hope, charity, these three; but the greatest of these is charity” (1 Corinthians 13:13). See The Bible.

  • “to stand with one foot in the sea and one foot on the shore”: “Men were deceivers ever, / One foot in sea and one on shore, / To one thing constant never” (Much Ado About Nothing 2.3). See Shakespeare.
  • “all flesh, she said, is as the burned grass”: “For all flesh is as grass, and all the glory of man as the flower of grass. The grass withereth, and the flower thereof falleth away” (1 Peter 1:24). This metaphor, spoken by Miss MacIntosh during a moment of intense revelation and struggle with Vera (the narrator), equates human flesh with grass that has been burned. Something once living and vibrant but now scorched, fragile, and destined to wither away. It conveys the stark reality that human life, no matter how vibrant or full, is ultimately fleeting and fragile, much like grass that is scorched by fire and will not regrow.   • “every heart is married to death who is the bridegroom and the bride”: Death as the bridegroom, as in Chapter 15. It means that human life is fundamentally intertwined with death. This theme is woven throughout the novel, in the narrator’s reflections on life, death, and illusion. See Weddings and Funerals, Death and Mortality, and the Chapter 15 resources.

  • “Better be stung by a nettle than pricked by a rose”: An old proverb. The nettle’s sting is sharp but plain, while the rose, beautiful and seemingly gentle, wounds in hiding.

This line suggests that the one who seems to protect or shelter us (covering us with wings, like a bird shielding its young) can also cause harm or betrayal (biting us with his bill). It captures the paradox that those closest to us, who may offer care and safety, can also inflict pain and disappointment. The imagery implies vulnerability and the complicated nature of love and trust with those who protect have the power to hurt as well. The phrase conveys the idea that protection and harm are often intertwined in human relationships, emphasizing the complexity and sometimes painful duality of intimacy. See The Bible.

  • “an un-Christian institution for the sports of kings and fools”: The Roman circus was the arena for chariot racing, the greatest being the Circus Maximus, which held well over 150,000 spectators. Early Christian writers such as Tertullian, in On the Spectacles (around 200 AD), condemned the games as pagan idolatry, and tradition linked the arenas with the martyrdom of Christians.
  • “or were they angels in the furnace fire?”: The fourth figure in the fiery furnace, “like the Son of God” (Daniel 3:25), and Daniel in the lions’ den (Daniel 6). See The Bible.
  • “winter is summer’s heir”: The proverb of Chapter 15. See the Chapter 15 resources.
  • “The tallow is burning low. We have no oil.”: The foolish virgins of the parable say, “Give us of your oil; for our lamps are gone out” (Matthew 25:8), and miss the bridegroom. See The Bible and Weddings and Funerals.
  • “the rose at the North Pole is the rose immortal”: In a spiritual sense it signifies the coexistence of death and eternal life, the presence of enduring beauty and essence in a world otherwise marked by loss, coldness, and transience. It evokes a mystical, almost sacred dimension beneath the surface of harsh reality or an eternal “rose” blooming in the frozen heart of the North Pole. See Exploration and the Poles.
    • The Compass Rose (Rose of the Winds) represents spiritual direction, awakening, and discovery. The directions of the compass represents infinite possibility, the present/past/future or the direction of your True North. There are two North Poles (magnetic and true). It signifies the challenge of navigating through the complexities of life and consciousness, where certainties dissolve, and one must find or create meaning amid flux and paradox. This theme resonates with the broader novel’s exploration of illusion versus reality, identity, and the search for truth.
  • “What if the whole world should turn to salt?”: Lot’s wife “became a pillar of salt” (Genesis 19:26). See Genesis.
  • “A good husband makes a good wife”: Miss MacIntosh in this case is taking on the role of the groom in molding Vera to be the bride. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “robbing Peter to pay Paul”: or other versions that have developed over the centuries such as “to borrow from Peter to pay Paul”, and “to unclothe Peter to clothe Paul”, are phrases meaning to take from one person or thing to give to another, especially when it results in the elimination of one debt by incurring another. Within the context of the chapter, it emphasizes the frustration with life’s compromises and the lack of genuine progress or resolution in their difficult circumstances. It conveys the sense of being caught in a cycle of compromises and sacrifices that do not truly improve the situation but merely shift burdens around, reflecting the broader themes of futility, suffering, and the search for meaning in the novel.
  • “we must prepare to go into the winter solstice”: Chapter 15 sets this night in “the hot August night,” so Miss MacIntosh is looking ahead to winter. “Solstice” comes from the Latin sol and sistere, “the sun stands still,” which Young spells out: “when the sun should apparently stand still.”
  • “triple-crowned kings”: The triple crown is the papal tiara. See the Chapter 16 resources.
  • “the Anatolian coast”: Anatolia is the peninsula of Western Asia that makes up most of Turkey.
  • “the Bay of Biscay”: The Bay of Biscay is a gulf south of the Celtic Sea.
  • “the Shetland Islands”: The Shetland Islands are an archipelago in Scotland.
  • “and Archangel”: Archangel, or Arkhangelsk, is a Russian port on the White Sea.
  • “Archimedes”: Archimedes was a Greek mathematician, physicist, engineer, inventor, and astronomer. In the chapter he is a representation of reason, science, and the human quest for comprehension amidst the mystical and often inscrutable realities that Vera confronts in her dreamlike journey. His figure anchors the narrative’s oscillation between the mystical and the rational, the historical and the personal. See the Chapter 15 resources.
  • “fuss and feathers”: “Old Fuss and Feathers” was the nickname of General Winfield Scott, known for his love of uniform and ceremony.
  • “O, temporal O, mores!”: Cicero’s “O tempora, o mores!” (“Oh, the times! Oh, the customs!”), from his speech Against Catiline (63 BC), garbled by Miss MacIntosh.
  • “for he is unworthy to live who lives only for himself and not somewhat in others”: The wording has not been traced to Luther. His On the Freedom of a Christian (1520) closes on a like thought, that a Christian “lives not in himself, but in Christ and in his neighbor.” The line expresses a moral and philosophical viewpoint about the nature of a meaningful life. It suggests that a person who lives solely for their own self-interest, without regard or connection to other people, lacks true worth or dignity. This idea aligns with Miss MacIntosh’s practical and no-nonsense worldview, emphasizing selflessness, community, and service to others as fundamental values. She believes that life’s purpose is found in caring for and contributing to the well-being of others (the poor orphans of this world) and that the “fool’s paradise” of self-indulgence and isolation is not the way to live a good or worthy life. The phrase means that a life confined within selfishness and isolation is considered lacking, and true worth comes from empathy, love, and living in connection with other souls. It underscores one of the novel’s recurrent themes about the importance of shared humanity and moral responsibility toward others.
  • “if ever I told what I had seen, she would kill me”: A person who rapes another often threatens their life or the life of someone they care about in order to ensure they will never tell anyone what happened.
  • “She had never seen it herself except in a mirror”: Until this night, only a mirror had seen her baldness. See Mirrors and Reflections.
  • “a man’s bedroom was a man’s castle”: “A man’s house is his castle” (Sir Edward Coke, 1628). See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “Common sense was the rarest jewel, more precious than rubies”: Wisdom “is more precious than rubies” (Proverbs 3:15), and “the price of wisdom is above rubies” (Job 28:18). See The Bible and The Book of Job.
  • “I needed a father to guide me”: Miss MacIntosh believes Vera should have had a father and this might refer to a husband since in earlier marriages a daughter is passed from her father to her husband as her guardian. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “tempus fugit”: Tempus fugit is Latin for “time flies.” It comes from Virgil’s Georgics (3.284), “fugit inreparabile tempus,” time flies, never to be regained.
  • “Age is the most difficult thing to achieve”: Life expectancy in the United States in 1900 was 47 years and in 1950 rose to 68 years. Neil deGrasse Tyson has said, “The fact that you and I are alive is against stupendous odds. One day we will die, and that’s sad, but there are people who will never die because they were never born. We’re the lucky ones, we get to die” (in conversation with Vikas Shah, Thought Economics). Miss MacIntosh whispers the line as Vera falls asleep, just after “Alas, I was several years younger then than I am now, for tempus fugit.” To achieve age is to endure loss, hardship, and change, not simply to count the years.
    • Society shapes human health because social factors like income, education, stress levels, access to healthcare, neighborhood environment, social support networks, and cultural norms significantly influence people’s health behaviors, prevailing marketing around lifestyle choices, and overall health outcomes, essentially determining the level of health an individual can achieve within a given community. The World Health Organization calls these the social determinants of health, “the conditions in which people are born, grow, work, live, and age.”
