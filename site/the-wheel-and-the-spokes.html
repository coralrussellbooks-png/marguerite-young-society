Young's Own Structural Metaphor and the Novel as Radial Architecture

I. Young Speaks

Marguerite Young described the structure of Miss MacIntosh, My Darling in her own words, in interviews, and the description is precise: the characters are the spokes of a wheel with Miss MacIntosh at the center. This is not a critic's retrospective metaphor. It is the architect describing the blueprint. And it is a description that, once heard, reorganizes everything about how the novel is read.

The wheel metaphor appears twice in the research assembled for this project, drawn from two separate interview sources. Its repetition confirms it was not a passing remark. Young returned to this image when asked to explain what she had built. The wheel was her word for it. The spoke was her word for each character. Miss MacIntosh, the practical governess from What Cheer, Iowa, who walks into the ocean in the first pages and is never seen again, is not a character among characters. She is the hub. She is the structural center around which every other life in the novel rotates.

What the wheel metaphor does that linear narrative metaphors cannot: it explains why the novel can be 1,198 pages long without feeling like it is going somewhere in the conventional sense. A spoke does not go anywhere. A spoke rotates. Each character in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is in motion relative to the center, but the motion is circular rather than progressive. The novel does not build toward a climax. It accumulates revolutions. Each chapter adds another arc of a character's orbit around the absent center.

II. Hegel and the Circle of Circles

Beside Young's wheel metaphor a second structural image sits: Hegel's philosophical concept of the circle of circles, noted in connection with the phrase things have worlds and worlds inside. This is not a casual juxtaposition. Young read seriously and placed her images with intention. The Hegelian circle of circles describes a philosophical system in which each concept is itself a whole, and the whole is a system of wholes, each internally complete and each in relation to all the others.

This maps onto the novel's structure with remarkable precision. Each character in Miss MacIntosh is a world complete in itself. Catherine Cartwheel's chapters are not interruptions of Vera's quest; they are their own cosmos, their own accumulation of grief and illusion and history. Mr. Spitzer's meditation on love and death and the silent music he composes for an audience of the dead is not subordinate to any other narrative thread; it is complete in itself. Esther Longtree's perpetual pregnancy and her stillborn children are not a subplot; they are a world, existing fully alongside every other world in the novel.

The wheel and the circle of circles are the same structure described at different scales. The wheel describes the spatial relationship of the characters to the absent center. The circle of circles describes the internal organization of each character's consciousness: each is itself a world, and the novel holds all of them simultaneously, in the same way that Hegel's system holds each concept simultaneously as part and as whole.

Young was not illustrating Hegel. She arrived at the same structural principle through her own eighteen years of building. But knowing that the principle has a name, and that the name comes from the same philosophical tradition she was drawing on for the Symposium and Boethius and James, confirms that what Young built was not intuitive but architecturally deliberate.

III. What the Hub's Absence Does

The most architecturally significant fact about the wheel metaphor is what it implies about Miss MacIntosh herself. If she is the hub, and she is absent from the novel almost immediately, having walked into the ocean before the first chapter ends, then the novel is a wheel rotating around an empty center. The spokes are in motion. The hub is gone.

This is not a flaw in the structure. It is the structure's central argument. Every character in the novel is organized by their relationship to something that is no longer there. Vera's quest is literally the search for Miss MacIntosh, for What Cheer, Iowa, for the stable reality that Miss MacIntosh represented. Catherine's opium dreams are the inverse: the construction of an alternative hub, an imaginary center that keeps the world from spinning into chaos without Miss MacIntosh to hold it. Mr. Spitzer's endless meditations on love and death are the orbits of a spoke that has lost its center and keeps moving anyway, composing music for no one, practicing law in courts that may not exist, faithful to the structural motion of his orbit even though what he orbits is gone.

The wheel with the missing hub is also Young's philosophical argument about the self. The plural self, which is the first pillar of the AND philosophy, cannot be unified around a single center because the center the self was organized around is always already lost. Miss MacIntosh is the wig. The wig comes off in Chapter 13. The spokes keep rotating because that is what spokes do: they rotate. What they rotate around after the hub is gone is the memory of the hub, the absence of the hub, the shape of the space where the hub was.

IV. Esther and Mr. Spitzer as Poles

Young told interviewers that Esther Longtree, who appears at the end of the novel, is the mother of us all. In the same interview she said Mr. Spitzer is the father of us all. This pairing, the mother and father of all the novel's characters, is the wheel's outermost poles: the two spokes that are furthest from each other while remaining attached to the same center.

Esther Longtree is perpetually pregnant, perpetually losing her children to stillbirth, perpetually carrying what cannot be born. Mr. Spitzer is perpetually composing music that cannot be heard, practicing law in courts that may not exist, faithful to a twin brother who is dead and to a love he never declared. Between them: all the other spokes, all the other characters, all the other orbits. The wheel turns. The hub is absent. The spokes keep their distance from each other and their attachment to the missing center.

The novel's last movement is Vera's arrival in Indiana, the heart of the Middle West, where she encounters Esther and the stone deaf man she falls in love with. The quest that began with the loss of Miss MacIntosh ends not with the recovery of the center but with Vera accepting her place as one of the spokes. She has found the mother of us all. She has completed her orbit. The wheel does not stop turning.

V. Reading with the Wheel in Mind

Knowing Young's wheel metaphor changes what a reader looks for and what a reader notices. The novel is not a linear narrative that has gone wrong by being too long and too circular. It is a radial structure that is exactly the right length for what it is: a system in which each spoke completes enough of its orbit to demonstrate that the orbit is real, and that the center it orbits is gone, and that the motion continues anyway.

The question the novel asks at the level of its whole structure is the same question it asks at the level of every character's interior: how does a consciousness organize itself when the organizing principle has been removed? The spokes answer: by continuing to rotate. By staying in motion relative to the empty center. By accumulating the memory of the hub in the very structure of the orbit.

That is the wheel. That is the novel. Young built it in eighteen years and described it in one word: spokes.
