Definition from research

Miss MacIntosh keeps the plainest faith in the book. She has no use for mystery or ceremony, and she distrusts anything that cannot be worked with the hands or checked against the world. The opulence of her employer's house, its phantom guests and grand rituals, strikes her as so much pretense, and the visions of angels and saints in Catherine's opium dreams she waves away as nonsense. Her God is not a monarch on a throne but a craftsman, a worker, someone closer to a carpenter than a king. She holds to "truth to nature" and expects people to "know their place" in an ordered world.

Her creed is mostly conduct: work, self-control, and the acceptance of hardship as part of the bargain. She is not an atheist, but she is no comfortable believer either. She doubts the neat Christian picture of God and the afterlife, suspects the creation was left unfinished, and wonders whether even God has regrets. She takes no solace in guaranteed salvation. What she offers instead is fortitude and plain charity, and she has no patience for self-pity dressed up as faith.

Death she treats as ordinary, neither to be feared nor made beautiful. She is wary of the drifting state between living and dying, seeing it as a place of confusion rather than revelation, and she means for her own end to be quiet and unremarkable. With Vera she is stern but caring, steering the child toward self-reliance and real work and away from the dreams that pull people loose from the ground. Her faith is finally a practical one: common sense, discipline, and a sober respect for the limits of what anyone can know.
