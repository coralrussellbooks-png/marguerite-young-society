How Each Pillar Developed Over Time

Pillar 1, Not One Self: first felt in her childhood as Harry and in the mirror of "Transition" (1930), argued in "That Cloud" (1944), made doctrine in the novel (1965), then qualified by her claim of a steady self (1965) and by great-man history in the Debs work. It peaks in the middle of her career and is partly pulled back late.

Pillar 2, Reality Is Constructed: moves from the epistemology of the graduate essays and "Bishop Berkeley" (1944), to the social danger of common sense in 1946, to the economic illusion of capitalism in the Debs work. Throughout, she holds that experience, including dream, is real, so the construction she attacks is the common-sense world rather than reality itself.

Pillar 3, Grief Doesn't End: the most constant pillar, present from her first poem at five through "Recurring" (1930), "the wound of absence" (1945), the novel's elegy, and the Debs book's closing elegiac sense "perhaps long afterward." In the 1940s it widens to extinct species. From the start, grief also produces beauty and comedy.

Pillar 4, Witnessing: begins in personal attention with Minna and the hunter who only watched (1934), becomes complicit in "The Clinic" (1941), becomes a tool of power in "Angel in the Forest" (1945), becomes refused along a color line in the novel, and splits into surveillance and testimony in the Debs work. It grows more institutional and more political over time.

Pillar 5, Build Anyway: stated about her own art in "Song's Dissemination" in the early poems, given its model in the New Harmony utopias (1945), first refused and then accepted in the novel's nestless birds, and measured by the small gain of an eight-hour day in the Debs work. Across her late interviews it sits beside a real wish for permanence.

Pillar 6, Unfinished Is Honest: formulated as the theory of the flaw in "A Crystal Principle" (1942), carried into the undertaker of "Angel in the Forest", built into the open form of the novel, and finally enacted by accident in the unfinished Debs book. It moves from a designed principle to the condition of her own life's work.

Pillar 7, The Margins: present from the weeds and the broken ass of the 1930s poems, stated as philosophy in "The Contravalid" (1945), given a population in the Ozark tents and the novel's dispossessed, and made into policy in Debs's union of all workers. Period blind spots run through every phase, though her racial awareness is sharpest in the novel and the Debs work.

Pillar 8, The Past Doesn't Go Away: ambivalent in "Lot's Wife" (1930), argued both ways in 1944 and 1945 before "Elegy" settles on universal memory, laid down as buried graves in "Angel in the Forest", made psychic in the novel, and documented in the Debs work. It moves from myth toward history.

Pillar 9, Language: begins with "even the nouns act like verbs" in the graduate essays, becomes a critique of language that invents false wholes in the 1940s drafts, turns political in "Angel in the Forest", shows inherited language and counter-language in the novel, and becomes the politics of naming in the Debs work. Its political half arrives late.

Pillar 10, The Sacred Is Here: travels from the theist nature-worship of "Worship" (1927), to God subtracted in the Patmore essay, to God as "creation's flaw" in "Noah's Ark" (1943), to "no God but what we dreamed" in the novel, to the sacred as labor in the Debs work. The vocabulary of angels, resurrection, and exile stays with her the whole way.

Pillar 11, The Crossing Point: the pillar that grows most over time. It is faint in "Biography" (1932), takes clear shape in "Slow Motion" and "The Funeral" (1941), gains the miller's thumbprint at the end of "Angel in the Forest" (1945), becomes the unlocatable conception in the novel, and reaches its strongest image in Dostoyevsky in the snow in her last book.

What the Record Shows

Taken in order, Young's writing shows a philosophy that was not stated once and then repeated. It was built in stages. It began as a feeling in the early poems: the lost mother's tracks in the snow, the mirror that multiplies, the God seen "only / In stars and flowers and shining eyes." In the Chicago years it gained a method from William James, a founding act of witness beside Minna Weissenbach’s bed, and its first prose statements in the graduate essays: sanity as "sharing the hallucinations of our neighbors," a world where "even the nouns act like verbs," and a God who "would hardly be missed." Between 1938 and 1945 it was argued in verse against Berkeley, Descartes, and Aquinas, and several pillars got their clearest statements anywhere in her work: the flaw that makes being "valid," the crippled dancer as "the only truth," "the wound of absence is forever ours," and God as "creation's flaw." In "Angel in the Forest" it moved into history and community. In "Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" it was lived by characters across twelve hundred pages. In the interviews and essays Young explained it in her own voice. In "Harp Song for a Radical" she turned it toward labor, power, and the working dead.

The pillars did not all develop the same way. Grief is the most constant. The witnessing, the past, language, and the crossing point grew steadily and gained political weight late, and the crossing point grew most of all, from the minnow net to Dostoyevsky in the snow. The plural self peaked in the middle of her career and was partly pulled back late.

Some frictions never changed. The language of transcendence runs from "Worship" in 1927 to the "transcendental socialism" of her last decades. The soul as an exile from elsewhere, strongest in the poems of the early 1940s, is never resolved. Her wish to be "known and loved beyond their time" sits beside her bridge "which might end before you reach the other side." The period's racial and other blind spots appear in every phase, though the novel and the Debs history show her aware of them and writing toward them. These frictions do not show the philosophy is absent. They show how Young held it. She argued for the past pressing up and for a universe haunted by memory, for God as creation's flaw and for an angel she insisted was real, for the plural self and for a self that changes no more than "the color of our eyes." She did not see a contradiction, because in her world "the illogical is logic."

The record supports crediting Young with developing Radical Immanence, with one qualification. Every pillar appears in her work, most of them early, and she returned to each one over six decades and deepened it. That is how a writer develops a philosophy rather than touching on one. The qualification is that the name and the eleven-part structure are an articulation of what she built, not a design she laid out herself. She worked in images and scattered statements across poems, histories, a novel, and fifty years of occasional prose, and she left the ideas to be gathered. Her own last book supplies the terms for that gathering. Of the utopian dreamers she wrote that the dream "never really dies as some other dreamer must take over the dream." Naming the philosophy and giving it a structure is a way of taking over the dream, and the evidence shows the dream was hers.

The Sentence That Hasn’t Ended

This account of Young's philosophy began with a sentence about a sentence. Marguerite Young spent eighteen years writing a novel that refuses to end. The refusal is the argument. The continuation is the ethics. The sentence that hasn't ended is not a failure of resolution. It is the most honest description available of what it means to be alive in a world that does not resolve.

Young's philosophy, assembled from its pillars, followed across sixty years of writing, and placed in conversation with the thinkers it illuminates and is illuminated by, and extrapolated toward the political life it makes possible, is not a completed system in the way that philosophical systems usually claim to be complete. It is a recursive one. It knows that the building is provisional. It builds anyway. It knows that the grief does not resolve. It witnesses anyway. It knows that the self is plural and the real is constructed and the honest ethical stance requires a quality of attention that is exhausting to sustain. It sustains it anyway. Not because the sustaining is easy or because the arrival is coming. Because sustaining is what integrity looks like in a world built the way this world is built.

What Young gives us is not comfort in the cheap sense. It is companionship in the honest one. The companionship of a thinker who looked at the full weight of what existence contains and did not flinch and did not conclude and did not replace the difficulty with a consolation that would have required pretending the difficulty wasn't there. She stayed in the room. She stayed in the room with the hardest questions her philosophy asked.

The pillars together are the philosophical account of why the sentence has not ended, why it cannot end without lying, why the ongoing sentence is the only honest form that the engagement with this world can take.

It is saying that the world is here, and plural, and grief-saturated, and constructed, and sacred in the immanent sense, and incomplete in the honest sense, and populated at its margins by the people whose complexity the sorting systems cannot process, and layered with time that the present moment carries without always knowing it carries, and expressible only in a form that refuses to simplify, and worth building in, and worth witnessing in, and worth staying in the room with, without flinching and without concluding.

That is radical immanence.

That is the philosophy of Marguerite Young.

The question Young leaves us with is not how to end it. It is how to keep writing it with honesty and the attention and the willingness to return to the beginning and find what we missed the first time that the sentence, if it is to mean anything, requires.
