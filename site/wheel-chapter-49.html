Chapter 49

On Chapter 49 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of The Wheel and the Spokes.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 48 · Wheel and Spokes Home · Chapter 50 →

Research notes: Chapter 49 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 49

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

"It seemed as if he were disintegrating into many images, phantoms blown by storm far beyond his knowledge"

Mr. Spitzer is never singular. He is simultaneously the old lawyer, the old musician, the pigeon mourner, the shipwreck, the clock, the lighthouse, the storm's center, and the shadow of all of these. The chapter refuses to resolve this multiplicity into a stable identity. When he asks "who was this tramp" sheltering in his own body the question is genuinely unanswerable, not because information is missing, but because the premise of a singular answerable self is itself false.

The most devastating articulation comes late:

"He did not know which brother he was, and this was the crux of his mystery. Perhaps the mystery would never be revealed."

This is not amnesia or confusion. This is Not One Self as ontological condition. The twin structure, Mr. Spitzer and his dead or living brother, is not a plot device but a philosophical architecture: the self is always already doubled, always already uncertain which version of itself is the one doing the experiencing. The chapter does not resolve this. It insists on it.

Furthermore, his plurality extends outward into the world. He informs all things with his dying spirit meaning the self is not contained within the body but distributed across pigeons, waves, stars, Martha, the old caretaker, the blind man, the deaf musician. Not One Self here is not merely psychological fragmentation; it is ontological diffusion.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

"There was no mortice work between his future and his past"

"It seemed as if all his problems were doubled by his doubts, his creative doubts, mirrored doubts"

The chapter systematically dismantles the distinction between real and illusory. Mr. Spitzer mistakes his shadow for his mother's coach. He argues with a traffic policeman for hours before discovering the policeman is wooden. He conducts "long musical dialogues where there was no one". He attempts to board the shadow of a boat rather than the boat itself.

But crucially, the text does not treat these as errors to be corrected. The mirrored prow of the boat is described as potentially "more real than the boat". The reflections in water are asked: "Were only the unreal things real?". This is not epistemological failure; this is Reality Is Constructed. Reality is not discovered beneath illusion; illusion is the medium through which reality is constituted.

The cave metaphor from the philosophy finds its equivalent here in the chapter's persistent fog:

"Dawn was seldom more than another aspect of watery greyness scarcely touched by rays of light, seeming an extension of twilight palpable with all those mysteries which never would be revealed and perhaps had always been concealed from the eyes of man."

There is no sunlit world outside. There is only the fog, more or less dense, more or less gold-veined. The fog is the world.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

This pillar may be the chapter's most fully realized theme. The entire chapter is written in grief the way other texts are written about it. Grief here is not an emotion Mr. Spitzer experiences; it is the climate in which he exists.

The clearest emblem is Martha, the last passenger pigeon, who died September 14, 1918 at one o'clock in the afternoon at the Cincinnati Zoo. Mr. Spitzer has written her date of death on his shirt cuff. He has been writing her epitaph ever since. He arrived too late with his white rose. He rode a train all night through a starless countryside in mourning. He wept in a broken cab through Washington.

But this grief is not moving toward resolution:

"Grief does not move through stages and arrive at resolution. It accumulates."

The chapter enacts this accumulation structurally. Martha's death compounds with the death of all passenger pigeons, which compounds with the death of all species, which compounds with the death of love, which compounds with the death of the brother, which compounds with Mr. Spitzer's own perpetual dying. The grief does not resolve; it sediments (anticipating Pillar 8):

"He had tried to include all possibilities and even the rejected possibilities, the way taken and the way not taken, the life he might have lived, the death he had died, all that had happened, all that had not happened, and much that was impossible."

The honest life of this philosophy "stops pretending otherwise." Mr. Spitzer has stopped pretending. He does not seek consolation. He weeps opalescent tears "almost like tears of joy", not because grief has transformed into happiness, but because grief, fully inhabited, becomes its own form of presence. Grief is his weather, and he has learned to live in it without shelter.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

"He had been this watcher of the innumerable stars."

Mr. Spitzer's fundamental ethical posture is witnessing. He witnesses Martha's death, the extinction of the passenger pigeon, the death of stars, the sleeping of passengers, the drowning of cities, the expiration of species. He does not fix any of it. He cannot. But he stays in the room.

His attempt to place a mirror before the dead Martha so she might see another pigeon is the one moment he tries to intervene, and he is refused. The caretaker's refusal is philosophically correct within this framework: the witness does not manufacture consolation. The witness sees.

"He had noted it in a war-torn world. He had written down this date upon his shirt cuff."

This is the ethics of attention in its purest form. The world is at war. Millions are dying. And Mr. Spitzer writes down the exact minute, three minutes after one o'clock, of a pigeon's death. This is not triviality. This is the radical insistence that every individual extinction matters, that the witness's obligation does not scale with the world's priorities. To witness the death of the last passenger pigeon during a world war is to refuse the hierarchy of grief, to insist that all endings deserve a notation on the shirt cuff.

The chapter also gives us witnessing as staying with the unresolvable:

"He was like an old clock always winding himself up when he was running down"

He does not stop. He does not arrive at peace. He winds the clock again. The Witness does not flinch and does not rush toward resolution. He simply continues to see.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

"He had tried to include all possibilities and even the rejected possibilities"

"This music wrote itself. It was written by the tide, washed out by the tide."

Mr. Spitzer's music is the central image of Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary. He composes music that he himself cannot hear, that is "never to be repeated and heard twice though it went on and on when the musician was dead". He writes it knowing it is approximate, provisional, incomplete. He returns to it again and again: the chapter itself is structured as a return, circling back through the same images (waves, ice, pigeons, stars, the brother, the clock) with variations, like a musical theme developed through a long movement.

The building is never finished:

"This was surely, though he had never intended it, for no one could have intended it, that approximate music which Mr. Spitzer tentatively wrote"

The word tentatively is crucial. He does not write with certainty or completion. He writes tentatively, provisionally, recursively. And the chapter enacts this: it spirals rather than progresses, returns rather than advances, accumulates rather than concludes. The form is the philosophy.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

He "had tried to reverse last judgments in every court paved with broken moonstones where the waters ran"

Mr. Spitzer is explicitly described as "the golden advocate of reversals, the pleader of lost causes" a "minority representing a majority". His causes are never exactly stated, never concluded, perhaps never even winnable. This is not failure; this is Unfinished Is Honest as vocation.

His music is the clearest instance:

"music never to be heard like the snowflake whispering on the wave... music never to return when the music was no more, when the silence was complete"

The demand for completion (the finished symphony, the concluded legal case, the resolved grief, the identified self) is described throughout as a kind of violence against experience. The chapter's own form refuses completion: it is one of the longest, most syntactically unresolved passages in literary fiction, a single sustained sentence of consciousness that does not arrive anywhere because arrival is not the point.

The ongoing sentence is the integrity. The concluded one would be the lie.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

"He trusted all persons suffering some fatal defect, all who were blind as he was, yet saw... all who were deaf as he was, yet heard the beating of the silent heart."

This pillar finds its fullest expression in Mr. Spitzer's explicit catalogue of loves: the blind train conductor, the blind lighthouse keeper, the deaf musician, the deaf conductor of the mute orchestra, the crippled dancer, the mute swan. These are not figures of pathos. They are figures of epistemological privilege. The marginal person, the one who cannot fully participate in the normative sensory and social world, is the one who sees most clearly what the constructed nature of that world actually is.

The blind man in the fog is the best guide:

"This sudden darkness over the city, that through which you grope as one suddenly blind, is an advantage to me, for I am the best guide through the night who never knew the light"

The deaf are described as living in a world without time's false divisions:

"They knew not time, this human abstraction, this division of time into compartments"

And Martha herself, the last passenger pigeon, is the ultimate marginal figure: the final member of an extinct species, the one for whom the world has no place, who "had no soul" according to the caretaker and yet for whom Mr. Spitzer weeps his most sustained grief. She is the evidence. Her extinction makes visible what the world does to beauty, to excess, to the "many, the beautiful, the excessive who had made a wilderness of this life".

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This is perhaps the chapter's most structurally embedded philosophical principle. The entire extended passage on ice is a meditation on sedimentary time:

"Ice had grown old, formation over formation, snow pack over snow pack, unmelting snowflake over unmelting snowflake, star over star, and flames were frozen."

The ice does not erase what came before; it preserves it. Every layer of ice contains the layer beneath it. The frozen world is a world of accumulated time, each moment pressing up through the ones above it.

Mr. Spitzer's consciousness operates on exactly this principle. He does not remember sequentially; he is constituted by accumulation. The date on his shirt cuff, Martha's death, the brother's death or life, the lost causes, the music written for dead ears; none of this recedes. It presses up:

"He had tried to include all possibilities and even the rejected possibilities, the way taken and the way not taken, the life he might have lived, the death he had died, all that had happened, all that had not happened"

The past is not behind him. It is beneath him, still pressing. When the ice melts:

"Icebergs were melting with a crackling, buzzing sound as if the sea gave up its dead"

The dead rise. The sediment surfaces. What was deposited returns. This is not metaphor; this is the chapter's ontology of time.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter's prose style is itself the argument. The sentences are not vehicles for delivering pre-existing meaning; they are the construction of a world that could not exist without them. The syntax of the chapter (its endless subordinate clauses, its refusal of full stops, its accumulation of "or" and "perhaps" and "it seemed") enacts the philosophical claim that language is always approximate, always constructing rather than reporting.

Note the persistent hedging:

"perhaps only one grace note" "it seemed to Mr. Spitzer" (recurring throughout) "perhaps he had never sailed, had only thought he sailed"

This is not authorial uncertainty. This is language naming its own location, acknowledging that it speaks from somewhere in particular, from inside a consciousness that is dying, plural, fog-bound, and provisional. The language refuses to pretend it speaks from nowhere.

The most explicit statement:

"He was like a blind man staggering where the shadows whispered and increased"

Language here is the shadow; it shapes the darkness, gives it form, but it is not the light. It is what we have instead of the light.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The chapter explicitly and repeatedly refuses transcendence in the conventional sense. Martha dies without benefit of clergy. The caretaker insists she had no soul. Mr. Spitzer himself states:

"He knew there was no death because there was no life."

And yet the chapter is saturated with the sacred. The sacred is located not beyond the world but in it, in the pigeon's iridescent neck in the exact minute of Martha's death in the barnacle on Mr. Spitzer's cheek that sometimes "shone like the moon which gave the only light" in the firefly that "lighted its light before the world began".

The most direct statement of The Sacred Is Here:

"He had translated his passion for immortality into mortality, making of this earth his heaven — for had he not missed earth as some might miss heaven, an island in the flood?"

Heaven is not elsewhere. Earth is the island. The flood is the ordinary condition. The sacred is this (the grey bus, the permanent grief, the plural self) not despite its ordinariness but because of it. The barnacle is the jewel. The pigeon dropping on the hat brim is the consecration.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This pillar may be the one that most completely describes Mr. Spitzer's ontological situation. He is not a person moving through time; he is the point where time crosses itself:

"He died at his zenith because he was at his nadir, died at his nadir because he was at his zenith."

"He lived through dying, crying, and silence. He died at the first breath. He died perhaps at the last because he had died at the first."

He is constituted by what was deposited before he arrived (the dead brother, the dead Martha, the dead passenger pigeons, the frozen centuries of ice) and he is constituting the ground for what comes after. His music, unheard by him, will be heard by someone. His epitaph for Martha, written in "the timeless clouds" is the ground the not-yet-born will stand on.

The most explicit formulation of the Crossing Point:

"Perhaps he was the center of the great storm blowing around him, through him, blowing through many apertures"

He is not in the storm. He is where the storm crosses itself. He is the crossing point, of time, of the living and dead, of music and silence, of self and other, of his brother's life and his own.

And the obligation of this position is not chosen:

"He died through everything which lived, lived through everything which died"

The carrying continues because it cannot stop. He is structural, not voluntary. The ancestors (Martha, the passenger pigeons, the frozen dead, the brother) are in the sediment. Mr. Spitzer is the ground through which they press upward. He walks through the city, tapping his cane, weeping his opalescent tears, carrying what cannot be put down.
