Chapter 47: The Skirt Over the Wound

← Chapter 46 · Guide Home · Chapter 48 →

Research notes: Chapter 47

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer

Themes: Death and Mortality · Fear and Courage · Love · Music, Sound, and Silence · Women, Gender, and Marriage

Symbols: Clothing, Hats, and Skirts · Mountains · Music and Instruments

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft · Plato and Platonism · Schopenhauer · Spinoza · Wittgenstein

Allusions: Women's Suffrage

Mr. Spitzer lays the skirt over her body.

He speculates, in the long meditation that follows, that “perhaps her love was her skirt”: the thing “hiding her great wound, her disappointment, her bleeding heart.” The skirt she called for in the delirium. The thing that kept the rain out in the way the rubber coat kept the rain out, the way Miss MacIntosh kept the rain out, except that the rain got in anyway, as it always does with the things we love most fiercely by covering.

Chapter 47 is a chapter of laying-to-rest and of the thoughts that come in the long hours after a death when there is nothing left to do but think. Mr. Spitzer thinks. He thinks about Cousin Hannah and his brother and the nature of identity and the road not taken and the rejected possibility and the way time fails to tell him the time. He thinks about what it means that he feared change more than he feared death, and about whether a person should be held accountable for their “entire life or for one moment of” transmutation that happened beyond consciousness or memory or desire.

This is one of the novel's great reckoning chapters. It is quiet and immense in equal measure.

What actually happens in Chapter 47

We remain with Mr. Spitzer in the long aftermath of Cousin Hannah's death. The executor's work continues: papers, objects, the alpenhorn already claimed. He lays her skirt over her body as a final gesture, and the gesture opens into the chapter's long meditation.

He reflects on his twin brother, on owing much to him, financially and spiritually, on the possibility of forming “a Welcoming Committee of One on the other side,” waiting for himself to arrive. The relationship between the twins, Peron and Joachim, surfaces here as a philosophical pairing: materialism and immaterialism, the external world and the internal one, the reckless embrace of life and the cautious withdrawal from it.

He is “agonized by attributing consciousness to everything”: clouds, waves, shadows, stars. The world will not stay inanimate for him. Everything is alive in a way that troubles rather than comforts, because if everything is conscious then the dead are conscious too, which means they are somewhere suffering their unfinished business, crying at many gates, forever searching for the image of what they lost.

He meditates on the rejected possibility: “among all things possible, there was the rejected possibility.” The road not taken. The love not said. The life that existed as pure potential and was never actualized, which shapes reality just as powerfully as the life that was.

The skirt as love, love as wound

Young returns one final time to the skirt, and here she gives it its fullest meaning. “Perhaps her love was her skirt hiding her great wound, her disappointment, her bleeding heart.”

The logic is precise and terrible. The skirt was what Cousin Hannah refused: the feminine convention, the domestic enclosure, the mark of the life she chose not to live. And it was also, in the delirium, what she called for most desperately. She wanted the thing she had refused. She was covered, in death, with the thing she spent her life refusing to wear.

Young is saying: the love and the wound were the same thing. The cause that covered her heart (the suffrage movement, the iron keys, the decades of campaigning) was both the means by which she protected the wound and the wound itself. You cannot separate them. The skirt covers the body. The cause covered the grief. Both coverings are real. Both conceal something that was real underneath.

Mr. Spitzer lays the skirt over her because it is the right thing, the only right thing, and because he understands now what it meant that she called for it. He covers her wound with the symbol of her wound, which is also the symbol of her love, which is also the symbol of everything she refused and could not stop wanting.

Peron and Joachim: matter and spirit

Chapter 47 develops the twin pairing more explicitly than any chapter before it, and the philosophical framework To All My Darlings offers is clarifying: Peron represents materialism, the external world, its pleasures and its follies, its impermanent beauty. Joachim represents immaterialism: the internal realm, thought, memory, spiritual seeking.

In the novel's cosmology, neither twin is complete without the other. The material world without the inner life is just appetite, just flux. The inner life without the material world is just abstraction, just ghosts. The twins together made a whole person, which is why the death of one half left the surviving half as a counterfeit, a man at a second remove from life, unable to be fully present in either world.

And the question the chapter presses: which twin survived? To All My Darlings has suggested it was Peron, not Joachim, who continued. If so, what is the materialist doing in Joachim's life? The reckless one playing the cautious one, the one who embraced chance living out the life of the one who feared it? This possibility, that Mr. Spitzer has been performing a borrowed identity for decades, deepens everything. The man who valued his dignity more than his life may have been living his dead brother's dignity all along.

He feared change more than death

This is the chapter's most psychologically precise formulation, and Young sets it down with characteristic quietness: “for more than life or death he feared change, change of any kind.”

Death is at least a stable concept. Death is a destination, however unknown. Death is the end of change, or the beginning of a different kind that you are no longer around to suffer. But change (the ongoing transformation of identity, of circumstance, of what you thought you knew) that never stops. That is what Mr. Spitzer cannot bear.

He is a man already profoundly altered by the loss of his twin, already living between two worlds, already uncertain which of the Spitzer brothers he actually is. Any further change threatens to dissolve what little continuity he has managed to maintain. So he keeps the silk hat. He keeps the routine. He keeps the dignity. He does not vote because “the dead cast no vote—and” he is not sure which side of that line he stands on. He maintains appearances because appearance is all that is holding the shape of him together.

Young is not dismissive of this. She is precise about it. The man who fears change more than death is not simply cowardly; he is a man whose sense of self has been so thoroughly disrupted by loss that any further disruption feels like erasure. His conservatism is a form of survival. It is the only form available to him.

Panpsychism and the agonized attribution

“He was agonized by attributing consciousness to everything.”

This is one of the novel's most exact descriptions of a particular kind of suffering: the suffering of a mind that cannot maintain the boundary between the animate and the inanimate, that perceives consciousness in clouds and stars and shadows and waves, that cannot move through a room without registering the inner life of every object in it.

Panpsychism (the philosophical position that consciousness is a fundamental property of the universe, not confined to biological life) is ancient, running from Thales and Plato through Spinoza and Leibniz to contemporary philosophy of mind. Young would have known it. But she gives Mr. Spitzer not the philosopher's intellectual pleasure in the idea but the experiential cost of living it without choosing it. He did not adopt panpsychism as a theory. He suffers from it as a condition. Every cloud has a mind. Every wave intends something. Every shadow of Cousin Hannah's passing life is still present somewhere, carrying its freight of unfinished consciousness. This is why he cannot fully grieve. The dead are not gone for him; they are mislocated. He keeps looking for them because he cannot stop attributing consciousness to the universe, and the universe keeps offering him shapes that might be them.

The rejected possibility

“Among all things possible, there was the rejected possibility.”

This is Young's formulation for the unlived life: the life that existed as potential and was not taken, which does not simply disappear but shapes reality from its position of non-actualization. The road not taken is as real as the road taken. The love not spoken is as present as the love that was. The skirt not worn for fifty years is what she called for at the end.

Young is not making a case for regret. She is making a case for completeness: a life is not only what happened in it. A life includes its rejections, its suppressions, its great disciplined refusals. The rejected possibility is the shadow that gives the actualized life its shape. Without it, there is no contrast, no definition, no meaning to the choices that were made.

“Should one be held accountable for one’s entire life or for one moment of unforeseen” transmutation “beyond all consciousness or memory or desire?” This question, which Mr. Spitzer turns over in the long hours after the death, is unanswerable, and Young knows it is unanswerable and asks it anyway. That is what the novel does. It asks the unanswerable questions with complete seriousness, gives them the space they deserve, and moves on.

Music never to be played

Mr. Spitzer imagines “music written never to be played”: a composition that encompasses all sounds and all silences, all the notes that were sounded and all the notes that were not, the complete score of a life including everything that went unperformed.

This is the novel's image for what Mr. Spitzer is doing with Cousin Hannah's estate: not just the papers and the alpenhorn and the legal documents, but the incompletable archive of what she was. The music that was never played is the love she never said, the life she never lived, the skirt she never wore. It is real music. It exists, as all unplayed music exists: in potential, in the silence that contains it, in the ear that knows what it would have sounded like if it had been given air.

Whereof one cannot speak

Wittgenstein's closing proposition from the Tractatus surfaces here: whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent.

Young invokes this not as capitulation but as the appropriate frame for everything Mr. Spitzer cannot say. There are things that exceed language: the full truth of another person's private life, the experience of the dying, the exact quality of a love that was never expressed. These are not things you can say precisely. You can approach them. You can circle them with prose that circles. You can lay a skirt over a body and know, without being able to say, what the gesture means.

Mr. Spitzer is fluent in this silence. He has practiced it his entire life. He held the secret. He kept the vow. He was silent in the dread presence of death as Susan B. Anthony was silent at her sister Hannah's deathbed. The silence is not emptiness. It is the form that respect takes when language is insufficient. Young knows this. Wittgenstein knew it. Mr. Spitzer, in his limited and faithful and inadequate way, knows it too.

What to hold onto

Chapter 47 is a chapter of vigil and reckoning: the long thoughts that come when the body is laid out and the papers are sorted and there is nothing left to do but sit with what you know and cannot say. Mr. Spitzer is the last person who saw Cousin Hannah alive. He is the keeper of the great secret. He feared change more than death, and now the greatest change of his life, the death of the woman who organized his existence around her absence, has happened, and he is still here, still in the silk hat, still keeping vigil.

What to carry forward: the skirt as love as wound. The twins as matter and spirit, and the question of which one survived. The rejected possibility as the shadow that gives a life its shape. The “music written never to be played.” And the silence that is not emptiness but the only honest response to what exceeds language.
