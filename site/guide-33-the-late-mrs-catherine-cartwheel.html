Chapter 33: The Late Mrs. Catherine Cartwheel

← Chapter 32 · Guide Home · Chapter 34 →

Research notes: Chapter 33

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Music, Sound, and Silence

Symbols: Music and Instruments

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: The History of Opium

Chapter 33 is a quieter chapter after the great set pieces of the Cousin Hannah sequence, but it does important work. It gathers together threads from across the novel (the newspaper that declared Catherine dead, Mr. Spitzer’s faithfulness, the wedding that may or may not have happened, the music he is still writing) and settles into something that functions almost as a pause, a breathing space before the novel expands again.

It also contains one of the most tender portraits of their relationship we have had: Mr. Spitzer’s decision that her inaccuracy is a kind of freedom, and his determination, for all his impatience, to keep opening the door.

What actually happens in Chapter 33

A Boston newspaper has referred to Catherine as the late Mrs. Catherine Cartwheel, reporting her dead. Mr. Spitzer has not told her. He has allowed the error to stand, reasoning that she is “an opium taker in love with her starry illusions—and” the illusion “should not be corrected, at least not by him.”

He also notes that the same newspaper reported Peron Spitzer “seen recently walking along Benevolent Street, peering into old doorways.” Mr. Spitzer walked down the same street the very next evening “when the lamp-lighters were lighting the lamps,” “his black cape whirling around him like the sobbing of the rain upon the river Styx.” He saw a carriage where for years there had been no carriages.

Catherine’s memory of her wedding is revisited: the bridegroom was a white camel veiled in lace, a church steeple, the wind. “She had been married to Death while” sleeping. She cannot remember the details clearly. Sometimes she thinks she has never been married at all. She is sure she never married Mr. Spitzer.

Mr. Spitzer meditates on Catherine’s inaccuracy, her distended memory, and arrives at his most generous understanding of her: her conscious states are “like the unconscious states of other people,” and she is “probably no more distant from reality than were the lamp-lighters going their lonely rounds,” those who punch time-clocks, “those who must turn great wheels.” They, too, can live only while they dream.

The chapter ends with Mr. Spitzer getting up to open a door Catherine is asking a human-sized blackbird to open.

The newspaper error

The Boston newspaper that declared Catherine dead, and then reported Peron seen peering into doorways on Benevolent Street, is doing what newspapers do in Young’s world: reporting from a position of partial knowledge, getting the essential things wrong and the incidental things right, being corrected by time in ways no one can anticipate.

Mr. Spitzer’s response to the first error is characteristic: he allows it to stand. He is not in the position to correct it. The newspaper will eventually be right, he reasons, having merely mistaken the time. This is Mr. Spitzer’s philosophy of error applied to journalism: inaccuracies are often premature truths. The mistake is not that Catherine is dead but that the timing is off.

The second error, Peron seen on Benevolent Street, is something Mr. Spitzer investigates in person. He walks the same street the next evening. He sees a carriage. For years there had been “no carriages on the streets of Boston.” Young gives us this detail and moves on, but its weight is exact: on the street named for kindness, on the evening after his dead brother was reportedly seen peering into old doorways, Mr. Spitzer sees a carriage that should not exist. He makes no comment. He continues.

The dead letter

Near the chapter’s center, Young gives us one of her most perfect images of Mr. Spitzer: he has been going “to the post office day after day to inquire for a dead letter,” asking that they trace a letter he “mailed to himself many years ago” that was never “delivered though it was marked clearly with his return address.” The envelope contained a piece of blank paper.

A letter to himself, containing nothing, sent years ago, never arrived, still being traced. This is Mr. Spitzer’s entire situation in three sentences. He has been trying to communicate with himself across the gap of his brother’s death, across the years of inaccuracy and grief, and the letter (blank, addressed to himself, never delivered) is the silent music he has been composing all his life. The message is empty because there is nothing to say. The return address is there because he needs to know where he lives. He keeps going to the post office because the alternative is to stop expecting the letter to arrive.

Mr. Spitzer’s defense of Catherine’s inaccuracy

The chapter’s most important passage is Mr. Spitzer’s extended meditation on Catherine’s distended memory, and it represents his greatest act of generosity toward her.

He has come gradually to believe, he says, that her conscious states are “probably like the unconscious states of other people.” She is “enslaved by her drug habit which had robbed her of her sense of time,” but she is “probably no more enslaved than others” are by their predisposition toward illusion, “no more distant from reality than were the lamp-lighters going their lonely rounds or those who must punch time-clocks in great factories.” They, too, can live only while they dream. They, too, are never quite awake.

This is Mr. Spitzer dissolving the distinction between Catherine and the rest of humanity. He is not excusing her. He is insisting that her position is not exceptional: that the opium dreams are a more visible version of what everyone does, and that her inaccuracy is a greater freedom than accuracy would have been. She lives in a wider space. She has not “closed a door or written finis to a deathless dream as” his brother surely did.

And so he gets up and opens the door when she asks the blackbird to open it. Not because he believes the blackbird can hear her. Because the door needs opening, and he is there.

The harp music of bridges

One further detail deserves attention. Mr. Spitzer is writing harp music that “should be the exact imitation of the wind playing through the strings and cables of a bridge”: the bridge reflected upon water, his music to “be finished only with the dying wind, wind dying over the water.” He is “studying the velocity of wind over bridges, the tonnage of wind pressure against cables, ascertaining that sometimes twenty thousand tons of wind” break against a single string.

This is the silent music finally given a specific form. Not silence exactly, but the sound of a bridge: the structure designed to connect two shores, to bear the weight of crossing, trembling under the force of what passes through it. Mr. Spitzer has spent his life as a bridge between the living and the dead, between his brother and the world, between Catherine and reality. Of course his music sounds like the wind through bridge cables. Of course it can only be finished when the wind dies.

What to watch for as you keep reading

Light spoilers: This section hints at what happens later in the novel. Skip it if you want to meet the story fresh.

The novel is approaching the end of Volume One. We have spent thirty-three chapters in and around Catherine’s coastal house, inside the minds of Vera, Miss MacIntosh, Mr. Spitzer, and Cousin Hannah. The world is about to expand dramatically when Volume Two begins. Hold what you have. Every new character, every new geography, will be understood against this foundation.

Mr. Spitzer’s reasoning that the newspaper will eventually be right about Catherine, having merely mistaken the time, is Young’s statement about the novel’s relationship to truth. The novel is not interested in what is factually accurate at any given moment. It is interested in the longer truth, the one the inaccurate clock will eventually strike. Patience with inaccuracy is not moral failure. It is, in this novel’s world, the condition of understanding.

The human-sized blackbird Catherine is talking to when Mr. Spitzer gets up to open the door is one of those small perfect images Young places without comment. Catherine holds a perfectly rational conversation with a blackbird. The blackbird’s response, apparently, is quite rational. Mr. Spitzer looks around, cannot hear the answer she hears, gets up and opens the door. He has learned to make himself useful in the space between what she perceives and what is there.
