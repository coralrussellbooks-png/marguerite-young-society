Chapter 13. Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

← Previous · Philosophy of MMMD Home · Next →

See also: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Chapters discussed: 31, 51, 52, 82

Definition from research

The hardest and most honest stance is to stay in the room with something painful and complicated without rushing to fix it or wrap it up.

Mr. Spitzer is the novel’s witness. He sits for years beside Catherine’s bed, listening to her conversations with the dead. He does not correct her. The narrator explains that “he indulged my mother’s moods so long as they were not of reality, he being profoundly, ever and increasingly convinced that reality would kill her as it had killed her canary” (ch. 31). He is not a believer in her dreams. He simply refuses to be the one who destroys them.

His stance is summed up in a single line: “He had learned to respect other people’s mirages, especially as he existed only as a mirage himself” (ch. 51). The witness does not stand above the scene as a clear-eyed judge. He stands inside it, as unsure of his own reality as anyone else, and that uncertainty is what makes his respect possible.

The forty trunks show the witness at work. He could break them open, but he does not, because he has “a due respect for damaged properties which should not be further damaged by him” (ch. 52). He accepts that “There was no master key” and leaves the locks unbroken. A lesser searcher would have forced the answer. The witness lets the mystery stand.

Vera learns this stance by the end. When she meets Esther Longtree, she listens to the waitress’s long story of stillbirths and does not correct her, pity her, or try to cure her. She hears. And in the last chapter she imagines telling Esther all her own losses and hearing Esther reply, “So had not everyone lost something?” (ch. 82). The witness is witnessed in return.
