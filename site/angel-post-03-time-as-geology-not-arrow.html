Angel in the Forest

Chapter 3. Time as Geology, Not Arrow

← Chapter 2 · Angel in the Forest: A Reading in Fourteen Chapters · Chapter 4 →

Marguerite Young / Angel in the Forest, Chapter 3

Most histories are shaped like an arrow. They start early and end late, and the reader is carried from cause to effect along a line that runs one direction only. Angel in the Forest does not move like that, and the difference is not a stylistic quirk. It is the structural heart of the book, and it is the place where Young comes closest to an idea this study keeps circling: that the past is not behind us but beneath us, laid down in layers we are standing on right now.

Notice first how the book is built. It opens in the summer of 1940, on the ferry. Then it goes back to Rapp's Pennsylvania, comes to the Wabash, tells the Rappite story and the Owenite story across their thirteen years. But it never stays back there. Chapters keep surfacing into 1940. There is a chapter called "The Children of the Ozarks, 1940." There is one called "Glaucas, 1940." The last full chapter, "Farewell to New Harmony," is set on a summer day in 1940 in the town graveyard. The present is threaded all the way through the past, so that you are never allowed to sink into the nineteenth century and forget where you are standing. Every time the history gets going, the ground of the present pushes back up through it. The book will not let time behave like an arrow because Young does not believe time is an arrow. She believes it is a column, and she drills you up and down through it until you can feel the whole depth of it under your feet.

The past you can still buy

The evidence that the past is underfoot rather than behind is scattered through the 1940 sections as a plain, concrete fact.

The Owenites planted golden rain trees. The Owenites failed and scattered, their New Moral World never arrived, and yet the trees kept seeding, and in 1940 the shoots are still for sale in the town shops, six for twenty-five cents, guaranteed to grow. The failed future is literally still propagating. You can carry it home in your hand.

Father Rapp's angel came down, so the story went, and left its footprints pressed into a slab of stone. Rapp is gone. The angel is gone. The stone endured all through the nineteenth century, weathering slowly, until by 1900 the right footprint was still whole but the left had begun to wear away. And the children of New Harmony played hopscotch on it. The holiest sign the first utopia produced, the print of an actual angel, has become a worn square in a children's game, still there, still stepped on, its meaning half rubbed off by weather and feet. That is the past exactly: not gone, not preserved, just underfoot and repurposed by whoever is standing on it now.

And the one unbroken line in the whole town is the undertaker's. Every utopian scheme failed. The only dynasty left in New Harmony in 1940 is the business of burial. Young draws him almost fondly, a man always on his way from the grave to the cradle, holding mortgages he will not foreclose, on whom the entire town depends. He is the figure of accumulation itself, the one whose work is to handle each layer as it goes down. The utopias promised to end death and were themselves buried. The undertaker remains.

The chapter where the sacred is the depth

The book's clearest statement of all this is a chapter called "An Adult View," and it is one of the most extraordinary passages in American prose. In the middle of a history of two communities, Young stops and narrates the entire geological and evolutionary past of the Wabash valley, and she does it in the language of scripture.

She calls it the Book of Nature, written, she says, on leaves of sandstone and limestone, no everlasting material. For thousands of years, she tells us, what is now Indiana was a plain of granite under a deep, tideless salt sea. Transparent fishes appeared and left their fins printed on the rock. The waters withdrew, she writes, without the benefit of Noah. Pterodactyls rose out of the mist. The sea came back, glassy as death, and each time that Lazarus of birds and flowers rose again to new life. Glaciers came down from the north like crystal rivers, and what could not move was destroyed. When the ice pulled back it left rich soil, and elephants browsed the Wabash hills, and where New Harmony now stands there was a crocodile whose open mouth made a dormitory for small birds. It was, she says, an Adamless Eden. And then the line the whole chapter turns on: when man finally arose, he was already old and corrupt, like the earth before him, a creature with a history. There was never a first dawn.

Read what she is doing with the borrowed words. Book of Nature, Noah, Lazarus, Eden, resurrection: she takes the entire vocabulary of the sacred and pours it, not upward toward heaven, but straight down into the rock and the fish and the ice. The holiness in this passage does not point past the world to something above it. It points deeper into the world, into the sheer unimaginable depth of what is actually beneath the cornfields. This is what the sacred looks like from inside the philosophy. Not a truth located somewhere else, but the ordinary ground revealed to be layered thousands of feet deep with everything that ever lived here, and that depth treated with the reverence usually reserved for God. The salt sea is scripture. The glacier is scripture. And "there was never a first dawn" is the flat refusal of the whole arrow model: no clean origin, no starting line, no moment before the layers began. The present is not the point the arrow was aimed at. It is only the most recent layer, and a thin one.

Oil, dinosaurs, and the preacher who could not hold it

Young keeps the geology pressed against the present in the smaller chapters too, and never more sharply than in the talk of the farmers around Glaucas, the barefoot sewer cleaner who goes down under the town and comes back up. The farmers talk, among the price of corn and sheep's lice and Byrd's polar expedition, about the oil under the fields, and they say it is made of the melted bodies of dinosaurs that once roamed these hills. The town runs, in other words, on the liquefied deep past. The engine of the present is the rendered flesh of the ancient dead, pumped up from below.

The preacher will not have it. He says the oil talk is propaganda against the word of God, because there could not have been a garden of Eden and dinosaurs too, and glacial ages, and monkeys for ancestors. He is right that they do not fit together in a single tidy story. His mistake is thinking they have to. He needs one truth and has to throw out the others to keep it. Young does the opposite. She holds Eden and the dinosaurs and the glaciers all at once, in the same breath, without letting any of them win, which is the discipline this philosophy calls witness: staying in the room with contradictory truths instead of forcing one to defeat the rest. The preacher wants an arrow with a clean beginning. Young gives you the column, oil and Eden and ice all stacked and all real, and lets you feel the strain of holding it.

The graveyard hill, and the sensation of the whole

By the time you reach the last chapter, the book has drilled you so thoroughly through the layers that its final image lands with a particular force. On the graveyard hill above New Harmony, on a summer day in 1940, a barefoot pregnant woman the town calls Fuzzy Wuzzy sits with her children, eating raw hamburgers out of a paper sack, overlooking, Young writes, all time and space. Her children toss a ball above tombs inscribed on one side "All die at sunset" and on the other "The spirit of man is immortal." A dead sparrow lies at her feet. The baby turns in her stomach.

She has no idea what she is sitting on. Beneath that picnic is the whole column the book has shown you: the failed Owenites and their rain trees, the celibate Rappites and their angel, the elephants, the crocodile where the town now stands, the salt sea, the granite. She is a living crossing point, the exact place where all of that accumulated past presses up from below and the child not yet born presses forward from above, and she is simply eating her lunch. But the reader is not innocent anymore. The reader, drilled up and down through the layers for four hundred pages, feels the entire depth under that hill. That is the sensation this philosophy points at when it says you are the place where time crosses itself: the moment of finally seeing the structure you were always standing inside. Young does not tell you about it. She builds the book so that you feel it, from underneath, at the last possible moment.

What geology knows that a timeline does not

A timeline gives you sequence and causation, one thing leading to the next, and it quietly promises that the story is over, that the present is where the arrow finally came to rest. Geology promises nothing of the kind. It says the present is a surface, and under the surface is everything, undestroyed, pressing up, still for sale in the shops and still worn into the hopscotch stone and still burning as oil in the engines. Nothing arrived. Nothing concluded. The Rappites did not fail into the past; they failed into the ground, where they still are.

This is why Young could not have written a normal history of New Harmony and said what she needed to say. A line would have let the reader believe the utopias were finished, safely behind us, a closed case with a lesson attached. By refusing the line and building the column instead, she keeps the failed futures exactly where they actually are, which is beneath our feet and holding us up. The past does not go away. It accumulates. And the honest shape for a book about it is not an arrow but a core sample, drilled straight down through the layers, every one of them still there.
