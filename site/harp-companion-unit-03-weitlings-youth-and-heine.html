Unit 3, pp. 33-48: Weitling's youth and Heine

← Unit 2 · Harp Song for a Radical: A History Companion · Unit 4 →

Background for this unit: The History in Harp Song for a Radical

Young sets two German exiles side by side: Wilhelm Weitling, the tailor jailed in Switzerland for preaching communism, and Heinrich Heine, the poet exiled in Paris. Around them she gathers the religious visionaries, from Ann Lee to the Saint-Simonians, who shared their hunger for a new world.

Major entries

Weitling's imprisonment and prison poems (pp. 33-40)

  • What happened: Swiss police arrested Weitling in Zurich in June 1843 as he prepared to publish The Gospel of a Poor Sinner, which presented Jesus as a communist. He was convicted of blasphemy and incitement, served about ten months, and was deported to Prussia in 1844.

  • The poems: He wrote a cycle published as Kerkerpoesien (Prison Poems, 1844). Young lists titles such as "Conscience," "The Betrayer," "To My Judge and to My Prosecutor," and "Forty-eight Hours in the Dark" (p. 35). "The Monster" is the detective staring through the peephole of his cell.

  • Why it matters: His arrest made him famous across the radical underground. The London Communist Chronicle named him with Babeuf and Joshua Jacob as the three "illustrious communist martyrs" since the French Revolution (p. 33).

  • How Young uses it: The cell, the peephole, and the sense of being buried alive all prefigure Debs in Atlanta. Weitling becomes Debs's ancestor in suffering.

  • Further reading: Carl Wittke, The Utopian Communist (1950).

Heinrich Heine (pp. 40-48)

  • Who he was: German-Jewish poet (1797-1856), born in Düsseldorf. He studied in Berlin, where he heard Hegel lecture, and converted to Protestantism in 1825, calling baptism his "ticket of admission" to European culture. He moved to Paris in 1831 and never lived in Germany again.

  • Why it matters: His Book of Songs made him the most popular German lyric poet of the century. His satire Germany: A Winter's Tale (1844) mocked Prussian censorship and was banned. He knew Marx in Paris in 1843 and 1844.

  • Family: His rich uncle Salomon Heine, a Hamburg banker, paid him an allowance and resented his writing. Heine's unrequited love for his cousin Amalie, Salomon's daughter, fed his early verse. His wife Mathilde was a Paris shop girl he married in 1841. From 1848 he was paralyzed and bedridden in what he called his "mattress grave." He is buried at Montmartre.

  • Where the record differs: Young calls Heine's weavers' poem popular with "old Hamburgers" and ties it to power-loom competition (p. 42). The poem, "The Silesian Weavers" (1844), was written about the uprising of hand-loom weavers in Silesia that June, which the Prussian army crushed.

  • Further reading: Jeffrey L. Sammons, Heinrich Heine: A Modern Biography (1979).

Ann Lee and the Shakers (p. 37)

  • Who she was: A Manchester mill worker (1736-1784) whose followers believed her to be the second appearance of Christ, in female form. She led a small group to New York in 1774. Young describes her supporting the group by laundering near City Hall.

  • Why it matters: The Shakers (the United Society of Believers in Christ's Second Appearing) practiced celibacy, equality of the sexes, and common property. At their peak in the 1840s they had some six thousand members in about twenty villages. The central community at New Lebanon (later Mount Lebanon), New York, was organized after her death.

  • Further reading: Stephen J. Stein, The Shaker Experience in America (1992).

Andreas Dietsch and New Helvetia (p. 35)

  • Who he was: A Swiss brush maker and follower of Weitling who wrote a utopian tract, The Thousand-Year Kingdom (1842). In 1844 he led a band of Swiss emigrants to found a communal colony, New Helvetia, in Missouri.

  • What happened: The colony dissolved almost at once. Young writes that its founder died "alone in St. Louis of a broken heart" (p. 35). Weitling was offered free passage to join it and declined.

  • Why it matters: It is one of dozens of small German communal colonies of the 1840s that left almost no trace. Young rescues it from oblivion in exactly the way Debs wanted forgotten pioneers remembered.

Saint-Simon and the Saint-Simonians (pp. 45-46)

  • Who they were: Henri de Saint-Simon (1760-1825) was a French aristocrat who argued that society should be run by producers, industrialists, scientists, and workers, rather than idle nobles. After his death, Barthélemy Prosper Enfantin turned his followers into a quasi-religious sect that preached the "rehabilitation of the flesh" and sought a female messiah.

  • Why it matters: The Saint-Simonians shaped French banking, railroads, and the Suez Canal, which Enfantin promoted. Heine admired them and dedicated a book to Enfantin.

  • Where the record differs: Young places Enfantin's followers in a community "near the forest of Rambouillet" on Fourier's pattern (p. 45). The Saint-Simonian retreat of 1832 was at Ménilmontant, outside Paris. The phalanstery near Rambouillet, at Condé-sur-Vesgre (1832), was a Fourierist project. Saint-Simon did propose a Panama canal to Spanish officials in his youth.

  • Further reading: Frank E. Manuel, The New World of Henri Saint-Simon (1956).

Shorter entries

  • Gracchus Babeuf (p. 33): French revolutionary executed in 1797 for the "Conspiracy of Equals," an attempt to abolish private property. He is often called the first modern communist.

  • Joshua Jacob (p. 33): An Irish Quaker who founded the "White Quakers" in Dublin and was jailed for contempt of court. The Communist Chronicle counted him a martyr.

  • The Communist Chronicle (p. 33): A London paper edited by Goodwyn Barmby, an early English communist, in the 1840s.

  • Father George Rapp (p. 37): Leader of the Harmony Society, who came from Württemberg. He is covered in Unit 6.

  • The Mormons in Ohio, Missouri, and Illinois (p. 36): The church moved from Kirtland, Ohio, to Missouri, where it was expelled in 1838, then to Nauvoo, Illinois. Unit 7 covers them.

  • Moses Moser (p. 42): Heine's close friend in Berlin and a founder of the Society for the Culture and Science of the Jews.

  • Julius Campe (p. 43): Heine's Hamburg publisher, who kept his books in print despite censorship.

  • Charles Fourier (p. 45): See Unit 2. Young calls him "the cloth merchant," which was his trade.

Allusions

  • Swedenborgian (p. 33): Followers of Emanuel Swedenborg, the Swedish mystic who taught that the spiritual world surrounds and penetrates this one.

  • Samson (p. 41): The Hebrew strongman who pulled down the Philistine temple. Heine used the image of himself.

  • Aristophanes, The Birds (p. 46): The Greek comedy in which birds build an ideal city in the sky, Cloudcuckooland.

  • Dante's Divine Comedy (p. 44): Heine compared his own exile to Dante's.

  • Beelzebub (p. 38): A name for the devil.

  • Zion (p. 48): The biblical holy mountain, used for any promised land.
