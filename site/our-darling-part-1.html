Marguerite Young, Our Darling, Part 1: What It Reveals About Young and Her Work

Source: Marguerite Young, Our Darling: Tributes and Essays, edited by Miriam Fuchs (Dalkey Archive Press, 1994). This covers the front matter, Fuchs's introduction, Martha J. Sattler's chronology, and the first tributes. What follows keeps only the material that sheds light on Young and her work. Fond praise with nothing behind it has been left out. Quoted phrases from the book are marked with quotation marks.

The shape of a life

Young was born in Indianapolis on August 26, 1908, daughter of Chester Ellis Young and Fay Knight Young. Her parents separated in 1911, and she and her younger sister were raised by their maternal grandmother, Marguerite Herron Knight. She began writing poems as a child and joined a young authors' league at eleven. After the University of Indiana she took a BA in English and French from Butler University in 1930, the same year her poems first appeared in Poetry. She earned an MA at the University of Chicago in 1936, studying Elizabethan and Jacobean literature under Robert Morss Lovett, Ronald S. Crane, and T. V. Smith, and sitting in on Thornton Wilder's seminar alongside Gertrude Stein and Paul Goodman.

Two teaching facts matter for her place in American letters: at Shortridge High School in Indianapolis her students included Kurt Vonnegut, and at the University of Iowa in the mid-1950s she taught John Gardner. Her first book, Prismatic Ground, appeared in 1937, followed by Moderate Fable (1944) and Angel in the Forest (1945), her account of the utopian communities at New Harmony, Indiana. Her monumental novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling was published by Scribner's in 1965, roughly two decades after the first forty pages, then titled “The Worm in the Wheat,” were accepted by the editor Maxwell Perkins. She spent her later decades on a vast biography of the socialist Eugene V. Debs, setting aside an earlier biography of the Indiana poet James Whitcomb Riley to do it.

The literary company she kept is part of the record: early readers and editors of her work included Mark Van Doren and Albert Erskine; she met Mari Sandoz and Anaïs Nin in the 1940s, formed a lasting friendship with Nin, spent time at Yaddo with Carson McCullers and Truman Capote, and served as fiction editor of the journal Tiger's Eye. In Rome in the early 1950s she met Princess Marguerite Caetani, whose review Botteghe Oscura published her story “The Opium Lady.”

How she worked

Fuchs draws out a working method that explains the strangeness of Young's output. Young completed major portions of her narratives long before they reached print, and she said in 1988 that “All the books were written together, from the beginning.” Her books were not sealed off from one another; Fuchs describes borders in her work as no more than “the thinnest line,” thinner than a thread of starlight. The same preoccupations run through her journalism of the 1940s and 1950s as through the novel: utopias, the Midwest, dolls, horses, the world of sounds, and the realm of the deaf.

Amy Clampitt records the pivot from poet to novelist in Young's own account. Having begun Angel in the Forest as verse, Young found she “couldn't fit in all the facts, which are fabulous,” and inclusiveness became her method. Clampitt reads this drive to take in the concrete, the peculiar, and the local as what made it so hard for Young to stop writing Miss MacIntosh. John J. Stephan's memory of visiting Young's hotel room in Rome makes the method physical: a “paperscape” of unopened onion skin, ridges of typescript, and crumpled discards, with a New York editor eager to publish and unable to pry the manuscript loose.

What the critics actually argue

Anna Balakian places Young among the innovators who turn the regional into the mythic. She argues that Miss MacIntosh breaks the ordinary rules of how myth is thought to form, deliberately sowing new myths, and she puts the novel beside Proust's work as something the era has not yet caught up with. For years Balakian taught a course, “The Metamorphosis of the Novel,” that ran from Dostoevsky to Young, and she notes that Young's reputation was spreading abroad faster than at home.

Clampitt's essay is more than reminiscence. She reads Young as a distinctly Midwestern imagination shaped by the flat interior, “the depressed middle,” and she ties Young's suspicion of authority to that landscape and to her sympathy for Debs. The detail Clampitt keeps returning to, a passage on George III in his madness idly picking out a note on a harpsichord, she offers as typical of how Young undercuts power.

Fuchs's introduction usefully summarizes the scholarly essays that follow. Charles Ruas traces Young's epic imagination across Angel in the Forest, the unpublished Riley biography, and the Debs project, treating them as chapters of American history. Suzanne Oboler reads the novel's largesse as an argument about diversity and multiculturalism in American culture. Martha Sattler emphasizes its turn toward female personages and a female culture. Fatima Shaik attends to its language, poised between “the flimsy” and “the sublime.” Susan Strehle argues the novel replaces linear historical time with what Kristeva called “Women's Time.” Steven Shaviro finds a phantasmagoria that “expends itself to infinity” yet celebrates the ordinary. Fuchs's own reading treats the book's world without exclusions as a rare instance of genuinely “feminine” writing.

The real people inside the fiction

Young insisted she did not invent the characters she wrote about, and the chronology names several living models. Minna Weissenbach, to whom Young read as a young woman in Chicago, became “The Opium Lady.” The artist Howard Mitcham was the model for the stone-deaf man in Miss MacIntosh. Stephan's family, who ran Tiger's Eye from Bleecker Street a few doors from Young, watched the character Mr. Spitzer take shape; a 1948 note in the journal already predicted the novel was “reaching epic proportions.”

One small cultural trace is worth keeping: Anne Tyler used Miss MacIntosh as her wandering hero's traveling companion, and a hardback copy turns up in the film of her novel The Accidental Tourist, tucked into the hero's suitcase.

In her own words

Fuchs closes with Young's answer to a question she used to put to her students, about why anyone chooses to write prose fiction: “They want to be known and loved beyond their time. Here was I, this was what I thought, this was what I felt.”
