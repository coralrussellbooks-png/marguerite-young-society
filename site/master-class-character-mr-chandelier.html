Characters: Mr. Chandelier

He hangs from the ceiling of the mother's sealed room, and in "some slow moment of awakening" she looks up and sees that "the chandelier was Mr. Chandelier, her companion, not always faithful." He is a light fixture made a lover, "with seven lighted candles in his seven crystal hands," "with his seven bent flames lighting, to its four corners, a dark, empty heaven." He is the beautiful one among the mother's imaginary guests, all prisms and reflected fire, and the single word she attaches to him tells his whole character: unfaithful. The light that comes and goes, that flares and dims, that will not stay, she has understood as a fickle companion, and she loves him anyway.

He is the constructed reality at its most gorgeous, the ordinary object charged past bearing with significance. A chandelier is glass and candle and borrowed flame, and the mother has made of it a seven-handed being lighting the void, a suitor with prisms for a face. The philosophy this book keeps testing has a sense of the sacred that points not upward to heaven but deeper into the ordinary thing, and Mr. Chandelier is that exactly, an everyday fixture becomes a small radiant god who lights "a dark, empty heaven" from inside the room. The mother asks "no further verification" than that he shines. His reality is his light, and his light is real while it lasts.

But he is "not always faithful," and that is the truth of him. He is the vision that will not stay, the beauty that flares and goes dark, the companion made of flame that cannot promise to keep burning. The mother, who has lost the visible world and lives among her dreamed guests, has chosen for her most beautiful companion the one who is by nature intermittent, present only while the candles hold, faithless the way light is faithless, here and then gone. She loves the flaring thing knowing it will dim.

The turn for a writer is a small, bright one. Mr. Chandelier is the beautiful image you conjure that will not stay faithful, the vision that lights the empty heaven for a moment and then goes dark, the companion made of glass and borrowed fire. Every maker knows him, the radiance that arrives unbidden and cannot be commanded to remain, that flares through the room and then leaves one in the dark ceiling with the cold prisms.

He is the sacred ordinary at its most seductive and least reliable, the everyday object that becomes, for as long as the flame holds, a seven-handed god, and then is only a chandelier again. The book lets the mother love him without demanding he be constant, "asking no further verification than just that" he shines, which may be the only honest way to love the beautiful thing that comes and goes: to take the light while it burns and forgive it for being light.
