Chapter 6

  • Marguerite Young did comment about how all people wear masks. They have at least an inner component and an outer component that is shown to the public.

Chapter 6

  • “She was breaking into two, herself and the other, this face unknown”: Young seems to be describing through Madge a form of depersonalization-derealization that is now labeled a disorder and can be brought about by stress or trauma such as rape.

Chapter 9

  • “There are many gay deceivers”: A “gay deceiver” was an old name for a rake or seducer. Gay here could mean happy or may be in reference to homosexuals. The term gay to describe immorality or someone addicted to pleasure was in use in the 17th century. Gertrude Stein in 1922 used the word gay to specifically reference homosexuals. In 1950, the earliest reference found to date for the word gay as a self-described name for homosexuals came from Alfred A. Gross who used it in the June 1950 issue of SIR magazine. In a series of interviews with Susan S. Neville, Marguerite Young said she had lovers both male and female. There was a relationship where she would not marry him but said he could visit her every Tuesday for the rest of her life.

Chapter 13

  • Marguerite Young was a reader of Anatole France’s work. Anatole France was a French author, poet, and critic who won the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1921. His short story “Marguerite” first appeared in book form in Paris in 1920, with woodcuts by Siméon, and in J. Lewis May’s English translation in 1921. France was also known for his socialist sympathies and support of the Russian Revolution in 1917.
  • “a perpetual garden party omitting the tragic spirit”: The Epicureans met in the Garden, the school Epicurus founded in Athens.

Chapter 22 · Technique

  • Symbolism: The chapter abounds with symbolic references to ships, storms, and phantoms, enhancing themes of memory and loss.
  • Stream of Consciousness: Vera’s thoughts flow freely between observation, memory, and philosophical reflection, emphasizing her inner turmoil. Young said both things about the method. She told Studs Terkel, “This is not a stream of consciousness novel. It’s a very controlled, rather realistic treatment of that kind of attitude” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965), and in 1983, asked whether the stream of consciousness was the method of the novel, she answered, “Yes, the stream of consciousness” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983).
  • Allusion: Biblical references to creation days, historical figures (Emperor Constantine, King Canute), and biblical figures (Jeremiah) deepen thematic complexity.
  • Contrast: Between Vera’s emotional state and Miss MacIntosh’s external cheerfulness and routine.

Chapter 30

  • “Eustace, Eustace! Oh, is it you, Eustace dear”: At Iowa (1955-57), Young “would pause to greet Henry James at the door” of her classroom (David O. Dowling, “The Guru,” in A Delicate Aggression: Savagery and Survival in the Iowa Writers’ Workshop, Yale University Press, 2019). She kept portraits of dead writers on her walls. Catherine greets the invisible Eustace the same way. Young also told Studs Terkel of a friend in a New York hospital who overheard “one of the richest ladies in America,” dying, greet guests who were not there: “Eustace, turn to the left. Oh, isn’t that Mr. Henry James? How do you do, Mr. James?” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).

Chapter 33

  • “those who must punch time-clocks in great factories, those who must turn great wheels”: Willard Bundy patented the time clock in 1888. Chaplin’s Modern Times (1936) opens on workers at the time clock and swallows Charlie in the great wheels. Young said, “Charlie Chaplin has influenced every book I’ve ever written” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983).

Chapter 34

  • “that great Crusader was not a man’s woman”: There is an uncomfortable trope about women/feminists that Young uses against Cousin Hannah. She was so vehement against men when she was so ugly that men didn’t find her attractive and so there was nothing for her to defend against. It keeps coming up even to the present day. Young admitted that “there was a tendency to be satirical about her,” but she used the Alpine story of the lost skirt to explain Hannah, and in the trousseau trunks of her real source found that “she had a dream too” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).

Chapter 36

  • “But who knew the hidden man, that man he might never be, neither himself nor his twin brother”: In her first draft “Mr. Spitzer knew that he was really Peyron the gambler,” which “seemed so tricky to me” that she rewrote it so that he does not know (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983).

Chapter 39

  • “Oh, come in, she would say”: This is what Young would do in her classroom. At Iowa she “would pause to greet Henry James at the door” (David O. Dowling, “The Guru,” in A Delicate Aggression: Savagery and Survival in the Iowa Writers’ Workshop, Yale University Press, 2019). She told Studs Terkel of Miss MacIntosh’s source, dying and greeting guests who were not there: “Oh, isn’t that Mr. Henry James? How do you do, Mr. James?” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). That is a big clue as to what is going on here. The novel is rich with a dialectical engagement of ideas and beliefs that operate beyond the limits of time and space, reflecting on the nature of existence, identity, memory, and the interconnectedness of all souls. This ongoing conversation is woven into the narrative structure and the psychological depth of the characters throughout the book.
  • Young’s Angel in the Forest (1945) tells of New Harmony’s two utopias, the Rappites and the Owenites.

Chapter 42

  • Young said, “I thought of Margaret Fuller in relation to not only Cousin Hannah, but some of the other characters.” Fuller drowned off Fire Island in 1850 (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).
  • “a secret woman, a bride”: Young told Studs Terkel that the woman behind Cousin Hannah brought home sealed trunks for forty years, and at her death each held a bridal trousseau: “She had a dream too” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).
  • “he who had played his last spade”: The ace of spades has long been called the death card. Peron was a gambler: in Young’s first draft “Mr. Spitzer knew that he was really Peyron the gambler.”

Chapter 43

  • “the spokes of the wheel”: Young described the characters in this book as the spokes of a wheel coming out from Miss MacIntosh in the center of the story: “I always thought of Miss MacIntosh as the center of the wheel, the hub,” with the other characters as “the spokes” (Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs, “A Conversation with Marguerite Young,” Review of Contemporary Fiction 23, no. 1, Spring 2003 (1989 original)). In this chapter Mr. Spitzer lets “the spokes of the wheel” extend as far as possible before they reach “the clouded rim of doubt.”

Chapter 44

  • “I must find my skirt”: This is the Alpine story Young told Studs Terkel, of two bloomer girls who shed their skirts at a summit, and the younger, going back for hers, was lost: “the lady lost her life for a skirt” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).
  • “For all dead loves and all remembered things”: The epigraph Young gave the novel is in this chapter “For all dead loves and all remembered things. I have travelled through many seas.” The phrase reflects the novel’s overarching themes of memory, loss, and the blending of reality and dream, emphasizing that the journey through these “seas” is both personal and universal, touching on the shared human experience of love and remembrance.

Chapter 45

  • Young said that Esther Longtree, at the end of the book, “is the mother of us all, I would say, quoting Gertrude Stein” (Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs, “A Conversation with Marguerite Young,” Review of Contemporary Fiction 23, no. 1, Spring 2003 (1989 original)). Stein’s opera The Mother of Us All (1947), with music by Virgil Thomson, is about Susan B. Anthony. Steven Shaviro also reported that Young called Mr. Spitzer the father of us all.
  • In Young’s first draft, she told Kay Bonetti, “Mr. Spitzer knew that he was really Peyron the gambler” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983).
  • “We were two lone women. There was no man.”: This is Hannah’s confession, and the secret Mr. Spitzer will keep. See the bridal trousseaus Young described to Studs Terkel under “a secret woman, a bride” in the Chapter 42 resources.

Chapter 49

  • All of this, Young accomplished within the framework of a deeply poetic and existential meditation with the main character Mr. Spitzer. This chapter vividly dramatizes the Problem of Universals by dissolving traditional boundaries between particulars and universals, questioning the independent reality of universals, and exploring the fluid, relational, and perceptual nature of universals. It suggests a skeptical or nominalist stance toward universals while simultaneously hinting at an elusive unity or underlying essence, thus reflecting the enduring philosophical tension central to the problem.
  • Instead of explaining the Problem of Universals, Young’s approach is to immerse the reader in a narrative world that embodies a different metaphysical perspective. This perspective suggests that existence is characterized by perpetual transformation, blurred boundaries between individuals and forms, and a subjective, fluid reality where: the distinction between the “one” (universal) and the “many” (particular) is not a problem to be solved but a condition to be experienced. The novel’s style, with its long, winding sentences, repetitions, and associative leaps, mirrors this thematic dissolution of linear time and discrete entities, reinforcing the idea that reality is a complex, interwoven, and ever-changing tapestry of impressions, memories, and imaginative constructs, rather than a collection of fixed particulars related to stable universals. Marguerite Young does not offer a historical explanation for the Problem of Universals. Her novel presents a world where the premise of the problem: the existence of distinct particulars and their relation to universals is dissolved by a pervasive sense of fluidity, interconnectedness, and the illusion of individual form. Her perspective is a unique exploration of a reality where identity is mutable, categories are blurred, and the abstract and the concrete are intertwined in a perpetual, dreamlike state. The Problem of Universals is implicitly addressed not by solving it, but by creating a narrative world where the traditional terms of the problem no longer apply in a straightforward manner. This is not an explanation in the philosophical sense, but rather a literary and metaphysical exploration of a world constructed on principles that challenge the assumptions behind the Problem of Universals.

Chapter 53

  • Included is Althea Garrison because she was the first transgender person to be elected to a state legislature although she was not active in politics at the time Young wrote this novel.

Chapter 59

  • "Might not a musician fear music as he feared death, dying into his music, he sometimes asked? For what was more ephemeral than music…" Music here embodies both the randomness of life’s moments and the human impulse to find or create meaning and continuity. Young uses the character of Mr. Spitzer as a vehicle to explore the philosophical dialectic between chance and randomness in human existence. Chance is portrayed simultaneously as a cosmic principle underpinning the universe's unpredictability and as a source of meaning amid apparent chaos. Randomness, by contrast, is the disorienting, fragmenting force that challenges identity, perception, and certainty. Mr. Spitzer’s reflections reveal a deep ambivalence: he recognizes the role of chance in shaping life and self but struggles with its implications for trust, continuity, and purpose. The chapter suggests that while randomness may govern individual events, chance might be understood as an underlying rhythm or poetic order that gives shape to the flux of existence. This tension between chaos and order, randomness and meaningful chance, forms the philosophical core of the chapter.

Chapter 63

  • Poverty is a form of stress that research has shown alters a person’s behavior and development. The Great Depression would have been recent history for the characters in this story and Marguerite Young herself. The average American family lived by the Depression-era motto: “Use it up, wear it out, make do or do without.” Many tried to keep up appearances and carry on with life as close to normal as possible while they adapted to new economic circumstances. Households embraced a new level of frugality in daily life. They kept kitchen gardens, patched worn-out clothes and passed on trips.

Chapter 65

  • "Selah, Selah" functions as a call to pause and reflect, often used in biblical or poetic contexts, and Marguerite Young employs it to deepen the reader's engagement with the mood or theme at that point in the story.

Chapter 74

  • “...she had done much lying but no laying. They were wrong. There was no one equal to her, no one who could surpass her, and that was why she was so lonely. If she had been a man, of course, she would have chased after all the women! She would have left her children in every state of the union and in all foreign countries, pieces of her, pieces of their father. But she would not have claimed any of them, so she would have left a trail of broken hearts and crying children if she had been a man. Yet in spite of a few little worries and no fears, she was happy and self-sufficient, immeasurably suffused with radiance which made her shine… and no one from the outside world could annoy her or frighten her.” Young seems to at least in part use Esther to explore the sexual double standard where men who have many sexual experiences are considered - a stud. Whereas women who have many sexual experiences are considered - a slut. A double standard is the application of different sets of principles for situations that are, in principle, the same. Research has found that casual sexual activity is regarded as more acceptable for men than for women. Double standards between men and women can potentially exist with regards to: dating, cohabitation, virginity, marriage/remarriage, sexual abuse/assault/harassment, domestic violence and singleness.

Chapter 74

  • “She guessed it had all been over too soon, a whirl, an aching, and dullness.” Young may be referring to rape culture in which rape is pervasive and normalized due to societal attitudes about gender and sexuality. Behaviors commonly associated with rape culture include victim blaming, slut-shaming, sexual objectification, trivializing rape, denial of widespread rape, refusing to acknowledge the harm caused by sexual violence, or some combination of these. Men and women who are victims of sexual violence may not report the assault because of shame, the stigma, not being believed and the criminal justice system. Traumatic memories since there is a heightened emotional response often are remembered as specific details about the event as opposed to false memories. Research shows that forgetting, not thinking about, and even misremembering an assault may be both necessary and adaptive to help survivors cope with their reality.

Chapter 74

  • “For everything was so sad, and the sky bled in her dreams, and she had no menstrual period, and she was always pregnant. Who was innocent? Who was guilty? Her own childhood reproached her.” This section may refer to the death of Esther’s baby when she was young and how that traumatized her even now. Rape has been used as a plot device in literature and other media. It has been criticized for using rape as a metaphor that displaces the actual, violent and traumatic act of rape. It is considered misogynistic in the way female rape victims continue to be cast as collateral damage and the assault and rape of women is used as a plot point in a narrative that is fundamentally about men. Young is making this about Esther and showing how things would be different if she were a man. Young makes many references to mythology. The struggle to escape from sexual pursuit is one of the most popular motifs of Greek mythology: Persephone, Callisto, Daphne, Europa, Io, etc. Greek mythology depicts women as vulnerable to assault, able to escape only through death, or metamorphosis like Daphne who becomes a laurel tree. The consequence of fleeing sexual violence is another type of force on the body, the loss of the human form where women who survive sexual violence become pregnant, and when gods as the rapists, produce a hero child. The Chicago detective is described as raping Esther because he thinks she’s dead. Chione in Greek mythology was said to be so beautiful that she was the object of a thousand men's desire. Hermes used magic to make Chione to fall into a deep sleep and proceeded to rape her. Since Young has said Esther is the mother of us all and Mr. Spitzer as the father of us all, these could be archetypes for Adam and Eve and possibly Lilith. In On the Origin of World, part of the Nag Hammadi Library, Eve is described as being raped by archangels and bears children. In the Kabbalist myth Eve is raped by Lilith, Adam’s first wife, in the guise of Samael, Lilith’s demon lover or Satan. The Kabbalists continued the story to include Eve, through her menses, to have defiled Adam which then lead to Lilith being able to rape Adam, become impregnated and birth Lilin or demons by Adam.

Chapter 77

  • Abortion has been used to separate good women from bad women. This stigma punishes the bad woman in society. Even if the abortion is necessary to save a woman’s life, religion often stipulates that a woman must sacrifice herself. It is better to die than live with the stigma of having an abortion. The only good heroine is a dead one. When Young was writing this over eighteen years, abortion and contraception were not legal.

Chapter 77

  • “But he only dealt with the deaths of little white children, for the deaths of the little black children were not his business, there being so many and so unimportant.” When Young wrote this the civil rights movement had been going on since 1954.
