Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Part of To All My Darlings, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).

Definition from research

Chapter 1

  • Marguerite Young in the Dalkey Interview said she talked to coal miners in the Ozarks and it was because of them that she decided to write about New Harmony (a utopia in Angels in the Forest) and that it is based upon social consciousness.

Chapter 3

  • In an interview Young said she had no idea there was a real town by the name of What Cheer, Iowa. She thought she had made it up.

Chapter 3

  • The author Virginia Woolf who committed suicide by filling her pockets with stones and walking into the River Ouse in March 1941 only several years before Marguerite Young started writing this novel.

Chapter 6

  • Marguerite Young did comment about how all people wear masks. They have at least an inner component and an outer component that is shown to the public.

Chapter 6

  • “She was breaking into two, herself and the other, this face unknown”: Young seems to be describing through Madge a form of depersonalization-derealization that is now labeled a disorder and can be brought about by stress or trauma such as rape.

Chapter 9

  • “There are many gay deceivers”: A “gay deceiver” was an old name for a rake or seducer. Gay here could mean happy or may be in reference to homosexuals. The term gay to describe immorality or someone addicted to pleasure was in use in the 17th century. Gertrude Stein in 1922 used the word gay to specifically reference homosexuals. In 1950, the earliest reference found to date for the word gay as a self-described name for homosexuals came from Alfred A. Gross who used it in the June 1950 issue of SIR magazine. In a series of interviews with Susan S. Neville, Marguerite Young said she had lovers both male and female. There was a relationship where she would not marry him but said he could visit her every Tuesday for the rest of her life.

Chapter 13

  • Marguerite Young loved Anatole France’s writing and read him widely, in French as well as in English. When her grandmother died, her first thought was that “now she can tell Anatole France that she has a little granddaughter on Earth who adores him” (Marguerite Young, interview with Charles Ruas, “The Art of Fiction No. 66,” The Paris Review 71, Fall 1977). Anatole France was a French author, poet, and critic who won the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1921. His short story “Marguerite” first appeared in book form in Paris in 1920, with woodcuts by Siméon, and in J. Lewis May’s English translation in 1921 (Project Gutenberg edition of the John Lane translation, 1921). France had socialist sympathies and was an outspoken supporter of the 1917 Russian Revolution, and in 1920 he backed the newly founded French Communist Party. (Wikipedia)
  • “a perpetual garden party omitting the tragic spirit”: The Epicureans met in the Garden, the school Epicurus founded in Athens.

Chapter 22 · Technique

  • Symbolism: The chapter abounds with symbolic references to ships, storms, and phantoms, enhancing themes of memory and loss.
  • Stream of Consciousness: Vera’s thoughts flow freely between observation, memory, and philosophical reflection, emphasizing her inner turmoil. Young said both things about the method. She told Studs Terkel, “This is not a stream of consciousness novel. It’s a very controlled, rather realistic treatment of that kind of attitude” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965), and in 1983, asked whether the stream of consciousness was the method of the novel, she answered, “Yes, the stream of consciousness” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983).
  • Allusion: Biblical references to creation days, historical figures (Emperor Constantine, King Canute), and biblical figures (Jeremiah) deepen thematic complexity.
  • Contrast: Between Vera’s emotional state and Miss MacIntosh’s external cheerfulness and routine.

Chapter 30

  • “Eustace, Eustace! Oh, is it you, Eustace dear”: At Iowa (1955-57), Young “would pause to greet Henry James at the door” of her classroom (David O. Dowling, “The Guru,” in A Delicate Aggression: Savagery and Survival in the Iowa Writers’ Workshop, Yale University Press, 2019). She kept portraits of dead writers on her walls. Catherine greets the invisible Eustace the same way. Young also told Studs Terkel of a friend in a New York hospital who overheard “one of the richest ladies in America,” dying, greet guests who were not there: “Eustace, turn to the left. Oh, isn’t that Mr. Henry James? How do you do, Mr. James?” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).

Chapter 33

  • “those who must punch time-clocks in great factories, those who must turn great wheels”: Willard Bundy patented the time clock in 1888. Chaplin’s Modern Times (1936) opens on workers at the time clock and swallows Charlie in the great wheels. Young said, “Charlie Chaplin has influenced every book I’ve ever written” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983).

Chapter 34

  • “that great Crusader was not a man’s woman”: There is an uncomfortable trope about women/feminists that Young uses against Cousin Hannah. She was so vehement against men when she was so ugly that men didn’t find her attractive and so there was nothing for her to defend against. It keeps coming up even to the present day. Young admitted that “there was a tendency to be satirical about her,” but she used the Alpine story of the lost skirt to explain Hannah, and in the trousseau trunks of her real source found that “she had a dream too” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).

Chapter 36

  • “But who knew the hidden man, that man he might never be, neither himself nor his twin brother”: In her first draft “Mr. Spitzer knew that he was really Peyron the gambler,” which “seemed so tricky to me” that she rewrote it so that he does not know (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983).

Chapter 39

  • “Oh, come in, she would say”: This is what Young would do in her classroom. At Iowa she “would pause to greet Henry James at the door” (David O. Dowling, “The Guru,” in A Delicate Aggression: Savagery and Survival in the Iowa Writers’ Workshop, Yale University Press, 2019). She told Studs Terkel of a dying lady in a New York hospital, the source for Catherine’s greetings, who greeted guests who were not there: “Oh, isn’t that Mr. Henry James? How do you do, Mr. James?” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). That is a big clue as to what is going on here.
  • Young’s Angel in the Forest (1945) tells of New Harmony’s two utopias, the Rappites and the Owenites.

Chapter 42

  • Young said, “I thought of Margaret Fuller in relation to not only Cousin Hannah, but some of the other characters.” Fuller drowned off Fire Island in 1850 (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).
  • “a secret woman, a bride”: Young told Studs Terkel that the woman behind Cousin Hannah brought home sealed trunks for forty years, and at her death each held a bridal trousseau: “She had a dream too” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).
  • “he who had played his last spade”: The ace of spades has long been called the death card. Peron was a gambler: in Young’s first draft “Mr. Spitzer knew that he was really Peyron the gambler.”

Chapter 43

  • “the spokes of the wheel”: Young described the characters in this book as the spokes of a wheel coming out from Miss MacIntosh in the center of the story: “I always thought of Miss MacIntosh as the center of the wheel, the hub,” with the other characters as “the spokes” (Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs, “A Conversation with Marguerite Young,” Review of Contemporary Fiction 23, no. 1, Spring 2003 (1989 original)). In this chapter Mr. Spitzer lets “the spokes of the wheel” extend as far as possible before they reach “the clouded rim of doubt.”

Chapter 44

  • “I must find my skirt”: This is the Alpine story Young told Studs Terkel, of two bloomer girls who shed their skirts at a summit, and the younger, going back for hers, was lost: “the lady lost her life for a skirt” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).
  • “For all dead loves and all remembered things”: The epigraph Young gave the novel is in this chapter “For all dead loves and all remembered things. I have travelled through many seas.”

Chapter 45

  • Young said that Esther Longtree, at the end of the book, “is the mother of us all, I would say, quoting Gertrude Stein” (Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs, “A Conversation with Marguerite Young,” Review of Contemporary Fiction 23, no. 1, Spring 2003 (1989 original)). Stein’s opera The Mother of Us All (1947), with music by Virgil Thomson, is about Susan B. Anthony. Steven Shaviro also reported that Young called Mr. Spitzer the father of us all.
  • In Young’s first draft, she told Kay Bonetti, “Mr. Spitzer knew that he was really Peyron the gambler” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983).
  • “We were two lone women. There was no man.”: This is Hannah’s confession, and the secret Mr. Spitzer will keep. See the bridal trousseaus Young described to Studs Terkel under “a secret woman, a bride” in the Chapter 42 resources.

Chapter 49

  • In this chapter Mr. Spitzer lives the problem of universals rather than explaining it. He is “many countries, many times, many dimensions,” with no unitary self; there is “no stone which was not a cloud”; music “was ever approximate and not actual,” and “none should live to hear its completion”; signs and gestures have “many and indeterminate meanings.” The imagery leans toward nominalism or conceptualism, with universals as shadows and reflections, yet a mystical unity remains: “He lived between the golden spokes of light. He lived between the spokes of darkness.” Young does not solve the problem; she builds a world where its terms dissolve. For more, see The Problem of Universals, Platonism and nominalism in the Chapter 2 resources, and Abelard, Roscellinus, and William of Champeaux in the Chapter 52 resources.

Chapter 53

  • “People were indifferent to those things which interested Mr. Spitzer”: Young drew Peron’s world from her own reading: “I’ve read a great many books on gambling, and I’ve been interested in it since early childhood, because I knew gamblers in early childhood.” Of her research she said, “Baseball, gambling, vaudeville, burlesque jokes, all this became part, as well as Moby Dick, the Bible” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).
  • “the one chance in a million”: Young told Studs Terkel, “Mr. Spitzer is really two people. There are two Mr. Spitzers. One is a musical composer who has lived in memory of his gambling brother, the suicide, and who since his brother’s death has never composed a music which can be heard.” Peron “is a gay gambler, an impresario in the world of games and sports,” who describes himself “as the gambler who took one chance in a million, committed suicide, and lived.” In the third draft she realized “that it was not just a case of twins, but the two parts of the same man… a beautiful case of duality, which might be in all of us, like left and right, shadow and sunlight” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).

Chapter 63 · The Wig and Baldness

  • Miss MacIntosh’s wig symbolizes the facade of social norms and appearances, which she ultimately reveals as a protective but deceptive covering. Young described the wig as the illusion itself, “the dream by which she lived” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965), and the hair it replaces as “the symbol of the dream and of the feminine in life that she never had” (Marguerite Young, interview with Duncan MacDonald, Observation Point, 1965).
  • Her baldness, exposed in a moment of vulnerability, represents raw truth, mortality, and the stripping away of illusions.

Chapter 65

  • Young claimed descent from John Knox on her mother’s side. While writing the novel she “discovered that the Macintoshes, this really astonished me, were descendants from a prince, a bald prince, who was washed up on the shores of Scotland,” so that “Miss Macintosh is really a bald prince, disguised with a red wig and the woman’s gear” (Marguerite Young, interview, WNYC radio, New York).

Chapter 74

  • “an old maid plucking at a daisy’s petals”: “He loves me, he loves me not.” In French the game is effeuiller la marguerite, plucking the daisy, and marguerite is the French for daisy.
  • “This Chicago detective was creating quite a problem even now”: The detective’s suit “was based upon a real case which happened in Chicago,” which Young read about in a New York paper, “and I wove it into my story” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983).

Chapter 77

  • “He had been told by his father that his father would give him the secret”: “When I started to write about New Harmony, the utopia, I was inspired to do so by an account of a flour miller who tried to pass on his thumbmark to his son. That cannot be done, and the son ended by committing suicide.” Young wrote a short story about him that failed, and “I have put that flour miller and this thumb into ‘Miss MacIntosh, My Darling’ somewhere. In fact, I’ve put that flour miller and his thumb into everything I’ve ever written since,” “the idea of identity and passage of the soul” (Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs, “A Conversation with Marguerite Young,” Review of Contemporary Fiction 23, no. 1, Spring 2003 (1989 original)).

Chapter 82

  • “how my mother, after many years of sleeping, arose from her bed and walked”: So did Weissenbach. She “had been able to get out of bed after many, many years, but this is what killed her, so that she turned old overnight. She had never shown the mark of time.” She died in 1943 in New York, and Young, who kept writing to her, learned of it months later from her daughter: “as long as you kept writing to Mother and I read your letters, I felt as if my mother was somehow alive” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983).
  • “the reason no one had heard his music since Peron’s death was that he was Peron”: Young compared Mr. Spitzer’s “great impersonation in this life” to “the Titchborn claimant” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965), Arthur Orton, who claimed in 1866 to be the lost heir Roger Tichborne and was convicted of perjury in 1874. In her first draft “Mr. Spitzer knew that he was really Peyron the gambler,” which “seemed so tricky to me” that she rewrote it so that he does not know (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983).
  • “Little boidies don’t have no tits”: The stone-deaf man was Young’s friend Howard Mitcham, a deaf painter in Greenwich Village. “He used to love to sing this, little boydies don’t have no tits. Little boydies don’t have no umbilical cords,” and “he knew that I was putting him in” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983). For a magazine article on the deaf she interviewed deaf leaders in New York and heard the Lord’s Prayer in sign language at St. Mark’s (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).
