Definition from research

Chapter 18

  • “Where was Ratisbon?”: Ratisbon is the old name of Regensburg, in Bavaria, which Napoleon stormed in 1809. Browning’s “Incident of the French Camp” (1842) begins, “You know, we French stormed Ratisbon.”

Chapter 24

  • “seemed to think that he was waiting on Wellington”: Duke of Wellington was a leading figure in 19th-century Britain, victor over Napoleon at Waterloo (1815).

Chapter 29

  • “the bed of Charlemagne, the amethyst bed of Catherine the Great”: Historical people in the chapter are symbolic bridges between the personal and the universal, the real and the mythic, the past and the present. They enrich the novel’s exploration of identity, memory, mortality, and the elusive nature of reality, reinforcing its complex, dreamlike atmosphere and thematic depth.

Chapter 30

  • “Wellington riding at the head of his troops”: Arthur Wellesley, first Duke of Wellington was a famous military leader and political figure in 19th century England.

Chapter 35

  • Napoleon at the gates of Moscow may refer to Moscow being occupied in 1812 by French Emperor Napoleon Bonaparte’s army during the Napoleonic Wars.

Chapter 36

  • Waterloo Bridge is a road and foot traffic bridge crossing the River Thames in London.

Chapter 42

  • The taking of Venice was done by Napoleon Bonaparte and the Austrians in 1797.

Chapter 42

  • Napoleon was a French military and political leader who rose to prominence during the French Revolution.

Chapter 42

  • Waterloo may refer to Napoleon who was defeated in the Battle of Waterloo by the Duke of Wellington.

Chapter 54

  • “Napoleon’s invasion of Russia” is a symbolic and thematic allusion rather than a direct historical account. The phrase typically evokes themes of grand, disastrous endeavors marked by ambition, hubris, loss, and the harshness of an unforgiving environment. Napoleon’s invasion of Russia in 1812 was a monumental military campaign that ended in catastrophic failure due to the vast distances, severe winter, and logistical difficulties, leading to massive loss of life and retreat. In the novel, such historical references often serve metaphorical purposes, reflecting characters’ inner struggles, doomed quests, or the futility of certain human efforts: especially in the context of memory, identity, and the elusive nature of reality and dreams. Given the novel’s dense, dreamlike, and symbolic style, this likely signifies a personal or existential battle or perhaps an overwhelming challenge or a journey fraught with loss and disillusionment. It may also mirror the protagonist’s or another character’s confrontation with overwhelming odds, a harsh “winter” of the soul, or the collapse of grand illusions. Since the novel frequently intertwines personal histories with vast symbolic landscapes, this phrase might reflect the sense of an epic but doomed undertaking within the narrative, paralleling the historical event’s themes of ambition, defeat, and endurance through suffering.

Chapter 66

  • China has been referred to as “the sleeping Dragon.” In China, the dragon stands for power, strength, and good luck. In the past, the Chinese emperor was referred to as the Dragon. China’s economy covers thousands of years with cycles of prosperity and decline. "Let China sleep, for when she wakes up, she will shake the world." - Napoleon Bonaparte.
