Mrs. Hathaway: The Name That Would Not Change

← Musidora · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Beatrice Fitzgerald →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Lisa Lunde · Peron Spitzer

One brother burned to death, and within a few days she married the other, and wore black to the wedding. Mrs. Hathaway is a tale carried in the tavern gossip, a widow twice over, and she is the book's twin knot told in its plainest and cruelest form, because keeping the name while the man beneath it changes.

The facts are few and terrible. One brother, Rudolph, "burned to death and was a long time burning." Before the funeral was cold she married the second, Josh, "still in mourning for his brother," with "only a few days between the marriage and the funeral." Josh married her with his eyes open, "suspicious," wedding her "to make sure, to find out," and what he found out killed him. The town tells his death as a locked room and a frozen face, "the lights went out of his eyes," a door that shut by "nobody's hand," a man who "seemed to die when she came in," his cries "muffled under so many coats" that no one could hear. And it tells her answer, the one thing she is ever reported to say: "I'll still be Mrs. Hathaway."

Ask the wrong question and this is a murder ballad, the black widow and her two dead men. Ask the right one and Mrs. Hathaway is Mr. Spitzer's knot pulled tight and handed to a stranger. The plural self, the person who is not one, is the book's deepest subject, and where Spitzer gets the longest section in the novel to be two brothers in one uncertain body, Mrs. Hathaway gets a paragraph of gossip to prove the same thing from the outside.

The two brothers "had always loved each other," and she says the sentence that dissolves them into each other, that she has "married both brothers," that in mourning the first she lost the second. Two men, one marriage, and a widow who can no longer keep them apart. It is Peron and Joachim in another town, one brother dead and the other uncertain which he is, except that here the confusion is the wife's rather than the man's. "You would think she was married to Rudolph," the gossip says. "She never speaks of Josh." She grieves the dead brother through the living one and then through no one, exactly as Catherine grieves a composite of lost men and pours the tea for the one she cannot recognize.

What she holds onto is the name, and the name is the point. She was Mrs. Hathaway, she became Mrs. Hathaway, and "now she is Mrs. Hathaway who was twice widowed." The men change and die and the title stays, a fixed label over a shifting referent, which is Lisa Lunde's blackboard law walking around a small town, "all a's are c's." Hathaway is the constant that lets one brother stand for another, the way the black coachman could be "any old coachman" and the wrong passenger could ride in the right coach and death would not check the fare. She is not loyal to a husband. She is loyal to a name, and the name is loyal to no one, which is why it can outlast both men who wore it.

The turn for a writer is how much of a self can be carried by a single refused sentence. Mrs. Hathaway never explains, never confesses, never grieves out loud. She says five words, "I'll still be Mrs. Hathaway," and the five words hold everything, the murder she will not name, the brothers she cannot separate, the identity she has decided to keep no matter which man has to die to leave it intact. Young gives her no interior, because the gossip has no access to one, and the withholding is the character. A writer learns here that a figure glimpsed only in rumor can be built as precisely as one rendered from the inside, if the one thing she is allowed to say is chosen well enough. The whole Spitzer section circles the question of whether a person is one or two. Mrs. Hathaway answers it in a doorway, in a borrowed name, over a muffled body, and the answer is that a person can be neither, only a title that stays while the people underneath it keep changing places and dying.
