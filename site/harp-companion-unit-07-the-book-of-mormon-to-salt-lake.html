Unit 7, pp. 121-152: The Book of Mormon to Salt Lake

← Unit 6 · Harp Song for a Radical: A History Companion · Unit 8 →

Background for this unit: The History in Harp Song for a Radical

Young tells how the Book of Mormon was financed, translated, lost in part, and printed, largely through the story of its patron Martin Harris. She then leaps to Salt Lake City in 1861 through the eyes of Mark Twain and his brother Orion. The thread connecting them is the American dream of a kingdom built from nothing, and what it cost.

Major entries

Martin Harris (pp. 123-138)

  • Who he was: A prosperous farmer of Palmyra, New York (1783-1875), and one of Joseph Smith's first believers. His religious restlessness led him through several churches before Mormonism, as Young lists (p. 124).

  • The lost 116 pages: In 1828, while acting as Smith's scribe, Harris begged to show the manuscript to his skeptical wife. Smith allowed him to show it only to her, his brother Preserved Harris, and a few others (p. 128). The 116 pages vanished and were never recovered. Many suspect his wife destroyed them.

  • The mortgage: In August 1829 Harris mortgaged his farm to the printer Egbert B. Grandin for three thousand dollars to pay for five thousand copies. The Book of Mormon appeared in March 1830, and Harris later sold land to cover the debt.

  • Later life: He left the church in the 1830s but never denied his testimony. He went to Utah at eighty-seven, rejoined, and died there in 1875.

  • Further reading: Susan Easton Black and Larry C. Porter, Martin Harris: Uncompromising Witness of the Book of Mormon (2018).

The Three and Eight Witnesses (pp. 131-139)

  • What they were: Printed in every copy of the Book of Mormon are two statements. Oliver Cowdery, David Whitmer, and Martin Harris swore an angel had shown them the plates. Eight other men, among them Joseph Smith, Sr., his sons Hyrum and Samuel H. Smith, several Whitmers, and Hiram Page, swore they had handled them.

  • The translation: Much of the work was done in 1829 at the farmhouse of Peter Whitmer in Fayette, Seneca County, New York (p. 131), where the church was formally organized on April 6, 1830.

  • David Whitmer's later testimony: Whitmer broke with Smith in 1838 and settled in Richmond, Missouri. He gave newspaper interviews into old age, including one Young cites from the Chicago Tribune of December 15, 1885 (p. 132) and another from the Kingston, Missouri, Times of December 27, 1887 (p. 135). He died in 1888 affirming the book, as the Richmond Democrat reported (p. 139).

  • How Young uses it: She dates Whitmer's recollections against the eight-hour strikes and the Haymarket executions of November 1887. The same newspapers carried a dying prophet's witness and the hanging of the anarchists.

  • Further reading: Richard Lloyd Anderson, Investigating the Book of Mormon Witnesses (1981).

Brigham Young (pp. 136-152)

  • Who he was: A Vermont-born carpenter and painter (1801-1877) who joined the church in 1832. After Smith's murder he led the majority of the Saints from Nauvoo to the Great Salt Lake valley in 1846-1847. Young calls him "the Golden Lion of the Tribe of Zion" (p. 139), a play on his nickname, the Lion of the Lord.

  • Why it matters: He built one of the most successful colonizing efforts in American history, planting hundreds of settlements across the Great Basin. He was territorial governor of Utah from 1850 until the Utah War of 1857-1858, when federal troops were sent to assert control. He practiced plural marriage on a large scale, and his family lived in the Lion House and the Beehive House in Salt Lake City (pp. 142-143).

  • The beehive: The beehive was the Mormon emblem of cooperative industry. Twain described the crest as "a Golden Bee Hive, with the bees all at work" (p. 142). It remains Utah's state emblem.

  • Further reading: John G. Turner, Brigham Young: Pioneer Prophet (2012).

Mark Twain, Orion Clemens, and *Roughing It (pp. 139-152)

  • What happened: In 1861 President Lincoln appointed Orion Clemens secretary of the new Nevada Territory. His younger brother Samuel, whose career as a Mississippi River pilot had been ended by the war, paid both fares and went along. They crossed the plains by stagecoach and spent two days in Salt Lake City in August 1861.

  • The book: Twain turned the trip into Roughing It (1872). Its chapters on the Mormons mock Brigham Young's many wives and call the Book of Mormon "chloroform in print."

  • Where the record differs: Young dates the stagecoach trip to 1862 (p. 139). It was the summer of 1861.

  • How Young uses it: Twain is her guide through the Gilded Age. Here he is still Sam Clemens, a young man going west for silver, the gold rush dream in its Nevada form.

  • Further reading: Ron Powers, Mark Twain: A Life (2005), chapters on Nevada.

Shorter entries

  • Oliver Cowdery (pp. 131-138): A schoolteacher who became Smith's main scribe. He was excommunicated in 1838, practiced law, and returned to the church in 1848, two years before his death.

  • Kirtland, Ohio (pp. 136-138): The Mormon headquarters from 1831 to 1838. Its temple was dedicated in 1836, when Smith and Cowdery reported visions of Moses, Elias, and Elijah (pp. 137-138).

  • Nephites and Lamanites (pp. 133, 139): Peoples of the Book of Mormon, descendants of Israelites who sailed to America. The Lamanites were taught to be ancestors of the American Indians, which shaped Mormon missions to Native peoples.

  • The Salt Lake House (pp. 141-142): The city's main hotel in 1861, where the Clemens brothers stayed.

  • The Tithing House (pp. 142, 150): The church storehouse where members paid their tenth in goods, the core of the Mormon cooperative economy.

  • Anson Call (p. 144): A Mormon pioneer and colonizer. Young retells a family legend of Brigham Young by his fireside.

  • Polygamy (pp. 134, 143-152): The church publicly acknowledged plural marriage in 1852. Federal pressure, including the Edmunds Act of 1882, forced its official end in 1890, and Utah became a state in 1896.

Allusions

  • The Golden Calf (pp. 126-127): Exodus 32, the idol Aaron made while Moses was on the mountain.

  • Mot and Anat (p. 127): Canaanite gods of death and war, from the Ugaritic myths.

  • Klopstock's The Messiah (p. 126): The German religious epic of 1748-1773.

  • Golconda (p. 127): An Indian city famous for diamonds, meaning any source of great wealth.

  • Aladdin (p. 122): From The Thousand and One Nights, the boy with the magic lamp.

  • Haman (p. 152): The villain of the Book of Esther.
