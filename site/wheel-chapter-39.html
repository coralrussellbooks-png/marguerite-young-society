Research notes: Chapter 39 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 39

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Mr. Res Tacamah

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

This pillar is the structural spine of the entire chapter. The mother does not have a self; she is a proliferation of selves that cannot be gathered into one.

"She was everybody and the rose petal, too, the cause of this dissolution which was this creation, and she was the street lamp beaming through the fog like one great pearl."

"She was Brooklyn Bridge, dissolving when a rose petal fell on Brooklyn Bridge, dissolving at a single footstep."

"She was the divan, the mirror, the lamp, the rug, all of which might suddenly move away in the evening light."

The mother does not merely imagine herself as other things; she ontologically disperses into them. She is simultaneously the object, the observer, and the dissolution. The text does not present this as madness to be cured. It presents it as the condition of her being.

Crucially, the text asks the philosophical question this pillar demands:

"Which of many selves should she have knowledge of, and how should she know which self of selves was true, reigning supreme?"

The question is never answered. It is not meant to be. The irresolvability is the point. And the text goes further; it suggests that the unified, "true" self would be worse than the plural one:

"Should she be able or willing, even if she knew, to give up all the beautiful false selves for the sake of one who was true and, in all probability, quite dull and void of thought, a dreamless idiot bereft of sense."

This is a profound philosophical move. The plural self is not a wound. It is the site of richness. The singular self is the impoverishment.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The mother knows she produces illusions. She says so explicitly. And yet the text refuses to place "real reality" on the other side of her illusions as a corrective standard.

"Except for her most demented moments, however, my mother knew only too well that she was capable of producing fantasies, altering the appearances so that she should see what others did not see."

But then the text immediately pivots: her argument is that everyone is equally drugged:

"It would be her argument, at this point, that everyone else was just as drugged as she was, perhaps more so, others perhaps not knowing that they were drugged."

This is not a defense mechanism. It is a philosophical position: the person who knows they are constructing reality is epistemically more honest than the person who believes they are simply perceiving it. The mother's opium is visible. Everyone else's opium (convention, habit, social consensus) is invisible, and therefore more dangerous.

The cave metaphor from Plato is here inverted. There is no Form of the Good outside. There is only:

"A scheme of mirages, even for those who lived."

The word "even" is doing enormous work. Even those who are supposedly awake, supposedly living in the real; they too inhabit mirages. The mother is not the exception. She is the honest case.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The chapter is saturated with loss, and crucially, no loss is resolved. The grief is not sequential. It is atmospheric. It is the medium through which everything moves.

"So my mother had felt, then as now, as she had felt from the earliest times...her isolation, her distance from life."

The phrase "then as now, as she had felt from the earliest times" collapses temporal sequence. The grief is not something that happened and is being processed. It is the permanent weather of her interior world; it was there before the losses that might explain it, and it persists after them.

The dead are not mourned and released. They accumulate:

"She had known several murdered archdukes in several centuries and many great khans who were sleeping in their satin beds and would not awaken again."

The dead of multiple centuries press upon her simultaneously. This is grief as sedimentary accumulation (which connects to Pillar 8), not grief as a wound that heals. And the suffrage captain, Cousin Freemount-Snowden, is mourned not through tears but through the impossibility of her absence being real:

"Should she fade? Was she not herself the phantom, a creature of the immortal night?"

To mourn the dead by insisting they cannot truly die is not denial; it is the honest acknowledgment that grief does not permit clean endings.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator, the "I" of this text, performs this pillar structurally. The chapter does not diagnose the mother, does not conclude about her, does not resolve her into a case study. It stays in the room with her, following every corridor of her consciousness without rushing toward a verdict.

The narrator witnesses the mother witnessing herself:

"My mother knew that she was helpless and bereft, even though she seemed so independent, this alienated and this jeweled corpse who should dazzle every stranger with her prismatic wit."

The phrase "jeweled corpse" is both devastating and precise. It does not simplify her into either victim or eccentric. It holds both her brilliance and her deadness in the same phrase. This is the act of the witness: the refusal to resolve the tension.

The text also refuses to simplify Cousin Freemount-Snowden:

"She had seemed a person not quite of this modern world in which all romance had already been destroyed by telephones and Western Union messengers."

The suffragist who fought against romance is herself the object of romance in the mother's imagination. The text does not correct this irony. It witnesses it. The complexity is the truth.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The mother's soul is explicitly described as a structure that is always under construction and always recursive:

"My mother's soul was like a many-spiraling house with many rooms, many cubits adding to dazzling cubits, many stairways leading to many heavens."

"She could never seem to escape from this house which continually extended like the idea of infinity."

The house does not reach completion. It extends, or it diminishes, but it never arrives. And crucially, this is not presented as failure:

"Every room being but the partial reflection of every other."

Each room is built from the partial reflection of what came before. This is recursive construction; each new space is made from the materials of the previous space, imperfectly mirrored. The mother moves through these rooms not toward an exit but as an ongoing act of inhabitation. The building is the life.

The narrator's own prose performs this: the sentences spiral back, repeat with variation, add new clauses to old ones. The form enacts the content.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

This pillar is perhaps most visible in the chapter's treatment of the suffrage cause and of the mother's social life; both of which are incomplete, and both of which the text refuses to judge as failures.

Cousin Freemount-Snowden's cause was unfinished:

"She had fought against the dragons of despair...She had slain the minotaur sleeping in the drowned garden."

But she died before full victory. The text does not treat this as tragedy in the conventional sense. It treats incompletion as the nature of the knight errant's existence: "Her boots were in the stirrups, and her long-maned horse rolled in the flood, and she must ride, ride, ride to that victory which would be her own extinction."

Victory and extinction are the same thing. To complete the quest is to end. The riding, the incompletion, is the life. To demand completion is to demand death.

The mother's own memoirs are explicitly incomplete and multiplied:

"Her charmed personal memoirs, one for the living and one for the dead and one for a murdered archduke."

There is no single, finished memoir. There are multiple, partial ones. This is not failure of authorship; it is the honest form that a plural self's life must take.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

The mother exists at the intersection of every binary the social world uses to organize experience, and her existence at those intersections reveals the binaries as constructions.

She is simultaneously:

  • Social hostess and recluse
  • Traveller and immured
  • Living and dead
  • Sane (she knows she is insane) and insane
  • Present and absent

"She was this great social hostess entertaining so many guests...and at the same time, she was this recluse with the stained cheek, hidden away in this dark, many-turreted house—never travelling, yet always travelling."

She does not occupy one side of these categories. She occupies the edge, and her occupation of the edge makes the categories themselves visible as constructions. If she were simply mad or simply sane, the category would hold. Because she is both, the category dissolves.

Cousin Freemount-Snowden is similarly marginal: a woman who fought against the romantic construction of womanhood while herself becoming the object of romantic imagination. She is the evidence that the category "rational woman vs. romantic woman" is itself a construction.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This is perhaps the pillar most literally enacted in the chapter's treatment of time.

"The present moment was already freighted with the burden of the past, fusions of other moments, faces like old town clocks seen through a blowing mist, each clock striking a different deranged hour."

The present is not a clean moment. It is "freighted": weighted down by accumulated past. The clocks strike different hours simultaneously because the past does not recede to a single point. It layers, accumulates, presses up through the present in multiple registers at once.

The mother lives in multiple centuries simultaneously:

"She had known several murdered archdukes in several centuries."

"She had been seen waving to herself in passing, or she had made banal remarks to the beautiful lady who, under her drooping ostrich plumes, nodded and smiled from the other carriage, seeming to recognize her, though this lady had lived and died in the twelfth century."

The twelfth century is not behind her. It is beneath her, pressing up. The dead woman in the carriage is not a hallucination in the pejorative sense; she is the sediment of history made visible by the mother's particular permeability to time's accumulation.

Even the Phoenicians, the Gloucester fishermen, the seal boats from Labrador; they are all simultaneously present, expected, imminent:

"She knew when the Phoenicians were about to sail...She knew when to expect the Gloucester fishermen who had gone down a hundred years ago, wrapped in their torn sails...She knew the hour of the charioteers."

This is sedimentary time made into a mode of perception.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter explicitly thematizes the instability of the first-person pronoun: the most basic unit of linguistic identity:

"It was as if the perpendicular pronoun I had come to see my horizontal mother."

"The personal pronoun I did not always mean, of course, the same being or thing to my mother."

This is a direct philosophical statement about language as construction. The pronoun "I" does not name a stable referent. It names a position, and the position shifts. The perpendicular I (upright, active, visiting) meets the horizontal I (recumbent, passive, receiving). They are grammatically identical but ontologically different.

The mother's relationship to the pronoun enacts the instability:

"How often, eating an apple, had she said—I am eating myself? How often had she said—I am a cloud eating the moon—I am eating an hour-glass—I am eating a door knob?"

The "I" here is not a stable subject performing an action on an object. The subject and object collapse into each other. Language, which is supposed to maintain the subject/object distinction, here reveals that distinction as its own construction.

The text's prose style itself performs this: the sentences are extraordinarily long, recursive, self-modifying. They do not arrive at clean conclusions. They enact the instability of language to fix meaning.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The chapter refuses to locate transcendence outside the world. The sacred is not in heaven or in a rational utopia. It is in the fog, the drug bottle, the ringing of fog bells, the porcelain genie.

"Mr. Res Tacamah, the author of so many fantasies...that drug bottle which contained the purple fog and the glowering genii, spirits of fire or air, sparks upon a far horizon, gleamings of distant cities upon the moving waves, stars, clocks, faces."

The drug bottle is a vessel of the sacred. It contains spirits, gleamings, stars. This is not metaphor in the decorative sense; it is ontological. The transcendent is immanent in the material object.

Similarly, the mother's dreams are not escapes from reality into a transcendent elsewhere. They are deeper penetrations into the immanent:

"Should she even hear the secretive ocean crying or know so many of the ocean's unrevealed secrets, the secrets of the living and of the dead scarcely to be distinguished from each other?"

The ocean's secrets are not supernatural. They are the secrets of the immanent world, of the living and the dead who share the same ground. The sacred is the depth of the ordinary, not its transcendence.

Even God is immanent here, dreaming inside the world rather than above it:

"Was not God dreaming, too, dreaming these opium dreams, so who could be right if God was dreaming?"

God is not the external guarantor of truth. God is another dreamer inside the dream. The sacred is fully immanent; it does not stand outside to correct or redeem.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is the deepest pillar, and the chapter enacts it most profoundly in its treatment of Cousin Freemount-Snowden and the mother's relationship to her.

The suffrage captain is dead. But she is not gone. She constituted the mother: her visits, her cause, her presence shaped the mother's imagination and self-understanding. The mother is, in part, the sediment left by Freemount-Snowden's passage:

"Cousin Hannah, and might God rest her soul, had been determined, indomitable in every way, never recreant from her great cause when she was still alive."

And the mother, in her horizontal dreaming, is constituting something for the narrator: the "I" who tells this story, who is the not-yet-born standing on the ground the mother deposited.

The crossing point is most explicit here:

"The dead were those who travelled fastest."

The dead do not stop. They travel faster; they move through time more efficiently because they are no longer constrained by the present moment. They are pure sediment, pure constitution of the future.

And the chapter's final meditation on God dreaming connects to this structural obligation:

"Should there be no dream—which was another way of saying—should there be no reality—for were they not commingled and perhaps the same?"

The dream and the reality are the same because both are constitutive acts. To dream is to lay down sediment for what comes after. The mother's dreams are not private escapes; they are the ground being laid for the narrator, for us, for whoever reads this. The obligation is not chosen. It is structural.
