Pillar: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End · Across every book

Or, Why Refusing to "Get Over It" Disrupts the System

We are constantly told that healing means moving on. Marguerite Young says grief is our permanent weather, and carrying it honestly is a profound act of political resistance.

We live in a society that treats grief like a temporary illness. We are told there is a neat little roadmap with stages: you deny it, you get angry, you bargain, you feel depressed, and finally, you arrive at "acceptance". The goal is always to get through it so your life can resume its forward motion. We tend to view grief as a passing storm, and if someone is still mourning years later, mainstream culture often worries that they are stuck, broken, or failing to heal.

But Marguerite Young says this story we tell about grief is a false comfort. In her system, grief isn't a temporary season: it is the climate.

The Climate of Loss

Young argues that grief is the permanent atmospheric condition of our world. Why? Because we live in a reality where everything we love is always already changing, the people we need are absent, and nothing holds still.

Young doesn't see this accumulation as a sickness. She sees it as the only honest response to an irreplaceable loss. To fully "get over" something means deciding it was replaceable, which Young finds dishonest. Grief doesn't diminish you: it gives you depth and education that you cannot acquire any other way.

Disrupting the Forced Performance of Recovery

So, how does carrying our sorrow permanently push back against authoritarian control?

Authoritarian systems pull off a massive "long con" by demanding a clean, shiny narrative of national triumph, quick recovery, and constant progress. Dictatorships love to hide the structural violence their power is built upon: the historical theft of land, centuries of exploitation, and the systematic exclusion of human beings. To keep the illusion going, the system forces a performance of recovery. It asks its most damaged people to demonstrate resilience, move on, and act like everything has been resolved.

When we practice honest grief, we directly jam that propaganda machine.

We refuse to look away: By staying in the room with our losses, we refuse to replace our genuine pain with a shallow performance of recovery.

We keep history present: Honest grief tells us that the past isn't safely behind us, but right beneath our feet, forcing us to reckon with the historical layers that still press down on the present landscape.

We demand public honesty: A politics built on this pillar avoids the fantasy of an ideological arrival, opting instead to build structures for ongoing, collective acknowledgment of what has been broken.

By refusing to wrap up our sorrow in a tidy political bow, we remain anchored in what is real, rendering us immune to the false promises of leaders who try to sweep historical harms under the rug.

The Weight of Refusal: The Madres de Plaza de Mayo

There are moments in history when the act of continuing to grieve becomes the most dangerous form of resistance a person can offer.

During Argentina's military dictatorship from 1976 to 1983, approximately 30,000 people disappeared. They were not executed publicly, where their deaths could be witnessed and mourned. They were taken from their families, their fates unknown. The regime's strategy was deliberate: create uncertainty, deny everything, make the disappeared unspeakable. Then, demand that the families move on.

The mothers of these disappeared people refused.

Every week, the Madres de Plaza de Mayo gathered in the plaza in front of the presidential palace in Buenos Aires. They wore white headscarves embroidered with the names of their disappeared children. They did not perform acceptance or recovery. They held photographs. They called out names. They stood in public, in their grief, refusing the regime's demand that they forget, move on, and let the violence become invisible.

The regime hated this. They tried to scatter the mothers with violence. They tried to discredit them as hysterical and stuck. They tried to offer them compensation, as if money could replace a child or fill the space where a human being used to be. The official message was clear: healing means moving on. Get over it. Stop making trouble.

But the mothers understood something that Young teaches: grief that refuses to resolve is not a personal failure. It is a form of testimony. It is a way of saying: this loss is permanent. This person mattered. This violence was real. We will not pretend it didn't happen so you can feel comfortable. We will not replace our sorrow with a false narrative of recovery. We will stand here, week after week, in our unhealed grief, and we will make it impossible for the world to forget.

Their refusal to move on, to perform recovery, to treat their children as replaceable by time or compensation, became the most powerful form of resistance to the dictatorship. Because they would not allow the violence to become history, it remained news. It remained real. Their permanent grief forced everyone around them to reckon with what had been taken.

After the dictatorship fell, many of the disappeared were never found. The grief did not resolve into closure. The mothers continued to gather. Decades later, they are still there. This is not a failure of healing. This is integrity. This is the refusal to lie about what has been lost.

Young would recognize them immediately. They understand that grief is not a problem to be solved but a permanent condition of honoring what was irreplaceable. Their refusal to get over it is what kept the disappeared from becoming truly gone. It kept the violence from disappearing into official forgetting.

The Deeper Work

Honest grief does not mean wallowing or being stuck in despair. It means acknowledging that what was lost cannot be replaced, and that carrying that knowledge changes you forever. A society built on this pillar does not pretend to quick fixes. It does not ask its most damaged people to perform resilience for the comfort of those in power. It builds institutions for ongoing acknowledgment. It holds space for permanent sorrow alongside the work of continuing to live.

This is the grief Young writes into her work. The characters in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling do not recover from their losses. They carry them. They integrate them. They learn to live in a world that will never be whole again. And from that integration, something real becomes possible: not false hope, but actual clarity about what matters and what does not.

The Practice

Give yourself permission to stop trying to "fix" your old sadness. If a wave of grief for a person, a past version of yourself, or a collective loss hits you, do not treat it like a step backward. Let it be the weather. Remind yourself that carrying that sorrow honestly is not a weakness: it is a form of collective integrity.

Notice when you are pressured to perform recovery: by your workplace, your family, your social circle, or the larger culture. Notice the subtle message that moving on is what mature, healthy people do. Pause, and ask yourself: is this my grief asking to be put away, or is someone else asking me to put it away for their comfort?

If it is the latter, you can refuse. You can hold your sorrow openly. You can be the person who remembers, who speaks the name, who refuses to let what was lost become invisible. That is not pathology. That is love. That is resistance.
