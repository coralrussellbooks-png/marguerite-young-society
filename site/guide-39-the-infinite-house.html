Research notes: Chapter 39

Themes: Faith, God, and the Supernatural · Madness and Sanity · Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos · The Search for Meaning

There is a sentence in Chapter 39 that stops the novel in its tracks (gently, the way a hand on a shoulder stops you, not violently) and asks you to see exactly what you are looking at.

It is “as if the perpendicular pronoun I had come to see my horizontal mother.”

Read it again. The perpendicular I. The horizontal mother. Vera, upright, moving through the world under her own locomotion. Catherine, flat, having surrendered the vertical axis entirely. Two orientations, two modes of being, meeting across the impossible geometry of love and need and the fact that one of them gave birth to the other and neither of them can fully reach across.

Chapter 39 is a portrait of Catherine's interior: her consciousness mapped as architecture, her madness examined with the patient precision of someone who has lived inside it long enough to give it a floor plan. It is one of the most sustained and searching portraits of a mind in the novel. Settle in.

What actually happens in Chapter 39

Vera describes how her mother thinks, and why. Catherine receives visitors, or imagines she receives them: Cousin Hannah's ghost, Lord Murdock Burdock Thrushmorton (whose name alone tells you the register Young is operating in), Mr. Res Tacamah, who controls his madness by dreaming rather than acting. Real visitors, imagined visitors, symbolic visitors: Catherine does not always distinguish between them, and Young does not always tell you which is which.

The chapter is also, quietly, about opium. Catherine acknowledges, with the clarity that is only available to someone deep inside a thing, that the drug has “poisoned all pleasures at their source.” She does not say this with the guilt of someone who intends to stop. She says it as a description. This is what happened. This is the swirling fog. This is how the stars got moved out of their stations.

The chapter ends at the shoreline: sea meeting land, the dead meeting the living, the fog as the habitat of absent-minded persons who walk “with their eyes hidden behind their hands.” And then a final question, one that the novel has been circling since its first page: if everything is a dream, is God dreaming too? Is religion itself the longest opium dream of all?

The infinite house

Young gives Catherine's soul the shape of a house. Not a modest house: an infinite one. Many rooms, many stairways, many heavens. Each room contains a different version of Catherine, or a different guest, or a missing element she has been looking for in all the other rooms. The floors waver like water. The walls seem to move. She has been walking through this house for decades and has not found the exit, and may not be looking for one.

This is one of the novel's great metaphors, and it rewards attention. The infinite house is not a prison exactly: Catherine is not suffering in any simple sense. She is genuinely at home in this architecture. It is the only space she knows. But it is a space from which she cannot leave, and a space in which she cannot be reliably found by anyone standing outside it.

Vera is outside it. Vera has always been outside it. The perpendicular I visits the horizontal mother in the house of the horizontal mother's mind, and they speak through the wall between their orientations, and something passes between them, and it is not nothing. But it is not the same as being in the same room.

Sanity's strange definition

Watch for the moment when Catherine offers her definition of her own sanity: her “sanity was simply this—she knew she was insane.”

This is not irony, though it sounds like it. Young means it as a genuine philosophical position, and it echoes a long tradition of thinking about madness and self-knowledge. The person who cannot see their own condition is more thoroughly lost than the person who can name it. Catherine names hers. She has given it a shape, a floor plan, a set of recurring guests. She knows the dragons in her labyrinth by name. This is a form of mastery, even if it is not a form of escape.

Troubles are cyclical, Catherine observes. They appear in different guises (dragons turning into saints, saints turning back into dragons) but the cycle itself is stable. She has learned not to be surprised. This is cold comfort, but it is comfort.

The novel is doing something careful here about the relationship between self-knowledge and cure. Catherine knows herself extraordinarily well, in the particular, partial, introspective way that opium and decades of horizontal time allow. This knowledge has not saved her. Young does not suggest it should have. Self-knowledge in this novel is not a therapeutic tool. It is a form of company: the self keeping itself company in the infinite house, learning the contours of its own labyrinth.

The multiple selves, and the cost of choosing

Catherine contemplates, in Chapter 39, the possibility of selecting one of her many selves and becoming it fully. She considers this and rejects it. To choose one self would mean losing all the fascinating false selves that have sustained her: the great courtesan, the avant-garde traveler, the woman who might have gone to Egypt, to Italy, to the land of the Kurds, to any of the other destinations her imagination has furnished with such care.

Young is working here with William James, whom she named as a major influence. James wrote that the self is not a single thing but a stream: a continuous flow of consciousness that contains many potential selves, many versions of the person we “might be or might have been.” To crystallize into one self is to foreclose the others. Catherine has refused this foreclosure. She has chosen multiplicity over coherence, and the cost is a self that cannot be pinned down, cannot be reliably located, cannot be found in the same room twice.

There is something Young admires in this, even as she shows its costs. To remain multiple is to remain open. Catherine's infinite house has more rooms than a life devoted to a single, coherent self would ever build. Young, who spent decades writing a novel as vast and various as a city, knew something about this refusal herself.

Opium and religion, religion and opium

The chapter arrives, near its end, at one of the novel's most daring juxtapositions: opium as religion, religion as opium.

Marx said it plainly: religion is the opium of the people, a drug that dulls the pain of social reality and makes the unbearable bearable, at the cost of preventing the sufferer from addressing the causes of their suffering. Young takes this analogy and turns it inside out. If religion is opium, then opium is religion: it offers transcendence, visions, a world of angels and ethereal light, an extension of experience beyond the physical. Catherine's drug-induced paradise is furnished with the same materials as any heaven.

But Young complicates both the opium and the religion. She does not simply condemn Catherine's illusions as escapism. She shows that for a person in Catherine's condition (caught in the labyrinth, unable to stand, surrounded by loss) the illusions may be what is keeping her alive. The fog is suffocating and it is also breath. The dream is a prison and it is also the only room with a window.

Miss MacIntosh's critique hovers over this passage like a weather front: face reality, face the bare face of things, refuse the comfortable lie. But Miss MacIntosh is gone. And Catherine is still here, in the infinite house, dreaming. Young leaves the argument open. She does not award the point to either position.

The visitors: real and assembled

Lord Murdock Burdock Thrushmorton and Mr. Res Tacamah deserve a moment of attention. These are not minor characters in the conventional sense; they are more like ideas Catherine has personified, given names and social manners, invited in for tea.

Lord Murdock Burdock Thrushmorton twitters like a cage of canaries. His name is a Gothic comedy: the kind of name that announces itself as excessive, as too much, as something assembled from parts that don't quite cohere. He represents the chaotic and the absurd in Catherine's world: the element that cannot be reasoned with or predicted, that arrives without warning and fills the room with incomprehensible sound.

Mr. Res Tacamah controls his madness by dreaming rather than acting, which is to say, he has chosen Catherine's strategy, the strategy of the infinite house. His drug bottle is Catherine's drug bottle. He is a self-portrait she has distributed into someone else's name, given a separate residence in the house so she can look at herself without looking.

Young learned this technique in the classroom. The note in To All My Darlings points to it directly: she would say, 'Oh, come in,' to every idea that arrived. Every voice got a chair. Every contradiction got a name and a face and a place at the table. This is how the novel is built. This is how Catherine's mind is built. The guests are ideas, the house is a philosophy, and the hostess has been receiving visitors for longer than anyone can remember.

The shore, the fog, and the question God leaves open

The chapter closes at the water's edge, which is where Young returns when she needs to mark a threshold. Sea meeting shore: the dead meeting the living, the known meeting the unknown, the boundary that moves with every wave and can never be fixed.

The fog, Young tells us, is where absent-minded persons walk “with their eyes hidden behind their hands.” Catherine is one of them. Perhaps all of us, at certain hours, are. The fog is not only fog; it is the condition of being in between, of not being able to see clearly ahead or behind, of moving through a space where the usual landmarks have dissolved.

And then the final question: if everything is a dream, is God dreaming too? Is the universe itself a hallucination generated by a consciousness larger than any individual one, and if so, does that make it more real or less?

Young does not answer. She is not being coy. She genuinely believes the question should stay open: that to close it would be to commit the error Catherine's opium commits: to insist on a particular vision of the architecture when the architecture keeps shifting. The fog has its own kind of truth. The absent-minded walkers with their eyes behind their hands are seeing something, even if it is not what the upright, perpendicular world sees.

What to hold onto

Chapter 39 is a chapter about a woman who built a world inside her mind because the outside world was not sufficient: was too thin, too narrow, too insistent on coherence. The inside world is vast and labyrinthine and costs her everything. And she has chosen it anyway.

You don't have to agree with her choice to understand it. You have to understand that it is a choice, made by a person with intelligence and imagination and a history that drove her here, and that it has its own dignity alongside its costs.

Vera stands perpendicular at the door of the infinite house, watching. She has been standing there her whole life. She came here looking for Miss MacIntosh, who once stood in this house and insisted it face reality, and was eventually swallowed by the same fog she tried to dispel. The door is still open. The mother is still horizontal. The house has no exit that anyone has found.
