The Studs Terkel Interview, 1965 (two-part radio program)

Part of Young's Interviews Analyzed, a study of what Marguerite Young (1908 to 1995) said in interviews about her work and life.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Young's Interviews Analyzed · Next →

A reading for the Miss MacIntosh project.

The interview at a glance

The host is not named in the transcript, but external records from the Marguerite Young archive confirm this is the 1965 Studs Terkel radio interview, recorded around the publication of Miss MacIntosh by Scribner's. It is the longest interview in the set and a distinctive one. Terkel runs it as a two-part musical program, weaving folk songs, hymns, and blues through the talk, and it plays almost as a duet: he keeps intuiting her "secret key images," the prospector, the bottomless pitcher, the roulette wheel, until she says he has named "three of the secret key images by which I wrote." Young performs the book, reading long passages aloud, so this is the source that best preserves her voice on the page. As in some of the other home recordings, a few stray asides to a pet intrude and are set aside here. Two framing points stand out. Young insists the book is "not a stream of consciousness novel" but "a very controlled, rather realistic treatment," and she explains that she chose a bus over a train because a bus "does not go on a rail" and can be "erratic," echoing William James, who preferred "the stream of consciousness" to "the train of memory." Read beside the 1965 Duncan MacDonald interview, this is the second publication-moment source, and the richest.

Where the book came from

This interview is unusually generous with origins, and revealingly it gives several. Young says she began writing about the rich for a plain reason: she was in "an apartment which did not have any heat," so "I'll write about very rich people so that I won't notice I was living in a cold apartment," and "wrote about the richest people I could think of." She then names a real court case as the true germ, "a famous court case" in which "a rich girl who declared publicly that she loved her nursemaid more than her mother," rejecting "the fabulous rich mother," so that, she says, "that is actually where the idea of the book came from." Later she names another seed entirely, a senile doctor she knew in Iowa City who kept delivering imaginary babies, "the idea of the senile doctor still carrying on his practice was one of the things which started me on the book." The multiplicity matters for the project: across the interviews Young offers the opium lady, a childhood poem, this court case, and this doctor as points of origin, and the Terkel interview alone holds three of them.

Reality checked, images filed, the book as experience

Young restates that "everything in the whole book came from observation of life, or from a reading of newspapers, or reading of biography," with "not a single invented episode or character," because "I distrusted pure imagination," a habit she traces to reading at "about 14" that Dickens studied shorthand to be a reporter, so "I instantly took shorthand." She checks her inventions against real people, as when she asked a hairdresser whether a bald woman ever came in and got the retort she gave a barber in the book, "I couldn't grow one hair on your head for a million dollars, and if I could, I wouldn't need a million dollars." Her aesthetic is stated plainly against the fashionable one. There are "putter-inners, as the Wolf said, and the taker-outers, as Scott Fitzgerald said," and Young defends putting in: "it takes terrible discipline in architectonics to put it in," managed by "a file-case mentality" in which every image is assigned its place for a psychological reason. The aim was not a "tight novel" but, in the words of the editor Margaret Cousins that Young embraced, "this book is not a book, it's an experience," "a searchlight upon experience." She read Jung "to give me courage along the way," and reports a University of Iowa researcher who measured brain waves telling her that her prose spent "as much energy in a morning as if you had heaved a ton of coal," which so horrified her that she "didn't write for at least three days."

The dragnet sentence

The clearest craft statement in any of these sources is Young's defense of the long sentence, delivered here as she reads the enormous catalog describing the pregnant girl on the bus. She uses long sentences, after Proust, Joyce, and Sterne, because a single sentence "gives an emotional unity to the description" and forces the writer to "catch every nuance of your own thought," where a short sentence catches "maybe a tenth of what you think." She calls it "the dragnet sentence," "like casting your seine upon the waters," and tells young writers that "if you can write a long sentence, you can write a short one." She invokes William James, that "we can never catch all our thoughts on the wing," and offers the long sentence as the nearest approach. She also loves the short sentence, "like ripples coming in after a storm," and believes finally in "variation of sentence length depending upon the emotional mood." She reports that Elizabeth Bowen, reading the manuscript, said "I knew on page two that it was a great book because of that sentence," and "burst into tears," thinking "here's a new voice."

Guilt and the albatross

When Terkel guesses that the opium lady's hidden birthmark is "the albatross on the neck of the mariner," Young answers, "you frighten me by being so absolutely right," and states a thesis she gives nowhere else so directly: "the secret heart of my novel is the albatross and guilt." Opium, through Coleridge, is "guilt and turning away," and the opium lady faces Coleridge's own final choice, to be "cured of opium and die or continue with opium and die," so that "like Coleridge, she comes back to reality as she dies," aging into "an old hag" at the end. The guilt theme runs through the book's Calvinism, which Young ties to Melville. "Moby Dick is my favorite of all American novels," she says, noting that she lives on Bleecker Street "in what is probably the exact site of his birthplace," and she accepts that her novel, like his, is "anti-Calvinist." The purest carrier of guilt is the Christian hangman, Mr. Weed, who married into a family in which "the Olivers always hang themselves," and who paused to put on his galoshes on the night his wife hanged herself, so that Young leaves open "was he a murderer because he put on his galoshes," "a man absolutely ruled by habit."

Doubles and the missing sense

Young gives her fullest account of Mr. Spitzer, the composer who has written no audible music since his gambler twin's suicide and who, at the end, tries to confess to the dying opium lady that he is really the gambler she loved, "like the Titchborne claimant," only for her to die "at the beginning of his confessions." The key was a discovery in the writing: "about the third draft" she realized this was "not just a case of twins, but the two parts of the same man," a "duality which might be in all of us, like left and right, shadow and sunlight, or illusion and reality," and that Spitzer becomes "the archetypal wandering Jew who cannot die." The missing-sense theme, which she treats as the book's "great obsession," is grounded in real research: to write the deaf painter Vera marries, Young wrote "a long article for a magazine called The World of Silence," interviewing "all the deaf leaders of New York," hearing "the Lord's Prayer in sign language" at St. Mark's, and meeting "the only deaf dentist in New York," who "couldn't hear the screams of the patients." She draws from it a social point that connects to her sense of the book as political: the deaf apologized to her for her being "in the minority" as a hearing person, and she links this to an affinity among people set apart, "the blind, the deaf, the crippled," and "the Negro," who help one another. Her one real invention here, she jokes, would seem to be the blind veteran touring Europe's galleries with a guide dog, except that she met exactly such a man "in the hotel lobby in Rome."

The women who dreamed

Two women carry the book's theme of the dreamed life, and both get fuller treatment here than elsewhere. Esther Longtree, the waitress "pregnant now for 21 months," embodies "dream pregnancies, which I wrote long before Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf," a coincidence that produced a warm exchange with Albee, who, hearing that the Herald Tribune had titled her review "Who's Afraid of Marcel Proust," said his next play "will be called Who's Afraid of Both of Them." Young calls Esther "the mule woman," inspired by Chaplin's "The Gold Rush," where a mule arrives for dinner and Chaplin plays the gracious host, and by a Chagall painting of a pregnant mule-faced bride Young saw only after writing, "a kind of folk dream" that is "universal." Beneath the comedy is a dark core: Esther, "a song of songs of schizophrenia" and yet "the mother of us all," has in fact killed "the only real child which was ever hers," a mixed-race baby left "in a drawer in a hotel room in the deep south," who returns as an eight-year-old conscience asking "mother, why did you kill me," a figure Young ties to the Child ballad of "the cruel mother." The other is the suffragette, Cousin Hannah, drawn from stories told by the NAACP founder Charles Studin about a "battle-axe" who fought men all her life, "one of the original bloomer girls," yet brought home sealed trunks for forty years that proved at her death to hold "a complete bridal trousseau" each, "she had a dream too." Young fuses her with a mountaineering story in which two bloomer girls shed their skirts at an Alpine summit and the younger, going back for hers, is lost, "the lady lost her life for a skirt," and with Margaret Fuller, "who drowned off Fire Island" within a step of a sandbar, perhaps by a suicidal wish, returning to America with a husband "to whom she was not really married."

Dreams need no passport

Terkel keeps steering toward the Cold War fear of annihilation, and Young answers with the book's largest claim. Her narrator projects herself into every stranger she meets until "she is all of them," which Young calls "a Walt Whitman theme, the one and the many," and, as in the later interviews, "a sad Walt Whitman." She grounds it in Dostoevsky's image of lifting the rooftops off Moscow at midnight to find that the sleepers, however divided by "reason, custom, social class," are "all dreaming the same dreams," so that "we all dream of mother, maze, child, water. Our dreams bind us, not our reasons." She adds the Fiji belief that the soul is a firefly and concludes that "the dream needs no passport." It is the political vision she names elsewhere as the book's hidden argument, here stated as brotherhood reached through dream rather than through "treaties and borderlines," against a present she and Terkel agree is threatened with being "blown to pieces."

Set against the other interviews

As the second 1965 source, this interview confirms the publication-moment story of the Duncan MacDonald talk, the roughly two-hundred-page intention, the last scene known from the start, Scribner's faith in a long novel, the sea-walk and the suicide on page six, while adding far more texture. It reinforces the "17 years and 8 months" figure, here with the detail that "the last five months were spent simply typing," and the Joyce anecdote in which Joyce's sister tells Young she "just missed my dear brother's record by four months." It repeats the Woolf walking-stick source, now attributed to Alan Pryce-Jones, and the black-gloves-as-guilt reading with the Long Island suicide that followed her writing. Two things are worth carrying forward in particular. First, Young's explicit rejection of the "stream of consciousness" label in favor of a "controlled, rather realistic" book, which cuts against the common description of her work. Second, the sheer number of origin stories she tells, the cold apartment, the poor-little-rich-girl court case, the senile doctor, alongside the opium lady and the childhood poems of other interviews, which suggests that Young thought of the book as having many seeds rather than one, and told whichever fit the moment. The interview also names early champions worth noting for the record, Elizabeth Bowen and Margaret Cousins, and her first Scribner's editor Maxwell Perkins, Thomas Wolfe's editor, which places her briefly in that lineage.
