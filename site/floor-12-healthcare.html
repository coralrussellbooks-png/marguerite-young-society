The Right to a Body That Is Cared For

A Companion Document to the Constitution

The Philosophical Foundation

Healthcare in the United States is organized around the extraction of profit from human need. The person who is sick needs care. The system that provides care charges for it. The person who cannot pay does not receive the care, or receives it in a form that leaves them with debt that compounds their circumstances, or delays receiving it until the condition is more serious and more expensive to address. This is not a neutral market outcome. It is a political choice: the decision to organize the provision of care around the profit motive rather than around the needs of people who are sick.

The belief that the market is the appropriate mechanism for providing healthcare is presented as economic law rather than political choice. The market price of insulin does not reflect the cost of producing insulin. It reflects the negotiating power of the pharmaceutical company relative to the patient who will die without it. The market price of a hospital stay does not reflect the cost of the care provided. It reflects the billing codes, the insurance contracts, the administrative architecture built around extracting maximum revenue from the encounter between a sick person and the system that can help them. This is a construction. It was built. It can be rebuilt.

The second foundational principle is that the body is not a singular acceptable body. It is plural: it has a history, it carries what it has been given, it presents differently in different people, it responds differently to the same interventions, it has needs that do not fit diagnostic categories built from studies of the median white male patient. The history of medical research is the history of whose body was studied and whose was not, whose symptoms were taken seriously and whose were attributed to hysteria or drug-seeking or non-compliance, whose pain was treated and whose was dismissed. The healthcare system has not been built to receive the full range of human bodies and experiences. This document describes what it would look like if it were.

Universal Healthcare

The Case

Every wealthy democracy except the United States provides universal healthcare coverage to its citizens. The outcomes in those countries, across virtually every measure of population health, life expectancy, infant mortality, chronic disease management, preventable death rates, are better than the outcomes in the United States, at lower per capita cost. The United States spends more on healthcare than any other country in the world and achieves worse outcomes than countries that spend significantly less. This is not a mystery. It is the predictable result of a system organized around profit extraction rather than health outcomes.

The administrative cost of the American healthcare system is itself a substantial fraction of the total cost: the billing departments, the insurance claims processors, the prior authorization systems, the coding and documentation requirements, the collection agencies. A significant portion of every healthcare dollar does not purchase care. It purchases the administration of a system whose complexity is itself a product of the multiple competing financial interests the system serves. Single-payer systems eliminate most of this administrative cost by eliminating the multiple competing payers whose existence generates it.

The argument that universal healthcare requires giving up choice is exactly backwards. The person with comprehensive coverage can choose any provider and receive any indicated care. The person navigating a network of in-network and out-of-network providers, prior authorization requirements, formulary restrictions, and coverage limits has no meaningful choice. They have the bureaucratic appearance of choice within a system designed to deny as much care as possible while collecting as much premium as possible. The choice the current system protects is the choice of the insurance company to deny care. That is not a choice worth protecting.

The Structure

Universal healthcare means every person receives comprehensive coverage for every medically necessary service: primary care, specialty care, mental healthcare, dental care, vision care, prescription drugs, long-term care, reproductive healthcare including abortion, and gender-affirming care. The coverage is not contingent on employment, income, age, citizenship status, or any other characteristic. It is the right established in Article VII of this constitution, enforced by the obligation of the healthcare system to provide it.

The financing is through progressive taxation: the people and institutions with the most resources contribute the most to the system from which everyone benefits. The current system finances healthcare through a combination of premiums, deductibles, copays, employer contributions, and tax revenue that is profoundly regressive: the person who is sickest and poorest pays the highest proportion of their income for the least adequate care. The universal system reverses this: the contribution is proportional to the ability to pay, the benefit is proportional to the need.

Providers are paid fairly and consistently, not through the current system of negotiated rates that produce wildly different payments for the same service depending on who is paying, but through a rate schedule that reflects the actual cost of providing care plus a reasonable return to the provider. The administrative burden on providers, which currently consumes a substantial portion of physician time and contributes significantly to burnout, is dramatically reduced by the elimination of multiple payers with multiple requirements.

Mental Healthcare

Mental healthcare is healthcare. The separation of mental health from physical health in insurance coverage, in delivery systems, in public understanding, and in the allocation of research funding reflects a decision that the suffering of the mind is less real, less urgent, and less deserving of the full resources of the medical system than the suffering of the body. The grief that does not resolve, the anxiety that does not lift, the psychosis that reorganizes reality into something unrecognizable: these are conditions that require care with the same urgency as any other medical condition.

The deinstitutionalization movement of the 1970s was right that the psychiatric hospitals of the era were often sites of abuse and violation rather than care. It was wrong that closing them without building replacement infrastructure would produce better outcomes. The replacement infrastructure was never built. The community mental health centers that were supposed to provide it were never adequately funded. The gap was filled by the criminal legal system, the emergency room, the homeless shelter, and the street. The person with a serious mental illness who cannot access consistent care cycles through these systems indefinitely at enormous human and financial cost to themselves and to the systems that process them without treating them.

Universal mental healthcare means adequate reimbursement for mental health providers that makes the profession financially viable and attracts enough practitioners to meet the need. It means the removal of prior authorization barriers that currently make accessing mental healthcare a bureaucratic obstacle course. It means the investment in community mental health infrastructure that deinstitutionalization assumed would be built. It means the integration of mental healthcare into primary care so that the person who presents to their doctor with depression is not referred away to a system they will never navigate, but treated in the place they already came to for help.

Reproductive Healthcare

Reproductive healthcare is healthcare. The decision to reproduce, to not reproduce, to continue a pregnancy, to end a pregnancy, to use contraception, to seek fertility treatment: these are medical decisions that belong to the individual under the bodily autonomy provision of this constitution. The state has no legitimate interest in compelling any of these decisions. The history of state intervention in reproductive decisions is a history of the control of women's bodies, of the eugenic sterilization of disabled people and people of color, of the criminalization of choices made by people the system has decided should not reproduce or should not stop reproducing.

Universal healthcare coverage for all reproductive services, without restriction based on the political preferences of whoever controls the relevant legislative body at a given moment, is the constitutional requirement. The coverage includes abortion, contraception, fertility treatment, prenatal care, maternal care, and postpartum care. The person who cannot afford to travel to a state where abortion is legal, cannot afford to take time off work, and cannot find childcare for the children they already have is not making a free choice when she carries an unwanted pregnancy to term. She is experiencing compulsion delivered through economic rather than legal mechanism. The economic floor and the reproductive healthcare provision together close that gap.

Gender-Affirming Care

Gender-affirming care is medically necessary healthcare for transgender and nonbinary people. The evidence base is extensive and consistent: access to gender-affirming care produces dramatic improvements in mental health outcomes, reductions in suicidality, and improvements in overall wellbeing. The denial of gender-affirming care produces the opposite outcomes. The restriction of that care by legislation is not a neutral policy choice about the allocation of medical resources. It is the use of law to enforce a singular acceptable body: the body that presents in the gender the state has decided is appropriate based on the characteristics present at birth. It enforces conformity that the medical and psychological communities with expertise in this area do not endorse, against people whose sense of themselves does not match what the dominant culture requires.

Universal healthcare coverage includes gender-affirming care for people of all ages, consistent with the established standards of care developed by the relevant medical and psychological communities. The political interference with medical decision-making in this domain, which has produced legislation in dozens of states restricting care that the relevant medical authorities endorse, is a constitutional violation under the bodily autonomy provision, the equal protection provision, and the due process provision simultaneously.

Disability and Healthcare

The medical model of disability treats disability as a problem located in the individual body that medicine should fix if possible. The social model of disability locates the problem in the mismatch between the built environment, the social structures, and the bodies and minds of the people living in them. The truth contains both: some conditions cause suffering that is intrinsic to the condition and that medicine can alleviate, and some suffering is produced by the failure of the environment to accommodate the full range of human variation.

A healthcare system built on genuine inclusion starts from a different question: not how do we make this person approximate the norm, but what does this person need to live fully, given who they are? The person who uses a wheelchair needs a healthcare system that is physically accessible, that does not require them to be carried up stairs to reach the examination room. The person who is deaf needs a healthcare system that provides interpreting services rather than requiring them to lipread a masked provider. The person with an intellectual disability needs a healthcare system that communicates in ways they can understand and that takes their report of their own symptoms seriously rather than deferring entirely to their caregiver.

Long-term care, the care of people who need ongoing support with the activities of daily living because of age, disability, or chronic condition, is currently funded through a patchwork of Medicare, Medicaid, private insurance, and personal resources that leaves enormous gaps and produces enormous impoverishment. The person who needs long-term care spends down their assets to qualify for Medicaid, which then pays for care at rates that produce inadequate staffing and inadequate quality. Universal healthcare includes long-term care as a covered benefit. The care of people who cannot care for themselves is not a private problem to be solved by families with whatever resources they happen to have. It is a collective responsibility that the collective is obligated to fund.

The Male Default in Medical Science

Medical research has been built predominantly on the study of male bodies. For most of the twentieth century, women were routinely excluded from clinical trials, first informally and then through formal policies justified by concerns about fetal risk and hormonal variability. The practical consequence is that the standard treatments, standard dosages, and standard diagnostic criteria for a wide range of conditions were developed from data collected almost entirely from men and then applied to everyone.

This is not an abstract problem. It has documented consequences for how women are diagnosed and treated. Heart attack symptoms present differently in women than in men, but the symptoms taught in medical training and depicted in public health campaigns are the male presentation. Women having heart attacks are more likely to be sent home from emergency rooms without diagnosis. The diagnostic criteria for conditions from autoimmune diseases to sleep disorders were developed from male populations and perform worse when applied to women. Drug dosages calculated from male body weight and metabolism produce different effects in female bodies, a fact that was not systematically studied until 1993, when the NIH Revitalization Act required the inclusion of women in federally funded clinical research and the FDA lifted its 1977 rule excluding women of childbearing potential from early drug trials. The decades of research conducted before that requirement produced a body of evidence with a built-in gap.

The exclusion was not accidental. It reflected a set of assumptions about whose body was the default human body and whose was a variation requiring special handling. The male body was the standard. The female body was the complicated version. Those assumptions are present in the research literature, in the diagnostic tools built from that literature, and in the clinical training built from those tools. Correcting them requires more than adding women to future studies. It requires actively identifying where the gap has produced inadequate care and investing in the research needed to close it.

What Investment in Female Healthcare Requires

The conditions that affect only or predominantly women have been systematically underfunded relative to their prevalence and their impact on quality of life. Endometriosis affects roughly one in ten women, causes chronic pain that can be disabling, and takes an average of seven to ten years to diagnose. The diagnostic delay is partly a consequence of inadequate research into the condition and partly a consequence of a medical culture in which women's pain is more likely to be attributed to psychological causes or dismissed as normal. The research investment in endometriosis is a fraction of what comparably prevalent conditions affecting men receive.

The same pattern holds across a range of conditions: polycystic ovary syndrome, fibroids, pelvic floor disorders, perimenopause and menopause, postpartum depression, and chronic pain conditions that disproportionately affect women. These conditions are underresearched, undertreated, and underrepresented in medical education relative to their burden on the people who have them. The woman who spends years seeking a diagnosis for a condition that should have been identified quickly, who is told her symptoms are stress or anxiety or normal variation, who adjusts her life around pain the medical system has decided is not a priority: she is experiencing the consequences of a research agenda that did not include her.

This constitution's healthcare provisions require the correction of this gap through specific obligations: research funding allocated in proportion to disease burden across the full population rather than on the basis of which conditions have historically attracted the most investment; diagnostic criteria reviewed and updated to reflect the presentation of conditions in female bodies; medical education that teaches the full range of symptom presentation rather than only the presentation documented in male-default research; and clinical guidelines that reflect the evidence base for female patients specifically rather than extrapolating from male data.

The requirement that women be included in clinical trials, established by the NIH Revitalization Act of 1993 and strengthened in subsequent years, is a floor. This constitution's obligation goes further: not only must women be included in trials, but the data must be analyzed by sex so that differences in response, dosage, and side effects are documented rather than averaged away. The averaged result that looks adequate for the population as a whole can mask significant harm to a subset of that population. The disaggregated result shows what is actually happening.

Maternal Mortality

The United States has the highest maternal mortality rate of any wealthy country, and it is one of the few wealthy countries where the rate has been rising rather than falling. Black women die in childbirth at three times the rate of white women, a disparity that persists across income and education levels and is not explained by differences in access to care alone. The research on this disparity consistently identifies a contributing factor that the data cannot fully capture but that clinicians and researchers have documented: the systematic undertreatment of Black women's pain and the systematic discounting of Black women's reported symptoms by medical providers.

The 1619 Project and subsequent historical scholarship have documented the origins of this pattern in nineteenth-century medical practices that treated Black people as having higher pain tolerance as a justification for conducting procedures without anesthesia. That belief has no scientific basis. It has persisted in measurable form in the attitudes of medical providers today. The medical student who believes, incorrectly, that Black patients feel less pain is more likely to undertreat Black patients' pain. The provider who discounts a Black woman's report of her own symptoms is more likely to miss the warning signs of a preventable complication.

Reducing maternal mortality requires investment in the full range of factors that produce it: adequate prenatal care, adequate staffing of labor and delivery units, the reversal of hospital closures in rural and low-income communities that have left women without access to obstetric care, and the specific training and accountability measures that address the documented disparities in how different patients are treated. It requires that every maternal death be reviewed by an independent body with the authority to identify contributing factors and recommend changes to prevent the next one. Most states have maternal mortality review committees. Most of them are inadequately funded and their recommendations are not binding.

The Transition

The transition from the current system to universal healthcare is politically difficult and administratively complex, but the elements are known. Countries that have made this transition provide the evidence base. The Canadian single-payer system was built from a provincial experiment in Saskatchewan in the 1960s that was then extended nationally. The German multi-payer regulated system achieves universal coverage through a combination of public and private insurers operating under a common regulatory framework. The British National Health Service employs providers directly. Each model has different advantages and different transition requirements. The specific design is a political question. The commitment to universal coverage is a constitutional one.

The transition requires the protection of the people who currently work in the healthcare administration industry whose jobs are eliminated by the simplification of the system. It requires the negotiation of drug prices that the current system has prohibited through the explicit ban on Medicare negotiating pharmaceutical prices, a ban whose only beneficiary is the pharmaceutical industry. It requires capital investment in the physical infrastructure of care in communities that currently lack it: the rural areas without hospitals, the urban communities without primary care providers, the regions where the market has decided care is not profitable enough to provide.

The floor, once established, is not retracted. The care, once provided, is not withdrawn. The person who is sick is treated. That is what the system is for.
