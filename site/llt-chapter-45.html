Chapter 45

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary · Language Shapes What's Possible

The Questions

Cousin Hannah’s letters are all stamped “Answered,” but there was “neither street nor name nor road.” What is an answer that reaches no one and says nothing?

“Never had Mr. Spitzer known an individual who did not crumble into waters and clouds if he watched long enough.” Is there a solid person anywhere in this book?

He had “yearned for a final answer and certainty beyond all doubt” and is “left with these questions unanswered.” What does the longing for certainty cost the man who cannot have it?

“Love’s image was that which was never reached by love.” Why would reaching love be the death of it?

The letters are the chapter’s emblem of an answer that answers nothing. Cousin Hannah’s correspondence, inquiries after “lost loves and dead brides and wandering husbands,” is all marked with her stamp “Answered,” yet Mr. Spitzer cannot see “what had she answered and to whom if there was neither street nor name nor road.” The stamp is the form of a resolution with no content, closure performed over an open question. This is Language Shapes What's Possible at its most poignant failure: the word “Answered” builds the appearance of a settled matter over a matter that was never settled and could not be, a label doing the work that no reply ever did.

And no person in the chapter holds solid. “Never had Mr. Spitzer known an individual who did not crumble into waters and clouds if he watched long enough.” This is the book’s account of the self stated as flat observation: watch anyone closely enough and the sealed, single person dissolves into the plural, shifting, unfixable thing they always were. Cousin Hannah crumbles as everyone crumbles; there is no bedrock individual under the watching, only the crumbling. The chapter does not offer this as despair but as what Mr. Spitzer has simply come to know, the way a man knows the tide.

His suffering is the longing for exactly what the book denies. He “had yearned for a final answer and certainty beyond all doubt and no further equivocation even if it had meant the death of love,” and he is left, always, with “questions unanswered.” He is the man who wants the finished blueprint and has learned he cannot have it, who would have been “happier, much happier” with one great star instead of the scattered many, and who lives among the many anyway. His pain is not that he holds the construction open easily but that he holds it open against his own deepest wish for it to close. The openness the book trusts is, in him, a discipline worn over a longing for the seal.

The chapter’s deepest turn is about love. “If one knew all hearts, one should know none,” and “love’s image was that which was never reached by love.” Should love be reached, “there would follow… the death of love.” This is Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary applied to the heart: love lives by not arriving, by staying reaching rather than reached, and the moment it closes on its object it dies of the closing. Cousin Hannah’s lifelong unreached love, and Mr. Spitzer’s, are not failures to complete something. They are love kept in the only condition love survives, open, unarrived, forever moving toward. “There was a rift in his lute,” and the rift is where the music gets out.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes the stamp “Answered” at its word, as if the questions had been settled, and the reading that takes Mr. Spitzer’s unanswered longing for simple defeat. Neither the settled matter nor the beaten man is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that a label can build the form of an answer over a question that was never answered, closure performed rather than reached; that no person holds solid under long watching, all crumbling into the plural thing they are; that the longing for a final certainty is a real and unsatisfiable ache, so that openness is a discipline worn over the wish for a seal; and that love lives only unreached, dying the moment it closes on its object. The answer that answers nothing is a construction, and love kept open is love kept alive. Mr. Spitzer is left with his questions and his rift, and the chapter offers him no final answer, which is the honest thing to leave him.

For Reflection

What in one’s own life has been stamped “Answered” over a question that was never really answered?

If everyone crumbles into something unfixable when watched closely enough, what is one wanting when one wants to know a person fully?

Is there a love that lives by staying unreached, and would arriving at it end it?
