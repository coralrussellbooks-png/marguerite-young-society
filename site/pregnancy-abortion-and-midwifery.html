Definition from research

Chapter 6

  • Maternal mortality rates were 30-40% higher before Roe v. Wade according to New York State, which saw maternal mortality fall by that much after New York legalized abortion in 1970, three years before Roe v. Wade. Depending on the state, maternal and infant mortality rates are rising due to inconsistent access to abortion. Some states that restrict abortion, rather than face the rise in mortality, have simply stopped reporting or disbanded the councils that were meant to monitor mortality rates in women and infants.

Chapter 6

  • “for she died in giving birth”: At the time Young started this book maternal mortality was pervasive for multiple reasons. Around 1900 six to nine women died for every thousand births, and rates stayed highest through 1930. They fell 71% between 1939 and 1948 with the move to hospital births, antibiotics, and safe blood transfusions.

Chapter 34

  • “My mother’s beauty was already fading”: In 2017 the Pew Research Center found that, among those who see differences, 61 percent of men said it was biology that determined what things men and women excel at in the workplace, while 65 percent of women said it was due to societal expectations (Pew Research Center, “Americans are divided on whether differences between men and women are rooted in biology or societal expectations,” December 5, 2017). One of those expectations is that women are valued for their physical beauty and nurturing. In July 2022, at the Turning Point USA Student Action Summit in Tampa, Representative Matt Gaetz asked why “the women with the least likelihood of getting pregnant” were “the ones most worried about having abortions” and said, “Nobody wants to impregnate you if you look like a thumb.” That May he had called abortion-rights protesters “over-educated, under-loved millennials” (Matt Gaetz, Twitter, May 4, 2022).

Chapter 71

  • “forceps, and something which looked like a tuning fork”: Obstetric forceps were invented in the 1600s by the Chamberlen family of London, who kept them a family secret for more than a century. René Laennec invented the stethoscope in 1816.
  • “Why was Sarah laughing?”: “Therefore Sarah laughed within herself, saying, After I am waxed old shall I have pleasure, my lord being old also?” (Genesis 18:12, KJV): the old woman who laughed at the promise of a child.

Chapter 72

  • “all the Fallopian tubes”: Fallopian tubes (in a female mammal) is either of a pair of tubes along which eggs travel from the ovaries to the uterus. They are named for Gabriele Falloppio, the Italian anatomist who described them in 1561.
  • “had never had to take a stitch”: “The stitch” may refer to a procedure called an episiotomy, also known as perineotomy and is a surgical incision of the perineum and the posterior vaginal wall generally done by a midwife or obstetrician. It was in the 50s in the US called the “husband stitch” because it was believed to return a woman’s vaginal opening to its original size. The routine is no longer recommended for vaginal births except in emergencies. Despite this, it is one of the most common surgical procedures specific to women.
  • “buried layettes laid out in long white boxes”: Young’s first poem, written at sixteen, “was about preparing a layette for my unborn fatherless baby,” and “it does become the character of Esther Longtree in Miss Macintosh, My Darling, many decades later.” An English teacher told her “that a young girl should not have such thoughts on her mind” (Marguerite Young, interview, WNYC radio, New York). On a train to Chicago a man told her of a cocker spaniel whose false pregnancy was eased only by giving “her toys to play with as if the baby were really there” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).
  • “She had never eaten ergot”: Ergot is a fungus that in the 1930s companies marketed to women whose pregnancy was not progressing since abortion, drugs that aborted a fetus and birth control devices were illegal. The FTC deemed the pills unsafe and ineffective and stopped their sale. In 1774 it is documented that midwives used ergot to ease delivery of a baby. In 1808 the New York doctor John Stearns published his use of ergot, “pulvis parturiens,” to hasten labor.
  • “a breech birth”: A breech birth occurs when a baby is positioned feet or bottom down instead of head-down near the end of pregnancy (around 36-37 weeks). While most babies turn head-down, about 3-5% remain in this position, which carries a higher risk for vaginal delivery, often requiring a C-section.

Chapter 73

  • “In her vast super-fecundation”: In medicine, superfecundation is the fertilizing of two or more eggs from one cycle in separate acts of intercourse, so that twins may have different fathers.
  • “the birth control devices littering the bare ground”: Before 1965, birth control in the US relied on barriers, withdrawal, and, later, hormonal methods, with access heavily restricted by the Comstock Act of 1873. Common methods included condoms (vulcanized rubber by 1839), diaphragms, spermicides, and dangerous, ineffective douches marketed as “feminine hygiene.” The first oral contraceptive pill was approved in 1960.
  • “the old Chicago detective was trying to find the proof”: England’s Act to Prevent the Destroying and Murthering of Bastard Children (1624) presumed that an unmarried mother who hid the death of her newborn had murdered it, unless she could prove “by one witness at the least” that the child was born dead; the penalty was death. It stood until 1803. In Massachusetts Hannah Piggen was reportedly the last woman hanged for concealing a birth, in 1785 (Wikipedia). Concealment of birth is still a crime in many states.
  • “she might endure the birth of nothing”: “We have been with child, we have been in pain, we have as it were brought forth wind” (Isaiah 26:18, KJV).

Chapter 74

  • “she had lost her toxic condition”: Toxemia of pregnancy, now called preeclampsia, brings high blood pressure, swelling, and sometimes convulsions (eclampsia); before modern prenatal care it was one of the great killers of mothers.
  • “a woman abortionist who enjoyed police protection”: Abortion was a crime in Illinois from 1867 until 1973. In Chicago, the historian Leslie J. Reagan found, the state prosecuted chiefly abortionists, most often after a woman had died, and police questioned dying women about their abortionists and lovers to obtain “dying declarations.” Reagan, When Abortion Was a Crime (1997).

Chapter 75

  • “Often, she pondered upon difficult questions”: “...pondered upon difficult questions, some legal, some illegal… often, was raped when there was no one present but a ghostly, mean, mean child… She could not always blame men for her disappointments. Sometimes she blamed the child…” The children ruined her body and “...her raped, wounded sky, the whole world?” During and after pregnancy, a woman’s body undergoes significant changes, including increased blood volume and heart rate, breast enlargement, and skin changes like stretch marks. Postpartum, the uterus shrinks, abdominal muscles may separate (diastasis recti), and some muscles may be weakened, potentially leading to urinary incontinence. Other common after-effects include changes in shoe size, wider hips, and hair loss after an initial period of thicker hair. During pregnancy, organs like the uterus, stomach, and intestines are pushed and shifted by the growing fetus to make room. After pregnancy, these organs gradually return to their pre-pregnancy positions over several weeks or months. Vaginal stretching happens as the baby moves down the birth canal. As the baby’s head descends, it interacts with the vaginal walls and pelvic floor, causing the tissues and muscles to stretch significantly. Computer models show that some pelvic floor muscles can stretch to more than three times their normal resting length.
  • “it might be a benign tumor, this growth”: In pseudocyesis, false or phantom pregnancy, a woman believes she is pregnant and her body follows: the menses stop, the belly swells, she feels the child move. Mary Tudor, Queen of England, believed herself pregnant in 1554 and 1555; London rang its bells for a prince who was never born.
  • “she ate geraniums and paper and string”: Pica, the craving to eat things that are not food (clay, chalk, paper, ice, starch), is common in pregnancy and has been described since antiquity; the word is Latin for magpie, the bird that will eat anything.

Chapter 76

  • “no rubber corporation or Wall Street corporation”: Esther means she never used a condom. Rubber condoms followed Goodyear’s vulcanization of rubber (1844); thinner latex condoms came in the 1920s. Under the Comstock Act of 1873 mailing contraceptives or information about them was a federal crime.
  • “had murdered her twin by kicking it out early”: Physicians have long known that one twin may die early in pregnancy and be shed or reabsorbed. With ultrasound in the 1970s this “vanishing twin” proved far more common than anyone had guessed.

Chapter 77

  • “was natural and a mother should suffer”: This may refer to Genesis 3:16 as Eve and women’s punishment for being cast out of the Garden of Eden. “In sorrow thou shalt bring forth children” (Genesis 3:16, KJV).
  • “pregnant in her dreams even though there was neither child nor father”: Esther may be suffering from False pregnancy or pseudocyesis. It is the appearance of clinical or subclinical signs and symptoms associated with pregnancy although the individual is not physically carrying a baby. The mistaken impression that one is pregnant includes signs and symptoms such as tender breasts with secretions, abdominal growth, delayed menstrual periods, and subjective feelings of a moving fetus. False pregnancy has a prominent psychiatric component as well as physical manifestations of pregnancy. It can be caused by physical or mental trauma, a chemical imbalance of hormones, and some medical conditions. See pseudocyesis in the Chapter 75 resources.

Chapter 78

  • “She would turn, bend, spill a baby”: A precipitate labor, three hours or less from the first contractions to birth, can deliver a baby before the mother has time to lie down.

Chapter 79

  • A "dark woman" who assisted with births ,may refer to the following: 1) the Biblical midwife Shiphrah, who was a Nubian woman mentioned in the Book of Exodus. Shiphrah, along with her colleague Puah, defied the Pharaoh's order to kill newborn Hebrew males. 2) Biddy Mason, who was born into slavery, was a midwife and advocate for other enslaved people in 19th-century California. After gaining her freedom, she became a successful entrepreneur and philanthropist. 3) Margaret Charles Smith was an African American midwife in Alabama who delivered over 3,000 babies during a career spanning over 30 years, often in rural areas with limited healthcare access. 4) Mary Francis Hill Coley was a midwife from Georgia who delivered over 3,000 babies during her career. Her work was featured in the 1952 documentary, All My Babies. 5) Susie Carey was a third-generation midwife featured in a photograph at the National Museum of African American History and Culture. 6) Sarah Hudson Grant was a Black Southern midwife whose work is remembered today.

Chapter 79

  • Artificial insemination is the deliberate introduction of sperm into a female's cervix or uterine cavity for the purpose of achieving a pregnancy through in vivo fertilization by means other than sexual intercourse or in vitro fertilisation. It is a fertility treatment for humans, and is common practice in animal breeding. The first recorded case of artificial insemination was John Hunter in 1790, who helped impregnate a linen draper's wife. The first reported case of artificial insemination by a donor occurred in 1884 when William H. Pancoast, a professor in Philadelphia, took sperm from a handsome student in his class to inseminate an anesthetized woman without her knowledge. The incident was not published until twenty-five years later in a medical journal. The sperm bank was developed in Iowa starting in the 1950s in research conducted by University of Iowa medical school researchers Jerome Sherman and Raymond Bunge. In the United Kingdom, the British obstetrician Mary Barton founded one of the first fertility clinics to offer donor insemination in the 1930s. Mary Barton and her first husband were medical missionaries in pre-partition India, then still ruled by Britain. She witnessed the way in which women would be punished or even killed for being childless. At the time, it was taboo to suggest that it might be the husband, and not the wife, who was infertile - not only on the Indian subcontinent but also in the United Kingdom. Barton understood that both men and women could be infertile and in 1943 was the lead author on an article for the British Medical Journal. It was signed by many of the researchers active in the field in Great Britain at that time. With her second husband, Bertold Wiesner, Mary Barton successfully inseminated an estimated 1500 women, the majority with sperm provided from Wiesner, some 100-200 from neuroscientist Derek Richter as well as an unknown number from as yet unidentified donors. It is estimated that Wiesner is the biological father of around 600 children born following these procedures, although some believe this figure could be as high as 1000. When Barton and Wiesner published their paper on the procedure it was publicly condemned by the Pope who called it a sin, and the Archbishop of Canterbury who called for the British parliament to make human artificial insemination illegal. Although it was not criminalized it was not legalized either and therefore the status of artificial insemination was ambiguous. Consequently, the activities of Barton and Wiesner at the fertility clinic were conducted in secrecy and all inseminated women were instructed to tell nobody about it.

Chapter 79

  • Instruments to assist with childbirth have evolved from rudimentary tools for destructive purposes in antiquity to sophisticated instruments like forceps and vacuum extractors today. Early interventions included destructive tools for delivering stillborn fetuses and instruments for procedures like symphysiotomy, a painful procedure to widen the pelvis. Modern forceps, introduced by the Chamberlen family in the 16th century, were a breakthrough for delivering living infants, and their use, along with vacuum extractors, remains a vital part of modern obstetrics.

Chapter 80

  • Esther Longtree's character carries symbolic weight that can be interpreted in relation to the abortion debate in the U.S., although the symbolism is complex, multifaceted, and embedded within the novel’s rich, dreamlike narrative. Esther Longtree is portrayed as a woman who is "everlastingly pregnant," carrying many stillborn children, miscarriages, and unfulfilled pregnancies over the years. She is described as both "brilliant but dumb," "mulish," "big-footed," and “pregnant with doubtfulness,” yet also maternal, loving, and burdened by her mysterious, repeated losses. Her pregnancies are often linked to a haunting mystery involving many absent or sterile fathers and the ambiguous presence of children who are never fully born or recognized. The narrative suggests that Esther has tried repeatedly to give birth to a "child who lived" but is also surrounded by countless stillborn, ghostly children, and shadows: symbolizing loss, grief, and the elusive nature of life and creation. In this light, Esther Longtree can be seen as embodying the tensions surrounding abortion debates: the conflict between life and death, fertility and sterility, birth and loss, hope and despair. Her "everlasting pregnancy" and the repeated miscarriages may symbolize the ongoing societal struggle over reproductive rights, the moral and emotional complexity of abortion, and the societal judgment and silence that often surround it. The ambiguity about the paternity of her children, the legal threats she faces, and her isolation also reflect the politicized and often stigmatized dimensions of abortion in American society. Esther’s story touches on themes of bodily autonomy, societal control, guilt, and secrecy. She is pursued by a detective who accuses her of murder, threatened with legal action, and yet she remains evasive, protective of her secret “pregnancies,” and burdened by the consequences of societal judgments. This can be interpreted as symbolic of the legal and moral scrutiny women face in abortion debates, where personal agency conflicts with external control and societal norms. The novel frames Esther’s experiences within a broader cosmic and existential context, blurring lines between life and death, reality and dream, and individual and universal experiences. This deepens the symbolism, suggesting that the abortion debate is not simply a social or political issue but also a profound human and metaphysical dilemma about creation, destruction, and the meaning of existence. Esther Longtree may symbolize the abortion debate in the U.S., the detailed portrayal of her character’s perpetual pregnancies, miscarriages, societal judgment, and emotional complexity strongly suggests an allegorical reflection on the themes central to abortion discourse. Her story embodies the profound personal and societal conflicts surrounding reproductive rights, loss, and the nature of life itself.
