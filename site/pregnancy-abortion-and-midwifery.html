Chapter 72

  • “The stitch” may refer to a procedure called an episiotomy, also known as perineotomy and is a surgical incision of the perineum and the posterior vaginal wall generally done by a midwife or obstetrician. Chapter 74

  • Esther Longtree in this chapter is portrayed as a profoundly tragic and complex figure, embodying themes of loss, mystery, maternal suffering, and societal judgment. Her character is marked by an overwhelming burden of perpetual pregnancy and stillbirths, an existence both physically and emotionally laden with pain and unanswered questions. Esther’s situation is unique and unprecedented in the legal realm, as she faces the threat of arrest and trial for the disappearance or death of her children: children who may never have been born or who remain unseen by others. She is caught in a web of suspicion and gossip, misunderstood and condemned by her community and the justice system, yet she remains deeply isolated, bearing her sorrow silently and without defense. This isolation is compounded by her physical condition (repeatedly pregnant yet without the relief of childbirth) and by the haunting presence of her absent children, described metaphorically as her “tears” and “stillborn” who haunt her mind and body. Her relationship with the Chicago detective, who is implicated as the father of her lost children, adds layers of complexity and darkness to her narrative. The detective’s repeated appearances (disguised as various characters) are both invasive and inscrutable. Esther’s encounters with him are marked by violence, mystery, and a sense of inevitability: she is raped by him, yet the experience is described with a strange passivity and detachment, as if she were “sleeping in the grass” and indifferent. This rapist-father figure represents a brutal intrusion into her life, one that leaves physical and psychological scars, but also a tangled mystery of conception, conception without life, and lost pregnancies. Esther’s internal world is rich with symbolism and metaphor: the “stillborn” children, the “broken gooseberry bushes,” the “buzzard hanging rounded like a period” all evoke a landscape of death, loss, and unfulfilled potential. Despite her suffering, Esther is radiant and self-sufficient in a strange way, carrying her burden with a stubborn dignity. She is aware of the social judgments (accusations of insanity, immorality, or deceit) but she resists these with a fierce inner knowledge of her own innocence and the mystery of her condition. Her loneliness and estrangement are deeply felt, yet she remains luminous, a figure of both vulnerability and strength. The chapter also explores Esther’s reflections on gender and societal double standards. She contrasts her own experience as a woman burdened with loss and silence to the freedom a man might have in fathering children without consequence or shame. Her pregnancy and stillbirths are not only physical but symbolic of her emotional and existential state: a life marked by “murder of her heart,” repeated disappointments, and unanswered questions about innocence and guilt. Esther Longtree is a character defined by enigmatic suffering, caught between the natural and the supernatural, the legal and the personal. She embodies a tragic maternal figure whose story is one of loss made visible and invisible, a woman burdened by an unexplainable condition and an unresolvable mystery. Her narrative is steeped in poetic imagery and a haunting sense of inevitability and silence, marking her as both victim and enigma within the novel’s broader themes of reality, illusion, and the passage of time.

Chapter 76

  • The labia majora are the two outer, fleshy folds of skin that form the "lips" of the vulva, protecting the other external genitalia. They are located on the mons pubis and extend down to the perineum, and contain sweat and sebaceous glands. Chapter 77

  • Esther Longtree in this chapter is portrayed as a deeply tragic and complex figure, embodying sorrow, loss, mystery, and a profound connection to themes of fertility, death, and motherhood. Esther is described as a large, pregnant waitress, often weighed down not only by her physical condition but by the immense emotional burden of repeated stillbirths and lost children she carries within her memory and body. Her pregnancy is symbolic, described as “watery” and “wild,” with her body “pregnant with death,” indicating a continuous cycle of creation and loss intertwined in her existence. She is caught between hope and despair, weighted down "by the death of a small thing," her body swollen but also fragile, her spinal column curved by the weight of memory. Esther represents the paradox of fertility and sterility simultaneously. Though she is repeatedly pregnant, the children are stillborn or lost, rendering her motherhood incomplete and haunted by absence. This condition makes her a symbol of the "Song of Songs of the sterility which is the creation," where creation is intertwined with loss and death. Her pregnancy is not just physical but metaphysical, involving the vastness of the universe, stars, and the earth, all of which seem to be "killed" or destroyed, mirroring her personal tragedy. Her mental state is marked by suspicion, confusion, and a profound sense of mystery. She contemplates who might have "killed the heavens," the earth, and love itself, reflecting a cosmic grief that parallels her personal losses. She imagines a detective who is both a pursuer and a murderer, embodying the threat of exposure and judgment, while the children she once had or never had remain elusive, "the child that never was". Esther’s relationship with the detective is complex: she suspects him of having raped her and leaving her for dead, yet he is also the one who threatens to uncover her secret sorrows. Her narrative includes a haunting sense of being pursued and judged unfairly, and she is both victim and accuser in her own story. The chapter also touches on racial issues through the detective's selective interest in "white" deaths while neglecting "black" ones, revealing societal biases and injustices. Esther, being associated with the "quiet, black mystery," is marginalized and ignored by official justice, highlighting themes of racial invisibility and systemic neglect. Esther is haunted by old murders, tragedies, and lost children, both personal and historical. She reflects on a mass murderer, abortionist and other cases of maternal and infant death, drawing parallels to her own suffering and guilt. These old stories serve as a backdrop to her present torment and sense of isolation, deepening the theme of a past that refuses to be forgotten. Despite her suffering, Esther shows moments of defiant acceptance and even joy, laughing in her sleep and finding some beauty in the natural world and her own strange, wild condition. She is stubborn, proud, and insists on her innocence in the face of accusation. Her condition is "no dream" but a persistent reality, and she prefers the mystery and silence of her pain to exposure or judgment. Esther Longtree is a figure of immense sorrow and mystery, embodying the tragic intersection of life and death, fertility and sterility, hope and despair. She is pregnant with loss, carrying both physical and metaphysical burdens, haunted by memories, accusations, and societal neglect, especially in the face of racial injustice. Her story is a poignant, symbolic exploration of motherhood, tragedy, and the human condition.

Chapter 77

  • Esther Longtree symbolizes a tragic and complex history of motherhood, loss, and social neglect in the United States, especially reflecting the fates of unmarried or marginalized women and their children. Her story evokes the suffering of countless women who experienced stillbirths, miscarriages, or the loss of children under harsh social conditions. She is "always pregnant" yet often gives birth to "nothing," representing the grief of "stillborn" children and the immense sorrow that accompanies such loss. This condition is described as a vast, mysterious, and diffused crime or guilt that is both personal and cosmic in scale, involving death and sterility in creation itself. Esther Longtree's narrative intertwines with the grim history of illegal or dangerous abortions, as suggested by the story of the old woman abortionist who killed unmarried mothers and their babies in a hospital disguised as care. This history reflects the brutal reality many women faced in times when reproductive rights and safe medical care were inaccessible. The abortionist's killing of a woman who was "pregnant in her dreams" but had no child underscores the terror and stigma surrounding female reproduction and the societal condemnation of women’s bodies. Esther’s interactions with the white detective reveal the racial and social divisions in America’s history of violence and injustice. The detective focuses only on the deaths of white children, ignoring the countless black children lost or murdered: a commentary on racial indifference and systemic racism. Esther’s world is described as a "quiet, black mystery," where black children's deaths are overlooked, symbolizing the historical neglect and invisibility of black lives in American social and legal systems. Her burden of loss and the constant shadow of death she carries symbolize not only individual grief but also a collective history of women’s suffering, social marginalization, racial injustice, and the silencing of certain kinds of pain. Esther Longtree is a figure embodying the intersection of these historical realities: motherhood, loss, racial discrimination, and societal neglect in the US.

Chapter 78

  • Esther Longtree symbolizes a deep and painful history of struggle, loss, and marginalization that can be read as a reflection of certain aspects of American history, particularly those involving the complex legacies of race, family, and survival. Her lineage is described as mixed: "partly Irish and partly Indian and maybe even had a Negro streak in them," suggesting the multiracial and multicultural heritage that is part of the American fabric, especially in the context of colonial and frontier histories where Indigenous peoples, European settlers, and African descendants intersected. This mixed heritage points to the tangled histories of racial identity and the often painful intersections of different communities in the United States. Esther’s life is marked by hardship, loneliness, and repeated loss: she has borne many children who have died before or shortly after birth, symbolizing the high rates of infant and maternal mortality historically faced by impoverished, marginalized, or rural populations in the US. Her mother’s accusation that Esther "killed a little child before she was ever born," referring to the death of her twin Rosemary, suggests the tragic fate of many families who suffered such losses quietly and without support. Her father, a passive policeman whose "life was a prison," represents the ordinary working-class man trapped in a limited existence, disconnected from emotional life and unable to protect or nurture fully. This dynamic echoes the social and economic constraints faced by many in American history, especially in small, insular communities where traditional roles and isolation prevailed. Esther’s story also touches on the theme of women’s experiences of oppression and misunderstanding: her mother’s coldness, the abuse from schoolmates, and her own physical and emotional pain symbolize the harsh realities women have faced, including neglect, judgment, and the burden of reproductive suffering. Esther’s "mulish" nature and stubbornness reflect resistance and survival despite these adversities, embodying the resilience necessary to endure "long trial, long suffering". Esther Longtree’s narrative can be understood as a microcosm of the American historical experience involving racial mixture, frontier hardship, the struggles of working-class families, the tragedy of loss, and the resilience of women caught in cycles of suffering and survival. Her character embodies the silent histories of pain and endurance that underlie much of the American story.

Chapter 79

  • A "dark woman" who assisted with births ,may refer to the following: 1) the Biblical midwife Shiphrah, who was a Nubian woman mentioned in the Book of Exodus. Shiphrah, along with her colleague Puah, defied the Pharaoh's order to kill newborn Hebrew males. 2) Biddy Mason, who was born into slavery, was a midwife and advocate for other enslaved people in 19th-century California. After gaining her freedom, she became a successful entrepreneur and philanthropist. 3) Margaret Charles Smith was an African American midwife in Alabama who delivered over 3,000 babies during a career spanning over 30 years, often in rural areas with limited healthcare access. 4) Mary Francis Hill Coley was a midwife from Georgia who delivered over 3,000 babies during her career. Her work was featured in the 1952 documentary, All My Babies. 5) Susie Carey was a third-generation midwife featured in a photograph at the National Museum of African American History and Culture. 6) Sarah Hudson Grant was a Black Southern midwife whose work is remembered today.

Chapter 79

  • Artificial insemination is the deliberate introduction of sperm into a female's cervix or uterine cavity for the purpose of achieving a pregnancy through in vivo fertilization by means other than sexual intercourse or in vitro fertilisation. It is a fertility treatment for humans, and is common practice in animal breeding. The first recorded case of artificial insemination was John Hunter in 1790, who helped impregnate a linen draper's wife. The first reported case of artificial insemination by a donor occurred in 1884 when William H. Pancoast, a professor in Philadelphia, took sperm from a handsome student in his class to inseminate an anesthetized woman without her knowledge. The incident was not published until twenty-five years later in a medical journal. The sperm bank was developed in Iowa starting in the 1950s in research conducted by University of Iowa medical school researchers Jerome Sherman and Raymond Bunge. In the United Kingdom, the British obstetrician Mary Barton founded one of the first fertility clinics to offer donor insemination in the 1930s. Mary Barton and her first husband were medical missionaries in pre-partition India, then still ruled by Britain. She witnessed the way in which women would be punished or even killed for being childless. At the time, it was taboo to suggest that it might be the husband, and not the wife, who was infertile - not only on the Indian subcontinent but also in the United Kingdom. Barton understood that both men and women could be infertile and in 1943 was the lead author on an article for the British Medical Journal. It was signed by many of the researchers active in the field in Great Britain at that time. With her second husband, Bertold Wiesner, Mary Barton successfully inseminated an estimated 1500 women, the majority with sperm provided from Wiesner, some 100-200 from neuroscientist Derek Richter as well as an unknown number from as yet unidentified donors. It is estimated that Wiesner is the biological father of around 600 children born following these procedures, although some believe this figure could be as high as 1000. When Barton and Wiesner published their paper on the procedure it was publicly condemned by the Pope who called it a sin, and the Archbishop of Canterbury who called for the British parliament to make human artificial insemination illegal. Although it was not criminalized it was not legalized either and therefore the status of artificial insemination was ambiguous. Consequently, the activities of Barton and Wiesner at the fertility clinic were conducted in secrecy and all inseminated women were instructed to tell nobody about it.

Chapter 79

  • Instruments to assist with childbirth have evolved from rudimentary tools for destructive purposes in antiquity to sophisticated instruments like forceps and vacuum extractors today. Early interventions included destructive tools for delivering stillborn fetuses and instruments for procedures like symphysiotomy, a painful procedure to widen the pelvis. Modern forceps, introduced by the Chamberlen family in the 16th century, were a breakthrough for delivering living infants, and their use, along with vacuum extractors, remains a vital part of modern obstetrics.

Chapter 80

  • Esther Longtree's character carries symbolic weight that can be interpreted in relation to the abortion debate in the U.S., although the symbolism is complex, multifaceted, and embedded within the novel’s rich, dreamlike narrative. Esther Longtree is portrayed as a woman who is "everlastingly pregnant," carrying many stillborn children, miscarriages, and unfulfilled pregnancies over the years. She is described as both "brilliant but dumb," "mulish," "big-footed," and “pregnant with doubtfulness,” yet also maternal, loving, and burdened by her mysterious, repeated losses. Her pregnancies are often linked to a haunting mystery involving many absent or sterile fathers and the ambiguous presence of children who are never fully born or recognized. The narrative suggests that Esther has tried repeatedly to give birth to a "child who lived" but is also surrounded by countless stillborn, ghostly children, and shadows: symbolizing loss, grief, and the elusive nature of life and creation. In this light, Esther Longtree can be seen as embodying the tensions surrounding abortion debates: the conflict between life and death, fertility and sterility, birth and loss, hope and despair. Her "everlasting pregnancy" and the repeated miscarriages may symbolize the ongoing societal struggle over reproductive rights, the moral and emotional complexity of abortion, and the societal judgment and silence that often surround it. The ambiguity about the paternity of her children, the legal threats she faces, and her isolation also reflect the politicized and often stigmatized dimensions of abortion in American society. Esther’s story touches on themes of bodily autonomy, societal control, guilt, and secrecy. She is pursued by a detective who accuses her of murder, threatened with legal action, and yet she remains evasive, protective of her secret “pregnancies,” and burdened by the consequences of societal judgments. This can be interpreted as symbolic of the legal and moral scrutiny women face in abortion debates, where personal agency conflicts with external control and societal norms. The novel frames Esther’s experiences within a broader cosmic and existential context, blurring lines between life and death, reality and dream, and individual and universal experiences. This deepens the symbolism, suggesting that the abortion debate is not simply a social or political issue but also a profound human and metaphysical dilemma about creation, destruction, and the meaning of existence. Esther Longtree may symbolize the abortion debate in the U.S., the detailed portrayal of her character’s perpetual pregnancies, miscarriages, societal judgment, and emotional complexity strongly suggests an allegorical reflection on the themes central to abortion discourse. Her story embodies the profound personal and societal conflicts surrounding reproductive rights, loss, and the nature of life itself.
