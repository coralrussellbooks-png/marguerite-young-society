"Exorbitance and Death," a critical essay by Steven Shaviro

← Previous · Young's Interviews Analyzed · Next →

See also: Marguerite Young, Our Darling, Part 6

A reading for the Miss MacIntosh project.

The source at a glance

This is "Exorbitance and Death: Marguerite Young's Vision" by Steven Shaviro, first published in the Review of Contemporary Fiction in 1989 and reprinted in the festschrift "Marguerite Young, Our Darling" (ed. Miriam Fuchs, 1994), pages 122 to 128. It is the companion to Shaviro's Critique essay "Lost Chords and Interrupted Births," which is paywalled and now hard to find, and the two share an argument, so this piece effectively delivers that reading: the phrases "lost chords" and "interrupted births" appear here as themes even though they title the other essay. It is the second sustained critical treatment in the set after the Fuchs essay, and the most philosophically ambitious, reading the novel through a Nietzschean and Deleuzian frame of excess, immanence, and death. It should be weighed as criticism, an argument about the book, not as evidence about Young's life.

Exorbitance: the novel as excess

Shaviro begins where every reader begins, in disorientation. Entering the novel there are "no guideposts," and the "hypertrophy of its language," the page-long sentences and infinite variations, leaves the reader with nothing solid to hold, exactly as it leaves Vera Cartwheel. He calls the book "a delirious phantasmagoria of memories that cannot be recaptured," "an epic lament of loss and disappointment." His turn, and the heart of his reading, is that this space of absence is not empty but full: "a space of profusion and excess," "outrageously hyperbolic," "a universe of plural affirmations." The novel produces "a strange vertigo," a sensory and epistemological overload, because there is simply "too much to read," a surplus he insists is absolute rather than merely quantitative. Reiteration becomes a principle of variation, any point of origin is erased, and the book stages "the interchangeability of beginning and end." It resists fixed categories because it is "pleonastic to the point of excess," so that the empty space of repetition becomes an inexhaustible reservoir of meaning. This is the "ex-orbitance" of his title, a text that expends itself to infinity, "an inverted circle whose circumference is everywhere, and whose center is nowhere."

Against the academy, and against the feminist reading

Shaviro argues that the novel foregrounds exactly what interpretation usually brackets, the material thickness of the words, and so produces what he calls a "pre-interpretive hysteria" located "on the part of the reader" rather than, in a pointed aside, "as has been carelessly maintained in certain recent discussions of women's fiction," on the part of the woman writer. This is a direct counter to the feminine-writing reading that Fuchs applies, relocating the hysteria from author to reader. He holds that the book "does not sit well in the academy," since academic criticism is built for modernism's allusiveness and self-reflexivity, which Young deploys in abundance, but is "much less able to deal with passion," and that "the (usually male) academic reader's critical distance," his mastery, is what the book threatens. The novel is therefore "decidedly anti-formalist," not self-reflexive or metafictional but expending itself outward into "the chaos of unresolved passions." By virtue of this exorbitance it marks "a mutation in the history of English-language modernism," one that cannot spawn an academic industry like Joyce or Nabokov but instead creates "a community of readers" bound by a common experience rather than a fraternity of scholars, a prediction the book's later cult following bore out.

Mistaken life and a pluralistic ontology

Shaviro reads the novel as one that "affirms, and even glorifies, 'mistaken life'" instead of trying to set it right, and he traces this to "Angel in the Forest," where Young writes that perfectionist orders would have excluded the mistaken life that is life itself. The book is "strewn with suicides," and the one American myth absent from this very American book is "the myth of success," but failure here is the positive consequence of passion, its refusal to compromise. He aligns this with Young's admiration for William James while pushing past pragmatism toward a Nietzschean sense that truth is only a particularly stubborn error, and a "delirious aestheticism" that celebrates multiple errors for their own sake. He notes that there is "no conversation, no dialogue" in the book, "only multiple monologues," because dialogue would imply common ground, and the novel is too radically pluralistic for even Bakhtinian dialogism; Vera can only accept the impossibility of resolution, finding not harmony but "the beautiful malignancy of the ocean." In this ontology the fantastic and the real collapse into each other, "a delusion, a hallucination, is an actual event," and even lost or despised realities are granted their own positivity, which he calls the novel's most impressive achievement.

Exorbitance and death

The essay's second key term is death. The novel "swarms with an incredibly rich and various life," yet is "utterly obsessed with death," which Shaviro calls "the superabundant void" that no profusion can fill, "the excess which inhabits all multiplicities" and the condition of every obsession. Here the Critique title surfaces as argument: "the chord that would complete our harmonies is always already lost," the lost chord, and yet even death is not conclusive, so there is "no final cadence" and "no return to silence." He gathers the book's deprivations, birth and the failure to be born or to give birth, blindness and deafness, lost loves and deluded ambitions, as the "interrupted births" of the companion title, the moments of trauma and transition that define experience. His close is affirmative: loss is "the necessary condition of beauty and exuberance," and Young's "exorbitant vision is in no way transcendent or otherworldly," offering instead a celebration of something "available to us, at every moment, in ordinary life."

Set against the other sources

This essay is the second critical anchor of the compilation and the sharpest counterweight to the Fuchs reading. Where Fuchs reads Miss MacIntosh as feminine writing in Cixous's sense, Shaviro explicitly moves the "hysteria" from the woman writer to the reader and reads the book instead as a Nietzschean, immanent celebration of mistaken life and a mutation in modernism that is neither modernist nor postmodernist. Placed beside Young's own insistence, in the Friedman and Fuchs interview, that she is a traditionalist of the Grand Tradition, the three positions map the whole critical debate about her: feminine avant-garde, exorbitant singularity, and traditional epic. Shaviro's reading also tracks Young's own words more closely than Fuchs's does, since his "mistaken life" and failed utopias are exactly the "paradise lost" and lost utopias she names as her single theme, and his emphasis on William James, on death, and on the pluralistic real echoes her own. Because it carries the argument of the paywalled Critique essay, including the lost chord and the interrupted birth, this piece closes the one gap that remained in the archive, and it should be the source cited for the philosophical, as opposed to the feminist, account of the novel's form.
