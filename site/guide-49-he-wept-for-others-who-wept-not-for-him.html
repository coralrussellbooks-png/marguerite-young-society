Chapter 49: He Wept for Others Who Wept Not for Him

← Chapter 48 · Guide Home · Chapter 50 →

Research notes: Chapter 49

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

Themes: Light and Darkness · Love · Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos

Symbols: Birds · Lamps, Candles, and Light · The House

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: American Writers · The Bible · Women’s Suffrage

Martha died on September 14, 1918, “at one o’clock in the afternoon,” at the Cincinnati Zoo. She was the last passenger pigeon on earth. The clock was tolling one as she died. Young records this with the precision of a monument inscription, which is what it is: the novel borrowing the language of memorial for a death that was also the end of a species, the closing of a door that had once darkened skies across the continent.

Mr. Spitzer weeps for Martha.

He is, by this point in the novel, a man who can weep for a pigeon dead before he was born (or at least, dead at the far edge of his life's possible span) while not weeping for himself, because weeping “for himself would seem an excess since he was already dead or dying to this world, this world” itself dying. He directs his sorrow outward, toward the extinct, toward all those the world has not wept for. “He wept for others who wept not for him.” This is his form of love. It is the only form available to him. It is also, Young makes clear, a form of profound loneliness.

What actually happens in Chapter 49

Chapter 49 is the deepest we have gone into Mr. Spitzer's interior: the chapter where Young gives us the full inventory of his condition: the shipwreck whose hull is broken, “paying out more water than the seas contained”; the living lighthouse seeing his own light only at the moment of dying; the man who knows not whether he is “himself or an image contained within an image.”

He moves through a frozen cosmos, ice formed into shapes of domes and eggs and “spires and minarets and bell towers,” a universe in suspension, waiting for something that does not come. He contemplates the paradox of his own existence: “he died at his zenith because he was at his nadir, died at his nadir because he was at his zenith.” He “died through sickness, died through health.” “Died through happiness which should have saved him, died through sorrow, sorrow by which he lived, died by not dying, died by living, lived by dying.”

He is, To All My Darlings notes, something like Schrödinger's cat: simultaneously alive and dead, the state unresolved until observed, the observation itself changing the state. He does not know which he is. He has stopped being certain anyone outside him knows either.

He mourns Martha. He mourns his brother. He mourns Cousin Hannah. He mourns everyone who has been extinct or forgotten or lost to the indifference of time. He is, as he has always been, the unofficial unelected representative of causes that were “never popular in any public sense”: a minority representing “a hidden majority like those sleeping in the churchyard.”

Martha and the weight of extinction

The passenger pigeon was once the most numerous bird in North America. Accounts from the nineteenth century describe flocks so vast they darkened the sky for hours, the sound of their wings like approaching weather, the weight of their roosting breaking tree branches. By 1914, Martha was the last. By the time her date appears in Chapter 49 (September 14, 1918, one o'clock) the species had been gone for four years, and the world had not particularly noticed.

Aldo Leopold, who would later write A Sand County Almanac, erected a monument to the passenger pigeon in 1947. Young would have known this monument and its inscription, which mourns not just the bird but the human capacity for annihilation: the ability to reduce something that once filled the sky to a single bird dying in a zoo cage, the clock tolling one.

Mr. Spitzer mourns Martha with the same intensity he mourns Cousin Hannah. Young is making something precise here: the extinction of a species and the death of a person are not the same scale of loss, but they are the same kind of loss. Both are irreversible. Both leave a silence that cannot be filled by substitution. Both represent the closing of a world that will not reopen. Martha is every Martha: every last thing, every final instance, every door that shuts for the last time as the clock is tolling one.

The shipwreck lighthouse

Young gives Mr. Spitzer two contradictory images in rapid succession: he is like a shipwreck, his hull broken, “paying out more water than the seas contained and” he is the living lighthouse, seeing his own light at the one moment when he is dying, his own light shining from him.

The shipwreck is disintegration: the structure that was built to float, now taking on more water than the ocean, overwhelmed from the inside by the thing that was supposed to be kept outside. Mr. Spitzer is flooding. His memories, his griefs, his panpsychist suffering, his attribution of consciousness to everything: the interior is now exceeding the container.

But the lighthouse, at the moment of his dying, sees its own light. This is extraordinary. A lighthouse does not normally see its own beam; it projects outward, for others, and is itself in the dark at the source. But Young gives Mr. Spitzer, in the moment of extinction, the simultaneous experience of being the light and seeing the light. He is both the source and the witness. For one moment, the sporadic, intermittent, second-remove consciousness achieves the simultaneous presence it has never been able to sustain in ordinary life.

This is what dying gives him. Not resolution, not peace, but for once, the complete experience of himself.

Trusting only the false

So he had come to trust only the false.

Why “should he trust the truth which had already failed him if the false would never fail” (if inconstancy was the only constancy, if the stone crumbled like a cloud) then trusting the false is not delusion but accurate accounting. It is the epistemological position of someone who has been paying attention.

Young makes this formulation with complete seriousness. Mr. Spitzer is not endorsing dishonesty. He is noting that the things that present themselves as stable (truth, constancy, the solid foundation) have repeatedly proved less reliable than the things that admit their instability from the start. The false is honest about being false. The uncertain is honest about being uncertain. The inconstant never pretends to be permanent. In a world where the stable crumbles and the permanent passes, trusting the false may be the most rational available option.

This is the epistemology of a man who has watched the suffrage captain die sobbing, who has seen his brother go before him into the dark, who has observed the most formidable person he knew reduced by death to a woman crying for her skirt. The stable things failed. The constant things changed. The stone crumbled. What remains is the honest admission of fragility, and the paradoxical reliability of things that never claimed to be solid.

An image within an image

“He knew not whether he was himself or an image contained within an image.”

This formulation has been building since the first chapter: the doubles, the twins, the possible identity substitution, the faces that are not quite what they seem. Now it arrives at its fullest expression: not just the uncertainty about which twin survived, but the more radical uncertainty about whether the self is an original or a copy of a copy of something that may itself have been a copy.

To All My Darlings compares this to Schrödinger's cat: the thought experiment in which a cat in a sealed box is simultaneously alive and dead until the box is opened and the state is observed. Mr. Spitzer is the cat. The box is his own consciousness. He cannot observe himself from outside himself, which means the state remains unresolved. He is both the man he thinks he is and the image of that man, and possibly also the image of his brother, and the image of the man his brother thought he was. Images within images, with no original that anyone can locate.

Young offers this not as a crisis exactly, Mr. Spitzer has been living inside this uncertainty for so long that it has become his habitual atmosphere, but as a description of the furthest reach of his condition. He is as fragmented as the novel itself, as multiple as Catherine, as displaced as Cousin Hannah's spirit wandering through posthumous geographies. He is, in his own modest and cautious way, as vast as any of them.

He could love only by indirection

One of the chapter's most important and least emphasized lines: he could love only by indirection.

Mr. Spitzer is drawn to those who have lost everything: the dispossessed, the wanderers through the storm of years, the hidden majority sleeping in the churchyard. He trusts them. He is nearest them in the city. They are all his loves. And the reason is that he cannot love directly: cannot look a person in the face and say I love you and have that be sufficient to the scale of what he feels, or sufficient to the distance between himself and everyone he has ever wanted to reach.

Loving by indirection is loving Martha the passenger pigeon. Loving by indirection is weeping for the extinct when you cannot weep for yourself. It is carrying the alpenhorn. It is keeping the secret. It is being the lamplighter of stars that no one asked you to tend. All of it is love, Young insists: the oblique form, the sideways approach, the love that does not announce itself because announcement is a mode of presence Mr. Spitzer cannot manage. It counts. She means for it to count.

The pigeon-colored dawn

“If there were no pigeons, could there be people or pigeon people or the pigeon-colored dawn, rose and blue and silver and gold mottled like the pigeon’s wing”?

Young's description of a dawn that is pigeon-colored (“rose and blue and silver and gold mottled,” variegated, the complex light of a sky that once held millions of them) is one of the novel's most beautiful ecological elegies. The passenger pigeon, in its extinction, has become the color of loss itself: the dawn that was once the backdrop for their flight, the light that fell on their wings, now a shade that has outlasted them. The dawn remembers the pigeons even though the pigeons are gone.

Mr. Spitzer has “lost all sense of the hour.” He cannot tell the dawn from the dusk, cannot locate himself in the day's progression. The pigeon-colored light (the light of before-dawn, before-loss, before-the-extinction) is the only time he recognizes, and it does not correspond to any hour on the clock.

What to hold onto

Chapter 49 is one of the novel's most spacious and most sorrowful chapters: the chapter where Mr. Spitzer is given, perhaps for the first time, the full scale of his own consciousness. He is simultaneously the shipwreck and the lighthouse. He is the cat in the box, both states unresolved. He is the mourner for everyone who has not been mourned, the unofficial representative of the causes the churchyard sleepers never got to argue for.

What to carry forward: Martha dying as the clock tolled one, the precision of that record, the finality of it, the way Young turns an ecological catastrophe into a philosophical emblem without diminishing either. The lighthouse seeing its own light at the moment of dying. And the love that can only be expressed by indirection, which is still love, which Young insists is still love, even when the lover is a shipwreck and the beloved is extinct and the dawn is the color of what has been lost.
