Movement Five: The Traditions the Novel Absorbs

Allusions: Gambling and Playing Cards · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · The History of Opium · Women's Suffrage

Definition from research

It is an epic of what might be called the Arabian Nights of American life.

Preface to Miss MacIntosh, My Darling

The Frame

The Arabian Nights begins with a premise that is also a death sentence: the sultan Shahryar, betrayed by his first wife, has taken to marrying a new woman each night and executing her in the morning before she can betray him. Scheherazade, the vizier's daughter, volunteers to marry him. Each night she tells him a story and stops at dawn before the story ends. He keeps her alive to hear the ending. She tells him another story the next night and stops again. She does this for one thousand and one nights. By the end, she survived. The sultan has been transformed by hearing. He does not execute her. The structure of survival is the structure of the telling: you live as long as you are still in the middle of the story.

The preface to Miss MacIntosh, My Darling names this directly. The novel is an epic of what might be called the Arabian Nights of American life. This is not a casual comparison. It is the identification of a structural model. The novel is organized as Scheherazade organizes her survival: by telling everything, by not stopping, by never reaching an ending that would allow the execution to proceed. The 1,198 pages are not a length problem to be edited down. They are the operational logic of the survival strategy. The telling keeps going because stopping the telling means death.

The Opium Lady as Scheherazade

Vera's mother entertains visitors. This is the novel's central ongoing situation, its recurring condition: the opium lady in her bedroom, receiving an endless procession of imaginary guests, keeping them coming through the night. The opium lady keeps herself alive through entertaining. When the drugs wear thin, she loses her guests. When the dreams thicken, the crowd returns. Sometimes a thousand roaring voices. Sometimes a thousand canaries blown here by the storm, singing in the candle flames.

This is Scheherazade's situation exactly, inside out. Scheherazade tells stories to keep the sultan from killing her. The opium lady keeps visitors coming to keep herself from the alternative: to give up these drugs, those which she had taken for her continued life on this star or another star. The drugs are the medium through which the guests arrive. To stop the drugs is to lose the visitors. To lose the visitors is to face the world as it is without the stories. The opium lady cannot do this. She does not stop the drugs. She keeps entertaining. She keeps the house full of the dead and the distinguished and the imaginary. The telling goes on for years. This is her survival.

Rimski-Korsakov's orchestral suite Scheherazade appears in the opium lady's bedroom visitor catalog, listed among the distinguished callers Mr. Spitzer has missed. Young places Scheherazade as music, as composition, in the exact location of the novel's survival-through-telling: the opium bedroom, the crowd of visitors, the nightly entertainment that keeps the inhabitant alive.

The Stories Within Stories

The Arabian Nights is a frame narrative: Scheherazade's story contains the stories she tells, which contain stories told by characters within them, which sometimes contain further stories. The depth is recursive. There is no bottom level. Every story opens into another story. The frame never fully encloses the content because the content keeps generating new frames.

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is structured the same way. Vera's narration frames Mr. Spitzer's narration of Vera's mother's history, which frames the stories of all the people in her mother's bedroom, who tell their own histories in the process of being entertained, whose histories open onto further histories: Cousin Hannah's suffrage campaigns, Mr. Spitzer's twin brother's gambling career, the bus driver's monologue, the landlord's drowning history, and the yacht voyage. Each story is nested inside another story. The novel does not proceed linearly from one story to the next. It nests. It recurses. It opens inward. The structure is the Arabian Nights structure: you are always already inside a frame, and the frame inside which you are already sitting is itself inside another frame. The preface offers the reader explicit instruction in how to navigate this: abandon one's self to the detours, wanderings, elliptical and tangential journeys, accepting in return miraculous surprises. This is advice about how to read a Scheherazade story. You do not read toward a resolution. You submit to the frame. You let the tangent take you where it goes. The digression is not a departure from the story. The digression is the story. Scheherazade's survival depends on the sultan's willingness to follow the tangent: if he ever reached the ending before dawn, she would die.

What Cheer as Arabian Nights

The preface calls the novel an epic of the Arabian Nights of American life. The phrase earns its paradox. The Arabian Nights is the literature of the exotic, the distant, the fantastical: Persian sultans, magic lamps, flying carpets, the city of Baghdad at its most imagined. The American life in this novel is Iowa, a bus journey through the flooded Midwest, a New England seaside house, a Boston lawyer in a black suit. The exotic and the plain American are the same material. The opium lady's bedroom is Baghdad and it is also New England. The bus is Sinbad's ship and it is also a Grey Goose running out of road. The novel's deepest formal claim is that the Arabian Nights structure does not require Arabia. It requires only the compulsion to keep telling, the threatened death that the telling defers, and the listener who cannot leave until the dawn comes.

The AND Philosophy's premise that serious ideas belong to everyone is enacted by this structural claim. Scheherazade's survival strategy is not exotic. It is the most democratic of structures: you have a story, you tell it, you do not stop, and this keeps you alive for one more night. Iowa has this. New England has this. The opium lady has this. The bus driver has this. The novel has this. The one thousand and one nights of the American interior are not lesser than the Persian original. They have the same formal requirement: keep talking until dawn, because dawn is when the execution happens, and it is always almost dawn.

The AND Philosophy Reading: Survival as Form

The Arabian Nights structure is the AND Philosophy's recursive construction pillar operating at the level of the novel's survival logic. The frame keeps generating new frames. The story keeps generating new stories. This is not structural weakness. It is the operational requirement of the survival strategy. You cannot stop at 400 pages because the sultan is still listening and the dawn has not come and the ending would be death. You continue. The continuation is the form. The form is the survival.

Scheherazade does not tell her stories to instruct the sultan. She tells them to stay alive. The instruction happens as a byproduct of the survival: the sultan is transformed by a thousand and one nights of hearing, by the accumulated weight of all the stories, by the experience of being inside a frame that keeps opening inward. He does not execute her because he has become, through the telling, a different man. This is what the AND Philosophy claims for its readers. The transformation is not the goal. The continuation is the goal. The transformation is what the continuation produces when you give it enough time, enough frames, enough stories nested inside stories nested inside stories, enough nights just before dawn.
