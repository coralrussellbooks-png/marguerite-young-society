Chapter 15

On Chapter 15 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Woman in Young's Magnum Opus.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

On her fourteenth birthday Vera walks along the sea, and the sea is the chapter's whole argument. Its dissolving boundary between sky and water, its wreckage and lost statues, its upheaval, is the real and the dream as one country made elemental, the place where fixed things come apart. Vera embraces it. She wants the imperfection that leads to "higher perfection," she loses her fear of the "outer darkness," and she gives in to the pull of the water.

Miss MacIntosh cannot follow her there. She cannot swim, she bundles against the cold, she keeps clear of the "greatest waves," and the narrator reads in her a "coldness of heart," a "deadness." She is the pole that resists dissolution, and her refusal of the sea is the setup for the day she will walk straight into it. Her austere room holds the tell: an abandoned dressing table, a woman who will not look in her mirror, "shades of death" already on her. The refusal to meet her own image is the body and the self coming apart held just out of sight, the face she will not look at the same one whose wig and baldness the book is preparing to reveal.

Miss MacIntosh’s counsel is bleak and against the sorting. Death is a "bridegroom," marriage a "padlock," men "poor, forked animals," and "no man should marry woman." Vera answers it with a "secret dream of the false wedding" to Mr. Spitzer, a surreal ceremony attended by natural and supernatural forces, the crossing point returning as a dream, the wedding to a dissolving man staged again in the mind that most wants the fixed to give way. The old blind sheep dog barks at the waves at the chapter's edge, a severed sense set against the dissolving sea, and the two together are the book in miniature.
