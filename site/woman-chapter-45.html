Chapter 45

On Chapter 45 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Woman in Young's Magnum Opus.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

The woman the record cannot hold returns, and her unreachability is the pillar. The suffragette is a "great wanderer" who never answered his knocks or letters, her "empty house" the barren landscape of a life that would not be pinned to marriage or memory, and Mr. Spitzer's craving for certainty meets only her ambiguity. "Love's image was that which was never reached by love" turns the unattainable into the chapter's law, and her tears for a "clouded mirror," her refusal to reproduce herself, are a woman declining the reproductive destiny the world assigns her.

Her secret dual and triple life is the plural, contested selfhood the novel prizes, and Mr. Spitzer's disbelief that she was "no one in whom one should place his already shattered faith" is patriarchy's failure to hold a woman who lives past its categories. The volumes of lovelorn letters she never answered make her a repository of human longing she cannot or will not satisfy, "brides even when there were no loves," and the image names the illusions of marriage and the solitude of the woman who refuses them.

Beneath the deathbed visions runs the fracture of female solidarity. Her broken "eternal troth," the abandoned "girl's hand," and her contradictory claim to have both let go and been abandoned, are the personal costs of a life given to a public cause, loyalty strained by commitment. Her wandering through deserts and mountains, her message that "he who loves shall lose himself in love as in death," carries feminism's internationalism and its knowledge that love and loss are one motion. The chapter closes on mountains, snow, and the "roaring sea shell," the harsh and mysterious terrain of a woman's life, with Mr. Spitzer the silent mourner who could not reach her while she lived, and the record still open where she should have been kept.
