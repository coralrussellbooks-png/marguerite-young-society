Music, Sound, and Silence

Part of To All My Darlings, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).

Definition from research

Chapter 13 · The Garden of the Deaf

  • Vera wanders gardens where, in Catherine’s visions, historical and literary figures stroll at a perpetual garden party, a symbol of the mind’s landscape where past and present, life and death intermingle.
  • The garden is where the “ear is dead,” meaning silence reigns and sound is absent, which symbolizes isolation, the limits of communication, and the finality of death.
  • The dialogue between Catherine, who insists on the silence, and Mr. Spitzer, who argues against it yet cannot escape it, dramatizes the struggle to find meaning or connection in a silent, soundless world.

Chapter 13 · Life, Death, and Silence

  • The chapter frequently reflects on mortality and the disappearance of sound, which metaphorically represents the death of communication and presence.
  • The silence is described as pervasive and absolute, underscoring the theme of existential isolation and the ineffability of certain experiences.

Chapter 13

  • “his music was the silence”: John Cage’s 4′33″ (1952) is a piece of silence, in which the audience hears only the sounds of the room. Mr. Spitzer has written “only silent music” (Chapter 2).

Chapter 24 · Isolation and Communication

  • Catherine attempts to communicate with distant or deceased figures through a telephone that only connects to the servants’ quarters, symbolizing her isolation and failed attempts at meaningful contact.
  • This motif underscores the theme of disconnection between Catherine’s inner world and external reality.

Chapter 25

  • “those heavenly harmonies”: The push and pull between silent music and the music of the spheres or harmonious music form much of Mr. Spitzer’s tale, particularly in light of Schopenhauer’s view of music as the highest form of art. The philosophy of silence in music is that silence can be a powerful tool for making a statement, conveying emotion, and organizing structure. The novel embodies Schopenhauer’s belief in music as the highest art through its intricate portrayal of Mr. Spitzer’s silent and profound music, the use of musical metaphors to explore existential themes, and the depiction of music’s transcendence beyond the physical and intellectual world. The novel’s deep engagement with music as both expression and mystery reflects the Schopenhauerian vision of music as the direct and ultimate language of the will. Schopenhauer sets this out in The World as Will and Representation (1818), Book 3, where music, unlike the other arts, is a copy of the will itself.

Chapter 25 · Music and Silence

  • Mr. Spitzer is an ex-musician; music (or the lack thereof) symbolizes harmony, loss, and silent elegy.
  • The “cryptic silence” represents unfulfilled potential and the ineffable nature of existence.

Chapter 26

  • “only his elegiac music”: An elegy is a song or poem of mourning. Mr. Spitzer’s “elegiac music” is music he has written “only for himself or for no mortal ear.”
  • “the heavenly harmonies forever fixed above the floods of time, dark time, those unmoving golden spheres”: “Musica universalis,” the music of the spheres: Boethius, in De institutione musica (c. 500), divided music into musica mundana (the music of the spheres), humana, and instrumentalis. The idea goes back to Pythagoras. The “Musica Universalis” here symbolizes the harmonious and eternal cosmic order that underlies the apparent chaos of mortal life and death. It is depicted as a sublime, transcendent music that governs the movements of the stars and planets, reflecting a divine harmony beyond human perception. Mr. Spitzer, a semi-retired lawyer and composer of “silent music,” embodies this principle as someone who is attuned to the “music of the spheres” or the eternal, silent harmonies that exist beyond earthly sounds. His compositions, unheard by living ears and played only for the dead or in the celestial realm, metaphorically represent this cosmic music. The chapter emphasizes the contrast between earthly, temporal music and this higher, immutable harmony, suggesting that the tangible world is but a fragment or shadow of a greater metaphysical reality. Catherine’s opium dreams expand this theme by blurring the boundaries between the real and the illusory, the living and the dead, reflecting the ineffable nature of the universal music that ties all existence together. The “Musica Universalis” is used to articulate a metaphysical principle that all existence, from the smallest earthly details to the vast cosmos, is bound by a harmonious, eternal music. This music transcends the limitations of physical reality and human understanding, offering a vision of unity amid fragmentation and a cosmic order beneath the apparent chaos of life and death.

Chapter 27 · Music and Harmony

  • Mr. Spitzer finds solace in musical metaphors: the "Pythagorean harmonies," "music of the silent spheres," and the discordant experiences of life.
  • Music represents both order and chaos, reflecting the tension in Mr. Spitzer’s internal state.

Chapter 33 · Music and Sound

  • Mr. Spitzer’s harp music inspired by wind and bridge cables symbolizes the blending of natural forces and artistic creation.
  • Music serves as a metaphor for the ephemeral, invisible forces shaping human experience and memory.

Chapter 40 · Language and Communication

  • Language is a major motif: Cousin Hannah’s polyglot abilities symbolize the bridge between past and present, life and death.
  • Vera’s mother understands languages not through formal study but through mystical or dreamlike insight.
  • The narrative suggests some languages transcend spoken word, being part of an ineffable, universal communication.

Chapter 41 · The Ear as a Metaphor for Consciousness and Entrapment

  • Catherine describes the ear as a vast, labyrinthine continent, a "doorway to the soul," where she is lost and wanders endlessly.
  • The ear symbolizes the inner world of sound, memory, and consciousness, filled with echoes, voices, and spectral presences.
  • She pleads to be let out of her ear, symbolizing a desire to escape mental imprisonment.

Chapter 46 · Music and Silence

  • Mr. Spitzer, a musician, reflects on music as a metaphor for life and death, where silence and sound intermingle. Music arises from chaos and holds a kind of ephemeral power, much like life itself.

Chapter 47 · The Role of Music and Silence

  • Music is a metaphor for life and memory, incomplete and elusive, containing unplayed notes and unuttered words.
  • Silence and the “music of the dead” symbolize absence, loss, and the unspeakable aspects of death and grief.

Chapter 48 · Memory, Lost Meaning, and the Music of Existence

  • The chapter frequently references music as a metaphor for life’s meaning and its transient, often elusive nature. Missing notes, echoes, and lost music symbolize forgotten memories or lost meaning in life and death.
  • The idea that the greatest music might be that which has no clear meaning, symbolizing the ineffability of existence.

Chapter 51 · The Role of the Lawyer and the Musician

  • Mr. Spitzer’s profession as a lawyer handling wills and testaments serves as a metaphor for dealing with endings, legacies, and the passage from life to death.
  • He is also a musician, but one whose music is silent, unplayed, or forgotten, symbolizing unrealized potential and the fragmentation of harmony in life.
  • The blending of these roles points to the complex interplay between order (law) and chaos (music, dreams).

Chapter 54 · Music and Creation

  • Music symbolizes order, creativity, and the attempt to impose meaning. Mr. Spitzer is described as a composer of life’s elegy, writing fragments of music on his clothing, representing his fragmented perception and desperate attempt to create coherence.

Chapter 59 · Music as Metaphor

  • Music recurs as a symbol of life’s ephemeral beauty and the fleeting nature of existence.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s compositions, based on patterns of migratory birds or billiard balls, represent the unpredictability and chance in life.
  • The music of the dead and the living intertwine, suggesting a continuum beyond mortality.

Chapter 60 · Music and Sound as Metaphor

  • Music is portrayed as a transcendent, intellectual force that shapes reality and the imagination.
  • The "music of the world" and the "music of the track" symbolize different aspects of life, artistic creativity versus raw, primal existence.
  • Mr. Spitzer reflects on musical composition, the clef, bar, and rhythm as pathways dividing sound from chaos, highlighting sound as a spiritual and intellectual phenomenon.

Chapter 60 · Silence and Sound

  • Silence is portrayed as unimaginable to the dead brother, emphasizing the importance of sound as life and presence.
  • Sound and silence share a complex relationship, where sound moves “like the human spirit through heavens of imagined silence,” suggesting spiritual continuity beyond death.

Chapter 61 · Silence and Communication

  • The mute’s silence contrasts with Mr. Spitzer’s music, which ironically is unheard.
  • The frog’s voice bridges silence and speech but is ephemeral, highlighting the fragility of communication.
  • Sign language and gestures represent a form of expression beyond spoken words, filled with deep ambiguities and mysteries.

Chapter 61 · Music and Sound vs. Silence

  • Mr. Spitzer’s music is described as a physiological act yet deeply meaningful to him, though unheard by others and especially the mute.
  • Silence is given primacy over sound; silence is eternal and encompassing.

Chapter 82 · Silence and Communication

  • Extensive meditation on deafness, silence, and the limitations and beauty of communication beyond words. The stone deaf man represents the world of silence yet full of inner sound and love.
