Walter: The Named Child

Part of Teaching a Writing Masterclass, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Rosemary Longtree · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Joe Goldberg →

Spoiler note: This portrait is written for readers who have finished the novel. It discusses the character’s whole story, including how it ends.

Characters: Esther Longtree · Rosemary · Vera Cartwheel · Walter

Of all Esther Longtree's stillborn, the eye, the ear, "a long gold hair, a fingernail, a cobweb, nothing," only one has a name, and having a name he has everything the others lack: a voice, a face, a growing body, a red muffler, "his little aviator's goggles, his little boxing gloves, his red sweater," and a reproach. "Walter," she tells Vera, lowering her voice, "was my first-born. He is alive." He was "not bigger than her hand." She "left him in a far-away place, left him as dead," and heard him cry once as she turned away, and hears him now everywhere. The others fell from her as pieces and were buried unmourned. Walter she named, and the name would not let him die.

This is the book's clearest lesson about what naming does, and it is the philosophy's ninth pillar made flesh: language is where a thing gets made real, where it becomes a person who counts. The unnamed stillborn do not officially exist, snowflakes and cobwebs that fall away. Walter exists. Because she said the word, he has an age, or rather all the ages at once, "nine years old," "ten years old," "twelve years old," with a gap where "the eleventh year" went missing in her sieve of a mind. Because she named him, he can speak, and what he says is unbearable. "Mother, why did you kill me?" "Mother, you have led a wicked life." "Mother, you will never find me." The named child is the one who can turn around and accuse.

He is also black, and that is not incidental. "My skin is black, and the snow is white, mother, and I am killed." He is her "black mystery whom the white detective will not find," "black as my true love, black as my heart," the child of a father who "was no salesman," hidden away, "being cared for by nice people in another county, by white people." He is the buried thing the town would judge her for, the loss that carries both her shame and the world's, the child un-findable precisely because he is a black child in a white landscape, with no place in the law. He is the past pressing up from under, the deposit in the ground that will not stay down, and the philosophy's eighth pillar is written across him: the past is not behind you, it is beneath you, and it presses.

He is her reason to live, and the book says so flatly: "This memory was what caused Esther Longtree to live." "Only for him do I live now." He is "the beloved obstacle." She keeps having children in order to have him back, or to have one that will not accuse her, and none of them is Walter, "he was not Walter, though." She calls into the dark for him to wrap his muffler against the cold, to not catch his death, a mother tending a boy who is either lost in another county or lost in the ground or never was, and she cannot tell which, and neither can the book, and it refuses to settle it. "Could not have killed him, because the murder was not finished, he is alive and comes to tell me so." His asking why she killed him is her proof that he lives.

The turn for a writer is the one that cuts closest to the act itself, because Walter is the named creation, the first one, the thing you called into being with a word and therefore made real enough to leave you, real enough to grow on its own, real enough to come back and ask why you killed it. The unnamed pieces cost nothing and stay buried. The named one has a life you did not authorize, ages you cannot account for, a voice that reproaches the maker and then stops its ears when she comes near. To name a thing is to lose control of it. It is also to become responsible for it forever, to live for it, to call into the dark after it. Set him beside Rosemary, who was never named and never lived and stays perfect, and Walter is the opposite kind of ghost, named and flawed and alive and accusing and gone. He is what the writer made and abandoned "in a far-away place," the one work she gave a name and a life and could not keep, and it followed her, and it grew, and it will forgive her, she knows, too late, when she is where he is.
