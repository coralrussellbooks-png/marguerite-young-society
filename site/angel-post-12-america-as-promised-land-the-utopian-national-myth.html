Chapter 12. America as Promised Land: The Utopian National Myth

← Chapter 11 · Angel in the Forest: A Reading in Fourteen Chapters · Chapter 13 →

The question at the center of the book is one Young mostly leaves for the reader to notice. Why here? Why did a German peasant and an English cotton lord, two men who agreed on almost nothing, both cross an ocean to build their perfect worlds on the same muddy bend of the Wabash in Indiana? They were not drawn by the soil, which bred malaria, or by the river, which was unnavigable half the year. They were drawn by an idea about the place, and the idea was older and larger than either of their utopias. America was the country where the past had been left behind, where the ground was clean, where a person or a people could start over from nothing. Young titles a chapter “America, the Promised Land,” and the book is, underneath its two failed communities, an examination of that phrase and what it costs.

The clean start is the American myth in its purest form, and it is exactly the lie that the eighth pillar of Radical Immanence exists to refuse. The past does not go away. It does not stay behind you. It accumulates beneath you, in the ground, in the arrangements of the present, in the bodies of the people the story stepped over. New Harmony looked, to Owen and to Rapp, like neutral ground, a blank slate on which a better human nature could be written. Young keeps showing you that the ground was never blank. Under the nameless graves of the Rappites, she notes, lay an Indian burial ground, its warriors and squaws and papooses and pottery and beads, one layer down. The Rappites in their gray gowns were only one layer above the people whose land it had been. The promised land was promised to the newcomers by erasing who had been promised it before. The clean start was a burial.

The two faces of one promise

The two utopias are the sacred and the secular versions of the same clean start. Rapp offered a scriptural redesign: leave the corrupt Old World, cross the water like the Israelites, and wait in the wilderness for the New Jerusalem. Owen offered a rational redesign: leave the superstitions of the past, apply the science of society, and manufacture a better human nature the way you would order a better cloth. Young quotes Owen turning to his son in the rose garden and proposing, “as casually as if he were ordering a fabric at the cotton mills,” that they should “manufacture a better human nature.” One promise wore Scripture and one wore science, but both promised the same thing, which is the thing America itself promised: that you could be made new by moving to a place where the past did not apply. Between them, the peasant and the cotton lord cover the whole of American faith.

Robert Dale Owen, from utopia to empire

The clearest single demonstration of where this promise leads is the life of Robert Dale Owen, and Young’s handling of it is one of the quietest, sharpest things in the book. He begins as a New Harmony radical, a young man who came to Indiana partly for utopia and partly for love of a mill girl named Jessie, and who believed in birth control, the unreality of God, liberation from the fear of death, and votes for women. Then the community fails, and he stays in America, and he goes into politics. He becomes an Indiana state senator, then a congressman. He lends his rhetoric to real and admirable causes, free public schools and the property rights of women. And then the same gift for the language of promise carries him somewhere else. He stands for the annexation of Texas. When the Mexican War comes, he takes the apologist’s line, that America had been invaded and the Mexicans deserved punishment for their crime. The rhetoric that once described a New Moral World now describes 918,000 square miles of “virgin territory” waiting to be taken.

Young does not call this a betrayal, and that restraint is the point. She treats it as the ordinary behavior of a plural person moving through time, a man who changed his mind, drew in his horns, and was, in her words, “merely human.” But she also lets you see, without underlining it, what has happened. The vocabulary of utopia and the vocabulary of empire turn out to be the same vocabulary. The “land of promise” becomes the “land of destination.” The dream of a clean start for everyone becomes the justification for taking the continent from the people already on it. Robert Dale did not abandon the promise. He followed it to its end.

The most consequential fiction

This is where the second pillar meets the eighth. Reality is built, and whoever gets to build the shared version of it holds enormous power. “The promised land” is not a description of North America. It is a construction, laid over the actual continent with its actual prior inhabitants, and it is arguably the most consequential constructed reality in the book, more consequential than either utopia, because it outlived them both and is with us still. Young sees this clearly. Right beside her question about experience she sets the answer: man is “the creator of those values by which he lives” and perhaps dies, and they are “not handed down from heaven, usually.” The promised land was created by people, here, and then treated as if it had been handed down. That treatment is the trick. A made thing gets presented as a given thing, and once it is a given, no one has to ask who built it or who paid for it.

The Devil is delighted

Young lets the deepest irony sit almost entirely without comment, in the form of a poem. When Owen’s community was starting, a piece of doggerel circulated in Philadelphia against it, and Young reprints it. In the poem, the Devil scrambles up out of a hole at the North Pole, hears that people are going to live on the Wabash with Mr. Owen, and rushes to be there. He is delighted. He has founded inquisitions and set sects to shedding blood and made kings swallow his lies, but he has never, in all his wandering, hit on a plan like this one, a community that believes men are held entirely by “power of circumstance” and owe no allegiance to heaven or to him. “This plan of free living beats all my schemes hollow,” the Devil says. “Here’s a plan beats the Devil.” Then he hurries home to enlarge his dominions.

What the poem is doing, and what it means is that Owen’s community was built, in part, on the rejection of irrational religion, on the refusal of Devil and Hell and holy lies. And here is the popular imagination of 1826 answering back: the community that rejects the Devil is the Devil’s masterpiece. To the pious American who wrote the poem, a world run purely on circumstance, with no God and no Devil, was not the absence of the Devil’s work. It was its perfection. Young does not tell you what to make of this. She lets the two readings stand in the same room, the utopia that thought it had banished the Devil and the Devil who welcomes it as his finest achievement, and lets you feel the vertigo of not being able to say which one is right.

New Harmony is America in miniature

That is why the book has not aged. The two utopias are gone, filling stations and dolls under glass where the angels and the machines used to be. But the promise that drew them, the promise of neutral ground and a clean start, is not gone. It is the national myth still, and the questions the book keeps alive are the ones worth carrying out of it. Who benefits from calling the ground neutral? Whose past has to be treated as over, as buried, as one layer down beneath the new graves, for the clean start to look clean? Young does not answer these questions for you, because answering them would be its own kind of clean resolution, and she does not believe in those. She only makes sure you cannot stop asking them. The promised land is still being promised. The Indian burial ground is still one layer down.
