To All My Darlings

Chapter 58

On Chapter 58 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 11, 2026

← Chapter 57 · Home · Chapter 59 →

Summary

On the Library steps Mr. Spitzer meets an old violinist with a wounded chin and a wild white mane who plays an imaginary violin, and he recognizes the soundless tune at once: a forgotten waltz by a minor nineteenth-century composer, remembered from his childhood. Watching, he decides that the player might have been anyone with a different stick in his hand, batter, billiard player, shepherd, judge, fisherman, king, or conductor, for reality is made of just so many sticks. The music turns his thoughts to Peron, to the broken old sportsmen Peron befriended, and to Peron’s fatal leap. He fears the old man is having a fit or is already dead, but is afraid to applaud or offer money. He has come to distinguish less and less between reality and his dreams, and suspects that this may be his only wisdom.

Key passages

From the research

  • Mr. Spitzer watches an old violinist who plays invisible notes on an imaginary violin. Despite the silence, Mr. Spitzer “knew immediately what music he was playing” and recognized it as an obscure minor composition from childhood, something almost lost to modern ears. This passage establishes the mystical and imaginative nature of the music. It exists purely in the mind and memory, evoking nostalgia for a past that is fading. The description of the old musician’s expert emotional gestures and Mr. Spitzer’s empathetic response shows a deep connection between memory, art, and the ineffable experience of music beyond sound.
  • The narrative elaborates on the qualities of the music and the sounds of nature, city life, and ephemeral sensations woven seamlessly into the violinist’s silent performance. Mr. Spitzer experiences a profound detachment from his corporeal self and is deeply moved, his soul almost slipping from his body. The music is described as “pre-musical music,” existing before human consciousness or after it no longer perceives sound, suggesting a timeless, transcendent art form. This passage highlights the boundary between reality and imagination, sound and silence, life and death.
  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates how the old violinist might have been many other figures (an athlete, a gentleman, a fisherman, a king) if only the instruments and tools in his hands had changed. This reflection suggests the fluidity of reality and identity, how reality is “made up of just so many sticks and not one stick more.” The passage explores the transformative power of imagination and the interchangeable roles people might play, blurring boundaries between art, sport, and life.
  • Mr. Spitzer recalls his brother and old sportsmen, tying these memories to ideas of death, legacy, and continuing influence. This section reveals his obsession with memory and how past lives and experiences persist beyond physical death, transforming but never disappearing. It also contrasts Mr. Spitzer’s sensitivity to silent, subtle music with Peron’s preference for the rougher, more physical world of sports, highlighting differing responses to life’s challenges and art.
  • Mr. Spitzer questions whether the old musician is alive or dead, a dream or reality, reflecting on the ambiguous boundary between life and death. The violinist’s wild, convulsive movements are seen both as music’s highest expression and possibly a sign of illness or death. This ambiguity deepens the chapter’s themes of mortality, the unknowability of existence, and the persistence of art and spirit beyond physical life.
  • Mr. Spitzer reflects on himself as a “silent composer” and “walking score board,” someone who carries music and memory within but whose music is intangible and largely unheard. This passage underscores his isolation and the internal nature of artistic creation, the idea that much of life’s music is invisible or unperceived by others but deeply real to the creator.
  • The chapter closes with Mr. Spitzer’s reflection that he no longer distinguishes clearly between reality and dreams, life and death. This indistinction, while sometimes embarrassing, is also his “only wisdom,” a protective cloud through which he moves. The metaphor of his black cape and drifting shadow emphasizes his liminal state between worlds, carrying the darkness as a cloak.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • Mr. Spitzer “knew immediately what music he was playing, soundless though it was.” How can he recognize music he cannot hear?
  • “Reality was made up of just so many sticks and not one stick more.” What does the list of sticks show about identity?
  • Mr. Spitzer’s “admiration would always be his pity—and his pity, his admiration.” How do pity and admiration mix in his response to the violinist?

Characters

  • Who might the old violinist be, a derelict, a ghost, or a part of Mr. Spitzer himself?
  • Peron preferred “the unwritten music of the turf.” How do the two brothers’ kinds of music differ?
  • Mr. Spitzer is afraid to applaud “to interrupt the silence with a sound.” What does his silence protect?

Connections

  • The violinist plays “pre-musical music.” How does this chapter carry on the silent and theoretical music of Chapter 55?
  • Mr. Spitzer wonders whether “the dead man lived and dreamed.” Where else in the novel does Young blur the living and the dead?
  • For Mr. Spitzer, “a problem of existence was not illuminated—it was darkened by elucidation.” How does this sentence describe the way the novel itself works?

Reflection

  • Can music that is never heard still be music?
  • Mr. Spitzer finds that “if he waited long enough, most people would answer the questions he did not ask.” Is waiting a kind of wisdom?
  • His confusion of dream and reality “was perhaps also his only wisdom.” When can not knowing protect a person?

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • Mr. Spitzer is a large, reflective man, draped in a black cape, who is a silent composer and connoisseur of music. He is caught between life and death, reality and imagination. His deafness contrasts with his profound musical understanding, highlighting a spiritual or metaphysical connection to music beyond sound. He embodies melancholy, wisdom, and a deep sense of loss, especially connected to his dead brother and the passing of time.

The Old Violinist

  • The Old Violinist is an enigmatic figure with white hair and shaking fingers, who plays silent music on an imaginary violin. His physical condition suggests age and wear, but his music transcends physical limitations, becoming a symbol for the persistence of art and spirit beyond bodily decay. He may represent forgotten or marginalized artists, or even symbolize the border between life and death.

Research: Themes

Silent Music

See: Music, Sound, and Silence

  • Music in this chapter exists beyond audible sound. The old violinist plays silent, imaginary music that Mr. Spitzer “hears” through intense observation and imagination. This silent music is described with rich auditory imagery (whispers, sobs, bird calls, and natural sounds) suggesting music as a universal language transcending physical constraints.

Memory and the Past

See: Memory and the Past

  • Mr. Spitzer’s reflections evoke buried childhood memories and a past nearly lost to time. His thoughts on his dead brother and old sportsmen convey a deep sense of mourning and nostalgia. The music, described as an old, nearly forgotten waltz, symbolizes this connection to the past and the fading of cultural memory.

Life and Death

See: Death and Mortality

  • The chapter blurs the lines between living and dead. Mr. Spitzer contemplates whether the old violinist is alive, dying, or already dead, and imagines music existing before consciousness and after hearing ceases. The text wrestles with existence, identity, and what remains after physical death.

Art, Sport, and Identity

See: Identity and the Self

  • Mr. Spitzer draws analogies between music and sports, imagining the violinist as a baseball player, a billiards player, or other figures, emphasizing how art and life are intertwined and fluid. Reality itself is depicted as made from “sticks” or tools, with identity shaped by perspective and imagination.

Pity and Admiration

  • Mr. Spitzer admires the violinist with a mingled feeling of pity, reflecting on the loneliness of the artist and the fragility of human endeavor. This ambivalence highlights the complex human response to suffering and greatness.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

The Imaginary Violin

See: Music, Sound, and Silence

  • The violinist’s “imaginary bow” and “imaginary strings” represent art beyond the physical realm, emphasizing creativity as a spiritual act. The music is described with natural and cosmic imagery: “singings of blind moths,” “bird calls,” “sea shells buzzing like bees,” and “sparks sparking on the strings.”

The Black Cape

See: Clothing, Hats, and Skirts

  • Mr. Spitzer’s attire symbolizes his somber, reflective nature and his boundary state between darkness and light, life and death.

The Hour-Glass of the Heart

  • The hour-glass of the heart is a metaphor for the passage of time and the reversal or renewal of life, connected to the emotional impact of the music.

Sticks, Wands, and Games

See: Cards, Games, and Chance

  • Baseball, billiards, croquet, polo, fishing, and even kingship are used metaphorically to describe potential alternate lives or identities for the violinist, highlighting the fluidity of reality and identity.

Sky and Clouds

  • Frequent references to the sky, clouds, stars, and celestial bodies evoke a cosmic scale and the mystery of existence.

Research: Resources

  • “his white Steinkirk drifting to his feet”: See the Steinkirk scarf in the Chapter 56 resources.
  • “his pearl-buttoned spats”: Pearl-buttoned spats were a popular, elegant fashion accessory for both men and women from the late 19th century to the 1920s. Originally designed to protect footwear from dirt on unpaved roads, they became a symbol of social status and high fashion, especially during the Victorian and Edwardian eras. Spats faded in the late 1920s, as paved streets and motorcars made them unnecessary and men’s dress grew more casual. See On pearl-buttoned spats and Clothing, Hats, and Skirts.
  • “his head turned permanently to one side as if he still carried a box under his chin—which was abraded”: Young’s wound is real. Violinists and violists develop “fiddler’s neck,” a reddened, sometimes broken patch of skin under the left jaw where the instrument presses.
  • “This was not the angel singing in the whirlwind”: Compare Joseph Addison’s angel who “Rides in the whirlwind, and directs the storm” (“The Campaign,” 1705).
  • “This was not the singing candle flame”: A flame can sing. In 1777 Bryan Higgins found that a hydrogen flame inside a glass tube gives out a steady note, and Pieter Rijke made a tube sing over heated gauze in 1859.
  • “a minor composition by one of the lesser-known nineteenth-century composers”: Young names no piece; the waltz is lost, remembered only by Mr. Spitzer. See On the minor composition and Music and Composers.
  • “embalmed in old music boxes”: The Geneva watchmaker Antoine Favre made the first comb-and-pin music movement in 1796, and Swiss cylinder music boxes carried the waltzes and opera airs of the nineteenth century into parlors everywhere. See Music and Composers.
  • “the old bellwether jumping over the cliff”: The bellwether is the belled ram that leads the flock. In Rabelais’s Pantagruel, Panurge throws the leading ram overboard and the whole flock follows it into the sea (Book 4, ch. 8, 1552).
  • “weavings of the threads of sound upon the loom of time”: In Goethe’s Faust, Part One (1808), the Earth Spirit weaves the living garment of God at the roaring loom of Time.
  • “such as should have awakened the deaf and caused the dust to stir”: In Greek mythology, Orpheus was a Thracian bard, legendary musician and prophet. He was also a renowned poet and, according to legend, travelled with Jason and the Argonauts in search of the Golden Fleece, and descended into the underworld to recover his lost wife, Eurydice. His music charmed beasts, trees, and stones. See Greek Mythology.
    • “Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf shall be unstopped” (Isaiah 35:5, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “sea shells buzzing like bees”: The sea heard in a shell is the noise of the room around it, echoing in the shell’s hollow, which sounds some pitches more than others. See The Sea and Water.
  • “sound of the sand moving”: Some dunes really sing. Booming sands give out a low hum or roar when sand slides down their faces; Marco Polo heard of desert spirits making music in the sands of the Lop desert.
  • “pre-musical music”: The earliest musical instruments demonstrate that early humans possessed abstract thought, symbolic expression, and sophisticated cognitive and social skills. These instruments, made from bone, shell, and ivory and dating back over 40,000 years, show that music played a crucial role in rituals, communication, and social bonding, helping to unite communities, convey spiritual beliefs, and even coordinate collective work or warfare. The existence of these complex tools indicates that early humans were innovators, capable of abstract thought and creating objects with symbolic and functional meaning beyond mere survival needs. See On pre-musical music and Music, Sound, and Silence.
  • “this infant crying in the dark”: See Tennyson’s “infant crying in the night” in the Chapter 53 resources. See Romantic Poets.
  • “prided himself that music and baseball were fused in his psychology”: Young also writes of his “intuitive understanding of this relation of music to sports.” Music and baseball (sports) are similar in their shared need for practice, discipline, teamwork, and performance. Both fields involve individual roles within a collective, rely on rhythm and coordination, cultivate passionate fanbases, and have rich, evolving traditions and narratives that contribute to their cultural significance.
  • “knew a one-handed pianist”: Compare Paul Wittgenstein, brother of the philosopher Ludwig, a concert pianist who lost his right arm in the First World War and went on playing with his left; Ravel wrote his Piano Concerto for the Left Hand (1930) for him. See Wittgenstein.
  • “shepherd, if his cane had been a staff”: “Thy rod and thy staff they comfort me” (Psalm 23:4, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “a croquet player knocking his ball through the last wicket”: At the Queen’s croquet ground in Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland (1865), the mallets are flamingos and the balls hedgehogs, and the soldiers bend double to make the arches. See Alice in Wonderland.
  • “a polo player furiously riding with his mallet”: See Isfahan’s polo square in the Chapter 56 resources.
  • “fishing for lost souls”: “Follow me, and I will make you fishers of men” (Matthew 4:19, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “the master of the mace”: The mace, once a war club, became a symbol of authority; the Serjeant-at-Arms carries it in the House of Commons, as the Sergeant at Arms does in the United States House of Representatives.
  • “the old King of Cracow under the railway bridge”: See Krakus, king of Cracow, in the Chapter 54 resources.
  • “the lacrosse player’s crosse or the flowering crosier, perhaps flowering with hawthorn”: Lacrosse grew from games played by the Haudenosaunee and other nations; French missionaries named it for the stick, which looked to them like a bishop’s crosier.
    • A flowering staff is an old sign of grace. Joseph of Arimathea’s staff, planted on Wearyall Hill at Glastonbury, was said to grow into the Glastonbury Thorn, a hawthorn that flowers at Christmas; in Wagner’s Tannhäuser (1845) the pope’s barren staff bursts into leaf.
  • “his stupor as if he had been some old loggerhead turtle”: The loggerhead sea turtle is a species of oceanic turtle distributed throughout the world. It is a marine reptile, belonging to the family Cheloniidae. The average loggerhead measures around 90 cm in carapace length when fully grown. The adult loggerhead sea turtle weighs approximately 135 kg (298 lb), with the largest specimens weighing in at more than 450 kg (1,000 lb). (Wikipedia) See On the loggerhead turtle and the hawk moth.
    • The Sphingidae are a family of moths commonly called sphinx moths, also colloquially known as hawk moths, with many of their caterpillars known as hornworms. It includes about 1,450 species. It is best represented in the tropics, but species are found in every region. (Wikipedia) See Butterflies and Moths.
  • “the rattle of dice in an ivory box”: See playing the bones in the Chapter 55 resources. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “old fighters with glass knuckles, glass fists, glass chins”: A boxer with a glass jaw, or glass chin, is one who is easily knocked out.
  • “killing the song sparrow in his fall”: “There is special providence in the fall of a sparrow” (William Shakespeare, Hamlet, 5.2), after “one of them shall not fall on the ground without your Father” (Matthew 10:29, KJV). See Shakespeare and The Bible.
  • “loud Te Deums”: “Te Deums” may refer to Te Deum laudamus which means Thee, O God, we praise. It is a Latin Christian hymn. Legend says Ambrose and Augustine sang it in alternate verses at Augustine’s baptism in 387. See On the Te Deum and Augustine and Christian Theology.
  • “an oyster bed invaded by a starfish derby”: Starfish are the oysterman’s plague: a starfish pulls an oyster’s shell open with its tube feet and turns its own stomach inside out into the shell to digest it. See The Sea and Water.
  • “no dividing net between the heavens and the heavens”: Compare Robert Frost on free verse: writing without meter is “like playing tennis with the net down” (address at Milton Academy, 1935).
  • “both moon and earth moving like the neap tide”: Is a tide just after the first or third quarters of the moon when there is least difference between high and low water.
    • Is the period when the tide is going out, or the water level is falling from high to low, with water flowing away from the shore. It is the opposite of a flood tide, which occurs when the water level is rising. The term can also refer to the seaward flow of water in estuaries or tidal rivers during this lowering water phase.
  • “shaking like an aspen tree of silver leaves”: Aspen leaves tremble in the slightest air because their stems are flattened sideways; legend says the aspen has trembled ever since the Cross was made of its wood.
  • “a tree with its bare branches filled with singing birds”: Young reverses Shakespeare’s winter tree: “Bare ruin’d choirs, where late the sweet birds sang” (William Shakespeare, Sonnet 73, 1609). See Shakespeare.
  • “playing on and on as if, as he had never begun, he could not stop”: This conveys a sense of endless, continuous, and perhaps futile musical performance. The music is portrayed as persistent and unceasing, embodying a kind of immortal or eternal quality that transcends ordinary experience of time and completion. This ongoing music creates a haunting atmosphere where the boundaries between beginning and end blur, much like a dream that persists indefinitely. Mr. Spitzer’s experience of “ringings in his ears for several days,” to the point that he almost forgot his own troubles, highlights the overwhelming impact of the music on his consciousness. The ringing in his ears symbolizes not only the literal auditory effect but also the emotional and psychological immersion in the music, which momentarily distracts him from his personal hardships and sorrows. His troubles, though significant, are temporarily overshadowed by the profound effect of the violinist’s performance. This passage suggests both the haunting, unending nature of the music and its power to momentarily transport Mr. Spitzer beyond his own suffering, providing a brief escape or solace. It also reflects the novel’s broader themes of time, memory, and the elusive nature of reality and art.
    • The idea of doing something without being able to stop has occurred in history with the Dancing plague of 1518 where 50 to 400 people danced until they dropped exhausted then got up and danced again; the Strasbourg authorities first hired musicians to keep them dancing, then sent the dancers to a shrine of Saint Vitus at Saverne.
  • “only the echo of the echo fading into silence”: In Ovid’s Metamorphoses, the nymph Echo pines for Narcissus until her body wastes away and her bones turn to stone, and only her voice is left (Book 3). See Greek Mythology and Music, Sound, and Silence.
  • “the snail because it could shed its skin”: Mr. Spitzer’s envy mixes its creatures: snakes shed their skins, but snails keep their shells for life, adding to them as they grow.
  • “the dying dolphin because it could change its moods and its colors”: See Byron’s dying dolphin in the Chapter 56 resources. See Romantic Poets.
  • “a hollow sound like a last trumpet blowing”: “In a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trump: for the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised incorruptible” (1 Corinthians 15:52, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “might have knocked upon so many ivory doors”: See the gates of horn and ivory in the Chapter 42 resources. See Greek Mythology.
  • “this submerged river of silence flow through a cave”: “Where Alph, the sacred river, ran / Through caverns measureless to man / Down to a sunless sea” (Samuel Taylor Coleridge, “Kubla Khan,” 1816). See Romantic Poets.
  • “such as he might have composed for a black keyboard only”: In Chopin’s “Black Key” Étude (Opus 10, No. 5, 1830) the right hand plays only on the black keys. See Music and Composers.
  • “notchings of tallies”: The English Exchequer kept its accounts on notched wooden tally sticks until 1826; burning the old tallies in a stove under the House of Lords started the fire that destroyed the Palace of Westminster in 1834.
  • “as if he were the recording angel”: See Sterne’s recording angel in the Chapter 55 resources.
  • “the first rosy light of star-lit dawn”: Homer’s dawn is “rosy-fingered,” the epithet that opens many mornings in the Iliad and the Odyssey. See Greek Mythology.
  • “the pearl-mantled loon singers on the back of the old glider whale”: Compare the voyage of Saint Brendan, whose monks landed on an island to celebrate Easter, lit a fire, and found the island was a whale named Jasconius (Navigatio Sancti Brendani, ninth century).
  • “as if it were he who was drawn by strings like a mad puppet”: See Kleist’s marionettes in the Chapter 56 resources.
  • “showers of imaginary sound like golden rain”: Zeus came to Danaë, locked in a bronze tower, as a shower of gold, and she bore Perseus. See Greek Mythology.
  • “they were those who wore their hearts upon their sleeves”: “I will wear my heart upon my sleeve / For daws to peck at” (William Shakespeare, Othello, 1.1). See Shakespeare.
  • “that glassy flute music which brought the rocks hurtling from the sky”: In Greek myth Amphion played his lyre and the stones moved by themselves into the walls of Thebes. See Greek Mythology.
  • “the star lilies swimming through dark waters”: Young’s star lilies are crinoids, sea lilies and feather stars, animals that look like flowers. A feather star begins life on a stalk like a lily and then breaks free and swims. Their “sea-change” is Ariel’s: “Nothing of him that doth fade / But doth suffer a sea-change / Into something rich and strange” (William Shakespeare, The Tempest, 1.2). See The Sea and Water and Shakespeare.
  • “dying that he might live”: “As dying, and, behold, we live” (2 Corinthians 6:9, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “a dead sea shell was not dead but only sleeping”: Some sea shells do strike. Cone snails harpoon their prey with a venomous tooth, and the sting of the geography cone has killed people. See The Sea and Water.
