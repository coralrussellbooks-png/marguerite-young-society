Summary

On the library steps, Mr. Spitzer meets an old violinist with shaking fingers who plays an imaginary violin. Mr. Spitzer recognizes the silent piece at once, a forgotten minor composition, and hears what is not there. He admits he distinguishes less and less between reality and his dreams, and suspects this may be his only wisdom.

Key passages

From the research

  • Mr. Spitzer watches an old violinist who plays invisible notes on an imaginary violin. Despite the silence, Mr. Spitzer "knew immediately what music he was playing" and recognized it as an obscure minor composition from childhood, something almost lost to modern ears. This passage establishes the mystical and imaginative nature of the music. It exists purely in the mind and memory, evoking nostalgia for a past that is fading. The description of the old musician’s expert emotional gestures and Mr. Spitzer’s empathetic response shows a deep connection between memory, art, and the ineffable experience of music beyond sound.
  • The narrative elaborates on the qualities of the music and the sounds of nature, city life, and ephemeral sensations woven seamlessly into the violinist's silent performance. Mr. Spitzer experiences a profound detachment from his corporeal self and is deeply moved, his soul almost slipping from his body. The music is described as "pre-musical music," existing before human consciousness or after it no longer perceives sound, suggesting a timeless, transcendent art form. This passage highlights the boundary between reality and imagination, sound and silence, life and death.
  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates how the old violinist might have been many other figures (an athlete, a gentleman, a fisherman, a king) if only the instruments and tools in his hands had changed. This reflection suggests the fluidity of reality and identity, how reality is "made up of just so many sticks and not one stick more." The passage explores the transformative power of imagination and the interchangeable roles people might play, blurring boundaries between art, sport, and life.
  • Mr. Spitzer recalls his brother and old sportsmen, tying these memories to ideas of death, legacy, and continuing influence. This section reveals his obsession with memory and how past lives and experiences persist beyond physical death, transforming but never disappearing. It also contrasts Mr. Spitzer's sensitivity to silent, subtle music with Peron's preference for the rougher, more physical world of sports, highlighting differing responses to life's challenges and art.
  • Mr. Spitzer questions whether the old musician is alive or dead, a dream or reality, reflecting on the ambiguous boundary between life and death. The violinist’s wild, convulsive movements are seen both as music’s highest expression and possibly a sign of illness or death. This ambiguity deepens the chapter’s themes of mortality, the unknowability of existence, and the persistence of art and spirit beyond physical life.
  • Mr. Spitzer reflects on himself as a "silent composer" and "walking score board," someone who carries music and memory within but whose music is intangible and largely unheard. This passage underscores his isolation and the internal nature of artistic creation, the idea that much of life’s music is invisible or unperceived by others but deeply real to the creator.
  • The chapter closes with Mr. Spitzer’s reflection that he no longer distinguishes clearly between reality and dreams, life and death. This indistinction, while sometimes embarrassing, is also his "only wisdom," a protective cloud through which he moves. The metaphor of his black cape and drifting shadow emphasizes his liminal state between worlds, carrying the darkness as a cloak.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • Mr. Spitzer is a large, reflective man, draped in a black cape, who is a silent composer and connoisseur of music. He is caught between life and death, reality and imagination. His deafness contrasts with his profound musical understanding, highlighting a spiritual or metaphysical connection to music beyond sound. He embodies melancholy, wisdom, and a deep sense of loss, especially connected to his dead brother and the passing of time.

The Old Violinist

  • The Old Violinist is an enigmatic figure with white hair and shaking fingers, who plays silent music on an imaginary violin. His physical condition suggests age and wear, but his music transcends physical limitations, becoming a symbol for the persistence of art and spirit beyond bodily decay. He may represent forgotten or marginalized artists, or even symbolize the border between life and death.

Research: Themes

  • Music in this chapter exists beyond audible sound. The old violinist plays silent, imaginary music that Mr. Spitzer "hears" through intense observation and imagination. This silent music is described with rich auditory imagery (whispers, sobs, bird calls, and natural sounds) suggesting music as a universal language transcending physical constraints.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s reflections evoke buried childhood memories and a past nearly lost to time. His thoughts on his dead brother and old sportsmen convey a deep sense of mourning and nostalgia. The music, described as an old, nearly forgotten waltz, symbolizes this connection to the past and the fading of cultural memory.
  • The chapter blurs the lines between living and dead. Mr. Spitzer contemplates whether the old violinist is alive, dying, or already dead, and imagines music existing before consciousness and after hearing ceases. The text wrestles with existence, identity, and what remains after physical death.
  • Mr. Spitzer draws analogies between music and sports, imagining the violinist as a baseball player, a billiards player, or other figures, emphasizing how art and life are intertwined and fluid. Reality itself is depicted as made from “sticks” or tools, with identity shaped by perspective and imagination.
  • Mr. Spitzer admires the violinist with a mingled feeling of pity, reflecting on the loneliness of the artist and the fragility of human endeavor. This ambivalence highlights the complex human response to suffering and greatness.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

  • The violinist’s “imaginary bow” and “imaginary strings” represent art beyond the physical realm, emphasizing creativity as a spiritual act. The music is described with natural and cosmic imagery: “singings of blind moths,” “bird calls,” “sea shells buzzing like bees,” and “sparks sparking on the strings”.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s attire symbolizes his somber, reflective nature and his boundary state between darkness and light, life and death.
  • The hour-glass of the heart is a metaphor for the passage of time and the reversal or renewal of life, connected to the emotional impact of the music.
  • Baseball, billiards, croquet, polo, fishing, and even kingship are used metaphorically to describe potential alternate lives or identities for the violinist, highlighting the fluidity of reality and identity.
  • Frequent references to the sky, clouds, stars, and celestial bodies evoke a cosmic scale and the mystery of existence.

Research: Resources

  • Pearl-buttoned spats were a popular, elegant fashion accessory for both men and women from the late 19th century to the 1920s. Originally designed to protect footwear from dirt on unpaved roads, they became a symbol of social status and high fashion, especially during the Victorian and Edwardian eras. The trend began to fade in the late 1920s with the rise of more casual styles and shorter hemlines, which made intricate footwear and decorative buttons more visible.
  • “minor composition” This may refer to Paul Lincke’s Luna Waltz from 1911. Carl Emil Paul Lincke was a German composer and theater conductor. He is considered the "father" of the Berlin operetta. His well-known compositions include "Berliner Luft" ("Berlin Air"), the unofficial anthem of Berlin, from his operetta Frau Luna; and "The Glow-Worm", from his operetta Lysistrata. Luna Waltz is an instrumental piece adapted from the 1899 operetta Frau Luna and published in the United States in the early 20th century. The waltz, originally a dance tune within the original operetta, became a popular, standalone composition that showcased Lincke's success as a composer of Berlin operettas, a genre he helped define with this work.
  • “pre-musical music” The earliest musical instruments demonstrate that early humans possessed abstract thought, symbolic expression, and sophisticated cognitive and social skills. These instruments, made from bone, shell, and ivory and dating back over 40,000 years, show that music played a crucial role in rituals, communication, and social bonding, helping to unite communities, convey spiritual beliefs, and even coordinate collective work or warfare. The existence of these complex tools indicates that early humans were innovators, capable of abstract thought and creating objects with symbolic and functional meaning beyond mere survival needs.
  • “prided himself that music and baseball were fused in his psychology” and ‘intuitive understanding to this relation of music to sports” Music and baseball (sports) are similar in their shared need for practice, discipline, teamwork, and performance. Both fields involve individual roles within a collective, rely on rhythm and coordination, cultivate passionate fanbases, and have rich, evolving traditions and narratives that contribute to their cultural significance.
  • “Te Deums” may refer to Te Deum laudamus which means Thee, O God, we praise. It is a Latin Christian hymn.
  • “loggerhead turtle” The loggerhead sea turtle is a species of oceanic turtle distributed throughout the world. It is a marine reptile, belonging to the family Cheloniidae. The average loggerhead measures around 90 cm in carapace length when fully grown. The adult loggerhead sea turtle weighs approximately 135 kg (298 lb), with the largest specimens weighing in at more than 450 kg (1,000 lb).
  • “hawk moth” The Sphingidae are a family of moths commonly called sphinx moths, also colloquially known as hawk moths, with many of their caterpillars known as hornworms. It includes about 1,450 species. It is best represented in the tropics, but species are found in every region.
  • “...playing on and on as if, as he had never begun, he could not stop. Mr. Spitzer had heard ringings in his ears for several days—almost to the extent that he had almost forgotten his own troubles, which were not inconsiderable.” This conveys a sense of endless, continuous, and perhaps futile musical performance. The music is portrayed as persistent and unceasing, embodying a kind of immortal or eternal quality that transcends ordinary experience of time and completion. This ongoing music creates a haunting atmosphere where the boundaries between beginning and end blur, much like a dream that persists indefinitely. Mr. Spitzer's experience of "ringings in his ears for several days," to the point that he almost forgot his own troubles, highlights the overwhelming impact of the music on his consciousness. The ringing in his ears symbolizes not only the literal auditory effect but also the emotional and psychological immersion in the music, which momentarily distracts him from his personal hardships and sorrows. His troubles, though significant, are temporarily overshadowed by the profound effect of the violinist's performance. This passage suggests both the haunting, unending nature of the music and its power to momentarily transport Mr. Spitzer beyond his own suffering, providing a brief escape or solace. It also reflects the novel’s broader themes of time, memory, and the elusive nature of reality and art.
  • The idea of doing something without being able to stop has occurred in history with the Dancing plague of 1518 where 50 to 400 people danced until they dropped exhausted then got up and continued until they were taken to a hospital.
  • In Greek mythology, Orpheus was a Thracian bard, legendary musician and prophet. He was also a renowned poet and, according to legend, travelled with Jason and the Argonauts in search of the Golden Fleece, and descended into the underworld to recover his lost wife, Eurydice.
  • “neap tide” is a tide just after the first or third quarters of the moon when there is least difference between high and low water.
  • “ebb tide” is the period when the tide is going out, or the water level is falling from high to low, with water flowing away from the shore. It is the opposite of a flood tide, which occurs when the water level is rising. The term can also refer to the seaward flow of water in estuaries or tidal rivers during this lowering water phase.
