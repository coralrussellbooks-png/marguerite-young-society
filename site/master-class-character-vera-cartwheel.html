Vera Cartwheel: The One Who Watches and Will Not Fix Them

← Catherine Snowden Cartwheel · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Miss Georgia MacIntosh →

Spoiler note: This portrait is written for readers who have finished the novel. It discusses the character’s whole story, including how it ends.

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · The Stone Deaf Man · Vera Cartwheel

She is on a bus in the mist, looking at a stranger she will never be able to name, and she is already telling his story. That is how you meet Vera Cartwheel because looking hard at people she cannot hold onto and telling their stories anyway is the whole of what she does. The double-headed man by the roadside, the pregnant girl in her painted silk, the whiskey-drinking driver going home to a wife who may not exist: she will never learn one of their names, and she keeps every one of them. He would remain, she says, engraved on memory's whirling disc. She does not act on these people. She does not save them or sort them or decide what they mean. She witnesses them, and she refuses to let them go.

She is Catherine's daughter, the pale rejected child whose eyes, her mother believed, had not craved reality. She was raised not by that mother, who kept to her bed and her dreams, but by a red-headed nursemaid with a broken nose who preached truth to nature and then walked into the sea. Now grown, Vera travels alone, by train and plane and evening comet plane and at last this erratic bus, into interior America, searching for one who is already dead. Every fact of her life is a fact of loss, and every fact is delivered in the same searching, circling voice, so that her grief and her looking are not two things. The looking is what the grief does.

Ask the wrong question and Vera is the weakest figure in the book: the passive narrator, the lonely watcher, the empty frame around vivid people. Ask the right one and she is the center of the novel's ethics. The question Radical Immanence puts to her is not why is she so alone, but what is a person doing when she stays present with someone she cannot resolve? Vera's answer, page after page, is to witness, and witnessing turns out to be the hardest and most active thing anyone in the book does.

Consider what she actually does with the adults around her. She examines her mother without curing her and without condemning her. She examines Mr. Spitzer without deciding whether he is alive or dead, himself or his brother, and she leaves him uncertain because uncertain is what he is. She examines Miss MacIntosh, the woman she loved and lost, without ever shrinking her to the lesson she was hired to be. She examines Esther Longtree, pregnant for years, and does not look away from a grief that will not deliver. In every case she comes close, stays long, and declines to fix. She does not turn a person into a verdict. This is not weakness of will. It is a discipline, and it is rarer than any action in the book.

She is the frame around the two still figures the first half rests on, and she holds them both without ranking them. Mr. Spitzer cannot throw anything away. Catherine cannot stop making things up. Vera watches the man to whom nothing happens and the woman to whom everything happens, and she refuses to choose between them, refuses to call one sane and one lost. Spitzer keeps the dead by paying their debts, Catherine keeps them by dreaming them into her rooms, and Vera keeps them by narrating them, which is why the whole novel is her voice. Her witnessing is not a stance she takes inside the book. It is the book. One woman recalling the dead into the present tense and refusing to let them be gone, is the third technique for the one task the other two are also performing, which is bearing what keeps leaving.

The easy reading calls all of this alienation. Vera walks alone, she says so herself, the seeker through mazes of sorrow, cut off, half sleeping, reaching all her life for the tangible and watching it fade. It is tempting to take her at her most despairing word and file her as the isolated modern soul. The novel does not let that stand. The same narrator who mourns that every dream must fail also writes that every heart is the other heart, that the individual is the one illusion. Her loneliness is not the last word about her. It is the condition out of which she does her real work, which is to find, in strangers she will never meet again, the secret relationship, deep calling to deep. She is not cut off from the others. She is the one who insists, against all the evidence of separateness, that the others are hers.

And then she does the thing none of the resolved characters can do. She set out to find Miss MacIntosh, or the truth behind her, a quest with a destination. She does not reach it. She lands in southern Indiana, a state as yet unknown to her, in the mist, on a road that seems to go back the way it came. The book that was supposed to arrive somewhere arrives here, in the ordinary here-and-now of a bus that plows nowhere, and treats the failure to arrive as the point rather than the defeat. There is no What Cheer at the end of the line. There is only the actual place the traveler is standing, which is the only place anyone is ever standing.

What she builds there, she builds knowing it cannot last. She loves the stone deaf man, the one who heard with his eyes, who could not hear her voice and never would. She marries him anyway, and she conceives a child in a silent world. This is not a rescue and not a perfect union. It is love chosen inside a limitation that will not lift, communion built across a silence, love that comes, in his words, before words were or ever will be. She does not wait for the whole man, or the moment when nothing is broken. She builds in the only conditions there are, which are broken ones, and she builds anyway.

What stays standing is that Vera's stillness was never emptiness. The reader arrives expecting the narrator to be a window, a clear pane the vivid people are seen through, and leaves having watched that window turn out to be the most active thing in the house. She does not resolve her mother, or Spitzer, or Miss MacIntosh, because resolving them would be the failure. To witness a person without concluding her, to stay in the presence of a contradiction and neither flee it nor solve it, is the whole ethical labor the novel is asking for, and Vera is the one who performs it start to finish. The lesson for a writer is that a narrator does not need a plot to be the strongest figure on the page. She needs only to refuse the two easy exits, judgment and rescue, and to keep looking. Presence, held long enough and honestly enough, is an action. It may be the only one the book finally trusts.
