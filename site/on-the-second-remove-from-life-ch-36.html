On the second remove from life.

Young's phrase for Mr. Spitzer's condition is one of the novel's quiet philosophical achievements. To be at a second remove is to be twice displaced: not in the world directly, but observing your observation of it. This is not quite solipsism and not quite dissociation; it is something subtler, a consciousness that has learned to cushion itself against arrival. Mr. Spitzer has been so shaped by loss that he no longer trusts the first impression of anything. He waits. By the time he receives it, the moment has already moved on.
