Section 8. The Reference Index

Allusions: Dante · Don Quixote · Edgar Allan Poe · Exploration and the Poles · Fairy Tales and Folklore · Genesis · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · Milton · Revelation and the Apocalypse · Romantic Poets · Shakespeare · The Bible

Definition from research

The full index of references is kept outside this volume, in a companion spreadsheet. It holds about two thousand entries covering more than seven hundred and sixty distinct references across all 82 chapters of the novel. For each entry it records the chapter, a line of the surrounding text, the source or tradition, the character who carries it, and its category. A printed table of that size would run to hundreds of pages and would be hard to read. This section explains instead what the index holds and how a reader can use it.

What the Index Holds

The references fall into a handful of broad kinds.

Places make up the largest share. Boston, Chicago, the Arctic, the Alps, Iowa, and What Cheer recur so often that they form the novel's map as much as its setting.

History comes next: kings and emperors, explorers, presidents, generals, and the great wars and crusades.

Scripture follows, from Genesis to Revelation, with the genealogies of Israel as its most repeated thread.

Myth and folklore follow that: the gods of Greece and Rome, the ferryman of the dead, the old woman in her shoe, the talking horse of the Grimm tale.

Literature proper is a smaller share than readers might expect, a little over a hundred entries, though it includes the references that carry the most weight: Bunyan, Cervantes, Calderón, Dante, Milton, and Browning.

A small but telling group of entries is made up of unnamed allusions. These are phrases from the Bible, Shakespeare, Virgil, Coleridge, Poe, and the prayer book that Young weaves into her sentences without naming their source.

How to Use It

The index is most useful for following a single name through the book. A reader who wants to see how the ferryman of the dead travels can find Charon in chapters 3, 5, 8, 30, 46, 51, 52, 53, 59, and 82, and can see from the carrier column that he passes from Catherine to the bus-driver, to Cousin Hannah, to Mr. Spitzer, and back. A reader curious about Miss MacIntosh's quarrel with the calendar can find the Emperor Constantine in chapters 22, 23, 63, 64, 65, and 66. Every one of those entries belongs to her except the one in chapter 65, where Mr. Bonebreaker's sermon seems to have planted the grudge.

The index can also be read one chapter at a time. A reader starting a chapter can see in advance which names will appear there and where they come from. The densest chapters, 65, 22, 54, and 29, reward this most.

How to Add to It

The index is built to grow. When a reader finds an allusion it missed, the new reference can be added as a line, with its chapter, its source, and the character who carries it. The counts in the spreadsheet update on their own. The same standards apply to every addition: the reference must be found in the novel's text, and a connection the reader brings, with no anchor in the text, is marked as a reader connection.
