Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Pillars: Reality Is Constructed

The Questions

Miss MacIntosh gives lessons “dressed as for a long voyage,” in whaler’s hat and sea boots, though “we were not going anywhere.” What is common sense wearing a costume for a journey it will not take?

She dresses so “for self-protection… common sense demanded we should be dressed for the gale.” What gale, in a windless nursery, is she armored against?

“She was just like other people,” she insists, “absolutely normal.” Why must the normalcy be insisted on so hard?

Is the plainest, most sensible self in the book the least costumed, or the most?

The chapter catches common sense in the act of dressing up. Miss MacIntosh gives the “monotonous factual lessons” while “dressed as for a long voyage, wearing her whaler’s hat, her sea boots,” though, as she reminds Vera, “we were not going anywhere.” The plainest, most sensible figure in the book is in full costume for a voyage she will never take, and the costume is her common sense made visible. Her groundedness is not the absence of construction but an elaborate one, a nautical outfitting worn indoors, and the chapter lets the reader see the plainness as the disguise it is, sea boots in a nursery, a navigator dressed for a gale in a room.

The costume is armor, and the gale is the one that never comes. She dresses so “for self-protection,” because “common sense demanded we should be dressed for the gale,” in a nursery where no gale blows. This is the sealed blueprint’s deepest logic laid bare: the readiness is a defense against a storm that is not there, the practical outfitting a wall raised against a threat the practicality itself conjures. Miss MacIntosh arms against the gale of the uncontrolled, the dream, the open sea of everything her framework cannot hold, and the armor is so constant that she wears it to teach a child her lessons. The common sense is a suit worn against the flood.

Her insistence on being normal is the tell. “She was just like other people,” she says, “absolutely normal,” and the harder the insistence, the clearer that normalcy is the thing being constructed rather than the thing being described. A person absolutely normal does not need to say so; the saying builds the normalcy it claims. This is Reality Is Constructed at the level of the plainest self-account: Miss MacIntosh manufactures her own ordinariness by asserting it, dressed for a voyage, armored against a windless day, declaring herself just like everyone while looking like no one, the normalcy the most worked-at fiction in the room.

So the chapter completes the reversal the return to Miss MacIntosh has been performing. The figure who entered the book as the daylight against Catherine’s dream, the least constructed thing, the bedrock, is shown to be the most costumed, the most armored, the most insistently self-fashioned. Her plainness is a whaler’s outfit, her steadiness a defense against an imaginary gale, her normalcy a claim she has to keep making. The book does not despise her for this; everyone is costumed, everyone builds. It only refuses, finally and completely, to let her plainness be the exception, the one unbuilt thing. The most sensible self turns out to be the most dressed.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Miss MacIntosh’s common sense for the least constructed thing in the book, the bedrock, and the reading that takes her nautical costume for mere harmless eccentricity. Neither the bedrock nor the harmless quirk is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that her common sense is an elaborate costume, a whaler’s outfitting worn for a voyage never taken; that the costume is armor against a gale that never comes, the practical readiness a wall raised against a storm the practicality itself conjures; and that her insistence on being “absolutely normal” builds the normalcy it claims rather than describing it, the most worked-at fiction in the room. The plainest self is the most costumed. Miss MacIntosh teaches in sea boots against a windless day, and the chapter refuses, completely, to let her plainness be the one unbuilt thing.

For Reflection

What practical readiness in one’s own life is really armor against a gale that is not actually blowing?

Why does insisting hard on being normal tend to build the normalcy rather than describe it?

Is the plainest, most sensible self one knows the least costumed, or the most?
