Unit 6, pp. 105-120: The Rappites and the roots of Mormonism

← Unit 5 · Harp Song for a Radical: A History Companion · Unit 7 →

Background for this unit: The History in Harp Song for a Radical

Weitling travels up the Ohio River toward Pittsburgh and the Harmony Society at Economy, which gives Young the occasion to tell the Rappites' story from their first town to their third. She then turns to the young Joseph Smith, the treasure seeker of upstate New York, and the folk magic from which Mormonism grew.

Major entries

George Rapp and the Harmony Society (pp. 109-114)

  • Who he was: A vinedresser and lay preacher (1757-1847) from Iptingen in Württemberg, persecuted by the Lutheran church for separatism. He came to America in 1803 and was followed by several hundred families.

  • The three towns: The society built Harmony, in Butler County, Pennsylvania (1804-1814), then New Harmony on the Wabash in Indiana (1814-1824), then Economy, on the Ohio River below Pittsburgh (1824 onward), now Ambridge. They held all property in common and adopted celibacy in 1807, which Young calls a "community of married celibates" (p. 112).

  • Beliefs: They expected Christ's return within their lifetimes. They identified with the Sun Woman of Revelation 12, who flees into the wilderness, a figure Young returns to again and again (pp. 110-113). A limestone slab at New Harmony bearing two footprints was shown as the place where the angel Gabriel had stood (pp. 110-111).

  • Why it matters: The Harmonists were superb manufacturers and investors. Through Frederick Rapp, George's adopted son and business manager, they became major investors in Pennsylvania railroads and oil. The society dwindled with no children to succeed it and dissolved in 1905.

  • Further reading: Karl J. R. Arndt, George Rapp's Harmony Society, 1785-1847 (1965).

Frances Wright and Nashoba (pp. 111-112)

  • Who she was: A Scottish heiress (1795-1852), orphaned young, who toured America and wrote Views of Society and Manners in America (1821). She was a companion of Lafayette and a protégée of Jeremy Bentham.

  • What happened: In 1825 she bought land near Memphis and founded Nashoba, where enslaved people bought from their owners were to work off their purchase price and be educated for freedom. The experiment collapsed amid illness and scandal. In 1830 she took its thirty-one remaining residents to Haiti and freed them.

  • Why it matters: She became one of the first women in America to lecture publicly to mixed audiences. With Robert Dale Owen she edited the Free Enquirer in New York and backed the Working Men's Party of 1829, calling for free public schools. Opponents coined "Fanny Wrightism" as a slur for radicalism.

  • Where the record differs: Young places Nashoba on "the Fox River in Tennessee" and dates it to 1827 (p. 112). It was on the Wolf River and was founded in 1825. She also calls Wright "Bentham's ward," which is too strong.

  • Further reading: Celia Morris Eckhardt, Fanny Wright: Rebel in America (1984).

Joseph Smith, treasure seeking, and the golden plates (pp. 115-120)

  • Who he was: Born in Vermont in 1805, he grew up on a poor farm near Palmyra and Manchester, New York, in the "burned-over district," so called for its repeated religious revivals.

  • Treasure seeking: As a young man Smith was hired to find buried treasure with a seer stone. In 1826 he was brought before a justice of the peace as a "glass-looker." Local lore of the kind Young retells told of buried Spanish gold guarded by spirits, such as her "mysterious Spaniard" with his throat slashed (p. 119).

  • The plates: Smith said the angel Moroni led him in 1823 to golden plates buried in a hill near his home, later called Cumorah. He said he received them in 1827 with the Urim and Thummim, two seer stones for translating them. The Book of Mormon was published in 1830.

  • How Young uses it: For her, Smith's treasure digging and his scripture spring from the same American hunger for hidden gold, earthly and heavenly at once.

  • Further reading: Richard Lyman Bushman, Joseph Smith: Rough Stone Rolling (2005).

Emma Hale and Isaac Hale (pp. 117-118)

  • Who they were: Isaac Hale was a farmer and hunter in Harmony, Susquehanna County, Pennsylvania. Smith boarded in his house in 1825 while digging for a lost silver mine for Josiah Stowell (Young's "Stoal," p. 117).

  • What happened: Hale disapproved of Smith's trade and refused permission to marry. Smith and Emma eloped in January 1827. Emma became his scribe and, after his death, refused to follow Brigham Young west.

  • Where the record differs: Young places Hale's house "in Economy" (p. 117), the Rappite town. The Hales lived in a different Harmony, in northeastern Pennsylvania, unrelated to the Rappites. The shared name seems to have tangled the two.

  • Further reading: Linda King Newell and Valeen Tippetts Avery, Mormon Enigma: Emma Hale Smith (1984).

Shorter entries

  • Frederick Rapp (pp. 112-113): George Rapp's adopted son (1775-1834), architect and business head of the society. He negotiated the 1825 sale of New Harmony to Robert Owen for about \$150,000.

  • Abraham Ziegler (p. 114): The buyer of the original Harmony, Pennsylvania, in 1814, at a heavy loss to the society.

  • Robert Dale Owen (p. 112): Robert Owen's son (1801-1877). He was later an Indiana congressman who helped found the Smithsonian and pressed for emancipation during the Civil War.

  • Pontiac (p. 109): Ottawa leader of the 1763 uprising against the British in the Great Lakes country.

  • Anthony Wayne (p. 109): The general who defeated the Ohio Indian confederation at Fallen Timbers in 1794.

  • Red Pole (p. 109): A Shawnee chief who signed the Treaty of Greenville in 1795 and died in Pittsburgh that year.

  • Captain William Kidd (pp. 116, 119-120): Scottish privateer hanged at Execution Dock in London in 1701 for piracy. He really did bury treasure on Gardiners Island, New York, which fed a century of treasure legends.

  • The Gilded Age (p. 113): The title of Mark Twain and Charles Dudley Warner's 1873 satirical novel, which gave the postwar era its name. Young plays on it as the "Gelded Age" for the celibate Rappites.

Allusions

  • The Sun Woman (pp. 110-113): Revelation 12, the woman clothed with the sun who flees into the wilderness from the dragon.

  • The Golden Rose of Micah (p. 111): A Rappite emblem, drawn from Micah 4:8. It was carved over the door of their church.

  • Solomon's mines (p. 116): King Solomon's legendary gold mines of Ophir.

  • Atlantis (p. 119): Plato's lost island civilization.

  • El Dorado (p. 120): The Spanish legend of a golden kingdom in South America.

  • Antinous and Minerva (p. 112): The beautiful youth loved by the emperor Hadrian, and the Roman goddess of wisdom.
