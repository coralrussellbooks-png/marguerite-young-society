Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, Chapter 3

Every Bird and Beast

Young came to her novel already a student of birds. As the Preface tells, her 1936 master's thesis at the University of Chicago was on the birds and beasts of John Lyly's Euphues, and she told Charles Ruas in the Paris Review, “I had to study the history of every bird and beast there ever was.” She kept the habit all her life: “I am interested in the birds and the beasts for their symbolic value,” “their value as icons, as ways of saying things about people.”

The novel shows it. The words bird and birds appear more than seven hundred times, and the kinds are named one by one: ravens and doves, canaries and gyrfalcons, terns and petrels, loons and bitterns, whooping cranes, mandarin ducks, hoopoes, gannets, lovebirds, and the last passenger pigeon. To All My Darlings traces them through more than fifty chapters. Lyly used a bird to teach a lesson. Young uses a bird to say something about a person that no plain sentence could. This essay follows a few of them.

The Nestless Birds

The first great bird passage belongs to Vera's childhood. In Chapter 3, remembering Miss MacIntosh's teaching, Vera says: “To kiss the rod was better than not to know self-discipline, than to imagine we were free and our own architects, building heaven as the sea builds its shells, as the birds build their nests.” And then: “We were the nestless birds, even like the seagulls wading before us through lines of surf, even like the baby sandpipers who were the color of the sand which sheltered them and whose mother might have been the sand.” Then she adds, “Or so I thought then.”

A sandpiper whose mother might have been the sand is a fair picture of Vera herself, raised by a mother who was mostly absent in her dreams and by a nursemaid who walked into the sea. And Miss MacIntosh has no patience for the other kind of bird: “Little she cared for all these fine-feathered birds of paradise, the dream of heaven as it should be realized on earth.” The birds of paradise came to Europe as trade skins with their feet cut off, and people believed they had no feet and lived all their lives in the air until they fell to earth dead. In Chapter 37, Catherine thinks of sending a message “by the footless messengers.” Miss MacIntosh's birds stand on the sand. Catherine's never land.

The Canary and the Ravens

Catherine's life hangs on a bird. In Chapter 2: “Her only faithful companion had been nothing human, only a plain little bird, only a yellow canary with a glassy eye and ruffled wing, and on this, her life and everything else depended.” It sleeps in a gilded cage at her elbow, “delicate as a puff of opium-colored smoke, its smooth head tucked under its wing,” and “so long as this bird lived, she could not die.” A woman who has shut herself in for good keeps a caged bird as her hold on life. Intertextuality as Construction Method follows the many names Catherine gives her canaries.

The same chapter lists among her ghostly visitors “two sister ravens who had created the universe, the dead creation.” In Chapter 11 they come again with “the sister ravens, the brother doves.” European fairy tales tell of brothers turned into birds and saved by a sister, in the Grimms' “The Seven Ravens” and Giambattista Basile's “The Seven Doves.” Young makes the ravens sisters. And ravens who make the world belong above all to the peoples of the Northwest Coast. In the stories of the Haida, Raven brings the world and its light into being (John R. Swanton, Haida Texts and Myths, 1905). The Haida live on the Queen Charlotte Islands, now called Haida Gwaii, and in Chapter 11 Catherine entertains “Queen Charlotte Islands” too, asking after “a dead, snowy gyrfalcon who had been blown upon that desolate beach, a goldeneye no longer nesting on that rock.” The creator ravens and the islands sit in the same bedroom.

The Red Rooster's Wing

Birds also mark the people Vera loves. Chapter 4 opens: “Long years after her disappearance or death, I had dreamed of no one but Miss MacIntosh, my darling, poor lost soul, her cheeks reddening in the terrible sunlight, her hair red as the red rooster’s wing.” The red rooster is the bird of plain farmyard mornings, and it fits a woman from What Cheer, Iowa. It also fits a wig. In Chapter 14, Catherine sees a grander cock: “Was Mr. Spitzer blind that he could not see the golden cock perched at the foot of her bed, that it was really Pythagoras shedding his golden feathers?” In Lucian's old dialogue “The Dream, or the Cock,” a rooster reveals that it is Pythagoras reborn. The farmyard rooster and the golden cock are the two sides of the novel, the plain and the dreamed, wearing feathers.

Martha, the Last Passenger Pigeon

The novel's deepest bird grief belongs to Mr. Spitzer. Passenger pigeons once flew over North America in flocks of billions; John James Audubon watched one flight pass for three days in 1813. Hunters shot, netted, and clubbed them by the millions, and the last one, a bird named Martha, died at the Cincinnati Zoo on September 1, 1914.

In Chapter 45, Mr. Spitzer is “but an old pigeon-roost himself,” and sometimes there come to him “passenger pigeons now extinct though they had once darkened the heavens over this wilderness, roaring, clanging like church bells in clouds, making a twilight of sunlight.” In Chapter 49, “Whenever he thought of the death of an individual, he wept for the death to the last passenger pigeon known to earth—Martha, who expired September 14, at one o’clock in the afternoon just as the clock was tolling one, 1919, A.D., at the Cincinnati zoo.” Young's date is not the real one, and the working copy of the novel gives a different year, 1918. What matters to Mr. Spitzer is that “her passage should scarcely have been noted, but he had noted it in a war-torn world. He had written down this date upon his shirt cuff.”

He knows how her kind died: “All others of her kind had been shot down by great guns, trapped by nets like the fishes of the sea, clubbed, killed simply because they were the many, the beautiful, the excessive.” And he does not let the pigeons stand alone. “He would have presented his indictment to the Senate on account of what America had done to passenger pigeons once as thick as stars in the clouds and had done to Indians in their lonely tepees and yet might do to the whooping crane and the lute bird trembling from heaven to earth.” The pigeon's death sits beside what the country did to the Native peoples of the land, in one sentence, with no “but” between them. He would have been thrown out of court, the novel says, accused “of giving a voice to all those beauties which never should have been expressed.” Mr. Spitzer witnesses for those no one else will.

The Lighthouse and the Star

Migrating birds that fly at night are drawn to lighthouse beams and strike the glass, and keepers long found them dead at the foot of the tower in the morning. Young uses that fact twice. In Chapter 48, Mr. Spitzer remembers “that under the lighthouse it is darkest, even though the lighthouse seems a star against which migratory orioles will dash, having mistaken the lighthouse for the star. Even Mr. Spitzer might have done so.” In Chapter 60 he stands still while wild ducks stream past him, he “being like the burning of the lighthouse on the distant rock,” and they “like those poor birds of passage who, with their eyes blinded by the light, are dashed to pieces against a lighthouse looming through the fog, for they have mistaken the lighthouse for the light, the reflection of a star.”

That is the whole novel's danger in one picture. Every person in it is following some light. Some of the lights are stars, and some are only lighthouses, and from the dark no one can tell which. In Chapter 41, Catherine's visitors leave cards with “comments as to ancient shipwrecks and storms and birds of passage, birds of omen.” In this novel the bird of passage and the bird of omen are often the same bird.

Esther's Lovebirds and Cock Robin

At the end of the book, the birds come to Esther Longtree. In Chapter 72, at home, she is “breeding lovebirds for their big brain boxes and their talking and making long sentences with subject and predicate that would spill out in the dawnlight when they wanted their water and their mama and their papa.” The woman whose children are born dead raises birds that call for their mother. The same chapter asks the old nursery question: “Who killed cock robin?” Esther cannot answer it either: “The wind, the night, the day, the child, the little child, the many fathers, the sky.” In Chapter 74, a man she has just slept with talks of “the poisoned blackbirds falling from the sky upon the picnic grounds,” one more death in his long list of other people's murders.

And the novel ends with a guest list full of birds. In Chapter 82, Vera and the stone deaf man will invite to their wedding “whippoorwill and bluejay and little jenny wren and bobolink and woodpecker and little robin redbreast, all the wild flowers and all the birds and little tommy titmouse.” The robin whose killer no one could name comes back alive, invited.

The Radical Immanence Reading

In the language of Radical Immanence, Mr. Spitzer's mourning for Martha is Pillar 3 (Grief Doesn't End): he weeps for her whenever he thinks of any death, years after hers. His wish to bring the pigeons and the Indians before the Senate, and the date written on his shirt cuff, are Pillar 4 (Witnessing Is the Core Ethical Act). And the nestless birds, the sandpiper whose mother might have been the sand, and Esther's lovebirds calling for their mother are Pillar 7 (The Margins Reveal the Truth): the smallest creatures at the edge of the surf carry the novel's truth about who is sheltered and who is not.
