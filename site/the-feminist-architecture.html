Movement Two: The Five Pillars in the Traditions

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Esther Longtree · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft · Plato and Platonism · Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres · William James and Pragmatism

Allusions: American Religious Movements · Ancient Egypt · Angels and Archangels · The History of Opium · Women's Suffrage

Definition from research

Feminist scholarship on Miss MacIntosh, My Darling has tended to focus on Cousin Hannah as its primary feminist figure: the militant suffrage captain, the woman who gave everything to the cause, the pioneer. This is not wrong, but it is incomplete, and the incompleteness matters architecturally. Young does not give us one feminist position. She gives us four, each one embodying a different relationship to the five pillars, each one operating as a distinct structural element in the novel's argument about what it means to be a woman inside the conditions of the world Young was examining. The four positions are: Miss MacIntosh (the pragmatist who names the condition), Cousin Hannah (the activist who wages war on the condition), Esther Longtree (the body that the condition moves through), and the mother Catherine (the dreamer who escapes the condition into illusion). To understand any one of them requires understanding all four, because Young built them as a system, a feminist architecture that can only be read whole.

Miss MacIntosh is the novel's most overlooked feminist, precisely because her feminism is so completely naturalized into her common sense that it never announces itself as feminism. She does not fight for women's rights. She embodies them as a daily practice, so thoroughly that the practice becomes invisible. "He is bare whom virtue hath not clothed," she says, and the pronoun is not incidental. Her world is one in which character is the only reliable garment, the only protection against weather, and she teaches this to Vera with the same flat certainty she brings to everything: the Titanic's sinking date, the population of Kansas, the straight line between two points. Her pedagogy is a feminist pedagogy in its deepest structure, even when it is most exasperating: she insists that Vera reckon with catastrophe, with scale, with the count of the unidentified dead, because a woman who has not reckoned with catastrophe honestly is a woman who will be undone by the first catastrophe she meets personally. Miss MacIntosh's practical ethics, "common sense is the finest sense," are not the opposite of feminist ambition. They are its foundation. She is what women can be when they are not required to perform either helplessness or grandeur. The witness pillar runs through her entire characterization: she sees clearly, she names what she sees, she refuses the consolations of illusion, and she does this as an ordinary practice on an ordinary day, which is the most radical feminist claim Young makes. Clear seeing should not be exceptional.

Cousin Hannah is where the novel's feminist architecture becomes most explicitly structural, and the structure is deliberately paradoxical. She is "cuirassed by suffrage," her shield "emblazoned with a cherub carrying a lightning bolt," a woman who bearded human lions in their dens in Persia, who girded her loins "with suffrage like that iron girdle worn by a great Crusader's wife whose husband was away in Holy Wars." The military imagery is not ornamental. Young is making a precise argument about what the suffrage movement required of its participants: the total mobilization of the self, the same complete commitment demanded by warfare, and it was waged against an opponent with equivalent resources and equivalent willingness to inflict casualties. "Was not marriage, as that great suffrage captain had always said, the death of a woman's soul, the sacrifice of her finest impulses, the wasting of her shining talents?" This is Hannah's public position, her suffrage argument, her tactical claim. And it is true as far as it goes.

Where Young's feminist architecture exceeds the documentary record of suffrage history is in what she reveals about Hannah's private life: "the confession of this great suffrage captain's personal love, dead love which had prompted her suffrage movement and battles against overwhelming odds." Hannah had loved and lost, and the loss had become the movement. "False modesty killed my love," she cries in her dying, and the grief is as precise and physical as any battlefield wound, a grief she had channeled into forty years of public activism that never, until the end, she allowed herself to name as grief at all. Young is not diminishing Hannah's public achievement by revealing its private root. She is demonstrating the plural self operating at feminist scale: Hannah is simultaneously the great captain who believed marriage was the death of a woman's soul, and the woman who wept when a snowflake touched her hand because she was thinking of someone she had lost. Both are fully real. Neither cancels the other. The AND holds them both, and holding them both is the only honest account of how feminist history is actually made: not by people who have transcended personal grief but by people who have found a way to make personal grief into public action, which is its own kind of transformation and its own kind of loss.

Esther Longtree is the feminist figure no feminist taxonomy quite fits, and that resistance to category is itself the point. She is "brilliant but dumb," a big-boned slow-moving waitress with a deformed foot and a swollen womb, "known for her everlasting pregnancy and her loving heart which knew no lover and her everlasting loneliness." The men who pass through are the General Electric representative, the soap salesman, the antichrist, the rubber salesman, the toy salesman: the whole commercial apparatus of mid-century American capitalism moving through her body on its way from here to there, leaving her with children she carries to stillbirth while the fathers write years later asking if the child was born, if it was a little Jehovah's Witness, how old now. "Nobody here really knows me as I am," she says, "but the stillborn and the white birds and the blackbirds and the men who are passing through." What she carries is the weight of a grief with no adequate name: not widowhood, not single motherhood, not infertility, but something Young invented a character to contain, the grief of perpetual expectation perpetually unmet, a "hovering expectation of the great bubble which precedes creation."

Young gives Esther more detailed bodily attention than any other character in the novel: the "shape of the head, shadow of the hand, the foot, the little shadow boxer" moving under her heart; the ears without eyes, the eyes without ears, the fingernail, the cobweb, the stillborn who embarrass her because she cannot explain them. This bodily precision is not a naturalistic description. It is the grief as permanent weather pillar given a specifically female form. A woman's body is the site where the permanent weather of grief manifests most literally and most publicly: the swollen womb visible to everyone, the pregnancy everyone knows about, the birth everyone will eventually ask after. Esther carries her losses in a form that cannot be hidden or managed or channeled into public action. She carries them as body, "a memory of her stillborn inhabited Esther Longtree's every slow act and awkward motion," which is Young's most direct statement of what grief as permanent weather means when it is lived in a woman's body rather than discussed as a philosophical condition.

The mother Catherine represents the fourth feminist position, and it is the most troubling architecturally because it is the most seductive: the total retreat from the world's conditions into a private interior universe so complete and so populated that the external world becomes irrelevant. Catherine never loved any living being. She chose a dead man for her permanent attachment because the dead cannot disappoint, cannot leave, cannot demand a reckoning. Her opium rooms are full of Platonists of Athens and golden-turbaned Egyptians and cherubim and Pythagoras shedding golden feathers, a world entirely of her own making in which she is omniscient and sovereign and attended by everyone she has ever found interesting regardless of century or existence. The illusion as primary pillar flows through Catherine, but so does a feminist critique of what illusion costs: she is beautiful, she is brilliant in her way, she is the most widely travelled person in the novel without ever leaving her bed, and she is also the woman Hannah spent forty years trying to save, the woman whose withdrawal from the world Hannah experienced as a personal defeat.

Young does not adjudicate between these four positions. She does not decide that Hannah's activism is right and Catherine's retreat is wrong, or that Miss MacIntosh's plain practicality is more admirable than Esther's oceanic suffering. She builds all four into the novel's structure as equally necessary, equally true, equally partial. The feminist architecture of Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is not an argument for any particular feminist position. It is a demonstration that the conditions women face, the conditions that produce a Hannah and a Miss MacIntosh and an Esther and a Catherine, are so various, so contradictory, so fully capable of generating completely incompatible responses in completely different women, that the only honest account of feminism is the one that holds all four simultaneously. The AND again. Not this or that response to the world's conditions, but this and that and that and that, all of them real, all of them earned, none of them sufficient.

Young built four feminist architectures into a single novel published in 1965, twenty-three years before her death, in a single act of imaginative construction that feminist theory has not yet fully absorbed. This is the genius claim: not that she wrote about feminism but that she built, in narrative form, a structural argument about feminist experience that exceeds any single theoretical framework because it was built to contain contradiction rather than resolve it. The novel is 1,198 pages long because contradiction at that scale requires that much room.
