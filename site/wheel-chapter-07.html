Research notes: Chapter 7 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 7

Characters: Gertrude Hathaway Tidings · Homer Capehorn · Jacqueline White · Justice O’Leary · Madge Capehorn · Musidora Tidings

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The chapter refuses to stabilize any single consciousness. The bus driver speaks in a voice that slides between direct address, interior monologue, reported speech, and what appears to be hallucination, often within the same sentence:

"Am I seeing with my eyes? Am I hearing with my ears? Is my heart still beating or not beating? Is this my body corporeal? Am I in my body or out of my body?"

This is not rhetorical flourish. It is a genuine ontological crisis about the unity of selfhood. The bus driver cannot locate himself as a singular, bounded person. He exists as a cluster of competing voices and perspectives simultaneously.

The same multiplicity applies to old Doc. He is described as both present and absent, both the man the bus driver sees and the man he dreams, both a practicing physician and someone who has "lost his practice". Doc is several people at once: the young man who slept through calls, the old man who answers calls no one has made, the figure in the flood, the figure in the bus driver's memory, the figure who may be only a projection of the bus driver's own anxiety about death.

Homer and Madge similarly enact plural selfhood through their argument. Homer holds multiple, contradictory positions (trusting the world, denying murder, believing in the afterlife) not as a coherent philosophy but as simultaneous, irresolvable orientations. Madge herself speaks with a voice that is part gossip, part prophet, part detective, part moralist. Neither of them is one person.

Most strikingly, the text multiplies Gertrude into several selves: she is Mrs. Hathaway who was, Mrs. Hathaway who is, Rudolph's widow, Josh's widow, and possibly her own mother's continuation:

"She's more like an old man cursing over her bottle... I guess she's Mr. Hathaway now."

The self here is not even gender-stable.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter systematically refuses to establish a stable boundary between what is real and what is imagined. Old Doc's car is described in terms that make its physical existence doubtful from the start:

"No steering wheel, no tires, no windshield, no motor, no head lights, no running board."

And yet the bus driver sees it. And yet it is there in the flood. The text never resolves whether old Doc's car exists, whether old Doc himself is alive or dead, whether the bus driver is awake or dreaming:

"Oh, no, he must be only dreaming this, for it was not possible that old Doc's car could still be stalled out there in the whirlpool."

But the dream and the waking are never separated. The cave has no exit. The bus driver's perception is his reality, and the text offers no external vantage point from which to correct it. Similarly, the entire narrative of Josh's death, Gertrude's guilt, Mrs. Tidings's crimes, and Musidora's condition is constructed entirely from Madge's voice: a voice that explicitly acknowledges it has no eyewitness authority:

"If you were not there, how did you know so much about it?" "Use your head, Homer Capehorn. Have you no imagination? I didn't have to be there. If I had been there, I might not have known so well."

This is the chapter's most explicit statement of Pillar 2. Knowledge constructed through imagination and inference is presented as more reliable, not less, than eyewitness testimony. The line between illusion and reality is not just blurred; it is inverted.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The chapter is saturated in loss, but crucially, the losses do not resolve or conclude. They accumulate and overlap. Josh is dead. Rudolph is dead. The unnamed baby is dead. Old Doc may be drowning. The bus driver's world is a landscape of losses that have never been processed and never will be:

"Reuben, Gad, Manasseh, Jahaziel, Eliezer, Luke, Dan, Saul, blind as a bat... old Tim Dorey and Red Mayhew and Skinflint and Buck, all gone over the hill."

This litany (biblical names mixed with local names, the ancient dead mixed with the recently dead) is not mourning in any therapeutic sense. It is the bus driver's mind simply registering, again and again, that the world is full of the departed. Grief here is not an event. It is the texture of consciousness.

The flooding landscape itself embodies this. The rain-drowned road, the submerged fields, the "tips of grain like rusted spears lifted above the glassy flood": the world is literally underwater, literally saturated. The weather is grief made physical.

Even Homer's response to Josh's death, his vision of Josh "still handing out the drinks, only more of them", is not consolation so much as the mind's refusal to let loss conclude. He keeps Josh alive by making death continuous with life, which is itself a form of unresolved grief dressed as optimism.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The bus driver's extended monologue is the chapter's central ethical act. He does not stop to help old Doc. He does not turn around. But he watches. He narrates. He refuses to look away even as he refuses to intervene:

"Damned if I'll stop to tow him in... For I am the bus-driver. He's the doctor."

This is a complex ethical position. The bus driver distinguishes his role (the one who carries, who moves forward, who keeps the road) from Doc's role, the one who stops, who attends to the individual. But the bus driver's witnessing is itself a form of attendance. His monologue is an act of sustained attention to Doc's condition, to the landscape, to the accumulated dead.

Madge performs a similar witnessing function in the Homer-Madge dialogue. She has constructed, through imagination and inference, a complete account of Josh's death, Gertrude's psychology, Musidora's condition, and the entire genealogy of the Tidings family. She refuses to simplify. She refuses Homer's repeated attempts to resolve the ambiguity:

"Nobody ever killed anybody. You ought to try to be more broad-minded." "I just face the truth when I see it coming... and it's inevitable."

Madge's insistence on complexity against Homer's desire for resolution is a direct enactment of the Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act pillar. She will not rush toward the comfortable conclusion.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The bus driver's monologue is structurally recursive. He returns repeatedly to the same images (old Doc's car, the missing parts, the flooded road) each time adding new detail, new angle, new implication. The description of Doc's car is rebuilt from scratch multiple times: First: "the ruined chassis of an old Ford among poles, its top dented"

Then: "No steering wheel, no tires, no windshield, no motor, no head lights, no running board"

Then: "No tires, no spare tire, no instruments, no instrument board... no lights, no rear view mirror"

Each return to the car strips it further, builds it differently, arrives at a slightly different conclusion about what it means. This is not repetition for emphasis. It is the text modeling how understanding actually works, not linearly, but recursively, spiraling back to the same material with what was learned in between.

The same structure governs the Homer-Madge dialogue. They return repeatedly to the question of Josh's death, Gertrude's guilt, which baby was killed. Each return adds a layer. Nothing is resolved. The building continues.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter's sentences themselves perform this pillar. The bus driver's speech is a continuous, barely punctuated outpouring that refuses to conclude:

"He's pinned under the steering wheel, and it's rusted in the flood. He's tinkering with her old heart, dead heart, tin heart. He's wrecked from the shoulders up. Let him rot where he is. Quit following my tail lights."

The syntax enacts incompletion. Sentences pile onto each other without resolution. The bus driver never arrives at a verdict about Doc. He never decides whether Doc is admirable or foolish, alive or dead, real or imagined. The text treats this irresolution not as failure of thought but as the honest condition of thought.

Most explicitly, the chapter's central mystery (the death of Josh, the guilt of Gertrude) is never resolved. Madge builds the case with great energy and detail, but the text never confirms or denies it. Homer's counter-arguments are never definitively refuted. The case remains open. The sentence continues.

"It cannot be both true and false."

Homer's logical demand for resolution is presented as naïve, as a failure to understand how experience actually works. Madge's willingness to hold contradictory possibilities (Gertrude guilty, the key possibly turned by a ghost, Josh possibly complicit in his own death) is the chapter's model of intellectual integrity.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Musidora is the chapter's most sustained embodiment of this pillar. She exists entirely at the edges of every category: not quite sane, not quite insane; not quite a mother in any socially recognized sense, yet undeniably a mother; not quite human in the way the other characters are coded as human, yet more alive in some ways than any of them:

"She's flabby and grey-colored, kind of shapeless... those marble-colored eyes, that mottled skin, those grey, ragged curls, water running down her legs."

Musidora's marginality is precisely what makes her the chapter's most revealing figure. She exposes the constructed nature of categories like mother, wife, person, sane. She was sold by her mother for sixteen dollars and a roof that was never built. She exists in a transaction economy that reduces personhood to commodity. And yet she carries her children to the graveyard to feed them, she wears a tin laurel wreath she found in the grass, she fanned herself with what may have been a bird's wing. She has her own dignity, her own ritual, her own relationship to the world; none of which fits any available category.

Old Doc is similarly marginal. He practices medicine without equipment, without patients who have called him, without a functioning car, possibly without a living body. He is the doctor who exists at the edge of what a doctor can be, and it is precisely at this edge that the chapter's deepest questions about care, attention, and the will to help are located.

Even the bus driver occupies a marginal position: the man who moves between places, who belongs nowhere, who witnesses everything and intervenes in nothing.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The chapter's time structure is explicitly geological. The past does not recede; it presses upward through the present. The biblical names in the bus driver's litany (Reuben, Gad, Manasseh) sit directly alongside local names like Red Mayhew and Skinflint, making no distinction between the ancient dead and the recently dead. All of them are equally present, equally pressing.

The Tidings family history operates the same way. The baby killed in the fireplace, an event from before Gertrude and Musidora were old enough to understand it, continues to determine the present. Gertrude's behavior is explained through her mother's behavior. Musidora's condition is explained through what she witnessed as a child. The county's plan to take Musidora's oldest daughter at age twelve is explained as a response to what that child has already seen, and what she will remember having never seen. The past is not behind these people. It is beneath them, constituting the ground they stand on.

The flooding landscape is again the physical correlative. The flood does not erase the land; it covers it while leaving the tips of things, "grain like rusted spears", still visible. The past is submerged but not gone. It presses up through the water.

Most explicitly:

"Transition seemed the only permanence."

This is not a statement about change replacing stability. It is a statement about how the past accumulates as transition: how the sediment of change is itself the only stable thing.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter is acutely self-conscious about language as a constructive act. The bus driver's speech is full of moments where language visibly shapes reality rather than describing it:

"He said it was Mozart, but it did not sound like Mozart to the old bus-driver."

The naming of the music as Mozart is an act of construction: Doc imposes a category, the bus driver refuses it, and neither can appeal to any neutral standard. The music is what the language makes it.

Madge's narrative construction of Josh's death is the chapter's most extended demonstration of language as reality-making. She builds the entire scene (Gertrude sitting with the whiskey bottle, the locked door, the key in the keyhole) from inference and imagination, and her language is so precise, so detailed, so confident that it becomes real within the text's world:

"She was wide awake, old, greasy Gertrude that married two men, brother... wide awake, sitting there with an empty whiskey bottle in her hand, and you can't tell me any different."

The phrase "you can't tell me any different" is revealing. It acknowledges that her construction could be challenged, but asserts that her language has already closed the space for challenge. She has spoken the scene into existence.

The bus driver's repeated catalogues of Doc's missing parts, "no steering wheel, no tires, no windshield", are also linguistic constructions. By naming what is absent, he constructs the presence of absence. The car becomes more vivid through its negations than it would be through positive description.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter is entirely located within the ordinary, the broken, and the marginal. Old Doc: the figure who most clearly carries transcendent significance, the healer who comes without being called, who tends to those not yet born and those already dead: drives a car with no parts, plays violin music that sounds like "old dogs howling, tree branches clashing in the winter winds", and may be drowning in a flooded ditch.

The sacred is not despite these conditions. It is in them. Doc's willingness to answer calls no one has made, to come in the dead of winter with no overcoat, to tend to "the poor humanity that was dead before it was ever born"; this is the chapter's vision of grace. And it is entirely immanent, entirely embodied in the broken, the rusted, the waterlogged.

The bus itself carries this quality. It is described as "wheezing like an old horse", its motor flooded, its wheels turning through oily water. And yet it moves. It carries its passengers. The bus driver, for all his complaints and contradictions, keeps driving. The ordinary act of driving the bus through the flooded night is the chapter's central sacred act, not because it transcends the ordinary but because it is the ordinary, fully inhabited.

Musidora in the graveyard (eating raw hamburgers among the tombstones, wearing a tin laurel wreath, fanning herself with what may be a bird's wing) is the chapter's most concentrated image of immanent transcendence. She has created, without knowing it, a ritual space. The graveyard, the laurel, the feeding of children among the dead: these are the materials of ancient sacred practice, enacted by someone who cannot speak and does not know what she is doing. The sacred is here, in this.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The chapter's deepest structural principle is the crossing point: the self as the place where time intersects itself, constituted by what came before and constituting what comes after.

Old Doc embodies this most completely. He tends to "those generations of his, that's all he thinks of, the poor humanity that was dead before it was ever born". He is the figure who stands at the crossing of the not-yet-born and the already-dead, trying to pull life through. His medical kit is a violin case: the instrument of generation and mourning simultaneously.

The bus driver's litany of names, biblical patriarchs alongside local dead, places him explicitly at the crossing point of historical time. He carries within his consciousness the sediment of all these generations, and his driving forward through the night is the act of carrying that sediment into the future.

Most explicitly, the chapter ends with the question of Musidora's oldest daughter, Wonet (or Jacqueline: the text gives her two names, which is itself significant):

"She will remember the things of the night, and she will tell."

This child, who has seen what she should not have seen, who will remember what she never consciously witnessed, who will carry the knowledge of Musidora's suffering and Gertrude's crimes and Mrs. Tidings's original violence forward into a future the text does not show; she is the crossing point made explicit. She did not choose this obligation. It is structural. The ancestors are in the sediment. The carrying continues.

The chapter's final exchange (Homer still hungry, Madge correcting him about which child she meant) refuses any transcendent conclusion. The bus moves forward. The rain continues. The crossing point is not a moment of revelation. It is the ongoing condition of being alive in a world where the past presses up through the ground and the not-yet-born are already making claims on the present.
