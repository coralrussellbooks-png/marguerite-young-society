Anatole France, William James, and the Philosophical Architecture of Miss MacIntosh, My Darling

William James's Principles of Psychology is the work Young named when she spoke of what shaped her. She studied it “as if it were a Bible” (American Audio Prose Library interview with Kay Bonetti, New York, May 1983).

This chapter argues that the architecture of Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is built on three interlocking foundations that have not previously been described as a unified structure: the philosophical method of Anatole France, the psychology of William James, and the unresolved tension between Epicurus and Plato that both France and the novel hold permanently open. Understanding this structure does not simplify the novel. It explains why the novel refuses to be simplified, and why that refusal is its most serious philosophical commitment.

I. The Garden: Anatole France and the Method of Irresolution

Marguerite Young was a reader of Anatole France. Through her grandmother he became the writer she loved most as a girl, and she read all of him in French; when her grandmother died, Young's first thought was that "now she can tell Anatole France that she has a little granddaughter on Earth who adores him" (Marguerite Young, interview with Charles Ruas, "The Art of Fiction No. 66," The Paris Review, Fall 1977). France was not merely a favorite; he was, for Young, a model of what literary philosophy could do when it refused to conclude. France won the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1921, the year his short tale Marguerite, which he called "this little trifle," appeared in English. He was known for his socialist sympathies, his wit, his deep skepticism of dogma in any form, and his extraordinary gift for holding incompatible ideas in the same hand without crushing either.

The work of France's that bears most directly on Miss MacIntosh is The Garden of Epicurus (1894), not a novel but a book of short reflections, followed in the English edition by a handful of tales and dialogues. One of them, "Aristos and Polyphilos on the Language of Metaphysics," is a conversation between two friends about how philosophers grind words down like old coins until their physical meaning is worn away. Another, "In the Elysian Fields," is set among the shades of the dead, "souls of all times and all countries," great philosophers "side by side with poor savages." But the book is not an Epicurean tract. It is a staging of the encounter between Epicurean materialism and the persistent human longing that materialism cannot satisfy. France was, throughout his career, drawn to the transitional period between paganism and Christianity: the historical moment when a philosophy of the body and its pleasures met a philosophy of the ascending soul and neither could eliminate the other. He staged that encounter rather than resolving it. His garden is not a place of settled doctrine. It is a place where the argument continues.

France also inhabited the Platonic tradition, not as a believer but as an ironist who found the ladder of Eros both beautiful and unreachable. In The Revolt of the Angels (1914), arguably his most philosophically dense novel, Satan himself discovers that if he overthrew God, he would become God: the same trap wearing a different name. The lesson is not that revolution is wrong but that any system that replaces one authority with another has missed the actual problem. The actual problem is interior. France located it in the self's irresolvable desire for something it cannot name and cannot stop reaching toward. That desire has a name in Plato. It is Eros.

The Revolt of the Angels speaks directly to Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, and it is perhaps the most philosophically compressed of all his works. It tells the story of Arcade, the guardian angel of young Maurice d'Esparvieu. While Maurice, “fordone with the toils of a sensual life,” lies asleep, Arcade reads his way through the vast d'Esparvieu library and comes out saying, “I have learnt. I have thought. I have lost my faith.” He takes human form, without wings, and joins a revolutionary movement of fallen angels. When the revolution is ready, its leader, Satan, dreams of victory and wakes knowing something that stops the revolution entirely: “God, conquered, will become Satan; Satan, conquering, will become God.” If he overthrew God, he would become just like God. The structure he was rebelling against would simply be reproduced under a new name. Arcade understands the lesson. Replacing one authority with another changes nothing unless, as France writes, in ourselves and in ourselves alone we attack and destroy Ialdabaoth. Ialdabaoth, according to France, is God's secret name. It means the child who wanders.

The child who wanders is Vera Cartwheel. This is not a casual correspondence. Vera's entire journey in the novel, from her farewell to Miss MacIntosh in Chapter 13 through the long bus ride that frames the narrative and the accumulated weight of memory and hallucination and grief that constitutes her consciousness, is a wandering. She is not going somewhere. She is moving through the only territory available to her: the interior, where the authorities that once organized the world have collapsed and no replacement authority has arrived. The Ialdabaoth principle, that the problem is not the wrong authority but the assumption that authority resolves anything at all, is Young's deepest philosophical commitment. Vera does not find a new Miss MacIntosh. She finds that the wandering is the life, and that the child who wanders is not lost but is rather the only kind of self that is being honest about what selfhood actually is.

What France gave Young, understood as a structural principle rather than a set of doctrines, is this: the philosophical work happens in the space between the garden and the ladder. Not in the resolution of the tension between Epicurus and Plato but in the sustained inhabitation of it. The novel that takes this seriously cannot end with a position. It can only end when the conversation has been fully conducted. France's novels are long because the conversation is long. Young's novel is longer because she understood that the stream of consciousness is the only medium adequate to the full duration of that conversation. And the child at the center of that stream is Ialdabaoth: the wanderer, the one who cannot be returned to the cave once she has seen what the cave contains.

II. The Stream: William James and the Shape of Consciousness

Young stated directly, in interviews, that she admired and was influenced by both Henry and William James. She spoke of “the profound influence upon me of William James and Saint Augustine in ‘The City of God’” (Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs, “A Conversation with Marguerite Young,” Review of Contemporary Fiction 23, no. 1, Spring 2003 (1989 original)). William James's Principles of Psychology, published in 1890, is the book that gave the phrase stream of consciousness to literature and philosophy.

James's argument in the Principles can be summarized in four propositions: every state of consciousness is personal; states of consciousness are always changing; consciousness is sensibly continuous; and within consciousness there is a shifting and focused attention. Young keeps three of them and breaks with the third. For James, consciousness “does not appear to itself chopped up in bits”; “It is nothing jointed; it flows” (The Principles of Psychology, 1890, chapter 9). In Chapter 48 Mr. Spitzer finds otherwise: “Consciousness was ever that which was sporadic, fluttering, not constant even in this life, just as sustained attention was impossible.” Young builds the break out of James's own book. He conceded that “There is no such thing as voluntary attention sustained for more than a few seconds at a time” (chapter 11) and described our mental life as “an alternation of flights and perchings” (chapter 9). Where James holds that consciousness after a gap feels as if it “belonged together with the consciousness before it,” Mr. Spitzer asks whether consciousness could “know when consciousness faded for the last time,” and sees it as “fading, flickering, going out.” That image sits close to the Abhidharma Buddhist account of mind as a series of momentary events, each arising and ceasing, a parallel Young does not name.

But James gave Young more than a model of the mind. He gave her permission to take seriously what the scientific and rationalist traditions had decided to exclude. In Is Life Worth Living?, James makes the point that much of human mental activity is forever closed to other minds in the way that the meaning of a surgical procedure is closed to the dog on the operating table. This is not a counsel of despair for James. It is liberating: it means that the things we cannot prove are not therefore absent, and that the irreducible interior life is not a failure of rigor but a feature of what consciousness actually is. Young's characters, especially Mr. Spitzer, inhabit this insight as their permanent condition. They know they cannot fully know. They continue anyway.

James also described what he called the stream's fringes: the felt sense of relations, the halo of half-formed meaning that surrounds every clear thought, the transitional states that connect one moment of consciousness to the next. These fringes are what most narrative fiction omits because they are difficult to render and seem to impede the plot. Young renders nothing else. The fringes are the novel. They are where the philosophical work is done: in the almost-thought, the remembered-and-not-quite, the grief that is not grief yet but has already begun.

III. The Blind God: Plato's Eros and the Novel's Two Poles

The Symposium is Plato's most dramatic philosophical work and his most direct treatment of Eros. It is, structurally, a contest of speeches: multiple voices, multiple frameworks for understanding love, none of them finally authoritative. Socrates arrives late, having become lost in thought on the way. Alcibiades arrives drunk and crashes the proceedings. The party ends in disorder with people falling asleep mid-conversation, and Socrates rises in the morning and walks to the Lyceum as though nothing unusual had occurred. The form of the Symposium enacts its argument: no single speech on Eros is sufficient, the truth is approached through the accumulation of competing accounts, and the one who knows most is the one who can hold the most perspectives simultaneously without demanding that they resolve.

This is the formal model for Miss MacIntosh, My Darling. The novel is a symposium at 1,198-page scale. Each major character holds a position on love, on reality, on death, on the self. None of them wins. The structure is the argument.

The specific Platonic concept that surfaces in the novel is Diotima's Ladder, also called the Ladder of Eros: the ascent from love of a particular beautiful body, through love of all beautiful bodies, through love of beautiful souls, through love of beautiful institutions and knowledge, to the final rung, the Form of Beauty itself, beauty as it purely is rather than as it appears in any particular instance. Diotima teaches Socrates that Eros is not a god but a daemon, a spirit intermediate between the human and the divine, born of Plenty and Poverty at Aphrodite's birthday banquet. Eros inherits from his father the pursuit of beauty and from his mother the fact of never having enough. He is perpetually reaching and perpetually lacking what he reaches for. That is his nature. That is love's nature.

Mr. Spitzer, the novel's central consciousness in its second half, is the character who carries this. He is an old lawyer who has never married, never reached his love, never arrived at the rung of the ladder where beauty is finally known. He dreams instead of the blind Eros. Young's exact phrase, in Chapter 51, is this:

yet he dreamed of that blind Eros for whom there was no time and no face of love and no act of superficial recognition of subject or object and no love in this flesh and in this body but the love exceeding the limitations of the individual and of life and death

The blindness is not incidental. The traditional image of Eros is blindfolded, shooting arrows at random. But Young's blind Eros is something else: a love that has moved past the individual body, past the individual face, past the recognition of subject and object, into something that exceeds the limitations of individual life and death entirely. This is Diotima's highest rung, the Form of Beauty itself, but filtered through France's irony: Mr. Spitzer dreams of it. He does not reach it. The dream is real and the ladder is real and the arrival is not.

This is not tragic in the conventional sense. It is Young's philosophical position about what love actually is: the perpetual motion of reaching, not the attainment. And this maps directly onto Epicurus's counterpoint. The Epicurean maxim that death is nothing to us, that what is good is easy to get and what is terrible is easy to endure, that consciousness simply ceases and therefore the fear of death is irrational: this is the philosophy of the body, of the present moment, of the physical world taken seriously on its own terms. Young did not accept it as sufficient. But she did not discard it. She put it in the same novel as the blind Eros and let them address each other for 1,198 pages.

IV. The Pivot: Chapter Thirteen and the Garden of the Deaf

The moment in the novel where the France-Epicurus-Plato structure becomes most explicitly traceable is Chapter 13, the chapter of the Garden of the Deaf. This is the pivot of the entire novel: the chapter that opens with Vera's "Farewell to the last certitude," her farewell to the Miss MacIntosh she thought she knew, and looks ahead to the night her secret will be seen (Chapter 63); the chapter in which the stable world Vera had organized around Miss MacIntosh as anchor of the real is destroyed. After Chapter 13, Vera enters the novel's long second movement, the search for what was lost.

Chapter 13 opens with the farewell to Miss MacIntosh and passes immediately into the surreal, neglected garden where the ear is dead, sound is absent, communication has failed. Vera wanders through ghostly images: statues, flowers that fly away when touched, chessmen moving invisibly, the presence of historical figures from the history of medicine. The garden is not just a setting. It is the philosophical condition: the world after the loss of the organizing principle that made reality legible.

Young placed Anatole France's garden, Epicurus, and Heraclitus together in this chapter, in her own words rather than their names. Life in Catherine's gardens is "a perpetual garden party omitting the tragic spirit." The life-sized chessmen move "as if played by invisible players who could perceive an order in multiple confusions of movements and sounds, a secret plan," Heraclitus's "Time is a child playing draughts" and "The hidden harmony is better than the obvious" retold in Young's language. His river runs through the whole novel the same way: "That same stream which a child could wade in was that same river which a man could drown in" (Chapter 5); "all things flowed into all things like water flowing into water" (Chapter 52). This is not background. It is the philosophical frame she built into the chapter's foundation. The garden of the deaf is the Epicurean garden stripped of its consolation: not the garden of pleasure and tranquility but the garden after the ear is dead, where even the Epicurean promise that what is terrible is easy to endure has broken down because the person carrying the burden is fourteen years old and has just lost the only thing she trusted.

And beneath it, Heraclitus: the river that is never the same twice, the war that is the father of all things, the opposites in perpetual conflict that produce the only harmony available. Young did not choose Epicurus in isolation. She chose the conversation between Heraclitus and Epicurus, the philosopher who said everything flows and the philosopher who said the flow is all there is and therefore there is nothing to fear. France staged that conversation in his garden. Young staged it again, at greater length, with a fourteen-year-old girl as the witness.

V. The Structure: Miss MacIntosh as Symposium

The argument of the previous four sections can now be stated as a unified claim about the novel's architecture.

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is constructed on the model of Plato's Symposium as filtered through the ironic method of Anatole France and informed by the psychology of William James's Principles of Psychology. The novel is a symposium: a sustained multi-voice contest of positions on love, reality, consciousness, and death, in which no single voice prevails and the form of the contest is itself the philosophical argument. The Symposium's structural principle, that the truth about Eros is approached through the accumulation of competing accounts rather than the declaration of a single correct position, is the structural principle of Miss MacIntosh at every level from the sentence to the whole.

The novel's two philosophical poles are Epicurus and Plato. Vera inhabits the Epicurean pole: the body, the present moment, the physical world taken seriously, the garden where pleasure and grief are both immediate and both real. Mr. Spitzer inhabits the Platonic pole: the blind Eros, the love that exceeds the individual, the perpetual reaching toward a form of beauty that is never arrived at, the ladder whose highest rung is always one rung higher than where he stands. Neither pole cancels the other. The novel holds them both for 1,198 pages.

France gives the method. He showed Young that the philosophical novel does not argue toward a conclusion. It stages a conversation that has been going on since Heraclitus and Epicurus looked at the same river and drew different conclusions. The conversation is still going. The novel is not an intervention in the conversation. It is the conversation. James gives the psychology: a consciousness that is always personal and always changing, whose attention will not hold, and which, in Young's hands, flickers rather than flows.

Plato gives the animating question. What is the soul reaching toward when it reaches? Diotima's answer is the Form of Beauty, apprehensible finally only through reason, after the long ascent from body to soul to knowledge to the thing itself. Young's answer is the blind Eros: the dream of a love that exceeds the limitations of the individual and of life and death, held by a man who never arrives, who has spent his entire long life in the approach, and who dreams rather than attains. This is not a failure of the Platonic ladder. It is France's ironic translation of it: the ladder is real, the reaching is real, the highest rung is what the reaching is for, and the reaching is the life.

VI. The Shadow Count and the Symposium Structure

Two numerical facts about the novel support this reading and merit their own brief treatment.

The word shadow appears over 800 times in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling. The two shadows that matter most are the ones that go missing. Vera dances with her shadow in Chapter 9 ("I danced with my shadow"), and after the farewell of Chapter 13 she never speaks of her shadow again. Miss MacIntosh, once Vera has seen her truly, fears that very loss: "what should I be if I should lose my shadow, if I should have no shadow here?" (Chapter 64). The revelation destroys the stable surface that a shadow requires.

This is Plato's Allegory of the Cave made literal and distributed across 1,198 pages. In the Cave, shadows are all the prisoners can know about the world. The philosopher who escapes the cave and sees the sun directly cannot return to the world of shadows without confusion. Vera's loss of Miss MacIntosh is her forced exit from the cave: she sees Miss MacIntosh as she actually is rather than as the shadow she had been content to accept. The seeing does not liberate her. It destroys the organizing structure of her world. This is Young's revision of Plato: the philosopher who escapes the cave does not arrive at wisdom. She arrives at the garden where the ear is dead. The ascent is real but the landing is not what was promised.

The question mark appears almost 4,000 times in the novel. Young built the entire novel around the Socratic method of relentless questioning. This is the Symposium's formal principle applied at the sentence level: the question is the unit of philosophical inquiry, not the assertion. The novel that questions 4,000 times is not a novel without answers. It is a novel that understands that the answer to a question about consciousness, love, death, and the self is another question, and that the stream of consciousness is the medium in which those questions are continuously posed and never finally resolved, because resolution would require the stream to stop, and streams do not stop.

VII. What This Means for Reading the Novel

Knowing this structure does not make the novel easier to read. Nothing makes Miss MacIntosh, My Darling easy. But it changes what you are looking for and what you are experiencing when you find it.

When Mr. Spitzer thinks of the blind Eros, you are not reading an obscure Platonic allusion buried in a difficult novel. You are at the novel's Platonic pole, the point furthest from the Epicurean garden, the point where the reaching has become so refined and so persistent that the specific face and body of love have dissolved and what remains is the pure motion of desire toward something beyond individual life and death. This is Diotima's highest rung. Mr. Spitzer never arrives there. He dreams of it. The dream is real.

When Vera wanders the Garden of the Deaf in Chapter 13 and Anatole France, Epicurus, and Heraclitus are beside her in that garden, the reader is at the novel's Epicurean pole, the point where the body's grief is immediate and unmediated and the philosophical consolations have not yet arrived, may not arrive, may be irrelevant to a fourteen-year-old whose world has just ended. France is there not as an authority but as a model: this is what it looks like when the conversation between materialism and transcendence is staged honestly, without premature resolution, in a garden where the ear is dead.

And the 1,198 pages give the stream of consciousness, which is personal and changing and broken and flickering and full of fringes and half-thoughts and griefs that are not yet grief, the room it requires to be honest about what it is. Young did not write a 1,198-page novel because she had too much to say. She wrote it because anything shorter would have been a lie about consciousness.

The novel is a symposium. France taught her the method. James taught her the psychology. Plato taught her the question. She spent eighteen years answering it without ever answering it, which is exactly what the question requires.
