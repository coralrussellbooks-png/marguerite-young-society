Chapter 14

On Chapter 14 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Woman in Young's Magnum Opus.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Musidora Tidings

Mr. Spitzer comes apart in this chapter, quietly and early. There are two Mr. Spitzers, then four brothers, then a "case of Spitzer versus Spitzer," and the shadowy doubles turn up in the playing cards whose suits become crosses and roses. This is the instability of male authority, and it is plural selfhood arriving in its central figure, a man who cannot be counted as one, litigating against himself across a deck of cards.

Catherine cannot say which of them is real and which imagined, and the not-knowing is the real and the dream as one country, the two versions equally present at her bedside.

Her fixation is the black coachman, who served the family in a role between the living and the dead and is now buried, and whom she insists on keeping present in full livery with his carriage and horses. This is the invisible labor of the servant and the erased figure of the minority, and it is the book's practice with the erased: it keeps them at the threshold, present and unaccounted for, the coachman held at the seam between life and death exactly as Musidora was and as Martha will be.

Spitzer names himself a "lost soul" in a woman's world, and the phrase is not only his defeat. It is a man reaching the same dissolving bottom the women reach, which is why he belongs at the center and not at the margin. His talk of law as the "practice of liars," of a cosmic harmony beyond human law, is the book setting aside the whole apparatus of sorting, the counting and owning and judging, for a world where the categories no longer hold.
