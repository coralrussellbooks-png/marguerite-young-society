Definition from research

Chapter 7

  • “Old Jesse Ferguson” - may refer to the following: 1) Jesse Babcock Ferguson (1819-1870) was a highly acclaimed Christian preacher known for his eloquent sermons and ability to establish congregations in rural Kentucky. Later in his career, he became involved with Spiritualism and even worked as a stage manager for the Davenport brothers, who were exposed as frauds. He is also known for advocating Universalism, the belief in the eventual salvation of all souls through divine grace. 2) Jesse Ferguson (1824-1900) was a pioneer in the settlement of Tumwater, Washington. He was one of the founders of the first American settlement in Western Washington. In 1847, he partnered in the first sawmill on Puget Sound. He was considered a "venerable pioneer" and the "patriarch of the Belmore community" in his later years. He was the last surviving adult from the original Tumwater pioneer party that arrived in 1844/45. 3) Plessy v. Ferguson, the Supreme Court decision in 1896 that upheld racial segregation.

Chapter 79

  • Modern Spiritualism is widely considered to have begun in March 1848 in Hydesville, New York, following the claims of the Fox sisters, Margaret and Kate, who reported hearing mysterious rappings that they believed were spirit messages. The movement originated in an area of upstate New York known as the "Burned-over District," which was already a center for religious fervor and movements like Millerism and Mormonism during the Second Great Awakening. The movement quickly spread, with early adherents seeking direct communication with the spirits of the deceased. By the 1850s, it had spread across the country and internationally. In the 1850s, the movement attracted the attention of scientists who began investigating the reported supernatural occurrences. The credibility of the movement was challenged, especially in 1888 when the Fox sisters confessed to faking the original rappings. However, many believers continued their faith, and the movement adapted. Influential figures like Emma Hardinge Britten, a medium and lecturer, used the platform of Spiritualism to advocate for social causes like women's rights and temperance. Spiritualism experienced a major revival after World War I, as many people sought to connect with loved ones who had died in the war. The movement continued to develop organizations and gather followers, with communities like Cassadaga, Florida, becoming known as centers for spiritualist activities. Throughout the mid-20th century, Spiritualism persisted, adapting to new generations and social changes, and leaving a lasting impact on American religious and cultural history.

Chapter 79

  • Spiritualism is the metaphysical school of thought opposing physicalism and also is the category of all spiritual beliefs from ancient to modern. They believe that spirit mediums are gifted to carry on such communication, but that anyone may become a medium through study and practice. They believe that spirits are capable of growth and perfection, progressing through higher spheres or planes, and that the afterlife is not a static state, but one in which spirits evolve. The two beliefs, that contact with spirits is possible, and that spirits may dwell on a higher plane, lead to a third belief, that spirits can provide knowledge about moral and ethical issues, as well as about God and the afterlife. Many believers speak of spirit guides or specific spirits, often contacted, and relied upon for worldly and spiritual guidance. According to Spiritualists, anyone may receive spirit messages, but formal communication sessions are held by mediums, who claim thereby to receive information about the afterlife. Spiritualism developed and reached its peak growth in membership from the 1840s to the 1920s, especially in English-speaking countries. By 1897, spiritualism was said to have more than eight million followers in the United States and Europe, mostly drawn from the middle and upper classes.
