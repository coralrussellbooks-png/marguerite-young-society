Chapter 48

← Chapter 47 · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 49 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary · Unfinished Is Honest

The Questions

Cousin Hannah left forty keys for forty trunks, and “among them all, no master key.” What does a lock with no master key say about a life?

Mr. Spitzer dreads a world where “every argument had been solved…every quarrel had been finished.” Why would the finished be the thing to fear?

His music has notes missing and false notes added by an engraver. Is “the greatest music…that which had no meaning”?

A palsied violinist, he thinks, makes errors “of greater beauty than the perfections of others.” What is the beauty in the flaw?

The forty trunks with no master key are Unfinished Is Honest made into an inheritance. Cousin Hannah left “forty keys with which to open forty trunks,” kept in her bed so none might find them, “and among them all, no master key.” There is no single key that opens everything, no final answer that unlocks the all of a life, and Mr. Spitzer, fumbling key against lock, half-asleep, wishes for “a locksmith to open all locks, to turn all keys at once,” and there is none. Worse, “there might be one box more than forty boxes.” The count itself will not close. The sorting of a life cannot be completed, and the master key that would complete it does not exist.

What Mr. Spitzer dreads is the finished. He imagines with horror a world “if every argument had been solved…if every quarrel had been finished like an echo no longer reproducing itself,” if there were “neither theme nor counter-theme,” if the last question were answered and the last silence closed. The completed world is the dead one. This is Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary stated as dread: the thing to fear is not the unresolved but the resolved, the sealed totality where nothing more can move or sound. Mr. Spitzer, who longs for a final answer, also knows that the final answer would be the end of the music, and the chapter holds both his longing and his dread without choosing.

His music is made of gaps and errors, and the chapter dares to prefer them. Notes are “missing from his score,” an engraver has added false notes “to confuse future musicians,” and he cannot always tell the intended sound from the punctuation mark mistaken for a sound. “Perhaps the greatest music was that which had no meaning.” The unfinished, the corrupted, the gapped score is not a lesser music but possibly the greater one, the meaning fading “just when” he almost sees it, the almost-seeing worth more than any settled sense. This is Unfinished Is Honest at the level of art: the flawed score, the missing note, the meaning that will not resolve, held as richer than the perfect and complete.

The palsied violinist carries the all of it. “A great violinist whose wrist shakes with palsy as his bow touches the strings is still greater than the mediocre violinist whose wrist shakes not.” The error is not the enemy of the beauty; it can be the beauty. Mr. Spitzer’s own “numb fingers,” whose “errors were of greater beauty than the perfections of others,” are the book’s image of a life valued for its flaws rather than despite them, the shaking hand truer than the steady one. The perfect performance, like the solved world and the master key, would be the lesser thing. The trembling, the gap, the unfinished sorting, are where the greatness is.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that wants the forty trunks finally sorted, the master key found, and the reading that takes the missing notes and the palsy for defects to be corrected. Neither the completed inheritance nor the perfected performance is what the chapter values.

The footing is that there is no master key, no single answer that opens a life, and the count will not even close; that the finished world, every argument solved, is the thing to dread, the sealed totality where nothing more can move; and that the gapped score and the palsied hand are not lesser but possibly greater, the error a beauty rather than a flaw. The unfinished is the richer thing, and the completed is the dead one. Mr. Spitzer fumbles at the locks with no master key, and the chapter does not hand him one.

For Reflection

What in one’s life would a master key open, and is the wish for one worth wanting?

Why might a world with every quarrel finished be a world to dread rather than to hope for?

Where is the beauty in a flaw one has spent effort trying to correct?
