Chapter 50: The Hyphen Was All

← Chapter 49 · Guide Home · Chapter 51 →

Research notes: Chapter 50

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Death and Mortality · Dreams and Imagination · Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos

Symbols: Fireflies · Keys, Doors, and Locks · The Sea and Water

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Astronomy · Music and Composers · The Bible · The History of Opium · Women's Suffrage

There is a moment near the end of Chapter 50 where Mr. Spitzer recalls Cousin Hannah's hyphenated name, Freemount-Snowden, and reflects that the hyphen was more important to her “than the two names it joined.” The hyphen was all.

Read that slowly. The two names on either side of the hyphen are the public record, the inheritance, the official account. The hyphen is the connection itself: the joining, the and-also, the refusal to be reduced to only one thing. Cousin Hannah was Freemount and Snowden and the passage between them. She was the hyphen. And when she lost her love, she lost what the hyphen had been pointing toward: the person or the life that would have made the joining make sense.

Chapter 50 is the chapter where Mr. Spitzer finally turns away from Cousin Hannah's death and back toward his own existence, and finds that his own existence is the most uncertain thing he has yet tried to describe. He is a dream dreamed by another dreamer. He is a flyleaf between testaments. He is the one who dreamed not, left to live inside other people's dreams because he has no dream of his own. He is alive after his death, or dead during his life, and he cannot reliably distinguish which state he occupies at any given moment.

What actually happens in Chapter 50

The chapter opens with Mr. Spitzer's universe going “off key and out of course”: familiar structures multiplying, two golden chairs where there should be one, the question of which chair is real and which is its dream. He is cautious: he cannot touch “the strings of the constellation Lyre for fear” of touching something imaginary and finding it has more substance than he expected, or less. Everything seems slightly offset from itself, the sensorium failing not in any catastrophic way but in the way of a clock that runs a few minutes slow: the readings are all there, but they no longer correspond reliably to the time.

He reflects on what he learned and did not learn from Catherine: the opium lady, the invalid, the woman who blurred life and death so thoroughly that her experience offers him no reliable map. He did not learn from her the sense of mortality, or the failure of perception. He absorbed something else instead: the atmosphere of a world where the borders are permeable, where the ordinary distinctions have been smoothed away by years of disregarding them.

“All that happened happened only once.” This is the chapter's most quietly devastating formulation: the non-recurrence of events, the impossibility of going back, the knowledge that every moment is singular and cannot be repeated or revisited. A train derailed in grass. A star derailed. The world spinning slightly off its expected axis, and no one coming to correct it.

The chapter closes on the firefly (his key of gold, his soul, the small light he reads by in the vast dark) and on Cousin Hannah's hyphen, and on the pause in a broken melody that her death represents. A pause that may be a rest. A silence that is not the end of the music, only a place where the music held its breath.

A dream dreamed by another dreamer

This phrase, the central formulation of Mr. Spitzer's condition in Chapter 50, reaches back through the novel to Chapter 37's unanswered question: but who was the dreamer? Young has been circling this question for thirteen chapters, and here she gives Mr. Spitzer's version of the answer: he is not the dreamer. He is the dream. He is what happens inside someone else's dreaming, a figure that arose in another consciousness and has been navigating a world he did not generate and cannot fully read.

This is not despair, though it sounds like it. Young is working through a genuine philosophical possibility: what if some people (the cautious, the second-remove, the ones who missed the present moment by arriving a minute late) are simply not the originators of the reality they inhabit? What if they are always living by other people's terms, other people's dreamwork, other people's versions of what the world is? This would explain why Mr. Spitzer's sensorium is always slightly off: he is reading the transcript of someone else's direct experience, and transcripts are always approximate.

The novel's own structure mirrors this. Vera is the narrator, but she is narrating something she partially witnessed and partially imagined and partially inherited from the people who came before her. The novel is itself a dream dreamed by several dreamers simultaneously, each one contributing a fragment of the whole, none of them holding the master copy.

A flyleaf between testaments

He sees himself as a “mere flyleaf stuck between two passages or testaments.”

A flyleaf is the blank page at the beginning or end of a book: the paper between the cover and the first page of text, or between the last page of text and the back cover. It is part of the book. It is also not part of the text. It is the place where someone might write a dedication, or a name, or nothing at all. It exists between what comes before and what comes after, belonging fully to neither.

Mr. Spitzer between the Old Testament and the New. Between his brother's life and his own. Between Catherine's household and the world outside it. Between Cousin Hannah's death and whatever comes next for the novel. He is the transition: the connective tissue, the white space, the pause that allows two very different things to be bound together in the same volume without directly touching.

And yet the flyleaf matters. Remove it and the cover damages the text. The flyleaf protects. The flyleaf receives the inscription that makes the book personal, that makes it this copy rather than any copy. Mr. Spitzer is the novel's flyleaf: not the story, but the thing that holds the story in its hands and keeps it from being damaged by the world. This is not nothing. Young means it not to be nothing.

The sea urchins who knew not

Young gives Chapter 50 one of the novel's most philosophically dizzying sentences: the “sea urchins who knew not whether they lived or were dead or were sleeping—or if they knew, we knew not that they knew, and they knew not that we knew not that they knew not.”

This is a precise rendering of the problem of other minds, taken to its recursive extreme. We cannot know whether sea urchins are conscious. We cannot know whether they know they are conscious. We cannot know whether they know we are uncertain about whether they are conscious. And they cannot know any of this about us. Each layer of ignorance reflects another layer, and the reflection continues past the point where any of the parties involved can track it.

The sea urchin is an apt figure for this meditation: it is one of the longest-living creatures on earth, some individuals surviving two hundred years, yet it is famously difficult to tell a live sea urchin from a dead one. It exists in a state of apparent ambiguity that is entirely real: the animal is either alive or not, the question is only our ability to tell. Young finds in this creature the perfect emblem for Mr. Spitzer's own condition: alive or dead or sleeping, and perhaps no messages have ever arrived to clarify the matter. Perhaps no messages were ever sent.

Gleaming cells that knew not death or life

Mr. Spitzer contemplates “those gleaming cells which knew not death or life or these definitions, knew not whether they lived or were dead, for perhaps no messages had ever arrived.”

Young is pushing panpsychism in a new direction here: not the agonized attribution of consciousness to everything, but its inverse, the possibility that certain things exist in a state prior to the categories of consciousness and death, never having received the information that would allow them to know which side they're on. Not ambiguous. Not uncertain. Simply prior to the question.

To All My Darlings points to Henrietta Lacks and the HeLa cell line: cells taken without consent from a dying woman in 1951 that became the most widely used human cell line in history, still alive in laboratories worldwide while Henrietta Lacks herself died and was buried. Her cells are immortal. She was mortal. The gleaming cells knew not death or life; they simply continued, dividing and dividing, neither the body that generated them nor the woman who was that body, something else entirely. Young was writing in 1965; the HeLa story was not yet widely known. But the image she gives Mr. Spitzer is prescient: life persisting past the point where anyone intended it to, in forms that have escaped the definitions that once organized them.

Nothing twice the same

“All that happened happened only once.” “There was nothing twice the same.”

This is the chapter's most austere formulation, and it hits differently after fifty chapters of a novel that has done everything it can to make time non-linear, to make the past present, to insist that memory and reality and dream are all equally real. You might expect Young, given all that, to offer some consolation through repetition: some sense that what happened happens again, that nothing is finally lost because everything recurs.

She does not. She gives Mr. Spitzer this instead: once, only. Every moment is singular. Every departure is the last time anyone leaves by that particular door in that particular light at that particular age. Every encounter with Cousin Hannah, in memory or dream or the afterimage the dead leave in the consciousness of those who knew them; each one is different from the last. The event happened once. What we have now are the echoes, and echoes are not repetitions; they are the evidence of the original's passage, distorted by every surface they have bounced off since.

This is, Young suggests, both the sorrow and the dignity of existence. The singularity is the sorrow. The singularity is also what makes each moment the thing it is rather than a copy of something else. There was nothing twice the same. Including Mr. Spitzer. Including you.

The hyphen was all

Cousin Hannah's hyphenated name, Freemount-Snowden, carried in it the story of her identity: the two family lines she was born between, the social structures she inhabited, the formal record of who she was supposed to be. But Young's insight is that for Cousin Hannah, none of that was the point. The hyphen was the point. The connection itself. The joining.

The suffrage movement was a hyphen: between the world as it was and the world as it should be, between the legal status of women before the vote and after it, between this life and a life not yet available. Cousin Hannah devoted herself to the hyphen, not to either of the things it connected. She was the passage, the transition, the place where one thing becomes another thing.

And when she lost her love (whatever that love was, whoever the blackamoor, whatever the secret the hyphen was meant to join) she was left with the two names and no connection between them. The hyphen remained in her name. The thing it had been reaching toward was gone. She carried the form of the connection without the content. This is, Young suggests, a particular kind of grief: to be defined by a relationship to something you can no longer reach.

The firefly as key of gold

Among all the vast and complex imagery of Chapter 50 (the frozen cosmos, the Cassiopeia chair, the constellation Lyre, the golden horn of Istanbul, the sidereal clocks) Young gives Mr. Spitzer a single small light as his key of gold: the little firefly. His soul. The light he reads by.

The firefly has appeared throughout the novel as one of its recurring emblems of consciousness: brief, intermittent, visible only in darkness, the light produced not by combustion but by a chemical process that generates no heat. Cool light. Living light. A light that flashes and goes dark and flashes again, following a pattern no individual firefly chooses, each one part of a larger display that only makes sense from a distance.

Mr. Spitzer is a firefly. His consciousness is a firefly. His love, expressed by indirection, mourning the extinct, carrying alpenhorns into the fog: all of it is firefly light: brief, cool, intermittent, beautiful specifically because it is not the sun. You need the dark for it. You need the absence of stronger light. In the great blazing consciousness of Catherine or the blazing trajectory of Cousin Hannah, Mr. Spitzer would be invisible. In the long dark of the aftermath, he is exactly visible enough.

What to hold onto

Chapter 50 is Mr. Spitzer's most fully self-aware chapter: the chapter where he looks at himself with the same steady patience he has given to everything else and reports what he sees without flinching. He is a dream dreamed by another dreamer. He is a flyleaf. He is the one who dreamed not. He reads by firefly light. He is a tide that advances and retreats without ever quite arriving or quite receding.

What Young is doing with this, across these chapters of his long vigil, is building the novel's most unexpected argument: that the cautious, the second-remove, the men who value their dignity more than their lives and arrive a minute late to every experience and cannot maintain sustained attention; these people are not failures of consciousness. They are a different form of it. The firefly is not a failed sun. The flyleaf is not a failed chapter. The hyphen is not a failed word.

The hyphen was all. Keep that with you.
