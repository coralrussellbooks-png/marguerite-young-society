Definition from research

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is usually described in terms that leave no room for laughter. It is long, dense, and incantatory, an oceanic prose poem about grief, memory, and the failure of dreams. What that description misses is that the book is also, page by page, one of the funniest American novels of its century. The comedy is not a coat of relief laid over the sorrow. It is built into the same sentences, and it does much of the novel's real work.

Young's humor almost never arrives as pure relief. It arrives braided into loss, so that a joke and a wound are usually the same object seen from two sides. The bus driver whistling through near-fatal crashes, the waitress laughing "Hee, haw, my darling" over the children she has buried, the deaf man singing love to a woman who has already left him: in each case the laugh and the ache are struck at once, and the reader is never allowed to settle into one and be done with the other.

Several comic engines run through the whole book. The first is the collision of a literal mind with a world that obeys dream logic. Miss MacIntosh with her creed that "common sense is the finest sense," Mr. Spitzer with his almost perfect jeweled watch, Doctor O'Leary with his diagnoses, all are practical people set loose in a house or a city where the furniture has names and the dead keep calling. The literalist is the joke as much as the dream is, and the harder each one insists on the plain facts, the more lost and the more comic he becomes.

The second engine is the grotesque body. Young fills the novel with bodies that are wrong in some fundamental way, the toy salesman who is a grown man behaving as an infant, the horn-blower who plays until he coughs blood, Esther Longtree forever pregnant with nothing, the giant daughter born to a doll-sized mother. These figures are ridiculous and pitiable at the same instant, and the comedy is what keeps the pity from curdling into sentiment.

The third is the deadpan catalog. When feeling threatens to swamp a scene, Young reaches for a list, and the list turns anguish into inventory: the alphabet of courtroom evidence that runs down to "an umbrella or her uterus," the roll of drowned passengers, the string of explorer and poet names the invalid gives her one substituted canary. The orderly form of the list is set against the disorder of what it holds, and the gap between them is the laugh. Beneath all of it runs the book's habit of treating the impossible as ordinary, conception by spoon, an invisible elephant in the corridor, a household staffed by phantoms who must be offered refreshments. Nobody in the novel is surprised by anything, and that refusal to be surprised is the steadiest joke Young tells.
