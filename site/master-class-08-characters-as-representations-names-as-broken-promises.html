Characters as Representations: Names as Broken Promises

← Lucie Bell · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · The Setting →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Moses Hunnecker

Marguerite Young used to call out to famous but dead literary figures in her classroom, Edgar Allan Poe, Henry James, William James, welcoming them in and offering them a seat, addressing them as if they had walked through the door. From students' accounts the habit entertained and enlivened the room.

The section that follows takes a liberty with that vignette and tries the same relationship on the characters in the novel. These are speculations, but they point to something a writer can use. Young does not name a character after a famous figure to flatter the comparison. She does it to make a promise she intends to break. The borrowed name raises an expectation in the reader of the prophet, empress, savior, and then the character is built to defeat it. The allusion is not a resemblance. It is a load the novel places on a familiar shape so it can show the shape failing to hold.

These three comparisons work, and they work because in each one the text itself plants the name as a flag and then labors against it. The reader carries the borrowed figure into the chapter and watches Young dismantle it. That is the craft lesson: a name can function as a controlled subversion, a way of summoning a whole set of expectations in one word so the character can spend the book refusing them.

Moses Hunnecker and the biblical Moses

The bus driver's name is the plainest flag in the novel, and the book makes the connection explicit by mentioning Moses leading the Israelites. Both figures lead a group on a journey across a wilderness. There the promise ends and the inversion begins.

The biblical Moses is a prophet with a clear vision, receiving divine messages and leading his people toward a promised land, delivering the law, moving in a straight line toward freedom. Moses Hunnecker leads toward "the jumping-off place," a place "where creation stops." His vision is fueled by whiskey and personal folklore, his pronouncements rambling and hard to read for any clear direction. He is a self-proclaimed prophet whose prophecies arrive drunk. Where the biblical Moses delivers the law, the driver disregards it, driving erratically and drinking openly. Where the biblical Moses leads out of slavery toward a new beginning, this Moses ferries his passengers toward an end.

Every point of the comparison is a reversal. The name promises a guide to the promised land and delivers a guide to the end of things. Young summons the most familiar deliverer in the culture precisely so she can invert his direction, and the reader feels the inversion because the name did the setup work before a single chapter unfolded.

Catherine Cartwheel and Catherine the Great

This is the strongest of the three because the novel does not merely hint at the name, it has Catherine become Catherine the Great outright, sleeping in a bed with pillars of twisted amethyst. Young plants the flag inside the text and in the character's own imagination.

Both Catherines are figures of influence who reject the limits of the ordinary world, and both exercise control over their environments. But the empress wielded political power to shape Russia, engaging with the world to expand and modernize it. Catherine Cartwheel wields power only over her own reality, retreating from the "brutal world" into an "opium paradise" of dreams and phantoms, "oblivious to the realities of flesh and blood." Her bedroom is "filled with visitors as the Grand Central Station," a court of the dead and the imagined rather than a court of state.

The inversion is the direction of the power. The empress pushed outward to remake a world. Catherine Cartwheel withdraws inward to build one. The name promises worldly command and delivers total retreat, and the comparison holds because Young herself put the empress in Catherine's head, then showed the throne to be a bed she never leaves.

Esther Longtree and the biblical Esther

Both characters carry the name that means "star" in Persian, and the name hints at radiance and significance. Both carry heavy burdens and endure. There the promise breaks.

The biblical Esther has a clear purpose and achieves it: a queen who uses her position and her rhetoric to save her people from destruction, decisive and successful, ascending to power and influence. Esther Longtree has no such purpose and no such ascent. She remains on the margins, a "dumb, brilliant waitress" defined by poverty and isolation, driven by a formless desire for connection she never satisfies. The biblical Esther's sexuality is bound to her royal duty and the saving of a nation. Esther Longtree's is a source of shame and endless ill-fated pregnancy. The biblical Esther has a clear vision; Esther Longtree's is clouded, and she is never sure what she sees. One saves a people. The other saves no one, herself least of all.

The name promises the star who intercedes and delivers a woman who cannot deliver at all. The subversion is the whole point of the borrowing: Young takes the culture's model of the woman whose action redeems her people and builds against it a woman to whom things simply happen, so that the shining name falls on someone the world refuses to see.

For a writer the lesson is when a character has a famous name then the reader is handed an entire set of expectations for free. The interesting work is not honoring them. It is breaking them, on the page, where the reader can feel the promise give way.
