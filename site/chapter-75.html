To All My Darlings

Chapter 75

On Chapter 75 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 10, 2026

← Chapter 74 · Home · Chapter 76 →

Content note: The Esther Longtree chapters (72 to 81) deal with stillbirth and pregnancy loss, rape, infanticide, racist violence including lynching, and cruelty to children. This chapter describes a miscarriage Esther forced by beating her womb, the dead infant thrown into a river, and her dream that her own father fathered it.

Summary

Vera listens to Esther all morning, perhaps through many mornings. Esther wonders whether she carries a child or a benign tumor, and admits that she told the Chicago detective, who raped her and left her to face the disgrace, that he would be a father when the child was already dead. Long ago, young and frightened, she knelt by the river beating the child in her womb; a fisherman who heard her screams helped her wrap the dead baby in a gunny sack and throw it into the river above the dam. Years later it comes back at night, a mud baby bigger in death than in life, a funeral wreath hanging from its navel, and in her dreams it is the policeman’s son: her father was the father. She sets out food on the window sill for a tall child who never comes in, nurses her own hand, and fits two wedding rings on her nipples. The detective now writes that he will prove she murdered his three children. She hopes the coming war, “killing all the men and some of the children,” will make one or two old murders matter less.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, Esther reflects on her condition (her swelling body compared to a “face under water”) and the ambiguity of whether her growth is motherhood or a “benign tumor.” This metaphor captures her conflicted feelings about pregnancy, desire, and loss. She is simultaneously hopeful and fearful, uncertain whether the growth inside her is a child or a symbol of past trauma and miscarriage.
  • A pivotal passage reveals that Esther was raped by a detective who left her to face disgrace alone. Her complex feelings toward the detective (wanting to deceive him, to mystify the “solver of mysteries,” yet also wanting to express her pain) highlight her isolation and trauma. She acknowledges that the child in her womb was already dead when the detective came, underscoring a sense of hopelessness and despair.
  • Long ago, young and frightened, Esther knelt by the river “beating the child in her womb” until it came, dead; a fisherman who heard her screams helped her wrap it in a gunny sack and throw it into the river above the dam. The fisherman’s uncertain state, wondering if he is dreaming or sunstruck, adds a surreal quality to the event. This passage symbolizes Esther’s struggle to reconcile with loss and the societal invisibility of such tragedies.
  • Esther’s recurring nightmares of the dead baby who “would come floating back to her” in various forms (mud baby, seed, star) serve as a powerful motif for unresolved grief and memory. The child is both “ugly” and “beautiful,” “bigger in death than it had been in life,” representing how trauma continues to grow and haunt her psyche despite physical absence.
  • The chapter explores Esther’s suspicion that her own father, the policeman, might be the father of the dead child, a deeply disturbing and taboo notion. The imagery of the funeral wreath and the policeman’s whistle evokes a chilling connection between family, authority, and violation. This passage sheds light on Esther’s fractured family relationships and the blurred boundaries of victimhood and agency.
  • Despite her losses, Esther yearns for motherhood and imagines children flocking around her. She tries to nurture an “eldest son” who never comes indoors, hiding food for him and hoping for his return. This passage illustrates her loneliness and maternal longing, contrasting with the harsh reality of repeated miscarriages and social alienation.
  • Esther’s fear of going to court, accused of murdering her children, is a critical passage that highlights her vulnerability and the social stigma she faces. The detective’s threatening letters and the possibility of being blamed for deaths without physical evidence deepen her anxiety and sense of injustice.
  • The metaphor that once made pregnant by a man, Esther “was always bearing his children ever afterward,” underscores the cyclical and inescapable nature of her trauma. This passage suggests that her past haunts her present, with multiple “children” representing various losses, mysteries, and unresolved issues.
  • Toward the end, Esther’s stubbornness and refusal to explain herself unless compelled reveal her resilience and complexity. She hopes for a future free of past shadows and miscarriages, longing for the happiness and fulfillment that motherhood might bring, despite the chaotic and painful history she carries.
  • The closing lines reflect on the coming war and its capacity to kill “all the men and some of the children,” suggesting that in the larger scale of human suffering, Esther’s personal tragedies might seem insignificant. This gives a broader social and historical context to her story, emphasizing the pervasive nature of loss and death.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “She had the miscarriages, and other women had the carriages.” What does the pun reveal?
  • Esther says “her tears were her children.” What does she mean?
  • “A woman of her character preferred uncertainty, the great question mark.” Why would she?

Characters

  • In her dream the mud baby “was the policeman’s son, and her father was the father.” Why does this child, alone among the stillborn, keep coming back?
  • The fisherman “was not sure he himself was not dreaming.” Why does he never speak of it again?
  • The tall child is “one who was not dead, one who called her mother.” Who is he?

Connections

  • The mourners enter “through the ivory portals.” In Homer, false dreams pass through the gate of ivory. Is Esther’s dream true or false?
  • The detective’s “wife said that he was sterile.” How does that change his accusation?
  • “If she was made pregnant once by a man, she was always bearing his children ever afterward.” How does this explain the “little detectives”?

Reflection

  • “The only anguish would be the anguish of knowing entirely what was so.” Is uncertainty ever a mercy?
  • “Nothing had been washed away.” What cannot be washed away?
  • “One or two old murders should not have mattered so much” with a war coming. Does a war make private grief smaller?

Research: Characters

Esther Longtree

  • The protagonist of this chapter; a woman burdened by miscarriages, trauma, and social suspicion, yet clinging to hope and faith.

The Chicago Detective

  • A figure of violence and threat; he raped Esther and later accuses her of murder.

The Fisherman

  • A compassionate figure who helps Esther dispose of a stillborn child and silently acknowledges her suffering.

Esther’s Father

  • The night policeman. Esther “loved her father from a distance,” and in her dream the mud baby is his son.

Mud baby

  • The “mud baby” described in this chapter is a deeply symbolic and haunting figure that embodies Esther Longtree’s complex grief, trauma, and sense of loss surrounding her pregnancies and miscarriages. The mud baby is not just a physical stillborn child but also a manifestation of Esther’s psychological and emotional torment.
  • The mud baby represents one of Esther’s miscarried or stillborn children, described as soft as a marsh butterfly and wrapped in a gunny sack, thrown into the river to be carried away. Despite being physically lost, the mud baby returns repeatedly in Esther’s dreams and waking nightmares, symbolizing the persistence of unresolved trauma and guilt. It is described as “bigger in death than it had been in life,” swollen and covered with mud and brambles, with “awful starlight which was not starlight” on its face. This suggests a grotesque enlargement of the grief and memory that refuses to fade.
  • Esther’s relationship with the mud baby is complex; she both rejects and agonizes over it. She beats the child in her womb when young and frightened, an act reflecting self-directed anger and confusion. Yet, she also nurses her own hands to soothe pain and fits wedding rings on her nipples, blending maternal care with symbolic acts of mourning and endurance. The mud baby appears as a spectral figure in her life, embodying the unfulfilled desire for motherhood and the burden of loss that haunts her daily existence.
  • The mud baby is entwined with Esther’s fears and uncertainties about the identity of the father. It is linked ambiguously to various men in her life, including her father, a policeman, and a detective who raped her. The funeral wreath hanging from the mud baby’s navel and its male organs hidden from the moonlight symbolize secrecy and shame, highlighting Esther’s conflicted feelings about her pregnancies, the men involved, and the societal judgment she faces.
  • The mud baby transcends physical tragedy to become a figure of Esther’s haunted mind. It “come floating back to her in the roar of dreams” and “spreads its fingers wide,” a vivid image of how her past sorrows invade her present reality. The child is “ugly and beautiful,” “glass, twigs, brambles, mud, sand, starlight,” a surreal amalgam symbolizing the mixture of despair, hope, and confusion that defines her inner world.
  • Esther’s attempts to forget or deny the mud baby’s existence (throwing it into the river, hoping it would be “washed away”) reflect her struggle to cope with trauma. Yet the persistent return of the mud baby in dreams and memories illustrates the impossibility of erasing such deep wounds. Her uncertainty, the “great question mark” she lives with, is epitomized by the mud baby’s elusive and mutable nature.
  • The mud baby in this chapter is a powerful and multifaceted symbol of Esther Longtree’s suffering, representing the physical loss of her miscarried children and the psychological scars that remain. It captures her grief, the ambiguities of motherhood and paternity, her alienation, and the haunting persistence of trauma that she cannot escape. The recurring imagery of the mud baby (swollen, ghostly, covered in mud and brambles) vividly conveys the complex interplay of pain, memory, and identity that defines Esther’s experience.

Tall Child

  • The tall child is not the mud baby. He is “her other, other, other child, one who whistled at the window, one who wore no funeral wreath, one who was not dead, one who called her mother.”
  • He is “her eldest son, her little boy who would not come in out of the darkness, who would not eat, who said he did not trust his mother.” Each night, even in sub-zero weather, she sets food on the window sill for him: cold cereal, old bones, toast stolen from the restaurant, apples, cheese. “But there was never the print of a tooth.”
  • Her mother scolds her for hiding bread under her pillow and apples everywhere, not knowing whom the food is for.

Esther and the Abortion Rights Movement

  • Esther Longtree is a complex, symbolic character whose life and experiences can be analyzed in the context of the abortion rights movement in the US.
  • Esther Longtree is portrayed as a woman burdened by an “everlasting pregnancy,” haunted by multiple stillborn children, miscarriages, and the symbolic weight of unborn or lost children. She is at once maternal and mysteriously barren, pregnant yet never fully delivering living offspring that the world can recognize or accept. Her pregnancies are often described as “clouded,” “mysterious,” or “stillborn,” and she experiences isolation, societal judgment, and legal threats because of her reproductive history.
  • Esther faces accusations, lawsuits, and societal condemnation for the loss or absence of children, implicitly tied to notions of infanticide or abortion. She dreads being brought to court, exposed to public scrutiny, and misjudged by a jury that cannot understand her unique suffering and mystery.
  • Esther’s narrative is filled with ambiguity since she does not always know the fathers of her children, nor can she fully explain the fate of her pregnancies. This ambiguity echoes the clandestine and complex nature of abortion and reproductive control for women of her era, especially those marginalized by society.
  • Despite her “mulish” stubbornness and physical strength, Esther is powerless against the forces governing her body and fate. She is caught between societal expectations, personal despair, and the biological realities of her reproductive health. Her identity as a mother is fraught with loss, secrecy, and yearning.
  • The stillborn children and the “little shadow boxer” under her heart symbolize both the tangible and intangible losses women endure: lost potential lives, societal invisibility, and the emotional and spiritual costs of reproductive control or failure.
  • Esther’s fear of legal entanglement, the threat of arrest, and the impossibility of proving her innocence reflect the harsh realities women faced when dealing with unwanted pregnancies or abortions before legal reform. Her case is described as unprecedented, with no legal precedent to protect her.

Historical Context

  • Abortion was a criminal offense in most states, punishable by fines, imprisonment, and social disgrace. Laws were stringent, and enforcement was often harsh, particularly for poor or marginalized women.
  • Abortions were generally only permitted to save the life of the mother, and even then, access was restricted and dangerous. Women often sought illegal and unsafe procedures.
  • The early abortion rights movement began to take shape in the 1950s and early 1960s, advocating for legal reform to protect women’s health and autonomy. Activists challenged the criminalization and pushed for exceptions and ultimately for the right to abortion itself.
  • Abortion was associated with moral failure and criminality. Women who had abortions or miscarriages were often stigmatized as sinners or murderers, a theme echoed in Esther’s fear of public judgment and legal accusation.
  • Esther’s secretive pregnancies, miscarriages, and the threat of legal action mirror the precarious position of women in the pre-1964 US abortion context. Her fear of exposure, societal condemnation, and her isolation reflect the lack of support or understanding women faced.
  • Esther’s case is described as not being covered by any law, highlighting the legal vacuum and absence of rights for women like her before reform. This underscores the historical reality where women had little recourse and were vulnerable to prosecution.
  • Esther internalizes and externalizes the stigma attached to reproductive failure, much as women were publicly and privately judged harshly for abortions or miscarriages. The accusations of murder, infanticide, or madness parallel societal responses to women’s reproductive choices or misfortunes.
  • The narrative portrays figures such as doctors, detectives, and the legal system as intrusive and judgmental, echoing the control and surveillance women’s bodies were subjected to, especially regarding reproduction and abortion.
  • Esther’s uncertain reproductive history, multiple possible fathers, and the surreal quality of her pregnancies evoke the secretive and often misunderstood nature of abortion experiences before legalization, where women’s stories were fragmented and hidden. Esther Longtree embodies the tragic, mysterious, and socially fraught experience of a woman caught in the web of reproductive loss, societal judgment, and legal peril characteristic of the era before the first abortion rights reforms in the US. Her story, rich in symbolism and emotional complexity, reflects the harsh realities faced by many women: criminalization, stigma, fear, and isolation. Esther Longtree’s character and her narrative can be seen as a poignant, allegorical exploration of the consequences of restrictive reproductive laws and the deep personal and societal costs borne by women in that historical context.

  • “So I listened to Esther Longtree’s tale of woe”: Esther Longtree in this chapter is portrayed as a deeply tragic and complex figure whose life is marked by sorrow, loss, and an overwhelming burden of maternal suffering. Her narrative is laden with symbolism and a haunting sense of ambiguity between life and death, reality and illusion. Esther is described as a large, swollen woman who is often pregnant or carrying the weight of pregnancies, many of which have ended in miscarriage or stillbirth. She is physically affected by her experiences, with her body growing and swelling “like a face under water” and her heart “roaring” with pain and confusion. This corporeal imagery reflects her emotional turmoil and the heavy burden she carries. Esther is haunted by the memory of her stillborn children, especially a particular “dead baby” who recurs in her dreams and visions. This child appears as a ghostly, mutable figure: sometimes a mud baby, sometimes a star, sometimes walking and whistling like a policeman (her father). This spectral child embodies Esther’s unresolved grief and the mysterious, painful legacy of her reproductive history. The child’s presence symbolizes the inescapable past that “spread its fingers” into her present, confusing her maternal identity and her sense of reality. Esther’s narrative is fraught with uncertainty about the paternity of her children, including both the living and the dead. She suspects her father, the night policeman, might be the father of one child, which adds a disturbing layer of incestuous implication and deepens her psychological conflict. She also grapples with the menace of a detective who raped her and later threatens her with accusations of murder regarding her lost children. Esther’s sense of guilt and fear is compounded by this external pressure, making her situation even more tragic and isolating. Despite the miscarriages and losses, Esther’s yearning for motherhood remains central to her identity. She imagines herself surrounded by children, calling to them, feeling on the verge of “something big.” Yet, she is tormented by doubts, wondering if what she feels is not motherhood but a “benign tumor,” a metaphor for something unnatural or pathological growing inside her. This tension between hope and despair, life and death, defines her tragic longing. Esther is a stubborn, “mulish” woman who resists explanation or confession. She keeps her pain hidden, crying aloud only when she is sure no one is near. She is wary of judgment and the legal consequences she might face, especially from the detective’s accusations. Her isolation is both physical and psychological, as she navigates a world that neither fully understands nor sympathizes with her. The chapter uses rich, poetic imagery to convey Esther’s inner life: the “quail rustling among the purple shadows,” the “marble children,” the “funeral wreath,” and the “mud baby” all evoke the blending of life, death, memory, and dream. The river motif, with the stillborn children thrown into its current, suggests the flow of time and the attempt to wash away pain and guilt, though ultimately unsuccessfully. Esther’s story reflects broader themes of female suffering, reproductive trauma, and social marginalization. Her experiences are not merely personal but speak to the harsh realities faced by many women, especially those who are poor, vulnerable, and victimized. Her connection to various men (the detective, the policeman father, other “dying fathers”) and her ambiguous reproductive history also hint at societal failures and abuses. Esther Longtree is a profoundly tragic character embodying the painful complexities of motherhood, loss, and survival. She is caught between hope and despair, reality and dream, the past and an uncertain future, her life shadowed by death and the haunting presence of lost children. Her narrative is a poignant exploration of grief, guilt, and the human desire for connection and meaning amid suffering. See Esther Longtree.
    • Esther Longtree embodies a deeply tragic and complex symbol of American history, particularly the history of marginalized and dispossessed women who have endured cycles of loss, violence, and erasure. Her story is a series of miscarriages, stillbirths, and pregnancies marked by trauma and abandonment that can be read as an allegory for the broader historical experiences of women in the United States who have borne the consequences of social neglect, patriarchal oppression, and systemic injustice. Esther’s repeated miscarriages and the haunting presence of her dead children symbolize the lost potential and silenced voices of countless women who suffered physical and emotional damage, often in anonymity and without justice. The references to her being raped by a detective, the mysterious paternity of her children including insinuations of incest (her father possibly being the father of one child), and the threat of legal prosecution for supposed murder suggest the historical reality of women victimized by those in power (law enforcement, family patriarchs, and societal structures) that failed or actively oppressed them. Esther’s condition (physically swollen, burdened with grief, and haunted by ghostly children) also metaphorically represents the burden of historical trauma that accumulates over generations. Her narrative touches on themes of women’s reproductive rights denied or controlled, the stigma and silence surrounding miscarriages and stillbirths, and the societal tendency to blame or shame women for such tragedies. Esther’s life reflects the harshness of economic and social hardship, implied by her work as a waitress, her isolation, and the absence of support. Her struggle resonates with the stories of working-class women who faced exploitation and abandonment. Esther Longtree symbolizes the history of American women who have been subjected to violence, reproductive suffering, and social marginalization: a history marked by loss, injustice, and resilience. Through her, Young evokes the silent, painful legacies of many such women, whose experiences are written into the fabric of the nation’s troubled past.
  • “Often, she pondered upon difficult questions”: “...pondered upon difficult questions, some legal, some illegal… often, was raped when there was no one present but a ghostly, mean, mean child… She could not always blame men for her disappointments. Sometimes she blamed the child…” The children ruined her body and “...her raped, wounded sky, the whole world?” During and after pregnancy, a woman’s body undergoes significant changes, including increased blood volume and heart rate, breast enlargement, and skin changes like stretch marks. Postpartum, the uterus shrinks, abdominal muscles may separate (diastasis recti), and some muscles may be weakened, potentially leading to urinary incontinence. Other common after-effects include changes in shoe size, wider hips, and hair loss after an initial period of thicker hair. During pregnancy, organs like the uterus, stomach, and intestines are pushed and shifted by the growing fetus to make room. After pregnancy, these organs gradually return to their pre-pregnancy positions over several weeks or months. Vaginal stretching happens as the baby moves down the birth canal. As the baby’s head descends, it interacts with the vaginal walls and pelvic floor, causing the tissues and muscles to stretch significantly. Computer models show that some pelvic floor muscles can stretch to more than three times their normal resting length. See Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery.
  • “it might be a benign tumor, this growth”: In pseudocyesis, false or phantom pregnancy, a woman believes she is pregnant and her body follows: the menses stop, the belly swells, she feels the child move. Mary Tudor, Queen of England, believed herself pregnant in 1554 and 1555; London rang its bells for a prince who was never born. See Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery and Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “had not borne witness against herself”: Esther had resisted and not “...borne witness against herself, for it would have been illegal to do so, and her own father was the policeman.” This may refer to the Fifth Amendment in the United States where, Pleading the Fifth, is often used to invoke the self-incrimination clause when witnesses decline to answer questions where the answers might incriminate them. The amendment (1791) says no one “shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself.” See Guilt, Innocence, and Justice.
  • “While dangling a red geranium for bait”: A red geranium may be herb Robert (Geranium robertianum), the small wild red cranesbill, also called red robin, stinking Bob, and death-come-quickly; folk magic linked it with luck and fertility. Geranium comes from the Greek geranos, crane, for the beak-shaped seed pods, and in German the plant is Stinkender Storchschnabel, stinking stork’s-bill. Because the stork was already an established European symbol of birth, family, and new babies, people linked the stork’s-bill herb to conception and depicted it alongside storks in folk art. See Roses and Flowers and Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “wearing its birth veil like a bridal veil”: See the caul in the Chapter 74 resources. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “They had thrown the stillborn into the river above the dam”: This may refer to the Mississippi Burning Case on August 4, 1964, FBI agents discovered the bodies of three civil rights workers (James Chaney, Michael Schwerner, and Andrew Goodman) buried in an earthen dam on a farm in Neshoba County, MS. The workers had been murdered by members of the Ku Klux Klan and a local deputy sheriff on June 21, 1964, and their bodies were buried within the construction of the dam. The novel was published in 1965. See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “Sometimes it was a mud baby”: Mud baby may refer to Tar-Baby in the second of the Uncle Remus stories published in 1881. It is about a doll made of tar and turpentine used by the villainous Brer Fox to entrap Brer Rabbit. The more that Brer Rabbit fights the Tar-Baby, the more entangled he becomes.The term tar baby has come to refer to a problem that is exacerbated by attempts to struggle with it, or by extension to a situation in which mere contact can lead to becoming inextricably involved. Although the term “tar baby” is documented as coming from a folktale of African origin, its modern meaning in America is different. Many consider tar baby to be a racial term for African Americans. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.

  • “through the ivory portals”: In the Odyssey (Book 19) Penelope says that dreams come through two gates: those through the gate of ivory deceive, those through the gate of horn come true. Virgil sends Aeneas back from the underworld through the ivory gate (Aeneid 6). See Homer’s Epics and Dreams and Imagination.
  • “some little windowpeeper”: In the Coventry legend, Peeping Tom, the tailor who looked out at Lady Godiva as she rode naked through the town, was struck blind. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “hear the lovebirds crying for their mother”: See Esther’s lovebirds in the Chapter 72 resources. See Birds.
  • “She was the Poles, she guessed”: See Exploration and the Poles and the ice floes in the Chapter 74 resources.
  • “she ate geraniums and paper and string”: Pica, the craving to eat things that are not food (clay, chalk, paper, ice, starch), is common in pregnancy and has been described since antiquity; the word is Latin for magpie, the bird that will eat anything. See Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery.
  • “whose little hands had stung like nettles upon her cheeks”: Esther’s mother slapped her as a child. See Esther’s Father and Esther’s parents in the Chapter 72 resources.
  • “Was she a maypole or what”: A maypole is a tall wooden pole erected as a part of various European folk festivals, around which a maypole dance often takes place. The festivals may occur in May or Pentecost, although in some countries it is instead erected at Midsummer. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “even if there were no corpses derelicti”: Esther’s “corpses derelicti” mangles corpus delicti, “the body of the crime,” the legal rule that a crime must be shown to have happened before anyone can be convicted of it; she mixes in corpses and derelicts. The phrase is often misread to mean that there can be no murder conviction without a body. See Guilt, Innocence, and Justice.
  • “for it was not her business if his wife said that he was sterile”: The detective is married, and he and his wife each say the other is sterile. See The Chicago Detective.
  • “It was the world of this creation that was wrong, O’Leary said”: See Justice O’Leary.
  • “especially when there was going to be another war soon”: Esther couldn’t understand why anyone would “...bother a poor creature like her… especially when there was going to be another war soon, killing all the men and some of the children, and one or two old murders should not have mattered so much, especially if it was nothing murdered?” This phrase expresses a sense of futility and the overwhelming insignificance of individual tragedies in the face of much larger, impending catastrophes, namely, war and mass death. The “other war” is the Second World War, then coming.
