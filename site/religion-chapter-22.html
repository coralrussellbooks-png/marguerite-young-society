Chapter 22

On Chapter 22 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Christianity and Religion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.

Characters: Miss MacIntosh

Allusions: Genesis · The Bible

Miss MacIntosh's relentless questions about what God made on each day, the stars and lights of Wednesday, the whales and fish of Thursday, are her way of pinning creation to a fixed order and of catching God in his mistakes, the things begun and left unfinished. She blames Emperor Constantine for muddling the calendar and moving the day of rest, an old grievance she treats as a live one.

"the genealogies of the Biblical shepherds" points to the shepherds who run through Scripture's family lines, Abel, the patriarchs, David, down to Christ the Good Shepherd.

"the proverbs, the jeremiads" pairs the short wisdom-saying with the long lament named for Jeremiah.

“We loved no graven image”: “Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image” (Exodus 20:4). See The Bible. Earlier in Chapter 3.

“The first was to have begun what He could not finish”: Because creation is incomplete, the characters, particularly Miss MacIntosh, emphasize the importance of practical, honest living in the present world rather than dreaming of distant, perfect worlds or afterlives. The unfinished world demands human effort and self-discipline, and moral clarity, rather than escapism or fantasy. The 8th day of creation is often used symbolically to represent a new beginning, a time of renewal or a significant event beyond the initial creation narrative, often associated with the covenant with Abraham and the practice of circumcision on the 8th day after birth, signifying a new life within the covenant with God. Some Christians interpret the 8th day as a reference to Jesus’ resurrection. “He that is eight days old shall be circumcised among you” (Genesis 17:12), and the Epistle of Barnabas (15) speaks of “a beginning of the eighth day, that is, a beginning of another world… we keep the eighth day with joyfulness, the day also on which Jesus rose again from the dead.”

“the Emperor Constantine’s having changed the calendar”: On March 7, 321, Constantine ordered that “on the venerable day of the Sun” magistrates and city people should rest. The Seventh-day Adventists and Seventh Day Baptists teach that Rome changed the Sabbath to Sunday, and Miss MacIntosh’s quarrel with Constantine is theirs. See American Religious Movements and Augustine and Christian Theology. Miss MacIntosh’s quarrel is with the seven-day week and its day of rest. She counts from Sunday, works every day, and says God should have “created the world in seven days and rested on the eighth.” Young may be telling us that we are out of balance with nature, the seasons, and the natural order. The early Roman calendar followed the seasons more closely. It reinforces Miss MacIntosh’s advocacy for common sense and living within the realities of the present moment, acknowledging the limitations imposed by time and mortality. The Roman calendar was used by the Roman Kingdom and Roman Republic. The earliest Roman calendars were lunar calendars that tracked the moon’s 29.5 day cycle. It also had an eight-day week, with a market day every eight days. The Julian calendar was a reform of the Roman calendar that was based on the Egyptian solar calendar. It had 365.25 days in a year, with leap days added every four years. It was replaced by the Gregorian calendar in 1582. The Romans were generally pagans for more than a thousand years. Paganism is a term used to describe people who worship multiple gods and nature, rather than one god as in monotheistic religions. The Roman Empire eventually became a Christian majority state, and pagans were persecuted. In February 391 Emperor Theodosius “prohibited sacrifices and the visiting of temples,” and in November 392 he forbade the worship of the pagan gods altogether (Encyclopaedia Britannica).

“what God had created on Wednesday”: “And God made two great lights; the greater light to rule the day, and the lesser light to rule the night: he made the stars also” (Genesis 1:16). See Genesis.

Constantine "shedding his tears of repentance which fell... as pearls paving the pavement" turns his conversion into an image of pearls, an echo of the pearl gates of the New Jerusalem, though the book eyes him with suspicion for changing the calendar.

“were created on Thursday last. I think it must have been around four o’clock in the afternoon”: “And God created great whales” (Genesis 1:21). Vera’s mock precision recalls Archbishop James Ussher, who dated the creation to the evening before October 23, 4004 BC (Annales Veteris Testamenti, 1650). See Genesis. Earlier in Chapter 2.

“by the sweat of my brow”: “In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread” (Genesis 3:19). See Genesis. Earlier in Chapter 3.

“whose garments smelled of myrrh and aloes and cassia”: “All thy garments smell of myrrh, and aloes, and cassia, out of the ivory palaces” (Psalm 45:8). Young adds “of the dead.” King Ahab built “the ivory house” (1 Kings 22:39). See The Bible.

"What created God on Monday, a very busy day?" flips the creation account on its head, asking what could have made the maker, another of her cracks in the doctrine of an uncreated God.

“the firmament in the midst of the waters to divide the waters from the waters”: “Let there be a firmament in the midst of the waters, and let it divide the waters from the waters” (Genesis 1:6-7). See Genesis.

“God created man on Friday, the sixth day”: “And the evening and the morning were the sixth day” (Genesis 1:31). See Genesis.

“The sheep cannot guard the sheep against the thief”: “He that entereth not by the door into the sheepfold, but climbeth up some other way, the same is a thief and a robber” (John 10:1). See The Bible.

"the very Tower of Babel" names the "confusion of tongues" of Genesis 11, the scattering of language and the failure of understanding.

“He sat next to God, and he thought that he was smarter than God”: Lucifer of Chapter 21. See the Chapter 21 resources. Earlier in Chapter 21.

“The first thrashing had its origin, I have no doubt, in heaven, and one fell”: “And there was war in heaven… and the great dragon was cast out” (Revelation 12:7-9). The dragon’s “tail drew the third part of the stars of heaven” (Revelation 12:4), as he goes “dragging the stars with him.” See Revelation and the Apocalypse.

“He was fallen from morning until seven o’clock, from evening until morning”: Milton’s fallen angel Mulciber: “from Morn / To Noon he fell, from Noon to dewy Eve, / A Summer’s day” (Paradise Lost 1.742-744). Luke has Satan fall “as lightning from heaven” (Luke 10:18). See Milton.

“Dog races in Heaven as on earth”: “Thy will be done in earth, as it is in heaven” (Matthew 6:10). See The Bible.

“What is a man?”: “What is man, that thou art mindful of him?” (Psalm 8:4), pondering the value and nature of human beings. This question is a reflection on human fragility and insignificance compared to God’s power, yet also highlights the special attention God gives to humanity.

“these poor tenements of clay”: The “houses of clay” of Job 4:19, as in Chapter 18. See The Book of Job and the Chapter 18 resources. Earlier in Chapter 18.

“Where are those towers of gold which God created before creation officially began? Where is that sea of jasper and of light?”: “A sea of glass like unto crystal” (Revelation 4:6), and “the building of the wall of it was of jasper” (Revelation 21:18). See Revelation and the Apocalypse. Earlier in Chapter 13.

"Where is Mt. Ararat," where "Noah's Ark was foundered," and "Were there butterflies in the ark?" reach for the Genesis flood and its landing on Ararat, then bend the story toward the fanciful, asking after butterflies and sleepers on the water.

“Where are the Easter Islands, the Barents Sea, the Sandwich group, Shackleton Shelf, Enderby Land, Keeling Islands?”: Easter Island is in the Pacific Ocean and belongs to Chile. See the Chapter 18 resources. The Barents Sea is where temperate and cold waters meet off the coast of Norway and Russia. It is named for Willem Barentsz (1596). Sandwich group may refer to a British Territory in the southern Atlantic Ocean. These are probably the South Sandwich Islands, named by Cook in 1775. Cook also named the Hawaiian islands the Sandwich Islands (1778). The Shackleton Shelf is in East Antarctica. It was charted by Mawson’s expedition (1912) and named for Shackleton. Enderby Land is in Antarctica, discovered by John Biscoe in 1831. The Keeling Islands are an Australian territory in the Indian Ocean. Darwin visited in 1836 and worked out his theory of coral atolls there. Earlier in Chapter 18.

"Where is the angel of death?" is asked not in fear but in Miss MacIntosh's flat wish to locate death as a plain fact, a thing to be met rather than dreaded.

“We are always the thing we see”: May refer to “We don’t see things as they are, we see things as we are,” which is commonly attributed to Anaïs Nin. The line appears in Nin’s Seduction of the Minotaur (1961), credited to the Talmud.

“Moody’s Bibles”: The hotel Bible of Chapter 19. See the Chapter 19 resources. Earlier in Chapter 19.

“She would rather sow the whirlwind and reap it”: “For they have sown the wind, and they shall reap the whirlwind” (Hosea 8:7). See The Bible. Earlier in Chapter 1.

“the inner man was all that counted”: “Though our outward man perish, yet the inward man is renewed day by day” (2 Corinthians 4:16). See The Bible.

“What cared she for Issachar, Aaron, Absalom, Togarmah?”: The significance of these names is to situate the novel’s meditation on mortality, loss, and the passing of time within a biblical and mythic framework, underscoring the themes of decay and the search for meaning in the face of inevitable death. See Judaism. Issachar: The Talmud argues that Issachar’s description in the Blessing of Jacob (“Issachar is a strong ass couching down between two burdens: and he saw that rest was good, and the land that it was pleasant; and bowed his shoulder to bear, and became a servant unto tribute,” Genesis 49:14-15) is a reference to the religious scholarship of the tribe of Issachar, though scholars feel that it may more simply be a literal interpretation of Issachar’s name. (Wikipedia) Aaron: The rabbis particularly praise the brotherly sentiment between Aaron and Moses. When Moses was appointed ruler and Aaron high priest, neither betrayed any jealousy; instead they rejoiced in each other’s greatness. When Moses at first declined to go to Pharaoh, saying: “O my Lord, send, I pray, by the hand of him whom you will send” (Exodus 4:13), he was unwilling to deprive Aaron of the high position the latter had held for so many years; but the Lord reassured him, saying: “Behold, when he sees you, he will be glad in his heart” (Exodus 4:14). Indeed, Aaron was to find his reward, says Shimon bar Yochai; for that heart which had leaped with joy over his younger brother’s rise to glory greater than his was decorated with the Urim and Thummim, which were to “be upon Aaron’s heart when he goeth in before the Lord” (Exodus 28:30). Moses and Aaron met in gladness of heart, kissing each other as true brothers, and of them it is written: “Behold how good and how pleasant [it is] for brethren to dwell together in unity!” (Psalm 133:1) Of them it is said: “Mercy and truth are met together; righteousness and peace have kissed [each other]” (Psalm 85:10); for Moses stood for righteousness and Aaron for peace. Again, mercy was personified in Aaron, according to Deuteronomy 33:8, and truth in Moses, according to Numbers 12:7 (Kaufmann Kohler, “Aaron,” Jewish Encyclopedia, 1906). Absalom: “He was a prince who could have had almost anything. The only things he wanted were things he could not have… Wealth is not determined by possessions but by mindset. In his mind, Absalom was a pauper. He only looked at what he did not have.” (Wikipedia) Absalom had erected a monument near Jerusalem to perpetuate his name: “Now Absalom in his lifetime had taken and reared up for himself a pillar, which is in the king’s dale: for he said, I have no son to keep my name in remembrance: and he called the pillar after his own name: and it is called unto this day, Absalom’s place” (2 Samuel 18:18). He wanted his father’s throne and led a revolt against David (2 Samuel 15-18). It was his half-brother Amnon who lusted after their sister Tamar, and Absalom killed Amnon for it (2 Samuel 13). Togarmah “is a figure in the Generations of Noah in the Book of Genesis that represents the peoples known to the Hebrews. Togarmah is among the descendants of Japheth and is thought to represent some people located in Anatolia. Medieval sources claimed that Togarmah was the legendary ancestor of several ethnic groups in the Caucasus, including Armenians and Georgians” (Wikipedia, “Togarmah”). He was believed to have 10 sons.

“What cared she for any translation of the Word of God and flatteries to earthly kings”: The King James Version of Chapter 21. See the Chapter 21 resources. Earlier in Chapter 21.

“or even the Baptists or even the Free Methodists”: The Free Methodist Church was founded in 1860. See American Religious Movements.
