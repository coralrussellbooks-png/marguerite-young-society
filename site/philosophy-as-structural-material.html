Philosophy as Structural Material

Movement Two: The Five Pillars in the Traditions

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Philosophy: Aristotle · Augustine and Christian Theology · Kant and Hegel · Marguerite Young on Her Craft · Plato and Platonism · Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres · Spinoza

Allusions: Exploration and the Poles · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · Music and Composers · Shakespeare

Definition from research

Most novels that invoke philosophers do so decoratively. A character reads Spinoza. A scene is set near a portrait of Kant. The philosopher's name floats on the surface of the prose as a signal of the character's seriousness, or the author's education, or the novel's ambitions. In Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, philosophy does not float. It bears weight. Young uses named philosophers and their specific arguments as load-bearing elements in the novel's architecture: not references but structural materials, each one doing a particular kind of work inside each of the five pillars that hold the novel together.

The most startling instance of this is not one of the famous names. It is a pair of socks. Mr. Spitzer wears his old socks mended and patched over and over until he finds himself asking "the philosopher's question": his socks have been darned and redarned so many times that "technically, he might be considered to be wearing, made of so many darns and patches covering so many holes, new socks, or that he would be when the last original thread had disappeared." Young is invoking the Ship of Theseus, the ancient paradox asking whether an object that has had all of its components replaced remains fundamentally the same object. She transplants it from the abstract register of metaphysics into the most intimate, domestic register imaginable: the worn sock on a grieving man's foot. The plural self, the first pillar, does not need to announce itself as a philosophical problem when Young has already embedded the paradox of identity and continuity into the fabric of the character's literal clothing. The philosopher's question is not something Mr. Spitzer encounters in a book. It is something he lives inside every morning when he gets dressed.

Aristotle appears twice in the novel and both appearances are architecturally precise. The first is through his claim that the ear is the doorway to the soul, which Young takes and builds into one of the novel's most sustained passages: Vera's mother is "lost for years and years inside her ear, trying to find her way out, out of this vast continent where there were so many doors and porticoes and fallen columns, secret arcades and weird deserts of stone." What Young does here is take Aristotle's philosophical claim literally and then pursue it to its spatial consequences. If the ear is the doorway to the soul, then the soul is a place, and that place has interior architecture, and someone can become genuinely lost inside it. The pillar of illusion as primary is operating here with full force: the mother's interior geography is as real and as mappable as any external landscape, and Aristotle's metaphor is not decorative but directional, opening a literal door into the interior world the novel inhabits. The second Aristotle reference, to the old legend that hedgehogs stand with their backs always to the sea, has the same quality: classical authority cited with a gentle uncertainty ("was it not Aristotle who said so?") that puts the entire edifice of philosophical knowledge under the same questioning pressure the novel applies to everything else.

Pythagoras enters the novel through harmony and transformation simultaneously. He appears at the mother's side as one of her permanent phantom guests, "who had a golden thigh and had discovered heaven's harmony, the symphonious music of the silver-toned spheres," and he appears again when the mother sees the golden cock at the foot of her bed and recognizes it as "Pythagoras shedding his golden feathers." Young is drawing on two different Pythagorean traditions at once: the mathematical discovery of harmonic ratios that became the basis of the music of the spheres, and the belief in metempsychosis, the transmigration of souls through different forms. Both are essential to the novel. The music of the spheres is the cosmic order that Mr. Spitzer's silent music strains toward; the transmigration of souls is the mechanism by which the novel's characters inhabit multiple identities simultaneously. Pythagoras in Young is not a historical reference. He is the philosophical source of two of the novel's central architectures: the cosmic harmony that grief interrupts, and the plural self that grief makes necessary.

Plato arrives differently, in the servants' argument about whether the blue room is "already occupied by Plato's boat, et cetera," alongside Spinoza in the green room and Shakespeare already installed. The casualness of this placement, the et cetera, the house-keeping practicality of assigning philosophers to bedrooms without regard for century, is Young at her most deliberately disorienting. Plato is given a boat, not a form or a Republic, a boat, because in the mother's world where the dead attend as freely as the living, what Plato needs is transportation, and the philosophical legacy is as subject to the logistics of room assignment as any other guest. This is the witness pillar at work: the sustained, undiscriminating attention that sees Plato's arrival with the same calm it applies to the Irish setter and the French poodle that always wore a crown.

St. Augustine enters in a list alongside Charon and Cheops and the Appian Way and the ghost of St. Simeon the Stylite, all described as subjects the head-hunter raises at the mother's bedside: "St. Augustine in the city of God, all those things which, strictly speaking, were nobody's business." The dismissal is Miss MacIntosh's sensibility filtered through the prose: to the plain-speaking Middle Westerner, the City of God is nobody's business, which is to say that transcendence and metaphysics are exactly the kind of illusion she has spent her life refusing. Miss MacIntosh is herself a philosophical position, what the source document correctly identifies as a kind of moral realism built from common sense and the rejection of abstraction. "Common sense is the finest sense," she insists, and her ethics, the path of virtue rugged and hard and girt about by rocks, is a pragmatic moral philosophy without transcendental grounding, virtue for its own stubborn sake, virtue as simply what a good housekeeper does. She is the particularity against which all the novel's Platonisms must measure themselves.

Boethius appears in the company of Balboa, Columbus, and Hugh Capet, listed among the kings and explorers who populated the mother's visionary world. His placement here is not accidental. Boethius wrote his Consolation of Philosophy while awaiting execution, and the mother assigns him to her household in the same breath as discoverers and wanderers, recognizing him as someone who crossed a threshold and came back changed. The grief as permanent weather pillar recognizes this placement: Boethius is the philosopher of Fortune's wheel, of what it means to think clearly at the edge of ruin, and the mother's inclusion of him in her intimate circle of the dead is one of the novel's quiet architectural claims that the thinking done in extremity is the most honest thinking there is.

The image of the philosopher collapsing into its opposite: "she saw an idiot who had become a great philosopher, a philosopher who had become a great idiot. She saw one who was both an idiot and a philosopher." This is not a mockery of philosophy. It is Young's statement of the recursive construction principle through which the entire novel operates. The end of the creation is also the beginning. The philosopher who follows knowledge far enough arrives back at unknowing. The idiot who has never known arrives at the same place by different roads. The categories reverse and merge and generate each other, just as the darns in the sock eventually replace the sock, just as Pythagoras the mathematician becomes Pythagoras shedding golden feathers, just as the Aristotle who said the ear is the doorway to the soul becomes a door that opens into a continent that no one has ever completely explored.

Philosophy in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is not a set of ideas that Young has read and incorporated. It is a set of materials she has understood structurally, each one contributing something specific to a particular load-bearing pillar: the Ship of Theseus to the plural self, Aristotle's ear to illusion as primary, Pythagoras's harmony and metempsychosis to grief and recursive construction, the practical philosophy of Miss MacIntosh as the witness who refuses transcendence, Boethius to grief and Fortune's wheel, the philosopher-idiot to the recursive undoing of every certainty the novel has accumulated. Young handles these materials the way a builder handles different grades of stone: knowing what each one can bear, placing it where its particular weight will hold.
