Belonging, Presence, and the Obligation of History

A Companion Document to the Constitution

The Philosophical Foundation

Citizenship as it currently operates is a system that presents itself as natural law. The line between citizen and non-citizen, between the person whose presence is legitimate and the person whose presence is illegal, was drawn by the people who were already inside it, encoding their interests as the condition of belonging. That line has moved many times in American history, always in the direction of excluding the people whose inclusion was most politically inconvenient for the people doing the excluding. The Chinese Exclusion Act. The national origins quota system, which from 1924 to 1965 set strict caps on immigration from Southern and Eastern Europe, Asia, and Africa while favoring Northern and Western Europeans. The systematic exclusion of people from countries whose governments the United States found threatening. The construction of the undocumented person as criminal rather than as a person whose circumstances the law has failed to accommodate.

This is a system that presents its own choices as the natural order of things. The citizen is real. The non-citizen is the deviation from the real. That framing is a political choice made by people with the power to make it. It can be remade. The question is not whether to have a system of membership and belonging. Communities require that. The question is what the system is built on: the bureaucratic achievement of a legal category, or presence and subjection to law as the foundation of belonging.

The answer this document builds from is the second. The person who lives here, works here, raises children here, pays taxes here, is arrested under laws made here, buries the dead here, builds the relationships and community that constitute a life here: that person belongs here. The paperwork has not kept up with the belonging. The obligation runs to the person, not the paperwork.

Citizenship: Three Paths

Birthright

Every person born on this soil is a citizen. This is the current rule and it is the right rule. The Fourteenth Amendment, passed after the Civil War to ensure that the descendants of enslaved people could not be denied citizenship, established it precisely because the alternative, citizenship contingent on the citizenship of one’s parents, was the mechanism that had been used to exclude them. The effort to end birthright citizenship is not a neutral policy proposal. It is the attempt to restore the mechanism the Fourteenth Amendment was written to destroy. It is a demand to return to a system the country already tried and already repudiated.

Naturalization

Naturalization is currently a process designed as an obstacle course. The fees, which can exceed a thousand dollars and are not waived for people who cannot pay them. The wait times, which run to years and in some categories to decades. The English language requirement, which has no functional relationship to the capacity for democratic participation and significant relationship to the national origin of the applicant. The civics test, which asks questions about American history and government that the majority of native-born citizens could not answer. The discretion given to individual officers that produces wildly inconsistent outcomes for similarly situated applicants.

The naturalization process should be accessible, affordable, timely, and designed to receive the person rather than to exclude them. The language requirement is replaced by language support: the government provides translation and interpretation throughout the process. The civics requirement is replaced by a civic orientation: not a test but a conversation, an introduction to the rights and responsibilities of membership conducted in a spirit of welcome rather than gatekeeping. The fees are eliminated or income-adjusted. The wait times are addressed by adequate staffing and resources. The discretion that produces inconsistency is constrained by clear standards and robust appeal rights.

Long-Term Residency

The person who has lived here for a defined continuous period, who has built a life here, who is subject to the laws here, belongs here in every meaningful sense. The bureaucratic category of undocumented does not change the fact of belonging. It only names the gap between the belonging and the legal recognition of it. The obligation runs to closing that gap, not to enforcing it.

The specific period is a political question to be determined through the democratic process. The principle is not: it is the recognition that presence and community membership over time constitute a form of belonging that the legal system is obligated to recognize. The ten million or more people currently living in this country without legal status are not a problem to be solved through enforcement. They are people whose belonging has not been legally recognized, a failure of the legal system that the legal system is obligated to correct.

Indigenous Sovereignty

Indigenous peoples’ relationship to this land precedes the nation that now administers it by thousands of years. The framework of immigration and citizenship that applies to everyone else does not apply to people whose nations predate the nation that is now asking them to prove they belong. They are not immigrants on their own land. Their sovereignty is not a concession made by the federal government that the federal government can revoke. It is a political fact that predates the federal government and that the federal government is obligated to honor.

The history of federal Indian law is a history of the systematic violation of treaty obligations, the unilateral breaking of agreements made in the name of the nation, the forced assimilation of indigenous people into the dominant culture through educational and religious institutions, and the appropriation of indigenous land through legal mechanisms designed to produce that outcome while maintaining the appearance of legitimacy. The historical reckoning provision of the constitution names this history. The tribal sovereignty provision names the obligation it generates. This document adds the specific content of that obligation: every treaty made with every tribal nation is honored in full. Where treaties have been violated, the obligation to remedy the violation is active and ongoing. Tribal nations have the same standing in federal courts as states. The federal government’s obligation to tribal nations, a legal duty of care established by treaty and law, is treated as a genuine and enforceable commitment, not a rhetorical gesture.

It is worth noting that federal recognition of tribal nations is itself a bureaucratic category with a contested history: many indigenous communities exist and have always existed without having received or sought federal recognition, and the absence of that designation does not diminish the reality of their presence, history, or sovereignty.

The recognition of tribal citizenship alongside American citizenship, for members of tribal nations, is not a complication of the citizenship framework. It is the honest acknowledgment that this nation exists within a prior political landscape that it did not create and cannot erase. Two legitimate political identities coexisting without requiring one to eliminate the other.

The Asylum System

The Obligation of History

People do not abandon their homes and communities without cause. The causes are knowable: violence, persecution, poverty so extreme that survival requires leaving, environmental catastrophe, the collapse of the conditions that make ordinary life possible. Many of those causes are traceable to the decisions of this government. The coups supported, the governments destabilized, the trade agreements that destroyed local economies, the climate emissions that are making parts of the world uninhabitable. The nation that helped produce the conditions from which people are fleeing has an obligation toward those people that exceeds the current asylum framework.

The current framework asks: does this person face persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion? The question was written in the 1951 Refugee Convention, an international agreement drafted in the aftermath of World War II for a specific historical context, and has not kept pace with the actual causes of displacement in the current world. Climate displacement is not covered. Economic devastation produced by external trade policy is not covered. The violence of gangs and cartels that operate in the instability created by decades of failed drug policy is variably covered depending on which judge reviews the claim. The framework has become a filtering mechanism that excludes the people whose need is most urgent and most directly connected to the decisions of this government.

The rebuilt system starts from the question: what caused this person to leave, and what is this nation’s relationship to that cause? The answer determines the obligation. The person fleeing a government this nation supported has a stronger claim than the current framework recognizes. The person fleeing climate conditions this nation’s emissions contributed to has a claim the current framework does not recognize at all. The obligation is proportional to the causation and the causation is documented.

Humane Processing

No person seeking asylum or entry is held in conditions that violate the freedom from cruel punishment provision of the constitution. The detention centers that currently operate with conditions documented as cruel, degrading, and dangerous are closed. Processing is conducted in the community, with the person released to sponsors or community organizations while their claim is reviewed. The review is timely: not the years-long backlog that currently functions in practice as a denial for many applicants, but a process adequately staffed to reach decisions within months.

The person seeking entry is a full person with a full history. The processing system is built to receive that history rather than to sort the person into a category as rapidly as possible. Legal representation is provided: the asylum seeker without a lawyer is denied at rates that document the effect of legal representation on outcomes in a system too complex to navigate alone. The provision of counsel is not charity. It is the constitutional guarantee of fair process applied to the people most directly subject to its protections. Due process, in plain terms, means that a person subject to a legal decision has the right to understand the process, to be heard, and to have their case decided fairly. The asylum seeker is entitled to that. The current system frequently denies it.

Children

No child is separated from their family as a mechanism of deterrence or enforcement. The separation of children from parents as a policy tool is the cruelest available expression of the state’s power over the people who have the least: I will take what you love most if you cross this line. It is a constitutional violation under the bodily autonomy provision, the freedom from cruel punishment provision, and the due process provision simultaneously. It has no legitimate policy justification. It is deterrence through the deliberate infliction of trauma on children, and it is named here as what it is.

Children who were brought to this country before the age of legal decision-making, who were raised here, educated here, who have no meaningful connection to any other country, are citizens. The argument that they should be deported to countries they do not remember, whose languages they may not speak, to suffer for decisions made by their parents before they were old enough to make decisions, is the enforcement of a legal fiction at the expense of a human reality. The law has not caught up with the life. The obligation runs to the life.

Voting Rights and Residency

The vote is the mechanism by which the people governed by a law have a voice in the law. The person who lives here, works here, pays taxes here, sends children to school here, and is subject to arrest under laws made here is governed by those laws. The withholding of the vote from that person on the grounds that they have not achieved a particular bureaucratic status is the original grievance of this nation’s founding, applied to the people whose labor has been most essential to the nation’s functioning and whose political voice has been most consistently suppressed.

Voting rights for all residents subject to the laws, not citizens alone, is the honest application of the principle that the people are the government. Several American cities already practice this at the local level. The extension to state and federal elections requires the constitutional provision already established and the political will to implement it against the resistance of people whose power depends on the exclusion of the people who would vote against it. That resistance is not a neutral democratic objection. It is the defense of a system that serves specific interests, presented as the natural order of things.

The Transition

The transition from the current immigration system to this one is political before it is administrative. The current system has enormous institutional inertia, an enforcement apparatus of significant size, and a set of financial interests that profit from its continuation: the private prison industry that operates immigration detention facilities, the contractors who build the infrastructure of enforcement, the political actors whose constituencies are organized around the construction of the immigrant as threat.

The transition requires the simultaneous dismantling of the enforcement architecture that is inconsistent with this document’s principles and the construction of the processing and support infrastructure that the new system requires. It requires giving legal status to people currently living without legal recognition. It requires rebuilding the relationship with the countries from which most unauthorized migration originates: not through enforcement agreements but through foreign aid and investment, fair trade policy, and the climate action that would address the conditions producing displacement.

None of this is simple. All of it is necessary. The person who has been here for twenty years, who has raised children here, who has built a business here, who has been a member of a community here in every sense except the legal one: that person is waiting for the legal system to catch up with the reality the legal system has failed to recognize. The obligation to close that gap is not charitable. It is constitutional.
