The American Literary Tradition

← The Context · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · The Symbolism →

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is hard to place because it belongs to several traditions at once and sits comfortably inside none of them. It is a picaresque, a novel of the road, and yet almost nothing happens on the road; the journey is a voyage of the spirit through illusion rather than a sequence of adventures. It is a psychological novel of extraordinary interiority, and yet its interior is rendered in prose so incantatory and image-drenched that it reads closer to poetry than to the realist psychology the term usually implies. It is experimental and modernist in method, built on multi-level consciousness and the refusal of linear time, and yet its raw material is the plainest American folk life. The book's place in the tradition is precisely this refusal to settle into one lane, and the ambition that refusal serves.

What roots it firmly in American letters is its subject and its ground. Young's inspiration is explicitly the down-to-earth Middle West, and her achievement is to grant that ordinary interior the permanence of myth. Her cast of American types, the bus driver, the suffragist, the old maid, the clam-digger, the gambler, is rendered with a humor and extravagance that are distinctively American, and elevated to mythic scale much as Joyce elevated his Dubliners, though Young's soil is native and her comedy her own. The book has been likened to an American Arabian Nights, an epic that fuses myth and reality out of homegrown material, and the comparison is apt in one specific way: like that collection, the novel is a nesting of tales, a frame that keeps opening onto further stories, and it treats the tall tale and the folk type as vehicles for something far larger than local color.

Its thematic range looks, at first, like a catalogue of sensational subjects, drug addiction, suffrage, murder, suicide, real and imaginary pregnancy, schizophrenia, gambling, perfectionism. But these are not the novel's concerns so much as its instruments. Every one of them serves the single question the book keeps asking: "What shall we do when, fleeing from illusion, we are confronted by illusion?" The addiction, the doubled identities, the pregnancies that never deliver are all ways of pressing the one problem of a reality that will not separate from dream. In this the novel speaks directly to the central preoccupations of American modernism and anticipates the postmodern, the fractured self, the unreliable real, the collapse of linear time, while grounding those abstractions in flesh and folk.

Young's technique is the source of both the difficulty and the reward. She builds by what has been called a cellular expansion, unbroken bridges from word to word and image to image, so that the prose accumulates into a dense, immersive fabric rather than advancing by plot. The imagery is so pictorial it invites comparison to painting, and Young herself admired Chagall, whose floating figures and dream logic are a fair visual analogue to her pages. The events themselves resist explanation, staying ambiguous by design, which is why the book rewards and arguably requires a second reading. What keeps all this from collapsing into mere difficulty is the vision underneath it, which is human, compassionate, and often funny. The novel is hard, but it is not cold.

Its reception has matched its strangeness. At publication in 1965 the reviews were sharply divided, put off by the novel's extraordinary length and complexity and by the fact that Young had published little else to prepare readers for it. It has since been recovered and reassessed as a major, if demanding, American accomplishment, valued for exactly the qualities that once repelled: its ambition, its emotional force, its fusion of the mythic and the mundane. It stands now as a pioneering work that expanded what the American novel could attempt in scope, form, and subject, a book that made room for things the tradition had not previously held.
