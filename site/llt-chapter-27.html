Chapter 27

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: The Past Doesn't Go Away

The Questions

Mr. Spitzer wears his dead brother’s watch and lives in “inexplicable delays, shiftings of scenes.” What kind of time is a dead man’s watch keeping?

He anchors himself with routines and reminders against the “undertow of timelessness.” Are these a sealed dream like Catherine’s, or something else?

He “fears losing dignity” more than anything. What is dignity holding together?

If Mr. Spitzer is “an aggregate of lost impressions,” is there a self under the aggregate, or is the aggregate the self?

Mr. Spitzer wears his dead brother’s watch, and the detail explains the chapter. The watch keeps a dead man’s time, the past strapped to the living wrist, and around it his hours come loose: “inexplicable delays, shiftings of scenes, alterations of places, sudden snow storms or unexplained summers.” He lives in a “great undertow of timelessness,” pulled off the line of now into a present where seasons switch without cause and the dead are as near as the hour. This is The Past Doesn't Go Away at its most literal, the past worn on the body, and it is the medium he has to navigate.

And he navigates it with small provisional anchors. Routines, reminders, notes to himself, the watch, are the constructions Mr. Spitzer builds to hold together in the undertow. This is the crucial difference between him and Catherine. She built one sealed world and moved in; he builds just enough order to cross the next stretch and no more, temporary handholds he does not mistake for the floor. He is the book’s picture of how to live once the seals are gone, not by sealing a new dream but by keeping small constructions open and provisional, using them without believing them final.

What he holds hardest is his dignity. He “fears losing dignity most,” fears seeming ridiculous in others’ eyes, and it is easy to read this as vanity. It is closer to the last handhold. In a self that is “an aggregate of lost impressions,” fugitive thoughts, dead brother’s routes, dignity is the thread of coherence he keeps by, the sense of being a person and not only a scatter. And because the self is partly built in others’ looking, as Catherine’s gaze built him, his dignity is held partly in how he is seen, which is why losing it would be losing a piece of the self itself.

The chapter’s honest question is whether there is anyone under the aggregate. Mr. Spitzer is “an aggregate of lost impressions, his fugitive thoughts,” confused with his dead brother, mistaken for him, worn thin. The book does not supply a solid self beneath the scatter. It suggests instead that the aggregate is the self, that a person is exactly this gathering of impressions and losses and borrowed time, held in provisional order and in the regard of others, with no bare core underneath. Mr. Spitzer is not a whole man hidden inside a broken surface. He is the surface, kept, and the keeping is the dignity.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Mr. Spitzer’s routines and reminders for the same sealing as Catherine’s dream, order as one more escape, and the reading that takes his fear for his dignity as mere vanity. Neither the sealed anchor nor the vain man is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that Mr. Spitzer lives in The Past Doesn't Go Away made literal, a dead man’s watch and an undertow of shifting seasons, and he navigates it with provisional anchors he never mistakes for the floor, which is how the book shows one lives once the seals are gone. His dignity is not vanity but the last thread of coherence in a self that is an aggregate of impressions, held partly in how others see him. And there is no whole self hidden under the aggregate; the aggregate, kept in provisional order, is the self. One lives unsealed by keeping small constructions open and provisional, and the self is the kept gathering, not a hidden core. Mr. Spitzer holds together for now, by the watch and the routine and the dignity, and the chapter asks for nothing more final than that.

For Reflection

What small provisional anchors does one keep, and does one mistake any of them for the floor?

Is dignity vanity, or the thread by which a scattered self stays a person?

If there is no whole self hidden under the aggregate of one’s impressions, is the aggregate enough?
