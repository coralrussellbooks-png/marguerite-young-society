To All My Darlings

Chapter 24

← Chapter 23 · Home · Chapter 25 →

Summary

Vera’s thoughts move through the household. The servants have absorbed Catherine’s dreams so completely that they argue whether it is Roman in the morning and Greek in the afternoon, and assign rooms to famous phantom guests. Catherine plans her own funeral, calls Athens on a telephone wired only to the servants’ quarters, and tries to reach Cousin Hannah on a number long disconnected. Mr. Spitzer will come again in the evening with his vague certainties about heaven. The servants see the black coachman and the white horses, an old valet waits on Wellington, and a gardener believes he is Potiphar’s wife. Mr. Spitzer, in Catherine’s eyes, is a roundhouse for trains that run no more, a ghost ship with the captain’s table set for two. Vera asks herself to whom she can turn with her questions about love, marriage, birth, and death, and finds no one.

Key passages

From the research

  • The servants in the household are described as deeply immersed in the dream-like atmosphere created by Vera’s mother. They engage in elaborate fantasies, imagining themselves serving imaginary guests: historic figures, fictional personages, and legendary characters. This passage illustrates how the servants reflect and perpetuate Catherine’s opium-induced dreams, blurring the line between reality and illusion. For example, the old valet believes he serves Wellington, and the gardener imagines himself the spouse of Potiphar, while the Irish housemaid speaks only to herself to avoid confusion. The servants’ acceptance of this unreal environment underscores the theme of escapism and how the household is trapped in a timeless, surreal bubble.
  • The descriptions of the house being filled with imaginary guests sleeping “tier above tier” according to rank and station, the lighting of fires for guests who will never arrive, and the arrangement of Renaissance chairs for intimate conversation depict a grand but empty social world. This passage powerfully conveys Catherine’s craving for social connection and grandeur, yet it is all sustained by illusion. The “mad lady retouching her make-up” and the drunken second butler stumbling over her train amplify the eerie, ghostly quality of the household’s existence.
  • Catherine is obsessed with planning her own funeral and other grand events involving the dead returning to life, a motif that symbolically represents her preoccupation with mortality and the afterlife. Her social secretaries, invitations to ambassadors of Heaven and Hell, and the presence of fashionable dogs highlight her attempt to impose order and meaning on a world that is inherently chaotic and illusory. This passage reveals her complex psychology of wanting to be in control, her detachment from reality, and her engagement with the fantastic.
  • Catherine’s attempts to communicate by telephone with historical figures, relatives, and distant acquaintances only bring frustration and confusion. She calls Athens but reaches Rome, leaves messages for Emperor Hadrian who is never there, and receives only “a cackle of old hen voices” when calling Boston. The telephone is later revealed to be connected only to the servants’ quarters, emphasizing the isolation and breakdown of real connection. This passage poignantly illustrates Catherine’s alienation and the futility of her attempts to reach beyond her own dream-world.
  • Mr. Spitzer, described as a “roundhouse of the trains which run no more” and a “ghost ship with broken mainmasts,” symbolizes the unreliability and futility of seeking certainty or solace in Catherine’s world. He is a poor companion to her “eternal illness,” representing the failure of rationality or conventional comfort in the face of her decaying mental state. His presence contrasts with Vera’s desire for answers about life, love, and death, highlighting the gap between illusion and reality.
  • The chapter ends with Vera contemplating to whom she might turn for answers or to profound personal questions. This passage captures the existential uncertainty and search for meaning that underpin the narrative, emphasizing that even amidst the surreal, Vera seeks some grounding or truth.
  • “My mother herself might be, of course, the only genuine unbeliever, as everyone recognized, for she was very clever. My mother placed her faith only in the unreal.”

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “Just to hear the servants talk, anyone might have thought they were all real… especially when they were unreal.” What does Vera mean by “especially”?
  • Catherine’s telephone “was connected only with the servants’ quarters.” Who answers her?
  • “How else should they keep their jobs?” What do the servants gain by believing?

Characters

  • Catherine is “the only genuine unbeliever.” How can the dreamer be the one who doesn’t believe?
  • Mr. Spitzer is “a ghost ship… the captain’s table set for two, none aboard.” What does Catherine see in him?
  • Why does Vera turn from Catherine and Mr. Spitzer to Miss MacIntosh, “coarse-grained and ruddy”?

Connections

  • The empty ship of Chapter 22 had “the captain’s table still set for two or three or four.” Why does the image return here?
  • In Chapter 14, Catherine said the white horses were her will and the coachman her conscience. What are the servants seeing?
  • Catherine calls the Bishop of Hippo and Hadrian, and Miss MacIntosh scolded Hadrian and Constantine (Chapter 22). What do the two women share?

Reflection

  • Can a household share a dream?
  • What does it cost to keep one’s job by believing?
  • To whom do we turn with the questions that matter most?

Research: Characters

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • Central figure, caught in opium dreams, presiding over an imaginary world filled with historical, mythological, and invented guests.
  • She is fully immersed in her visionary, dream-like state, discussing grand affairs and social invitations as if real.
  • She is aware of her illusionary world but maintains a clever detachment, placing faith only in the unreal.
  • Her attempts to reach historical or imaginary figures and the failure of these calls reveal her isolation and the limits of her dream world.

Miss MacIntosh

  • Practical, grounded, and coarse-grained, she offers contrast to Catherine and the servants’ fantastical world.
  • She is a companion to Vera and represents the voice of reality amidst chaos.

The Servants

  • Immersed in Catherine’s dreams, they adopt roles and beliefs reflecting the surreal environment.
  • Some believe they serve historical figures (e.g., Wellington), others think they live in impossible settings (a forest of cuckoo clocks).
  • They maintain the household’s illusion by preparing for imaginary guests and engaging in conversations about impossible events.
  • The servants’ conversation about imaginary guests and their roles within the dream world encapsulates the blending of fantasy and reality.

Mr. Spitzer

  • A mysterious and somewhat unsatisfactory companion to Catherine, embodying confusion and lost memories.
  • He represents uncertainty about earthly matters while espousing nebulous spiritual certainties about an ultimate Heaven.
  • His characterization as uncertain, evasive, and disconnected from his own memories contrasts with Catherine’s intense visionary life, highlighting themes of memory and reality.

Research: Themes

Reality versus Illusion

See: Illusion and Reality

  • The chapter immerses the reader in a world where the boundaries between real and imaginary are fluid. Catherine’s opium dreams populate the house with imaginary guests, servants who believe in elaborate fantasies, and environments that shift unpredictably.
  • The servants interact with these illusions as if real, discussing imaginary dignitaries, polishing imaginary shoes, and setting places for guests who never arrive. This highlights how illusion can become a shared reality in certain states of mind.

Madness and Sanity

See: Madness and Sanity

  • The novel delves into how prolonged exposure to Catherine’s dreams affects the servants, leading some to lose their grip on reality and adopt bizarre identities or behaviors (e.g., thinking they serve Wellington or living among cuckoo clocks).
  • This theme raises questions about the fragile line between sanity and madness in a closed, surreal environment.

Time and Space Distortion

See: Time

  • The house is timeless and spaceless in a conventional sense, populated by figures from different centuries, places, and mythologies simultaneously.
  • Imaginary guests from various epochs co-exist, and the servants debate the time of day as Roman or Greek, reflecting a dislocation from normal temporal experience.

Isolation and Communication

See: Isolation and Connection · Music, Sound, and Silence

  • Catherine attempts to communicate with distant or deceased figures through a telephone that only connects to the servants’ quarters, symbolizing her isolation and failed attempts at meaningful contact.
  • This motif underscores the theme of disconnection between Catherine’s inner world and external reality.

Death and the Afterlife

See: Death and Mortality

  • Catherine’s dreams include discussions about her own funeral rites and arrangements for the dead returning to life, blending death with ongoing social rituals.
  • The presence of flowers associated with death and the continual reference to the “dead horses” and “black coachman” suggest a liminal space between life and death.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Imaginary Guests

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Imaginary guests and historical figures represent the blending of past, present, and fantasy, showing how Catherine’s mind holds vast, impossible histories simultaneously.

Black and White Horses and Coachman

See: Horses and Carriages · The Black Coachman

  • Black and white horses and coachman symbolize the passage between realms, possibly life and death, or reality and illusion. Earlier (Chapter 14) Catherine said the white horses were her will and the coachman was her conscience.

The Telephone

See: Isolation and Connection

  • The telephone is a device meant for communication but only connecting to servants’ quarters, symbolizing failed outreach and isolation.

Bronze Chrysanthemums

See: Death and Mortality

  • Bronze chrysanthemums are flowers for the dead, symbolizing mourning and death’s persistent presence.

Cuckoo Clocks and Elephants

See: Time

  • Cuckoo clocks and elephants represent distorted time and impossible realities within the servants’ transformed perceptions.

Snow Crystals and Halos

See: Moon and Stars

  • Snow crystals and halos are visual symbols of the dreamlike, kaleidoscopic nature of Catherine’s world, with multiple suns, moons, and shapes reinforcing the surreal atmosphere.

Research: Resources

  • “the servants in the servants’ quarters”: In the 1800s, servants were an essential part of households, performing a variety of tasks, including cooking, cleaning, and caring for children. They were often treated poorly, with low wages and long hours. In the early 1900s, servants worked in large households of wealthy families, performing a variety of tasks. They were expected to follow strict rules and hierarchies, and had little personal freedom.
  • “the life-sized chessmen moving across parterres of black and white”: Alice becomes a pawn on a living chessboard in Lewis Carroll’s Through the Looking-Glass (1871).
  • “old Beau Brummells coming to life in a crystal ballroom”: Beau Brummell (1778-1840) is a Regency Era figure who later became associated as a dandy, with fashion, and anything of good quality.
  • “one who had discovered a new planet, one who believed he dwelled on Uranus”: William Herschel discovered Uranus in 1781, the first planet found with a telescope. See Astronomy.
  • “Some had seen the black coachman”: The coachman and the white horses of Chapter 14. See The Black Coachman, Horses and Carriages, and the Chapter 14 resources.
  • “my mother’s opium dreams”: Before 1900, the typical opiate addict in America was an upper-class or middle-class white woman. As long as the opium user was a sick old lady there was no push to punish users. See The History of Opium.
  • “seemed to think that he was waiting on Wellington”: Duke of Wellington was a leading figure in 19th-century Britain, victor over Napoleon at Waterloo (1815). See Napoleon and Wellington.
  • “he lived in a forest of cuckoo clocks”: The cuckoo clock came from the Black Forest of Germany in the eighteenth century. See Time.
  • “Hannibal driving the elephants over the Alps”: Hannibal of Carthage crossed the Alps with his army and elephants in 218 BC to invade Italy.
  • “Admiral Peary was to be put into the blue room”: Admiral Robert Peary. See Exploration and the Poles and the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “which was already occupied by Plato’s boat”: Plato’s boat might refer to the Ship of State by Plato (Republic, Book 6). It is a metaphor comparing governing a city-state to being the captain of a ship and only philosopher kings should rule. See Plato and Platonism.
  • “Sir Christopher Wren and Jenny Lind were to be put into the rose”: Sir Christopher Wren was a highly acclaimed English architect, astronomer, and mathematician, the builder of St Paul’s Cathedral (1675-1710). Jenny Lind, “the Swedish Nightingale,” was a Swedish opera singer of the 19th century who toured America for P. T. Barnum in 1850-52. See Music and Composers.
  • “Spinoza and Shakespeare and Mrs. Sydington were already in the green”: Spinoza was a Dutch philosopher, author of the Ethics (1677). Shakespeare was a famous British playwright. See Spinoza and Shakespeare.
  • “Or was she a unicorn wearing a necklace?”: In the Lady and the Unicorn tapestries (c. 1500), the lady lays her necklace in a casket beneath the words “À mon seul désir.”
  • “believed he was the spouse to Potiphar, the Egyptian captain”: Potiphar’s wife tried to seduce Joseph and then accused him (Genesis 39). The gardener believes he is the wife. See Genesis.
  • “the bronze-colored chrysanthemums which are the flowers for the dead”: In France and Italy, chrysanthemums are laid on graves on All Saints’ Day. See Death and Mortality and Roses and Flowers.
  • “a sea change, a transformation”: “Nothing of him that doth fade, / But doth suffer a sea-change / Into something rich and strange” (The Tempest 1.2). See Shakespeare.
  • “Some were lions with wings”: The winged lion of St. Mark, as in Chapter 16. See the Chapter 16 resources.
  • “many mock suns and moons”: Sun dogs (parhelia) and moon dogs are bright spots beside the sun or moon, made by light bending through ice crystals, often on a halo. See Astronomy.
  • “The horizontal lady”: Catherine’s name for herself in Chapter 19. See Catherine Cartwheel and the Chapter 19 resources.
  • “some antiquated copy of the Boston or Philadelphia telephone directory… circa 1905”: The first telephone directory, a single card of fifty names, was printed in New Haven in 1878.
  • “She had called the Bishop of Hippo”: Bishop of Hippo is Saint Augustine who was also a theologian and philosopher. See Augustine and Christian Theology.
  • “She had left her messages for the Emperor Hadrian”: Emperor Hadrian was a Roman emperor from 117 to 138. See the Chapter 22 resources.
  • “King Canute had gone on a long voyage”: King Canute or Cnut the Great was King of England, Denmark, and Norway. See the Chapter 22 resources.
  • “How did it happen she never found the Lowells or the Saltonstalls at home”: The Boston Brahmins: “And this is good old Boston, / The home of the bean and the cod, / Where the Lowells talk only to Cabots, / And the Cabots talk only to God” (John Collins Bossidy, 1910).
  • “Why had Cousin Hannah never called”: Cousin Hannah. See Cousin Hannah.
  • “The Duchess of Spode had gone to a watering place”: The Duchess of Spode may refer to an English brand of pottery: Josiah Spode’s works at Stoke-on-Trent, founded in 1770.
  • “she should send her gold-turbaned blackamoor”: “Blackamoor” figures, carved and gilded Black servants in turbans, were a fashion of European decorative art. They turned enslaved people into ornaments. See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “Mr. Spitzer was a roundhouse of the trains which run no more”: In Dickens’s “The Signal-Man” (1866), a railway signalman is haunted by a ghost and a red danger light before a fatal crash, which fits Young’s “the red where there should be the green.” See Joachim Spitzer.
  • “a ghost ship with broken mainmasts and torn sails, the captain’s table set for two, none aboard”: The Mary Celeste was found adrift and abandoned in 1872, her crew gone. The empty ship of Chapter 22 had its table set “for two or three or four.” See the Chapter 22 resources.

Vocabulary

Blackamoor
An old term for a Black African or any dark-skinned person, common in European art and in decorative figures of Black servants. It is now considered offensive.
Celestial
Heavenly; of the sky.
Certitude
Certainty.
Chandelier
A branched hanging light.
Chimera
A monster of Greek myth, part lion, part goat, part serpent; any wild, impossible fancy.
Comme il faut
French for “as it should be”; proper, correct.
Concatenation
A linked series.
Conch
A large sea snail with a spiraled shell that can be blown like a horn. Held to the ear, it seems to hold the sound of the sea.
Cormorant
A large black diving seabird.
Emir
A commander or prince in the Islamic world.
Emissary
A messenger or agent.
Euphemistic
Using a mild word in place of a harsh one.
Halo
A ring of light around the sun or moon, caused by ice crystals high in thin clouds.
Mandarin
A high-ranking official of imperial China.
Medusa
A jellyfish in its free-swimming, bell-shaped stage, named for the Gorgon of Greek myth whose hair was snakes.
Obsequies
Funeral rites.
Parterre
An ornamental garden of flower beds laid out in a pattern. A parterred floor is inlaid in patterned sections.
Porte-cochere
A covered entrance where a carriage stops to let passengers out.
Roundhouse
A circular building for storing and turning locomotives.
Taffeta
A crisp, shiny silk.
Trackwalker
A railway worker who walks the line to inspect it.
