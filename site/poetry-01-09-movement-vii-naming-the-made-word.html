Movement VII. Naming: The Made Word

Part of Exploring Young's Poetry, a reading of The Collected Poems of Marguerite Young (2022).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Movement VI. The Crossing Point: What One Generation Spends · Exploring Young's Poetry · Poems That Bridge Pillars →

The last movement turns and looks at its own instrument. If grammar carries a worldview, if a noun freezes what a verb would keep alive, then the poem is not a clear window onto the world but a made thing with a maker's hand on it. Young knew this from the start. The early "Song's Dissemination" calls the poem an "edifice" she "welded together / And airily hammered," and then predicts it "will not stand." The Prismatic Ground poems keep the awareness alive. "Experience in Song" watches meaning condense out of sound into "pollen of a syllable," "Song's Preface" and "Anthology Piece" ask what a song is and what it can hold, and "Ballad-Loving Woman" measures a life by the songs it loved. To name a thing is already to shape it, and these poems keep that shaping in view instead of hiding it. The voice that pretends to have no hand at all, the one that claims to speak from nowhere, is the only voice this movement does not trust.

"Song's Dissemination"

The poem calls itself an "edifice" that the speaker "welded together / And airily hammered," and then admits it "Will crumble apart," a "Structural being" that "will not stand." Language Shapes What's Possible is enacted at the outset of her career: the poem names itself a made thing and forecasts its own collapse. It is the earliest proof that Young saw the word as built rather than given, and it doubles as Unfinished Is Honest, a made thing content not to last.

"Experience in Song."

A falling star survives only as "pollen of a syllable" and "the frosts of sound," its whole aftermath condensed into language. The poem watches meaning get made out of noise, the word as both veil and revelation. Language Shapes What's Possible is enacted rather than stated: the event exists now only as the words that hold it.

"Anthology Piece."

"Moon-struck syllables" carry a speech pulled by irrational, lunar force, expression shaped before sense arrives. The poem shows language as a made and moody thing, not a clear window. What gets said is built by the pull on the one who says it.

"Ballad-Loving Woman."

A woman who "lived until she died" is framed by the ballads she loved, a life measured in the songs that held it. The poem knows a life becomes a made thing when it is sung, and it also draws a portrait of a singular woman, which ties it to The Margins Reveal the Truth. It sits between Movement VII and Movement III, and reads richer for both.

"Song's Preface."

A song is "fleeting or ephemeral like the cherry blossom," blooming and gone, and the poem asks what this made thing can hold. Turning to speak about the song itself, it keeps the maker's hand in view. To preface a song with its own impermanence is Language Shapes What's Possible admitting what it is.
