Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 48

← Chapter 47 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 49 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer

Keys open this chapter and open nothing, and the failed key is its pillar. Mr. Spitzer, caught between worlds with a duty half legal and half musical, fumbles at locks; a woman returns from "suffrage journeys and wars" with "forty keys" to open her trunks and find a "nesting place"; and the keys that will not turn are women's dispossession under the law and the book's larger locksmithing, the "arrowsmith" one would need "to reach the dead heart," the "no master key" that recurs whenever a self proves unopenable. Widows appear early, wronged by "cruel husbands and lords," and their liberation from patriarchal imprisonment is figured as trunks that need forty keys, freedom stored behind locks a woman must carry herself.

Love fades and is not noticed fading, the "dead love not awakening" replaced by "a stranger," and the widow's emotional labor going unseen is invisibility and erasure in the register the book reserves for women's inner lives. Loyalty still holds "through all the hazards of disappointment," a resilience the chapter credits, and Mr. Spitzer's contrast with Peron's reckless, fiery life, the "matches" and "burning," sets a thoughtful, vulnerable manhood against the bravado the book keeps deflating. Consciousness, death, and love cross their boundaries here as everywhere, real and dream as one country, and the house fills with "mirror images which had dissolved," selves that reflected once and no longer hold.

The chapter thins Mr. Spitzer toward nothing. His clothing is a "backless suit," his "cape full of holes," and his fear of being seen "as he was" is the body as the place the self comes apart, the vulnerability under the costume the same one the wig and the corset hid on Miss MacIntosh. He passes "through a keyhole" and is "translated," and the passage is the crossing point turned metaphysical: born "after the moon set and dying before the moon arose," he questions whether he and his brother are "one and the same," male and female selves merging as the constellations lose their "glittering feathers" and the "great abyss" swallows "moons, suns, stars." The lamplighter and the ferryman, the "old bookkeeper" balancing accounts in a dream on a "bridge between two worlds," are the roles a self takes when it will not stay single.

The chapter ends inside ice and inversion. "Incorruptible dreams" freeze into "human landscapes," and the "beautiful ladies" are frozen into "ancient patriarchs," "the fathers of this world," ladies with "bearded faces" and "patriarchal robes." This is the crossing point at its most literal, women and men trading faces in the ice, and it is the subversion of patriarchal authority the book keeps performing, the fathers revealed to be beautiful ladies underneath, the categories fluid all the way down. The frozen scene preserves the inversion the way ice preserves anything, holding gender's collapse where the reader can look at it.
