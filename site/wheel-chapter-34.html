Research notes: Chapter 34 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 34

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Mr. Chandelier · Peron Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The chapter opens with one of the most explicit demonstrations of the plural self in literary fiction. The mother has been married:

  • in white
  • in black
  • at midnight
  • at noon
  • while asleep
  • in her riding habit
  • by a false preacher
  • by a false priest
  • by a St. Bernard dog
  • at home
  • at a Gretna Green elopement
  • during a funeral
  • during an auction sale

These are not competing memories of one event. They are simultaneous truths of a self that cannot be singular. The narrator does not frame these as delusions to be corrected. The prose gives each version equal ontological weight. The mother is not confused about what happened. She is constituted by the irresolvability itself.

"Nothing had happened at all as she had said before, or everything had happened simultaneously like one image repeated in many mirrors."

This is Not One Self stated with philosophical precision. The "one image in many mirrors" metaphor is exact: the self is not the image, nor any single mirror, but the entire system of reflections, none of which is primary. The demand to identify which wedding was "real" is precisely the violence Pillar 1 warns against: the insistence on resolving what is constitutively multiple.

The mother herself exists in at least three simultaneous registers throughout the chapter:

  • The romantic dreamer pursuing imaginary lovers
  • The lucid sufferer who knows the dreams are dreams
  • The horizontal invalid whose body has already surrendered what her mind still contests

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter does not merely describe illusion; it refuses to establish a ground from which illusion could be distinguished from reality. Consider the wedding sequence: we are never given access to what "actually" happened. The narrative voice does not stand outside the mother's consciousness and correct it. The larger cave is always more carefully lit.

The auction sale interpolated into the wedding/funeral is particularly striking:

"Someone else had told her that there was neither a funeral nor a wedding — there was an auction sale, and this abandoned house was going under the auctioneer's hammer, for a great beauty had passed away, dying in her sleep."

The auction sale (the dispersal of Louis Quatorze chairs, rock crystal candelabra, empty picture frames) is not a third competing narrative. It is the economic truth beneath the romantic and funereal truths. The beautiful life is being sold off. The frames are empty. This is not less real than the wedding. It may be more real. But the prose refuses to privilege it.

The empty picture frames are among the chapter's most devastating images. They appear without commentary, carried on men's heads through the street. Frames without pictures: the apparatus of representation without its content, the structure of meaning emptied of what it was supposed to contain. This is Pillar 2 made object. The St. Bernard dog who administers the Viaticum, simultaneously provisions for a journey and last rites for the dying, operates in the same register:

"This dog had given her the Viaticum, though whether the provisions for a journey or the last rites given to one when dying, she could not say."

The ambiguity is not resolved because it cannot be resolved. Marriage is simultaneously a beginning and an ending. The sacrament for departure and the sacrament for death are the same sacrament. Illusion is not layered over reality here; the two are constitutively identical.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The chapter's emotional atmosphere is grief, but grief of the accumulated, non-resolving kind that Pillar 3 describes. The mother's grief is not for a specific loss at a specific moment. It is the climate of her entire consciousness.

"For love had died when she was married. The sky was grey, filled with luminous mares'-tails, with horse-head clouds racing along the wind. Life was this one moment. The sea, seen through the fog, was the color of a dead pigeon's breast."

"Life was this one moment", but the moment contains all the grey weather of every loss simultaneously. The dead pigeon's breast is not a metaphor for sadness; it is a precise color, a specific texture of grief that has become the color of the world itself. The sea does not reflect grief; the sea is grief, externalized as permanent weather.

The passage describing Cousin Hannah's death and what follows is equally instructive:

"She might be now a cloud, a bird, a tree in the wind."

There is no consolation here, no resolution into peace. Cousin Hannah has simply dispersed into the world's weather. And the mother's grief for her (complicated, ambivalent, shot through with fascination and resistance) does not conclude. It accumulates alongside the grief for the unknown father, the grief for the marriage that may not have happened, the grief for the self that might have been otherwise.

The chapter's geographical imagination, those extraordinary passages about the terrain of human mortality, is grief rendered as landscape:

"...a land of many levels and abysses, chasms and maelstroms and whirlwinds, storm centers and seas of dead calm, many clouded promontories and shoreless seas, thunderheads and wild steppes and sudden declivities, terrible crags in unbelievable isolation..."

This is not metaphor decorating an argument. This is the argument. Grief is not a valley you pass through. It is the entire topology of existence: every feature of it, from the dead volcanoes to the seas of glass to the valleys where sunlight never shone. The honest life, Pillar 3 insists, stops pretending otherwise. This prose stops pretending.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrative voice of this chapter enacts Pillar 4 with unusual purity. The narrator, the "I" whose mother this is, does not intervene to correct, console, explain, or resolve. The narrator stays in the room.

This is ethically remarkable. The mother's consciousness is chaotic, drug-altered, possibly delusional. A different narrative ethics would frame this as pathology, would establish a sane perspective from which the mother's wandering could be observed and diagnosed. This narrator refuses that position entirely.

Consider what the narrator does not do:

  • Does not tell us what "really" happened at the wedding
  • Does not tell us whether the father is truly dead or merely absent
  • Does not adjudicate between the mother's romantic fantasies and Cousin Hannah's fierce realism
  • Does not resolve whether Cousin Hannah's suffragist critique of marriage is correct

The narrator witnesses. The narrator reports with extraordinary precision: the color of the sea, the jangling of iron keys, the twitching of Cousin Hannah's cheeks at the sound of a cock crowing. Attention is the practice. The refusal to simplify is the act.

The one moment where the narrator approaches interpretation is telling:

"My mother could not have answered these baffling questions — even though, as she sometimes realized now, she might have asked them herself and perhaps only long after Cousin Hannah's death..."

Even here, the narrator does not answer the questions. The narrator only notes that the mother could not answer them, and that she might have been their origin. The witness does not conclude. The witness continues to see.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The structure of the chapter is itself recursive. The wedding narrative begins, proliferates into variants, is interrupted by the auction, returns to the wedding, branches into the elopement, circles back through Cousin Hannah's arguments, returns again to the landscape of grief, arrives at the arithmetic passage, and does not end so much as accumulate toward the next beginning.

The mother's relationship to her own narrative is explicitly recursive:

"That was why she had always yearned for clarification — a teacher of arithmetic who would teach her to add, subtract, multiply, divide, find an integer — and then, when she knew arithmetic, she would learn plane and solid geometry..."

This is not a character who has given up on construction. She is building; she wants arithmetic, then geometry, then the identity of the geometer. She builds knowing the building is provisional, knowing that geometry will only reveal new questions about who designed the circles. The building is never finished. The mother returns to the beginning of her wedding memory again and again, each time with slightly different materials, slightly different understanding, never arriving at completion.

The mirror imagery reinforces this:

"My mother could see the mirrors dancing, flying toward the ceiling."

The mirrors that should reflect a stable self are in motion. The recursive construction does not produce a fixed image. It produces more mirrors, more reflections, more angles of approach to what cannot be finally seen.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter's form is its argument for Pillar 6. These are sentences that do not end so much as extend until they must stop for breath. Consider the great passage about the land of desolation:

"...never a charivari for a lonely bride, never the peal of church bells, never the drone of honey bees in buildings which were great hives of stone, never the rattle of stars like autumn leaves, never this sepulchral laughter, hollow and gruff, never the cracked bell, never the hullabaloo of the huntsmen, the screamings of the horses, the hoot of the great snow owl, the ululation of the dog, land where never would be heard the roar of the surf, the sound of a winding horn, the whistle of a night train, land of no insect cry, no wood-note."

This sentence is a catalogue of absences, and it refuses to conclude. Each "never" opens onto another never. The incompletion is the point. To have ended the sentence earlier would have been a lie: a false suggestion that the catalogue of what is absent could be bounded, that absence has edges.

Cousin Hannah herself embodies Unfinished Is Honest in her suffragist project:

"She had not been invited, but she had come in spite of my mother's desire — how often, making this old house echo with the sound of mighty hooves, echoes which had cracked the mirrors."

Hannah's project, the liberation of women from the violence of marriage, is never completed. She continues to come, continues to argue, continues to fail to convince the mother entirely. This is not Hannah's failure. This is the ongoing sentence of a life lived in honest engagement with an intractable problem. The demand for completion (for the mother to simply agree, to simply leave, to simply resolve) would be its own violence.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Cousin Hannah is the chapter's supreme figure of the marginal, and the prose treats her with the reverence Pillar 7 demands, not as exception or aberration, but as evidence of the constructed nature of the categories she exceeds.

She is simultaneously:

  • A woman who refuses womanhood as defined by marriage and submission
  • A figure imagined by the mother as a "tall, thin, dark, handsome gentleman with black plumes on his black velvet hat"
  • An "old warrior," a "great captain," a "chasseur, mountain-climber"
  • A "marvelous old maid"
  • Someone who "had seemed to wear great silver scales" and "shine in her own light"

Hannah does not fit the available categories of gender, of social role, of the romantic plot. She is not the husband, not the rival, not the friend, not the enemy. She is all of these and none. The mother's imagination keeps trying to resolve her into something nameable (the Renaissance gentleman, the suffrage captain, the warrior) and each resolution immediately proliferates into new contradictions.

"God forbid that she should be a man, this most marvelous creature, almost imperceptible and yet bright-eyed, a tall, thin, dark, handsome gentleman..."

The sentence forbids the category and then immediately inhabits it. Hannah is the evidence that the categories of man and woman are constructions, and she makes this visible precisely by being marginal to both.

The passage about the Moroccan women in purdah extends this:

"God pity the lip-reader in all those countries where, Cousin Hannah had said, the women wear veils — and God pity the deaf and the mute in all those countries where the human face is not seen."

The veiled woman, the deaf, the mute: these are the marginal figures who reveal the violence of systems that require the face to be hidden, the voice to be silenced. Hannah's iron keys, "numerous iron keys which had jangled, giving off dissonant music", are the keys to these locked categories. The music they make is dissonant precisely because it refuses harmony with the existing order.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The chapter's temporal structure is sedimentary rather than sequential. The mother's consciousness does not move forward through time. It presses down through accumulated layers of the past that have never receded.

"Rousing herself from a long period of sleep, years or months or hours, she would be sure, at last, how it had happened, this marriage with space."

"Years or months or hours": time has lost its sequential structure entirely. What remains is depth, not sequence. The wedding that may have happened decades ago is as present as the current moment, pressing up through the sediment of all the subsequent years.

The father's death is the most powerful sedimentary presence:

"...it was not my mother, it was my unknown father who had died. Perhaps the funeral came before the wedding."

The father who died before the narrator was born (or perhaps before the wedding, or perhaps at the wedding) is not in the past. He is beneath the entire structure of the narrative, the founding sedimentary layer on which everything else has been deposited. The narrator calls him "my unknown father," which is the precise language of Pillar 8: the lost is not behind us, it is beneath us, still pressing up.

Cousin Hannah's death operates similarly:

"...that death which had not been by fire or sword."

The deliberate vagueness about the manner of death refuses to fix it in a specific past moment. Hannah's death is not an event that happened and is over. It is a condition of the present, a layer in the sediment through which the mother's consciousness continues to move.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter is extraordinarily self-conscious about language as construction. The most explicit moment is the Viaticum passage:

"This dog had given her the Viaticum, though whether the provisions for a journey or the last rites given to one when dying, she could not say."

A single word, Viaticum, contains two incompatible meanings, and the prose refuses to resolve which applies. This is not ambiguity as literary decoration. This is the demonstration that language shapes what can be seen: the word "Viaticum" makes visible the terrifying proximity of departure and death, of beginning and ending, that a more precise vocabulary would conceal.

The Latin of the St. Bernard dog is similarly constructed:

"This dog had spoken in church Latin, my mother believed — though sometimes she thought it was Caesar's message to the Gauls..."

Church Latin and Caesar's military dispatches: the language of sacred authority and the language of imperial conquest. The dog who marries the mother speaks both. Marriage, the prose suggests, is conducted in the language of power, sacred and imperial simultaneously, and the mother cannot distinguish between them because they are not distinguishable. The language of the wedding ceremony and the language of conquest are the same language.

The passage about the arithmetic teacher is about language's constructive power:

"...if she knew geometry, she would know who the geometer was, whether that which began with a rational abstraction should end with an irrational dream."

The geometer (God, fate, the architect of the self's circumstances) is known only through the language of geometry, of circles and ellipses and compass movements. To learn the language is to gain access to the constructor. But the prose immediately reveals the limit: the geometer may be "sane or insane," and the rational abstraction may end in an irrational dream. Language names its own location as insufficient.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter is entirely immanent: present in the most ordinary and the most broken objects, pointing deeper into the world rather than outward to a beyond.

The St. Bernard dog who administers the sacrament is perhaps the chapter's most concentrated image of immanent transcendence. The sacred rite of last rites, the sacred rite of marriage, are performed by an Alpine rescue dog in a dream. This is not blasphemy. It is the insistence that the sacred does not require elevation above the ordinary; it lives in the ordinary, in the dog's tongue licking the bride's face, in the provisions for a journey that are also the last rites.

"...cherubim riding on the backs of bottle-nosed dolphins, tapestry kings and queens among the long grey shadows..."

The angels in the cathedral are riding dolphins. The sacred has descended entirely into the creaturely, the aquatic, the comic. The tapestry kings and queens are among shadows: the sacred is inseparable from the grey, the fading, the textile.

The candle imagery recurs throughout as immanent transcendence:

"...the dying candle flames, the vases of fresh flowers."

The dying flame and the fresh flowers together: the sacred is in the simultaneity of dying and freshness, not in any transcendence of either. The flowers will die. The candle is already dying. The sacred is here, in this room, in this dying.

The most extraordinary passage of immanent transcendence is the description of Cousin Hannah herself:

"...as Mr. Chandelier or a seven-branched crystal candelabra glittering in a far heaven, moving from star to star which lighted in the reflection of that passing flame..."

Hannah is a candelabra in a far heaven, but the far heaven is reached by reflection, by the passing of a flame from one branch to another. The transcendence is entirely immanent: it is light passing through crystal, the ordinary physics of reflection producing something that looks like heaven.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The narrator, the "I" who observes the mother, is the chapter's most profound embodiment of Pillar 11. The narrator is constituted by what was deposited before their arrival: the unknown father's death, the mother's opium dreams, Cousin Hannah's fierce arguments, the wedding that may not have happened.

"...it was my unknown father who had died."

The father is unknown. The narrator has never known him. And yet the narrator is made of him: constituted by his absence, by the wedding that followed or preceded his death, by the mother's grief that has become the weather of the entire narrative. The father is in the sediment. The carrying continues.

The narrator's relationship to Cousin Hannah is equally structural:

"Cousin Hannah had never given up, though the years had passed, her belief that Joachim would cruelly disappoint my mother at the last, crucial moment, abandoning her to the furies and to the gods of revenge, even as Peron had done at the first when he had apparently committed suicide, taking a coward's way out — leaving Joachim to accept that vicarious responsibility which had always been the slow brother's sense of peculiar guilt and of inestimable loss."

This passage reveals the full sedimentary structure: Peron's suicide deposited guilt onto Joachim, who deposited it onto the mother's life, which deposited it onto the narrator's existence. The narrator did not choose this inheritance. It is structural. The obligation is not chosen. The carrying continues.

The chapter's closing movement (the mother hearing stars rustling like autumn leaves, the cold winds through empty corridors, the slamming of a door) is the crossing point made audible:

"There was always the slamming of a door — though whether someone was going or coming, she could not say."

The door slams at the crossing point where going and coming are indistinguishable, where the not-yet-born are arriving as the already-dead are departing. The mother cannot say which direction the movement goes because she is the crossing point itself: constituted by what came before, constituting the ground the narrator stands on.
