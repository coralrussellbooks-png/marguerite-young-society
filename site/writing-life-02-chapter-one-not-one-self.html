Chapter One: Not One Self

← Introduction · Radical Immanence Across Marguerite Young's Writing Life · Chapter Two: Reality Is Constructed →

Pillar: Pillar 1 Not One Self · Across every book

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

The first thing Young asks you to give up is the one you are most attached to. Not your beliefs, not your certainties, not even your story about where you came from and what it made you. She asks you to give up the assumption underneath all of those: that there is a you doing the believing, the knowing, the remembering. A stable center that receives experience and integrates it into something coherent and continuous. A self that, whatever it has been through, remains fundamentally itself.

This is the assumption philosophy has protected most ferociously. It is the assumption Descartes was protecting when he stripped everything else away and found the thinking thing that could not be doubted. It is the assumption Kant was protecting when he built the transcendental subject, the organizing "I" that makes experience possible by being prior to it. It is the assumption that runs underneath liberal political theory, psychoanalysis, developmental psychology, and most of what passes for common sense about human identity. You are someone. That someone persists. That persistence is what makes you responsible for your actions, capable of relationship, legible to others and to yourself.

Young does not argue against this assumption.

She does something more unsettling.

In "Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" she builds a world in which you can watch it fail.

Young’s central claim is there is no truth underneath. Young is showing that plurality is not a symptom. It is the condition. It is the structure. Young shows another reality, sustained by a self that is genuinely living in it, and the self that lives in it is as real as any other self.

This is the philosophical move. Not the claim that all realities are equal in some lazy relativist sense. The claim that the self is not the kind of thing that lives in one reality. The self is the kind of thing that lives in several realities simultaneously, navigating between them with varying degrees of awareness and control. The demand that it choose one and call that one the true self is a demand that amputates most of what the self actually is.

Characters are performing and Young does not present this as a tragedy, though it contains tragedy. She presents it as information. This is what selves are. This is what you are working with. Now what?

Hume got closer than most to understanding this. Hume looked for the unified self in his own experience and found, famously, that he could never catch himself without a perception. He found the bundle, the stream of experiences with no experiencer underneath, and it troubled him enough that he retreated from the full implications. Young does not retreat. She built an investigation of what the bundle actually looks like from the inside, which is something a philosophical treatise cannot do because it cannot sustain the investigation long enough or in the right form.

William James named the stream of consciousness and understood that the self was a process rather than a thing, that the "I" was always already in motion rather than stable behind its experiences. Young takes James seriously in a way that most of the tradition did not, extending the stream until it becomes clear that it does not flow in one direction, does not empty into a single sea. It branches and doubles back and runs underground and surfaces somewhere unexpected and is still, somehow, the water.

The debt to James is a matter of record, not only of affinity. In her graduate years Young studied his Psychology “as if it were a Bible,” and decades later she still told her students to read it as a writer’s central text. She took from him the many selves living “under one roof or dome or hat” and “the person you did not become.” By 1935 a poem of hers could say of a woman, “Oh, she was plural; / She multiplied,” and a poem of the war years could say that “within my skull the you becometh I.” Her writing did not discover the plural self. My MacIntosh, My Darling gave a position she had been writing toward for twenty years, a world large enough to hold it.

The thinker Young is most in dialogue with is Gertrude Stein, who was William James's student and whose investigation of repetition, of the continuous present, of identity as something that happens rather than something that is, runs parallel to Young's project with different formal tools. Where Stein strips language down to its mechanisms to show the self as a grammatical construction, Young piles language up until the construction becomes visible through its own excess. Different directions, the same destination: the self is not a noun. It is a verb.

The plural self is not the absent self. It is the self that is more than any single account of it can capture. It does not mean that accountability dissolves. It produces a more complicated picture of what it means to be responsible for actions taken by a self that is not singular and not fully transparent to itself. The plural self can be witnessed. It cannot be reduced.

The demand for singular selfhood is not just a philosophical error. It is a political technology. Every system that requires enemies requires a population of selves simple enough to be sorted: us and them, citizen and alien, deserving and undeserving. The more complex the actual population, the more pressure the system applies to force simplification. The people most damaged by this demand are the people whose plurality is most visible and most threatening to the sorting system. Young's plural self is not a therapy for that violence. It is a philosophical framework that names the mechanism producing it.

This is the first pillar. Everything else the system builds is built on this: the self is plural, the plurality is the condition, and the honest life is the one that stops trying to put it back together.

"Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" is where this pillar stands at full height, but it is not where it began. Young was writing toward the plural self long before Vera boarded the Grey Goose bus. It shows up first as feeling, in a family story that made the child a vessel for her dead cousin Harry and in the dropped mirror of “Transition” (1930), where “all things multiply.” It takes on method when she reads William James in Chicago, and it is argued outright in the poems of the war years. In her interviews she sometimes speaks of the self in terms the novel would not allow, saying in 1965 that she “became more myself all the time.”

Phase One, Childhood and the Early Poems (1908 to 1934)

Pillar 1: Not One Self

The plural self starts in Young's own origin, a child believed to carry a dead boy's life. "Transition" (1930) gives it its first image. A dropped mirror breaks, "all things multiply," one bluebird becomes a thousand, and the speaker is "lost forever now." "Biography" (1932) is about "young Harry playing chess / With Lucifer," a boy losing everything because his mind is on everything "but not the game." Whether Young meant the name to recall her cousin, the poem does not say. "Eclipse" makes the soul kin to an animal: "The lizard and my spotted soul / Kept their counsel well, / Remembering the sun."

The complication is just as old. "Little Fawn," undated and dictated late, so that its place in this phase is a guess, keeps asking "Can't you stay by yourself, little fawn?" because the mother's "tracks are lost in the snow." The lesson is "Better learn now to stay by yourself." From the beginning, the merged self (Harry, the mirror) sits beside the lonely self that must learn to stand alone. That is the same pairing as the novel's "the individual is the one illusion" and its narrator who has "walked alone."

Phase Two, The Chicago Years, Prismatic Ground, and the Graduate Essays (1935 to 1937)

Pillar 1: Not One Self

James supplies the theory: the many selves "under one roof or dome or hat," and "the person you did not become." The poems supply the images. "Elegy for the Body" (1935): "Oh, she was plural; / She multiplied; / All sparrows fell / If once she died." In "Like Secret Roots" (1935) the beloved enters the speaker's mind until "I must be both sky and field." In "Whorl," "No one can tell which is the tree / Of bloom reflected, which is she." "Wild Orchard" turns a man into a tree: "Man, the errant vegetable," with "Slow rain not blood" in his veins.

Complication: "Heart Who Stays" (1935) keeps something fixed: "the Head remembers the Heart who stays, / Remembers the Heart, who cannot go." A core remains, which anticipates the steady self Young claimed in 1965.

Phase Three, The Philosophical Poems (1938 to 1945)

Pillar 1: Not One Self

"That Cloud" states the pillar as an argument: "Difference of persons but a specious truth / When within my skull the you becometh I," and "in this confusion is our only existence possible." "Ventriloquist: The Coffee Hour" (1940) nests voice inside voice: "I am the tree, and he is the tree's shade," and the ventriloquist is himself "a wooden boy against the imponderable / Knee of what unseen man." In "Snow in the Mountains" (1941) the lovers are "twin souls / Of real and unreal, both real and our shadow face."

Complication: the war years make division frightening. "That Apple Was Mental" calls it "not ego but terror, ourselves dispersed." "Of Deed, Love, Death" (1945) imagines the soul splitting "like an annalid," and ends: "True terror is each soul to seem / The shrivelled body hanging from a floating tree." Separateness also returns. A draft prefers this earth to a counter-earth where "would I / Be but a repetition of a more perfect self." "Address to the Birds" knows that "between us the gulf forever roars." The giraffe lives as "only one, among the buds of stars."

Phase Four, "Angel in the Forest" and “Complex Life and Complex Letters” (1945 to 1946)

Pillar 1: Not One Self

The Rappite angel is built from mixed parts: "the breast of woman, the head and feet of man," "grass and fire and homely as an old shoe." Rappite doctrine holds that "Adam, before he fell, was possessed of both sexual elements." The plural self becomes theology.

Complication: the book ends on the miller's irreducible difference. The flour bears "the impression of the miller's thumb and the miller's soul," and "There will always be as many flours as there are millers." The difference is "so slight that nobody can name it." Owen gives a third view, that men are "all cut off the same bolt" and the mind is "a blank page." The book sets sameness, difference, and mixture side by side without choosing.

Phase Five, "Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" (written from the mid 1940s, published 1965)

Pillar 1: Not One Self

The novel opens with the pillar as doctrine: "Every heart is the other heart. Every soul is the other soul. Every face is the other face. The individual is the one illusion." Mr. Spitzer cannot tell himself from his twin, and Young saw them as "the two parts of the same man," a "duality which might be in all of us." Miss MacIntosh is "a bald prince, disguised," "neither male nor female." Vera projects herself into each stranger until "she is all of them." Catherine Cartwheel's first person dissolves into the divan, the mirror, and Brooklyn Bridge. The ending merges Vera with Esther: "Esther Longtree would be me." Fuchs reads the book as a critique of fiction that "fetishizes" a single self.

Complication: the opening treats unity as a question asked of "some omniscient eye," while the narrator has "walked alone." The oneness is believed in, not felt.

Against the earlier work, "Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" adds two things. The plural self becomes doctrine spoken on the first page rather than an image in a lyric, and it becomes something the narrator undergoes. The poems argued that “the you becometh I.” In the novel Vera becomes each stranger she meets, and the book closes on her merger with Esther.

Phase Six, The Occasional Prose, Interviews, and Self-Interpretation (1940s to 1995)

Pillar 1: Not One Self

Young called herself "a pluralistic person" who preferred "the many as opposed to the one." In the story "The Dead Women" the narrator feels like "two women, herself and someone else." Her changing accounts of her own origins enact the pillar.

Complication: this is where the pillar is most directly qualified. In 1965 she said she "became more myself all the time," and that "we don't change any more than we change the color of our eyes about the deepest things of our lives." She called herself "doomed to be a writer" and said she "never altered" her style. The steady core is her own claim.

Phase Seven, "Harp Song for a Radical" (written in her last decades, published 1999)

Pillar 1: Not One Self

The doubles return as history. Hope and despair are "two of the most famous twins of the nineteenth century." Theodore Debs is "almost his other self." Heine and Börne should be "a two-headed subject followed not by a plural but a singular verb." Lincoln is "many-sided," with "a sense of the many and not the one truth," against Wendell Phillips, "a nonrelativistic worshiper of the absolute."

Complication: great-man history keeps the single heroic figure at the center. The book resolves this through the dream, which "transcends the individual."
