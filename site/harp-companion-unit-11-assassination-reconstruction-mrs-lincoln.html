Unit 11, pp. 225-256: Assassination, Reconstruction, Mrs. Lincoln

← Unit 10 · Harp Song for a Radical: A History Companion · Unit 12 →

Background for this unit: The History in Harp Song for a Radical

While the schoolboy Debs wins a Bible for spelling, Young sweeps through the nation's postwar crises: Andrew Johnson's impeachment, Sand Creek and the Washita, and the long unraveling of Mary Todd Lincoln. The unit ends with Maximilian's execution in Mexico. Its theme is what the war's victors did with the peace.

Major entries

Andrew Johnson and his impeachment (pp. 229-236)

  • Who he was: Born poor in Raleigh, North Carolina, in 1808, he was apprenticed as a tailor and settled in Greeneville, Tennessee (pp. 231-232). He was the only senator from a seceding state to stay loyal to the Union, which made him Lincoln's running mate in 1864.

  • What happened: As president he pardoned former Confederates wholesale and vetoed civil rights measures, clashing with the Radical Republicans in Congress. When he fired Secretary of War Edwin Stanton in defiance of the Tenure of Office Act, the House impeached him in February 1868.

  • The trial: Chief Justice Salmon P. Chase presided in the Senate. The vote to convict was 35 to 19, one short of two-thirds (p. 234). Seven Republicans voted to acquit, among them William Pitt Fessenden of Maine, James Grimes of Iowa, and Lyman Trumbull of Illinois (p. 235). Senator Ben Wade of Ohio, next in line, had been so sure of conviction he had begun choosing a cabinet.

  • How Young uses it: Johnson, the former tailor, is a dark mirror of Weitling, the tailor who stayed poor. She treats the Reconstruction South as a feast for the "Lords of Grab and Graft" (p. 233).

  • Further reading: Eric Foner, Reconstruction: America's Unfinished Revolution, 1863-1877 (1988).

Marx's letter to Lincoln (p. 237)

  • What it was: After Lincoln's reelection in November 1864, the International Workingmen's Association in London sent him an address of congratulations, drafted by Marx. It hailed the war as a fight against slavery that mattered to workers everywhere. The American minister in London, Charles Francis Adams, sent a courteous reply.

  • Why it matters: It is a little-known meeting between the founder of modern socialism and the Great Emancipator. Young adds that it drew protest from the Communist Club of New York (p. 237).

  • Further reading: Robin Blackburn, An Unfinished Revolution: Karl Marx and Abraham Lincoln (2011).

Sand Creek, the Washita, and the Indian wars (pp. 238-240)

  • What happened: On November 29, 1864, Colorado volunteers under Colonel John Chivington attacked the peaceful Cheyenne and Arapaho camp of Black Kettle at Sand Creek, Colorado. Most of the dead were women, children, and the elderly, and many bodies were mutilated. Four years later, on November 27, 1868, George Armstrong Custer's cavalry attacked Black Kettle's village on the Washita River in present-day Oklahoma, killing him and his wife.

  • Why it matters: Congressional inquiries condemned Sand Creek, but no one was punished. Chivington resigned his commission before he could be court-martialed. Young links it to the Ludlow Massacre of April 20, 1914, when Colorado militia burned a tent colony of striking coal miners and about twenty people died, among them eleven children (p. 239).

  • Where the record differs: Young gives "approximately four hundred and fifty" dead at Sand Creek (p. 239). Most modern estimates run from about 150 to 230. Her story of Custer's son "Yellow Swallow" (p. 240), by the Cheyenne captive Monahsetah, is Cheyenne oral tradition that historians dispute.

  • General Nelson A. Miles: The Indian fighter who later commanded the federal troops sent to Chicago in the Pullman strike of 1894 (p. 240). Young quotes Mr. Dooley's line that Miles had faced "death and promotion in every form."

  • Further reading: Ari Kelman, A Misplaced Massacre: Struggling over the Memory of Sand Creek (2013).

Mary Todd Lincoln after the war (pp. 241-256)

  • Grief and debt: She lost three of her four sons: Eddie in 1850, Willie in 1862, and Tad in 1871. She ran up heavy debts and in 1867 tried to sell her old clothes in New York, a public humiliation.

  • Herndon and Ann Rutledge: In 1866 Lincoln's former law partner William Herndon lectured that Lincoln's only true love had been Ann Rutledge of New Salem, who died in 1835 (p. 246). The claim wounded her deeply.

  • Europe and the pension: She sailed with Tad for Bremen on the City of Baltimore on October 1, 1868 (p. 247). In July 1870 Congress granted her a pension of three thousand dollars a year after bitter debate.

  • The insanity trial: In May 1875 her son Robert had her tried for insanity in Chicago. Merchants testified about her compulsive buying (p. 253). The jury, which included Lyman Gage, later McKinley's treasury secretary, found her insane, and she was sent to a private sanitarium at Batavia, Illinois. She was released that September and declared sane in 1876. Robert hired Pinkerton operatives to watch her (p. 252).

  • Further reading: Jean H. Baker, Mary Todd Lincoln: A Biography (1987).

Maximilian and Carlotta (p. 256)

  • What happened: While the United States was absorbed in civil war, Napoleon III installed the Austrian archduke Maximilian as emperor of Mexico in 1864. After the war, Secretary of State William Seward pressed France to withdraw its troops. Mexican republicans under Benito Juárez captured Maximilian, and he was shot on the Cerro de las Campanas, the Hill of the Bells, near Querétaro, on June 19, 1867.

  • Carlotta: His wife, Charlotte of Belgium, went to Europe in 1866 to plead for help and broke down mentally. She lived in seclusion until 1927.

  • Further reading: M. M. McAllen, Maximilian and Carlota: Europe's Last Empire in Mexico (2014).

Shorter entries

  • William Starke Rosecrans and the St. Louis printers (p. 237): The Union general, who won at Corinth and Stones River and lost at Chickamauga, commanded in Missouri in 1864. He used soldiers to replace striking St. Louis printers and machinists, an early use of troops against labor.

  • The Ku Klux Klan (p. 233): Founded in Pulaski, Tennessee, in 1865-1866. It waged terror against freedpeople and their allies during Reconstruction.

  • Carl Schurz (p. 242): A German Forty-Eighter, Union general, and later senator. He toured the South for Johnson in 1865 and reported harshly on the treatment of freedpeople. Young calls him "a spokesman for the Deep South," but he was one of its sharpest critics.

  • Frederick Douglass (p. 244): The abolitionist leader. Mary Lincoln sent him one of the president's walking sticks as a keepsake.

  • Victoria Woodhull (p. 249): A spiritualist, stockbroker, and radical who ran for president in 1872 on the Equal Rights Party ticket. Young's claim that she was once a "tramp clairvoyant" in Indianapolis is unverified.

  • Susan B. Anthony and the vote (p. 249): Anthony broke with former allies when the Fifteenth Amendment (1870) enfranchised Black men but not women. She was arrested for voting in 1872.

  • Jay Cooke and Crédit Mobilier (p. 255): Cooke's bank failed in September 1873 over Northern Pacific Railroad bonds, setting off the Panic of 1873. Crédit Mobilier was the Union Pacific construction company whose bribes to congressmen became the Grant era's great scandal.

  • Quincy Adams Ward's Indian Hunter (p. 239): The sculptor's bronze in Central Park, one of the first by an American sculptor placed there.

  • Miss Abbie Flagg (p. 225): The Terre Haute teacher who gave the boy Debs a Bible as a spelling prize.

  • Diamond Dick (p. 226): Hero of dime novels. His stories began in the late 1870s, after Debs's school years.

Allusions

  • Benedict Arnold and Aaron Burr (p. 231): America's archetypal traitors.

  • Brutus, Othello, Iago (p. 234): Shakespeare's figures of betrayal, applied to Stanton and Johnson.

  • Dolley Madison and the Blue Room (p. 242): The first lady famous for her White House hospitality, and its oval reception room.

  • Saint Bernard (p. 245): The rescue dog of the Alps.

  • Dante, the Pitti Palace, Ghiberti (p. 248): Florence, where Mary Lincoln wandered in her exile.
