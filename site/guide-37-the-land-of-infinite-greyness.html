Chapter 37: The Land of Infinite Greyness

← Chapter 36 · Guide Home · Chapter 38 →

Research notes: Chapter 37

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Order, Chaos, and Discipline · The Search for Meaning

Philosophy: Aristotle · Descartes and Dualism · Epicureanism · Marguerite Young on Her Craft · Time, Physics, and Chaos Theory · Wittgenstein

Allusions: Suicide in History and Literature · The History of Opium

You are in the deep water now.

Chapter 37 is the chapter where Young asks her hardest question, and asks it without flinching: what if nothing holds? What if the self is not a fixed point but a series of dissolving reflections? What if the boundary between dreaming and waking, living and dying, is not a line at all but a fog that has no far edge?

If you find this chapter disorienting, that is the correct response. You are not supposed to find your footing here. The chapter's architecture is designed to make the ground shift beneath you, to show you what it feels like to live inside Catherine Cartwheel's consciousness: a place where the ceiling is the floor, trees grow with their roots in the sky, and the dreamer cannot be identified because everyone in the dream might be the dreamer.

Stay with it. Young is not being difficult for the sake of difficulty. She is being precise.

What actually happens in Chapter 37

We are still in Catherine's household. Vera narrates her mother's state of mind, and that state of mind is the chapter's entire landscape. Catherine is lying horizontal, sedated with opium, caught in the half-country between waking and sleep. She is not fully in the world. She is not fully out of it. She is, as she has been for most of the novel, in between.

The chapter opens with a vision (mirrors drifting, clocks striking hours that make no sense, dolphins behaving unexpectedly) and then poses its governing question: but who was the dreamer? Not which character is dreaming. Who, among all of them, is the one whose dream contains the others?

Young never answers this. The question is the point.

Catherine and Mr. Spitzer circle the question of death and order. Catherine declares, in one of the chapter's sharpest lines, that order is a mad man's dream, a suicidal plan that leaves out the irrational elements. Mr. Spitzer, ever the pessimist, contributes his own anguished uncertainty. And beneath all of it, Catherine's refusal (persistent, desperate, and entirely human) to surrender to what the chapter calls the land of infinite greyness.

Cousin Hannah enters the chapter as she always enters: in memory, in force, in the contrast she makes with everyone still breathing. Her death is final. Young is clear about this. She will not return. She has been throttled by death, and what follows that throttling is, the novel admits for the first time without evasion, silence.

The land of infinite greyness

This phrase is the chapter's center of gravity, and you should know what Young means by it.

The land of infinite greyness is not heaven or hell. It is not a destination at all. It is the oblivion Catherine fears above everything else: the place where there is no memory, no ego, no voice, no image. Not darkness: darkness has a quality, a texture, a presence. Greyness is the absence of contrast itself. It is the condition in which nothing can be distinguished from anything else, in which selfhood simply stops having meaning.

Catherine's entire life (the opium, the bed, the household ruled by dream and illusion) can be read as a strategy for avoiding this. She stays horizontal because to stand up would be to move toward a world that might eventually require her to arrive at the grey. She drowns herself in hallucination because hallucination, however unreliable, is still vivid. It still has color. It still has her in it.

This is not weakness, though it might look like it. It is a woman clinging to the fact of her own experience with both hands, refusing to let go even when everything around her has dissolved. Young has considerable sympathy for this refusal. So should you.

The philosophy of reversals

Young fills Chapter 37 with images of inversion: the ceiling as the mirroring floor, trees “growing with their roots in the clouded sky” and their branches underground, night as day, dawn as twilight, beginning as end. His end was his beginning. His sun was his moon.

This is not surrealism for its own sake. Young is working through a genuine philosophical position: that the opposites we rely on to organize reality (life and death, real and dream, self and other) are not opposites at all. They are the same thing seen from different angles. The boundary between them is a convention, a useful fiction, and in Chapter 37, that fiction is wearing very thin.

The image that captures this best is the one Young gives Vera: “love might wear the face of death, the grinning skull, as death might wear the face of love.” This is not a gothic decoration. It is an observation about the structure of deep attachment: that to love something completely is already to have anticipated its loss, and that loss, when it comes, carries the ghost of everything the love was. The two faces belong to the same person. They always did.

Catherine, order, and the irrational

Pay attention to Catherine's declaration that order is a mad man's dream.

She means this seriously, and so does Young. The desire for order (for things to proceed logically, to have causes that explain their effects, to have selves that remain continuous from moment to moment) is, in this novel's cosmology, a form of willful delusion. The universe does not operate this way. People do not operate this way. A plan that omits the irrational elements is not a plan; it is a wish wearing the clothes of a plan.

Mr. Spitzer, who is conservative and precise and trusts only what he can verify, is the character who most wants order. And Mr. Spitzer is the character most obviously undone by his inability to achieve it: hearing sounds a minute late, existing at a second remove from life, never arriving in the present moment that order requires.

Catherine, who has given herself over entirely to the irrational, is paradoxically more honest about reality's actual texture. She is not sane, in any conventional sense. But she is not lying to herself about what the world is like. There is something Young wants you to notice here, about which of these two failures is the more truthful one.

Cousin Hannah, and the finality that Catherine refuses

Young does something in Chapter 37 that she has been careful not to do before: she allows the word finality.

Cousin Hannah is gone. Not transformed, not traveling through posthumous geographies, not available to be found if Catherine looks in the right direction. Gone. Throttled by death. The echoes of her voice, her boots, her campaigns for the vote, her defiance of every seraglio and hierarchy that tried to contain her; they fade in this chapter into something that Young's prose rarely permits: silence.

Mr. Spitzer contributes the chapter's closing image. After the last requiem, he says, there is nothing left but the hearing of silence. Not the silence itself: the hearing of it. The consciousness that remains after the music stops, registering the absence of what was there before.

This is as close as the novel comes to a conventional statement about grief. It doesn't stay there long. But it is there, and it is real, and you should let it land before the next chapter carries you somewhere else.

The question the chapter leaves open

Who was the dreamer?

Young raises this at the chapter's opening and does not close it. Here are the possibilities the novel has been building toward: Catherine is the dreamer and everyone else is her dream. Vera is the dreamer and Catherine is her dream. The novel itself is the dreamer and both of them are its dream. Or, and this is the version that keeps the question alive longest, there is no individual dreamer, only the dream, and what we call selfhood is the dream's way of pretending it has a source.

You don't need to resolve this. Young didn't resolve it. But hold the question as you read forward. It will keep mattering.

What to hold onto

If Chapter 37 feels like it is asking too much of you, come back to this: Catherine is afraid of disappearing. Not dying, exactly; she is already half-dead, and she has made her peace with the border region. What she is afraid of is the grey. The absolute colorless erasure of everything that makes her her.

That fear is entirely recognizable. You don't need opium dreams or a household ruled by hallucination to understand it. It is the fear that runs under all the novel's philosophical circling, all its reversals and cosmic imagery and blurred borders between waking and sleep. Strip everything else away and what remains is a woman who does not want to stop being.

Young treats this with complete seriousness. It is not a small thing to want to exist. It is not a small thing to refuse the grey. Even when the refusal looks like madness, even when it keeps you horizontal in a bed for decades, even when it means you cannot go out to meet the people who love you because going out would mean acknowledging a world that will eventually take you under, even then, the refusal is human, and it counts for something.
