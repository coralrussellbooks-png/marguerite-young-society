Research notes: Chapter 2

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Jock Cartwheel · Mr. Chandelier · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Love

Symbols: Birds

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Ancient Egypt · Gambling and Playing Cards · The History of Opium

If Chapter 1 dropped you into a grey bus moving through uncertain mist, Chapter 2 drops you into a bed.

Specifically, into the bed of Catherine Helena Cartwheel, Vera’s mother, where she has been lying, largely motionless, for years. She is not asleep. She is not awake. She is somewhere in between, sustained by opium, visited by hallucinations, and more alive in her dreams than most people are in their waking lives.

New readers sometimes find Catherine’s chapter the most disorienting in the entire novel. It helps to know, going in, that this disorientation is the point. You are inside a mind where the dream is more real than reality. Young is not asking you to follow a plot here. She is asking you to inhabit a consciousness.

What actually happens in Chapter 2

Mr. Spitzer, the family lawyer, a semi-retired, absent-minded man broken by grief visits Catherine every evening, as he has for years. Catherine lies in her enormous bed, surrounded by jewels and phantoms and the light of her chandelier, and receives him. An Egyptian sailor arrives at the house, lost and looking for Mr. Spitzer’s twin brother, Peron, who is dead. Catherine insists the sailor is real and gives him elaborate directions through the labyrinthine house. Mr. Spitzer tries to explain to the sailor that his brother is gone. The sailor dances with joy at seeing Mr. Spitzer, convinced he has found who he is looking for. Nobody fully agrees on what is happening.

Meanwhile, Catherine’s little yellow canary sings in its gilded cage. When the canary dies which it has, many times Mr. Spitzer secretly replaces it, so that Catherine will never know. She believes it is the same bird. It isn’t. It never is.

Who Catherine is, and how she got here

Catherine’s full name is Catherine Helena Cartwheel, née Snowden. Catherine means “innocent.” Helena means “the bright one.” Snowden means “snow hill.” She is, in Young’s naming logic, an innocent brightness buried under snow which is exactly what she is.

She grew up under a domineering father of fierce orthodox faith, whose rituals governed every hour of the household. She married Jock Cartwheel, a reckless sportsman who disappeared in the Alps, and was abandoned. She was socially humiliated. She carries an invisible birthmark on her left cheek that no one else can see, but which she is convinced marks her as flawed, set apart, not quite belonging to the world.

So she left it. The bed is her kingdom now. And in her kingdom, dead kings visit. Angels appear. Movie stars from silent films drift through. An Egyptian sailor arrives bearing a papyrus that turns out to be a horse-racing tip sheet.

The world inside Catherine’s head is not chaos. It is extraordinarily populated, extraordinarily detailed, and governed by its own logic. Young gives us access to it not to show us a mad woman, but to show us a mind that has decided, quite rationally, that the dream world is more honest than the real one.

The love triangle at the center of everything

Much of Chapter 2 establishes a triangular relationship that will echo through the entire novel. Catherine loved Peron Spitzer, the vivid, unreachable twin, who did not love her back. Joachim Spitzer, the devoted, steady, lawyerly twin, loved Catherine and was not loved in return. Peron died young, by his own hand. Joachim stayed.

Their very names encode this: Joachim means “raised by God.” Peron means “rock.” The man raised by God stayed earthbound, faithful, forgotten. The rock fell into the sea. This is why the Egyptian sailor’s arrival matters so much. He comes looking for Peron, the dead one, the beloved one, and sees him in Joachim’s face. Catherine understands this instantly. Mr. Spitzer does not want to.

The canary

The little yellow canary in its gilded cage is one of the novel’s most quietly devastating images. Catherine believes her life is tied to the bird’s: that if it dies, she dies. So Mr. Spitzer, faithfully and without ever telling her, replaces each dead canary with a living one. The illusion of continuity is maintained. The same bird has died a hundred times and is always singing.

Notice that this is also what Young is doing with time in this chapter and in the novel as a whole. The same moments return, slightly altered. The same losses repeat. The same questions are asked again and the answers are never quite the same, and yet the singing continues.

Mr. Chandelier

Among Catherine’s phantoms is one she calls Mr. Chandelier, the actual chandelier of her room, personified, a companion “not always faithful,” holding seven lighted candles in seven crystal hands. He is not decoration. He is what happens when a mind so thoroughly animated by imagination turns even its furniture into characters. The seven candles evoke ritual, illumination, the sacred number that recurs throughout the novel’s mythology. But the light they cast is chandelier light: prismatic, beautiful, not quite revealing anything clearly.

What to hold onto

Catherine is not a cautionary tale about addiction. She is a philosopher who has chosen her medium. Her argument, spoken in this chapter with full lucidity, is that the real world is inevitably a disappointment, a series of promises that fail to materialize, while the dream world is “expansive and protective,” alive with what life actually should have been.

You do not have to agree with her. But you have to take her seriously. Young does.

When you feel lost in this chapter, return to the canary. A woman who believes the same bird has been singing to her for years, and a man who makes sure it always is. That is Chapter 2, held in a single image.
