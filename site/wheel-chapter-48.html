Research notes: Chapter 48 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 48

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

"Or were he and his brother one and the same as he sometimes dreamed — though recognizing that this might be his own extinction — one and the same shining in the morning, evening"

Mr. Spitzer is constitutively multiple. He cannot determine whether he and his brother Peron are distinct beings or a single self appearing at different times, in different lights. The morning star and evening star, believed for centuries to be two stars, are revealed as one and this astronomical fact becomes the chapter's central metaphor for selfhood: you may be two and one simultaneously, and the confusion is not error but structure.

Further, Mr. Spitzer oscillates between identities without resolution: lawyer, musician, lamplighter, ferryman, stowaway, gambler, bookkeeper, customs officer, flag signalman. He does not choose among these. He is all of them, provisionally, simultaneously. Not One Self here is not a psychological disorder; it is the honest acknowledgment that identity is a superposition, never a fixed point.

The chapter refuses to collapse this multiplicity. Mr. Spitzer never arrives at a singular self. He is the irresolvable, and the text honors that irresolution by never forcing a conclusion.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

"He had navigated himself blindly into mirrors as into clouds, distinguishing not between glass and clouds, windows and doors, clouds and glass, doors and windows"

The entire architecture of the old house (its doors, windows, mirrors, and sealed rooms) is an epistemological labyrinth in which the distinction between the real and its reflection has collapsed. Mr. Spitzer cannot find the exit because he cannot reliably distinguish entrance from exit, surface from depth, glass from air.

The "court of gold and silver flagstones, moonstones burning with fires within" is not described as metaphor; it is described as the actual terrain of his legal practice. The world Mr. Spitzer inhabits is one where the constructed and the given are indistinguishable. There is no sunlit exterior. The cave has only more rooms.

Most strikingly, the engraver who adds false notes to the musical score, so that future musicians will play punctuation marks as sounds, is the chapter's figure for how reality itself is contaminated by construction. What we perform as truth may be someone else's editorial insertion. The original music may be entirely lost.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

perhaps "it was many years before one recognized that long ago his love had died—or that she had betrayed him... or both had died and yet lived on in memory, each assuming that the other was the ghost"

Grief in this chapter does not resolve. It sediments. Mr. Spitzer's grief for his brother, for lost love, for missing musical notes, for the fading moon, for the vanishing stars; none of it moves toward acceptance or closure. Instead it accumulates, layer upon layer, each new loss folding into the prior ones.

The chapter's grammar enacts this: the sentences themselves refuse to end, piling subordinate clause upon subordinate clause, each qualification adding weight rather than resolution. The grief is the weather of the prose. It is the climate in which every thought occurs.

The recognition that one may have lived for years with a stranger while believing it was one's love, and remained loyal to that mistake, is grief not as event but as condition. The honest life, as Radical Immanence would have it, stops pretending otherwise. Mr. Spitzer does not pretend. He is saturated.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

"There were so many papers still to go through, some not marked — old calling cards, some which were blank, bearing no name"

Mr. Spitzer's entire function in this chapter is witnessing. He is the unemployed lawyer who comes to "make a last account tentative as all things must be, to sum up all which had been missing, all which had never been". He does not fix. He does not conclude. He opens boxes, rattles dice cups, attempts keys in locks, and when no key fits, he does not force the lock. He stays in the room.

This is the chapter's ethical center: the refusal to rush toward resolution. Mr. Spitzer examines blank calling cards, scraps of tinsel, reindeer heads staring into mirrors forty keys with no master key among them. He witnesses the full inventory of incompletion without flinching and without falsifying it into meaning.

The darkness in which he works, where he cannot even see his own face unless in a moon-lined mirror, makes this witnessing heroic in the most quiet possible way. He lights matches despite his terror of burning. Attention, here, is courage.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

"He was always posting, trotting, gaiting, cantering, pacing, racking, running, single-footing, slowing his music to a walk so that he would be prepared for all eventualities"

Mr. Spitzer builds and rebuilds. He returns perpetually to the same questions (about death, about consciousness, about his brother, about his own identity) and each return brings not resolution but refinement of the question. The building is the returning.

The hourglass image makes this explicit:

"must the hour-glass of his heart be ever reversed, and must he live twice, and if so, must he die twice"

The reversal of the hourglass is not failure; it is the structure of his existence. He does not move forward in a line. He moves in spirals, returning to the same shore with slightly different eyes, the same wentletrap catching new shells. The recursive construction is not a symptom of his confusion. It is the method by which a consciousness this honest can operate.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

"to open old boxes, close old doors... lock that which he could not open, open that which he could not close"

This is perhaps the pillar most perfectly embodied by the chapter's form. The prose itself is the argument. These sentences do not conclude; they extend, qualify, double back, add new subordinate clauses long after any conventional sentence would have ended. The form is the ethics.

The forty keys with no master key is the chapter's central image of honorable incompletion. Mr. Spitzer does not fabricate a key. He does not pretend one of the forty fits when it does not. He holds the incompletion honestly, understanding that "the demand for completion is its own violence against experience."

The legal documents he cannot read the blank calling cards, the music with missing notes: all of these are instances where Mr. Spitzer refuses to fill in what is absent with false certainty. The unfinished is not failure. It is, here, the only form of integrity available.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

"For psychologically he was this old bindlestiff who wore a backless suit, a crownless hat, and paper shoes. His clothes were rags, tatters, scraps of his dead psyches like faded rainbows"

Mr. Spitzer is the chapter's marginal figure par excellence: the unemployed lawyer with no clients the customs officer at an empty port the flag signalman where no horses run the lamplighter who may also be the one who extinguishes the lights. He occupies every threshold position: between life and death, between self and brother, between morning and evening, between the real and its reflection.

His marginality is not incidental. It is evidential. He is positioned at every boundary precisely so that the constructed nature of those boundaries becomes visible. The suit with no back, which cannot be turned in, is the perfect image: a self that cannot fully present itself, that exists only in the aspect it shows, with nothing behind it. The marginal person does not reveal an exception to the rule. They reveal that the rule was always a construction.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

"All things which happened happened twice to him, had he not always said? — as his death would happen twice — perhaps also his love"

The past, in this chapter, does not recede. It accumulates beneath Mr. Spitzer until he cannot distinguish what has happened from what is happening, what is memory from what is present experience. He is like "a traveller who had passed through a city in the night before he knew that he had passed it—and by that time he was looking forward to it again".

This is sedimentary time made phenomenological. The city is already beneath him, already pressing up through his anticipation of it. The loss of his brother is not behind him; it is under him, constituting the ground he walks on. The dead love is not past; it is the substrate through which all present experience is filtered.

The image of the stowaway king "wound in winding cloth of silver and gold" sinking through miles of water while sleeping, never knowing when the ship went down, is the chapter's most concentrated figure for this: the past event that constitutes us without our knowledge, the sediment we stand on without knowing it was once a living thing.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

"perhaps what was required was an arrowsmith to make that arrow which should reach this heart, dead heart"

The chapter is acutely aware that it is made of language, and that language is inadequate to its subject while being the only available tool. Mr. Spitzer's legal documents (contracts, wills, notary seals) are the chapter's figure for language that pretends to authority it does not possess. He affixes seals "as if to give the mystery of authority to that which had mystified him."

The missing musical notes, the black notes absent from the score, are the chapter's figure for what language cannot say, the gaps that haunt every utterance. The engraver's false additions figure language that has been corrupted at the source, so that what we speak as truth is someone else's interpolation.

Most tellingly, Mr. Spitzer's own legal identity, the profession by which he names himself, is something he cannot verify:

"he sometimes feeling an almost absolute certainty that he was not the practitioner of Blackstone as he supposed"

The name we give ourselves is a construction. The language of identity speaks from somewhere particular, and that somewhere may be wrong.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

"for here was surely no jewel, no jewel dimly burning unless it was the water-freighted jewel of consciousness burning but one moment before it went out or burning but one moment after it went out"

The sacred in this chapter is entirely immanent. It does not point outward to a heaven or a beyond. It burns here, in the water-freighted jewel of consciousness itself, in the moonlight on a frayed shawl in the match spark in a waxen hand in the silver button rolling in the tide in the frog's croak and the cricket's cry and the flame's whisper.

The court of gold and silver flagstones is not a transcendent realm; it is described as the actual site of Mr. Spitzer's legal practice. The sacred does not lie beyond the ordinary. It is the ordinary seen with sufficient attention. The grey house between two water towers, the reindeer heads in the dark hallway, the forty keys jangling around a dead woman; these are the sites of whatever holiness this chapter knows.

The immanent transcendence is most concentrated in the image of the pearl:

"Did the pearl know it was the pearl, or did the moon know it was the moon winding its nacreous walls around the sleeping flame"

The sacred is the winding, the enclosure, the layers of ordinary matter building around a living irritant. It is not elsewhere. It is this.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

"And what did he know as to these fixed and immutable boundary lines... between life and death, death and life — for if he could move forward to tomorrow... could he not cross from death to life, for could he not, by the same token, move backward to yesterday when perhaps he had died"

This is the chapter's final and most radical claim. Mr. Spitzer is not a subject moving through time. He is the point where time folds back on itself. His consciousness is constituted by what was deposited before he arrived (his brother's life and death, the old suffragette's journeys, the music of dead composers, the love that died before he recognized it) and he is, in his wandering through this house, constituting the sediment that will press up through whatever comes after.

The lamplighter who may also be the one who extinguishes the lights, the morning star that is also the evening star, the death that happens twice: all of these are figures for the crossing point. Mr. Spitzer is not at the crossing. He is the crossing. His obligation to witness, to account, to stay in the room with the incompletion; this is not chosen. As Radical Immanence insists, it is structural.
