CODA: The Sentence That Hasn't Ended

← Chapter Seventeen: What Comes After Witnessing · Radical Immanence Collection · Bibliography and Endnotes →

This account of Young's philosophy began with a sentence about a sentence. Marguerite Young spent eighteen years writing a novel that refuses to end. The refusal is the argument. The continuation is the ethics. The sentence that hasn't ended is not a failure of resolution. It is the most honest description available of what it means to be alive in a world that does not resolve.

Young's philosophy, assembled from its ten load-bearing pillars and placed in conversation with the thinkers it illuminates and is illuminated by, and extrapolated toward the political life it makes possible, is not a completed system in the way that philosophical systems usually claim to be complete. It is a recursive one. It knows that the building is provisional. It builds anyway. It knows that the grief does not resolve. It witnesses anyway. It knows that the self is plural and the real is constructed and the honest ethical stance requires a quality of attention that is exhausting to sustain. It sustains it anyway. Not because the sustaining is easy or because the arrival is coming. Because the sustaining is what integrity looks like in a world built the way this world is built.

What Young gives us is not comfort in the cheap sense. It is companionship in the honest one. The companionship of a thinker who looked at the full weight of what existence contains and did not flinch and did not conclude and did not replace the difficulty with a consolation that would have required pretending the difficulty wasn't there. She stayed in the room. For eighteen years she stayed in the room with the hardest questions her philosophy asked, and she built 1,198 pages of honest witness to what she found there.

The ten pillars together are the philosophical account of why the sentence has not ended, why it cannot end without lying, why the ongoing sentence is the only honest form that the engagement with this world can take.

It is saying that the world is here, and plural, and grief-saturated, and constructed, and sacred in the immanent sense, and incomplete in the honest sense, and populated at its margins by the people whose complexity the sorting systems cannot process, and layered with time that the present moment carries without always knowing it carries, and expressible only in a form that refuses to simplify, and worth building in, and worth witnessing in, and worth staying in the room with, without flinching and without concluding.

That is radical immanence.

That is the philosophy of Marguerite Young.

The sentence is still going. The work is still going. The world that made the work necessary is still going. We are still in the middle.

The question Young leaves us with is not how to end it. It is how to keep writing it with the honesty and the attention and the willingness to return to the beginning and find what we missed the first time that the sentence, if it is to mean anything, requires.

That is what she built. That is what we build on. That is what we build.
