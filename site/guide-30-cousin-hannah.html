Research notes: Chapter 30

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Eustace · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Grief, Loss, and Mourning · Women, Gender, and Marriage

Symbols: Clothing, Hats, and Skirts

Philosophy: Augustine and Christian Theology · Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Angels and Archangels · Astronomy · Gambling and Playing Cards · Napoleon and Wellington · Suicide in History and Literature · The History of Opium · Women's Suffrage

After thirty chapters of preparation, Cousin Hannah arrives. Not in person, she died years ago and is buried “in a grey New England churchyard where many tombs were marked only with the sign of the codfish,” but in full force, through Catherine’s memory and longing and Vera’s reconstruction of what she knows.

This chapter is the novel’s great opening of doors. Young has been building a closed, interior world: the coastal house, the opium bedroom, the Miss MacIntosh sequence, the Spitzer portrait. Cousin Hannah blows all the doors open. She has been to Persia, Babylonia, Arabia Deserta, the Alps, the Himalayas, the deserts of Moab. She has ridden against Spanish cavalcades disguised as an Arab king. She has heard muezzins and archangels and the whispers of dying sea captains to their wives. She is everything the coastal house is not.

Who is Cousin Hannah?

Cousin Hannah Freemount-Snowden is Catherine’s cousin, or was, before she died. She was a Boston feminist, suffragette, world traveler, balloonist, mountain climber, swordswoman, archer, markswoman, and spy. She traveled disguised as an Arab king, a Tibetan jockey, “a lazar ringing his bell in” holy cities. She rode her white horse El-Burraq, named for the horse Mohammed tethered at the Wailing Wall before ascending to heaven, through howling desert sands. She attacked Spanish soldiers who were assaulting a Riff caravan, routing them single-handedly, and disappeared back into the sandstorm.

She never stayed anywhere long. She left no forwarding address. When Catherine tried to call her by telephone, it rang through empty rooms in a house that had been torn down to make room for an asphalt highway. Cousin Hannah was always already gone.

She is buried “in a grey New England churchyard” with a “jeweled sword at her side and” a great star sapphire on her breast, pinned there by Mr. Spitzer when he laid her out. That detail, Mr. Spitzer as the one who dressed her for burial, is Young’s way of connecting the novel’s poles: the most adventurous spirit in the book was put to rest by its most earthbound one.

What Cousin Hannah represents

The novel has given us two responses to an untenable world. Catherine dissolves into hallucination. Miss MacIntosh insists on the practical surface. Cousin Hannah takes a third path: she acts.

She is the novel’s figure of pure will. She sees hazards before they occur and chooses the lesser danger, but she always chooses. The iceberg moved to allow her passage. She cut “through great cables of iron as if they were silken threads.” Where the road crumbled into a chasm, she went “on, crossing great stone deserts and viewless abysses as if they were city streets.” She found the dangers of the Alps less than the danger of crossing traffic-crowded Boston streets.

Young describes her as a being “dedicated to the overthrow of the dream and of the dreamer in all countries.” This is what makes her the opposite of Catherine and the uncanny double of Miss MacIntosh. Both Hannah and Miss MacIntosh are anti-dreamers, insisting on the real. But where Miss MacIntosh insists through proverbs and schedules and the plain surface of the domestic day, Hannah insists through action, through bodies in motion, through the actual material world of camels and swords and mountain passes.

She is also, Young makes clear through Catherine’s memories, an emancipated extension of Catherine herself: the “Captain Derring-Do who engaged in” the adventures Catherine only dreamed of through the endless years. Catherine’s longing for Hannah is a longing for her own unlived life.

The loss of a skirt

Young drops one detail about Cousin Hannah with characteristic lightness and moves on: “because of the loss of a skirt, it was known now, her entire life had been perhaps an act of sorrow and redemption.”

This is the novel’s most compressed statement of Cousin Hannah’s inner life, and Young never elaborates it. What skirt? Lost how? To what sorrow? We are not told. What we are told is the consequence: her entire life, all those years of adventure and disguise and camels and swords and mountain passes and Arabian nights, was a response to one lost garment. A sorrow to be redeemed through sixty years of action.

Young places this in the same chapter as the Miss MacIntosh sequence’s distant echo: the old nursemaid who never looked in mirrors, who carried needles and pins to forbid inquiry, who slept in the cold and kept the windows open. Both women have built elaborate lives around something that cannot be named directly. Both have turned a private wound into a public performance. Hannah’s is grander and more spectacular. The wound is the same order of magnitude.

Eustace

Before Cousin Hannah takes over the chapter, Young gives us one more of Catherine’s phantoms: Eustace. He is the most volatile of her imaginary visitors, taking many forms: a mocking bird, an old woman, an old bearded man like a cypress tree, Wellington “at the head of his troops,” a blind astronomer, a Venetian murderous valet, the murdered gentleman who walks abroad at midnight. He is limitless, “not confined to any one form by the laws of nature, which” have been suspended in his case.

Catherine calls to him the way she calls to all her visitors: “Eustace, Eustace! Oh, is it you, Eustace dear, and the golden-antlered stags, the large planets, the small planets? Come in, Eustace, and do close the door, for there’s a great draft.” It is the most ordinary hospitality, extended to the most extraordinary being.

Eustace functions as a hinge between the Spitzer chapters and the Hannah chapter: he is the figure of pure mutability, the visitor who cannot be fixed into a form, the one Catherine summons when Mr. Spitzer’s too-solid flesh becomes oppressive. He is the possibility of radical transformation. Cousin Hannah, in her own way, is Eustace made flesh.

Cousin Hannah’s views on the Spitzers

Young gives us one of the novel’s great comic passages through Cousin Hannah’s opinions of the twins. She “had never cared for the Spitzer boys,” had stanchly predicted “they would come to no good end,” and described them in terms that collapse the distinction between them: “the musical gambler with his piano-playing and horn-blowing friends, the horse-playing musician, the gambling lawyer who would take his last chance, the philanthropic dice-rattler,” “and the suicidal butterfly-chaser chasing himself with his butterfly net. Both brothers were profligates.”

She is partly right and wholly wrong simultaneously, which is Young’s method with characters who are powerful but limited. Hannah sees through things with a ruthless sangfroid. She cannot always see what she is looking through them at. “The suicidal butterfly-chaser chasing himself with his butterfly net” is the most precise and most wrong description of Mr. Spitzer ever offered. He is chasing himself. But not with a butterfly net.

What to watch for as you keep reading

Cousin Hannah will return in her own chapters later in the novel; she is one of the most important characters in the book, and Young will give her considerably more space. What Chapter 30 does is establish her presence, her history, and her relationship to the household. She is the great absence in Catherine’s life: the cousin who never answered the phone, who never left a forwarding address, who is now dead in a codfish churchyard but still more alive in the imagination than most living people.

The question Catherine keeps asking through Hannah (Have you seen my husband?) is the question that unifies the chapter. Catherine’s husband walked “between two frozen mountain peaks, and” was lost. Vera’s father walked a frozen cable between snow-capped peaks and fell. Young is doing something deliberate with this rhyme. The father who went into the mountains and did not return is the novel’s founding absence, the wound before the wound. Cousin Hannah, who knows the Alpine abysses with intimacy, is the only person in the novel who might have looked.

El-Burraq, Cousin Hannah’s white horse, is named for the horse that carried Mohammed on his night journey from Mecca to Jerusalem and then to heaven. It is the horse of the miraculous journey, the crossing from one world to another. That Cousin Hannah rode this horse through howling desert sands is Young at her most quietly magnificent.
