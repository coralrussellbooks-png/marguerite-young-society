Chapter 41: Let Me Out of My Ear

← Chapter 40 · Guide Home · Chapter 42 →

Research notes: Chapter 41

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Identity and the Self · Music, Sound, and Silence

Symbols: Silence and Deafness

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft · Phenomenology

Allusions: American Writers · Fairy Tales and Folklore · The History of Opium · Women's Suffrage

Catherine Cartwheel is everywhere and nowhere. She controls the tides. She is a great engineer, a mathematical genius, a builder of cities and bridges across impossible distances. She walks naked through warm opalescent blizzards. She rides night trains through the Gobi Desert. She raises the spectral dead. She counts pearls that are worlds.

She cannot walk.

Chapter 41 is the chapter where Young holds these two facts side by side without resolving them, the boundless interior empire and the body that has not left the bed, and asks you to feel the full weight of both simultaneously. Catherine is the most powerful person in the novel and the most confined. Her omnipotence is entirely imaginary and entirely real. It is the only kind she has, and she has made it vast.

What actually happens in Chapter 41

Vera describes her mother's condition with a precision that has accumulated over forty chapters into something close to grief. Catherine believes there is only an infinitesimal difference, a hair's breadth, between life and death. Her spirit moves outside her body regularly; multiple selves depart and may not return, and one of them, she suspects, may be immortal and lost somewhere she cannot retrieve it.

The house shifts around her. Furniture migrates. Mirrors hang lopsided. Windows look out on different seas depending on the hour. Objects appear without explanation: riding gloves, feather fans, footprints in rooms where no one has walked. Invisible guests come and go. The house is not a building; it is Catherine's mind given architecture, and it follows the same laws her mind follows, which are not the laws of physics.

Mr. Spitzer (his full name given here as Joachim, after his dead twin Peron) makes his complaint explicit in this chapter: Catherine has never seen him as a unique individual. If she had, he says, he would have known his own identity. This is one of the saddest lines in the novel. He has been waiting, all his life in this house, for her to see him clearly enough that he could see himself. She never has. She has been too busy being everywhere else.

And Catherine, when she considers Cousin Hannah, the suffrage captain who lived with a clear goal and died having stuck to it, does not envy her. This, too, lands. After everything: the admiration, the guilt, the long meditation on Hannah's life across a dozen chapters, Catherine does not want what Hannah had. The single coherent purpose, the life organized toward one legible end. She does not want to be that clear.

Lost inside the ear

The chapter's strangest and most resonant image is one Catherine offers of herself: she is lost inside her own ear.

Young treats the ear as a labyrinth: a vast continent of passages and echoes, a doorway to the soul through which she entered and cannot find her way back out. She pleads to be let out of her ear. She has been wandering its corridors, hearing the accumulated sounds of her life reverberating in the chambers, unable to locate the exit.

The ear as a labyrinth is anatomically suggestive: the inner ear is literally labyrinthine, a coiled structure of canals and chambers whose malfunction produces vertigo, the sense that the world is spinning without cause. But Young is reaching for something beyond anatomy. The ear is where sound becomes experience, where the external world crosses into the interior. To be lost inside your own ear is to be trapped in the act of receiving: to be unable to stop hearing everything, to be made entirely of the world's noise with no way to stand outside it.

Catherine has been in this condition for the entire novel. Chapter 41 finally gives it a name.

The omnipotent invalid

Young is doing something very deliberate with Catherine's claimed powers. She controls winds, tides, stars. She is a great engineer, a mathematical genius. She constructs bridges and cities. She raises the dead.

These claims, offered within a chapter that also establishes that she cannot walk and that others perceive her life as one long absence, are not simply the grandiosity of a disordered mind. Young takes them seriously as a description of what imagination actually is: a power genuinely exercised, genuinely creative, genuinely world-making, but exercised in a space that does not intersect with the space where other people live.

The novel has been asking, since its first chapter, about the relationship between what is real and what is imagined. Chapter 41 gives you the extreme version of that question in the person of Catherine: a woman whose inner life is so vast and so detailed and so genuinely felt that it constitutes a complete world, and whose outer life has contracted to a bed and a room and the sound of invisible servants moving through an infinite house. Which is more real? Young will not choose. But she will not let you dismiss either.

Mr. Spitzer and the looking-glass self

The complaint Mr. Spitzer makes in Chapter 41 (that Catherine has never seen him as a unique individual, and that this failure has cost him his identity) draws on what the sociologist Charles Horton Cooley called the looking-glass self: the theory that we develop our sense of who we are by observing how others perceive us, by reading ourselves in the mirror of other people's responses.

If the mirror is Catherine, Mr. Spitzer has been looking into a glass that shows him everything except himself. She looks through him toward the dead, toward Cousin Hannah, toward her own innumerable interior selves. He is present in the room and invisible in her perception, and after years of this (decades, perhaps) he has begun to wonder whether he exists at all.

This is one of Young's quietest and most devastating insights in the novel: that the loneliness of being unseen by the person you love is not simply emotional pain. It is an ontological problem. You need to be seen to know that you are there. Mr. Spitzer, who has been a second remove from life since his twin died, has been trying to use Catherine's gaze to confirm his own existence. She has never given him that. She has been too busy looking at the dead.

Catherine and Cousin Hannah: the final accounting

Here, forty-one chapters in, is where we get it plainly: Catherine does not envy Cousin Hannah.

Think about what this means in the context of everything that has come before. The novel has spent enormous space on Cousin Hannah: her strength, her campaigns, her magnificent refusals, her secret wish for the domestic life she publicly rejected, her death into permanence. Catherine has held her as a measure, a plumb line, a rebuke. And now, given the chance to say whether she would trade, she declines.

She does not envy a life organized around suffrage and then ended. She does not envy the clarity. She does not envy the coherence. And the title Young gives Hannah here, the patriarchal suffrage captain, is itself a small, sharp irony: a suffragette who operated within the structure of authority she was fighting, who had to make herself legible to the systems she wanted to change. Catherine has never tried to be legible to anything. She has slipped all the way out of legibility into pure interior, and she would not go back.

This is not an endorsement of Catherine's choice. But it is Young's refusal, one more time, to make the novel's verdict simple.

If she woke up, she would rapidly age

The note tucked into To All My Darlings about this passage is one of the novel's stranger keys: the suggestion that if Catherine really woke up, she would rapidly age and die.

Young is drawing on a deep vein of folk and fairy tale logic here: the enchanted sleeper whose suspension in dream is also a suspension of time, whose waking would mean catching up with all the years the dream held at bay. Sleeping Beauty reversed: not waiting to be woken into life but held in the dream to avoid the life that would kill her. Catherine's horizontal state is not only illness and addiction. It is, in the logic of the novel's symbolic world, a spell. The opium and the dreams are keeping her alive by keeping her out of time.

Miss MacIntosh tried to wake her. Miss MacIntosh is gone. The spell continues.

Sunt lacrimae rerum

Young drops a line of Virgil into Chapter 41: sunt lacrimae rerum, which translates as there are tears in things, or life is tragic. It comes from the Aeneid, where Aeneas, exiled from Troy and newly arrived in Carthage, sees paintings depicting the fall of his city and weeps, and then reflects that even in this foreign place, suffering is known, tears are known, the world mourns what is lost.

This is the emotional key to the chapter. Behind the omnipotence and the labyrinthine ear and the invisible guests and the shifting windows, behind all the magnificent excess of Catherine's interior life, there are tears in things. The absence of sorrow, Catherine reflects, is itself a sorrow. The inability to feel what should be felt is another kind of loss, added to all the others.

She counts her pearls, which are worlds. She loses count. She begins again.

What to hold onto

Chapter 41 is the fullest portrait the novel has given us of Catherine as she actually is, stripped of the mythology Young has been carefully building and reduced, or elevated, to a woman in a bed who is also, simultaneously, everywhere.

What to carry out of this chapter: the ear as labyrinth, and the plea to be let out of it. Mr. Spitzer's need to be seen, and Catherine's inability to see him. The fact that Catherine, given the choice, would not trade her boundless and ungovernable interior life for Cousin Hannah's focused and purposeful one. And underneath all of it, sounding like a bell struck once and still resonating: there are tears in things. The most spectacular consciousness in the novel knows this. It does not make her well. But it makes her true.
