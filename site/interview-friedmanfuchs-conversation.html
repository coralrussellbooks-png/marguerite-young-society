Young's Interviews Analyzed

"A Conversation with Marguerite Young," by Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs (Review of Contemporary Fiction)

← Previous · Young's Interviews Analyzed · Next →

See also: Marguerite Young, Our Darling, Part 7

A reading for the Miss MacIntosh project.

The source at a glance

This is a focused question-and-answer interview conducted by Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs, the editors of the anthology "Breaking the Sequence," and credited here to the Review of Contemporary Fiction (Spring 2003, 23.1). It was held while Young was alive and at work on the Debs biography, which she describes as three volumes underway, so the conversation predates her 1995 death. Two things make it important for the project. It is the source the Neville profile quotes, including the closing image of walking toward a utopia across a bridge that may end first, and because the interviewers are the very critics who read Young as an experimental, feminist writer, it is the one place Young answers that framing directly, and largely rejects it. It is also among the richest of the primary interviews, giving the clearest statement of her single lifelong theme and a good deal of new detail on the book's losses, the excised novellas, her reception as a woman, and her own sense of her tradition.

One book: paradise lost

Asked whether her long, varied career marks changes of direction, Young gives the flattest denial in the set: "All the books I have written have been one book, from the beginning." Her first poem, written at five and about loss, held "the themes of everything I would ever write," and her single subject has always been "paradise lost, always the lost cause, the lost leader, the lost utopia." The poetry, "Angel in the Forest," Miss MacIntosh, and the Debs book are all, in her account, one search for lost utopia. She restates the novel's structure in her own words, Miss MacIntosh as "the center of the wheel, the hub," with the other characters as spokes and the whole "endlessly expanding like a universe," which confirms that the wheel image the Neville profile uses comes from Young herself. Miss MacIntosh she calls "the orphaned angel, the asexual angel, neither male nor female," who without her wig becomes the denuded figure any person would be stripped of illusions. She keeps one formal distinction: the Debs book is as poetic as the novel but "balladry rather than epic."

The political thesis

The interview is the most explicit of any on the novel's politics. "It isn't that Miss MacIntosh has a social conscience," Young says, "but the book does," and she ties this directly to the theme of illusion: understanding hallucination is what keeps a person from "blindly following a leader," since one must know whether a leader is "sane or insane, over the abyss." She holds that "there can be no poetic novel without political consciousness," and faults poetic writers who lapse into realism for lacking a philosophy to sustain them. She extends the claim to all her work, insisting that "Angel in the Forest is based upon social consciousness," as "every real book is," and that any big book must have "more than one undertow." She even reframes the origin of the utopia book around labor: it was a band of wandering Ozark coal miners, met near her mother's New Harmony, who made her want to write it.

Loss made flesh: the missing breast and the flaw

Here Young answers the bodily detail the interviews otherwise omit and the criticism notices. Miss MacIntosh is not only bald but missing a breast "because everything is lost," and Young adds that after the mastectomy the character went to work in a bowling alley, "a place where she could get herself killed," having lost "her hair, her love, her brother, her identity." The flaw is generative rather than merely tragic: Esther marries the most demented man and Vera the stone-deaf man because a perfect being is impossible, and "out of the flaw may come the universe," as with "the crystal flaw" in her poems, imperfect and therefore able to come into being. This idea has a private root she names for the first time, a real flour miller who tried and failed to pass his thumbprint to his son, whose son then killed himself; Young could never write the story but "put that flour miller and his thumb into everything," calling it "the thing I never could write, the idea of identity and passage of the soul." The recurring objects that turn into other objects, a coin into a drachma into a poker chip, she names as "transmutation and transmigration, metempsychosis," the same passage of the soul through matter.

The novellas she meant to free

This is the fullest catalog of the excised material that haunts the whole archive. Young says she hopes to bring out four or five novellas cut from or adjacent to the book, and describes two: one in which Mr. Spitzer, who "always gets it and never wants it," asks a shopkeeper for a town clock and is then burdened with whatever absurd thing he named; and one of Mr. Spitzer's quest to King of Prussia, Pennsylvania, to find an ageless pony that had belonged to Queen Victoria, "a quest for something that cannot be found." Her Scribner's editor, she says, wanted to publish the novellas, but she always wanted to revise them first. She repeats the regret that runs from 1965 onward, that the book would have sold far better published "one volume at a time," and Esther Longtree she again calls, borrowing from Stein, "the mother of us all," a "song of songs of schizophrenia" for whom every dead butterfly is a lost child.

On being a woman writer

This interview draws out Young's fullest statement on gender and reception, and it complicates her usual line. She says she felt no resentment at the time that experimental men drew attention while women were neglected, since she always rated women highly, Kay Boyle, Katherine Anne Porter, Christina Stead, Mansfield, Anna Kavan, Jane Bowles, and thought of herself as "neither a woman or a man" but a person "doomed to be a writer." Yet she then describes the reception of Miss MacIntosh as an ordeal of "brutal male reviewers" whose refrain was that "if she had gotten married she never would have done this," a tone she calls "sometimes hysterical" and different from the cruelty of women reviewers, and she names, without naming names, men who seemed to want to destroy her, while crediting William Goyen with beautiful reviews. "I think there is a rage against women," she concludes, "I've come to see that now although at the time I did not notice it." The passage is the one Fuchs's essay cites, and it shows Young arriving late at a feminist reading of her own neglect, alongside the punishing teaching-and-writing schedule she kept, teaching until four, sleeping an hour, writing until midnight.

Traditional, not experimental

The most valuable disagreement in the set occurs when the interviewers press the experimental label. Young resists it plainly: "I see myself as traditional even though I know you see my work as experimental." She places Sterne, Joyce, and Proust in a long line, "the Grand Tradition, clear back to Don Quixote," and calls realism the recent thing. She denies the usual comparisons, saying she was influenced not by Joyce or Eliot but by Saint Augustine's "City of God," "Tristram Shandy," Gogol's "Dead Souls," Dickens, and Poe, and even more by "the poetry of the Mormons." She rebuts the Eliot echoes directly, "there are no echoes of Eliot," only "echoes of the things that influenced him," pointing to her Chicago study of epic under Robert Morss Lovett and of structure under Ronald S. Crane, with whom she read "Tom Jones" thirteen times. When asked why the novel has no beginning, middle, or end, she answers, "because life has no beginning, middle or end." This head-on rejection of the avant-garde label is exactly the claim the Fuchs essay reads against, and the two together frame the central critical question about Young: whether she is a traditionalist working the Grand Tradition to an extreme or an experimentalist writing what that criticism calls feminine writing.

Set against the other sources

As the interview Neville mined and Fuchs argued against, this conversation is a hinge for the whole compilation. It gives, in Young's own voice, the unifying theme the other sources only imply, that every book is one book about lost utopia, and it confirms that the wheel-and-spokes structure is hers. It answers questions the other interviews leave open: the missing breast, and the lost brother, as part of Miss MacIntosh's total loss; and it clarifies the Riley puzzle from the Neville profile, since it is Riley and Debs, not Young and Riley, who were the drinking companions, with Young reporting "four books ready," the three-volume Debs and the nearly finished Riley. Its Debs origin, the champagne evening when Mark Van Doren cheered her choice of Riley, is new, as is the reading map of admired writers from James Merrill to Toni Morrison and Cynthia Ozick. Two tensions are worth carrying forward. Young's claim to be traditional stands directly against the experimental and feminist readings of Fuchs and Friedman, a disagreement best presented as a live question rather than resolved. And her late recognition of a "rage against women" complicates the "neither man nor woman" stance she takes elsewhere. It also adds one more origin poem, written at five and about loss, to the several ages she gives across the interviews for her first, theme-setting poem, a small but telling instance of the pattern this whole set has traced, of a writer who told the story of her own beginnings many ways.
