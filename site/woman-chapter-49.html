Chapter 49

On Chapter 49 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Woman in Young's Magnum Opus.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Ice encases the world in this chapter, and the frozen field is where its erasures are kept. Mr. Spitzer is disoriented in a watery grave, walking "on frozen waters without wetting his feet," a "starfish on his sleeve" and barnacles on his cheek, and the recurring star is "honed away" over "many hidden depths" and "many whirlpools." This is the submerged history of women held under the ice and the dissolving boundary between life and death, a man neither drowned nor dry crossing a surface with everything lost beneath it. The "beautiful ladies" frozen into "ancient patriarchs" return, the crossing point set in ice again, and the drowned cities are the erased that the book keeps rather than releases.

Martha the passenger pigeon carries the chapter's grief, and she carries it as a woman erased. The last of her kind, she died "without confessing her pigeon peccadilloes," relegated to the baggage car, mourned only in the "whispered murmurs" of an old black porter, and the caretaker dismisses her soul as "not so much as a bubble of light," insisting "two passenger pigeons would be two more than there were." This is female extinction and reproductive futility and the intersecting erasures of race, and it is the book's practice with the vanished: Mr. Spitzer becomes the "old pigeon lobbyist," the advocate for "lost causes," the one who keeps the extinct present the way he keeps every threshold open. The impossibility of reviving the species "even upon some other star" holds the loss where the book will not let it close.

Mr. Spitzer's own dissolution runs alongside Martha's. He is the "pleader of lost causes," the "minority representing a majority," the "barcarolle heard not by the singer," his tears enclosing "suns, moons, flowers, butterflies," and "none mourned for him." The self with no single account of itself and the music that goes unheard arrive together, a man who winds himself "like an old clock" and cannot say whether he is a "rain drop falling from the wing of the great archangel Uriel" or a tear. His body is an "old, many-chimneyed tenement," his rounds a "watchman" and a "beagle" knocking on "frozen doorbells," and the knocking that no one answers is invisibility worked into a nightly ritual.

The chapter's deepest trust is given to those who have lost a sense. Mr. Spitzer relies on the deaf musician and the blind lighthouse keeper, on people "who had forgotten their origins," and the severed sense that the book uses for erasure becomes, here, an alternative way of knowing, sight and hearing set aside as the only paths to truth. Time thins to "signs of this moment only," the past and future fluid, and the self multiplies into a "living lighthouse" and a "weaver" caught in the storm, images doubling until self cannot meet self. The chapter closes on crowds and alienation, voices "like all dead souls returning," "pigeon murmurs" and "heart murmurs" running together, and music "coming when his heart should beat no more," the unheard song sounding past the death of the one who makes it, and the truth left unknowable and accepted as such.
