Chapter One. Not One Self: I Will Kill You If You Tell

← Prologue: The Instruction · Be Bak in a Whale · Chapter Two. Reality Is Constructed: Count the Objects →

Pillar: Pillar 1 Not One Self · Across every book

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

I knew Miss MacIntosh before I met her.

Not Young's Miss MacIntosh specifically. The type. The woman who holds everything together by insisting that everything has a place and everything stays in its place and the rules are the rules and good work is its own reward and dignity is non-negotiable and if you follow the structure the structure will hold. I grew up with that woman. I saw her with my grandmother. I absorbed her as the model of how a life could be organized against chaos, how a self could be made coherent against the pressure of a world that wanted to pull it apart.

I liked Miss MacIntosh in the beginning of the novel. I want to say that plainly because it matters for everything that follows. She was not a figure I read with suspicion or distance. She was familiar. She was the social contract as I had received it: be a good girl, do good work, be respectful, act with dignity, and these things will be recognized and rewarded. The world will hold if you hold. The glue is you.

I also knew Vera. The rebellious self, the one who looks at the organized world and feels the wrongness of it before she can name what is wrong. My teaching life gave me Vera: the student who won't fit the framework, who keeps exceeding or escaping the categories, who is too much and not enough simultaneously and who knows it and can't do anything about it yet. I had been both of those students. I had taught both of those students. And here they were in Young's novel, organized around each other, each one incomplete without the other, each one carrying something the other couldn't hold.

What I didn't understand in the first reading, what I was drowning in without knowing it, was that Young was not writing about two kinds of people. She was writing about the two selves that live inside one person and what happens when the dominant one is forced to reveal itself.

⁂

The first pillar of Young's philosophy is this: the self is plural, not unified, and the plurality is not a problem to be solved but the actual condition of being alive.

This sounds, stated plainly, like something you might encounter in an introductory philosophy course and file away without much trouble. The self is not unified. Fine. Noted. Moving on.

Young does not let you file it away. She builds a novel in which you cannot escape the demonstration. Not the argument. The demonstration. There is a difference that the tradition of philosophy, which advances through argument, has never fully reckoned with. You can follow an argument about the plural self and understand it intellectually and return to your life unchanged. You cannot read Miss MacIntosh, My Darling with full attention and return to your life unchanged, because Young does not argue the plural self into existence. She puts you inside it for 1,198 pages until you have no choice but to recognize it as your own condition.

The vehicle for this demonstration is not Vera, though Vera is the narrator. It is Miss MacIntosh. And the fulcrum is a single paragraph.

⁂

Vera has seen Miss MacIntosh without her wig.

The woman who represents order and reality and the solid world, the woman whose whole function in the novel has been to stand against Catherine Cartwheel's opium dreams as the proof that something is real and can be depended on, has a wig. And false teeth. And one breast. The solid woman is a construction. The empiricist is a performance. The Miss MacIntosh who insisted on reality was herself the most elaborately assembled illusion in the novel.

And then she says it.

I will kill you if you tell anyone.

I have read that sentence across five readings and it has not diminished. If anything it has deepened, the way a wound deepens when you finally understand what made it.

Young does not write that Miss MacIntosh felt ashamed. She does not write that Miss MacIntosh begged Vera to be discreet. She does not soften it or contextualize it or offer the reader a frame that would make Miss MacIntosh sympathetic in her vulnerability. She writes the threat at full force. The woman who represents the organized world, the rules, the dignity, the social contract, responds to exposure with the most primal available suppression. Not, please, not, I need you to understand, not, I am afraid.

I will kill you if you tell anyone.

That is the sound the dominant self makes when the subordinate self becomes visible. Young writes it without flinching because flinching would be dishonest. The threat is real. It has always been real. The nice version, the version the social contract offers, is: follow the rules and everything will be fine. But underneath that version, holding it in place, is exactly what Miss MacIntosh says to Vera. The wig stays on. Exposure is not permitted. The singular acceptable self must be maintained.

Or else.

⁂

The western philosophical tradition has spent centuries defending the unified self. Descartes found it in the thinking thing that could not be doubted. Kant built the transcendental subject, the organizing I that makes experience possible. Locke grounded it in memory as you are the same person across time because you remember being that person. The assumption underneath all of it is that there is a you, a stable center, and that the stability is what makes you responsible, legible, real.

Young does not argue against this assumption. She does something more unsettling: she builds a world in which you watch it fail, and the failure is not a tragedy but a revelation.

Miss MacIntosh's wig does not reveal that she was always a fraud. It reveals that the self she was performing was genuinely hers, as genuinely hers as the bald truth underneath it, and that both were real, and that the opposition between them, the real Miss MacIntosh versus the performed Miss MacIntosh, is exactly the false framework the novel is built to dismantle.

There is no real Miss MacIntosh underneath the wig. There is another Miss MacIntosh, equally constructed, equally real, equally a self that has organized its experience into something livable. The wig is not a lie and the baldness is not the truth. They are two selves, plural, simultaneous, and the threat Miss MacIntosh makes to Vera is the sound of one self trying to annihilate the other's visibility.

This is what Young is showing. Not that organized people are secretly chaotic, not that the rules are always wrong, not that the solid world is simply an illusion to be rejected. She is showing that the demand for a single coherent self is itself the violence. The wig is not the problem. The threat is the problem. The I will kill you is the problem. The system that requires one self to murder the other to maintain its dominance is the problem.

And Young builds this not in an essay, not in an argument, but in a paragraph so precise and so unsparing that sixty years later it still lands like a hand on your throat.

⁂

I held onto my Miss MacIntosh for a long time. Longer than I would have predicted, longer than I understood at the time, and the holding was not a weakness. It was the reasonable response of a person who had been told that the contract was real and had organized a life around honoring it.

The wig came off in June 2022 when Roe v. Wade was overturned.

Not metaphorically. Structurally. The belief that being a good girl, being respectful, acting with dignity, doing the work properly and honoring the rules, that these things would be recognized and would accumulate into something that held, collapsed in a way I had not been prepared for even after everything I had already understood about the world. I had maintained, underneath all the political understanding, all the feminist scholarship, all the years of reading and thinking and knowing better, a Miss MacIntosh. A belief that the contract was real. That if we followed it faithfully enough it would protect us.

It walked into the water and did not come back.

And I was already deep in Young's novel when it happened. Which means the framework was arriving at exactly the moment the personal wig came off. The novel and the world were doing the same thing at the same time, and I was standing at the intersection of both of them with Young's language beginning to form in me and the country announcing clearly that the solid woman had always been a construction and the contract had always been conditional on silence.

I will kill you if you tell anyone.

That sentence, which Young wrote in 1965, was the sentence the Supreme Court delivered in 2022 in different language but with the same essential content. The dominant self of the American social contract informing the subordinate selves that their visibility was not permitted. That the wig would stay on. That exposure would be met with annihilation.

Young had seen this. She had been seeing it since 1965, since before 1965, since the first poem she wrote at age nine about loss. She built the novel that contained the analysis before the analysis had a name, and she built it in fiction because fiction could do what the philosophical treatise could not: it could make the reader live inside the structure long enough to recognize it as their own.

⁂

The plural self, as Young builds it, is not a diagnosis. It is not the description of a pathology that certain troubled people suffer from while the rest of us maintain our coherent singular identities. It is the description of the actual condition, universal, inescapable, and denied at great cost.

Catherine Cartwheel's opium world is the clearest demonstration of this in the novel, because Catherine is the character the critical tradition most wants to read as the pathological pole, of the deluded one, the absent mother, the cautionary example of what happens when you abandon consensus reality for interior fantasy. Young refuses that reading at every turn by giving Catherine's world the same novelistic density, the same accumulated specific detail, the same insistence on its reality as anything else in the book.

Catherine's world is not thin. It is not vague or decorative. It has geography and population and internal logic. It has weight. And Young gives it that weight deliberately, because the philosophical point is not that Catherine is wrong and Miss MacIntosh is right. The philosophical point is that the framework that would let us adjudicate between them, the framework that sorts experience into real and delusional, sane and mad, grounded and unmoored, is itself the construction the novel is taking apart.

Miss MacIntosh made that framework. She insisted on it. She was the empiricist in the house of illusion, the woman who drew the line between real and unreal and stood on the real side and dared the unreal to cross. And then her wig came off. And the line dissolved. Not because Catherine was right, but because the line was always a wig too: a construction organized to protect the self that built it.

What Young is showing across the full impossible length of the novel is that every self does this. Every self organizes reality around a set of commitments and calls those commitments the truth. The plural self is not the self that has failed to organize itself. It is the honest account of what organizing actually is: one self among several, maintaining dominance through the same mechanism Miss MacIntosh uses with Vera.

The threat.

⁂

I said the scales are still falling. I am still walking through the cobwebs of awareness, even after all the work with the book. I want to stay with that for a moment because it is not a confession of incompleteness. It is the first pillar working as Young intended.

The plural self does not reveal itself all at once. It reveals itself recursively, each pass through the material finding a wig that the previous pass left in place, each returning to the beginning discovering that the beginning contains more than it appeared to the first time through. Young builds this into the structure of the novel itself: the images return, the grief accumulates rather than resolving, the journey circles through itself. The form is the argument. The experience of reading is the demonstration.

After the first reading I had the drowning and the last line and the knowledge that I would come back. After the second reading I had more of the characters and the beginning of the philosophy. After the third reading I had the wig sentence and what it meant about the social contract. After the fourth I had Jonah's whale and what it meant about Esther. After the fifth I had the system, the five pillars, the understanding that Young had built something complete and internally consistent and that the completeness was not the point but the building was.

And the scales are still falling. There are wigs under the wigs. The plural self keeps revealing new selves that the previous revelation left covered. This is not a failure of understanding. It is understanding working correctly, the way Young designed it to work, the way the honest life works when you stop demanding that it resolve into something simpler than it actually is.

The plural self is not a destination. It is the condition. The honest life is not the one that has finally achieved clarity about all its selves. It is the one that has stopped threatening the subordinate ones into silence.

⁂

There is a crab in the novel. Miss MacIntosh's wig ends up on the back of a crab, and the image is so specific and so strange and so exactly right that it stays in the mind long after the page is turned.

The crab wears the wig and goes about its crab business. It does not know what it is wearing. It does not know what the wig meant, what it cost, what it protected, what it suppressed. It carries the construction of Miss MacIntosh's singular acceptable self on its back as it moves through the water, and the water does not care, and the wig does not care, and Miss MacIntosh has walked into the water and the only thing remaining is the crab, the wig, the evidence of a self that was always more than one thing.

Young gives us that image and does not explain it. She does not need to explain it. The image is the argument, complete, self-contained, more honest than any prose exposition could be. The wig on the crab is what remains when the threat fails. When the killing doesn't happen. When Vera tells anyway, not in words but in the act of witnessing that the novel itself performs across 1,198 pages.

Vera told. Young told. The novel is the telling.

And what it tells is this: you are not one thing. You have never been one thing. The self that was organized around your Miss MacIntosh and the self that was Vera at fourteen choosing the grand adventure and the self that followed the rules faithfully and the self that felt the wrongness of the rules and the self that is still walking through the cobwebs of awareness years into the work: all of those are you. Simultaneously. None of them are more real than the others. None of them are entitled to kill the others into silence.

That is the first pillar. That is where Young begins.

Everything else the system builds is built on this: the self is plural, the plurality is the condition, and the honest life is the one that stops trying to wig it back together.

The crab keeps moving.

The water doesn’t care.

The wig is still there.

I want to stay with that image a moment longer, because Young is doing something in it that goes beyond the personal and the philosophical into the elemental. The wig is not buried. It is not destroyed or mourned or resolved. It is in the water, on the back of a creature that has been in the water since before Miss MacIntosh existed and will be in the water long after Vera and the novel and Young herself and I are gone. The crab does not know what it is carrying. It does not grieve the wig or understand its history or feel the weight of what it cost. It simply carries it, the way the Earth carries everything it has ever been given: without ceremony, without conclusion, without the demand that the carried thing be processed into something more manageable than what it actually is.

This is the image Young chooses for the moment when the dominant self’s construction enters the world’s keeping. Not a grave. Not a resolution. A crab in the water, moving wherever the water takes it, carrying what it was given without knowing it was given anything.

The natural world runs through this novel the way grief runs through it: constantly, underneath everything, never resolved. The water, the land, the weather, the creatures that move through all three without consulting the human arrangements built on top of them. Young returns to these elements again and again, and what they share is this: they carry without grieving. They hold without concluding. They are the only scale large enough for what individual human selves cannot hold alone.

The Earth was here before Miss MacIntosh’s wig and it will be here after. It has been carrying losses since before any human philosophical tradition existed to name what carrying means. The crab is Young’s image for that carrying: small, specific, unaware of its own significance, moving through the water with the full weight of what it was given on its back.

We will return to this. The Earth as the only archive large enough for permanent grief is the thread that runs from this image through the rest of the book. For now it is enough to see it here, in the first pillar, where Young plants it quietly at the water’s edge: the crab, the wig, the water that doesn’t care, which is another way of saying the water that holds everything without demanding that anything be resolved.
