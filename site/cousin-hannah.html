Chapter 30

Listed as: Cousin Hannah Freemount-Snowden

  • In stark contrast to Catherine, she is a robust, adventurous, and mercurial woman.
  • Known for her vast, almost mythical experiences across the globe, including deserts, mountains, and ancient cities.
  • Has a reputation for prescience, having foreseen many historical assassinations and events.
  • Skilled in various martial and survival arts: archery in Persia, dueling in France, mountain climbing, horseback riding, balloon ascensions.
  • Has traveled extensively in disguise, infiltrated hostile territories, and defied dangers.
  • Represents a force of action, clarity, and determination, dedicated to overthrowing dreams and illusions.
  • Symbolizes a bridge between myth and history, reality and fantasy, embodying strength and resilience.
  • She rode in Arab disguise against Spanish soldiers to defend a caravan of Riffs, for “her proud heart could not endure injustice of any kind in this world.”
  • She is buried in a New England churchyard, “her jeweled sword at her side,” in “her old grey army coat” worn in “wars for the emancipation of woman.”
  • Young built her from two true stories: Charles Studin’s suffragist “battle-axe,” “one of the original bloomer girls,” whose sealed trunks held “a complete bridal trousseau” each, and two bloomer girls on an Alpine summit, the younger lost going back for her skirt (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965).
  • She “had never cared for the Spitzer boys,” predicted they “would come to no good end,” and called both brothers “profligates.”
  • Catherine sees her as “an emancipated extension of herself,” who might “rescue my mother from the opium dreams of her dead loves.”
  • “Because of the loss of a skirt”: Young told Studs Terkel that this came from an Alpine story in which two bloomer girls shed their skirts at a summit, and the younger, going back for hers, was lost: “the lady lost her life for a skirt” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). Chapter 31

  • A formidable, adventurous woman, a “great captain” with a suffrage warrior’s spirit.
  • Known for her directness, fiery temper, and refusal to accept illusions or evasion.
  • Lived a nomadic life filled with symbolic and literal journeys across deserts, mountains, and forbidden cities.
  • She opposed Catherine’s love for the shadow, believing in facing harsh realities.
  • After death, she remains a spectral presence, her memory both revered and shrouded in secrecy.
  • Despite her public life and campaigns, she kept a secret of sorrow, which others hesitate to speak of.
  • Cousin Hannah disapproves of Catherine’s dreams and love for the “shadow.” Their conflict is ideological: directness and confrontation versus evasion and mystical longing.
  • She is portrayed as a mythic figure battling external foes and internal demons.
  • Her life and death symbolize the struggle between illusion and reality, love and loss.
  • The chapter describes her as a figure who fought battles greater than those recorded, with imagery of deserts, storms, and crusades.
  • She sent not one flower to Peron’s grave, neither a horseshoe of roses nor “two dice cubes in a lucky throw,” and ignored Joachim “as if it were he who had died.”
  • She called both brothers “equally insecure, as unsubstantial as flame and water.”
  • “Here lies one who searched all her life for her dead love, a body covered by the snow”: Young told Studs Terkel that Cousin Hannah came partly from an Alpine story in which two bloomer girls shed their skirts at a summit, and the younger, going back for hers, was lost: “the lady lost her life for a skirt.” She also drew on Margaret Fuller, who drowned off Fire Island in 1850 within reach of a sandbar (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See the Chapter 30 resources.
  • “Death snatched away her beautiful bride”: Young’s other source for Cousin Hannah was a story told by Charles Studin, a founder of the NAACP, about a suffragist “battle-axe” who fought men all her life, “one of the original bloomer girls,” yet for forty years brought home sealed trunks that proved at her death to hold “a complete bridal trousseau” each. “She had a dream too” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). In another telling the lawyer found “fifty wedding gowns,” and Young added that she “had read the histories of mountain climbers, of suffragette captains, of travelers to the Middle East” (Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs, “A Conversation with Marguerite Young,” Review of Contemporary Fiction 23, no. 1, Spring 2003 (1989 original)).

Chapter 32

  • Cousin Hannah is portrayed as a powerful, uncompromising woman who never concedes or softens her standards, even for strategic reasons. She advances by open hostility and brandishes her symbolic sword like a cane, even when there is no opponent.
  • She is deeply committed to the cause of women’s suffrage and liberation from male oppression. Cousin Hannah fights against man as the cruelest oppressor of beauty and womanhood, advocating for women to use their own "weapons" such as parasols and skirts as symbols of resistance.
  • She is described as a swift, meteoric figure, always on the move and engaged in battles and journeys that span deserts, mountains, and foreign lands. She is likened to a great courier of storms and a weathervane signaling change.
  • Despite her vigor, Cousin Hannah is also seen as ancient, worn by time, and somewhat removed from the natural cycles of life and love. She is described as never having fully experienced youth, romance, or the mating seasons of life.
  • She dies not on a distant battlefield but in obscurity after years of semi-invalidism caused by frostbite. Her deathbed confession reveals a shift in belief that the greatest battles are fought domestically, in the struggles between man and woman at home.

Chapter 34

  • A formidable, almost mythical figure symbolizing strength and independence.
  • She challenges Catherine’s illusions and urges her to face reality.
  • Known for her fiery spirit, sharp wit, and relentless energy, she is both a source of inspiration and tension.
  • Her distrust of men and disdain for conventional womanhood provide a counterpoint to Catherine’s experiences.

Chapter 35

  • A formidable, spirited woman and a leader in the suffrage movement.
  • Described as a “great captain,” “old New England suffragette,” “stone horseman,” and a pioneer who had traveled extensively and fearlessly.
  • Known for her strength, courage, and refusal to conform to traditional feminine roles.
  • She is remembered as someone who never sought pity or protection and who had no time for courtly graces or domestic bliss.
  • Her life was devoted to overthrowing man’s dominance, both socially and domestically.
  • She represents emancipation, freedom, and unyielding resolve.
  • Even in death, she remains vivid and influential, symbolized by images of snowy mountains and celestial journeys.
  • Refused memorial tributes like stained glass windows, showing her disdain for conventional commemoration.

Chapter 36

  • A "fearlessly adventuresome" suffrage captain, dynamic and rebellious in life.
  • She embodies personal unity, passion, and the struggle against societal constraints.
  • Post-death, she becomes an ambiguous, almost mythical figure, transcending time and space.
  • Her death and disappearance symbolize the fragmentation and mystery surrounding memory and legacy.

Chapter 37

  • A symbol of strength and defiance in the face of mortality.
  • Her story is a narrative of resistance, a woman who cannot be possessed or ruled.
  • Her death and burial represent the finality Catherine tries to avoid accepting.

Chapter 38

  • Described as a "great captain of extremes," a resolute and brave suffragette who sacrificed much for her cause.
  • Adventurous and daring, having traveled "to the outermost reaches of space," symbolizing exploration beyond physical and societal boundaries.
  • Exhibits masculine virtues: sharp, shrewd, incisive, relentless and compared to a "finely tempered blade."
  • Seen as singularly devoted to her cause, seemingly indifferent to love or personal attachments.
  • Her presence is associated with powerful imagery: whistling sounds, iron keys, jeweled swords, and snow storms.
  • A figure of mystery and paradox; she weeps for subtle reasons (e.g., a snowflake touching her hand), showing a hidden emotional depth.
  • Her life is characterized by perpetual motion and battle, never fully reconciled with peace or rest.
  • Represents strength, fortitude, and the harsh realities of activism and sacrifice.

Chapter 39

Listed as: Cousin Hannah Freemount-Snowden

  • A symbolic visitor, representing a heroic figure from a bygone era.
  • Described in mythic terms, she embodies ideals and struggles that contrast with Catherine’s condition.

Chapter 40

  • A suffragette and member of an old, aristocratic family ("Old Guard").
  • Portrayed as a spectral, dream-like figure, both vibrant and ghostly.
  • Speaks in multiple languages, including dead and mythical tongues, embodying the “languages of the dead” and those never spoken.
  • Eccentric, passionate, and combative, yet enigmatic in her presence and communication.
  • Her voice is described as powerful and haunting, a “Gothic cathedral weight.”
  • Seems to exist partly in the realm of dreams or memory rather than pure reality.

Chapter 41

  • A suffrage captain and historical figure in Catherine’s memories and imagination.
  • Represents a more practical, tragic, or "honest" life, contrasted with Catherine’s dreamlike and evasive existence.

Chapter 42

Listed as: Cousin Hannah (C. H. Freemount-Snowden)

  • A formidable, militant suffrage leader whose life was dedicated to battling the conventions of love, society, and gender roles.
  • She is depicted as a warrior, storm king, and mystic figure, feared and revered but ultimately isolated and sorrowful in her final days.
  • Her identity spans multiple cultural representations (Hannah in Boston, Hamid in the East, and the Great Moor) symbolizing a universal warrior figure.

Chapter 43

  • A once powerful figure, now fragile and approaching death.
  • Engaged in a fierce, chaotic battle against death, manifested in vivid, surreal visions.
  • Experiences wild dreams, hallucinations, and shifting identities in her final hours.
  • Struggles with recognition of self and the meaning of life and death.
  • Exhibits vanity, fear, ecstasy, confusion, and moments of clarity.
  • Her final moments are a “dance of death,” a paradoxical mix of seriousness and frivolity.

Chapter 44

Listed as: Cousin Hannah / The Great Suffrage Captain

  • A once-celebrated suffragette whose public life was marked by courage and activism.
  • In private, she was vulnerable, haunted by lost love, and self-doubt.
  • Her death is poetic and symbolic, involving imagery of skirts, snowdrops, mountains, and clouds.

Chapter 45

  • A heroic figure involved in suffrage, labor strikes, and adventurous exploits.
  • Her private life was a mystery, marked by lost love, guilt, and spiritual wandering.
  • She died with a sea shell at her feet, symbolizing her connection to the sea and the unknown.
  • She is described metaphorically as a "sleeping beauty," a "dead star," and a "great betrayer of woman," reflecting her complex legacy.

Chapter 46

  • A complex figure; a suffragette, a mountain climber, a symbol of liberation and love’s endurance. She is proud yet ashamed of her achievements, embodying contradictions of strength and vulnerability. Her death is portrayed almost mythically, with imagery of frost, mountains, and alpenhorns signaling her passage to another realm.

Chapter 47

Listed as: The Old Suffrage Captain (Cousin Hannah)

  • A symbolic figure representing a lost love, death, and unresolved mystery.
  • Her death and legacy inspire Mr. Spitzer’s reflections on love, mortality, and memory.
  • Her “skirt” symbolizes her love, her wound, her identity veiled and hidden.

Chapter 48

Listed as: Cousin Hannah, The Old Suffragette

  • A figure who carried keys from suffrage journeys, symbolic of struggles for freedom and the unlocking of mysteries.

Chapter 52

Listed as: The Old Suffragette

  • A mysterious woman shrouded in symbolism; she is a traveler, a rebel, and a figure of lost love and elusive identity. Known for her suffrage activism, she is also depicted as a wanderer through physical and metaphysical realms of mountains, deserts, and clouds. She carries the paradox of being both bride and bridegroom, representing the complex intersections of gender, love, and death.
