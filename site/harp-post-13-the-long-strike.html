Chapter 13. The Long Strike

1877, the railroads, the troops, and the young man in the warehouse

In July 1877, after years of wage cuts, railroad workers walked off the job across the country. The strike spread from West Virginia to Pittsburgh, Chicago, and St. Louis. Federal troops were sent in, and dozens of people were killed. It was the first great national strike in American history. The chapters from about page 475 to 545 tell its story and the story of the labor struggles leading up to it.

The uncivil civil war

Young sets 1877 inside a longer pattern. She begins with the eight-hour strikes in New York in 1872 and the police attack on unemployed workers in Tompkins Square in 1874, where a young cigar maker named Samuel Gompers was in the crowd (480). She had called the 1877 strike, back in the Gatling chapter, part of an "uncivil civil war" (197), in which federal troops were used to put down "what was called an insurrection inspired by Karl Marx, a man of whom few had heard" (197).

The phrase links 1877 back to the Civil War of Debs's childhood. For Young, the war between North and South had ended and the war between rich and poor had simply taken its place. That was exactly what Daniel Debs had predicted.

Terre Haute's strike

Young documents the strike on June 1877, the firemen of Vigo Lodge on the Vandalia line had suffered wage cuts of "twenty-three percent between 1873 and 1876 and ten percent between August 1876 and May 1877" (539). They asked the line's president, Riley McKeen, for a fifteen percent raise, gave him one night to answer, and prepared to stop the trains (539–540).

When the national strike came, President Hayes sent federal troops, including to Indianapolis and Terre Haute. In Indianapolis, Benjamin Harrison, then a Republican lawyer and former general, helped organize a citizens' committee against the strikers. Debs would later recall this as "the first in which the powers of the federal courts had been evoked to break a railroad strike" (538). Afterward, he said, "Upon the railroads the mere suspicion of belonging to a union was sufficient grounds for instant discharge" (538).

The watcher

Young calls the strike's long history something "of which Debs was the magnetized witness, the watcher on many fronts" (506). She places him during the summer of 1877 still "confined in the warehouse" (542) as Hulman's billing clerk, while the brotherhood's magazine was edited by others. In 1878, he became its associate editor (545).

That placement is significant, and it raises the most important historical question in these chapters. It is taken up below.

Utopia for the munitions makers

Near the end of this stretch, Young turns the book's central word inside out. War, she writes, "would be a state of perpetual utopia for munitions makers and military men" (544). The perfect society exists after all. It belongs to the people who sell the guns.

It is the sharpest single sentence in the book about The Sacred Is Here and its enemy. Every dream of a distant perfect world can be claimed by someone. Young's point is that the utopia of the arms dealer is already here, paid for by the people who die in it.

What the lens shows

These chapters bring together everything the book has built. The layers of The Past Doesn't Go Away are the Mollies, the Pinkertons, the Gatling guns, and the Tompkins Square clubs. Reality Is Constructed appears as the courts, which here begin to replace the army as labor's great opponent. The Crossing Point is Debs's own recollection that 1877 was the first time the federal courts were used to break a strike. In 1894, an injunction from those same courts would destroy his American Railway Union and send him to jail.

Checking the record

  • Debs's own position in 1877. This is the largest gap in Young's account. By the standard record, Debs did not support the 1877 strike. At the Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen's convention in Indianapolis that September, he defended the union against charges that it had backed the walkout, and the brotherhood's magazine condemned the strike. Young's placement of him in the warehouse, as a watcher rather than a participant, softens this. It is the clearest example in the book of a biographer protecting her subject. It is also, arguably, a place where Young's own principle of honest incompletion would have been better served by the harder truth: that the future leader of the Pullman strike began as a cautious union man who opposed a strike.

  • The 1876 election. Young writes that the Republicans "had stolen the office" from Samuel Tilden (539). The disputed election was settled by a special commission and a political bargain. "Stolen" states a view many historians share, but not all.

  • Troops at Terre Haute. Accurate. Federal troops were sent to Indianapolis and Terre Haute in July 1877.
