Chapter 57

← Chapter 56 · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 58 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: Not One Self · Unfinished Is Honest

The Questions

Mr. Spitzer is “many selves who had been none.” How can a multitude add up to no one?

He is like “a mystery which is completed and laid away… an empty shell.” Is the finished, solved self a fullness or a vacancy?

A tailor duns him for a suit already paid, a tailor who “perhaps… had never existed.” What is a debt owed to a phantom?

The paid bill keeps arriving. What account can never be closed?

The chapter states Not One Self in its most vertiginous form: Mr. Spitzer is “many selves who had been none.” The multitude does not add up to a rich fullness here but to a vacancy, a crowd that is also an absence, the many somehow summing to no one. This is the plurality pushed past celebration and past mere fraying into paradox: to be many can be to be none, the selves so numerous and so unintegrated that there is no one there at all, only the crowd and, at its center, nobody. The book has held the plural self as the ordinary truth; here it looks straight at the vertigo in that truth, the possibility that a self of many is a self of none.

And the chapter attaches the vacancy, tellingly, to the finished. Mr. Spitzer is “self-contained like a mystery which is completed and laid away, a psychic armor no one now investigates, an empty shell.” The completed mystery is the empty one. Once a thing is solved, sealed, laid away, no longer investigated, it is armor with no one inside, a shell. This is the book’s dread of the finished stated at the level of a person: to be a completed self, a solved and closed identity, would be to be an empty shell, and Mr. Spitzer’s refusal to cohere, his staying many and unsolved, is what keeps him from being that vacant finished thing. The empty shell is what a sealed self looks like.

The tailor’s bill is the chapter’s comic and exact image of the unclosable. A tailor keeps dunning Mr. Spitzer for a suit “he was quite certain he had already paid for,” a suit for the dead brother, and the tailor himself “perhaps had never existed.” So a phantom bills him for a settled debt, and the paid account will not stay paid. This is Unfinished Is Honest turned into farce: some accounts cannot be closed, some debts keep arriving after settlement, some matters refuse the final receipt. Mr. Spitzer has “no receipts, no cancelled checks,” nothing to prove the thing finished, because the thing cannot be finished, and the phantom tailor will go on dunning a debt that was paid and may never have been owed.

The three hold together. A self of many that is also none, a finished mystery that is an empty shell, and a paid debt that will not close are one recognition in three keys: that completion is vacancy and incompletion is life. What is solved is laid away empty; what stays open, the unclosable account, the uncohered self, the debt still arriving, is where the person actually is. Mr. Spitzer is kept from the empty shell precisely by his failure to be finished, and the tailor’s endless bill, absurd as it is, is the sound of a life refusing to be settled and filed.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes “many selves who had been none” for simple emptiness, a man reduced to nothing, and the reading that takes the phantom tailor’s bill for mere whimsy. Neither the reduction to nothing nor the whimsy is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that the plural self can sum to none, a crowd that is also a vacancy, and that this vertigo is the truth looked at squarely rather than a defect; that the finished, solved, laid-away self is the empty shell, so completion is vacancy and Mr. Spitzer’s refusal to cohere is what keeps him from being hollow; and that some accounts cannot be closed, the paid debt still arriving, the receipt never final, because incompletion is life. The solved self is empty and the open one is alive. The phantom tailor duns a settled debt forever, and the chapter lets the account stay open.

For Reflection

Can a self of many turn out to be a self of none, and is that emptiness or honesty?

Is the completed, solved, filed-away self a fullness, or an empty shell with no one inside?

What paid debt or settled matter in one’s life refuses, still, to close?
