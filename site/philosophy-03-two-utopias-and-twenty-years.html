Chapter 3. Two Utopias and Twenty Years

Part of The Philosophy in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).

Definition from research

Marguerite Young was born in Indianapolis in 1908 and died there in 1995. Between those dates she published two books of poetry, a work of history, a single enormous novel, and a collection of essays, and she left behind an unfinished biography of the labor leader Eugene V. Debs. On the surface this looks like a scattered career. Seen from the angle of this book, it looks like one long inquiry carried out in several forms.

New Harmony

The ground of that inquiry lies in a small town on the Wabash River in southwestern Indiana. New Harmony was the site of two of the most famous utopian experiments in American history. In 1814 the followers of George Rapp, a German religious leader, founded a community there to await the Second Coming. They lived celibate, worked in common, and prospered, and a decade later they sold the whole town and left. The buyer was Robert Owen, a Welsh industrialist and reformer, who hoped to build a new moral world on reason, science, and cooperative labor. Owen’s community fell apart within a few years.

Young’s mother and stepfather lived in New Harmony, and Young spent years there working on the book that became Angel in the Forest: A Fairy Tale of Two Utopias, published in 1945. The subtitle is exact. She studied two complete systems for organizing human life, one grounded in faith and one grounded in reason, and she watched both of them fail. Rapp’s faith could not survive the passing years in which the promised end did not come. Owen’s rationalism could not survive the actual people who arrived to live in it, with their quarrels and their laziness and their private dreams.

This matters for the argument of this book because of what Young did next. In 1945, the year Angel in the Forest appeared, she began Miss MacIntosh, My Darling. She expected the novel to take two years. It took nearly twenty. She came to it directly from the study of two collapsed utopias, and the novel can be read as that study turned inward. Where the history had examined whole communities organized around a single idea, the novel examines single lives organized around a single idea. The method is the same. Place the system among real people, let time pass, and record what gives way.

The Twenty Years

The long composition is often told as a story of obsession, and it was that. But it also bears on the argument. A novel whose method is accumulation could not be written quickly. Young worked on it daily from 1945 to 1964, and the book’s size is not an accident of temperament. It is structurally necessary. A system can only be shown failing under accumulation if the accumulation actually happens on the page. A short novel could state that common sense is an illusion. Only a very long one could let common sense live for hundreds of pages, grow familiar and beloved, and then break.

The twenty years also placed the novel in history. Young began it in the last year of the Second World War and finished it in the middle of the civil rights movement. Those decades saw the collapse of several grand systems that had promised to explain and order the world, and the rise of leaders who claimed to have the master key. The novel almost never mentions any of this directly. Its politics work at a deeper level, as Part Four will show.

The Political Conscience

Young was clear that her novel had a politics. In the interview with Friedman and Fuchs, she said: “It isn’t that Miss MacIntosh has a social conscience, but the book does. If you understand hallucination and illusion, you don’t blindly follow any leader.” She tied this directly to Mr. Spitzer and to one of the novel’s strangest minor figures: “Mr. Spitzer’s adventures and the passages on the little frog musician investigate the nature of illusion, and if there is no certain reality, the idea of following a leader must be scrutinized.” And she added a sentence that is close to a statement of method: “Some of the poetic writers who insert passages of realism in their texts have no underlying philosophy to uphold them and revert to realism. I don’t believe there can be a poetic novel without political consciousness.”

The phrase “underlying philosophy” is important. Young believed the poetic novel needed one, and she believed she had one. Her charge against other writers was not that they were too realistic but that they fell back on realism because they had nothing else to stand on. Realism, for Young, was not neutral ground. It was one more system, and a writer who retreated into it had simply chosen a comfortable room in the crowded house.

After the novel, Young turned to Eugene V. Debs, the Indiana-born labor organizer and five-time Socialist candidate for president. The Vietnam War years pushed her attention toward labor history and toward the American traditions of mutual aid and common ownership that Debs represented. She worked on the biography for the rest of her life. It was published after her death, edited by Charles Ruas, as Harp Song for a Radical: The Life and Times of Eugene Victor Debs. Even at more than six hundred pages, it covers mainly his early years and the world that formed him, and it stops before the strikes and campaigns that made him famous.

There is something fitting in that. A writer whose philosophy honored the unfinished left her last great work unfinished. The book about Debs grew the way her novel had grown, by accumulation, by digression, by the refusal to reduce a life to its headline events. It became another demonstration of her method, applied this time to history.

One Inquiry

Read together, the three prose books form a single line. Angel in the Forest tests two complete systems against the life of a community and watches them fail. Miss MacIntosh, My Darling tests many systems against individual lives and builds, from their failures, a way of living without a master key. Harp Song for a Radical turns that way of living outward toward politics, toward a leader who, unlike the blind leaders Young warned against, belonged to the people he led. The novel stands at the center of this line, and it is to the novel’s trials that Part Two now turns.

Each chapter in this part follows the same shape. It asks what a lived system promises, how the novel loads that system with experience, where the system breaks, and what is left when it does. The order matters. The trials begin with the most openly illusory life in the book and end with the life built to refute illusion, because only when realism falls is the demonstration complete.
