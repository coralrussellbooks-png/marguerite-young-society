Chapter 67

On Chapter 67 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Pillars: The Margins Reveal the Truth · Language Shapes What's Possible

The Questions

The bus-driver curses “old Doc, a grizzled, unseen man,” who cannot possibly hear him. What is speech addressed to the absent?

“Probably half the conversation in America occurs in this manner.” What does that say about how much of our talk reaches anyone?

He curses “unmaliciously, as if he cursed a friend.” What passes between a man and the phantom he addresses?

Why does the book give this observation to a cursing driver at the dead end of a journey?

The chapter opens on language spoken to someone who cannot hear it. The bus-driver curses “all the way through the sleeping Indiana country,” and he curses not his passengers but “old Doc, a grizzled, unseen man,” an absent figure who cannot possibly receive a word of it. This is Language Shapes What's Possible shown at its most exposed: speech that builds an address, a quarrel, a relationship, toward a listener who is not there. The words do not carry a message from one present person to another; they construct an interlocutor out of absence, a conversation with a phantom, and the driver conducts it for miles.

The chapter widens the observation into a claim about how people talk. “Drivers, of course, often address other drivers who cannot possibly hear a word they say. Probably half the conversation in America occurs in this manner.” A man will “argue for hours with a man who made the wrong turn, miles and miles back.” Half of all our talk, the chapter suggests, is addressed to the absent, spoken past the empty seat to someone who left or was never there. Language, which is supposed to pass between present speakers, turns out to spend much of itself building phantom listeners and quarreling with them, a construction running mostly on absence.

And something real passes to the phantom anyway. The driver curses “unmaliciously, as if he cursed a friend,” so that the address to the absent old Doc is not empty rage but something almost companionable, a relationship conducted with a man who is not there. This is the strange gift inside the construction: the phantom interlocutor, built entirely of words and absence, becomes a kind of company, the cursed friend a presence made by the cursing. The book has shown the dead kept alive by being addressed, the missing built up by the words aimed at them; here a living driver keeps an absent man company by quarreling with him down a dark road.

The book gives this to a cursing driver at a journey’s dead end for its usual reason. The insight that half our speech is addressed to the absent, that language builds its listeners as often as it reaches them, comes not from a philosopher but from The Margins Reveal the Truth, a long-haired driver at the ragged end of a bus route, wedged between a milk truck and a hearse. The margin sees the construction plainly: from the driver’s seat, talking for hours to old Doc, the built and phantom quality of ordinary conversation is simply obvious, where the settled speaker, sure of his listener, never notices how much of talk is aimed at no one there.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes the driver’s cursing for mere local color, a bit of road atmosphere, and the reading that takes speech to the absent for simple futility, words wasted on no one. Neither the atmosphere nor the futility is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that language often addresses the absent, building an interlocutor and quarrel out of a listener who is not there; that much of ordinary talk runs this way, aimed past the empty seat, so speech constructs its listeners as often as it reaches them; and that something real still passes to the phantom, the cursed absent friend becoming a kind of company made by the words. Language builds its listeners, and the built listener keeps one company. The observation comes from the driver’s seat at the dead end of the route, where the constructed quality of ordinary talk is plain, and the chapter leaves the driver quarreling companionably with a man who is not there.

For Reflection

How much of one’s own talk is addressed to people who cannot, or will not, hear it?

What passes between a person and the absent one they keep addressing?

Does language reach its listeners as often as it builds them?
