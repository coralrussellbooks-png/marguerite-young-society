I. The Philosophy: Your Color Has a History and It Did Not Go Away

It is comforting to treat a fresh tube of paint or a new canvas as a neutral beginning. You buy it, you open it, you start. Whatever happened before it reached your hands feels like someone else's story, finished and behind us. Radical Immanence says time does not work that way. The past does not recede behind you as you move forward. It accumulates, layer by layer, pressing down on the present. Historical injustice is not a past event. It is a geological fact about the ground you are standing on right now. You cannot build fairly on ground you are pretending is neutral.

The color in your hand is not a blank starting point. It is the top layer of a deep stack. Ultramarine carries the long trade in lapis dug from a few mountains and moved across the world at the price of a life's wages. Carmine and cochineal red carry the crushed insects and the colonial plantations built to farm them. Indigo carries the blue that enslaved and indentured people were worked to death to produce. Lead white carries the poisonings, and cadmium and cobalt carry the mines still open today. Your canvas carries cotton, and cotton carries the plantation. None of this is a footnote to the material. It is the material's actual composition, as real as the pigment index number on the tube.

This is not an argument for guilt or for refusing to make anything. It is an argument for honesty. When you act as if your color arrived from nowhere, you are building on ground you have agreed to pretend is neutral, and that pretense is exactly how the old harm stays buried and keeps working. To excavate the substrate is to ask where this came from and let the answer change how you handle it. The honest maker does not get to start clean. The honest maker starts by knowing what is underfoot.

II. Deep Journal Prompts

The Origin You Don't Ask About. Pick the color you reach for most often without thinking. Write down everything you actually know about where it came from: the pigment, the mineral or the insect or the lab, the land, the people who mined or farmed or made it. Then write down how much of that is a real answer and how much is a brand story printed on the tube to keep you from asking. Sit with the gap. What does it cost the work to not know?

The Cheap Color. Write about a material you buy because it is cheap: a student-grade paint, a bulk canvas, a bargain medium. Trace, as honestly as you can, why it is cheap. Cheapness is never free; someone or somewhere absorbed the cost. Follow the price down through the layers until you reach the ground it was paid from. Then decide, plainly, what you want to do with that knowledge.

III. Studio Exercises

The Material Core Sample

Choose one color you are about to work with and trace it back as far as you genuinely can before you lay down a single mark. Read the tube, then go past the tube. Find the pigment, the source, the country, the maker, the history of the color. Where the trail goes cold, mark exactly where your knowledge ends, because that gap is part of the truth too. Build a small painting that records the journey as layers, the oldest origin at the bottom, your own hand at the top, with a marked band for everything you could not find out. The piece is a core sample of the color's history, including the parts that were hidden from you.

The Honestly Sourced Picture

Make one painting using only materials whose history you can actually name out loud: an earth pigment you dug or can trace, a support you know the making of, charcoal you burned yourself, ink you can source. The constraint will be hard and will make the picture smaller and plainer than you wanted. That smallness is the lesson. Notice how much of your usual practice depends on not knowing, and let this one honest painting show you what working on known ground feels like.

IV. Cross-Medium Interference

The Layered Label

Write the supply chain of one finished painting as a timeline that reads from the deep past up to your hands, every stage you know and every stage you do not. Print or write it as a label and attach it to the back of the work so it travels with the piece. Most labels tell a clean story meant to sell. This one tells the geology. When someone reads it, they meet the picture standing on its actual ground, not the ground the market wanted them to imagine.
