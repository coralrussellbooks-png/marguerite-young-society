On Boethius and the Consolation of Philosophy.

Boethius wrote his most famous work in prison, awaiting execution on charges of conspiracy against the Ostrogothic king Theodoric. He had been a senator, a consul, a philosopher who had devoted himself to translating the Greek classics into Latin. In the cell, he wrote the Consolation of Philosophy, in which the personified figure of Philosophy visits him and argues that Fortune's wheel is always turning, that true goods are internal rather than external, that the wise person should not grieve over what Fortune has taken since Fortune gives and takes by nature. Young's Mr. Spitzer is the Boethius figure: the man of order and law who finds himself in a cell (the borderland, the liminal house) writing his own consolation, which is “that there was no threshold between world and world.”
