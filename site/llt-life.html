Life

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Madge Capehorn · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Pillars: The Past Doesn't Go Away · The Sacred Is Here

The word “life” appears 1,791 times in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, more often than any other word Young weighs. Its meaning is not scattered across borrowed traditions. It is answered by two terms of her own system: The Sacred Is Here and The Past Doesn't Go Away.

The Sacred Is Here holds that meaning is here, in the actual world underfoot, and not in any promised beyond. Life in the novel is the plain thing, and the ordinary thing is enough. Miss MacIntosh is its clearest carrier: “hale, hearty, sensible, dreaming no dreams like the others,” racing along the shore, shelling bean pods, gathering sea shells. These are not lesser activities set beneath some higher vision. They are where the sacred actually lives. She urges Vera to “lead a useful life and see America first, the broad interior, the spacious Middle West, that life which required no medium of the evil imagination to stand between one’s self and the clear reality of simple things.” The clear reality of simple things.

Against this stands Catherine’s “opium paradise,” and the contrast is exact. Catherine has traded the world underfoot for a promised elsewhere, a beyond she has built and then sealed shut. She “retired from the brutal world” and “slept for tideless years.” Vera’s quest is the effort to leave that sealed invention behind, to escape her mother’s “darkened and secluded house that I might find the life which needed no dream of death, that life Miss MacIntosh had spoken of in no uncertain terms.” The life that needs no dream of death is life kept in the ordinary, unransomed to any vision of a better place.

Madge, on the bus, marks the other way the ordinary can be refused. She resists her own “situation of growth, there being nothing languorous in her appearance, nothing which should give itself up to nature.” Her face is an “artificial complexion,” her eyes “not filled with the light which comes from love.” Madge chooses artifice over the fact of her body and its change, and in doing so turns from life as Young means it. To reach for artifice is to reach for a beyond of one’s own making. To stay in the growing, changing, ordinary body is to stay in life.

The Past Doesn't Go Away is the second term, and it explains why life in this novel never simply moves forward. The past is not behind the characters. It is beneath them, pressed into the present, so that living and dying are not two ends of a line but layers of the same ground. The bus driver states it flatly: “you died every minute that you lived, got a new skin every seven years, and you were born every minute that you died.” Birth and death are not sequential here. They are folded into each other. Madge, carrying new life, describes the growth inside her as “this dark valley from which she might never return,” and the image is right, because for Young creation and mortality are pressed into one layer.

This is why the bus journey works as the book’s figure for a life. It does not advance cleanly toward a destination. It loops, stalls, doubles back, and the ground it covers is thick with what came before. Life, read through The Past Doesn't Go Away, is not a road traveled but a sediment accumulated, and the dead keep shaping the living from underneath.

Taken together, the two terms give Young’s answer to the first third of Vera’s question. Life is not a prize reached at the end of the search. It is the ordinary ground underfoot, held honestly, thick with everyone who stood there before. The search for it is never finished, and that is not its failure. That is its nature.
