Chapter 19

On Chapter 19 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of The Wheel and the Spokes.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 18 · Wheel and Spokes Home · Chapter 20 →

Research notes: Chapter 19 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 19

Characters: Miss MacIntosh

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The narrator is not one consciousness. She is simultaneously the child who played dominoes under the storm lamp, the dreamer who cannot distinguish dream from waking, the witness who observes with "crucial clarity," and the retrospective analyst who has "always known" what she claims to be discovering for the first time.

"Was I awake or still asleep, even though I saw with such crucial clarity the cleanly sweeping dawn..."

The self here is layered and irresolvable. She cannot establish a stable ground from which to perceive: every observation is undermined by the question of whether she is dreaming it. But crucially, this is not presented as pathology. The narrator does not rush to resolve the question. She inhabits the multiplicity.

Miss MacIntosh herself enacts a plural self in a more literal, bodily register. She is simultaneously:

  • The rigid, perpendicular woman of common sense
  • The bald, corsetted, wigged, false-toothed figure revealed in sleep
  • The woman who preaches against gambling while keeping a deck of cards within reach
  • The Christian woman who chains a Bible to a table

She is not one person. She is the performance of one person layered over a very different interior. The chapter does not resolve which is "real." Both are.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Miss MacIntosh has been, throughout the narrator's life, the figure of reality itself: the antidote to the mother's opium-dreaming, the horizontal hallucinator. She was the perpendicular one. And yet:

"Her red wig, almost purple as the light shone through its ragged edges, was perched like a rare tropical bird on the hat rack..."

The wig, the false teeth, the corset, the painted-on eyebrows implied by their absence: Miss MacIntosh is revealed as a construction of the real, not its embodiment. The woman who wanted no ornament, no dust-catchers, no floating angels, was herself an elaborate ornament, a carefully maintained fiction of solidity.

This is not a cynical unmasking. The chapter does not say: she was false, therefore nothing is true. It says something more unsettling: the construction of reality requires this kind of labor. The "sunlit world" of Miss MacIntosh's common sense was itself a larger, more carefully maintained cave.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The room is saturated with accumulation and decay:

"The ceiling was claw-hammered and discolored by stains of water, cornices and molding broken."

"...crumbling heads of cherubim like cinders around a blackened fireplace..."

"...faded grey stockings of ribbed wool with patches at the toes, and her faded, patched and darned shirt waist..."

Nothing here is intact. Everything is worn, patched, stained, broken, chained, mended. This is not a single moment of loss. It is the accumulated weather of a life. The objects do not signify a specific grief; they are grief as climate, grief as the condition of matter itself.

The narrator's own posture enacts this. She does not weep. She does not react with shock. She lists. The grief is in the inventory, in the refusal to abbreviate, in the way each broken or faded object receives equal, patient attention. The chapter refuses the resolution that would make grief into an event with a before and after.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

This pillar may be the chapter's deepest structure. The narrator's entire ethical stance is one of sustained, unflinching attention:

"Miss MacIntosh herself, sleeping, was singularly unaware of my scrutiny."

She does not wake Miss MacIntosh. She does not flee. She does not conclude. She sees. The chapter is largely an act of witnessing: the long inventory of objects, the patient cataloguing of the room, the careful notation of what is present and what is absent (no eyebrows, no hair on the upper lip, not one long hair on the chin).

The final revelation is delivered in the same tone as the inventory of buttons and yarn:

"I observed what I must surely have always known, the fact that one breast had been amputated. What was left was no bigger than a baby's."

The restraint here is extraordinary. No exclamation. No interpretation. The sentence arrives like a fact among facts. This is witnessing as ethical practice: the refusal to dramatize, to sentimentalize, to rush toward meaning. Attention is the act. Seeing fully is the response.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The narrator explicitly positions this revelation as something she has always known:

"I observed what I must surely have always known..."

This is recursive construction in its purest form. She is returning to the beginning (to Miss MacIntosh, to the childhood bedroom, to the questions she had always had) with what the night has taught her. The knowledge is not new. It is the acknowledgment of the knowledge that is new.

The room itself embodies this pillar. The warped knitting of a grey hood with unravelled stitches, the patched patches, the mended and re-mended shirtwaist; these are objects of recursive labor. You build, it unravels, you return and build again. The building is never finished. The grey hood is still on the end table, still unfinished, still catching the August sunlight.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter's syntax performs this pillar. The sentences are long, accumulative, list-like, refusing to conclude:

"...faded grey stockings of ribbed wool with patches at the toes, and her faded, patched and darned shirt waist, that still bristling with rusted needles and pins, that which ordinarily would have been hanging on a hanger in the almost empty dressing closet with her twelve other worn shirt waists of identical pattern and vintage and her salt-and-pepper tweed and her good serge and other woolen skirts stained by water and mud..."

The sentence does not want to end. It accumulates. It adds. It is honest precisely because it refuses the violence of conclusion: the violence that would say: here is the point, here is what it means, here is where we stop.

Miss MacIntosh's own objects embody incompletion: the broken ship's compass she was "intending to repair", the warped knitting with unravelled stitches, the torn pages of the world's almanac turning in the wind. These are not symbols of failure. They are the honest record of a life in process, never finished, never meant to be.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Miss MacIntosh is the marginal figure made central. She is:

  • A woman without hair, without eyebrows, without lashes, without one breast
  • A woman whose body does not conform to the category "woman" as culturally constructed
  • A woman who performs femininity (the wig, the corset, the ribbon of Irish green) while her body refuses it
  • A woman who is neither the dreaming invalid mother nor the "normal" woman she performs

"...not one soft fuzz of hair upon the upper lip, not one long hair growing out of her chin."

The narrator's careful notation of these absences is not cruelty. It is the recognition that Miss MacIntosh's body is evidence: evidence that the categories of woman, of health, of normalcy, are constructions. She does not fit. And her not-fitting is not the exception to the rule. It is the rule, made visible.

The amputated breast is the chapter's final, most radical statement of this pillar. The body that has been surgically altered, that carries the mark of its own incompletion, is the body that tells the truth about all bodies: that they are mortal, contingent, subject to subtraction.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The room is a geological formation. Layer upon layer of time is present simultaneously:

  • The cherubim that "had always been there and could not be removed unless the walls were torn down"
  • The grandmother's rocking chair
  • The porcelain chamber pot that "had belonged, I believed, to a late king of France"
  • The twelve identical shirtwaists of "identical pattern and vintage"
  • The playing cards with "curled edges"

Nothing here is only present-tense. Every object carries its history. The walls themselves are sedimentary: the hunting scenes "dimmed or covered over by patches of plastering", the ceiling "discolored by stains of water". The past is not behind this room. It is in the walls, in the plaster, in the stains.

The narrator's own consciousness is sedimentary. The questions she now asks (about the eyebrows, the cap, the umbrella, the veil) are questions she has "not long ago asked", questions that have been accumulating beneath her awareness, pressing upward. The revelation of the bald head is not a new fact. It is a buried fact that has finally surfaced.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter's language is acutely aware of its own location. The narrator speaks from inside uncertainty, inside the dream-waking boundary, and the language marks this:

"Was I awake or still asleep, even though I saw with such crucial clarity..."

"Or was it an alabaster rose which had fallen during the night?"

The language does not pretend to speak from nowhere. It speaks from a consciousness that is unsure of its own ground.

More subtly, the language of Miss MacIntosh's moral universe is rendered with careful irony. Her aphorisms, "beauty is as beauty does," "it's an ill wind that blows nobody good," "she knew not, she had ever said, of these dualities in one heart", are the language of a woman who has constructed a verbal reality as carefully as she has constructed her physical appearance. The aphorisms are her wig. They are the language of someone who has decided what reality is and built a vocabulary to enforce it.

The narrator's language, by contrast, is the language of someone who cannot decide: accumulative, hesitant, recursive. It is language that names its own location: inside the dream, inside the uncertainty, inside the room.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred is everywhere in this chapter, and it is entirely immanent: pressed into the ordinary objects of a sparely furnished room:

"...the tarnished harp strings of the footboard seeming to quiver with music as the murmur of wind and water filled this lonely bedroom."

The harp strings are tarnished. The music is only seeming. And yet the sacred is there, in the quivering, in the murmur of wind and water, in the "opalescence" and "gold" and "rose" of the dawn light.

The bald head itself becomes sacred:

"...like something long polished by the sea and the action of the tides, was Miss MacIntosh's bald head as cold as marble seldom touched by sunlight..."

This is not grotesque. It is luminous. The head is compared to something shaped by natural forces over a long time: sea-polished, tide-worn. The sacred is in the body that has been reduced to its essential form, not in any transcendence beyond it.

The deuce of clubs, "that card which signifies the test of tears, that card which is the omen of death", is the immanent sacred at its most compressed. The transcendent (death, fate, the omen) is here, on a playing card with curled edges, within reach of a gnarled hand, in a room with mud-tracks on the floor.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The narrator is, in this chapter, precisely the crossing point of times. She is the child who played dominoes under the storm lamp, the young person waking in the dawn, and already the retrospective consciousness that will carry this knowledge forward. She did not choose to be in this room, in this bed, at this moment of revelation. The structure chose her.

Miss MacIntosh herself is a crossing point. Her body carries:

  • The surgery that removed her breast: a past that is permanently present
  • The wig that covers the baldness: a daily re-enactment of a loss
  • The objects of multiple generations (the king of France's chamber pot, the grandmother's rocking chair): the sediment of others' lives accumulated in her keeping

And the narrator will carry this forward. The chapter ends not with resolution but with the knowledge now deposited in the narrator's consciousness, where it will press upward through everything that comes after. The ancestors are in the sediment: the grandmother's rocking chair, the king's chamber pot, the cherubim that cannot be removed without tearing down the walls. The carrying continues.
