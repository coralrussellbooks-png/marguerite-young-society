Definition from research

Chapter 13

  • “flew away like luna moths”: The pale green luna moth lives about a week as an adult and has no mouth to eat with.

Chapter 28

  • “no patience to wait for the caterpillar to become the butterfly”: The Greek psychē means both “soul” and “butterfly.” In Apuleius’s The Golden Ass (2nd century AD), Psyche is the soul who marries Cupid.
  • “the Hesperides like spirit messengers”: Hesperides in Greek mythology are nymphs of the evening. They guarded the golden apples at the western edge of the world. The skippers, a family of butterflies, are the Hesperiidae, named for them.
  • “the harlequins, the painted ladies fluttering through the darkness”: Harlequin was the patched, masked trickster of the commedia dell’arte. The Painted Lady (Vanessa cardui) is a butterfly, and “painted lady” was also slang for a woman of the streets. Young lets both meanings fly at once.
  • “watching the amazed birth of a butterfly, the first tremblings of the feeble, dew-lit wings”: The emergence of the butterfly from the chrysalis is called eclosion.

Chapter 28 · Butterflies as a Symbol

  • Butterflies serve as a powerful metaphor for fleeting beauty, transformation, and the ephemeral nature of life and memory.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s passion for collecting butterflies reflects his attempt to capture and preserve moments of beauty and meaning amid loneliness and loss.
  • The novel details the vividness and variety of butterflies he treasured, symbolizing spiritual messengers or incarnations of beauty in various realms.
  • His butterfly collection and memories also contrast with the pragmatic and mundane approach of his brother Peron, who dismissed such ethereal concerns.

Chapter 28 · Butterflies

  • Represent transformation, beauty, and the fleeting nature of life.
  • Emblematic of Mr. Spitzer’s desire to preserve ephemeral moments amid existential uncertainty.

Chapter 29 · Butterflies as Symbols of the Soul and Immortality

  • Butterflies represent the psyche or soul, echoing the Greek notion where “psyche” means both soul and butterfly.
  • They symbolize transformation, immortality, and the elusive nature of life and death.
  • Despite their mortality, butterflies are portrayed as perfect images of immortality, transcending their ephemeral physical existence.

Chapter 29 · Butterflies

  • Transformation, resurrection, soul, ephemeral beauty, and immortality.
  • Seen as voyagers between stars, realms, and states of being.
  • Metaphors for memory, identity, and the human condition.

Chapter 29 · On Butterfly and Moth Distinctions and Flight Times (Day versus Night)

  • The ambiguous boundary and symbolic contrast between butterflies and moths, day and night, happiness and sorrow.

Chapter 29 · Butterflies as Psyche and Their Cosmic Journeys

  • The belief that butterflies wander seeking immortal love and may journey through the stars.

Chapter 29 · Mr. Spitzer’s Butterfly Collection as Memory and Identity

  • The collection holds symbolic and emotional weight, paralleling memories of his brother and his own identity.

Chapter 29 · Butterfly Names and Mythological Associations

  • Butterflies named after gods (Aphrodite, Apollo, Morpheus, Leto), mythological figures (Io, Atlas), and symbolic concepts.
  • These names embed butterflies in a rich symbolic and cultural context.
  • The rich tapestry of cultural and mythological references deepens the symbolism.

Chapter 30

Chapter 31

Chapter 31 · The Butterfly Card

  • The “great eye” of the planet, the “butterfly” symbol of resurrection, and references to historical and exotic locations enhance the dreamlike and timeless atmosphere.

Chapter 35

  • “the blind moth fluttered with jewels upon its wings”: “The desire of the moth for the star, / Of the night for the morrow” (Percy Bysshe Shelley, “One word is too often profaned,” 1821).

Chapter 37

  • “to death’s-head moths”: The death’s-head hawkmoth, Acherontia atropos, bears a skull on its thorax and is named for the Fate who cuts the thread of life.

Chapter 42

  • “Perhaps it was the lackey moth.”: The lackey moth is named for its caterpillar’s bright stripes, like a footman’s livery.

Chapter 45

  • “Had he not lost his butterfly”: In Greek, psyche means both the soul and the butterfly.

Chapter 45 · Butterflies

  • Transformation, fleeting perceptions and the fragility of life and consciousness.

Chapter 47

  • “an armada of white peacock butterflies”: White peacock butterflies are found in the southeastern United States, Central and South America.
  • “the Atlas moth containing its spirit in the cloud”: See the Atlas moth, among the largest moths in the world, in the Chapter 29 resources.

Chapter 49

  • “the star might be drawn by the moth”: Young reverses Shelley: “The desire of the moth for the star, / Of the night for the morrow” (Percy Bysshe Shelley, “One word is too often profaned,” 1824).

Chapter 50

  • “Should a demoniac butterfly appear”: A demoniac butterfly may refer to a restricted demon or grass demon butterfly found in Asia. The Greek psyche means both soul and butterfly, and the butterfly rising from the fire is the soul leaving the body.
  • “butterfly was sequestered like this mourning cloak”: The mourning cloak butterfly, dark with a pale border, sleeps through the winter as an adult hidden in bark and crevices.

Chapter 51

  • “drawers of his dead brother’s butterfly correspondence”: See Peron’s butterflies in the Chapter 29 resources.

Chapter 52 · Birds and Butterflies

  • Symbols of transformation, fleeting beauty, and the soul’s journey. The butterfly sailing out of the ear (a door) suggests escape or transcendence.

Chapter 55

  • “his great emperors and queens, his pearl-bordered moths”: Young’s collection is made of real butterflies and moths: the purple emperor, the queen, the pearl-bordered fritillary, the pebble prominent, the marbled white, and the mourning cloak, which the English call the Camberwell beauty.
  • “his little salts and peppers winging through the mist”: The peppered moth is speckled black and white like salt and pepper; in sooty industrial England a black form all but replaced it, a famous case of natural selection.

Chapter 55 · The Butterfly Collection and Stamps

  • Represent delicate beauty, memory, and past achievements, threatened by Peron’s recklessness.

Chapter 56

  • “the quivering bodies of dead luna moths”: The adult luna moth has no mouth and does not eat; it lives about a week, long enough to mate.

Chapter 57

  • “or perhaps he was the measuring worm”: The measuring worm, or inchworm, is the caterpillar of the geometer moths, whose name means earth-measurers; it moves by looping, as if measuring the ground.
  • “a Sphinx had settled on his coat sleeve”: This sphinx is a sphinx moth, or hawk moth, named for the caterpillar’s habit of rearing up its head like the Great Sphinx. See The Life of the Sphinx Moth in the Chapter 55 resources.

Chapter 58

  • The Sphingidae are a family of moths commonly called sphinx moths, also colloquially known as hawk moths, with many of their caterpillars known as hornworms. It includes about 1,450 species. It is best represented in the tropics, but species are found in every region. (Wikipedia)

Chapter 59

  • “his escape from this chrysalis”: The Greek word psyche means both soul and butterfly, and a butterfly leaving its chrysalis was an ancient image of the soul leaving the body.
  • “was not the moth married to the star which had extinguished it”: See Shelley’s “desire of the moth for the star” in the Chapter 57 resources.

Chapter 61

  • “flowers pale as tussock moths”: Tussock moths are found on every continent except Antarctica.

Chapter 62

  • “pearl-bordered fritillaries”: Fritillaries are a type of butterfly and a genus of flowering plants known for their checkered patterns.
  • “The death’s-head smiling, inviting pity for the moth”: The death’s-head hawkmoth carries a skull on its back and squeaks when handled.

Chapter 63

  • “Purple emperor!”: The purple emperor is a butterfly of European oak woods; imperial purple, dyed from Tyrian shellfish, was the color of emperors such as Constantine.
