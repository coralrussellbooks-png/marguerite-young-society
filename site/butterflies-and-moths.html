Chapter 28

  • “no patience to wait for the caterpillar to become the butterfly”: The Greek psychē means both “soul” and “butterfly.” In Apuleius’s The Golden Ass (2nd century AD), Psyche is the soul who marries Cupid.
  • “the Hesperides like spirit messengers”: Hesperides in Greek mythology are nymphs of the evening. They guarded the golden apples at the western edge of the world. The skippers, a family of butterflies, are the Hesperiidae, named for them.
  • “the harlequins, the painted ladies fluttering through the darkness”: Harlequin was the patched, masked trickster of the commedia dell’arte. The Painted Lady (Vanessa cardui) is a butterfly, and “painted lady” was also slang for a woman of the streets. Young lets both meanings fly at once.
  • “watching the amazed birth of a butterfly, the first tremblings of the feeble, dew-lit wings”: The emergence of the butterfly from the chrysalis is called eclosion.

  • Zhuangzi's "Butterfly Dream" It is a famous philosophical anecdote about a dream where the philosopher Zhuangzi transforms into a butterfly and then questions whether he is now a man who dreamed of being a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming he is a man. This story explores themes of self, reality, and the fluidity of existence within Daoist philosophy. Zhuangzi recounts a dream where he is a butterfly, flitting around happily and unaware of his human identity. He wakes up and realizes he is Zhuangzi, but is then filled with doubt about whether his current state is the true reality or just another dream. The core question arises: Is he the man who dreamed he was a butterfly, or is he a butterfly dreaming he is a man?

  • Chaos theory or the butterfly effect - The chapter uses rich metaphorical language and symbolism to evoke the essence of Chaos theory and the butterfly effect: small, subtle causes yielding large, unpredictable consequences in a complex, interwoven reality. The chapter highlights the fragile, transient, and ambiguous nature of existence, where the boundary between cause and effect, life and death, reality and illusion is blurred, and where every small event is potentially significant to the unfolding of life’s vast, mysterious patterns.
  • Chaos theory is the study of how seemingly unpredictable and complex systems, like weather or ecosystems, can have underlying order and patterns, even though they are highly sensitive to initial conditions. It highlights that small changes in these conditions can lead to vastly different outcomes over time, a concept often referred to as the "butterfly effect". Chaotic systems are often nonlinear, meaning that small changes in inputs don't necessarily produce proportional changes in outputs. This can lead to complex and unpredictable behavior. A tiny change in the starting point of a chaotic system can cause a large difference in its eventual state. The classic example is the "butterfly effect," where a butterfly flapping its wings in one location might eventually contribute to a tornado forming elsewhere. While chaotic systems appear random, chaos theory suggests that there are often underlying patterns, feedback loops, and self-organization within them. Fractals, geometric shapes with self-similar patterns at different scales, are often used to model chaotic systems. They capture the complexity and irregular nature of these systems. Chaos theory has applications in various fields, including weather forecasting, fluid dynamics, biology, economics, and even social sciences.

Mr. Spitzer's legal practice is described as existing in a liminal space between life and death. He practices "only in the courts which were paved with the moonstones of lost cases under a firmament of lost stars," evoking a sense of decay, loss, and the ephemeral nature of human endeavors. The images of a "dead moth with its wings trembling" and a "mouse in a paper nest" further emphasize the themes of decay and disorder. The mouse, in particular, embodies the chaotic forces that undermine Mr. Spitzer's attempts to impose order. Mr. Spitzer's preference for "the seething silence to the sound, preferring mystery to exposure, preferring a cloak to nakedness" is highlighted. Despite his "great sense of order," he is increasingly drawn to the chaotic and irrational aspects of life, symbolized by the "litigious starfish" and the bees buzzing around his head.

  • “Perhaps it was she who was the master of keys” grapples with the question of what happens after death. Mr. Spitzer contemplates whether the soul ("spirit") needs a physical exit, like a chimney, if the conventional routes ("doors and passage-ways") are closed. This reflects philosophical inquiries into the soul's immortality and its separation from the body. The idea that "death was unknown to those who loved, and death was unknown to those who loved not, and death was unknown to those who died" suggests that death remains an enigma regardless of one's experiences or beliefs. It's a state beyond comprehension, aligning with existentialist themes of the unknown and the limits of human knowledge. The paragraph questions whether our senses provide a complete picture of reality. The idea that "Even the closed door was not closed, for should death be better known than love? Not all the senses were ever sealed at once" suggests that there are realities beyond our immediate perception. The image of the butterfly escaping through the ear, "even when it was a door of the silence," implies that even in the absence of sound, there are avenues for experience or existence. The description of Mr. Spitzer as a night watchman in the "starry universe" raises questions about the nature of reality. Is he a real figure, or is he merely a figment of someone's imagination? His light awakening "other sleepers, those he never knew" suggests the interconnectedness of existence, even if we are unaware of it. The suggestion that he perhaps "had intended to lock and not to unlock" introduces the possibility that his actions may have unintended consequences, highlighting the limits of human control and the role of chance. The final lines emphasize Mr. Spitzer's role as the "only mourner among men," suggesting a profound sense of isolation and the burden of grief. His mourning for himself implies a recognition of his own mortality and the inevitability of loss. This connects to existentialist themes of individual responsibility and the search for meaning in a seemingly meaningless world.

Chapter 55

  • “The Life of the Sphinx Moth” The life of a sphinx moth involves four stages: egg, larva (caterpillar), pupa, and adult. After mating, the adult female lays eggs on a host plant, which hatch into ravenous caterpillars that feed and grow, molting multiple times. Once fully grown, the caterpillar burrows into the soil or leaf litter to form a pupa, overwintering in this stage. The following spring, or later if delayed, the pupa transforms into an adult moth, which emerges, feeds on nectar using its proboscis, mates, and then lays eggs to begin the cycle anew.

The variety of figures represents the vast, interconnected, and layered nature of existence. It suggests that human life, history, and nature are woven together in a complex tapestry that transcends time and space. The mention of these figures alongside celestial and natural imagery emphasizes the novel's theme of blurred boundaries between life and death, reality and dream. The dead are portrayed as continuing presences, their memories, and legacies lingering in the world and in the consciousness of the living, especially in the mind of Mr. Spitzer, the family lawyer who acts as a keeper of memory and history. The passage references metamorphosis explicitly and the transformation of earthly figures into ethereal or symbolic forms. This imagery aligns with the novel’s preoccupation with change, impermanence, and the fluidity of identity. The characters and elements listed symbolize various stages, states, and aspects of being, emphasizing that nothing remains fixed, and all life is subject to transformation. By mixing images of grandeur with decay, the list underscores the inevitability of death and the fading of all earthly glory. It stresses that all humans, regardless of status, are subject to mortality, a recurring theme in the novel. The cataloging of historical and mythical figures alongside ephemeral things like butterflies and moths, and symbolic references points to the fragility of memory and the losses that accumulate over time. Mr. Spitzer’s role is to navigate this complex legacy, but even he is often overwhelmed by the incompleteness and elusiveness of memory. The list contributes to the novel’s dreamlike and mythic quality. It mirrors the fragmented, layered storytelling style, where history, myth, and personal memory intertwine. This technique immerses the reader in the same blurred reality experienced by the characters, where time is non-linear and identities are fluid. The list is not meant to be read as a mere inventory but as a symbolic meditation on the themes of the novel: the passage of time, mortality, transformation, memory, and the intertwined nature of life and death. It reflects the complexity of existence that Mr. Spitzer faces as he attempts to make sense of the lives and legacies of those around him.
