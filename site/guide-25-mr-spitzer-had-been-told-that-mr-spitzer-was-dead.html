Chapter 25: Mr. Spitzer Had Been Told That Mr. Spitzer Was Dead

← Chapter 24 · Guide Home · Chapter 26 →

Research notes: Chapter 25

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Mr. Chandelier · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Death and Mortality

Symbols: Mirrors and Reflections

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Judaism · Suicide in History and Literature

Chapter 25 is the most sustained portrait of Mr. Spitzer we have had yet, and it arrives with a gift: at last, in full detail, we learn exactly how Peron died. We also learn what Mr. Spitzer did the day he walked through a glass wall and was told, afterward, that he was dead.

This chapter is one of the funniest in the novel, and also one of the saddest. Young achieves both simultaneously, which is her particular genius, and nowhere more clearly than here.

What actually happens in Chapter 25

Still at the breakfast table, still in the morning light, Vera’s mind moves through everything she knows about Mr. Spitzer. Young gives us the full account: his relationship with Catherine, his dead brother Peron, his habits and hesitations, his theory of his own existence.

We learn that Peron fell “through a glass window on the fourteenth floor of an office building.” Mr. Spitzer cannot decide whether it was suicide or accident. Perhaps Peron, rushing impetuously ahead, simply mistook “a window for a door. If the floor level had continued on the other side,” Peron would have lived. It didn’t. He fell through “the first gleamings of the first stars” to the pavement below, screaming as he recognized, too late, the void.

We learn that Mr. Spitzer once walked through a glass wall in another lawyer’s office. He felt nothing, heard nothing, noticed nothing. His valet found glass particles behind his ears that evening, like the prisms of a chandelier. Days later, he received a bill for the wall. He ignored it. More bills came, and threatening letters. Finally, seething with an uncharacteristic fury, he tried to collect for his own discomfort. He was told by a “desk clerk, that Mr. Spitzer was dead, having departed this mortal life several years ago.” His arguments fell upon the empty air.

The hesitant suicide

Young gives Mr. Spitzer one of her most precise self-descriptions in this chapter: he is not “the sudden, untimely suicide as his brother had been but rather the hesitant suicide who takes his luxurious time and considers, weighs and measures all the lost possibilities and disappointments until life ends upon its own account without anyone’s permission or objection or desire.”

This is one of the most exact things Young says about anyone in the novel. Peron was the decisive one: “quick to live, quick to die,” taking the last chance when the odds were all against him. Mr. Spitzer is the other kind: the man who rehearses his ultimate passage so many times, dying so many fugitive deaths, that when the final one comes he hopes it will be imperceptible, that no one will notice when quietly he slips away.

He has been dying slowly since his brother died. Perhaps, he suspects, since before. He cannot remember exactly when he started. He has gained weight as his sorrow has “increased. A fat man, well protected by his dreaming flesh,” treading softly where angels fear to rush ahead, never knowing whether the next step will bring stairway or dissolving stairway or chasm.

And he is proud, ridiculously and tenderly proud, of his little accidents. He has walked through a glass wall and survived. Peron walked through a window and did not. Mr. Spitzer considers this the essential difference between them.

How Peron died

The account of Peron’s death is the most specific thing Mr. Spitzer allows himself to know about it, and even here he cannot hold it steady. It shifts between suicide and accident in the same paragraph, Young giving us Mr. Spitzer’s mind working the question from every angle and arriving at no conclusion.

What is certain: Peron rushed impetuously ahead. The window was on the fourteenth floor. The building “looked out only upon the pavements of the evening clouds, the first gleamings of the first stars.” He screamed as he fell, recognizing too late “the void, the pavement of the city, and his nullification so final, so complete that he had perhaps hardly been able to judge” it.

What is uncertain: whether he knew. Perhaps, Mr. Spitzer reasons carefully, Peron simply mistook the window for a door, as Mr. Spitzer himself once mistook a door for a window. Perhaps he was not suicidal at all but only, in one bright disoriented moment, wrong about where the floor was. “If the floor level had continued,” he would have lived and carried on with his usual pleasures.

Mr. Spitzer cannot settle this. To decide it was an accident would relieve him of something. To decide it was suicide would give Peron’s death a meaning, a choice, a finality that Mr. Spitzer cannot quite claim for his own life. Either way, it was Joachim who has had to think so much about death, beautiful death, all these years since. Peron, smiling in his grave, would probably “not know that he was dead” until someone asked.

The glass wall

Mr. Spitzer walks through a glass wall and leaves a hole in it that he cannot remember making. He feels nothing. He hears nothing. He goes on about his errands. His valet finds the glass behind his ears like chandelier prisms at the end of the day.

Young plays this for comedy and she earns every laugh. The bills arrive. Mr. Spitzer ignores them. More bills. Threatening letters from the other lawyer. Finally, and this is where Young lets us see a flash of Peron in Joachim, he loses his patience. There is still, it seems, a spark of life in Mr. Spitzer. He decides to collect for his discomfort. He presents himself as the aggrieved party.

He is told he is dead.

The desk clerk is not wrong, exactly. Mr. Spitzer has been dying by degrees since his brother died. He has been mistaken for his brother on streets, at race tracks, at parimutuel windows. He has been allowing people to believe he is Peron. He has been living in his brother’s clothes, with his brother’s cufflinks, drinking his brother’s coffee, living in his brother’s house of wreckage. Of course the desk clerk thinks he is dead. The living Mr. Spitzer has made every possible effort to become a ghost. And his arguments fell upon the empty air. Young ends the episode there. The bill is never paid. The glass wall stays broken. Mr. Spitzer, told he is dead, has no further recourse. He was the one who should have died.

What Mr. Spitzer finds in Catherine

Young gives us something unexpected near the chapter’s center: Mr. Spitzer’s account of why he keeps coming to Catherine’s bedside, year after year, evening after evening. It is not entirely about love, or not only.

He has found in Catherine “the bulwark of his own greater delicacy.” She accuses him of falsehood, theft, unsound perceptions, faulty memories. She tells him he is dead, that he has been buried, that he is married to the worm. And through all of this, paradoxically, he gains a “sense of his own precarious being.” Without “her constant, fickle reminder that he” exists (even if only as a ghost, even if only to be accused of things) “he might have forgotten both brothers, the living and the” dead.

She needs him to be real enough to argue with. He needs her to insist that he exists, even wrongly. They sustain each other’s reality through mutual incomprehension. It is one of the novel’s strangest and most honest portraits of what keeps two people together.

What to watch for as you keep reading

The glass wall is not the last time Mr. Spitzer will walk through something that should stop him and feel nothing. Watch for the pattern: he moves through barriers that break around him, leaving damage he does not register, continuing on about his errands. It is his way of being in the world. He is always the one who survived the window.

The twin golden cradles beside the twin iron beds in Mr. Spitzer’s bedroom (his and Peron’s, from infancy) are one of the novel’s most precise images of what grief looks like lived in daily. He would sooner have parted from his life than from those cradles. They remind him of infancy and death. Everything in his house is simultaneously the beginning and the end.

Mr. Spitzer’s description of the only music worth hearing as the music he has “never heard, perhaps his own which he had never written, and the only friend” as the friend he has “never met, and the only loves” as the loves he has never loved; these are the novel’s statement of his condition in its most compressed form. He is a man defined entirely by absence. The unwritten score. The unlived life. The brother who should have lived.
