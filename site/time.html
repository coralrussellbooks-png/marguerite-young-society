Time

Definition from research

Chapter 8 · Memory and the Passage of Time

  • Vera reflects on the idea that drowning people relive their lives in moments before death, but here the memories seem fragmented and possibly belong to others.
  • The blending of past and present, and the uncertainty of reality and memory, permeate the narrative.

Chapter 12 · The Illusion and Reality of Time

  • Vera reflects on time as both a practical necessity and an illusion.
  • The Big Ben alarm clock symbolizes reliable, practical time, set for servants who must rise at certain hours.
  • Other clocks in the house are stopped at different times, symbolizing frozen moments or hesitation in the past, especially linked to Catherine’s retreat from the world.

Chapter 12

  • “the Big Ben alarm clock”: The Big Ben was the Western Clock Company’s alarm clock, sold from 1909 and the first alarm clock advertised across the country (1910). It takes its name from the great bell in London.
  • “though time was an illusion, it was not the illusion of servants”: Einstein wrote in 1955 that the distinction between past, present, and future “is only a stubbornly persistent illusion.” Servants still had to rise on time.
  • “perhaps it was we who passed through time”: Austin Dobson, “The Paradox of Time” (1877): “Time goes, you say? Ah no! / Alas, Time stays, we go.”

Chapter 22

  • “Time was circled in a single week”: Nietzsche’s eternal recurrence (The Gay Science 341, 1882): “This life as you now live it and have lived it, you will have to live once more and innumerable times more.”

Chapter 23 · Mortality and the Passage of Time

  • The inevitability of winter and death is anticipated, reflecting on the cycles of life.
  • Despite the harshness of winter, there is beauty in its muted colors and quiet persistence.
  • The chapter ends with a meditation on light and darkness as metaphors for life, suffering, and divine presence.

Chapter 24

  • “he lived in a forest of cuckoo clocks”: The cuckoo clock came from the Black Forest of Germany in the eighteenth century.

Chapter 24 · Time and Space Distortion

  • The house is timeless and spaceless in a conventional sense, populated by figures from different centuries, places, and mythologies simultaneously.
  • Imaginary guests from various epochs co-exist, and the servants debate the time of day as Roman or Greek, reflecting a dislocation from normal temporal experience.

Chapter 24 · Cuckoo Clocks and Elephants

  • Cuckoo clocks and elephants represent distorted time and impossible realities within the servants’ transformed perceptions.

Chapter 27 · Time and Memory

  • Time is portrayed as fluid, unreliable, and artificial.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s broken and inherited watches symbolize the fractured nature of time and memory.
  • The struggle with remembering and forgetting is a persistent motif, especially in relation to Mr. Spitzer’s brother’s death.

Chapter 27 · Watches

  • Mr. Spitzer wears both his own watch and his brother’s. Miss MacIntosh also has a watch, her “old dollar watch which was given to her by her dead love” (Chapter 22).
  • The brother’s unbroken, elegant watch contrasts with Mr. Spitzer’s broken, dust-clogged timepiece.
  • Watches symbolize the passage and distortion of time, memory, and connection between the brothers.

Chapter 28 · Philosophical Reflections on Birth, Death, and Time

  • The chapter explores the ambiguity between birth and death, suggesting moments in life where beginning and end are indistinguishable.
  • Mr. Spitzer muses on how one could never be sure if a certain moment signifies death or birth, reflecting on the cyclical and interconnected nature of existence.
  • This ambiguity inspired his lifelong study of the “impalpable butterflies,” symbolizing ineffable and transient moments in life.

Chapter 33 · Time and Reality

  • Time is fluid and unreliable in Catherine’s experience; she confuses noon and midnight, beginnings and endings.
  • Mr. Spitzer attempts to keep time (symbolized by his watch) as a measure of reality, but even he is caught between illusion and fact.
  • The novel questions the nature of reality itself and how much is defined by perception.

Chapter 34 · Death and Time

  • Death is a recurring motif, intertwined with marriage, dreams, and memory. Catherine contemplates the inevitability of death and the fading of beauty and life’s passions.
  • Time is fluid and fragmented, with references to sleep, awakening, and timelessness, emphasizing Catherine’s disoriented state.

Chapter 36 · Time and Perception

  • Time is experienced non-linearly; sounds arrive late, events are recalled out of sequence.
  • The narrative reflects a consciousness that is "always in danger" of missing or misinterpreting life’s moments.
  • This theme underscores the instability of human experience and the elusive nature of reality.

Chapter 39 · Timelessness and the Fluidity of Time

  • Time is portrayed as elusive, nonlinear, and sometimes meaningless.
  • Catherine experiences multiple times and places at once: simultaneous winters in different ski resorts, many historical eras.
  • The narrative questions the nature of time, blending past, present, and future, life and death.

Chapter 40 · Time and Order

  • The chapter challenges conventional notions of time and causality (“the echo came before the song,” “death came before life”).
  • Time is cyclical, layered, and full of “refractions” and “oblique passages,” making logic and sequence unreliable.

Chapter 42 · The Fluidity of Time and Reality

  • The narrative challenges linear notions of time. Catherine’s journey is described as transcending death and time, visiting Cousin Hannah when both are bed-ridden or dead. Dreams and waking reality blur, reflecting a mystical view of existence where farewell is "a matter of opinion as to the time, the place, the person"

Chapter 45 · Memory, Identity, and the Passing of Time

  • Mr. Spitzer struggles with memory’s frailty, likening it to fleeting natural phenomena.
  • The chapter meditates on the dissolution of identity.
  • Cousin Hannah’s secret life and true self remain ambiguous, veiled in clouds and mystery, reflecting the difficulty of truly knowing another person, especially after death.

Chapter 54 · Memory, Time, and Reality

  • Time is fluid and non-linear. Newspapers are dated years ago but read as current; streets and cities shift; memories and experiences overlap. This challenges the concept of objective reality and highlights the fragility of memory.

Chapter 59 · Time and Memory

  • Time is depicted as fluid and unreliable; Mr. Spitzer experiences memories of vanished streets, people, and moments.
  • His experience of time is cyclical and elusive, with the past, present, and future intermingling.
  • The idea that memories and sensations can persist beyond death is central to his reflections.

Chapter 65 · The End of Time and Apocalypse

  • Mr. Bonebreaker’s constant preaching about the “Last Day,” the destruction of Chicago, and the fall of creation permeates the narrative.
  • This apocalyptic vision frames the personal drama within a cosmic scale.

Chapter 70 · Time and Permanence

  • Mrs. Hogden talks about time running backward and forward, dreams of a motorboat, and eternal social seasons.
  • Her presence represents a temporary permanence, a fixture in a house and life otherwise transient.

Chapter 74 · The Passage of Time and Memory

  • The cyclical nature of seasons (autumn, winter, spring) parallels Esther’s repeated pregnancies and losses, symbolizing the persistence of trauma and hope over time.

Chapter 80 · Memory and Time

  • Esther’s memory is fragmented, cyclical, and tinged with confusion as she recalls her past pregnancies, loves, and losses.
  • Time is fluid; events from years ago feel present and immediate, especially as she contemplates the "unfinished murder" of Walter.
  • The narrative explores how memory shapes identity and the persistence of past trauma.

Chapter 81 · Apocalyptic Imagery and Fate

  • The storm at sea is described with cosmic and cataclysmic imagery, suggesting universal destruction and rebirth.
  • Prophecies of war and world destruction reflect existential dread.

Chapter 82 · Time and Timelessness

  • Time is experienced unevenly, accelerating at moments and suspended in others. Catherine’s slow death, Mr. Spitzer’s long wait, and the clam-digger’s timeless endurance reflect different relationships with time.
