Marguerite Young, Our Darling

Marguerite Young, Our Darling, Part 5: What It Reveals About Young and Her Work

← Part 4 · Marguerite Young, Our Darling: What It Reveals, Part by Part · Part 6 →

Source: Marguerite Young, Our Darling: Tributes and Essays, edited by Miriam Fuchs (Dalkey Archive Press, 1994). Part 5 gathers five scholarly essays on Miss MacIntosh, My Darling: Erika Duncan on the novel as elegy, Suzanne Oboler on the American dream, Martha J. Sattler on woman's experience, Fatima Shaik on the subconscious, and the opening of Susan Strehle on women's time. They converge on two claims, that the book is an American epic and that it narrates female experience in a new way. Only material that illuminates Young and her work is kept below; quoted phrases from the book are marked with quotation marks.

An epic elegy for all creation

Erika Duncan reads the novel as an elegy and locates its origin precisely: Miss MacIntosh was begun in the shadow of the atomic bomb on Hiroshima, and it becomes an ode to humanity's futile search for perfection through which the beauty of the flawed, striving soul is revealed. She hears its keynote already in the poem “Death by Rarity” in Moderate Fable, a lament for extinct birds built on the idea that “rarity precedes extinction,” a celebration of the singular thing never to be known the same way again. Duncan is especially good on Young's technique. Where ordinary narrative builds a sense of place and plot from escalating outlines of what is, Young's prose works by a rapid, kaleidoscopic falling away of possibilities, a visible disappearance of all that might be but is not, so that reading becomes a matter of seeing patterns emerge from a tapestry in which every sentence holds infinite possibility. Beneath the twelve hundred pages she finds a single simple duality: Vera Cartwheel is torn between her opium-addicted mother, who lives in a stationary world of preserved beauty and waking dream, and the nursemaid Miss MacIntosh, the mover and doer who searches only for tangible reality and so must drown. What Duncan calls Young's particular genius is simultaneity, the capacity to sustain overtly allegorical figures without losing their human warmth, so that the psychological and the philosophical do not cancel each other. The quest ends not in despair but in Esther Longtree gathered at the wedding as “our love, all our loves.”

The American epic and its cultural reality

Suzanne Oboler frames the novel as a study of American cultural reality and pairs it, pointedly, with García Márquez's One Hundred Years of Solitude, published the same year, 1965. Both books break the boundaries of their cultures by reorganizing time and space, yet one became a touchstone of Latin American identity while Young's remains neglected. Oboler's central argument is that Young treats American reality as irreducibly plural: it resembles, in Young's own image, “a hawk rather than a tamed canary easily caged in definitions.” The book journeys through the country's inherited institutions to expose common sense itself as the deepest American illusion, and it insists that a nation's culture is not external to its people but exists in how they interpret their daily lives, so that culture becomes the collective “organization of illusion, of memory.” Oboler ties this to Young's self-description in the Ruas interview, “I'm a pluralistic person,” a pluralism she sees expressed even in Young's layered Afghan and Latin American street dress. She sets the novel against the 1870 to 1930 span of American history and its ambivalence about electric modernity, and reports Young's own rueful line about the eighteen-year labor: “If I had known it was going to take me so long to finish, I might never have started it.”

An epic of woman, and women's time

Martha Sattler reads the same epic as a study of the nature and inner life of woman, organized around motherhood, the one thing only a woman can do. The novel opens with a pregnant wife who resists her own condition; Vera's mother assigns her child to a governess and retreats into opium; and at the center stands Esther Longtree, “the mother of us all,” a tragic figure of endless pregnancy whose births may all be imagined, whom Sattler links to Oriana Fallaci's Letter to a Child Never Born. Sattler catalogs the book's women as archetypes across class and age: the abandoned Catherine Cartwheel escaping into her own dream world; Miss MacIntosh the old maid, her scars hidden under a red wig, betrayed by the gospel salesman Bonebreaker; and Cousin Hannah the great suffragette, whose forty locked trunks turn out to hold forty unworn wedding dresses, a secret hunger for love beneath the public cause. Susan Strehle sharpens this into a formal claim using Julia Kristeva's essay “Women's Time.” Against linear, historical, teleological time, the time of conventional narrative with its beginnings and endings, Kristeva sets a female temporality of repetition and eternity, of cycles and gestation. Strehle argues that at every level, from its nonlinear syntax to its inversion of linear plot, Miss MacIntosh replaces historical time with this female subjectivity, which is exactly why the twelve-hundred-page book lacks the tidy progression critics expect. She is candid about the cost: the novel struck early reviewers as “unreadable,” drew as much condemnation as praise, and sank into obscurity while keeping an underground readership.

The poetry of the subconscious

Fatima Shaik, herself a fiction writer, reads the novel as poetry drawn straight from the subconscious. She argues that Young gives herself over to the wisdom of her deepest dreams, trusting that the poetic speech of the inner mind will yield universal wisdom out of particulars. In Shaik's account the prose works by association, one flashing image linked to the next until phrases close back on their beginnings like smoke rings, carrying recurring polarities of yin and yang, angel and devil, light and dark. Its method is to include everything, even what Young calls “trumpery and frumpery and junk,” so that seemingly chaotic rhyming runs of words lead into profound pronouncements, the book moving easily “from the flimsy to the sublime.” Shaik describes this as the transcription of an inner chant, a private rhythm that every reader can sense, which is why the dense continuity of the language nonetheless reads effortlessly. The unifying drive she finds beneath it is toward wholeness, the reconciliation of opposites, two halves of a divided heart made one.
