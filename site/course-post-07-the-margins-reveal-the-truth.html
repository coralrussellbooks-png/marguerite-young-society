Pillar: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth · Across every book

Act II: The Relational Edge

How a society treats its edges is the definitive test of whether it succeeds or fails.

The usual story of a society is told from the center: its capital, its markets, its biggest companies and loudest voices. The edges show up late in the story, as a footnote or a cost. Radical Immanence flips the telescope. The margin is not the leftover part of the picture. It is the frame that holds the picture together.

The Framework

In November 1977, weeks before his death, Senator and former Vice President Hubert Humphrey spoke at the dedication of the federal building that now bears his name. He said that "the moral test of government is how that government treats those who are in the dawn of life, the children; those who are in the twilight of life, the elderly; and those who are in the shadows of life, the sick, the needy and the handicapped." Humphrey was a politician, not a philosopher, yet his sentence carries an ontology. It says a whole is known by its edges.

Marguerite Young spent her life at those edges. Her first prose book, Angel in the Forest (1945), studied New Harmony, Indiana, where two utopian communities rose and failed in the early nineteenth century. She treated those failed dreams as central to the American story, not marginal to it. Her last book, Harp Song for a Radical, followed Eugene V. Debs, the labor organizer and socialist who told a federal court in 1918: "while there is a lower class, I am in it, and while there is a criminal element I am of it, and while there is a soul in prison, I am not free." In Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, the people who fill the pages are waitresses, bus riders, addicts, and the half-mad. The margin is the book's main street.

Ecology offers a plain picture of why this matters. The zone where a forest meets a meadow, called an ecotone, often holds more kinds of life than either the forest or the meadow alone. Edges are where categories show their seams, where things mix, and where new forms appear. The philosopher Jacques Derrida made a related point about the frame around a painting. The frame seems to sit outside the art, yet without it there is no inside at all.

Read socially, this becomes a critique. When a community measures itself by its wealthiest center, it misreads its own structure. The night custodian, the home health aide, the food pantry volunteer, and the neighbor who checks on an elderly widow are not peripheral. They are load-bearing. When care at the edges fails, the center eventually cracks.

The Art Companion

Jean-François Millet's painting The Gleaners (1857) shows three peasant women bent low, gathering the grain left behind after harvest. The wealthy farm glows in the background. The painting's weight sits entirely with the women at the edge of the field.

Agnès Varda's documentary The Gleaners and I (2000) begins with Millet's painting and follows modern French gleaners: people picking through market leftovers, discarded potatoes, and city trash. Varda films them with warmth and without pity. She even turns the camera on her own aging hand, placing herself among the edges she records.

Reflection Questions

  • Who keeps my daily life running whose name I have never learned?

  • Where are the edges of my town, and when did I last walk there?

  • When have I stood at the margin of a group, and what could I see from there that the center could not?

  • How would my community look if it were measured by its edges instead of its center?

The Exercise: Mapping the Load-Bearing Edges

  1. Draw a rough map of the neighborhood, town, or workplace. Mark its center, the place or thing it is proudest of.

  2. Now mark its edges: the bus stop outside the clinic, the overnight shift, the food pantry, the nursing home, the school with the oldest building.

  3. Draw a line from each edge to the center, and on each line write one way that edge holds the center up.

  4. Choose one edge and act on it this month. Volunteer an afternoon, attend a local budget meeting and ask what is spent on care, or learn the name and story of one person whose work keeps things running.
