Definition from research

Chapter 2

  • The Egyptian Sailor may refer to the “Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor.” A shipwrecked traveller engages upon a spiritual endeavour or quest, journeying through the cosmos, to meet a primordial god, providing to the traveller a gift of moral vision with which to return to Egypt. Further, Richard Mathews writes that this "oldest fantasy text contains archetypal narrative of the genre: an uninitiated hero on a sea journey is thrown off course by a storm, encounters an enchanted island, confronts a monster, and survives, wiser for the experience…” The tale itself begins with a framing device in which an attendant ("follower" or "the sailor") tries to comfort his master, who is returning from an apparently failed expedition and is anxious about how the king will receive him. (The Land of Punt was an ancient kingdom, likely located in the Horn of Africa, that traded extensively with ancient Egypt. It was a source of valuable goods like gold, ebony, ivory, and aromatic resins, which were highly sought after by the Egyptians. While the exact location remains debated, current evidence points towards a coastal region encompassing parts of present-day Somalia, Djibouti, Eritrea, and northeast Sudan. The kingdom's existence is primarily known through Egyptian records and depictions, particularly from the Middle and New Kingdom periods.) An attendant reassures him, advising him on how to behave before the king, and repeating the proverb, “The mouth of a man saves him.” To encourage his master, he tells a tale of a previous voyage of his in which he overcame disaster, including meeting with a god and the king. The tale ends with the master saying, “Do not make the excellent (that is, do not act arrogantly) my friend; why give water to a goose (literally, bird) at dawn before its slaughtering in the morning?” A Middle Kingdom text, "The Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor," describes a star falling to earth, which led to the extinction of giant serpents on an island. This is sometimes interpreted as a folk memory of a meteorite impact as in the Netflix series Ancient Apocalypse. The sailor’s voyage appears to be a type of Otherworld Journey, a dangerous crossing-over to a divine or magical realm beyond the edge of the known world. “William K. Simpson observes that the Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor is ...one of the most interesting, straightforward, and yet puzzling compositions of ancient Egyptian literature. A curious emphasis is placed on numbers, the height of the wave, the dimensions of the ship, the number of sailors, the length of the serpent’s hood, the number of months of the sailor’s stay, and so on. It is almost as if the tale is an allegory involving the movement of stars. The Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor is considered to contain esoteric knowledge, much of it related to the serpent’s story. Whatever its meaning, this tragedy is enshrined as the central motif of the narrative: it is element X in a structure A-B-C-X-C'-B'-A'. Unlike all of the other motifs, its form is mythic rather than folkloric; it seems to be a religious allegory. As a mythic island (X) set in the dead centre of a sea of folktale elements (A-C and A'-C'), it may even be the “payload” of the whole story.”

Chapter 2

  • The number twelve has been significant in religions and nature. In nature there are twelve lunations in the solar year, in the full cycle of Jupiter, in the Western and Chinese zodiac. In ancient Greek there are twelve Olympians, twelve Titans, Hercules carries out twelve labors and Athena summons twelve citizens to sit as a jury which is still common to this day in a trial by jury in the US. In Christianity Ishmael has twelve sons and Jacob twelve sons who are the progenitors of the twelve tribes of Israel, twelve Apostles, in the Book of Revelation there are many references to the number twelve, and the twelve days of Christmas.

Chapter 2

  • Seven has many cultural and religious meanings: it is a significant number in almost every major religion (Christianity, God rested on the seventh day; seven symbolizes the unity of the four corners of the Earth with the Holy Trinity; it is repeated in the book of Revelations); it is associated with luck and magical properties; people retain roughly seven items of information in their short-term memory; our attention holds approximately seven pieces of information.

Chapter 3

  • The philosophy of the end of the world, or "apocalyptic philosophy," explores the concept of the world's end, often involving catastrophic events, societal collapse, or human extinction, and examines its implications for ethics, meaning, and our understanding of existence. Philosophers have considered various end-of-world scenarios, from natural disasters to societal collapse, prompting reflection on human mortality, the nature of time, and the value of life.

Chapter 5

  • “old fool gives up his sceptre and his crown of stars and his golden throne” - Scepter is a staff or wand held in the hand by a ruling monarch as an item of royal insignia. A crown of stars is a circle of stars often representing unity, solidarity and harmony in flags, seals, and signs and is also in motifs related to the Woman of the Apocalypse and Baroque allegorical art depicting the Crown of Immortality. The Crown of Immortality is a metaphorical symbolic representation of eternal life, often depicted in art and literature. It's a traditional symbol of honor, achievement, and immortality, often associated with religious or spiritual concepts. It can be visualized as a laurel wreath or a circle of stars. A golden throne symbolizes divine authority, high status, and opulence. In various cultures, it represents the power and majesty of rulers, deities, or figures of significance. The use of gold, a precious and revered metal, further enhances this symbolism, connecting it to concepts of wealth, divinity, and triumph. The phrase captures a theme of resistance to inevitable change, the stubbornness of old power figures, and the futility of clinging to decaying authority in a world that moves on. It underscores the chapter’s atmosphere of decay, entrapment, and social criticism expressed through the bus-driver’s bitter, drunken monologue.

Chapter 5

  • Jezebel is in the Book of Kings in the Hebrew Bible. She is known for violently purging the prophets of God from Israel and instituting the worship of Baal and Asherah. She was later put to death by defenestration or being thrown from a high place or window. In the Book of Revelations she is symbolically associated with false prophets. "Jezebel" in this context represents a symbol of moral judgment and inner conflict within the bus-driver's troubled psyche during a moment of dark reflection and suffering.

Chapter 5

  • Wormwood has several meanings in the bible: 1) Wormwood, known for its bitter taste, is used metaphorically to represent bitterness, affliction, remorse, and punitive suffering. 2) Wormwood symbolizes the bitter consequences of turning away from God, like idolatry, disobedience, and injustice. 3) In Revelation 8:10-11, the falling star called “Wormwood” symbolizes a catastrophic event, divine judgment, and the consequences of spiritual infidelity. 4) In Lamentations, wormwood is used to describe the profound grief and suffering caused by God’s judgment.

Chapter 13

  • “a trembling sea of glass”: “And before the throne there was a sea of glass like unto crystal” (Revelation 4:6).

Chapter 14

  • “the four white horses”: White horses in mythology are often associated with the sun chariot, warrior-heroes, fertility, and an end-of-time savior. The white horses symbolize the solemn, mystical journey of the soul from life into death and beyond, carrying Catherine through the shifting landscapes of memory, dream, and existence by her will. They are a powerful emblem of transition, purity, fate, and the ethereal nature of mortality in the novel. In Revelation, “behold a white horse: and he that sat on him had a bow; and a crown was given unto him” (Revelation 6:2).
  • “Which was alpha, and which was omega?”: “I am Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the ending, saith the Lord” (Revelation 1:8).
  • “incongruent harpers harping congruously”: “I heard the voice of harpers harping with their harps” (Revelation 14:2).

Chapter 15

  • “when the sky rolls back like a scroll”: “And the heaven departed as a scroll when it is rolled together” (Revelation 6:14).
  • “harpers harping on those seas of glass”: “And I saw as it were a sea of glass mingled with fire… having the harps of God” (Revelation 15:2).

Chapter 17

  • “Myriads of dark horsemen thundered against a sky”: The four horsemen of Revelation 6.
  • “The stars fell like dried seed pods upon a dark and barren plain”: “And the stars of heaven fell unto the earth, even as a fig tree casteth her untimely figs” (Revelation 6:13).

Chapter 20

  • “those who dwell in hidden caves beneath the overhanging, moss-grown rocks”: The kings and great men “hid themselves in the dens and in the rocks of the mountains; And said to the mountains and rocks, Fall on us” (Revelation 6:15-16).

Chapter 21

  • “God’s crown which is a crown of stars”: Compare the woman clothed with the sun, “upon her head a crown of twelve stars” (Revelation 12:1).

Chapter 22

  • “The first thrashing had its origin, I have no doubt, in heaven, and one fell”: “And there was war in heaven… and the great dragon was cast out” (Revelation 12:7-9). The dragon’s “tail drew the third part of the stars of heaven” (Revelation 12:4), as he goes “dragging the stars with him.”
  • “Where are those towers of gold which God created before creation officially began? Where is that sea of jasper and of light?”: “A sea of glass like unto crystal” (Revelation 4:6), and “the building of the wall of it was of jasper” (Revelation 21:18).

Chapter 29

  • “God, it had been said, was the True Vine”: The list of ways that refer to God and/or Jesus holds deep symbolic and thematic significance. It encapsulates the multifaceted nature of the divine as understood within Christian theology and mysticism. The significance of this enumeration lies in its portrayal of God/Jesus as an entity with many aspects and attributes, each reflecting different dimensions of spiritual truth and human experience. It emphasizes the complexity and paradox inherent in the divine nature: simultaneously merciful and just, immanent and transcendent, suffering and triumphant. This multiplicity mirrors the novel’s broader themes of ambiguity, multiplicity of selves, and the intertwining of life and death. The list serves to underscore the novel’s exploration of spiritual reality versus human perception. The divine is depicted not as a singular, easily definable being but as a presence that manifests in diverse and sometimes contradictory ways, much like the fragmented identities and overlapping realities experienced by the characters such as Mr. Spitzer and Vera’s mother, Catherine. This reflects the novel’s preoccupation with the elusive nature of truth, the complexity of existence, and the blurred boundaries between reality and illusion. By invoking these various titles, the text taps into a rich tradition of Christian symbolism, inviting readers to contemplate the depth of spiritual meaning behind everyday experiences and the mysterious workings of grace, suffering, and redemption. It situates the characters’ personal struggles within a cosmic framework, suggesting that their lives are part of a larger divine narrative that encompasses both the mortal and the eternal. This list also contrasts with the practical, grounded character of Miss MacIntosh, who embodies common sense and reality, as opposed to the more mystical and dreamlike experiences of other characters. The divine names remind us of the novel’s tension between the earthly and the spiritual, the seen and the unseen, the real and the illusory. The list is a poetic and theological meditation on the nature of divinity, serving as a thematic focal point that enriches the novel’s exploration of identity, faith, and the search for meaning amidst uncertainty and paradox.

Chapter 34

  • “Was Cousin Hannah the first horseman? Was she the second”: The Four Horsemen of Revelation 6.
  • “seas of fire, seas of ice, seas of glass”: “A sea of glass mingled with fire” (Revelation 15:2).

Chapter 35

  • “a plague of fiery locusts, those who have no king”: “The locusts have no king, yet go they forth all of them by bands” (Proverbs 30:27). The locusts of Revelation do have one: “they had a king over them, which is the angel of the bottomless pit” (Revelation 9:11).

Chapter 36

  • “hailstones as big as cannon balls”: “And there fell upon men a great hail out of heaven, every stone about the weight of a talent” (Revelation 16:21).
  • “Had not the heavens divided, splitting apart”: “And the heaven departed as a scroll when it is rolled together” (Revelation 6:14) marks the destruction of the old world order, as the heavens are seen as being destroyed and replaced with a new creation. Surah Ar-Rahman (55:37) describes the heavens splitting apart and becoming rose-red like burnt oil. Others read this metaphorically as a profound spiritual or emotional transformation, where the old self is broken down to make way for something new. At Jesus’s baptism by John the Baptist, “he saw the heavens opened, and the Spirit like a dove descending upon him” (Mark 1:10). It serves as an emblematic expression of cataclysmic change, upheaval, and transition from one state to another, highlighting the novel’s exploration of mortality, spiritual crisis, and the thin boundary between life and death.
  • “beyond the pearly gates”: “And the twelve gates were twelve pearls” (Revelation 21:21).
  • “There was no sea”: “And I saw a new heaven and a new earth: for the first heaven and the first earth were passed away; and there was no more sea” (Revelation 21:1).

Chapter 38

  • “Babylon is fallen”: “Babylon the great is fallen, is fallen” (Revelation 18:2). It symbolizes the collapse of old worlds and the onset of a new, uncertain reality: one marked by fragmentation, loss, and the haunting presence of what once was but is no longer. It is a metaphor for the disintegration of familiar orders and the inescapable passage into a realm where past certainties give way to mystery and ambiguity.

Chapter 39

  • “the seven-headed monsters wearing golden turret crowns”: The seven-headed monsters may refer to the beast from the sea: “and saw a beast rise up out of the sea, having seven heads and ten horns, and upon his horns ten crowns” (Revelation 13:1). In the Sumerian religion it was killed by Ninurta and hung on his chariot. These monsters are part of the rich symbolic and mythological tapestry of the novel, representing the complex and often overwhelming forces, both internal and external, that the characters face. They emphasize the epic and surreal nature of struggle and the broader themes of conflict, transformation, and endurance in the story.

Chapter 40

  • “harpists harping on a sea of glass”: “And I saw as it were a sea of glass mingled with fire… stand on the sea of glass, having the harps of God” (Revelation 15:2).

Chapter 41

  • “someone whose eyes were the moon and the sun”: “His eyes were as a flame of fire” (Revelation 1:14), and the mighty angel’s “face was as it were the sun” (Revelation 10:1).

Chapter 42

  • “through the pearly gates”: “And the twelve gates were twelve pearls” (Revelation 21:21).
  • “each who dies must die twice”: “And death and hell were cast into the lake of fire. This is the second death” (Revelation 20:14).

Chapter 46

  • “If he knew not her omega, then how did he know her alpha?”: “I am Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the ending, saith the Lord” (Revelation 1:8).

Chapter 49

  • Alpha and Omega in the New Testament is used as a Christian symbol. “I am Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the ending, saith the Lord” (Revelation 1:8); it means that Christ has existed for all eternity and that God is eternal.
  • “as if the sea gave up its dead”: “And the sea gave up the dead which were in it” (Revelation 20:13).

Chapter 50

  • “an empire enduring a thousand years”: The Nazis called their state the Thousand-Year Reich; it lasted twelve years, 1933 to 1945. In Revelation the saints reign with Christ “a thousand years” (Revelation 20:4).
  • “many pages of the book of life had been transparent”: “And I saw the dead, small and great, stand before God; and the books were opened: and another book was opened, which is the book of life” (Revelation 20:12).

Chapter 52

  • “like the sea giving up its dead”: “And the sea gave up the dead which were in it; and death and hell delivered up the dead which were in them: and they were judged every man according to their works” (Revelation 20:13, KJV).
  • “Who heard the knock of her dead heart?”: “Behold, I stand at the door, and knock: if any man hear my voice, and open the door, I will come in to him, and will sup with him, and he with me” (Revelation 3:20, KJV). Here the knock comes from within, and no one opens. The phrase is a poetic metaphor for the character’s profound emotional isolation and the silent pleas of her wounded spirit that no one else perceives or answers.

Chapter 53

  • “Death rode a stumbling nag”: In Revelation, “I looked, and behold a pale horse: and his name that sat on him was Death, and Hell followed with him” (Revelation 6:8, KJV), and the swift horse of the archer is the white horse of Revelation 6:2: “he that sat on him had a bow” (KJV). The stumbling nag keeps the pale horse’s meaning, signifying the arrival of death and its accompanying consequences, but with an added emphasis on a slower, less forceful, or more uncertain approach. This imagery emphasizes a sense of the fragility or the unpredictable nature of death. It is a vivid metaphor conveying the imperfect, faltering, and perhaps unpredictable nature of death. The image of death riding a “stumbling nag” (a nag being an old or worn-out horse) suggests that death is not a graceful, certain, or dignified force but one that is awkward, uneven, and uncertain in its approach or effect. In the context of the chapter, which is deeply concerned with mysteries, dreams, identities, and the blurred boundaries between life and death, this metaphor emphasizes the theme that death is not a neat or final event but an ambiguous, elusive presence that stumbles through the lives of the characters. It aligns with the overall tone of the narrative that questions reality, identity, and the nature of existence itself. This metaphor also complements the broader imagery in the chapter of confusion, fading identities, and the overlapping of shadows and realities. Death, like the other great uncertainties and mysteries is portrayed as something that is at once inevitable and yet strangely unstable, not easily grasped or understood. It is part of the blurred, dreamlike world where distinctions between life and death, reality and dream, are not clear-cut
  • “a man who was a Bridge of Sighs, a Bridge of Lies, a lake of fire”: Bridge of Sighs is a bridge in Venice, Italy. Built about 1600 over the Rio di Palazzo, it led prisoners from the Doge’s Palace to the prisons, and their sighs at a last view of Venice gave it its name. Bridge of Lies is a legendary pedestrian bridge in Sibiu, Transylvania, Romania. Lake of fire is a place of after-death punishment in Egyptian and Christian religions. “And death and hell were cast into the lake of fire. This is the second death” (Revelation 20:14, KJV).

Chapter 61

  • “there were these two deaths which every man must die”: “And death and hell were cast into the lake of fire. This is the second death” (Revelation 20:14, KJV).
  • “when the graves gave up their dead”: “And the sea gave up the dead which were in it; and death and hell delivered up the dead which were in them” (Revelation 20:13, KJV). Young’s trumpets “at the four corners of the clouded sky” recall the “four angels standing on the four corners of the earth” (Revelation 7:1, KJV).
  • “Should that be added which had never been?”: “If any man shall add unto these things, God shall add unto him the plagues that are written in this book: And if any man shall take away from the words of the book of this prophecy, God shall take away his part out of the book of life” (Revelation 22:18-19, KJV).

Chapter 63

  • “she preferring either the light of the Apocalypse or of Genesis”: “And God said, Let there be light: and there was light” (Genesis 1:3, KJV); “And the city had no need of the sun, neither of the moon, to shine in it: for the glory of God did lighten it” (Revelation 21:23, KJV).
  • “The sun and the moon will shine in the same sky”: “The sun shall be turned into darkness, and the moon into blood, before the great and the terrible day of the LORD come” (Joel 2:31, KJV).

Chapter 65

  • “as the sheep from the goats, as the wheat from the tares”: “And he shall separate them one from another, as a shepherd divideth his sheep from the goats” (Matthew 25:32, KJV); the wheat and the tares are in Matthew 13:24-30.
  • “One woe is past”: “One woe is past; and, behold, there come two woes more hereafter” (Revelation 9:12, KJV).
  • “Babylon was sunken into the sea”: Babylon, in the Bible, is often associated with a great city symbolizing sin, decadence, and divine judgment. To say “Babylon was sunken into the sea” alludes to the biblical theme of the downfall and destruction of corrupt civilizations or cities as an act of divine punishment. Mr. Bonebreaker uses this imagery to emphasize the idea that great, sinful places and systems will be destroyed or overwhelmed, just as Babylon was in biblical prophecy, reflecting his belief in impending judgment and the end of the world. “And a mighty angel took up a stone like a great millstone, and cast it into the sea, saying, Thus with violence shall that great city Babylon be thrown down” (Revelation 18:21, KJV).

Chapter 68

  • “The Tavern, built in 1827 by God’s servants who were believers in the lamb”: The town is New Harmony, Indiana, the setting of Angel in the Forest (1945). “Very astute readers of people who have been there know that it is the same town to which Vera arrives. I left out all utopian references. It’s the same town exactly” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983). The bus “stops before an old hotel where there is a tattooed landlord. And my heroine meets her first encounter with rural reality as a hangman who tells of his excellent fruit orchards. These characters appear in both books” (Marguerite Young, talk on the reissue of Angel in the Forest, 1966). George Rapp’s Harmony Society founded the town in 1814 and sold it to Robert Owen in 1825.

Chapter 69

  • “a wormwood”: Culturally wormwood has been used in the Christian Bible to connote something incredibly bitter. In the Book of Revelation it is used as the name of the star. In Hamlet, he uses the word wormwood to describe the situation he believes is going on with his family. “And the name of the star is called Wormwood” (Revelation 8:11, KJV).

Chapter 72

  • “the skies had unrolled like a scroll on the last day”: “And the heaven departed as a scroll when it is rolled together” (Revelation 6:14, KJV).

Chapter 74

  • “an abyss into which she might fall, dragging the stars after her”: “And there appeared a great wonder in heaven; a woman clothed with the sun, and the moon under her feet, and upon her head a crown of twelve stars: And she being with child cried, travailing in birth. . . . And his tail drew the third part of the stars of heaven” (Revelation 12:1-2, 4, KJV). The dragon waits to devour her child.

Chapter 75

  • “she would be pregnant if the stars fell like leaves upon a rotted world”: “And the stars of heaven fell unto the earth, even as a fig tree casteth her untimely figs, when she is shaken of a mighty wind” (Revelation 6:13, KJV). Untimely figs are fruit dropped before it ripens.

Chapter 77

  • “she would be the doll, the scarlet woman”: “And the woman was arrayed in purple and scarlet colour… MYSTERY, BABYLON THE GREAT, THE MOTHER OF HARLOTS” (Revelation 17:4-5, KJV).
  • “and the sun blotted out and become as black as sackcloth of hair”: “And the sun became black as sackcloth of hair, and the moon became as blood” (Revelation 6:12, KJV).

Chapter 81

  • “a wild, raging storm between the cracking Poles”: The shipwreck described in this chapter is a powerful and symbolic event that carries deep thematic weight. The shipwreck is recounted primarily through the perspective of the little toy salesman, who is portrayed as the lone survivor of a catastrophic maritime disaster during a fierce storm at sea.
