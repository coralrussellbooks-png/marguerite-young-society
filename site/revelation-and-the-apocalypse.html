Chapter 2

  • The Egyptian Sailor may refer to the “Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor”. A shipwrecked traveller engages upon a spiritual endeavour or quest, journeying through the cosmos, to meet a primordial god, providing to the traveller a gift of moral vision with which to return to Egypt. Further, Richard Mathews writes that this "oldest fantasy text contains archetypal narrative of the genre: an uninitiated hero on a sea journey is thrown off course by a storm, encounters an enchanted island, confronts a monster, and survives, wiser for the experience…” The tale itself begins with a framing device in which an attendant ("follower" or "the sailor") tries to comfort his master, who is returning from an apparently failed expedition and is anxious about how the king will receive him. (The Land of Punt was an ancient kingdom, likely located in the Horn of Africa, that traded extensively with ancient Egypt. It was a source of valuable goods like gold, ebony, ivory, and aromatic resins, which were highly sought after by the Egyptians. While the exact location remains debated, current evidence points towards a coastal region encompassing parts of present-day Somalia, Djibouti, Eritrea, and northeast Sudan. The kingdom's existence is primarily known through Egyptian records and depictions, particularly from the Middle and New Kingdom periods.) An attendant reassures him, advising him on how to behave before the king, and repeating the proverb, “The mouth of a man saves him.” To encourage his master, he tells a tale of a previous voyage of his in which he overcame disaster, including meeting with a god and the king. The tale ends with the master saying, “Do not make the excellent (that is, do not act arrogantly) my friend; why give water to a goose (literally, bird) at dawn before its slaughtering in the morning?” A Middle Kingdom text, "The Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor," describes a star falling to earth, which led to the extinction of giant serpents on an island. This is sometimes interpreted as a folk memory of a meteorite impact as in the Netflix series Ancient Apocalypse. The sailor’s voyage appears to be a type of Otherworld Journey, a dangerous crossing-over to a divine or magical realm beyond the edge of the known world. “William K. Simpson observes that the Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor is ...one of the most interesting, straightforward, and yet puzzling compositions of ancient Egyptian literature. A curious emphasis is placed on numbers, the height of the wave, the dimensions of the ship, the number of sailors, the length of the serpent’s hood, the number of months of the sailor’s stay, and so on. It is almost as if the tale is an allegory involving the movement of stars. The Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor is considered to contain esoteric knowledge, much of it related to the serpent’s story. Whatever its meaning, this tragedy is enshrined as the central motif of the narrative: it is element X in a structure A-B-C-X-C'-B'-A'. Unlike all of the other motifs, its form is mythic rather than folkloric; it seems to be a religious allegory. As a mythic island (X) set in the dead centre of a sea of folktale elements (A-C and A'-C'), it may even be the “payload” of the whole story.”

Chapter 2

  • The number twelve has been significant in religions and nature. In nature there are twelve lunations in the solar year, in the full cycle of Jupiter, in the Western and Chinese zodiac. In ancient Greek there are twelve Olympians, twelve Titans, Hercules carries out twelve labors and Athena summons twelve citizens to sit as a jury which is still common to this day in a trial by jury in the US. In Christianity Ishmael has twelve sons and Jacob twelve sons who are the progenitors of the twelve tribes of Israel, twelve Apostles, in the Book of Revelation there are many references to the number twelve, and the twelve days of Christmas.

Chapter 2

  • Seven has many cultural and religious meanings: it is a significant number in almost every major religion (Christianity, God rested on the seventh day; seven symbolizes the unity of the four corners of the Earth with the Holy Trinity; it is repeated in the book of Revelations); it is associated with luck and magical properties; people retain roughly seven items of information in their short-term memory; our attention holds approximately seven pieces of information.

Chapter 3

  • The philosophy of the end of the world, or "apocalyptic philosophy," explores the concept of the world's end, often involving catastrophic events, societal collapse, or human extinction, and examines its implications for ethics, meaning, and our understanding of existence. Philosophers have considered various end-of-world scenarios, from natural disasters to societal collapse, prompting reflection on human mortality, the nature of time, and the value of life.

Chapter 5

  • “old fool gives up his sceptre and his crown of stars and his golden throne” - Scepter is a staff or wand held in the hand by a ruling monarch as an item of royal insignia. A crown of stars is a circle of stars often representing unity, solidarity and harmony in flags, seals, and signs and is also in motifs related to the Woman of the Apocalypse and Baroque allegorical art depicting the Crown of Immortality. The Crown of Immortality is a metaphorical symbolic representation of eternal life, often depicted in art and literature. It's a traditional symbol of honor, achievement, and immortality, often associated with religious or spiritual concepts. It can be visualized as a laurel wreath or a circle of stars. A golden throne symbolizes divine authority, high status, and opulence. In various cultures, it represents the power and majesty of rulers, deities, or figures of significance. The use of gold, a precious and revered metal, further enhances this symbolism, connecting it to concepts of wealth, divinity, and triumph. The phrase captures a theme of resistance to inevitable change, the stubbornness of old power figures, and the futility of clinging to decaying authority in a world that moves on. It underscores the chapter’s atmosphere of decay, entrapment, and social criticism expressed through the bus-driver’s bitter, drunken monologue.

Chapter 5

  • Jezebel is in the Book of Kings in the Hebrew Bible. She is known for violently purging the prophets of God from Israel and instituting the worship of Baal and Asherah. She was later put to death by defenestration or being thrown from a high place or window. In the Book of Revelations she is symbolically associated with false prophets. "Jezebel" in this context represents a symbol of moral judgment and inner conflict within the bus-driver's troubled psyche during a moment of dark reflection and suffering.

Chapter 5

  • Wormwood has several meanings in the bible: 1) Wormwood, known for its bitter taste, is used metaphorically to represent bitterness, affliction, remorse, and punitive suffering. 2) Wormwood symbolizes the bitter consequences of turning away from God, like idolatry, disobedience, and injustice. 3) In Revelation 8:10-11, the falling star called “Wormwood” symbolizes a catastrophic event, divine judgment, and the consequences of spiritual infidelity. 4) In Lamentations, wormwood is used to describe the profound grief and suffering caused by God’s judgment.

Chapter 20

  • “those who dwell in hidden caves beneath the overhanging” - Revelations 6:15 This verse paints a picture of a desperate plea for protection from the consequences of God's judgment. It highlights the universal nature of this fear, encompassing all social classes and statuses. It refers metaphorically to people who live in obscurity, isolation, or marginalization. The "hidden caves" symbolize places of concealment or secrecy beneath "overhanging" structures, which can be understood as the dominant or visible parts of society or life. This imagery suggests a subterranean or shadowy existence away from the light and public view. It reflects themes in the novel of hidden lives, unacknowledged suffering, or secret struggles that exist beneath the surface of ordinary reality. It fits within the novel’s exploration of the boundary between the visible and invisible, the known and the unknown, and the living and the dead. The phrase evokes a sense of mystery and the unseen aspects of human existence that are integral to the story's atmosphere and Vera’s journey.

Chapter 20

  • The measurement of a reed can refer to a measuring reed in the Bible. It was six cubits long. The length of a cubit depended on whether it was a common or long cubit. A common cubit was 8.75 feet and a long cubit was 10.2 feet. The reed was used to measure the dimensions of the temple in Ezekiel’s vision. The idea of a measuring reed also appears in the Apocalypse.

Chapter 36

  • “Had not the heavens divided, splitting apart?” Revelations 6:14 - the destruction of the old world order, as the heavens are seen as being destroyed and replaced with a new creation and Surah Ar-Rahman 37-45 describes the heavens splitting apart and becoming rose-red like burnt oil. Other interpretations interpret this metaphorically as a profound spiritual or emotional transformation, where the old self is broken down to make way for something new. Mark 1:10 describes a moment during Jesus’s baptism by John the Baptist where the heavens were torn open and the Spirit descended upon him like a dove. It serves as an emblematic expression of cataclysmic change, upheaval, and transition from one state to another, highlighting the novel's exploration of mortality, spiritual crisis, and the thin boundary between life and death.

Chapter 40

  • “Defender of the faith” is a title traditionally held by the British monarch, symbolizing their role as the supreme governor of the Church of England. The title was originally granted by Pope Leo X to Henry VIII in 1521 for his defense of the Catholic Church against Martin Luther. While the title was later revoked by the papacy, it was reestablished by Parliament and continues to be used by the monarch today. It symbolizes the religious zeal and moral authority Mr. Bonebreaker claims as he tries to guide Miss MacIntosh (and symbolically the reader) toward salvation and moral order in the face of an impending apocalypse. This phrase also serves to underscore the tension between faith and practicality, belief and skepticism, a recurring theme throughout the novel.

Chapter 52

Chapter 53

  • "Death rode a stumbling nag" - Revelation 6:8 "Death rode a pale horse," signifying the arrival of death and its accompanying consequences, but with an added emphasis on a slower, less forceful, or more uncertain approach. This imagery emphasizes a sense of the fragility or the unpredictable nature of death. It is a vivid metaphor conveying the imperfect, faltering, and perhaps unpredictable nature of death. The image of death riding a "stumbling nag" (a nag being an old or worn-out horse) suggests that death is not a graceful, certain, or dignified force but one that is awkward, uneven, and uncertain in its approach or effect. In the context of the chapter, which is deeply concerned with mysteries, dreams, identities, and the blurred boundaries between life and death, this metaphor emphasizes the theme that death is not a neat or final event but an ambiguous, elusive presence that stumbles through the lives of the characters. It aligns with the overall tone of the narrative that questions reality, identity, and the nature of existence itself. This metaphor also complements the broader imagery in the chapter of confusion, fading identities, and the overlapping of shadows and realities. Death, like the other great uncertainties and mysteries is portrayed as something that is at once inevitable and yet strangely unstable, not easily grasped or understood. It is part of the blurred, dreamlike world where distinctions between life and death, reality and dream, are not clear-cut

Chapter 59

  • “whirling darkness when earth itself and all the waters of earth rang like this dark bell” evokes a profound cosmic and natural imagery. The "whirling darkness" suggests a tumultuous, almost apocalyptic atmosphere as an environment charged with overwhelming forces, possibly reflecting internal turmoil or existential dread. The earth and all its waters ringing "like this dark bell" personifies the planet and its oceans as resonating with a somber, mournful sound. This ringing bell imagery connotes death, forewarning, or a call to attention, reinforcing the motif of mortality and transition that permeates the chapter.

Chapter 63

  • "Heaven preserve you from a beardless man. Heaven preserve you from a hairy woman." This opening line is a warning against unnatural or incomplete identities, possibly alluding to the disruption of traditional gender roles or the loss of conventional human distinctions. The "beardless man" might symbolize immaturity or failure to fulfill societal or spiritual masculinity, while the "hairy woman" could indicate a departure from expected femininity or an overwhelming, chaotic force. The invocation of "Heaven preserve you" is a religious appeal for divine protection from these unsettling or undesirable conditions. "Consider well, my dear, if you mean what you are saying, if this is your infinite desire for nothing." Miss MacIntosh challenges the listener (Vera) to reflect carefully on whether their longing for "nothing", a void, emptiness, or perhaps death or nihilism is truly what they want. It suggests a deep questioning of desire, existence, and meaning. “She clapped the air with one hand. ‘A screech owl flew by just then. Did you not see it, hear it?’” The screech owl is a traditional symbol of death, omens, or the supernatural. Its sudden appearance emphasizes the gravity and ominous tone of the warning. The rhetorical question underscores awareness and perception, suggesting that profound or unsettling truths may go unnoticed. "The sun and the moon will shine in the same sky, and yet the earth will not be lighted but will be wrapped in darkness like the night." This line juxtaposes the presence of celestial bodies (sun and moon), which symbolize light, guidance, and life, with the paradoxical image of the earth being wrapped in darkness. It could imply a world where natural order and illumination exist but fail to reach or enlighten humanity, reflecting spiritual darkness despite physical light. "All earth will be made bare like this. No birds will sing in the tree-tops ever again. There shall be bare and ruined choirs." The "bare and ruined choirs" is a direct allusion to John Milton's Sonnet 7 ("...the bare and level plains, / The ruin of the ancient cities..."), evoking desolation, decay, and the loss of harmony or divine order. The silence of birds, which often symbolize the soul or spiritual joy, suggests a profound loss of life or spirit. "The flower will perish while it is still in the seed. The tides will not return what the tides take out." These images symbolize premature death or failure to reach potential ("flower perishing in the seed") and irreversible loss ("tides will not return what the tides take out"). They evoke the idea that some losses are final and natural cycles of renewal are broken. "There will be only, my dear, the dream." The closing line suggests that after all the destruction and loss, only the "dream" remains, possibly illusion, memory, or an insubstantial reality. It can be interpreted as a reflection on the ephemeral nature of life and the dominance of subjective perception or fantasy when the physical world fails. The invocation of "Heaven" and "Heaven preserve you" draws on Judeo-Christian imagery. The allusion to "bare and ruined choirs" connects to Milton, whose work is steeped in Christian theology and themes of the Fall and apocalypse. The presence of the sun, moon, and the view of the earth wrapped in darkness resonates with biblical apocalyptic imagery, such as the darkening of the sun and moon in the Book of Revelation signaling the end times. The repeated themes of loss, desolation, and the finality of death align with Christian meditation on mortality, sin, and the hope or despair beyond life. This passage is a somber meditation on loss, identity, and the tenuous hold on reality, filled with symbolic and religious imagery that suggests a spiritual or existential crisis. Miss MacIntosh’s words warn of the dangers of embracing "nothingness" or illusion without acknowledging the profound reality of life and death.

Chapter 70

  • “cherub wrapped in a bath towel… or a lion’s skin” Cherubim are in the books of Ezekiel and Revelation. They are majestic beings with four faces (man, lion, ox, eagle) representing divine power, majesty, and guardianship of God’s presence. The lion face signifies strength and kinship, the ox represents service, the man wisdom, and the eagle swiftness, with their overall form symbolizing God’s all-encompassing glory and authority. A lion’s skin evokes strength, majesty, and mythic power (recalling, for example, the Nemean lion’s skin worn by Hercules). The uncertain reference “was it a bath towel or a lion’s skin?” suggests a blurring between fragility and pretension, innocence and grandeur, reality and illusion. Applied to Mrs. Hogden, who is described as fat, oddly adorned, and carrying a confusing assortment of objects, this phrase underscores her contradictory character: she tries to present herself with an air of dignity and importance (like a lion’s skin) but is also pathetic and vulnerable (like a cherub wrapped in a bath towel). It reflects the novel’s themes of illusion, identity, and the tension between appearance and reality. The phrase metaphorically captures the complexity and ambiguity of Mrs. Hogden’s presence as part farcical, part tragic, both grand and diminished which highlights her as a figure caught between opposing qualities and uncertain roles within the household and narrative.

Chapter 81

  • The storm and subsequent sinking of the ship represent a cosmic upheaval, described as "a wild, raging storm between the cracking Poles," with the sea rising "in billows to the dark heavens hammering," and the moon and stars falling like sparks and rain of blood. This imagery evokes an apocalyptic vision of the world’s end, suggesting the collapse of order and the overwhelming power of nature and fate.
