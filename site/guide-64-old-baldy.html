Research notes: Chapter 64

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Lorena · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Decay and Ruin · Motherhood, Pregnancy, and Birth

Symbols: Hair, Wig, and Baldness · Pregnancy, Birth, and Children

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Ecclesiastes · Greek Mythology · Music and Composers · Suicide in History and Literature · The Bible

Chapter 63 gave you the moment the wig came off. Chapter 64 is what comes after: Miss MacIntosh, still bald-headed, still in the fog, telling Vera the story of her entire life. And Vera, for once, listening, and beginning to understand something Miss MacIntosh cannot see about herself.

This is one of the most tender chapters in the novel.

What actually happens

Miss MacIntosh talks. She tells Vera about her childhood baldness, her wigs made from stolen horsehairs, the barber who told her nothing could be done, the boys who tried to hook her wig with fishing lines, the phrenologist who praised the magnificent shape of her skull, the Gaelic prince she claims as an ancestor, and the two orphans from her years as a matron: a forger who sent her Christmas cards from Alcatraz, and Lorena, the dancing girl she loved.

Vera listens. And somewhere in the listening, she realizes she can see Miss MacIntosh in a way that Miss MacIntosh cannot see herself. She begins, quietly, to compose an elegy for her.

At the end of the chapter, they are in the last month they will have together.

Bald from birth

Miss MacIntosh was born without hair and has never grown any. As a child, she wore a toupee. Later she stole hairs from horses to make her own wig. Boys knew about it and taunted her: fishing lines thrown at her head to hook the hairpiece. She was afraid of animals and people both. She wandered.

She tells Vera about the barber she visited, the barber pole that once meant bloodletting and leeches, the barber's eloquent helplessness in the face of her condition: there was nothing to be done, not one hair to be grown. She accepted it. She had to.

And then she traces her baldness back into legend: she is descended, she says, “from a Gaelic prince who was” also bald, who lost his golden crown at sea, who was “cast up on the bleak shores of Scotland” with nothing. His crown floated on the waves. His hair was gone. She claims this man as her ancestor and his loss as her inheritance.

This is not delusion. It is dignity. Miss MacIntosh is doing what humans have always done with their pain: finding it a lineage, giving it a history, refusing to let it be merely random. If she is bald, then she is bald like a prince. If she has no crown, then neither did he, and he survived it.

Lorena

Of all the orphans Miss MacIntosh cared for in her years as a matron, she loved only two well enough to keep in contact: a forger imprisoned at Alcatraz, who sent her beautifully decorated Christmas cards every year with signatures ranging “from Abraham Lincoln to William Jennings Bryan”; and Lorena.

Lorena was a dancing girl: dark, beautiful, with long hair like "the midnight curtain concealing the star until the star was revealed." She laughed at Miss MacIntosh when they first met, and then ran away, and eventually became a strip-tease dancer. Years later she was paroled into Miss MacIntosh's care, and she ran away again, and Miss MacIntosh could not pursue her because pursuing was not what a parole officer could do.

Lorena lived in the spotlight. Miss MacIntosh lived in the shadow. Between them, every possible trajectory of womanhood: the one who displayed herself and the one who concealed herself, the one who slept with many men and the one who learned to live without protection, the one who danced in the light and the one who saved on electric lights because she hated what they stood for.

Miss MacIntosh reflects that Lorena once invited her to join the strip-tease act, believing she would have made a sensational performer. The image is almost unbearably poignant: bald Miss MacIntosh, in a spotlight. The woman who spent her life keeping her wig on, imagined as the woman who takes everything off.

She says they were "very dear friends" for a time.

Vera sees what Miss MacIntosh cannot

This is the chapter's turning point, and Young handles it with great delicacy. As Miss MacIntosh speaks, Vera begins to perceive something: she can see Miss MacIntosh, in a way that Miss MacIntosh cannot see herself. She sees the courage inside the practicality, the loneliness inside the sternness, the love inside the woman who said love is "a system of mutual deception."

She sees the pale narcissus: that image appears here: a flower breaking through rock, fragile and improbable, insisting on blooming in barren ground. That is what Vera sees when she looks at Miss MacIntosh with her wig off. Not the exposure. The persistence.

And she begins composing her elegy. Not out loud. Internally. The way Mr. Spitzer composes music that no one hears. The way everything in this novel that matters most happens in the interior, where only the composer knows it exists.

"I wandered. I wandered on and on. I broke my nose. I lost my breast."

Miss MacIntosh compresses her life into a few spare sentences that Young gives her without decoration. She wandered from job to job, from city to city. She broke her nose: how, she doesn't fully explain. She lost her breast to a mastectomy. She had no place. She walked into the traffic flow.

That last detail: walking into traffic. Your notes suggest this may have been her first attempt at suicide. Young doesn't say so directly. She leaves it open, the way she leaves everything open: as something that might have been accident, might have been intention, might have been the moment between them that she survived without understanding why. Miss MacIntosh, who stood for reality against illusion, for life against dream, for the practical against the imagined; she also walked into traffic once, and did not die.

She learned, after all that, to live without protection. For a woman of her era, this is no small statement. Without a father's household and without a husband's, a woman of Miss MacIntosh's time had nothing in a very literal sense: no social standing, no legal protection, no safety net of any kind. She built her own shelter out of practicality and an umbrella and a wig made from stolen horsehair. She carried herself through the world with the one thing she could control: her willingness to face it.

"Vanity, vanity, all is vanity"

Miss MacIntosh reaches for Ecclesiastes in this chapter: the Preacher's great cry that everything under the sun is empty, fleeting, futile. Wealth, wisdom, pleasure, effort: all passes. All comes to nothing.

In Miss MacIntosh's mouth this is not despair. It is realism. She has never needed illusions to sustain her; she has sustained herself on reality alone. If reality is ultimately vanity, that is not a reason to stop. You knit your bones together even though they unravel faster than you can knit. You carry your umbrella. You go.

The last month

The chapter closes with the knowledge that this is Vera and Miss MacIntosh's last month together. Everything that has just been said (the baldness, Lorena, the wandering, the traffic) has been said in the shadow of ending. Miss MacIntosh knew, when she removed her wig in Chapter 63, that she was running out of time for pretense. Now the time itself is visibly running out.

Vera will lose the only person who ever told her the truth. And she is already, silently, beginning to mourn.

What to hold onto

Miss MacIntosh, bald-headed, telling Vera the story of the Gaelic prince who lost his crown at sea, and the dancing girl who ran away, and the forger who sent Christmas cards from Alcatraz. And Vera, listening, seeing her clearly for the first time, and beginning to understand that this is an elegy she is composing, even now, even while Miss MacIntosh is still speaking.
