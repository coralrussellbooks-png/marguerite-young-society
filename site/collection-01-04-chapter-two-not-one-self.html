Chapter Two: Not One Self

Part of Radical Immanence Collection, on Radical Immanence, the philosophy Coral Russell draws from the work of Marguerite Young (1908 to 1995).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Pillar: Pillar 1 Not One Self · Across every book

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

The first thing Young asks you to give up is the one you are most attached to. Not your beliefs, not your certainties, not even your story about where you came from and what it made you. She asks you to give up the assumption underneath all of those: that there is a you doing the believing, the knowing, the remembering. A stable center that receives experience and integrates it into something coherent and continuous. A self that, whatever it has been through, remains fundamentally itself.

This is the assumption philosophy has protected most ferociously. It is the assumption Descartes was protecting when he stripped everything else away and found the thinking thing that could not be doubted. It is the assumption Kant was protecting when he built the transcendental subject, the organizing "I" that makes experience possible by being prior to it. It is the assumption that runs underneath liberal political theory, psychoanalysis, developmental psychology, and most of what passes for common sense about human identity. You are someone. That someone persists. That persistence is what makes you responsible for your actions, capable of relationship, legible to others and to yourself.

Young does not argue against this assumption.

She does something more unsettling.

She builds a world in which you can watch it fail.

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling by Marguerite Young opens on a journey. Vera is traveling by Grey Goose bus from the east coast toward Indiana, toward the town of What Cheer, toward the woman who raised her and then disappeared into the water one morning, leaving her wig and her clothes and her sea boots filled with sand and her broken black umbrella and her copy of Pilgrim's Progress and her eye-glasses and her purse with two pennies in it. The novel is, in one sense, the account of that journey. In another and more accurate sense it is the account of everything that cannot be accounted for by a journey with a beginning and a destination.

Vera is not one person across those 1,198 pages. She is not unreliable in the way that literary criticism uses the term, meaning a narrator whose account we must read against the grain to find the truth underneath. There is no truth underneath, and that is not Vera's limitation. It is Young's central claim. Vera contains the opium world of her mother Catherine, a world as internally consistent and as saturated with meaning as any consensus reality the novel offers. Vera contains the rigid certainties of Miss MacIntosh herself, the Indiana pragmatist who turned out to be as constructed as everything she condemned. Vera contains her own grief, which does not process or resolve but accumulates, which is how grief actually works and not how we prefer to narrate it.

What Young is showing, across the full impossible length of the novel, is that this is not Vera's particular psychological condition. It is the condition. The plurality is not a symptom. It is the structure.

Catherine's opium world is the clearest demonstration of this. The critical temptation, and it is a temptation Young is aware of and refuses to satisfy, is to read Catherine's world as delusion and the Indiana plains as reality, to organize the novel around the axis of true and false, sane and mad, grounded and unmoored. Young dismantles that axis not by arguing against it but by giving Catherine's world the same density, the same internal logic, the same weight of accumulated detail as anything else in the novel. The opium world is not thin. It is not a distortion of something more real. It is another reality, sustained by a self that is genuinely living in it, and the self that lives in it is as real as any other self Catherine contains.

This is the philosophical move. Not the claim that all realities are equal in some lazy relativist sense. The claim that the self is not the kind of thing that lives in one reality. The self is the kind of thing that lives in several realities simultaneously, navigating between them with varying degrees of awareness and control. The demand that it choose one and call that one the true self is a demand that amputates most of what the self actually is.

Miss MacIntosh made that demand. She was the one real thing in Vera's childhood, the empiricist in the house of illusion, the woman who insisted on the solid world against Catherine's dissolving one. And then her wig came off. And there was nothing underneath the solid woman but another constructed self, bald and false-toothed and one-breasted, as much a performance as anything Catherine ever dreamed. The real Miss MacIntosh was not more real. She was a different performance.

Young does not present this as a tragedy, though it contains tragedy. She presents it as information. This is what selves are. This is what you are working with. Now what?

Hume got closer than most to understanding this. Hume looked for the unified self in his own experience and found, famously, that he could never catch himself without a perception. He found the bundle, the stream of experiences with no experiencer underneath, and it troubled him enough that he retreated from the full implications. Young does not retreat. She builds a novel-length investigation of what the bundle actually looks like from the inside, which is something a philosophical treatise cannot do because it cannot sustain the investigation long enough or in the right form.

William James named the stream of consciousness and understood that the self was a process rather than a thing, that the "I" was always already in motion rather than stable behind its experiences. Young takes James seriously in a way that most of the tradition did not, extending the stream until it becomes clear that it does not flow in one direction, does not empty into a single sea. It branches and doubles back and runs underground and surfaces somewhere unexpected and is still, somehow, the water.

The thinker Young is most in dialogue with is Gertrude Stein, who was William James's student and whose investigation of repetition, of the continuous present, of identity as something that happens rather than something that is, runs parallel to Young's project with different formal tools. Where Stein strips language down to its mechanisms to show the self as a grammatical construction, Young piles language up until the construction becomes visible through its own excess. Different directions, the same destination: the self is not the noun. It is the verb.

The plural self is not the absent self. It is the self that is more than any single account of it can capture. It does not mean that accountability dissolves. It produces a more complicated picture of what it means to be responsible for actions taken by a self that is not singular and not fully transparent to itself. The plural self can be witnessed. It cannot be reduced.

The demand for singular selfhood is not just a philosophical error. It is a political technology. Every system that requires enemies requires a population of selves simple enough to be sorted: us and them, citizen and alien, deserving and undeserving. The more complex the actual population, the more pressure the system applies to force simplification. The people most damaged by this demand are the people whose plurality is most visible and most threatening to the sorting system. Young's plural self is not a therapy for that violence. It is a philosophical framework that names the mechanism producing it.

We keep coming back to the wig because Young keeps coming back to it. It is the novel's central image and it earns that centrality. Miss MacIntosh's wig ends up on the back of a crab and something in the world becomes permanently different for Vera. Not because the wig reveals a truth that was hidden. Because it reveals that the solid woman was a construction, which means the solid world the solid woman represented was a construction too, which means the opposition between Catherine's illusion and Miss MacIntosh's reality collapses, which means Vera has to find a way to live without the opposition she built her childhood on.

This is the first pillar. This is where Young begins. Everything else the system builds is built on this: the self is plural, the plurality is the condition, and the honest life is the one that stops trying to wig it back together.
