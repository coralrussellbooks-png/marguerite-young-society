Chapter 65

On Chapter 65 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Woman in Young's Magnum Opus.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Lorena · Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Titus Bonebreaker

Mr. Bonebreaker preaches at Miss MacIntosh through this chapter, and the body coming apart and the unmasking that follows carry it. He is the Gospel salesman who casts himself as her spiritual rescuer, demanding she give up "carnal lusts" and worldly ambition, and his controlling religiosity is the paternalistic salvation the book keeps setting against female autonomy, a man who would save a woman by governing her. Her wounds, the amputated breast and the baldness, are the body as the place the self comes apart, and her decision to show them to him before the marriage, to reveal the bald head and the scarred side rather than let the wig lie, is the unmasking made an act of honesty, the self stripped of its cover offered as the truth it is.

He flees the sight of her, and the umbrella goes with him, and the flight is the failure of male authority to accept the real woman under the mask, the man who preached salvation unable to look at what salvation would have to love. Miss MacIntosh rejects his God and his way of life after, and the rejection is her refusal of the whole apparatus that would sort her into shame, the same refusal she brings to jewels and empire and illusion.

The wigs she wears in different places and times, black in memory of Lorena and red in memory of her brother, are the self as mask worn in colors that hold her dead, plural selfhood carried on the head, and the Chinatown where she learns solitaire and finds hairlessness admired inverts the beauty standard the West wraps around her. The solitaire she plays alone, wishing for a "watcher" to see that no one cheats even against an invisible opponent, is a woman keeping vigil over a fairness the world will not keep for her.

She works among the blind and is content there, among people who cannot see her difference, and the blind who cannot see her baldness are invisibility and erasure turned for once into shelter, the severed sense that the book uses for erasure become a place a marginalized woman can be whole. Her drift toward seeking her lost brother among Polynesian head-hunters carries the head motif to the far side of the world, and the honeymooners' hotel where she registers as a single woman, alone among couples, is the social order that has no room for her name without a raised voice. The chapter leaves her standing apart on the water while Mr. Bonebreaker's voice booms invisibly over it, erased and enduring at once, the woman the arrangement cannot place and cannot quite make disappear.
