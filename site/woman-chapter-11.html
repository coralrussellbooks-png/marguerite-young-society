Characters: Catherine Cartwheel

Catherine's dream fills this chapter with guests, and the guests are all, at bottom, versions of her. The Queen Maud Mountains arrive as an imperious, aloof woman holding a parasol, and the face the parasol conceals is Catherine's own. The reflections in her crowded musical evenings have no faces. Rameses fragments into several selves before her. This is the feminized geography and the powerful, inaccessible woman, and it is plural selfhood, Catherine scattered so far across her visitors that she can be found in a mountain range and a pharaoh and a stranger's reflection but not in any single self called Catherine.

The dream is treated as a place with duties. Catherine frets as a "great hostess" over whether there are "not enough pyramid-shaped ices," and the fret is not comic relief. It is the labor of holding a whole country together, real and dream as one country, kept furnished and lit by a woman who lives nowhere else. Her guests are drawn from every era and are separated from one another by time, space, and death, and none of that keeps them from her table.

Catherine herself is trapped between life and death, and the chapter keeps her there. Sir Walter Raleigh's atheist propositions about God are welcome in the dream beside the pharaohs and the knights, because the dream admits everything and sorts nothing. The reflections without faces are the illusion of the individual seen from inside a mind that is dissolving: a room full of selves, not one of them separable, not one of them whole.
