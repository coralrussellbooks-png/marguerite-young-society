Chapter 58: The Silent Violinist

Research notes: Chapter 58

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Themes: Music, Sound, and Silence · The Search for Meaning

Symbols: Clothing, Hats, and Skirts · Music and Instruments

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Deafness and Sign Language · Music and Composers

Here is what happens in Chapter 58, stripped to its plainest bones: Mr. Spitzer, the large, black-caped, melancholy composer we've come to know across these hundreds of pages, encounters an old man on the library steps playing an imaginary violin. That's it. That is the entire external event of the chapter.

And yet, as you've learned by now, what appears to be almost nothing in this novel is usually everything.

What actually happens

Mr. Spitzer is passing the library when he sees an old violinist (white-haired, fingers trembling) drawing an imaginary bow across imaginary strings, performing music that makes no sound at all. And here is the first astonishing thing: Mr. Spitzer knows exactly what piece he is playing. He recognizes it immediately as an obscure minor composition from childhood, something almost entirely lost to modern ears. He hears what isn't there.

This is not presented as madness. It is presented as a form of grace.

Mr. Spitzer and music

You've been traveling with Mr. Spitzer for many chapters now, and you know by this point that he is a man whose life is interior: a "silent composer," a "walking scoreboard," someone who carries music within him without ever externalizing it in a form others can easily perceive. He is deaf, or near-deaf, which Young uses not as irony but as logic: the man most attuned to music's deepest nature is the one least dependent on its physical sound.

When Mr. Spitzer watches the old violinist, he is watching a version of himself. Both men make music that exists primarily in the mind and memory. Both are artists whose work goes largely unheard. The encounter isn't just moving; it's a recognition.

The music that isn't there

Young describes the silent performance with extraordinary richness: "singings of blind moths," bird calls, sea shells buzzing like bees, sparks sparking on strings that don't exist. This isn't contradiction. It's Young's deepest argument about what music actually is, not sound waves, but something that precedes sound and survives its absence.

She calls it "pre-musical music": music that existed before human consciousness learned to organize it, and that will persist after individual hearing ceases. The old violinist isn't playing badly or playing nothing. He is playing at the source. This is the chapter's great gift to you as a reader: by the time you've spent this many pages inside Young's prose, you've been trained to hear what isn't audible. You have been practicing for exactly this moment.

The question of whether he is alive

Mr. Spitzer watches the old man's wild, convulsive movements, the intensity of the performance, and cannot determine whether what he is witnessing is music at its highest expression or a man in the process of dying. Young refuses to resolve this. The violinist is in a liminal space, and so is Mr. Spitzer, and so, by this point in the novel, are you.

This ambiguity is not evasion. It is the chapter's central claim: that the boundaries between living and dead, between sound and silence, between reality and dream, are not where we think they are. Mr. Spitzer concludes the chapter by acknowledging that he no longer clearly distinguishes between reality and dreams, between life and death, and calls this indistinction his "only wisdom." The confusion is not failure. It is what he has learned.

The surprising turn: baseball

In the middle of contemplating silent cosmic music, Mr. Spitzer starts thinking about baseball. And billiards. And polo. And fishing. And kingship.

Don't let this throw you. What he is doing is imagining the old violinist as someone else entirely: what if this man had become an athlete instead of a musician? What if the instrument in his hand had been a bat, a cue, a fishing rod, a scepter? Reality, Mr. Spitzer reflects, is "made up of just so many sticks and not one stick more." The violinist is a violinist because of the particular stick he picked up. He could have been anyone.

This is not a joke. It is Young's persistent argument about identity: that the self is not fixed, that we are each a convergence of contingencies, that another version of any one of us could be doing something entirely different with the same essential soul.

Mr. Spitzer, who prides himself on his belief that music and baseball are "fused into his psychology," is not being eccentric here. He is being consistent. Both require practice, discipline, rhythm, and the willingness to perform within a collective. Both produce beauty that disappears the moment it's made.

His brother

Woven through Mr. Spitzer's observations of the violinist is his grief for his dead brother, and his sense that the dead continue to shape us, that they do not simply vanish. The violinist's music, nearly forgotten, nearly lost, keeps pulling Mr. Spitzer back toward the people and sounds that time is trying to erase.

This is one of the chapter's quieter sadnesses: that Mr. Spitzer knows exactly how obscure that old waltz is, how few people would recognize it, and still, it is completely vivid to him. The things we love do not always survive us into the world's memory. But they survive in us, for as long as we last.

The black cape

Notice, as you've been doing, what characters wear. Mr. Spitzer's black cape has appeared throughout the novel as his signature: his boundary marker, his way of moving through a world that is too much for him. Young describes his shadow drifting, his cape as a cloak of darkness he carries. He is not morbid so much as honest: he knows he is a liminal figure, a man equally at home among the living and the dead, and he has learned to dress accordingly.

What to hold onto

An old man on library steps, playing an instrument that isn't there, performing a waltz almost no one remembers, watched by a deaf composer who hears every note. Two men making invisible music together. This is what art does, Young is telling you: it creates connection across the impossible distances, between the living and the dead, between sound and silence, between one interior world and another.

You have been learning, across the length of this novel, to hear the music that isn't audible. Chapter 58 is Young acknowledging that you've learned it.
