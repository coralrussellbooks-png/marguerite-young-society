Chapter 9. The Making of an Orator

Part of a reading of Harp Song for a Radical (1999), Marguerite Young’s life of Eugene Victor Debs.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 8 · The Unfinished Harp: Reading Harp Song for a Radical · Chapter 10 →

A first speech, three visitors, and a single term in the legislature

Between roughly 1875 and 1885, Debs turned from a union clerk into a public man. The chapters from page 283 to 315 track that change through four scenes: his first speech, the visits of three famous reformers he brought to Terre Haute, his years as city clerk, and his one term in the Indiana legislature.

Liberty

His first speech, given at the Occidental Literary Club in Terre Haute, took liberty as its subject. Young says he chose it as "a surefire winner" that would let him speak for workers laid off the rails, "perhaps because they had broken their necks or had lost their hands or their feet" (283–284). She quotes a line of his that could stand for his whole career as a speaker: "No man ever made a great speech on a mean subject. Slavery never inspired one immortal thought or utterance" (284).

Young is honest that the ease came later. Debs, she writes, "learned to seem as natural as he inherently was" (284). The orator was built slowly.

Three visitors

Debs brought to Terre Haute three of the most controversial speakers in America, and Young gives each a chapter.

The first was Robert Ingersoll, the famous agnostic. The town was papered with warnings against him, and "some God-fearing citizens had been afraid to go out of doors" (287). Young admires Ingersoll's generosity. He kept a pocket full of coins for beggars.

The second was the abolitionist Wendell Phillips, in November 1878. Phillips had made his name decades earlier with a speech honoring Elijah Lovejoy, the antislavery editor killed by a mob in Illinois.

The third, in 1880, was Susan B. Anthony, and her visit is the most dramatic. No respectable hall would rent to her, so Debs rented a small, obscure room. When she arrived, a crowd gathered, "a shrieking, screaming, howling mob, the women fiercer than the men" (294). Debs guided her through it.

Young adds a remarkable detail. A botched operation years earlier had left Anthony with one eye permanently turned, and she preferred to walk with her good profile toward her companion. That day, she walked beside Debs "calmly keeping the right side toward the storming mob" (299).

This is The Margins Reveal the Truth, and also Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act. The scorned woman walks through the jeering crowd with her damaged face turned toward her enemies. The young union man walks with her.

The clerk and the legislator

Debs was elected city clerk in 1879 and again in 1881, the second time by a margin of only two votes (302). Young calls him a "very Tolstoyan city clerk who was likely to favor the poor and the oppressed" (190).

In 1884 he won a seat in the Indiana legislature. His record there was a series of defeats. His bill requiring railroads to compensate workers injured on the job passed the lower house and was wrecked in the upper (307). His bill for woman suffrage was "roundly defeated" (307). He crossed party lines to vote for a Republican bill to abolish all racial distinctions in Indiana law, and it failed by three votes (308).

In June 1885, he decided not to run again, disgusted with "pettifogging political hucksters who delayed progressive decisions for years and acted in behalf of the vested interests" (314).

What the lens shows

This section introduces a theme that will run to the end of Debs's life: Unfinished Is Honest as lived politics. Nearly every cause Debs took up in these years failed in the short run. Suffrage, compensation for injured workers, and legal racial equality all lost. All three would eventually become law, long after the votes that defeated them. Young does not tell that later history here, but her reader knows it. The failed bill turns out to be the first draft.

Checking the record

  • Elijah Lovejoy. Young writes that Lovejoy was murdered "while coming out of his newspaper office in August 1835" (292). Lovejoy was killed on November 7, 1837, in Alton, Illinois, while defending a warehouse where his new printing press was stored. Phillips's famous speech honoring him followed a month later.

  • Legislative dates. The book's chronology gives 1885 for Debs's legislative service. He was elected in November 1884 and served in the 1885 session, as the text implies.

  • Anthony's visit. Young's account of the hostile crowd and the rented room agrees in outline with other biographies of Debs.
