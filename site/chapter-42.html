To All My Darlings

Chapter 42

On Chapter 42 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 4, 2026

← Chapter 41 · Home · Chapter 43 →

Summary

Catherine dreams that she rides in her glass carriage behind the black coachman and his alabaster horses, through the sleeping house and the sleeping city, to bid farewell to the dying Cousin Hannah, though both ladies are bedridden and the visit can have happened only in a dream. Mr. Spitzer, who goes back and forth between the two dying ladies, is grateful for her dream. He keeps lists of lost ships, the Racehorse, the Arcturus, the Ptarmigan, and searches census rolls from which one person, he suspects himself, is always missing. In Hannah’s house of war trophies he finds pictures of her glory, the bloomer girl, the strike leader, the Arab king, and he learns that in the East she was Al Hamid, that she once left a bridegroom at the church door, and that a sheik sent her gold. He expects to find a secret man in her life and finds instead “a secret woman, a bride.” Hannah lies in a white room like a desert tent, her face covered and her feet bare. She does not know him, and she cries out for a pavilion to hide her from the storm.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a dreamlike passage describing Catherine’s journey in a glass carriage driven by a black coachman with alabaster horses, passing through surreal landscapes of mirrors, fountains, tapestries, and sleeping cities. This journey is symbolic of transition, memory, and farewell, as she travels to visit Cousin Hannah in a realm beyond ordinary time and space. Catherine’s visit is described as hallucinatory, almost otherworldly, blending dream and reality, emphasizing the fluidity of time and the nature of farewell as something that transcends physical existence.
  • Mr. Spitzer is preoccupied with lists of sunken and lost ships, the mystery of their disappearance, and the elusive nature of time and memory. This section metaphorically ties the fate of lost ships to the human experience of loss, disappearance, and the unknowability of final destinies. His meditation on finding coordinates, lost passengers, and the ocean’s endless mysteries parallels the chapter’s broader themes of searching for meaning amid oblivion.
  • Much of the chapter is devoted to Mr. Spitzer’s detailed remembrance and reflection on Cousin Hannah, the fading suffrage captain. Her life is portrayed as one of militant energy, passion, and struggle by leading crusades, breaking conventions, and battling societal and personal constraints. Yet now, in her old age, she is reduced to a fragile figure, almost unrecognizable, lying in a vast, tent-like death chamber filled with memories and trophies of battles past. The contrast between her former fiery strength and present weakness is poignant, illustrating the inevitability of decline and the loneliness of a warrior’s final moments.
  • Mr. Spitzer speculates on the hidden personal life of Cousin Hannah, suggesting a secret and perhaps tragic love life that contrasts with her public image of fierce independence and detachment. There is mention of a mysterious bridegroom who was left at the church door and rumors of a secret man or caliph who supported her cause, and in the end Mr. Spitzer finds “a secret woman, a bride.” This passage explores the tension between public persona and private vulnerability, and the elusive nature of love and companionship even for the strongest figures.
  • The closing passages focus on Mr. Spitzer’s visit to Cousin Hannah’s death chamber, where he confronts her mortality and the enigma of her spirit. The imagery of snow, white curtains, desert tents, and the blending of life and death create a surreal atmosphere. Mr. Spitzer reflects on the nature of death as a journey and the dissolution of identity. Despite her indifference and silence, he remains faithful, accompanying her in her final hours, contemplating the mystery of the soul, the possibility of resurrection, and the universal fate that awaits all.
  • Throughout the chapter, there are numerous philosophical reflections on time, memory, identity, and the interconnectedness of life and death. Mr. Spitzer’s musings on eternal recurrence, the blending of opposites, and the fragile boundary between dream and reality deepen the emotional and intellectual impact of the narrative. His lament over forgotten heroes and lost loves, and his sense of being a witness to fading glory, add a layer of melancholic grandeur.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “For no dream dies except into another dream.” What does Catherine’s visit prove, if it never happened?
  • “Farewell was a matter of opinion as to the time, the place, the person.” Has Mr. Spitzer ever said farewell to anyone?
  • “The sheath had outlived the sword.” What is left of Hannah?

Characters

  • Why does Mr. Spitzer study census reports “from which one person was always missing”?
  • Hannah led “the needle workers to strike albeit she was the owner of a mill.” What does the contradiction tell us about her?
  • Mr. Spitzer expects a secret man and finds “a secret woman, a bride.” Who might she be?

Connections

  • In Chapter 38 Hannah “had wept if she heard a skirt whirling.” Here she is “the great bloomer girl who had taken off her voluminous skirts.” How do the skirts connect?
  • Hannah lies “stretched out like a clouded landscape,” as Catherine in Chapter 41 was “stretched out like a clouded horizon.” How are the two dying ladies alike?
  • Mr. Spitzer leaves with “Good-night and good-morning.” Where else in the novel does an ending turn into a beginning?

Reflection

  • “No one remembered that war in which she had fought.” Why are some heroes forgotten?
  • Is a farewell that happens only in a dream still a farewell?
  • “Each who dies must die twice.” What is the first death?

Research: Characters

Cousin Hannah (C. H. Freemount-Snowden)

  • A formidable, militant suffrage leader whose life was dedicated to battling the conventions of love, society, and gender roles.
  • She is depicted as a warrior, storm king, and mystic figure, feared and revered but ultimately isolated and sorrowful in her final days.
  • Her identity spans multiple cultural representations (Hannah in Boston, Hamid in the East, and the Great Moor) symbolizing a universal warrior figure.
  • “Her last years had been empty. She had lived in those last fleeting years as one whom time had outdistanced.”
  • After the wedding that did not take place, she signed her name “C. H. Freemount-Snowden,” on the advice of a Boston numerologist.

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • She journeys in a glass carriage driven by spectral horses and a coachman, making a dreamlike farewell visit to Cousin Hannah.
  • Her journey transcends time and space, emphasizing the fluidity of reality and memory.
  • “She had not even leaned on her cane. She had needed no one to show her the way.”

Joachim Spitzer

  • An observer and quasi-legal figure who researches lost ships, heirs, and ancient records.
  • He represents the search for meaning, order, and connection amid chaos and oblivion.
  • He reflects on his own precarious existence and the mysteries surrounding Cousin Hannah.
  • “He was the ambassador from one world to another world although he doubtless never would know which ambassador he was, which world it was.”

The Unknown Bridegroom

  • A mysterious figure tied to Cousin Hannah by rumor and symbolism.
  • He represents lost love, abandonment, and unfulfilled relationships, adding a layer of personal tragedy to Hannah’s public life.

Research: Themes

The Fluidity of Time and Reality

See: Illusion and Reality · Time

  • The narrative challenges linear notions of time. Catherine’s journey is described as transcending death and time, visiting Cousin Hannah when both are bed-ridden or dead. Dreams and waking reality blur, reflecting a mystical view of existence where farewell is “a matter of opinion as to the time, the place, the person.”

Death and the Afterlife

See: Death and Mortality

  • Death is portrayed as both an end and a transformation or a continuation into another dream or realm. The chapter explores death’s mysteries, the dissolution of identity, and the hope or doubt surrounding resurrection and eternal life.

Memory and Legacy

See: Memory and the Past

  • Cousin Hannah’s fading fame and forgotten battles symbolize how history erases even the greatest struggles. Mr. Spitzer’s reflections reveal the tension between public recognition and private sorrow, as well as the difficulty of preserving true legacy.

Love and Isolation

See: Isolation and Connection · Love

  • Despite her militant exterior, Cousin Hannah harbors hidden loves and intimacies. The narrative hints at secret lovers, lost opportunities for human connection, and the loneliness of a life lived in opposition to societal norms.

Identity and Transformation

See: Identity and the Self

  • Hannah’s multiple identities (Hannah, Hamid, Al Hamid, Great Moor) and her shifting roles reflect the multiplicity of self and the transformative power of myth and legend. Mr. Spitzer’s own uncertainty about his identity parallels this theme.

The Search for Meaning Amid Chaos

See: Order, Chaos, and Discipline · The Search for Meaning

  • Mr. Spitzer’s obsession with lost ships and records symbolizes humanity’s quest for order and meaning despite the inherent chaos of existence. The lost ships, passengers, and forgotten histories mirror Cousin Hannah’s own forgotten life.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Glass Carriage and Alabaster Horses

See: Horses and Carriages

  • Symbolize the ethereal, dreamlike journey between worlds or states of being. The horses’ heads turning like foam and their journey through clouds evoke a passage beyond the physical.

Mirrors and Reflections

See: Mirrors and Reflections · The House

  • The house with rooms of mirrors and endless reflections represents the multiplicity of perspectives and the elusive nature of truth and self.

White Tent and Desert Imagery

  • Cousin Hannah’s death chamber is likened to a desert tent or pagoda, blending Eastern and Western symbols of the sacred and transient. The desert and snow imagery evoke isolation, purity, and the harshness of the final journey.

Lost Ships and Stars

See: The Sea and Water · Moon and Stars

  • Ships represent journeys, lost hopes, and the passage of souls. Stars symbolize guidance, memory, and the unreachable. Mr. Spitzer’s cataloging of lost ships parallels the attempt to map lost lives and loves.

Snow and Ice

See: Snow, Ice, and Winter

  • Metaphors for death, stillness, and the crystallization of time and memory. The persistent snow on Cousin Hannah’s face and body symbolizes the inevitability of death and the fading of life’s passions.

The Bridegroom and Withered Flowers

See: Roses and Flowers · Weddings and Funerals

  • Represent unfulfilled love and abandonment, contrasting with Cousin Hannah’s militant independence and loneliness.

Further notes from the text

  • Catherine’s carriage ride through the sleeping house is full of driving directions: turn right, turn left, follow the winding road, watch for the road’s edge and the clouded void. “One could sometimes hear her directing the wind.”
  • Mr. Spitzer keeps lists of lost ships, some vanished long before his birth, some sunk in calm seas for reasons never known. Hannah’s dying is set among these lost vessels.
  • He travels back and forth “between two dying ladies,” Catherine and Hannah, past sundials with their numbers blotted out by fog.
  • Hannah’s past is drawn as a military epic: armies riding into war with silken petticoats for flags, camp followers, ladies who left home and husband to follow her. “Always by indirection, she had broken the hearts of many men.”
  • The town gossips about an abandoned bridegroom from Hannah’s past: some say he died early of grief clutching withered flowers, others that he lived to a great age and never married.
  • Finding her wrapped in a white sheet, Mr. Spitzer first thinks he has come too late and is in her tomb, then remembers that in the East sleepers cover their faces. He decides he must behave as if he has crossed a far frontier.
  • He sees himself as the blind lighthouse keeper, the deaf bell-ringer, the star that does not see its own light, the gatekeeper who does not know which gates he keeps.

Research: Resources

  • “the four alabaster horses”: The four alabaster horses of the opening become “the eight long-necked horses” a few lines later. See Horses and Carriages and The Black Coachman.
  • “Watch out for the swan boats”: See the swan boats of the Boston Public Garden in the Chapter 4 resources.
  • “fields of mountain asphodel”: In the Odyssey, Hermes leads the souls of the dead suitors down to the meadow of asphodel, where the spirits of the dead dwell (Odyssey 24). See Homer’s Epics and Greek Mythology.
  • “an ancient pharaoh sleeping in a swan boat”: See the solar barque of Ra in the Chapter 40 resources. See Ancient Egypt.
  • “victimized by routine itself”: Mr. Spitzer complains of being “victimized by routine itself” and “valued his dignity perhaps more than his life.” He is the old, traditional, for appearances’ sake symbol. This also paralyzes him because he can do nothing. He’s stuck as all conservative thinking leads to is the center must hold, the line must not be crossed. These phrases portray Mr. Spitzer as a deeply complex figure burdened by the weight of his responsibilities and the shadow of his brother, struggling with the constraints of his life and the decay of his own identity. His routine existence and his concern for dignity underscore the novel’s themes of mortality, identity, and the tension between appearance and reality.
    • “Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold” (W. B. Yeats, “The Second Coming,” 1920).
  • “his unflagging search for lost heirs to lost estates”: In Dickens’s Bleak House (1853), the suit of Jarndyce and Jarndyce over a lost inheritance “has, in course of time, become so complicated, that no man alive knows what it means” (chapter 1).
  • “Epithalamiums sadder still”: The epithalamium, from the Greek for “at the bridal chamber,” was sung outside the bride’s door. Edmund Spenser’s Epithalamion (1595) celebrates his own wedding. Mr. Spitzer finds such songs “sadder still” than elegies. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “Should the lost locate the lost?”: “For the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost” (Luke 19:10). See The Bible.
  • “if he knew the longitude, then he did not know the latitude”: Latitude can be read from the sun and stars, but longitude needs the exact time. The problem was solved by John Harrison’s marine chronometer H4 (1761), after the British Longitude Act of 1714 offered a prize. Mr. Spitzer carries “two wrist watches.” See Clocks and Watches.
  • “all orient pearls”: Orient pearls are the most lustrous, once brought from the East: “Ransack the ocean for orient pearl” (Christopher Marlowe, Doctor Faustus 1.1). See Pearls and Jewels.
  • “one eye burning to starboard, one to larboard”: Larboard, the old word for the left side of a ship, sounded so much like starboard that the Royal Navy replaced it with “port” in 1844. See The Sea and Water.
  • “Racehorse, lost in the China Sea”: Racehorse, lost in the China Sea may refer to HMS Racehorse in 1864 which sank off the China coast.
    • The gunvessel ran aground near Chefoo, now Yantai, on November 4, 1864, and 99 of her 108 men were lost.
  • “marine angels blowing mother-of-pearl horns”: Triton blows his conch shell to call back the waters of the flood (Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.333-342). See Greek Mythology.
  • “ships sailing into port with no one on board, the table set with plates of gold”: See the Mary Celeste, found adrift in 1872 with no one aboard, in the Chapter 24 resources.
  • “Oceanus which sank”: Oceanus was a Titan son of Uranus and Gaia and the husband of his sister Tethys in Greek mythology. He is the father of the water gods and the great river that circles the world. See Greek Mythology.
  • “Arcturus, found drifting”: Arcturus is the brightest star in the northern constellation of Bootes. See Astronomy.
  • “Ptarmigan, swallowed”: Ptarmigan is a bird in the grouse subfamily. See Birds.
    • Her captain “frozen in the snow… while writing his last log” recalls the legend of the Octavius. See the Chapter 9 resources. See Exploration and the Poles.
  • “Nautilus, turned upside down”: Nautilus is the name of Captain Nemo’s submarine in Jules Verne’s Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea (1870) and of the first nuclear submarine, which passed under the North Pole in 1958.
    • A ship “turned upside down before she ever left harbor” recalls the Swedish warship Vasa, which capsized in Stockholm harbor on her maiden voyage in 1628 and was raised in 1961, as Mr. Spitzer would raise all the sunken ships of the world.
  • “Roman galleys sunk with Egyptian slaves chained to their oars”: The chained galley slave belongs more to Lew Wallace’s Ben-Hur (1880) and its 1959 film than to Rome. Ancient war galleys were rowed mostly by free men, and slaves were pressed to the oars only in emergencies (Lionel Casson, “Galley Slaves,” Transactions of the American Philological Association 97, 1966).
  • “sunk off Peiraeus”: Peiraeus may refer to Piraeus which is a port city within the Athens urban area.
    • The Antikythera wreck, found by sponge divers in 1900, carried marble and bronze statues bound for Rome, and bronze statues of Apollo and Athena were dug up at Piraeus in 1959.
  • “many old ferry boats capsized”: The excursion steamer Eastland rolled over at her dock in the Chicago River in 1915, and 844 people drowned.
  • “He had sought for the elements of harmony, but this was chaos”: Aristoxenus wrote the Elements of Harmony in the fourth century BC, the oldest Greek treatise on music to survive. See Music and Composers.
  • “bringing with it the fullness of days”: “So Job died, being old and full of days” (Job 42:17). See The Book of Job.
  • “Should not eternal recurrence be the one law of life”: Nietzsche calls it the “moment.” There is an eternal path ahead and behind, and what one has is this moment. He wrote about it in Thus Spoke Zarathustra, in “On the Vision and the Riddle” (Part 3, 1884), and first put the thought experiment in The Gay Science 341 (1882). If one had to live this life over again for all eternity with all its ups and downs, pains and pleasures, would it be tolerable? Would one still choose life, to live? It is a thought experiment for self-reflection and a test of how life affirming a person really is. Nietzsche had the idea of “amor fati” (love of fate) (Ecce Homo, 1888). One would come to love one’s fate, the life that one has led. A willingness to accept all aspects of one’s life. The Ubermensch is Nietzsche’s concept of a higher type of human or humanity that is connected to eternal recurrence. A person is capable of loving their life, even in its entirety, is more likely to reach this state. Nietzsche also believed that eternal recurrence would force people to confront the meaninglessness of conventional morality and encourage them to create their own values. By contemplating eternal recurrence, Nietzsche hoped to spark an interest in personal values which would lead to a more authentic and fulfilling life. The chapter presents the “moment” as an eternal path stretching both ahead and behind, with the present moment being the only certainty and the profound locus of all experience, mystery, and transformation. See Nietzsche.
    • Compare the Wiccan Rede, “An it harm none, do what ye will,” first made public by Doreen Valiente in 1964. Nietzsche set no such limit.
  • “the great lamp-lighter going his rounds”: God and light have been closely linked in the Bible. The symbolism of lighting lamps can be found in many religions around the world. Maria Susanna Cummins’s The Lamplighter (1854) was a bestseller. It is about an orphan that is taken in by a lamplighter and raised with love and taught virtue and religious faith. See The Bible.
    • Its success provoked Hawthorne’s complaint that “America is now wholly given over to a d——d mob of scribbling women” (Nathaniel Hawthorne, letter to William D. Ticknor, January 19, 1855). See American Writers.
  • “Perhaps she had passed under the midnight rainbow”: A moonbow, or lunar rainbow, forms when bright moonlight strikes falling rain or spray.
  • “through the pearly gates”: “And the twelve gates were twelve pearls” (Revelation 21:21). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “Life was a dream, and death was another dream”: Life as a dream is explored in philosophical and spiritual contexts which suggests one’s waking life is a subjective experience and may not be a true representation of reality. Some interpret death as not an end but as a transition into another state of being, for example, a dream or a higher level of awareness. Dreams are often considered symbolic reflections of subconscious thoughts, emotions, and experiences. Dreams could be a mirror of waking life where concepts, themes, and memories are explored in a different way. It explores life as a dream by philosophically questioning the nature of reality, identity, and mortality, using spiritual metaphors and emphasizing the fluid, elusive qualities of existence. It presents a worldview where life and death are intertwined phases of an ongoing dream, urging a recognition of human limitations and a compassionate acceptance of impermanence and mystery.
    • “¿Qué es la vida? Un frenesí… que toda la vida es sueño, y los sueños, sueños son,” “What is life? A frenzy… for all life is a dream, and dreams are dreams” (Pedro Calderón de la Barca, La vida es sueño, 1635).
    • “All that we see or seem / Is but a dream within a dream” (Edgar Allan Poe, “A Dream Within a Dream,” 1849). See Edgar Allan Poe.
  • “But she had outlived her fame”: Cousin Hannah dying in obscurity is reminiscent of Shulamith Firestone’s funeral where mourners grappled with the idea that a whole generation of feminists had been unable to thrive in the world they had done so much to create (Susan Faludi, “Death of a Revolutionary,” The New Yorker, April 15, 2013). See Women’s Suffrage.
    • Young said, “I thought of Margaret Fuller in relation to not only Cousin Hannah, but some of the other characters.” Fuller drowned off Fire Island in 1850 (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See the Chapter 31 resources. See Marguerite Young on Her Craft.
  • “the sound of an ivory horn far away”: Roland’s oliphant, his ivory horn, is blown too late at Roncevaux to save him (The Song of Roland, about 1100).
  • “camp-followers”: Women followed armies as cooks, laundresses, and nurses. Mary Ludwig Hays, remembered as “Molly Pitcher,” is said to have taken her husband’s place at a cannon at Monmouth in 1778. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “She was that Jason who had not found the golden fleece”: Jason and the Argonauts did win the golden fleece (Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica). Hannah, who “had not found the Holy Grail or any object of her quest,” is a Jason who failed. See Greek Mythology.
  • “that beau sabreur of suffrage”: Beau sabreur means a dashing adventurer. Literally it is the “handsome swordsman.” The title belonged to Joachim Murat, Napoleon’s cavalry marshal, a Joachim like Mr. Spitzer. See Napoleon and Wellington.
  • “for the sheath had outlived the sword”: Young inverts Byron: “For the sword outwears its sheath, / And the soul wears out the breast” (Lord Byron, “So, we’ll go no more a roving,” 1817). Hannah, too, “could dance no more, go no more.” See Romantic Poets.
  • “the Peloponnesian Wars”: Peloponnesian Wars was an ancient Greek war fought between Athens and the Peloponnesian League (Sparta). It lasted from 431 to 404 BC.
  • “the Siege of Rome by Hannibal”: The Siege of Rome by Hannibal may refer to the Second Punic War where Hannibal tried to lure the Romans to fight. Hannibal never besieged Rome. In 211 BC he marched to its gates to draw the Romans away from Capua, and “Hannibal ad portas,” “Hannibal at the gates,” became a Roman byword for terror.
  • “the Saracen wars”: The Saracen wars may refer to the Arab raid against Rome. In 846 Saracen raiders sacked the basilicas of St. Peter and St. Paul outside the walls. See the Saracen entry in the Chapter 40 resources.
  • “the Civil War”: The Civil War may refer to the American Civil War (1861-1865), fought between the United States and the Confederate states, which seceded to preserve slavery. See The Civil War.
  • “the Boer War”: The Boer War may refer to the Second Boer war when Boer irregulars and militia attacked colonial settlements in nearby British colonies.
    • The British burned Boer farms and held Boer women and children and Black Africans in concentration camps, where some 26,000 Boers, most of them children, and at least 14,000 Black Africans died. Emily Hobhouse exposed the camps, and the suffrage leader Millicent Fawcett headed the women’s commission sent to inspect them in 1901 (Thomas Pakenham, The Boer War, 1979). See Women’s Suffrage.
  • “many Balkan wars”: Balkan wars are a series of conflicts that took place in 1912 and 1913.
  • “Custer’s Last Stand”: Custer’s Last Stand also known as Battle of Little Bighorn or Battle of Greasy Grass between Plains Indians and the US military. On June 25-26, 1876, Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, and Arapaho warriors destroyed Custer’s command.
  • “Sheridan’s March”: Sheridan’s March may join two campaigns of 1864: Sheridan’s Ride, Philip Sheridan’s gallop to rally his army at Cedar Creek, told in Thomas Buchanan Read’s poem “Sheridan’s Ride” (1864), and William Tecumseh Sherman’s March to the Sea through Georgia. See The Civil War.
  • “Paul Revere’s ride”: “Paul Revere’s Ride” is a poem by Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, written in 1860 and published in 1861, that commemorates the actions of Paul Revere on April 18, 1775. William Dawes and Samuel Prescott rode that night too, and Revere was captured before he reached Concord. See American Writers.
    • Sixteen-year-old Sybil Ludington is said to have ridden some forty miles through what is now Putnam County, New York, on the night of April 26, 1777, to rouse her father’s militia after the British burned Danbury. The story first appeared in print in Martha J. Lamb’s History of the City of New York (1880), and Paula D. Hunt found no reliable evidence of the ride from the time (“Sybil Ludington, the Female Paul Revere: The Making of a Revolutionary War Heroine,” New England Quarterly 88, no. 2, 2015). See Women’s Suffrage.
  • “the sleeping Minute Men”: Minute Men were civilian colonists who independently formed militia companies to fight during the American Revolution.
  • “the Siege of Atlanta”: The Siege of Atlanta may refer to the Battle of Atlanta during the American Civil War. Sherman took the city on September 2, 1864. See The Civil War.
  • “camel troops”: The United States Army tried camels in the Southwest from 1856, at the urging of Secretary of War Jefferson Davis.
  • “the burning of Carthage”: The burning of Carthage may refer to the Siege of Carthage or the Third Punic War where Romans attacked and destroyed Carthage. The city fell in 146 BC.
  • “the taking of Venice”: Napoleon Bonaparte took Venice in May 1797, ending the thousand-year Republic, and handed it to Austria by the Treaty of Campo Formio that October. See Napoleon and Wellington.
  • “Cousin Hannah as the great bloomer girl”: Amelia Bloomer’s The Lily promoted the loose trousers worn under a short skirt in 1851, and the costume took her name. See Young’s account to Studs Terkel of the bloomer girls who shed their skirts, in the Chapter 38 resources. See Clothing, Hats, and Skirts and Women’s Suffrage.
  • “like Napoleon on the eve of a battle”: Napoleon was a French military and political leader who rose to prominence during the French Revolution. Jacques-Louis David painted him with his hand in his waistcoat in The Emperor Napoleon in His Study at the Tuileries (1812). See Napoleon and Wellington.
  • “greater than Waterloo”: Waterloo may refer to Napoleon who was defeated in the Battle of Waterloo by the Duke of Wellington. The battle was fought on June 18, 1815. See Napoleon and Wellington.
  • “Cousin Hannah launching a balloon”: Sophie Blanchard, the first woman to make her living as a balloonist, died in 1819 when fireworks set her hydrogen balloon alight over Paris.
  • “wearing an old boater and striped shirt”: The straw boater and the shirtwaist were the uniform of the New Woman and the Gibson Girl of the 1890s. See Clothing, Hats, and Skirts.
  • “leading the needle workers to strike albeit she was the owner of a mill”: In the Uprising of the 20,000, the New York shirtwaist strike of 1909, wealthy women such as Alva Belmont and Anne Morgan, the “mink brigade,” paid bail and fees for the strikers. Earlier, the Lowell mill girls of Massachusetts turned out in 1834 and 1836. Harriet Hanson Robinson, who led her room out at eleven, became a suffragist (Harriet H. Robinson, Loom and Spindle, 1898). See Women’s Suffrage.
  • “dressed as an Arab king, wearing a burnoose”: Lady Hester Stanhope (1776-1839) dressed as a Turkish man and ruled her household in Lebanon, and Isabelle Eberhardt rode the Sahara dressed as a man. See the Chapter 36 resources.
  • “the narrow channels of a lost cause”: Suffrage for women took a hundred years and many rights for women while Young was alive were not in place. Women and children were still property. Seeing again the erosion of these rights and the incompleteness of them worldwide (2025 saw Bangladesh Islamists protesting the expansion of human rights to women and a general attempt at rolling back gender rights for most parts of the world) one can see where there has to be a complete love (obsession) for something that might never be achieved in one lifetime. See Women’s Suffrage.
    • From Seneca Falls in 1848 to the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920 took seventy-two years, and many Black women in the South could not vote until the Voting Rights Act of 1965, more than a century after Seneca Falls.
    • In Young’s lifetime a married woman could be refused credit in her own name until the Equal Credit Opportunity Act of 1974, Louisiana’s “head and master” law let a husband dispose of jointly owned property without his wife’s consent until Kirchberg v. Feenstra (1981), and marital rape was not a crime in every state until 1993.
    • On May 3, 2025, Hefazat-e-Islam rallied in Dhaka demanding that Bangladesh’s Women’s Affairs Reform Commission and its report be scrapped as “contrary to Islamic teachings” (2025 Hefazat Grand Rally, Wikipedia).
  • “For where had her great struggles reached”: Mr. Spitzer questions what war has truly accomplished. He reflects on the vast number of wars, the battles fought, and the heroic efforts, such as those of Cousin Hannah, who had been a fierce and courageous leader in suffrage battles and other struggles, but he wonders what her great struggles ultimately achieved. Mr. Spitzer considers that despite all the battles and conquests, there is little to show for them in the end, as Cousin Hannah herself seemed to be dying of a broken heart, forgotten by the world she had fought to change. He contemplates that her great battles and achievements have passed into oblivion, and that her life had been a long time dying, filled with illusions and thwarted hopes. He questions whether her life and struggles, like those of many others, may have been overshadowed by inevitable loss and erasure, leaving no lasting recognition or reward. Mr. Spitzer is deeply doubtful about the ultimate value or effectiveness of war and heroic struggle, seeing it as a tragic and often unacknowledged endeavor that ends in sorrow and oblivion.
  • “one Vulcan of the forge”: Vulcan, Hephaestus to the Greeks, is the smith of the gods. See Greek Mythology.
  • “organized the pickets around an old town clock”: See the Silent Sentinels of the National Woman’s Party, who picketed the White House in 1917, in the Chapter 32 resources. Arrested pickets were beaten and choked at the Occoquan Workhouse on the “Night of Terror,” November 14-15, 1917, and Alice Paul was force-fed. See Women’s Suffrage.
  • “in many hysterical and nervous persons”: Since ancient Greece, hysteria was an umbrella term for numerous symptoms that reinforced harmful stereotypes about women, sex, and gender. Often it was used to pathologize everything men found unmanageable in women. See the hysteria entry in the Chapter 2 resources. Freud and Breuer published Studies on Hysteria in 1895, and the diagnosis was dropped from the American Psychiatric Association’s manual (DSM-III) in 1980. See Freud and Jung.
  • “the crystalization of one sad, wild moment”: Miss Havisham stopped her clocks at the moment she was jilted. See the Chapter 4 resources. Hannah reverses her, leaving the bridegroom at the church door. See Clocks and Watches.
  • “pictures of the Taj Mahal”: Taj Mahal is an ivory-white marble mausoleum in Agra, India. Shah Jahan built it as a tomb for his wife Mumtaz Mahal, who died in 1631.
  • “two ragged mountain climbers climbing Mt. Everest”: George Mallory and Andrew Irvine were last seen climbing toward the summit of Everest on June 8, 1924, and vanished. Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay reached the top in 1953. See Exploration and the Poles.
  • “No one net could ever drag in all the stars”: “The kingdom of heaven is like unto a net, that was cast into the sea, and gathered of every kind” (Matthew 13:47). See The Bible.
  • “this sublunar world”: For Aristotle, all change and decay belong to the world below the moon, while the heavens above it are unchanging (On the Heavens 1). See Aristotle.
  • “Forever would a lost psyche wander down the wind”: While death is inevitable there are different forms of immortality, covered above, and St. Augustine’s perspective where he emphasized the eternal significance of the “hour of death” and the importance of being prepared for it since one has been promised forgiveness but not necessarily the time: “No one has promised you that you are going to be alive tomorrow” (Augustine, Sermon 40.5, trans. Edmund Hill, 1990). This line encapsulates the novel’s meditation on the soul’s vulnerability to disorientation within the vast, often unfathomable realms of life, death, and memory. See Augustine and Christian Theology.
  • “For every being, Mr. Spitzer had observed, must contain its opposite”: “And as the same thing there exists in us living and dead and the waking and the sleeping and young and old” (Heraclitus, fragment 88). See the philosophy of reversals in the Chapter 37 resources. See Heraclitus and Flux.
  • “Only the out-croppings had been seen”: “The dignity of movement of an ice-berg is due to only one-eighth of it being above water” (Ernest Hemingway, Death in the Afternoon, 1932). See American Writers.
  • “she was Hamid in the East”: One of the Beautiful names of Allah, al Hamid means the praiseworthy, laudable one, the object of all praise.
    • Hannah, from the Hebrew for grace, is the mother of Samuel in 1 Samuel 1. See The Bible.
  • “struck fear and trembling”: “Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling” (Philippians 2:12), the source of Kierkegaard’s title Fear and Trembling (1843). See The Bible.
  • “each who dies must die twice”: “And death and hell were cast into the lake of fire. This is the second death” (Revelation 20:14). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “was solitary as the ace of hearts”: Hearts is the suit of love. See the ace of spades under “he who had played his last spade,” below. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “the difference between right and left”: Nineveh held “more than sixscore thousand persons that cannot discern between their right hand and their left hand” (Jonah 4:11). See The Bible.
  • “Here comes the bride”: The tune is the Bridal Chorus from Wagner’s Lohengrin (1850). See the Chapter 2 resources. See Music and Composers.
  • “it was the gentleman who left the bride at the church door”: A jilted woman could sue for breach of promise. Indiana, Young’s home state, became the first to abolish such suits in 1935, by a law sponsored by Roberta West Nicholson, and other states followed with “heart balm” laws. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “the chief victim of the harem system”: Another argument for equal rights between men and women is that men would also benefit. Men would have more freedom to express themselves through careers, taking family time, parental leave, and flexible work schedules. Men will not be pressured to conform to the stereotypical male.
  • “the caliph who said that he was in a cage”: Ottoman princes were confined for life in the Kafes, “the Cage,” of the Topkapı Palace from the seventeenth century.
  • “a secret woman, a bride”: Young told Studs Terkel that the woman behind Cousin Hannah brought home sealed trunks for forty years, and at her death each held a bridal trousseau: “She had a dream too” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See the Chapter 31 resources and Chapter 43. See Marguerite Young on Her Craft.
  • “Where, he wondered, was the great tent-maker now?”: Omar Khayyam’s name means tentmaker. See the Chapter 43 resources. Paul, too, was a tentmaker: “for by their occupation they were tentmakers” (Acts 18:3). See The Bible.
  • “creeping under some great skirt or crinoline”: See the crinoline in the Chapter 41 resources. See Clothing, Hats, and Skirts.
  • “like a winding shroud”: In Islamic burial practices the body is wrapped in a plain, usually white shroud.
  • “with their faces covered like Ahaseuris”: Ahaseuris or Ahasureus was a king and the husband of Esther in the Bible. In Esther it is Haman’s face that is covered as he is condemned: “As the word went out of the king’s mouth, they covered Haman’s face” (Esther 7:8). See the Ahasuerus entry in the Chapter 40 resources. See The Bible.
  • “Good-night and good-morning”: In Arabic the formal phrase is “Tusbah ala Khair” which can be translated to “may you wake up to a good morning” or wishing someone goodnight and good morning. The answer is “Wa inta min ahlu,” “and you are of its people.”
  • “might there be resurrection and eternal life”: “I am the resurrection, and the life” (John 11:25). See The Bible.
  • “he who had played his last spade”: The ace of spades has long been called the death card. Peron was a gambler: in Young’s first draft “Mr. Spitzer knew that he was really Peyron the gambler.” See the Chapter 36 resources. See Gambling and Playing Cards and Marguerite Young on Her Craft.
  • “death, like love, makes for drawing into a narrow house”: The narrow house is the grave: “And breathless darkness, and the narrow house” (William Cullen Bryant, “Thanatopsis,” 1821). See American Writers.
  • “the ideal immortality from his point of view”: There are different views on immortality and what that looks like. Many beliefs and religions focus on the individual’s soul or spirit after death. In Buddhism there is a state of no-self (Anatta) where there is no permanent, unchanging self or essence, including the individual. There may be some kind of continuation after death but it is not a separate, conscious existence. Non-personal immortality is where philosophers and thinkers believe there may be a collective consciousness with or without an individual component. Symbolic immortality is where immortality lies in one’s legacy, impact, and memory is what is remembered after death. This forms a sort of immortality through history. It presents immortality as a complex, ambiguous state rather than a clear, absolute fact. It is entwined with the human experience of memory, love, and identity, and it challenges rigid distinctions between life and death, reality and dream in the chapter. See Buddhism.
    • Mr. Spitzer’s ideal is the resurrection of the body of the Apostles’ Creed: “It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body” (1 Corinthians 15:44). See The Bible.
  • “waiting for the spirit to return”: “I will return into my house from whence I came out; and when he is come, he findeth it empty, swept, and garnished” (Matthew 12:44). See The Bible.
  • “Perhaps it was the lackey moth.”: The lackey moth is named for its caterpillar’s bright stripes, like a footman’s livery. See Butterflies and Moths.
  • “no one so expert as he in conducting a dying soul on its last journey”: Hermes Psychopompos, the guide of souls, led the dead to the underworld, and Charon ferried them over. See the psychopomp in the Chapter 32 resources. See Greek Mythology.
  • “uridulations”: Uridulations appears in no dictionary. It is almost certainly a printing error for undulations, since the curtain is “blowing with long, slow” waves. Ululation, a long, high-pitched trilling cry, is another possibility.
  • “many polarities reversing themselves”: The earth’s magnetic poles have reversed many times. Fred Vine and Drummond Matthews read the reversals in the striped rocks of the sea floor in 1963 (“Magnetic Anomalies over Oceanic Ridges,” Nature 199, 1963). See the reversals in the Chapter 39 resources. See Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos.
  • “the deaf bell-ringer ringing these tides”: Victor Hugo’s Quasimodo, bell-ringer of Notre-Dame, was deafened by his bells (The Hunchback of Notre-Dame, 1831). See Silence and Deafness.
  • “a dim-eyed marble Aphrodite”: Aphrodite is an ancient Greek goddess associated with love, lust, beauty, pleasure, passion and procreation. Hannah keeps her among the war trophies. See Greek Mythology.
  • “dust to crystal dust”: See “earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust” in the Chapter 26 resources.
  • “never by robin redbreast covering him with the autumn leaf”: “Call for the robin-red-breast and the wren, / Since o’er shady groves they hover, / And with leaves and flowers do cover / The friendless bodies of unburied men” (John Webster, The White Devil 5.4, 1612). In the ballad of the Babes in the Wood, robins cover the dead children with leaves. See Birds and Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “acting not only against the human law but the divine law”: Mr. Spitzer is expressing patriarchy’s upheaval if women’s perspectives are allowed. In a patriarchal society these changes go against globally established religion and nature.
  • “flit from spa to spa under her great white umbrella”: Catherine takes the waters “under many white umbrellas” in the Chapter 39 resources. See The Umbrella.
  • “She had tented but for a night”: See Longfellow’s cares that “fold their tents, like the Arabs, / And as silently steal away” in the Chapter 29 resources. See American Writers.
  • “came like a thief in the night”: “The day of the Lord so cometh as a thief in the night” (1 Thessalonians 5:2). See The Bible.
  • “how fickle, how irresponsible this great lady was”: Francis 1, King of France is credited with saying, “Woman is always fickle; foolish is he who trusts her.” Brantôme says Francis I scratched “Souvent femme varie, bien fol est qui s’y fie” on a window at Chambord. Victor Hugo gave the line to the king in Le roi s’amuse (1832), and Verdi turned it into “La donna è mobile” in Rigoletto (1851). See Music and Composers.
  • “What mad dance was life, and what madder dance was death”: The dance of death, the medieval Danse Macabre, in which Death leads every rank of the living, became Saint-Saëns’s tone poem Danse macabre (1874). See the Chapter 43 resources. See Music and Composers.
  • “this whirligig of leaves and stars”: “And thus the whirligig of time brings in his revenges” (Twelfth Night 5.1). See Shakespeare.
  • “she being that ivory door”: See the gates of horn and ivory in the Chapter 41 resources. See Homer’s Epics.
  • “trailing her clouds of glory”: See Wordsworth’s “trailing clouds of glory” in the Chapter 39 resources. See Romantic Poets.
  • “huntress moon with her dogs”: Artemis, Diana to the Romans, is goddess of the moon and the hunt, and her hounds tore apart Actaeon, who saw her bathing. See Greek Mythology.
  • “where was now her armor of virtue”: See “the whole armour of God” (Ephesians 6:11) in the Chapter 20 resources. See The Bible.
  • “the strength of this snow maiden”: In the Russian tale of Snegurochka, the Snow Maiden, she melts when love enters her heart (Alexander Ostrovsky’s play, 1873; Rimsky-Korsakov’s opera, 1882). See Fairy Tales and Folklore and Music and Composers.
  • “should not all be as brides going to the bridegroom”: “Behold, the bridegroom cometh; go ye out to meet him” (Matthew 25:6). See The Bible.
  • “Perhaps the secret casket contained a vapor and not a jewel”: Morocco opens the golden casket and finds a skull: “All that glisters is not gold” (The Merchant of Venice 2.7). See Shakespeare.
  • “the void calling to the void”: “Deep calleth unto deep” (Psalm 42:7). See the Chapter 1 resources. See The Bible.
  • “a few Promethean sparks of life”: Promethean means bold, creative, or life-giving, like Prometheus. See Greek Mythology.
    • Prometheus was a Titan god of fire in Ancient Greek who stole fire from the gods and gave it to humanity.
  • “No slave might wait on the great Abolitionist”: The suffrage movement grew out of abolition. Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott met at the World Anti-Slavery Convention in London in 1840, where the women delegates were refused seats, and they called the Seneca Falls convention in 1848. See Women’s Suffrage.
    • The movement split over the Fifteenth Amendment. Stanton wrote, “Think of Patrick and Sambo and Hans and Yung Tung who do not know the difference between a monarchy and a republic,” making laws for Lucretia Mott and other learned women (Elizabeth Cady Stanton, “Manhood Suffrage,” The Revolution, December 24, 1868).
    • Frederick Douglass answered at the American Equal Rights Association in May 1869: “When women, because they are women, are hunted down through the cities of New York and New Orleans; when they are dragged from their houses and hung upon lamp-posts… then they will have an urgency to obtain the ballot equal to our own” (History of Woman Suffrage, vol. 2, 1882, 382). See Lynching and Racial Violence.
  • “Oh, is there no pavilion to hide me from the storm?”: “For in the time of trouble he shall hide me in his pavilion” (Psalm 27:5), and “thou shalt keep them secretly in a pavilion from the strife of tongues” (Psalm 31:20). See The Bible.

Vocabulary

Abyss
A bottomless pit or chasm.
Adipose
Fatty.
Alabaster
A fine, smooth, white stone used for carving.
Anvil
The heavy iron block on which a blacksmith hammers hot metal into shape.
Apocryphal
Of doubtful truth.
Apparition
A ghost, or a ghostlike image of a person.
Argosy
A large merchant ship, or a fleet of them.
Ascertain
To find out for certain.
Ashes of roses
A grayish pink; also a scent of roses.
Asphodel
A lily-like flower that, in Greek myth, covers the meadows of the underworld where ordinary souls go after death.
Astral
Having to do with the stars.
Athwart
Across, from side to side; also, against.
Barnacled
Crusted with barnacles, small shelled sea creatures that cling to rocks, pilings, and the hulls of ships.
Barouche
A fashionable four-wheeled carriage with a folding half-hood and two seats facing each other.
Blackamoor
An old term for a Black African or any dark-skinned person, common in European art and in decorative figures of Black servants. It is now considered offensive.
Buccaneer
A pirate.
Burnoose
A hooded Arab cloak.
Caliph
The title of the chief Muslim ruler, understood as a successor to the Prophet Muhammad.
Casket
A small, often ornate box for jewels or valuables. In American usage, also a coffin, so Young’s “casket containing no jewel” carries both meanings.
Catafalque
A raised platform on which a coffin lies in state.
Celestial
Heavenly; of the sky.
Certitude
Certainty.
Chandelier
A branched hanging light.
Cherubim
Winged angelic beings. In art they are often shown as chubby winged children. The singular is cherub.
Citadel
A fortress, usually on high ground, that protects a city.
Conch
A large sea snail with a spiraled shell that can be blown like a horn. Held to the ear, it seems to hold the sound of the sea.
Congealed
Thickened or stiffened, as with cold.
Contraband
Forbidden; smuggled.
Contrariety
Opposition; contrariness.
Cordage
Ropes, especially the rigging of a ship.
Crusader
A soldier in the Crusades, the Christian military campaigns to take the Holy Land, fought between 1096 and 1291.
Cuirass
Armor for the chest and back.
Declivity
A downward slope.
Delusive
Misleading; giving a false impression.
Deprivation
The lack or loss of something needed.
Despotic
Tyrannical.
Diffused
Spread out widely.
Dirge
A slow song of mourning.
Distraught
Deeply upset.
Dubious
Doubtful.
Echoic
Echoing.
Eclat
Brilliance; showy success.
Egregiously
Outstandingly; shockingly.
Elegiac
Mournful, like an elegy, a poem of lament for the dead.
Elegy
A poem or song of mourning.
Elicit
To draw out.
Elixir
A medicine sold as a cure-all.
Emaciated
Wasted away, very thin.
Empearled
Adorned with pearls, or having a pearly sheen.
Enigma
A mystery or puzzle; a person or thing that is hard to understand.
Epithalamium
A song or poem celebrating a marriage.
Eunuch
A man who has been castrated. Eunuchs were employed for centuries to guard harems and to serve as officials in royal courts in the Middle East, China, and Byzantium. See The eunuch (Ch 35).
Figurehead
A carved figure, often a woman, mounted on the bow of a sailing ship. See On the figurehead and intuition (Ch 40).
Fitful
Starting and stopping; irregular.
Glacial
Icy; like a glacier.
Grimace
A twisted expression of the face.
Hallucination
Something seen or heard that is not there.
Hallucinatory
Like a hallucination; seeming real but not there.
Hen-pecked
Nagged and ruled by one’s wife.
Hypothetical
Supposed; existing only as an idea.
Incalculable
Too great to be counted.
Innumerable
Too many to count.
Intrepid
Fearless.
Jasper
An opaque, often red, variety of quartz.
Keel and list
To keel over is to capsize, with the keel, the ship’s backbone along the bottom, turning upward. To list is to lean to one side, usually from water taken on.
Lacuna
A blank space or missing part; plural lacunae.
Larboard
The old word for the left side of a ship.
Livid
Bluish-gray, the color of a bruise. Also furiously angry.
Maharajah
A great king or prince in India. From the Sanskrit for “great king.”
Mandarin
A high-ranking official of imperial China.
Minaret
A tall, slender tower of a mosque, from which the call to prayer is given.
Minuscular
Tiny. Young’s form of minuscule.
Monsoon
A seasonal wind in South Asia that brings months of heavy rain.
Mosaic
A picture or pattern made by setting small pieces of colored stone, glass, or tile together.
Mother-of-pearl
The shining, rainbow-colored lining of some shells, used for inlay.
Muezzin
The man who calls Muslims to prayer.
Mummified
Preserved by embalming and drying, as in ancient Egypt.
Myopic
Nearsighted.
Nautilus
A sea creature with a spiral shell. The paper nautilus is a small octopus whose female makes a thin, papery shell.
Nocturnal
Of or happening in the night.
Numerologist
One who reads hidden meanings in numbers.
Oblivion
The state of being forgotten, or of being unaware of everything.
Ogre
A man-eating giant of folktale.
Pagoda
An Asian tiered tower with multiple eaves, usually a Buddhist temple or shrine.
Pavilion
A large tent; a light ornamental building.
Peregrine
A wanderer or pilgrim; also a falcon.
Perpendicular
Upright; at a right angle to the horizon.
Petticoat
An underskirt.
Precarious
Unstable and likely to fall or fail.
Precipitous
Very steep; headlong.
Prepossessing
Making a good first impression.
Prism
A clear piece of glass with flat sides that splits light into the colors of the rainbow.
Punctilious
Exact in matters of etiquette and detail.
Rapacious
Greedy; predatory.
Retort
A glass vessel with a long bent neck, used by alchemists for distilling.
Retrospect
A looking back on the past.
Reveille
The bugle call that wakes soldiers in the morning.
Scrutiny
Close, searching examination.
Sedimentary
Settled out like sediment.
Seine
To fish with a large hanging net.
Sequin
A small shining disk sewn on clothing; first the zecchino, a Venetian gold coin.
Sheik
An Arab chief or elder.
Skiff
A small, light boat for rowing or sailing. Young’s “skiff moon” is a crescent shaped like one.
Sloop
A sailboat with one mast.
Soughing
Making a soft sighing or rushing sound, like wind in trees.
Spangled
Covered with spangles, small glittering ornaments.
Starboard
The right-hand side of a ship, facing forward.
Sublunar
Beneath the moon; earthly.
Supernumerary
Extra; more than the number needed. In the theater, an extra with no lines.
Swooned
Fainted.
Taper
A slender candle.
Tapestry
A heavy cloth woven with pictures, hung on walls.
Tentative
Hesitant; not certain.
Testamentary
Of a will or testament.
Testimonial
A formal statement given as evidence or proof of something.
Tonnage
The size or carrying capacity of a ship.
Torpor
A sluggish, heavy, half-asleep state.
Transact
To carry out or carry through. Young uses it for crossing a distance, as in “transacted the gulf between two worlds.”
Transpired
Happened; took place.
Tumult
A loud, confused uproar.
Voluminous
Large, full, or great in volume.
Will-o’-the-wisp
A flickering light over marshes that leads travelers astray; anything that deludes.
