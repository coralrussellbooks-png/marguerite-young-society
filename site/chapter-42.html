Summary

Catherine rides in a glass carriage behind four alabaster horses, driven by the black coachman, through the sleeping house toward Hannah's deathbed. Mr. Spitzer, a keeper of lists of lost ships, keeps vigil beside the dying suffragette. He finds her crippled, wrapped in an old army coat, sobbing and delirious in a white tent of a room. He wonders what her battles truly won, and she cries out to be hidden from the eyes of men.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a dreamlike passage describing Catherine’s journey in a glass carriage driven by a black coachman with alabaster horses, passing through surreal landscapes of mirrors, fountains, tapestries, and sleeping cities. This journey is symbolic of transition, memory, and farewell, as she travels to visit Cousin Hannah in a realm beyond ordinary time and space. Catherine’s visit is described as hallucinatory, almost otherworldly, blending dream and reality, emphasizing the fluidity of time and the nature of farewell as something that transcends physical existence.
  • Mr. Spitzer is preoccupied with lists of sunken and lost ships, the mystery of their disappearance, and the elusive nature of time and memory. This section metaphorically ties the fate of lost ships to the human experience of loss, disappearance, and the unknowability of final destinies. His meditation on finding coordinates, lost passengers, and the ocean’s endless mysteries parallels the chapter’s broader themes of searching for meaning amid oblivion.
  • Much of the chapter is devoted to Mr. Spitzer’s detailed remembrance and reflection on Cousin Hannah, the fading suffrage captain. Her life is portrayed as one of militant energy, passion, and struggle by leading crusades, breaking conventions, and battling societal and personal constraints. Yet now, in her old age, she is reduced to a fragile figure, almost unrecognizable, lying in a vast, tent-like death chamber filled with memories and trophies of battles past. The contrast between her former fiery strength and present weakness is poignant, illustrating the inevitability of decline and the loneliness of a warrior’s final moments.
  • Mr. Spitzer speculates on the hidden personal life of Cousin Hannah, suggesting a secret and perhaps tragic love life that contrasts with her public image of fierce independence and detachment. There is mention of a mysterious bridegroom who was left at the church door and rumors of a secret man or caliph who supported her cause. This passage explores the tension between public persona and private vulnerability, and the elusive nature of love and companionship even for the strongest figures.
  • The closing passages focus on Mr. Spitzer’s visit to Cousin Hannah’s death chamber, where he confronts her mortality and the enigma of her spirit. The imagery of snow, white curtains, desert tents, and the blending of life and death create a surreal atmosphere. Mr. Spitzer reflects on the nature of death as a journey and the dissolution of identity. Despite her indifference and silence, he remains faithful, accompanying her in her final hours, contemplating the mystery of the soul, the possibility of resurrection, and the universal fate that awaits all.
  • Throughout the chapter, there are numerous philosophical reflections on time, memory, identity, and the interconnectedness of life and death. Mr. Spitzer’s musings on eternal recurrence, the blending of opposites, and the fragile boundary between dream and reality deepen the emotional and intellectual impact of the narrative. His lament over forgotten heroes and lost loves, and his sense of being a witness to fading glory, add a layer of melancholic grandeur

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Cousin Hannah (C. H. Freemount-Snowden)

  • A formidable, militant suffrage leader whose life was dedicated to battling the conventions of love, society, and gender roles.
  • She is depicted as a warrior, storm king, and mystic figure, feared and revered but ultimately isolated and sorrowful in her final days.
  • Her identity spans multiple cultural representations (Hannah in Boston, Hamid in the East, and the Great Moor) symbolizing a universal warrior figure.

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • She journeys in a glass carriage driven by spectral horses and a coachman, making a dreamlike farewell visit to Cousin Hannah.
  • Her journey transcends time and space, emphasizing the fluidity of reality and memory.
  • Mr. Spitzer An observer and quasi-legal figure who researches lost ships, heirs, and ancient records.
  • He represents the search for meaning, order, and connection amid chaos and oblivion.
  • He reflects on his own precarious existence and the mysteries surrounding Cousin Hannah.
  • The Unknown Bridegroom A mysterious figure tied to Cousin Hannah by rumor and symbolism.
  • He represents lost love, abandonment, and unfulfilled relationships, adding a layer of personal tragedy to Hannah's public life.

Research: Themes

The Fluidity of Time and Reality

See: Illusion and Reality · Time

  • The narrative challenges linear notions of time. Catherine’s journey is described as transcending death and time, visiting Cousin Hannah when both are bed-ridden or dead. Dreams and waking reality blur, reflecting a mystical view of existence where farewell is "a matter of opinion as to the time, the place, the person"

Death and the Afterlife

See: Death and Mortality

  • Death is portrayed as both an end and a transformation or a continuation into another dream or realm. The chapter explores death's mysteries, the dissolution of identity, and the hope or doubt surrounding resurrection and eternal life.

Memory and Legacy

See: Memory and the Past

  • Cousin Hannah’s fading fame and forgotten battles symbolize how history erases even the greatest struggles. Mr. Spitzer’s reflections reveal the tension between public recognition and private sorrow, as well as the difficulty of preserving true legacy.

Love and Isolation

See: Isolation and Connection · Love

  • Despite her militant exterior, Cousin Hannah harbors hidden loves and intimacies. The narrative hints at secret lovers, lost opportunities for human connection, and the loneliness of a life lived in opposition to societal norms.

Identity and Transformation

See: Identity and the Self

  • Hannah's multiple identities (Hannah, Hamid, Al Hamid, Great Moor) and her shifting roles reflect the multiplicity of self and the transformative power of myth and legend. Mr. Spitzer’s own uncertainty about his identity parallels this theme.

The Search for Meaning Amid Chaos

See: Order, Chaos, and Discipline · The Search for Meaning

  • Mr. Spitzer’s obsession with lost ships and records symbolizes humanity’s quest for order and meaning despite the inherent chaos of existence. The lost ships, passengers, and forgotten histories mirror Cousin Hannah’s own forgotten life.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Glass Carriage and Alabaster Horses

See: Horses and Carriages

  • Symbolize the ethereal, dreamlike journey between worlds or states of being. The horses’ heads turning like foam and their journey through clouds evoke a passage beyond the physical.

Mirrors and Reflections

See: Mirrors and Reflections

  • The house with rooms of mirrors and endless reflections represents the multiplicity of perspectives and the elusive nature of truth and self.

White Tent and Desert Imagery

  • Cousin Hannah’s death chamber is likened to a desert tent or pagoda, blending Eastern and Western symbols of the sacred and transient. The desert and snow imagery evoke isolation, purity, and the harshness of the final journey.

Lost Ships and Stars

See: The Sea and Water · Moon and Stars

  • Ships represent journeys, lost hopes, and the passage of souls. Stars symbolize guidance, memory, and the unreachable. Mr. Spitzer’s cataloging of lost ships parallels the attempt to map lost lives and loves.

Snow and Ice

See: Snow, Ice, and Winter

  • Metaphors for death, stillness, and the crystallization of time and memory. The persistent snow on Cousin Hannah’s face and body symbolizes the inevitability of death and the fading of life’s passions.

The Bridegroom and Withered Flowers

See: Roses and Flowers · Weddings and Funerals · The House

  • Represent unfulfilled love and abandonment, contrasting with Cousin Hannah’s militant independence and loneliness.

Research: Resources

  • Mr. Spitzer complains of being “victimized by routine itself” and “valued his dignity perhaps more than his life.” He is the old, traditional, for appearances sake symbol. This also paralyzes him because he can do nothing. He’s stuck as all conservative thinking leads to is the center must hold, the line must not be crossed. These phrases portray Mr. Spitzer as a deeply complex figure burdened by the weight of his responsibilities and the shadow of his brother, struggling with the constraints of his life and the decay of his own identity. His routine existence and his concern for dignity underscore the novel's themes of mortality, identity, and the tension between appearance and reality.
  • Nietzsche calls it the “moment”. There is an eternal path ahead of us and behind us and what we have is this moment. He wrote about it in Thus Spoke Zarathustra. If one had to live this life over again for all eternity with all its ups and downs, pains and pleasures, would it be tolerable? Would one still choose life, to live? It is a thought experiment for self-reflection and a test of how life affirming a person really is. Nietzsche had the idea of “amor fati” (love of fate). One would come to love one’s fate, the life that one has led. A willingness to accept all aspects of one’s life. The Ubermensch is Nietzsche’s concept of a higher type of human or humanity that is connected to eternal recurrence. A person is capable of loving their life, even in its entirety, is more likely to reach this state. Nietzsche also believed that eternal recurrence would force us to confront the meaninglessness of conventional morality and encourage us to create our own values. (i.e. Do what you will, harm none.) By contemplating eternal recurrence, Nietzsche hoped to spark an interest in personal values which would lead to a more authentic and fulfilling life. The chapter presents the “moment” as an eternal path stretching both ahead and behind us, with the present moment being the only certainty and the profound locus of all experience, mystery, and transformation.
  • Life as a dream is explored in philosophical and spiritual contexts which suggests one’s waking life is a subjective experience and may not be a true representation of reality. Some interpret death as not an end but as a transition into another state of being, for example, a dream or a higher level of awareness. Dreams are often considered symbolic reflections of our subconscious thoughts, emotions, and experiences. Dreams could be a mirror of our waking life where concepts, themes, and memories are explored in a different way. It explores life as a dream by philosophically questioning the nature of reality, identity, and mortality, using spiritual metaphors and emphasizing the fluid, elusive qualities of existence. It presents a worldview where life and death are intertwined phases of an ongoing dream, urging a recognition of human limitations and a compassionate acceptance of impermanence and mystery.
  • There are different views on immortality and what that looks like. Many beliefs and religions focus on the individual’s soul or spirit after death. In Buddhism there is a state of no-self (Anatta) where there is no permanent, unchanging self or essence, including the individual. There may be some kind of continuation after death but it is not a separate, conscious existence. Non-personal immortality is where philosophers and thinkers believe there may be a collective consciousness with or without an individual component. Symbolic immortality is where immortality lies in one’s legacy, impact, and memory is what is remembered after death. This forms a sort of immortality through history. It presents immortality as a complex, ambiguous state rather than a clear, absolute fact. It is entwined with the human experience of memory, love, and identity, and it challenges rigid distinctions between life and death, reality and dream in the chapter.
  • Mr. Spitzer questions what war has truly accomplished. He reflects on the vast number of wars, the battles fought, and the heroic efforts, such as those of Cousin Hannah, who had been a fierce and courageous leader in suffrage battles and other struggles, but he wonders what her great struggles ultimately achieved. Mr. Spitzer considers that despite all the battles and conquests, there is little to show for them in the end, as Cousin Hannah herself seemed to be dying of a broken heart, forgotten by the world she had fought to change. He contemplates that her great battles and achievements have passed into oblivion, and that her life had been a long time dying, filled with illusions and thwarted hopes. He questions whether her life and struggles, like those of many others, may have been overshadowed by inevitable loss and erasure, leaving no lasting recognition or reward. Mr. Spitzer is deeply doubtful about the ultimate value or effectiveness of war and heroic struggle, seeing it as a tragic and often unacknowledged endeavor that ends in sorrow and oblivion.
