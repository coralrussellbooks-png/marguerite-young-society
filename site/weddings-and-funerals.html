Definition from research

Chapter 4 · Nature’s Cruelty and Funeral Wreaths

  • The natural world is depicted not as nurturing but cold and indifferent.
  • Funeral wreaths and deaf old people symbolize death, silence, and abandonment.

Chapter 6 · The Wedding and Funeral Imagery

  • Madge’s “wedding” is a funeral; marriage equals death in her experience.
  • The absence of traditional wedding symbols intensifies the sense of loss and unrealized hopes.

Chapter 6

  • “until Death did them part”: The phrase comes from the marriage vows of the Book of Common Prayer (1549), which read “till death us depart”; the wording later became “till death us do part.” The same vows promise “for better for worse.”
  • “her little wedding cake bridegroom with his polished mustache”: Cake toppers of a tiny bride and groom became fashionable in the United States in the early twentieth century.
  • “this circle of creation, all of one piece having no beginning and no end”: The wedding ring has long stood for eternity. It is worn on the fourth finger, where an old belief placed a vein running to the heart.
  • “a handful of rice or confetti or white rose petals”: Rice was thrown for fertility, a bride wore “something old” such as her mother’s veil, and the bride who caught the bouquet would be the next to marry.
  • “to carry her across the threshold”: The custom goes back to Rome. A bride who stumbled at the door brought bad luck, and Madge “had stumbled on her way to the altar.”
  • “the Justice of the Peace to tie the knot”: A justice of the peace could perform a civil marriage without a church or guests.
  • “love, honor, and obey”: The older prayer book vows had only the bride promise to “obey.” Madge throws the word at Homer.
  • “She carried the yellow tuberoses at the commencement”: Tuberoses, heavily fragrant, were favorite flowers at Victorian funerals.
  • “Death comes to every wedding feast”: In Holbein’s Dance of Death (1538) a skeleton drums ahead of a newly married couple.

Chapter 7 · The Graveyard

  • A place of refuge and sustenance for Musidora and her children, representing the blurred line between life and death. One of the last public places where people can gather.

Chapter 8 · The Graveyard Hill

  • Represents the boundary between life and death, a literal and figurative crossroads on the journey.

Chapter 8 · Names on Tombstones

  • Names like Stonecipher, Longtree, Thistlewaite, Chesterfield, and others evoke lineage, memory, and the passage of generations, underscoring the permanence of death. Longtree is Esther’s last name, a character at the end of the novel.

Chapter 9 · Marriage and Reproduction Imagery

  • Miss MacIntosh’s lectures on marriage, ovaries, and reproduction are laden with symbolic meaning about societal expectations, femininity, and the passage into adulthood.
  • The metaphor of flowers needing a man to be impregnated (not just wind) symbolizes the necessity of union and procreation in life, reflecting the cultural norms around marriage and gender roles.

Chapter 10 · Wedding/Funeral Ceremony

  • Symbolizes the intertwined nature of beginnings and endings, union and separation.

Chapter 10

  • “pots and pans jangling in my ears”: A shivaree, or charivari, was the old custom of banging pots and pans outside newlyweds’ windows on their wedding night.
  • “we are gathered here in the sight of God and of this company to join man and woman together”: The Book of Common Prayer opens the marriage service, “Dearly beloved, we are gathered together here in the sight of God, and in the face of this congregation, to join together this man and this woman in holy matrimony.”
  • “Do either of ye know of an impediment to this marriage”: The phrase is from the Book of Common Prayer, which drew on the Sarum rite. It reads, “I require and charge you both, as ye will answer at the dreadful day of judgment when the secrets of all hearts shall be disclosed, that if either of you know any impediment . . . ye do now confess it,” and calls on anyone who knows a cause to “speak, or else hereafter for ever hold his peace.”
  • “Unadvisedly or lightly, marriage should not be entered in”: The prayer book says marriage is “not by any to be enterprised, nor taken in hand, unadvisedly, lightly . . . but reverently, discreetly, advisedly, soberly.”
  • “the rites for the burial of the dead”: Funeral rites originated from Jewish and Roman customs. The Order for the Burial of the Dead in the Book of Common Prayer is the service the preacher slides into.
  • “the turtle Solomon and the red geranium and an old, empty shoe”: An old shoe was thrown after the bride or tied to the carriage for luck.

Chapter 14 · The Funeral and the Graveyard

  • The funeral scenes, especially Mr. Spitzer’s brother’s funeral, symbolize mourning, loss, and the passage of time.
  • Catherine’s absence from funerals and the details of the mourning rituals emphasize her detachment and the fragmented nature of her existence.

Chapter 14

  • “the funeral gondola with the corrugated angel at its helm”: Venice carries its dead by black gondola to the cemetery island of San Michele. Liszt wrote “La lugubre gondola” (1882) foreseeing Wagner’s death in Venice, and Wagner’s body left by gondola in 1883.

Chapter 15

  • “is always the bridegroom”: The old image of Death and the Maiden, set to music by Schubert.
  • “wedlock is padlock”: From John Ray’s English Proverbs (1670).
  • “one should keep one’s eyes open, but after marriage, one should keep them half shut”: Poor Richard: “Keep your eyes wide open before marriage, half shut afterwards” (1738).

Chapter 16

  • “the black funeral gondola”: Venice’s black funeral gondolas.

Chapter 16 · The House as a Tomb and Water Castle

  • The grand house is described with imagery of tombs, water, and underwater life, creating a sense of mystery and timelessness. The references to the phoenix and musical tomb further emphasize themes of death and rebirth.

Chapter 32 · Death as a Wedding and Wedding as Death

  • The narrative blurs the boundaries between life milestones and death: “weddings are funerals, and funerals are weddings”. This imagery underscores the theme of transformation and loss inherent in marriage and life.
  • Catherine’s vague, elusive descriptions of her marriage (to a phantom, a porpoise, a star, an iceberg) symbolize the illusory or absent nature of her union and love. Marriage here is a symbolic death of the self or an eternal separation, while death is the final union.

Chapter 33 · The Wedding and Bridegroom(s)

  • Catherine’s ambiguous wedding(s) symbolize transitions, possibly to death or between states of consciousness.
  • The multiple, shifting bridegrooms (white camel, church steeple, wind) reflect the fluidity of identity and reality.

Chapter 33 · The Wedding Memories

  • Catherine’s hazy, fantastical memories of her wedding illustrates the blurred line between reality and fantasy.

Chapter 34 · White and Black in Marriage

  • White symbolizes innocence and first marriage; black signifies death, mourning, and second chances or loss.

Chapter 34 · The Funeral and the King of France

  • Represents grandeur, loss, and the blending of significant life events into one confusing memory.

Chapter 42 · The Bridegroom and Withered Flowers

  • Represent unfulfilled love and abandonment, contrasting with Cousin Hannah’s militant independence and loneliness.

Chapter 57 · The Black Funeral Suit and Tailor’s Bill

  • Symbolizes unresolved debts, both literal and metaphorical. The suit represents Peron’s dignity and the past that Mr. Spitzer attempts to honor and settle.

Chapter 68 · The Hanging/Marital Knot

  • Symbolizes the irreversible bond with death and the complexity of human relationships.

Chapter 71 · The Dogs’ Graveyard

  • Represents death, loyalty, and the neglect of forgotten beings, mirroring the hotel’s decay.

Chapter 75 · The Funeral Wreath

  • Symbolizes death and mourning; it appears on the dead baby and connects to Esther’s fears about paternity and legacy.

Chapter 78 · The Graveyard

  • A place associated with both death and life, symbolizing the cycle of existence and the blurred line between endings and beginnings.
