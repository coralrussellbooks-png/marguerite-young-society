Research notes: Chapter 50 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 50

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

"he had seen how one may be two or one may be many, many even in his dying"

Mr. Spitzer is constitutively multiple. The chapter never resolves him into a single, stable identity. He is simultaneously:

  • The living man and his own ghost
  • The dreamer and the one who cannot dream
  • The man who has already died and the man still walking
  • The lawyer without a brief, the doctor without patients: defined by absences that are themselves identities

The most devastating articulation comes here:

"what he feared most was doubtless not that one should be two—he feared that one should be one"

This is a direct inversion of conventional anxiety. Most philosophical traditions treat multiplicity as the problem and unity as the solution. Mr. Spitzer fears the opposite: unity is the death. To be collapsed into singularity (to have dream and reality, living and dead, fuse without remainder) is the true annihilation. The plural self is not his disorder. It is his survival strategy, his ontological condition, and ultimately his only form of grace.

The chapter also presents him as plural across time: "the same in his end as in his beginning" is offered not as comfort but as one possibility among several, immediately undermined by its opposite. He cannot determine whether he has changed or remained the same because both are simultaneously true. The self is not a continuous thread but a set of overlapping, irresolvable versions.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

"one chair might be only the dream, and one might be real, and one might collapse if he touched it. One might suddenly fly away with many tinkling bells."

The two golden chairs in the clouds (Cassiopeia's Chair, the dream chair, the real chair) constitute one of the chapter's central images. But Young never resolves which is which. The epistemological problem is not presented as solvable. Mr. Spitzer dares not touch the strings of the constellation Lyre "for fear he might touch only upon the imaginary strings".

The crucial move here is that illusion is not positioned as secondary or degraded. The dream chair is as present, as weighted, as consequential as the real one. Young's prose enacts this by giving equal syntactic weight to both:

"One might be only the shadowed part of life."

Note: only the shadowed part, and yet the shadowed part is what fills the chapter. The light exists only as something that casts shadow. There is no sunlit world outside the cave. The opium lady, the invalid, the suffragette: all live entirely inside constructed realities, and the chapter treats these constructions not as pathologies but as the primary mode of human existence.

The line "Or was there only the dream, and had nothing ever been real?" is not answered. In the framework of Radical Immanence, it need not be. The question itself is the territory.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

This pillar may be the one most completely embodied by the chapter's texture. Grief here is not an event or a response. It is the climate of Mr. Spitzer's consciousness.

"how often Mr. Spitzer had lamented as the wind whirred around his cape, this universe tacking like the wild white snow bird away from central fires"

The grief is not located. It has no object named, no single loss identified. It is atmospheric: the wind, the fog, the vanished moons, the stars that have followed "an old bellwether to his grave". The accumulation of images of dissolution (snowflakes, fading stars, dried-up streams, broken hourglasses) does not build toward catharsis. Each image simply adds to the sediment of loss.

The suffragette captain who "long ago had paused in a broken melody and would not take up her music in this life" is a figure of grief so total it has become structural; she stopped mid-sentence and never resumed. Mr. Spitzer's grief is the same: it does not resolve into mourning and recovery. It is the condition under which all his perception operates.

Most tellingly:

he "might live to mourn for her as for himself"

He mourns himself. This is grief without even the consolation of a distinct lost object. The griever and the grieved are the same person. This is grief as permanent weather in its most radical form; there is no outside of it, no position from which to observe it as something that happened and passed.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Mr. Spitzer is above all a witness: to others, to himself, to the moment of dying. But Young's treatment of witnessing is complex and troubling in ways that deepen the pillar rather than simply illustrate it.

"was thus a man continually checking, rechecking, altering his impressions which altered of themselves, making little notes to mark his journeys back and forth"

This is witnessing as practice, as discipline: the "making little notes" is the act of attention rendered literal. He does not rush toward resolution. He checks, rechecks, alters, and crucially, the impressions alter of themselves, meaning the act of witnessing is not passive recording but active, destabilizing participation.

His ethical stance toward the invalid lady is witnessing in its most sustained form:

"she had always lived by such indirections and evasions that she would never know when he was gone"

He stays in the room. He keeps returning, punctually "by his broken watch or by the shadow on a moondial in a flooded garden". The broken watch and the flooded moondial are instruments of witnessing that are themselves unreliable, and yet he uses them. The witness does not require perfect instruments. The witness requires presence and refusal to simplify.

The chapter also shows the cost of witnessing. Mr. Spitzer is frightened:

"He was frightened of loud noises. He was frightened of causing disturbances."

The witness is not heroic. The witness is terrified of his own impact on what he observes. This is the ethics of witnessing taken to its logical extreme: the observer so aware of the observer effect that he tries to breathe softly enough not to start an avalanche.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

"many changes prepared for greater changes, for by many small changes he saw what greater change would be, and many confusions prepared for greater confusions"

The chapter is itself a recursive construction. The prose returns again and again to the same images (the golden chairs, the fog, the stars, the tide, the mirrors) but each return is slightly altered, slightly further along in dissolution. This is not repetition. It is building with what was learned from the previous pass.

Mr. Spitzer's life is described as a series of provisional structures:

"many assumptions once taken for certain had become uncertain, and many premises had been abandoned"

This is not presented as failure or tragedy but as the natural rhythm of a conscious life. The abandoned premise was not wasted; it prepared the ground for the next construction. The building is never finished. The chapter enacts this by refusing closure even as it approaches death. The final images of the chapter are not resolutions but new openings: the suffragette's unfinished melody, the nocturne he writes for her, the continuing question of what the firefly is.

The recursive structure also appears in Mr. Spitzer's relationship to the opium lady. He returns visit after visit, and each visit is both the same (she may not know he has been gone) and different (he has changed "imperceptibly from point to point"). The relationship is a loop that is also a spiral: returning to the same place but at a different elevation each time.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

This pillar is perhaps most explicitly theorized in the chapter's central statement of Mr. Spitzer's condition:

"died wholly who had lived partially, who had lived not as the star but as the day's firefly lost in the sunlight streaked with the dead iris"

Living partially is not a failure; it is the honest mode. The star that burns completely and visibly is actually less truthful than the firefly lost in daylight: the firefly's invisibility is its integrity. It does not pretend to more luminosity than it has.

The chapter is full of unfinished things presented without apology:

  • The suffragette's broken melody
  • The calling cards piled up "as if he had come many times when he had not come": the record of visits that didn't happen, an incomplete archive
  • "his unuttered thoughts and his unrealized loves and his unfinished business, his

hope without foundation stone"

These are not catalogued as failures. They are catalogued as what a life is made of. The demand for completion (the finished melody, the realized love, the founded hope) is implicitly identified as a violence against the texture of actual experience.

Most powerfully:

"was but a moment's passage... was not the Holy Writ but was the mere flyleaf stuck between two passages or testaments"

The flyleaf. Not the text. Not the Old Testament, not the New. The blank page between them, which is also the page that holds them together. Mr. Spitzer is the ethics of incompletion made flesh: the connective tissue between things that never itself gets read.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Mr. Spitzer inhabits every margin simultaneously. He is:

  • Possibly already dead, possibly still living
  • Neither the dream nor the reality but the space between them
  • "stuck between the millstone above him and the millstone below him"
  • "caught between the penumbra and the penumbra"
  • Neither of the water nor of the land

"Were there not places which were neither of the water nor of the land, neither of the land-locked water nor of the water-locked land, neither one nor the other, places which could not be defined?"

This is the ontology of the marginal stated as cosmology. The liminal zone is not an exception or an edge case. It is a category of place that exists throughout the world. Mr. Spitzer does not occupy the margin because he is unusual. He occupies the margin because the margin is where the constructed nature of all categories becomes visible.

The chapter's gallery of marginal figures reinforces this:

  • The opium lady: neither sane nor mad, neither awake nor asleep
  • The suffragette: neither living nor dead before her death, her name hyphenated, "the hyphen was more important than the two names it joined"
  • Mr. Spitzer himself: "was not the Holy Writ but was the mere flyleaf"

Each of these figures is evidence, as the pillar states, not exception. They reveal that all categories (life/death, real/dream, self/other) are constructions with permeable, inhabited edges.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The chapter's treatment of time is explicitly sedimentary. The past is not behind Mr. Spitzer; it is beneath him, pressing up:

"this life was extended perhaps only one mournful moment longer than he lived, many pebbles rolling in the dried-up streams shining with light"

The dried-up stream still has pebbles rolling in it: the memory of water moving the stones even after the water is gone. This is sedimentary time as physical metaphor: the past's pressure continuing to produce present effects even after the cause has departed.

Most explicitly:

"the whispers lost under the silence, the voices not noticed then"

The voices were deposited in the past and are now pressing up through the silence. They were not noticed then; they are being heard now, from below. This is the sedimentary model of time: what was laid down continues to exert force. The image of the shore is the chapter's master image of sedimentary time:

"this shore of broken hourglasses where was no hour"

The broken hourglasses are time's instruments, shattered and accumulated on the shore. Time does not flow through them anymore, but they are still there, still constituting the ground he walks on. The shore itself is made of what time deposited and then abandoned.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Young's prose in this chapter is one of the most radical enactments of language-as-construction in American literature. The sentences do not describe Mr. Spitzer's consciousness; they perform it. The syntax itself is the content.

Consider the characteristic sentence structure: long, recursive, self-interrupting, qualifying each clause with another clause that partially contradicts it:

"perhaps the fact that he was not himself, that he had changed imperceptibly from point to point, perhaps the fact that he had not changed and was ever the same man"

The language cannot settle. Each perhaps is not a rhetorical hedge but an ontological statement: this is a consciousness for which certainty is not available, and the language must be built to match. Language that pretended to declarative certainty here would be lying; it would be speaking from nowhere, as the pillar says, while actually speaking from a very particular place: the place of false authority.

The chapter also makes language's constructedness explicit thematically:

"no word, sign, mark, image in the silent flood"

Mr. Spitzer will leave no writing. He is "the writings of mutes for the ears of the dead": language that cannot be received, communication that is structurally incomplete. And yet the chapter is full of language, dense with it. The gap between the silence Mr. Spitzer will leave and the extraordinary verbosity of Young's prose describing that silence is itself a statement about language: language is what we use to point at what language cannot contain.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

This is perhaps the pillar most beautifully realized in the chapter. Mr. Spitzer does not seek transcendence upward and outward. He finds (or rather, he is) transcendence folded into the ordinary and the dying.

"His little firefly was the soul of the dead. Perhaps his little firefly was all there was."

The firefly. Not the star, not the constellation, not the divine light: the firefly. Small, brief, organic, already dying. And yet: "perhaps his little firefly was all there was." This is immanent transcendence stated with absolute precision. The sacred is here, in the smallest and most fugitive thing, not despite its smallness but because of it.

The chapter repeatedly pulls transcendent imagery (stars, constellations, the Holy Writ, eternity) back into the immanent:

"There was no other star. There was no garden where the white peacocks screamed in a snow storm on any other star than this"

This is the theological statement of the chapter. There is no other star. The transcendence that exists is this transcendence: the peacocks screaming in the snowstorm on this star, this dying man with his firefly key, this moment. The chapter does not mourn the absence of a beyond. It insists on the sufficiency, the extraordinary sufficiency, of the here.

Even death is immanentized:

"this which he was living now—for when had he lived before his death?"

His afterlife is this life. The life after death is the life he is living now, after whatever death he has already passed through. Transcendence is not deferred to another world. It is located in the present moment of this one.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This pillar finds its most complete expression in the chapter's treatment of Mr. Spitzer's relationship to the dead and the not-yet-born:

"was not the Holy Writ but was the mere flyleaf stuck between two passages or testaments, the Old and the New"

He is the crossing point between what came before and what comes after. He does not move through time; he is the place where the Old Testament and the New Testament touch each other. He is the blank page that is also the hinge.

The structural obligation of the crossing point is present in his relationship to the suffragette:

"She had died before he knew, before he had come with his arrangements by which to deceive the chaos, before he had placed her with her head pointing North and her feet pointing South that she might sleep according to the turning of the earth"

He tries to orient the dead according to the earth's axis: to align the individual body with the planet's turning. This is the crossing point made ritual: the dead woman's body placed at the intersection of her individual death and the earth's structural movement. He is constituted by her (he learned from her) and he is constituting the ground of her remembrance.

Most explicitly:

"he was merely the dream of the dream or the reflection of the reflection"

He is not original. He is derived from what came before him, and he will be the origin of what comes after him, but as a reflection, not as a source. The ancestors are in the sediment. He is standing on them. The carrying continues, and he will become sediment for those who follow.

The chapter ends with him writing a nocturne for the suffragette. This is the crossing point's obligation fulfilled: he carries her forward into language, into music, into the not-yet. He does not choose this obligation. It is structural. She deposited herself in him; he deposits her in the work.
