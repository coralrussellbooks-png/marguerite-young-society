Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Young's contribution to feminist thought

← Woman's Experiences · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Decolonization in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh

Marguerite Young's contribution to feminism is easy to undersell, because on its face the novel looks like a catalog: the practical nursemaid, the opium-dreaming mother, the perpetually pregnant waitress, the militant suffrage captain, a spectrum of female lives laid end to end. Read that way the book adds range to feminist literature and little else. The contribution is deeper and stranger than range. Young takes the female condition down to the level of consciousness, to the place where the self comes apart, and what she finds there has no gender. Women are her route in and not her subject at the bottom. She could reach that floor through women because their selfhood had already been broken open by their conditions, and the breaking apart the culture had done to them was the opening she needed. This is how a novel so uninterested in programming still enlarges the feminist tradition: it reads what happens to women all the way down, past the social surface to the structure of a self under pressure, and that structure belongs to everyone.

The vocabulary this edition uses for that structure, the pillars of a Radical Immanence, is contemporary, offered as a description of what the novel enacts and not as a philosophy Young held. The claim is that she arrived at these forms through fiction before the language existed to name them, and that her women are where the forms are clearest because the women were already living them.

Miss MacIntosh looks like the book's plainest feminist figure, and she is: the disdain for jewels and furs and superficial beauty is a real critique of a womanhood built on appearance, and her groundedness, her labor, her care for the poor and the working and the marginalized, ties that critique to a wider demand for justice. But her deepest work is not the critique. It is the wig lifted off the bald head, the amputated breast, the self stripped to its substance, the "porcelain crown" she says is under every face. She is the character built to look most single, and she comes apart most completely, and the stripping is revelation rather than punishment, a woman taken down to the body as the place the self comes apart and shown to be, at that depth, simply a human creature under the covering everyone wears.

Catherine reads as idealized feminine passivity decaying into loss of agency, a caution against beauty and dependence. She is more than a caution. She is scattered across her dream-guests, found in a mountain range and a pharaoh and a stranger's reflection and no single self called Catherine, and her opium twilight is the fullest case in the book of a person held on both sides of the line between living and dead at once. Her dissolution is plural selfhood and real and dream as one country, and it is not the failure of a woman so much as a consciousness gone deep enough that the categories holding a self together give way.

Esther Longtree carries the female body's burdens as the repeated pregnancies, the stillbirths, the social judgment, and she carries something under them. Her perpetual pregnancy that never delivers is the interrupted birth, which is unfinished-is-not-failed and permanent grief in the same body, the self that cannot complete and the sorrow that has no bottom. The reproductive politics are real and they are not the floor. The floor is a condition the old doctor's phantom deliveries and the wild mares bearing "waters and winds" carried before Esther ever did, a shape of consciousness that takes a woman's flesh here because a woman's flesh is where it shows.

Cousin Hannah is the radical feminist icon, the suffrage captain who fought "the overthrow of man," and she is also the woman "disguised as an Arab king," the "secret man" within women made a whole figure, the person the record keeps failing to hold. Her militancy and her dissolution of the fixed self are one motion. She is the crossing point given a biography, and her feminism is inseparable from the fact that she will not stay one sex, one identity, one thing the record can file.

Mr. Spitzer is the proof the frame reaches past gender, and putting him beside the women is the argument. He cannot tell which brother he is, he thins out toward transparency, he reaches the same coming-apart the women reach, by a different door. If the floor Young reaches through women were female at the bottom, he could not stand on it, and he does. What breaks a woman open in this book is what breaks anyone open. The women are the route because history had already done the breaking apart to them, not because the thing at the end of the route is theirs alone.

This is why the illusion of the individual is not a spiritual dimension added onto feminism but the bottom the feminism reaches. The interconnectedness the novel keeps stating, "every heart is the other heart," is what Young finds when she goes through the female self far enough, and it is the same whether a woman or a man arrives at it. The dissolution of the self is not a separate philosophical layer laid over the portraits of women. It is what the portraits are for.

Young said she never thought of herself as either a woman or a man, only as a person born to be a writer. That could be understood as a writer keeping her distance from feminism. Here, it is the thesis in the author's own mouth: she wrote from the crossing point. And the "rage against women" she met from reviewers, the men who "seemed to want to destroy" her and her writing, is what happens to a writer who refuses the category the culture insists on, evidence of the cost and not a contradiction of the claim.

Young's feminism, then, is a feminism of consciousness. She does not argue for women so much as go through them, down to the place where the self a woman is made to perform comes apart, and there she finds the structure that holds for anyone. That is her original contribution, and it is why the book belongs to feminism and exceeds it in the same breath. It honors the specific weight the world lays on women, and it follows that weight all the way to the floor where it stops being only theirs.
