Pillar: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible · Across every book

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

The ninth pillar addresses the medium through which all of the other pillars are expressed and contested.

If the self is plural, if reality is constructed, if knowledge is situated, if community is held difference, if time is sedimentary, then language is not the neutral medium through which these truths are communicated. It is the medium in which they are constructed, contested, and either honored or betrayed. The ninth pillar: language is not the vehicle of truth but the medium of its construction, and must be held accordingly.

Gertrude Stein's investigation of language, conducted across her poetry, her prose experiments, and her theoretical essays, is an investigation of the relationship between grammatical structure and the structure of experience. Stein understood that the grammar of a language is not a neutral tool for expressing pre-existing thoughts. It is a set of commitments about what kinds of things exist, how they relate to each other, and what counts as a complete thought. The noun, in standard English grammar, is the primary unit of existence. Things are nouns. People are nouns. The verb is what happens to or between nouns. This grammatical structure encodes a metaphysical commitment: the world is made of stable things that persist through time, and change and action are secondary to the stable things that undergo them.

Stein's experiments with grammar are experiments in building a language that can hold a different metaphysical commitment: the commitment to a world made of processes rather than things, of becoming rather than being, of the verb rather than the noun. Young inherits this project and extends it. The long sentences of Miss MacIntosh, My Darling by Marguerite Young, the sentences that circle back and refuse to finish and accumulate subordinate clauses like geological layers, are sentences that resist the grammatical commitment to stable nouns and clean predications. They are sentences built for a world where the self is a verb and reality is a construction and grief is permanent weather rather than a temporary condition.

The language through which political life is conducted is not a neutral medium. It is a set of commitments about who exists, what counts as a person, what kinds of experience are real and what kinds are marginal. The people whose existence is not captured by the stable nouns of political grammar are not simply underrepresented. They are grammatically erased. The plural self cannot be a noun in the grammar of liberal political theory, which requires the unified rational agent as its primary unit. The person whose identity is multiple, whose interests are not unified, whose experience crosses the categories that the grammar of political discourse has built, is not a person that the grammar can process.

Young's language, with its resistance to the stable noun and its commitment to the ongoing process, is a political act in this sense. It is the construction of a grammar that can hold the plural self, that can give voice to the experience that standard political grammar erases, that can make visible the people who are invisible to the language of the long con.

Adrienne Rich's essay "Notes Toward a Politics of Location" brings the ninth pillar into direct conversation with feminist practice. Rich argues that political speech is always located, always spoken from a particular body in a particular place with a particular history, and that the pretense of speaking from nowhere is not a form of universality but a form of domination. The voice that claims to speak for everyone is always the voice of someone in particular. Rich's politics of location requires that speakers name their location, acknowledge the partiality of their perspective, and build their political speech from the honest acknowledgment of where they are standing rather than from the pretense of standing nowhere.

This is the ninth pillar's political expression. Language that names its own location is language that can be held accountable. Language that pretends to speak from nowhere is language that cannot be questioned, because there is no particular location to question. Young's novel is located. It is located in Indiana, in the Grey Goose bus, in the particular grief of Vera's childhood with Miss MacIntosh and Catherine. It does not pretend to speak from nowhere. It speaks from the accumulated sediment of a particular life, and the universality it achieves is the universality that comes from depth rather than from the pretense of height.
