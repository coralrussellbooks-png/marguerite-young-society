To All My Darlings

Chapter 73

On Chapter 73 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 10, 2026

← Chapter 72 · Home · Chapter 74 →

Content note: The Esther Longtree chapters (72 to 81) deal with stillbirth and pregnancy loss, rape, infanticide, racist violence including lynching, and cruelty to children. This chapter describes stillbirths and the hidden remains of infants.

Summary

Esther Longtree, everlastingly pregnant, has given birth so often to nothing that she remembers her stillborn only as fragments, “an eye, an ear, a long gold hair, a fingernail, a cobweb,” hidden in hollow stumps, streams, and snowdrifts, and the undertaker complains that she brings him no business. Dying fathers, salesmen and insurance agents, write to ask whether their children lived, and on old Doctor’s advice she says nothing. The strongest of them, Joe Goldberg, the Jewish featherweight champion now wasting from tuberculosis in the Colorado mountains, writes of the night he raped her among the tombs of a hillside graveyard and calls her the greatest victory of his life; she remembers the graveyard but not him. An old grey mule, barren, stands by at every love. She fears a trial for murder, for the old Chicago detective, another of the fathers, is hunting proof that she killed his three. She remembers a labor alone under a barren dwarf apple tree, summer turning to winter in a single night, and the birth of nothing, the hardest birth to bear, while a white feather drifted down from some Arctic bird.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens and repeatedly returns to the motif of Esther’s “everlasting pregnancy,” a state in which she is perpetually carrying children who are never fully born or who are stillborn, described as fragments like “an eye, an ear, a long gold hair, a fingernail, a cobweb.” This surreal condition embodies her maternal nature and the sorrow she carries, as well as her isolation and the mystery surrounding the fate of these children. Her swollen womb and the “whistle of the stillborn in her ears” symbolize this burden.
  • Throughout the chapter, the narrative emphasizes the letters sent to Esther by several dying men “the fathers” who inquire about the children they supposedly fathered with her. These men seek reassurance that their progeny live, clinging to hope at the moment of death. Esther’s silence in response to them, advised by her old, drunken doctor to preserve their vanity and peace, highlights her protective but tragic role. She cannot confirm their hopes because, in fact, the children are “snowflakes,” “marsh butterflies,” or other insubstantial forms.
  • A particularly poignant and detailed passage concerns Joe Goldberg, the champion featherweight boxer who wrote long, anguished letters to Esther as he wasted away from tuberculosis. He remembered her as the “peak of his existence,” the greatest “victory” among his battles, and begged for remembrance and forgiveness. Esther, however, cannot recall him clearly as his image is “lost in clouds” and like “the weight of a feather” in her mind, unlike the weaker men whose memories linger more distinctly. This contrast underscores themes of memory, loss, and the elusive nature of identity and connection.
  • Joe Goldberg’s memory of meeting Esther in a graveyard on a foggy, windy night is recounted in detail. It was rape. He “had taken her against her will,” he “had held her pinioned in his arms,” and he “had knocked off the nose of an angel as they struggled,” yet he remembers it as the greatest victory of his life. The graveyard setting ties the crime to death and decay, reinforcing the chapter’s motifs of mortality and the ambiguity of life and conception.
  • Repeatedly, the narrative returns to Esther’s silence and her solitary endurance of her condition. She neither confirms nor denies the existence of the children to the fathers and has no answers for the legal or emotional claims made upon her. Her silence is portrayed as a form of protection for herself and the dying men and as a reflection of the “imponderable answer” she cannot find.
  • One of the most emotionally charged passages describes a labor in which Esther endures all the pains of childbirth but delivers “nothing.” This “birth of nothing” symbolizes her profound loss and the futility she feels: her body is broken, her labor intense, but no child emerges. The passage conveys the deepest sorrow of her experience and her isolation in this repeated tragedy.
  • The chapter uses the image of an old mule, barren and mournful, as a recurring symbol of sterility and loneliness that “followed” Esther. Similarly, the white feather drifting down during her labor is interpreted as a possible “message from the silence,” a sign of hope amid despair. These symbols deepen the thematic exploration of fertility, loss, and the fragile boundary between life and death.
  • Esther faces the threat of legal inquiry and possible trial for “murder” due to the disappearance of her stillborn children, as the undertaker complains that she has not brought business to the graveyard. This introduces a grim social dimension to her plight, where her mysterious condition could be criminalized, adding to her burden of silence and secrecy.
  • Throughout, Esther struggles with memory: she cannot recall all the fathers or children, especially the strong featherweight champion, despite his vivid letters and pleas. This lapse underscores the chapter’s meditation on the instability of personal identity, the limits of memory, and the ephemeral nature of human connections.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “The birth of nothing was the hardest birth to bear.” Why would it be harder than any other?
  • “Her lack of any feeling was her deepest feeling now.” How can an absence be the deepest feeling?
  • “A woman’s faith was her pregnancy.” What does faith mean for Esther?

Characters

  • Joe Goldberg calls the night he raped Esther the “peak of his existence.” What does it say about him that he counts her among his victories?
  • “The strongest men were the weakest.” Is this true of Joe Goldberg?
  • Old Doctor tells Esther to say nothing to the dying fathers. “Supreme knowledge was always the last cruelty.” Is her silence kindness or cruelty?

Connections

  • Joe comes of a family of fighters “beginning with those who had fought the angels.” How is his last bout, with God on a peak above the clouds, like Jacob’s?
  • The salesman thinks his wife and her twin are two women “like the evening star and the morning star,” but Esther knows “the evening star and the morning star were one star.” Where else in the novel are two people one?
  • The old mule, “the barren animal who could not be mated,” stands by at every love. Why does it follow Esther?

Reflection

  • “Why were the sterile fathers never blamed?” Who is blamed for a lost child, then and now?
  • “Time would heal all wounds but hers.” Are there wounds that time does not heal?
  • The graveyard children were “cut down in the flower of their youth” and given funerals, while Esther’s had none. Why do some deaths get mourned and others hidden?

Research: Characters

Esther Longtree

  • Big-boned, slow-moving waitress, cross-eyed, with a deformed foot and swollen womb.
  • Maternal, compassionate, yet burdened by a mysterious, continuous pregnancy with stillborn children.
  • Silent and evasive when questioned by fathers, embodying endurance, sorrow, and resilience.
  • Symbolizes fertility, loss, and the complex intersection of memory and identity.

Joe Goldberg (Champion Featherweight)

  • A once-powerful boxing champion, now frail and dying of tuberculosis.
  • Writes long, emotional letters to Esther, hoping to be remembered and to claim his child.
  • Represents strength, vulnerability, and the tragic human fight against death.
  • He raped Esther in a graveyard and remembers it as the “peak of his existence”; she remembers the graveyard but not him.

Old Doctor

  • A drunken, disillusioned physician who advises Esther to maintain silence with dying men.
  • Offers a philosophical perspective on death, illness, and the illusions surrounding conception and fertility.

The Fathers (Traveling Salesmen, Sterile Men)

  • Many are weak, sterile, or self-centered men who impregnated Esther but never took responsibility.
  • They symbolize failure, disappointment, and the social stigma attached to Esther’s condition.

The Chicago detective

  • A symbolic figure representing societal judgment, scrutiny, and the external pressures faced by Esther Longtree. Though he appears only indirectly through Esther’s thoughts and fears, his presence is significant and multifaceted.
  • The detective symbolizes the potential legal and moral condemnation that Esther fears. She imagines a scenario where she might be arrested, tried, and accused of murder for the stillborn children she has carried and lost over the years. The detective’s investigation represents society’s attempt to impose order and assign blame on Esther for the tragic outcomes of her pregnancies, despite her innocence in her own mind.
  • The detective is “old” and “worm-ridden,” and he is himself one of the fathers, “another old, sterile father,” hunting proof “that she had killed his three whom she had never borne or who were born without eyes.” He stands for the many fathers who impregnated Esther and took no responsibility for the children. The detective’s search for proof and evidence reflects a broader theme of male absence and desertion, with Esther bearing the burden alone.
  • Esther fears the detective’s inquiries, which might lead to public exposure of her condition and the stillborn children. She worries about appearing before a jury while visibly pregnant, the social humiliation involved, and the invasive nature of such an investigation. This fear adds to her isolation and suffering, emphasizing the harshness of societal judgment towards women in her position.
  • The detective’s efforts to find evidence of murder or wrongdoing are ultimately futile, as Esther has hidden the stillborn children in natural places (streams, fields, under snowdrifts) making proof elusive. This futility underscores the theme of invisibility and loss that permeates Esther’s life and the narrative. The detective’s inability to find tangible evidence mirrors Esther’s own uncertainty about the fate and existence of the children.
  • Through the detective’s imagined role, the narrator Vera explores themes of justice, mercy, and the limits of human understanding. Esther’s silence and refusal to answer inquiries, partly motivated by kindness to the dying fathers and partly by self-preservation, contrast with the detective’s probing. This tension raises questions about who is truly culpable and how society deals with grief and loss.

Phoebe

  • Phoebe is mentioned in the context of a graveyard, specifically as “the old washwoman’s grave” with a statue that has a bulbous nose, warts, wrinkles, and a sunbonnet. Joe Goldberg names her statue in his letters as proof that he was in the graveyard with Esther.
  • Phoebe’s statue symbolizes one of the many tombs in the graveyard, representing the past and the presence of the dead in the narrative. She is part of the imagery of mortality and memory that permeates the story.
  • Phoebe is a symbolic figure within the narrative, evoking themes of death, memory, and the passage of time.

Coleman Bluebough

  • Coleman Bluebough is mentioned in the context of Esther Longtree’s story as a figure associated with old deaths and graves, named on a tombstone in the graveyard where Joe Goldberg raped Esther.
  • Esther Longtree recalls the name Coleman Bluebough, who died at the age of three, and his place is marked among other graves and memorials in the narrative.
  • The mention of Coleman Bluebough helps underscore themes of death, stillbirth, and loss that pervade Esther Longtree’s life and experiences, particularly her repeated pregnancies and the stillborn children she mourns.
  • Coleman Bluebough represents one of the many children who have died young or were stillborn, figures that are part of Esther Longtree’s troubled memories and grief.

Abigail Porter

  • Abigail Porter is referenced as a person whose grave is marked in the graveyard, and she is among the many figures evoked in the narration that surrounds Esther Longtree’s story.
  • The graveyard where she rests is described as a place filled with various symbolic elements like crosses, rustling owls, winged beings, inscriptions, and old statues. Abigail Porter is part of a broader meditation on death, memory, and loss within the narrative.

Research: Themes

  • Esther Longtree is a woman perpetually pregnant, carrying the memory and burden of many stillborn children: fragments such as an eye, ear, fingernail, cobweb, or snowflake rather than whole babies. This condition is both literal and symbolic, representing loss, unfulfilled motherhood, and isolation. Her pregnancy is a metaphor for enduring sorrow and hope intertwined.
  • Esther struggles to remember the fathers of her many lost children and even the details of conception, while the dying fathers vividly recall her and their supposed children. The featherweight champion Joe Goldberg, one of the few who wrote her, haunts her memory, but she cannot fully recall him, symbolizing the elusive and fragmented nature of memory.
  • Despite her perpetual pregnancy and maternal instincts, Esther is isolated, misunderstood, and burdened with silence. She mothered many children who never lived, and she bears the emotional weight alone, rarely responding to inquiries from dying fathers, protecting their illusions and her own fragile peace.
  • The novel explores the boundary between life and death, conception and loss. The children are often invisible and symbolized as feathers, snowflakes, or shadows which highlight the ephemeral nature of existence and the impossibility of closure or concrete answers.
  • Joe Goldberg represents a powerful figure in Esther’s life and the narrative: strong and victorious in his prime, yet reduced to a frail, dying man battling God. His letters to Esther reveal his longing for remembrance and his hopes for their child, underscoring themes of memory, regret, and the human desire for legacy.
  • Esther faces potential accusations, including murder of her stillborn children, reflecting societal judgment and misunderstanding of her condition. She fears trial and public humiliation, but she remains protective of her silence and her unborn “little shadow boxer.”

Research: Symbols and Imagery

  • Stillborn Children as Snowflakes, Feathers, Cobwebs: These fragile, ephemeral images represent the children that Esther carries but never fully births. They highlight invisibility, loss, and the intangible nature of her motherhood.
  • The Graveyard: The location of Esther’s encounter with Joe Goldberg symbolizes death, the boundary between life and afterlife, and the haunting memory of their union.
  • The Old Mule: Represents sterility, loneliness, and the persistence of life without fruition. It is Esther’s constant companion symbolizing the barrenness surrounding her fertility.
  • The Little Shadow Boxer: The unborn child she feels under her heart, symbolizing hope, continuation, and the enduring mystery of life and death.
  • Blood-Stained Clothes and Bruises: Signify the pain, violence, and social stigma Esther endures during her labors and life. They also underscore the physical and emotional toll of her condition.

Research: Resources

  • “said Esther Longtree, a big-boned, slow-moving waitress”: Esther Longtree emerges as a profoundly tragic and complex figure burdened by perpetual pregnancy and the sorrow of countless stillborn children. Her character is marked by an overwhelming maternal instinct intertwined with mystery, loneliness, and a haunting sense of loss. Esther is described as a “big, dumb-headed waitress,” slow-moving and physically altered by her condition: one foot is deformed, her womb swollen, and her body constantly expanding with what she perceives as an “everlasting pregnancy.” This physical state symbolizes her ceaseless burden and the weight of her unresolved grief. Her dark hair often drifts before her eyes, symbolizing both a literal and metaphorical veil between her and the world around her, emphasizing her isolation and internal confusion. Despite the numerous pregnancies resulting only in stillborns or incomplete births (“an eye, an ear, a long gold hair, a fingernail, a cobweb”) Esther remains intensely maternal. She has mothered everything from her childhood thumb and big toe to a bullfrog, underscoring her nurturing nature even in the face of continual loss. This maternal impulse is paradoxical; she is both the source of life and a witness to death and absence, which causes her embarrassment and deep sorrow. Many men, termed “fathers,” have impregnated her, yet none seem to have produced a living child. These men are generally weak, sterile, or dying, and several write to Esther as they face death, seeking acknowledgment of their supposed offspring. Esther, however, rarely answers these inquiries. She prefers to maintain silence to preserve the dignity and hope of the dying fathers, fearing the cruelty that truth would impose. This silence also protects her from the pain of confronting the truth about her stillborn children and the uncertain paternity of her current perpetual pregnancy. A significant figure in Esther’s narrative is Joe Goldberg, the champion featherweight boxer, who wrote to her desperately in his dying days. He remembers their encounter in a graveyard: he raped her there, “had taken her against her will,” “deflowered her,” and “had knocked her out, stone cold.” Despite his vivid memories and longing for recognition, Esther cannot recall him clearly, only the graveyard and the circumstances of their meeting. This failure to remember symbolizes her fragmented and overwhelmed state of mind. Joe represents the strongest of the fathers who becomes weak and ephemeral like a feather, and Esther’s inability to anchor him in her memory contrasts sharply with her vivid remembrance of the weaker men. Esther’s narrative is steeped in symbolic imagery: the stillborn children as snowflakes, feathers, cobwebs, and shadows; the old mule representing sterility and loneliness; the graveyard setting for the conception of Joe Goldberg’s child; and the recurring motif of silence as both protection and sorrow. Her “everlasting pregnancy” could be read as a metaphor for enduring grief, unfulfilled potential, and the human condition of bearing loss silently. Her story is riddled with ambiguity about reality versus memory, truth versus illusion, and life versus death. Esther is socially isolated, working alone in a restaurant with fewer and fewer customers, burdened by her physical state and the mystery of her pregnancies. There is an undercurrent of potential accusation against her, from a Chicago detective who suspects her in a forgotten murder of stillborn children to the threat of a public trial. This looming social judgment contrasts with Esther’s own self-view as a suffering, unselfish mother who has never demanded support or recognition from the fathers. Despite her suffering and the enormity of her loss, Esther exhibits resilience. She continues “waxing, waxing, never waning,” embodying a strange, almost supernatural endurance. Yet there is also resignation and confusion: she cannot fully grasp her own condition, cannot remember many fathers, and exists in a liminal space between hope and despair, life and death. Esther Longtree is a figure of tragic maternal devotion, loss, and mystery. Her physical and emotional existence is defined by an endless cycle of pregnancy and stillbirth, a heavy silence toward the men who impregnated her, and a haunting ambiguity about what is real and remembered. Her story explores themes of fertility, death, memory, and the human tendency to shield others from harsh truths through silence. She stands as a poignant embodiment of suffering and endurance. See Esther Longtree.
    • Esther Longtree symbolizes a complex and tragic history of loss, abandonment, and the elusive promise of fertility and continuity that can be read metaphorically as reflecting aspects of American history. Her character embodies the sorrow of countless lost hopes and unfulfilled potential: she is “everlastingly pregnant” yet only ever gives birth to stillborn children: fragments like “snowflakes,” “cobwebs,” “little feet,” or “golden hair.” These stillborns are the children of many fathers, most of whom are sterile or absent, and who only awaken a sense of conscience or responsibility at their deathbed, asking about the children they never knew. Esther’s silence toward these dying fathers symbolizes the unanswerable questions of lineage, legacy, and accountability in a fragmented, transient society where promises remain unfulfilled and where the past cannot be fully reconciled with the present. This condition can be interpreted as symbolic of the broader American experience, particularly the history of displacement, broken promises, and the elusiveness of the American Dream. Esther’s endless pregnancies with no living offspring reflect the cycles of hope and disappointment in a land marked by migration, loss, and the failure of paternal or institutional responsibility. The many “fathers” who impregnate her but then disappear or fail to support her children can be related to the many waves of settlers, immigrants, and transient individuals who have contributed to the making of America but whose legacies are uncertain or incomplete. The fact that Esther hides her stillborns “everywhere,” in streams, under snow, in tree stumps, and yet is threatened with accusations of murder, can be seen as a metaphor for the buried or forgotten histories of marginalized people and the silenced suffering that underlies American progress. The undertaker’s complaint that she brings no business because she hides the evidence of death symbolizes the societal reluctance or failure to confront these hidden tragedies. Her life is also connected to a landscape marked by graveyards, broken fences, and natural elements like wind, snow, and autumn leaves, evoking the American frontier and the harsh realities faced by those living on its margins. Esther Longtree’s tragic and ambiguous history symbolizes the haunted, fragmented, and often unacknowledged history of America itself: a land of many fathers but few fulfilled legacies; a place where life and death, hope and despair, fertility and barrenness coexist; and where the true stories of loss and endurance are obscured by silence and the passage of time. Esther Longtree stands as a profound metaphor for the unresolved burdens and contradictions in American history (the tension between promise and loss, presence and absence, birth and death) that shape the nation’s identity.
  • “the champion featherweight whose letters”: “Battling” was a popular ring name in the early twentieth century, carried by fighters such as Battling Nelson and Battling Siki. The featherweight class, set at 126 pounds, sits just above bantamweight.
    • Historical weight classes for boxing are: heavyweight at 200+ pounds, light heavyweight at 168-175 pounds, middleweight at 154-160 pounds, welterweight at 140-147 pounds, lightweight at 130-135 pounds, featherweight at 122-126 pounds, bantamweight at 115-118 pounds, flyweight at 108-112 pounds.
  • “his long practice of medica materia”: Materia medica (It encompasses ancient and medieval knowledge of remedies, with a foundational text being Dioscorides’ De Materia Medica) refers to the comprehensive body of knowledge, historically cataloging the origins, properties, and preparation of substances used to treat diseases. These remedies are derived from natural sources, primarily plants, but also animals and minerals, and are considered precursors to modern pharmacology.
  • “too, too solid for his tastes”: “O, that this too too solid flesh would melt, / Thaw and resolve itself into a dew!” (Hamlet, 1.2). Dew falls all through this chapter. See Shakespeare.
  • “worse than the Chaldeans”: In the Bible the Chaldeans are the Babylonians of Nebuchadnezzar, who burned the Temple and carried the people of Judah into captivity (2 Kings 25). Habakkuk calls them “that bitter and hasty nation” (Habakkuk 1:6, KJV), and in Daniel they are the king’s astrologers: “the magicians, and the astrologers, and the sorcerers, and the Chaldeans” (Daniel 2:2, KJV). For a Jewish fighter, no people could be worse. See The Bible and Judaism.
    • Chaldeans may refer to Chaldean Catholics who are adherents of the Chaldean Catholic Church, which originates from the historic Church of the East. The Chaldean community originates from groups of adherents of the Church of the East that entered into communion with the Roman Catholic Church in the 16th and 17th centuries. They were indigenous to the regions of northern Iraq, southeastern Turkey, northeastern Syria and northwestern Iran, many have migrated to Western countries including the United States, Canada, Mexico, Australia, Sweden and Germany. Many of them also live in Lebanon, Egypt, Palestine, Israel, Jordan, Iran, Turkey, and Georgia. (Wikipedia)
  • “the salesmen, the insurance agents”: American traveling salesmen (or “drummers”) were vital, iconic agents of commerce, bridging manufacturers with rural and small-town retailers throughout the 19th and early 20th centuries. They drove expansion by selling everything from textiles and machinery to household goods via rail and later automobiles, often utilizing direct-to-consumer, persuasive tactics before declining due to urbanization. Salesmen used personal charm and sometimes exaggerated pitches to move goods, often operating under a “caveat emptor” (buyer beware) market. They introduced items like sewing machines, pots, encyclopedias, and personal care products.
  • “In her vast super-fecundation”: In medicine, superfecundation is the fertilizing of two or more eggs from one cycle in separate acts of intercourse, so that twins may have different fathers. See Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery.
  • “the brave little Jew, hard as rock”: Jewish fighters ruled American boxing in the first half of the twentieth century: twenty-six Jewish world champions between 1910 and 1940, most of them wearing the Star of David on their trunks. Abe Attell held the featherweight title from 1906 to 1912 (he first claimed it in 1901) and was later tied to the fixing of the 1919 World Series. Benny Leonard was lightweight champion from 1917 to 1925, and Barney Ross, raised in Chicago, held titles in the 1930s. See Joe Goldberg and Judaism.
  • “Tuberculosis, God’s hand”: Tuberculosis was a leading cause of death in the US in the early 1900s, thriving in crowded, unsanitary, and poverty-stricken urban areas. Before effective antibiotics (streptomycin in 1944, isoniazid in 1952), treatment focused on sanatoriums, fresh air, and surgery. Mortality fell from 194 per 100,000 in 1900 to 40 by 1945 due to public health initiatives.
  • “Joe Goldberg, the golden cock”: The surname Goldberg is of German/Yiddish origin, meaning “gold mountain,” formed from the German words Gold (gold) and Berg (mountain/hill). It’s a common Ashkenazi Jewish surname, used both as a habitational name from places named Goldberg and as an artificial or ornamental name, often adopted for its sound or association with wealth. Joe, the gold mountain, dies in the mountains and calls Esther the “peak of his existence among the snowy peaks.” See Joe Goldberg.
  • “an Indiana waitress said to be everlastingly pregnant”: Indiana’s history spans from prehistoric Native American mounds to becoming the 19th state in 1816 and establishing itself as “The Crossroads of America” by 1937. Key milestones included significant Civil War participation, pioneering professional baseball in 1871, and becoming a manufacturing and agricultural hub. See Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest.
  • “in Chicago or St. Louis or Kansas City”: Chicago, founded in 1833 and incorporated in 1837, grew from a small trading post at the mouth of the Chicago River into a major industrial, rail, and manufacturing hub by the mid-20th century. Key eras included rapid growth after the 1871 fire, pioneering skyscraper architecture (1880s-90s), and becoming a postwar, segregated city by the 1950s.
    • Founded in 1764 by French fur traders, St. Louis grew from a strategic Mississippi River trading post into a major 19th-century industrial and transportation hub. Following its 1876 separation from the county, the city boomed as a manufacturing center, peaked in population around 1950, and began facing suburban flight and modernization, including early planning for the Gateway Arch.
    • Kansas City was founded in the 1830s as a Missouri River trading post, evolving from a frontier river town into a major 19th-century railroad hub and cattle center. Known as a wide-open “Paris of the Plains” during Prohibition, the city grew significantly through the mid-20th century, developing a strong jazz scene, a thriving downtown, and expanding its physical footprint significantly by 1960.
  • “a man dying in the Colorado mountains”: Colorado’s history was defined by Native American habitation, Spanish exploration, and a rapid transition from a mining frontier to a 38th state (1876). The 1858-59 Pike’s Peak Gold Rush sparked major settlement, while the late 19th-century economy shifted to ranching and farming.
    • Doctors sent consumptives west for the dry, high air, and Denver filled with them. National Jewish Hospital for Consumptives opened there in 1899 under the motto “None who enter shall pay; none who can pay shall enter,” and the Jewish Consumptives’ Relief Society opened at Lakewood in 1904. See Judaism.
  • “married to a twin whose identical twin was married to no one”: Twins who change places in the night echo the Spitzer twins, Joachim and Peron, one never sure which of the two he is. See Joachim Spitzer, Peron Spitzer, and Duality and Doubles.
  • “the evening star and the morning star were one star”: The evening star and the morning star are both the planet Venus, called Hesperus at dusk and Phosphorus at dawn; the Greeks credited Pythagoras or Parmenides with discovering they were one. In 1892 the philosopher Gottlob Frege made “the morning star is the evening star” his famous example of two names with one meaning. See Astronomy and Duality and Doubles.
  • “high above the tree-line where no birds sang”: “The sedge has withered from the lake, / And no birds sing” (Keats, “La Belle Dame sans Merci,” 1819). See Romantic Poets.
  • “the pygmies, the Egyptians, the Irish, whoever they were”: Fight posters billed boxers by nation and race, and Irish-American fighters such as John L. Sullivan, heavyweight champion from 1882 to 1892, ruled the American ring before the Jewish fighters rose. “Whoever they were” shows how little his opponents meant to Joe.
    • Pygmies are among Africa’s oldest inhabitants, with ancestors diverging from other populations roughly 60,000 to 100,000 years ago, inhabiting Central African rainforests as hunter-gatherers. They are characterized by short stature (often under 5 feet) and traditionally live in intimate, symbiotic relationships with neighboring Bantu farming communities.
    • In 1904 the St. Louis World’s Fair put Ota Benga, a Mbuti man from the Congo, on display in its anthropology exhibit, and in 1906 the Bronx Zoo caged him in its monkey house until Black ministers forced his release. He died by suicide in Lynchburg, Virginia, in 1916. See Society, Class, and Race.
    • Ancient Egyptian civilization, emerging around 3100 B.C. after the unification of Upper and Lower Egypt, thrived for over 3,000 years along the Nile River. Known for pharaohs, hieroglyphs, and monumental architecture like the pyramids. This advanced society was sustained by the Nile’s predictable floods. The history is divided into Old, Middle, and New Kingdoms. See Ancient Egypt.
    • Irish history spans from prehistoric hunter-gatherer settlements around 10,000 BCE to a modern, independent nation. Defined by Celtic (Gaelic) culture, it endured Viking raids, Norman conquests (1169), and English colonization, leading to the 17th-century Penal Laws, the Great Famine (1845-1851), and 20th-century independence.
  • “a fighter in the Jewish family, beginning with those who had fought the angels”: “And Jacob was left alone; and there wrestled a man with him until the breaking of the day” (Genesis 32:24, KJV). The man renamed him Israel, “for as a prince hast thou power with God and with men, and hast prevailed” (Genesis 32:28, KJV); Hosea says “he had power over the angel, and prevailed” (Hosea 12:4, KJV). Joe’s last bout, with God on a peak above the clouds, is Jacob’s, and he is losing it. See The Bible, Angels and Archangels, and Judaism.
  • “the birth control devices littering the bare ground”: Before 1965, birth control in the US relied on barriers, withdrawal, and, later, hormonal methods, with access heavily restricted by the Comstock Act of 1873. Common methods included condoms (vulcanized rubber by 1839), diaphragms, spermicides, and dangerous, ineffective douches marketed as “feminine hygiene.” The first oral contraceptive pill was approved in 1960. See Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery and Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “old Phoebe’s statue”: Old Phoebe was a washwoman, and the Phoebe of the Bible was a servant of the church: “I commend unto you Phebe our sister, which is a servant of the church which is at Cenchrea” (Romans 16:1, KJV; the King James spells her name Phebe). The Greek word translated “servant” is diakonos, deacon, and she is the only woman so named in the Bible; Paul trusted her to carry his letter to Rome. Where modern translations of 1 Timothy 3:11 speak of women deacons, the King James Version has “Even so must their wives be grave, not slanderers, sober, faithful in all things.” In Greek mythology Phoebe was a Titaness, grandmother of Apollo and Artemis; the name means bright. See Phoebe, The Bible, Women, Gender, and Marriage, and Greek Mythology.
  • “Ephemeral all die at sunset”: Mayflies belong to the order Ephemeroptera, from the Greek ephemeros, “lasting a day”; the adults live from a few hours to a day or two. Aristotle wrote of a creature on the river Hypanis that lives a single day. See Death and Mortality.
  • “Coleman Bluebough, dead at the age of three”: See Coleman Bluebough.
  • “Mrs. Abigail Porter, who folded up her tent and crept silently away”: “And the night shall be filled with music, / And the cares, that infest the day, / Shall fold their tents, like the Arabs, / And as silently steal away” (Longfellow, “The Day Is Done,” 1844). See Abigail Porter.
  • “cut down in the flower of their youth”: “He cometh forth like a flower, and is cut down: he fleeth also as a shadow, and continueth not” (Job 14:2, KJV). See The Bible and Death and Mortality.
  • “taken by the Ghostly Reaper’s sickle”: The Grim Reaper originated in 14th-century Europe, evolving from medieval personifications of death during the Black Death pandemic. Representing the inevitability of mortality, this figure is typically depicted as a skeleton in a dark robe carrying a scythe to harvest souls. It blends ancient mythologies, such as the Greek figure Cronus, with a profound, culturally pervasive fear of the unknown. See the Ghostly Reaper in the Chapter 68 resources. See Death and Mortality.
  • “The old grey mule had stood with head hanging over a fence”: See the barren mule in the Chapter 72 resources. See Horses and Carriages.
  • “under the frozen cranberry marsh”: A cranberry marsh (or bog) is a specialized wetland habitat where cranberries grow on low-running vines in acidic soil, typically in Wisconsin, Massachusetts, and other northern regions. Contrary to popular belief, they do not grow in water year-round, but are flooded in autumn for harvest, as the berries float, and in winter for frost protection.
  • “Something under the green quince”: In the US, green quince are typically under-ripe, hard, and extremely tart, often covered in a fuzzy coating that disappears as they turn golden-yellow in late fall. Primarily grown in California and imported from Chile, these fragrant fruits are available from August through December in specialty, Latin American, or Middle Eastern markets.
  • “the old Chicago detective was trying to find the proof”: England’s Act to Prevent the Destroying and Murthering of Bastard Children (1624) presumed that an unmarried mother who hid the death of her newborn had murdered it, unless she could prove “by one witness at the least” that the child was born dead; the penalty was death. It stood until 1803. In Massachusetts Hannah Piggen was reportedly the last woman hanged for concealing a birth, in 1785 (Wikipedia). Concealment of birth is still a crime in many states. See The Chicago Detective, Women, Gender, and Marriage, and Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery.
  • “the flowering horse chestnut, the bloody flower”: The horse chestnut, introduced to the US from Europe in the 1700s, is a popular, large ornamental shade tree featuring showy white spring flowers, palmately compound leaves, and inedible, shiny brown nuts enclosed in spiky, green husks. Common in urban landscapes, parks, and the Midwest, these trees thrive in zones 3-7.
  • “dead robin redbreast under a ten-foot snowdrift”: See “Who killed cock robin?” and the Babes in the Wood, covered with leaves by robins, in the Chapter 72 resources. See Fairy Tales and Folklore and Birds.
  • “she might endure the birth of nothing”: “We have been with child, we have been in pain, we have as it were brought forth wind” (Isaiah 26:18, KJV). See The Bible and Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery.
  • “a white feather of some far-flying Arctic bird”: To show the white feather is to show cowardice, from the cockpit, where a white feather in a gamecock’s tail was taken as the mark of a poor fighter. The feather falls for the featherweight, the golden cock. See Birds and Joe Goldberg.
