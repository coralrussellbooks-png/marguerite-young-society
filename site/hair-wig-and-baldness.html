Definition from research

Chapter 4 · Red Hair and Red Rooster’s Wing

  • Symbolizes vitality, passion, and the vividness of Miss MacIntosh’s presence.

Chapter 5 · Hair

  • Symbolizes identity, resistance, and the last vestige of personal freedom.
  • The bus-driver’s hair grows uncontrollably, reflecting his wild imagination and refusal to conform.

Chapter 12 · Hair

  • Symbolic of protection and sensitivity; also connected to historical and biblical references.
  • The meditation on hair foreshadows Miss MacIntosh’s baldness in Chapter 17.

Chapter 12

  • “the hair raising to warn us of our mortal danger”: “Then a spirit passed before my face; the hair of my flesh stood up” (Job 4:15).
  • “The most beautiful ladies of history had been noted for their hair”: Queen Berenice gave her hair to the gods for her husband’s safe return, and it was set among the stars as Coma Berenices.
  • “Absalom was caught among the apple boughs and hanged by his hair”: In 2 Samuel 18:9, Absalom’s “head caught hold of the oak.” Vera makes it an apple tree. His heavy hair was cut once a year (2 Samuel 14:26).
  • “Leonardo had forgotten to give eyebrows to his Mona Lisa”: The Mona Lisa (c. 1503-1519) is a half-length portrait by Leonardo da Vinci. The painting shows no eyebrows or lashes, though Giorgio Vasari (1550) praised its eyebrows, “the manner in which the hairs spring from the flesh.”

Chapter 17

  • “she was bald. She was bald as the egg”: Alopecia, the total loss of hair, was popularly blamed on “some sudden shock or amazement or illness.” The hair passage of Chapter 12 prepared for this, and in Chapter 19 Vera finds Miss MacIntosh’s red wig.

Chapter 17 · Baldness

  • Represents loss, emptiness, exposure, and transformation.
  • Compared to “the egg,” “the rock where nothing grows,” “the rose stripped of all its petals,” and “bare as the winter winds,” emphasizing barrenness and desolation.

Chapter 18 · Baldness of Miss MacIntosh

  • Represents the stripping away of illusion, revealing raw and uncomfortable truth.
  • Symbolizes mortality, vulnerability, and the loss of pretense.

Chapter 18

  • “The bald head is soonest shaved”: Miss MacIntosh turns old jokes about baldness on herself, here and in “No grass grows on a busy street.”

Chapter 19

  • “Her red wig, almost purple”: The wig Vera saw “upon the carven chair post” in Chapter 18.
  • “the lashless eyelids”: Alopecia universalis is the loss of all hair on the body, including the brows and lashes.

Chapter 19 · Red Wig

  • A mask or false identity, “perched like a rare tropical bird on the hat rack… within easy reach of her gnarled hand,” ready but separate from her true self.

Chapter 20

  • “There was no crowning glory of woman’s hair”: “If a woman have long hair, it is a glory to her: for her hair is given her for a covering” (1 Corinthians 11:15).
  • “Ugly is a field without grass, a plant without leaves, a head without hair, according to Ovid”: Ovid, The Art of Love (3.249-250): “Turpe pecus mutilum, turpe est sine gramine campus, / et sine fronde frutex, et sine crine caput” (“Ugly is a hornless bull, ugly a field without grass, a bush without leaves, and a head without hair”). The lines come in his advice to women on hiding the loss of hair, which includes buying false hair.
  • “Hair adds beauty to a good face and terror to an ugly one, according to the Greek”: Plutarch’s Life of Lycurgus (22) explains that the Spartans wore their hair long, remembering a saying of their lawgiver Lycurgus “that a fine head of hair made the handsome more comely still, and the ugly more terrible.”
  • “Was God bald, too”: Children mocked Elisha, “Go up, thou bald head” (2 Kings 2:23), and the Ancient of Days has “the hair of his head like the pure wool” (Daniel 7:9).

Chapter 20 · Hair and Baldness

  • Hair symbolizes beauty, identity, and vitality; baldness represents loss, vulnerability, and exposure.
  • Absence of hair is described as a “barren, impoverished landscape without flower or bush or hedge.”

Chapter 21 · Bald Head

  • Symbolizes raw truth, vulnerability, and the stripping away of superficial identity.

Chapter 21 · Miss MacIntosh’s Morning Appearance

  • Miss MacIntosh’s appearance contrasts sharply with the earlier portrayal of vulnerability, showing her resilience and normalcy despite suffering.

Chapter 22

  • “Take time by the forelock!”: The saying means to act quickly and decisively, especially to take advantage of an opportunity. It comes from the Greek Kairos, Opportunity, carved by Lysippus with a forelock in front and bald behind, so that he can be seized only as he comes. The Roman fabulist Phaedrus tells it of Occasio (Fables 5.8).
  • “Baldness is no cure for sorrow”: Cicero tells the philosopher Bion’s joke about the king who tore out his hair in grief, “as though baldness were a cure for sorrow” (Tusculan Disputations 3.62).

Chapter 63 · The Wig and Baldness

  • Miss MacIntosh's wig symbolizes the facade of social norms and appearances, which she ultimately reveals as a protective but deceptive covering.
  • Her baldness, exposed in a moment of vulnerability, represents raw truth, mortality, and the stripping away of illusions.

Chapter 64 · Baldness

  • Represents vulnerability, difference, and mourning. It is a physical manifestation of Miss MacIntosh’s inner loneliness and social alienation.

Chapter 64 · Wig

  • The wig symbolizes the mask people wear to hide their true selves and conform to societal expectations. Miss MacIntosh’s frequent removal and replacement of the wig depict her struggle with identity.

Chapter 65 · Baldness and Hairlessness

  • Represents vulnerability, loss, and the stripping away of pretense.
  • Contrasted with Mr. Bonebreaker’s idealized descriptions of hair and beauty.

Chapter 66 · The Red Wig

  • A mask hiding her baldness, symbolizing concealment of vulnerability.

Chapter 72 · Bald spot

  • Symbolizes the wear and loss Esther has endured.
