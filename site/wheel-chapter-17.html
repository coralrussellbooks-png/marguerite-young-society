Chapter 17

On Chapter 17 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of The Wheel and the Spokes.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 16 · Wheel and Spokes Home · Chapter 18 →

Research notes: Chapter 17 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 17

Characters: Miss MacIntosh

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The chapter refuses to locate the narrator in a single, stable subject position. She is simultaneously the terrified child, the laughing victor, the fading consciousness, and the future old woman looking back:

"my body seemed divided as if now there were two of us, one who fought, one who lay still and watched it all"

This is not metaphor for psychological stress. It is a structural claim about selfhood. The self that fights and the self that witnesses cannot be reconciled into one person. They coexist without resolution. The narrator also projects herself forward in time:

"ask no questions of this night until long years afterward, perhaps when I was old and grey and had no other occupation but to search through memory, asking the why, the wherefore, composing, recomposing, finding perhaps no answer"

The self is therefore plural across time as well as within the moment: the fourteen-year-old girl, the future old woman, the witness, the combatant, all present simultaneously. The chapter does not resolve these into a unified "I." It holds them open.

Miss MacIntosh herself enacts the plural self from the outside. She is the reliable nursemaid and the bald stranger and the male wrestler and the phantom, not sequentially but all at once:

"She was other than herself, and she had deceived me"

The deception is not a lie told at one moment. It is the revelation that she was always already multiple, that the singular "Miss MacIntosh" was a construction the narrator had imposed.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

This pillar is perhaps the chapter's most explicit philosophical preoccupation. The narrator does not simply discover that Miss MacIntosh was not what she seemed. She discovers that the very distinction between appearance and reality is unstable:

"What shall we do when, fleeing from illusion, we are confronted by illusion? When falling from illusion, we fall into illusion? Have we not deceived ourselves? Where was the real world?"

This is not a rhetorical question. The chapter provides no answer because no answer exists within its ontology. The "real" Miss MacIntosh (sensible, red-haired, knitting her stitches) turns out to have been the illusion. But the bald, violent figure who replaces her is equally phantasmagoric:

"determined to vanquish this phantom, this bald pretender who had no business in Miss MacIntosh's bedroom"

The word "pretender" is applied to both versions. The nursemaid with hair was a pretender. The bald figure is a pretender. There is no original beneath the masks, only successive layers of construction. The cave, to use the framework's language, goes all the way down.

Even the physical evidence of the encounter dissolves:

"the next morning, of course, my long braids were exactly braided down my back, and there were no scratches upon my cheeks but those which might have been made by leafless branches"

The morning light does not confirm the night's events. It neither validates nor refutes them. It simply offers another surface, another constructed reality, equally provisional.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The grief in this chapter is not the grief of a single loss. It is the accumulated grief of a foundational relationship revealed as something other than what it was, and the grief is amplified precisely because it cannot be dated. When did Miss MacIntosh become this? The answer is: she always was this. The loss is therefore not located in the present moment but distributed across all the years of the narrator's childhood:

"she had been pulling the wool over my eyes, and she was the bald pretender"

The phrase "all during the years" makes the grief retrospective and total. Every memory of the reliable nursemaid is now contaminated. The grief cannot be assigned to a moment of loss because the loss was always already happening. This is precisely what Pillar 3 describes: grief that accumulates rather than resolves, that is the climate of a world where everything is always already changing.

The narrator's cry for her mother mid-combat deepens this:

"made me cry out even now for my mother? Where was my mother that she did not rescue me"

The mother is the other absent anchor: the opium-addicted, absent mother established elsewhere in the novel. The narrator reaches for both of her primary figures of stability in the same moment and finds neither. The grief is not singular; it is structural. It is the permanent weather of a world without reliable ground.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The chapter stages a radical tension between the witness function and the participant function. The narrator is split, as noted above, into one who fights and one who watches. The watching self is described with particular precision:

"my body seemed divided as if now there were two of us, one who fought, one who lay still and watched it all"

The witness does not intervene. The witness does not resolve. The witness simply sees. This is the ethical stance of Pillar 4: to stay in the room, to see fully, without rushing toward resolution. But the chapter also tests the limits of this stance. The narrator does intervene (she laughs, she screams, she strikes) and each intervention produces not clarity but further complexity. The witness self is the more honest self. The acting self generates only more illusion.

The narrative voice itself performs the witness function at the level of prose. The long, recursive sentences do not summarize or conclude. They accumulate detail, qualify every claim, refuse to simplify:

"her eyes standing out like striped marbles as she moved through beams of broken moonlight, her head cold and sweating, a great mirror where one should see one's self if one looked closely and did not lose one's mind and fade into unconsciousness"

This is prose that refuses to look away. It is the practice of attention enacted at the level of syntax.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The narrator explicitly describes the process of returning to the beginning with what she has learned, composing and recomposing, finding no final answer:

"asking the why, the wherefore, composing, recomposing, finding perhaps no answer"

This is Pillar 5 stated almost directly. The building is never finished. The narrator will return to this night for the rest of her life, constructing and reconstructing its meaning. The chapter itself performs this recursion: the prose circles back repeatedly to the same images (the bald head, the moonlight, the curtains, the breathing) without ever arriving at a stable interpretation.

The physical encounter also has this recursive quality. Blow follows blow, each one building on the last, but the structure never resolves into victory or defeat; it simply continues until consciousness fades:

"blow after blow was still delivered — the rabbit punch which cracks against the base of the skull, the upward punch which thrusts upward to the heart, the Sunday punch"

The enumeration of blows is itself recursive: each named punch is another attempt to land on something definitive, and each one fails to end the encounter.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter ends without resolution. The morning after is described in a single subordinate clause that refuses to confirm or deny what happened:

"even though, the next morning, of course, my long braids were exactly braided down my back, and there were no scratches upon my cheeks but those which might have been made by leafless branches"

The "of course" is doing enormous work here. It performs normalcy while simultaneously undermining it: the qualification "those which might have been made by leafless branches" refuses to close the question. The sentence is structured to deny completion even as it appears to offer it.

The narrator's consciousness does not recover. It "fades into consciousness": a paradox that refuses the clean arc of unconsciousness followed by recovery:

"my consciousness faded into my consciousness"

This is the ethics of incompletion enacted at the level of grammar. The sentence cannot conclude because experience cannot conclude. The demand for a finished sentence (a resolved encounter, a confirmed identity, a clear answer about who Miss MacIntosh was) is itself identified as a kind of violence.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Miss MacIntosh's baldness is the chapter's central symbol, and it functions precisely as Pillar 7 describes: the marginal body as evidence of the constructed nature of all categories. Hair, specifically Miss MacIntosh's "red, shining hair, tawny as an August sunset", had been the marker of her femininity, her reliability, her legibility as a woman and a nursemaid. Its absence does not simply make her less feminine. It dissolves the category:

"She was bald as the egg or as the rock where nothing grows. She had no hair on her head, no hair at all, not one hair to cast its shadow, and her face was as absolutely impersonal and expressionless as a face may be and still be human"

"Still be human" is the key phrase. Baldness pushes Miss MacIntosh to the edge of the human category itself. She becomes the evidence that "human," "woman," "nursemaid," "reliable" are all constructions that depend on surface markers, and that when those markers are removed, the categories collapse.

The narrator's cry, "Miss MacIntosh, you are a man!", is not simply a discovery. It is a recognition that gender is itself a constructed category, and that Miss MacIntosh has been living at its margins all along. The marginal person is not the exception; she is the evidence. Miss MacIntosh's body, bald and one-breasted, is the place where the constructed nature of gender becomes most visible.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The chapter is saturated with the past pressing up through the present. The narrator's recognition of Miss MacIntosh in her altered state is achieved not through present perception but through accumulated sediment:

"I knew her by her broken nose and something that was definite in her manner, something suggesting the intimacies forgotten"

The "intimacies forgotten" are not gone. They are beneath the surface of the present encounter, pressing up through it, making recognition possible even in the face of radical transformation. This is sedimentary time: the past does not recede, it accumulates.

The historical references function similarly. The narrator's mind, even in the midst of physical combat, drifts to pharaohs, Sumerian wrestlers, Roman gladiators, Jacob at Peniel:

"perhaps even that holy angel with whom Jacob struggled at Peniel. Perhaps it was a reindeer"

These are not random associations. They are the sediment of human history pressing up through a bedroom fight in an American coastal house. The encounter is simultaneously this specific event and every struggle between humans and the forces that define them. The past (mythological, biblical, historical) is not behind the narrator. It is beneath her, constituting the ground she stands on.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter is acutely self-aware about the inadequacy of language to capture what is happening, and yet it proceeds anyway: generating simile after simile, qualification after qualification, in a performance of language's simultaneous necessity and failure.

The prose is structured around the word "perhaps" and its equivalents. The narrator cannot assert; she can only propose:

"Perhaps it was a reindeer. Perhaps, I reasoned, my consciousness slipping like a train through an airless mountain tunnel, it was no one, after all"

Language here names its own location: it is the language of a consciousness that cannot achieve certainty, that is always constructing rather than discovering. The similes are equally self-conscious: "like a train through an airless mountain tunnel" is a simile about the failure of consciousness to move through its own experience, and it is itself a labored, imperfect vehicle. The language knows it is not transparent.

The narrator's scream, "Miss MacIntosh, you are a man!", is the moment when language attempts to fix identity, to nail down a truth. And it is immediately undermined. The laughter that follows it, the acknowledgment that "it was a horse ballet, a Spanish carousel with many mirrors", dissolves the assertion. Language that claims to speak from certainty is exposed as speaking from a particular, limited, constructed position.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter is not elsewhere. It is in the bald head, the moonlight, the corset stays, the breathing. The chapter is full of imagery that reaches toward transcendence (pharaohs, angels, Jacob at Peniel, the Arctic Circle, the moon) but every transcendent reference is immediately pulled back into the immanent:

"the moon, dimly radiant but distant, invulnerable to our assault, the moon which draws the waves in its wake and has no life of its own"

The moon is not a symbol of higher truth. It is a cold, dented disc with no life of its own. It is physically present and metaphysically empty. The sacred, if it exists, is here, in this bedroom, in this bald woman, in this confused fourteen-year-old girl, in this violent and inconclusive encounter.

The description of Miss MacIntosh's bald head as a "great mirror where one should see one's self" is the chapter's most concentrated statement of immanent transcendence. The sacred is not in a beyond. It is in the reflective surface of another person's skull. To look at it fully is to see yourself, and potentially to lose your mind. The ordinary (a woman's baldness) becomes the site of the most profound and destabilizing revelation.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The narrator is explicitly constituted by what was deposited before her arrival. Miss MacIntosh has been her primary reality-anchor throughout her childhood. The revelation of Miss MacIntosh's constructed identity is simultaneously a revelation about the narrator's own constitution:

"that she had never been herself at all but someone else, this appalling apparition clothed with flesh and blood which should fade"

If Miss MacIntosh was always someone else, then the narrator who was raised by her, shaped by her, oriented by her, was shaped by a fiction. The narrator is the ground on which Miss MacIntosh's sediment was deposited, and that sediment is now revealed as unstable. This is the crossing point: the narrator is where Miss MacIntosh's constructed identity crosses with her own not-yet-formed identity.

The forward projection, the old woman who will spend her life "composing, recomposing, finding perhaps no answer", confirms the structural obligation of Pillar 11. The narrator is not moving through time toward a destination. She is the place where this night will continue to cross with every subsequent night. She is constituting, through this encounter, the ground on which her future self will stand. The carrying continues, even though, perhaps especially because, what is being carried is irresolvable.
