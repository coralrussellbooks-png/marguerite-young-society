On “Who Killed Cock Robin?”

The English nursery rhyme “Who Killed Cock Robin?” has been traced to the mid-eighteenth century in its written form, but is likely much older. It proceeds as a catalogue of witnesses and participants: each creature takes responsibility for some part of the robin’s death and burial. The sparrow killed him with its bow and arrow. The fly witnessed it with its little eye. The beetle made the shroud. The rook dug the grave. Everyone is implicated; no single cause is sufficient. Young gives this rhyme to Esther as a philosophical framework for making peace with the inexplicable deaths of her children: nobody killed them, and everybody did, and the forces involved are larger than any individual cause. Celtic mythology identifies Cock Robin with Lugh, the sun god who dies as the nights grow longer: a death that is seasonal, inevitable, cosmic, and always followed by return.
