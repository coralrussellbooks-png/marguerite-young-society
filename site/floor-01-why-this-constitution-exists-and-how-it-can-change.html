A Rationale for Revision and a Proposed Constitutional Convention

Why a New Constitution

The Constitution of 1787 was a remarkable document for its time. It established a framework for self-governance that was genuinely new in the world, that has proved durable across nearly two and a half centuries, and that contains principles worth preserving. It also contained exclusions built in from the beginning: the three-fifths compromise that counted enslaved people as partial persons for purposes of political representation while denying them any rights that representation was supposed to protect; the assumption that the people doing the governing were propertied white men; the absence of any mechanism for the people to govern directly between elections. Those exclusions were not incidental to the document. They were negotiated into it by the people whose interests they served.

The amendments that followed, the Bill of Rights, the Reconstruction Amendments, the Nineteenth Amendment, the Voting Rights Act's legislative extension of constitutional promises the document had not delivered: these were the work of generations of people insisting that the original exclusions be torn out. That work produced a better document than the one it started with. It did not produce a document adequate to the society it now governs or the challenges that society faces.

The concentration of political power in institutions designed for a nation of four million people now governing about three hundred and forty million. The capture of those institutions by the concentrated economic interests that the founders could not have anticipated at the scale they now operate. The expansion of executive power beyond anything the original document authorized or intended. The electoral system that gives some votes more weight than others based on geography. The absence of any enforceable right to healthcare, housing, education, or a livable environment. The original document has been stretched to cover a world it was not designed for and it shows.

This constitution does not discard what worked. It preserves the separation of powers, the protection of fundamental rights, the federal structure, and the commitment to democratic self-governance that the original document established. It adds what the original document left out: the direct mechanisms for popular sovereignty, the enforceable economic floor, the environmental rights, the explicit equal protection that the original document promised and did not deliver. It corrects what the original document got wrong: the electoral college, the unequal weighting of votes, the absence of term limits for the judiciary, the campaign finance framework that has allowed wealth to purchase political power at scale.

It is offered not as the perfect document but as the better one: the one that takes seriously the promises the original made and did not keep, that addresses the concentrations of power the original did not anticipate, and that builds in the mechanisms for ongoing revision that the original made too difficult.

How Constitutions Change

The original constitution's amendment process requires two-thirds of Congress and three-quarters of states to ratify any change. That threshold was designed to prevent hasty revision and to require genuine consensus before fundamental change. It has also made the document nearly impossible to amend in practice: only twenty-seven amendments have been ratified in two hundred and thirty-five years, and ten of those were the Bill of Rights ratified together in 1791. The practical effect is a document frozen in structures that no longer serve the people they were designed to govern, defended not by consensus but by the veto power of a minority.

This constitution's amendment process is more accessible without being easy. Two paths exist: the congressional path requiring two-thirds of Congress and approval by three-quarters of states measured by population, and the direct path requiring a national initiative approved by sixty percent of voters with at least fifty percent of registered voters participating. The direct path allows the people to amend their own governing document without depending on the willingness of the institutions the amendment might constrain. It requires genuine supermajority support: sixty percent approval is a high threshold that ensures amendments reflect broad consensus rather than temporary majorities.

The constitutional convention is the mechanism for more fundamental revision: the wholesale reconsideration of the document rather than the amendment of specific provisions. The original constitution provides for a convention called by two-thirds of state legislatures, a provision that has never been used and whose procedures are largely unspecified. The absence of specification is itself a problem: a convention called under ambiguous procedures and without clear limits on its scope creates uncertainty about what it could do and whose interests would dominate its proceedings.

The Constitutional Convention: What It Is and When It Is Needed

A constitutional convention is appropriate when the accumulated changes needed exceed what the amendment process can accomplish: when the document needs not revision of specific provisions but reconsideration of its fundamental structure. The history of constitutional change in democratic societies suggests that this need arises roughly generationally: the document that served one era adequately becomes inadequate to the next as the society it governs changes in ways the document's framers could not anticipate.

This constitution does not assume it will be the last word. It assumes it will need to become something different than it is, on a timeline no one can predict, in response to conditions no one can fully foresee. A constitutional convention would be the mechanism for that becoming. It would not be a crisis measure. It would be a democratic tool: the formal process by which the people reconsider the fundamental terms of their self-governance when those terms need more than amendment.

The draft constitution does not yet include a convention clause. This essay proposes one, to be added to Article VI, built on the following principles.

Who Calls It

Under this proposal, a constitutional convention could be called by any of three mechanisms: a petition signed by five percent of registered voters triggering a national referendum in which sixty percent approve the calling of a convention; a two-thirds vote of Congress submitting the question to a national referendum with the same sixty percent threshold; or the determination by the independent judiciary that a fundamental constitutional question has arisen that the existing document cannot adequately resolve, submitted to a national referendum with the same threshold. The multiple paths would ensure that the convention could be called by popular initiative, by legislative consensus, or in response to genuine constitutional crisis, without any single institution having unilateral power to call or block it.

Who Participates

The proposed convention would be a democratic body, not a body of experts. It would be composed of delegates selected through a process that combines election and sortition: some delegates are elected from geographic districts using the same ranked choice voting system that governs other elections; others are selected by stratified random sample from the registered voter population, ensuring that the convention reflects the demographic composition of the country rather than only the people most motivated and resourced to seek elected positions. Legal scholars, historians, and subject matter experts would participate as advisors to the convention rather than as delegates with votes. The people govern. The experts inform.

The convention would be required to conduct its deliberations in public, with full records available to every citizen. It would be required to receive testimony from the communities most directly affected by the provisions under consideration. It would be required to produce plain-language summaries of every proposed change accessible to the full voting population. The convention that deliberates in private, that excludes affected communities, or that produces documents readable only by lawyers is not doing democratic work. It is doing the work of the people with the resources to participate in opaque processes, which is what the original convention did.

What It Can and Cannot Do

The convention could reconsider any provision of this constitution and propose changes to the full voting population for ratification. It could propose an entirely new document. It could propose targeted revisions to specific articles. The scope of what it considers would be determined by the mandate under which it was called: a convention called to address a specific constitutional question has a specific mandate; a convention called for general revision has a broader one.

What the convention could not do is remove the floor. The rights established in Article VII, the direct democracy mechanisms established in Article I, and the equal protection principle that runs through the entire document are not subject to removal by any convention or any amendment. They may be expanded. They may never be contracted. The floor is permanent. A convention that proposed to remove it would have exceeded its mandate, and its proposals would be void.

This constraint is not a limitation on democracy. It is democracy's protection of itself. The history of democratic backsliding in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries is largely the history of democratic majorities voting away the rights of minorities, of temporary majorities using the mechanisms of democracy to entrench their own power and exclude their opponents. The floor provisions are the safeguard against that history repeating. They are what the people have agreed they will not do to each other regardless of what any majority at any moment wants.

Ratification

Proposals from the convention would be submitted to the full voting population for ratification. Ratification would require sixty percent approval with at least fifty percent of registered voters participating. The threshold would be the same as for constitutional amendments by initiative: high enough to require genuine broad consensus, low enough to be achievable when consensus exists. A proposal that cannot achieve sixty percent approval has not achieved the consensus required to change the fundamental terms of self-governance. It would return to the democratic process for further deliberation.

The ratification campaign would be subject to the same campaign finance provisions as all other electoral contests. The side with the most money does not get to drown out the side with less. The public media system is obligated to provide full and fair coverage of the ratification debate, including the perspectives of communities most directly affected by the proposed changes. The voter who goes to the ratification referendum would have access to the full text of the proposed changes, independent assessments of their likely consequences, and plain-language summaries prepared without advocacy for either side.

Why Revision Is Integrity

The document that cannot be revised is not a strong document. It is a frozen one: unable to respond to what it did not anticipate, defending its own inadequacies as though they were features rather than failures. The constitution that acknowledges its own incompleteness and builds in the mechanisms for honest revision is more trustworthy than the one that presents itself as finished.

This constitution is not finished. It is the best map its writers could draw of the territory they are trying to reach. The territory will keep revealing itself to be larger than the map accounts for. New conditions will arise that the map does not address. Provisions that seemed adequate will prove inadequate in application. Communities whose experiences the writers could not fully see will identify what the document has missed.

A constitutional convention would be the formal mechanism for responding to that ongoing revelation. Calling one would not be a sign that the document has failed. It would be the sign that the democracy is working: that the people retain genuine authority over the fundamental terms of their own governance, that they can use that authority when the document needs to become something different than it is, and that the work of building a more just society does not end with the ratification of any document however carefully written.

The sentence has not ended. The amendment is always possible. The convention, once added, would always be available. The people are always the government.
