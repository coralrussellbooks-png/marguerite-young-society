Martha the Passenger Pigeon

In the research: Chapter 45, Chapter 48, Chapter 49

Definition from research

Chapter 45

  • “passenger pigeons now extinct”: Audubon watched a flight of passenger pigeons pass for three days in 1813, “the light of noon-day” obscured “as by an eclipse” (John James Audubon, Ornithological Biography, 1831). The last, Martha, died at the Cincinnati Zoo on September 1, 1914. See Martha in the Chapter 49 resources.

Chapter 48

  • “circling earth with passenger birds”: Passenger pigeons once flew over North America in flocks of billions; the last, Martha, died at the Cincinnati Zoo on September 1, 1914.

Chapter 49

Listed as: Martha (Passenger Pigeon)

  • Symbolizes lost beauty, extinction, and irretrievable loss.
  • Her death is mourned as the death of a species, a cosmic event, and a personal loss for Mr. Spitzer.
  • “the last passenger pigeon known to earth”: Martha died at the Cincinnati Zoo on the afternoon of September 1, 1914, a month after the First World War began, alone since her last companions died in 1910. Young gives the date as September 14, 1918, the last year of the war, at one o’clock. Mr. Spitzer mourns her as the last of her kind: once something is lost, whether a species, a person, or a moment, it cannot be recovered, and mourning for her is mourning for all that is irretrievably gone. Aldo Leopold wrote for the monument to the pigeon at Wyalusing State Park in Wisconsin: “We have erected a monument to commemorate the funeral of a species. It symbolizes our sorrow” (“On a Monument to the Pigeon,” 1947).
  • “She was embalmed and put upon a train to be sent to Washington”: Mr. Spitzer says Martha was embalmed, but her body was frozen in about 300 pounds of ice and sent by fast train to the Smithsonian Institution in Washington, where it was made into a taxidermy mount and an anatomical specimen; the mount has been displayed there and lent to other museums.
  • “for Martha was no more, and Mark was no more”: Mark is Martha’s mate, for “it took two to make a bargain”; her last companions at the Cincinnati Zoo died in 1910. It may also refer to Mark Catesby, who painted pictures of animals including the passenger pigeon, for Mr. Spitzer asks, “What good did Mark ever do… for all the pigeons.”
