Everyone Here Is Made

Read the section back and a strange thing rises out of it. Line the characters up and try to sort them by how real they are, and the sorting falls apart in the hand.

At one end sit the openly invented, the figures no one pretends are solid: James the chauffeur built out of an absence, the black coachman with no face, Res Tacamah the drug bottle, Mr. Chandelier the light fixture with seven crystal hands. Next to them stand the town's invented dead, Mr. Tidings who never existed and was also murdered, Mrs. Hathaway's two brothers who dissolve into one loved absent man. Then the strangers Vera builds on the bus without ever speaking to them, Madge conjured from a sleeping face, Jacqueline from a name cried in sleep, Homer from a ragged football sweater and a name that is not his wife's. And then, at the end where the ground was supposed to be firm, the ones taken for real, Miss MacIntosh who turns out to be a wig, Cousin Hannah remade on a deathbed no one attended, Jock assembled from a garbled newspaper notice no one troubled to correct. There is no line to draw. The made and the found run into each other with no seam, because the book does not believe there is a seam.

The bus is where it says so plainly. The whole novel is framed by a ride through the mist, a woman looking at people she will never meet and giving them entire lives, husbands and rivals and griefs and dead sweethearts, out of a painted dress and a sleeping face. That is not a flaw in the narration, a narrator overstepping what she could know. It is the argument the whole book is built to make. To see a stranger and to invent one are not two acts. They are the same act, and the bus is the place where the novel stops hiding it.

Young said as much in her own voice. When she had examined all the illusions of life, she told Miriam Fuchs and Ellen Friedman, and knew "there isn't any reality," a person nevertheless goes on, and that going-on is what maturity is: accepting that "it is all mask and illusion" and that people move through the world in disguise. The novel was built to demonstrate it, an opium addict's paradise at the center and, spreading out from it, a whole country of people who are masks worn by the ones who need them.

There is a story told about her, that she would take her writing students out to watch strangers at the bus stop and set them to writing the lives of the people they saw. Whether or not it happened as told, it names exactly the exercise the book performs on every page. A writer looks at a stranger and makes a person. That is the craft stripped to its root, and Young put it at the wheel of her enormous novel and drove it through the mist for more than a 1,198 pages.

Which means this section was never quite a catalog of people. It was a catalog of makings, and sorting them, weighing one against another, deciding that Musidora is more real than Madge and Madge more real than the coachman, is itself the thing the book is about. To analyze these figures is to build them a second time. The reader who has come this far has been doing, all along, what Vera does on the bus and what the mother does in her bed and what the town does over its gossip, taking fragments of the barely seen and assembling whole souls out of them, and grieving the ones that will not hold.

So the section does not end on a verdict about which characters were real. It ends on the recognition that the question was the wrong one. The book never asks whether a person is made. It asks how. Its failures are the ones who are made badly: Weed who reduces the living to weights, the detective who sees a corpse where a woman sleeps, Lisa Lunde who shatters the strange person "at one blow" and calls it clarity, Bonebreaker who flees the real the instant it stands revealed, Mrs. Hogden who dreams other people's private griefs out loud as common stock. And the ones it trusts are the ones who make with attention and will not close the person down: Vera, who looks long and refuses the verdict, the stone deaf man who "heard with his eyes," Beatrice at the washtub named for the guide to Paradise. Making a person is not the sin. Reducing one is.

The last image the book leaves is the first one, a bus plowing nowhere through the mist, a woman looking hard at people she cannot hold onto and telling their stories anyway. The reader closing this section is on that bus too. The people here will not hold still, will not resolve into a single settled fact, will not stay found. They have to be made, with as much attention as the maker can spare, and that turns out to be the only way anyone is ever known, on a bus, in the mist, looking at a stranger, and beginning, already, to tell the story.
