Friday: The Blind Dog Who Saw the Angel

← Solomon · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Mrs. Hogden →

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Old Friday · The Chicago Detective · The Stone Deaf Man

Old Friday is Miss MacIntosh's dog, "the old sheep dog following always this shepherd of the surf," and he is blind. This matters, because Friday is blind and sees what his sighted mistress cannot. He goes "barking always at her heels as if, though he was blind and could not see the fringes of departing light, he saw what could not be seen, some other being in the purple fog and shadow, some golden, feathered angel soft as fog and dusk and woman's hair." Miss MacIntosh, walking right through that fog, "though herself she might seem the vision, could not see it." She had spent her life refusing the dream, bristling "like the porcupine" at the very thought, ready to beat back "with her stout umbrella, tides of angels wearing golden crowns." The blind dog perceives exactly what the clear-eyed woman has trained herself not to.

He is the book's image of the witness as pure faithfulness, and he holds together the two things the book will not let resolve. He has "known the denudation and the dream, the reality and the illusion, how each must live by some pretense." The bald truth under the red wig and the golden angel in the fog are both real to Friday, and he does not need one to defeat the other; he barks at both. This is exactly the attention the philosophy asks for, the holding of two contradictory truths at once without forcing a winner, and it is a blind dog who manages it while the humans around him choose one side and go blind in the other eye.

And when Miss MacIntosh walks into the sea and does not come out, Friday does the truest thing in the chapter. He keeps barking. After she has drowned, "the old dog to bark much as if he might still be aware of her, a moving shadow, a shadow of a black umbrella moving over water and sand, as if perhaps she had never gone or had returned." He keeps his vigil at the tide's edge, at the crossing point where the land meets the water that took her, "old Friday barking by the waves which return with all but her." He is grief that will not be told the loss is over. He barks for the drowned as if barking could call her back, or as if he can still see, in the fog he was always better at barking than seeing the shadow of her umbrella moving.

The turn for a writer is brief and steadying. Friday is faithfulness itself, the blind companion who attends to the invisible thing, who perceives the angel the clear-eyed refuse, who keeps barking at the shore long after the sensible have accepted that she is gone. He is the maker's stubborn attention to what has vanished, the refusal to file the loss as finished, the holding of the bald reality and the golden dream in the same breath without making them fight. Set him beside the Chicago detective, who has perfect eyes and sees a corpse, and beside the stone deaf man, who cannot hear and hears the fishes in the river. Friday cannot see and sees the angel. The book keeps handing its truest witnessing to the ones whose ordinary channel is closed, and the blindest, most faithful of them is a dog barking by the water for a woman who is not coming back, keeping her, in the only way left, by not letting the barking stop.
