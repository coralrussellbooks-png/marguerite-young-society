Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 9

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

The Questions

Miss MacIntosh tears down the Columbus myth yet calls marriage a natural order and dreams lies. Why can she see everyone’s construction but her own?

Is Vera’s dream-life the truth Miss MacIntosh’s plainness lacks, or just the opposite seal?

When Vera wonders whether she killed Miss MacIntosh “by opening her eyes,” what is guilt doing to memory?

Was the death a murder or “the key turning of itself in the lock,” and does the book let that be decided?

Miss MacIntosh is sharper here than her plainness first suggested, and the sharpness cuts unevenly. She debunks the Columbus story, she questions sacred narratives “derived from positions of authority,” she says a preacher is no necessity and worships outdoors. Toward other people’s inventions she keeps the construction open, seeing the received myth as a made thing that can be doubted. This is real, and it is to her credit.

But toward her own creed she seals the door. Marriage she presents as a natural order, like the mating of animals, not a built arrangement but a given; dreams and imagination she calls lies; her domestic training and her “rainy day” thrift she offers Vera as simply the way things are. The woman who will not let Columbus stand unquestioned will not question the frame she lives by. She can see everyone’s construction but her own, and that blind spot is the exact shape of the finished blueprint: open toward the far myth, sealed toward the near one.

Against her the chapter sets Vera’s inner life, the dreams and the imagined romantic future. It is tempting to make Vera’s dream-world the truth Miss MacIntosh’s plainness lacks. The chapter resists that too. Vera’s dream is not yet the open construction; it is the other pole, an inner sealed room answering the outer one, romance and adventure imagined as fixed as the domestic order it rejects. The book is not choosing dream over plainness. It is showing two seals facing each other.

Grief and guilt work on Vera’s memory throughout. She wonders whether she caused Miss MacIntosh’s death merely “by opening her eyes,” whether it was murder or “the key turning of itself in the lock.” The question cannot be answered, and the chapter does not answer it. Guilt has made her memory unreliable, folding the past into a present she cannot get clear of, and the unanswerable question is left standing, which is the honest thing to do with it.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Miss MacIntosh’s practical creed for its wisdom, and the equal and opposite reading that takes Vera’s dream-life for the truth it lacks. Both are seals. It refuses, too, the settling of Vera’s guilt into either verdict, murder or accident.

The footing is the precise diagnosis of Miss MacIntosh: open toward the far construction, sealed toward the near. That is how the finished blueprint usually works in a person, not total blindness but selective sight, doubting the myths that cost nothing to doubt and enshrining the frame one lives inside. Vera’s answering dream is no freer. The stance the book trusts is the one neither of them holds, to keep even the near construction open, especially the one you live by. The frame you inhabit is built too, and hardest to see. The death stays unjudged, and the chapter lets it.

For Reflection

Whose constructions are easy to see through, and why is one’s own frame the hardest?

When a fixed dream answers a fixed duty, is either of them open?

What unanswerable question from one’s own past does guilt keep folding into the present?
