Chapter 31

On Chapter 31 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of The Wheel and the Spokes.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 30 · Wheel and Spokes Home · Chapter 32 →

Research notes: Chapter 31 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 31

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The chapter refuses to stabilize any single consciousness. We move between Mr. Spitzer's perspective, the narrator's mother's perspective, the narrator's reconstruction, and Cousin Hannah's remembered voice, and these perspectives do not resolve into a unified account. Mr. Spitzer himself is multiply destabilized: he is simultaneously a lawyer making professional calls, a mourner who has forgotten he has mourned, a ghost visiting a ghost's house, and a man listed among the dead who nonetheless lives.

he had "found that the old clerk of the death rolls had made an inordinately great mistake, that he had listed Mr. Spitzer’s name among the dead? Yet he lived"

He cannot even settle the question of which brother died: his brother or himself. The self here is not fragmented in the clinical sense but constitutively plural: J.S., Mr. Spitzer, Mr. Spitzen, identities that bleed into one another without resolution.

"settling on the cognomen Spitzer in that case but never deciding which brother it was"

The mother, too, exists in multiple registers simultaneously: alive but already dead, dreaming but also perceiving, loving a man who is a shadow rather than a body. Cousin Hannah is reconstructed differently by every consciousness that remembers her. No single self in this chapter has edges.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The entire chapter is organized around the impossibility of distinguishing the real from the constructed. Mr. Spitzer cannot determine whether Cousin Hannah is home or away, whether she ever truly left, whether the eye at the keyhole is hers or a star or a candle flame.

"It was a star passing in a cloud, a star passing outside the house, perhaps a distant head light. It was a bird twittering in a snowy bush."

The newspaper reports journeys she did not take; she took journeys they did not report. The stock transfers are signed in her handwriting after her death, or are they? Mr. Spitzer is not so sure. Reality is not found here; it is endlessly, provisionally assembled from signs that refuse to cohere. The cave is not escaped. It is redecorated.

Most profoundly, the mother's love for a dead man, described as love for "a fleeting shadow, the mere shadow of a man", is not presented as delusion to be corrected. It is presented as a fully operative reality, perhaps more real than the alternatives Cousin Hannah offered. Illusion is not secondary. It is the primary substance of the world these characters inhabit.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

This is perhaps the pillar most fully embodied in the chapter's very texture. Grief here does not move. It accumulates and recirculates. The lace curtains billow with the memory of Cousin Hannah's rage. The glass prisms shake with it. The mother and Mr. Spitzer still hear the detonations of her wrath years after her death.

Mr. Spitzer continues knocking at a demolished house. He drops calling cards through the slot of a door that no longer exists. He composes epitaphs he cannot finish. He mourns Cousin Hannah while also mourning his brother while also, arguably, mourning himself: the man listed as dead.

"he feared that whenever one died, he should mourn for two, perhaps through sheer generosity"

Grief here is not an event with a before and after. It is the climate of the novel. The snow, the ice, the storm, the howling wind; these are not metaphors decorating grief. They are grief, materialized as the permanent weather of the world these characters move through. The chapter does not pretend otherwise. It does not offer resolution. It offers only more weather.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator of this chapter does not intervene. They do not correct the mother's opium dreams, do not adjudicate between Cousin Hannah's fierce realism and the mother's visionary love, do not tell us which brother died. The narrative stance is precisely that of staying in the room: attending fully to contradictions, to irresolutions, to the coexistence of incompatible truths.

Even Mr. Spitzer, in his strange way, practices a form of witnessing. He knocks on doors he knows are empty. He delivers papers he knows will not be signed. He attends a funeral almost no one else attends. His presence is not useful. It is testimonial. He witnesses Cousin Hannah's life and death without being able to summarize it, without being able to write her epitaph.

"not knowing what to say, hesitant as usual to make up his mind"

The refusal to simplify is not presented as weakness here. It is presented as the only honest response to a life as large and contradictory as Cousin Hannah's.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Mr. Spitzer's epitaph-writing is the chapter's central act of recursive construction. He writes epitaphs and crosses them out. He returns to the beginning. He tries again.

"He had written many epitaphs but had crossed them out."

What follows is a cascade of attempted epitaphs, "Here lies the great captain unhorsed... Here lies one who searched all her life for her dead love... Here lies one who was pitiful... Here lies the wounded dove", each one a building that is immediately abandoned, not because it is wrong but because it is insufficient. The building is never finished. That is not presented as failure. It is presented as the only honest response to a life that exceeds all available forms.

The same recursive structure governs the prose itself. Young's sentences loop back on themselves, qualifying, extending, contradicting, beginning again. The chapter does not move forward in the conventional sense. It circles, depositing layers.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The unfinished is everywhere in this chapter, and it is treated with ethical seriousness rather than anxiety. The tomb for Cousin Hannah exists only in Mr. Spitzer's amorphous mind. The epitaph is never settled. The headstone bears only a name he is not entirely sure of, with neither birth date nor death date.

"he had forgotten, after so many years, the date of her death and thus gave her, at least in his own memory, this extension of time"

This is not carelessness. It is described as charity: the charity of inaccuracy toward one who deserved more than the brutal finality of dates. Incompletion here is an ethical act. To complete the epitaph would be to conclude a life that resists conclusion. The ongoing sentence, the epitaph still being composed, is the honest form.

The demand for completion, in this chapter, belongs to Cousin Hannah herself; she who believed in being direct, who wanted to rescue the mother, who wanted to conclude the mother's love story by declaring the man dead. And she lost that battle. The ethics of incompletion vindicate the mother's refusal to resolve.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Every significant figure in this chapter exists at the margins of legibility. The mother is alive but effectively dead to ordinary social reality, sustained by opium dreams of a dead love. Mr. Spitzer is legally dead but physically present, a lawyer who cannot be recognized as living by insurance agents or desk clerks. Cousin Hannah is a woman who traveled into "forbidden Mecca or Medinah or Fez or sacred citadels of men", spaces categorically closed to her, and entered them anyway.

Cousin Hannah is the chapter's most powerful figure of the marginal. She is a woman who fought suffrage campaigns, rode Arab warhorses, negotiated with Berber kings, bearded "human lions in their dens in Persia", and yet she is the one who died almost forgotten, her fame outlived by years, her funeral attended by one mourner. Her marginality is not incidental to her significance. It is her significance. She is the evidence that the categories (of gender, of nation, of the living and the dead) are constructions that she repeatedly crossed and exposed.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The past does not recede in this chapter. It presses upward through every surface. The glass prisms shake with the memory of Cousin Hannah's rage. The great antlers from her last estate have "migrated" to the mother's house. The mirror from a desert tomb, brought by Cousin Hannah to reveal "the transience of human life", now hangs in the mother's rooms. The dead woman's horse is still rolling. Her turret still reaches above the mountain peak.

Mr. Spitzer's continued visits to the demolished house are the chapter's purest image of sedimentary time. He stands where the house was. The address has "disappeared as if by magic from this mundane world", yet he is there, knocking. The past is not behind him. It is beneath him, and he stands on it, and it is still pressing up.

The chapter's temporal structure enacts this directly. We cannot determine when anything is happening. Past and present fold into each other. The mother and Mr. Spitzer still hear Cousin Hannah's rage. The snow that fell on her funeral is the same snow that always falls. Time here is not linear. It is geological.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Young's prose is the most explicit possible demonstration that language shapes what can be seen. The chapter's extraordinary sentences (their length, their qualification, their recursive loops) are not stylistic excess. They are epistemological honesty. A shorter, cleaner sentence would imply a cleaner, more certain reality. The prose refuses that implication.

The epitaphs Mr. Spitzer composes and discards are a meditation on language's inadequacy and its necessity. Every formulation constructs Cousin Hannah differently:

"Here lies that great desert king. Death snatched away her beautiful bride. Here lies the dead love of the world."

These epithets contradict each other. She is knight, king, dove, Crusader, captain. Language cannot fix her. But language is also the only medium through which she continues to exist. The chapter knows this. It does not pretend to speak from nowhere. It speaks from the mother's opium dreams, from Mr. Spitzer's grief, from the narrator's reconstruction, and it names these locations, however obliquely.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter does not reside in any beyond. It resides in the specific, ordinary, and bizarre details of these lives: the calling card embossed with a butterfly, the St. Bernard with its Red Cross bottle sleeping at Cousin Hannah's grave, the great keyhole through which a butterfly could have flown, the old newspapers blowing on the porch.

The butterfly, Mr. Spitzer's "sign of resurrection and eternal life", is not a symbol pointing outward to a transcendent realm. It is a sign pushed through a mail slot. Resurrection is immanent: it happens in the returning of signed stock transfers from a dead woman's hand, in the continued shaking of glass prisms, in the horse still rolling in the flood.

Even Cousin Hannah's death is described in terms of immanent transcendence: "she was safe and dead, plucked up into the bosom of Abraham where sleeps the dove", but this is immediately undermined, qualified, held at ironic distance. The sacred does not reside in Abraham's bosom. It resides in the grey New England churchyard, the frost on the butterfly bushes, the one mourner tapping his cane on broken flagstones.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is the pillar that the chapter's entire architecture supports. Mr. Spitzer is not a self moving through time. He is where time crosses itself. He carries his dead brother (who may be himself). He carries Cousin Hannah (who ignored him, whom he continues to visit). He carries the mother's love for Peron (which Cousin Hannah fought and lost against). He is constituted by all of these deposits.

"he feared that whenever one died, he should mourn for two, perhaps through sheer generosity. Perhaps he should mourn for one who had died, one who was immortal and could not die."

The carrying is not chosen. It is structural. Mr. Spitzer did not choose to be the only mourner at Cousin Hannah's funeral. He did not choose to continue knocking at her demolished house. He did not choose to carry the epitaph he cannot finish. These obligations arrived with him. They were deposited before he could consent.

And crucially: the narrator is also at a crossing point. The narrator carries all of this (the mother's dreams, Mr. Spitzer's grief, Cousin Hannah's lost battle) and deposits it in the prose we are reading. The not-yet-born reader stands on this sediment. The carrying continues.
