Chapter 41

On Chapter 41 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of The Wheel and the Spokes.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 16, 2026

← Chapter 40 · Wheel and Spokes Home · Chapter 42 →

Research notes: Chapter 41 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 41

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

Catherine does not know how many selves she has, and she does not expect all of them to come home:

"Her spirit moved and lived outside of her, even now, going by many ways upon many errands, unnecessary errands in the void, and she did not know how many selves there were which came and went. Perhaps one of them would never return, she guessed. The one which did not return would be the immortal one. The immortal one would be the lost."

Here the plural self is not a theory about Catherine but her daily experience. Late in the chapter Vera says it plainly: "She could be many persons with many heads, limbs, arms. So that one should not know, when he addressed her, which person she was", and Catherine "sometimes failed to recognize herself and the person to whom she spoke".

Mr. Spitzer's plurality runs the other way. He is the twin who cannot be sure which brother survived, and he needs Catherine to see him as one person so that he can be one. Vera tells us that if Catherine had acknowledged his tragedy, "he would have been less sad, perhaps triumphant that she had finally recognized him as the unique individual he was. He would have known his identity." Catherine, who is many, cannot give him that. She has never been single enough herself.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

By this point in the novel the household no longer argues with Catherine's dreams. Vera says so directly:

"there were few people or none who remembered the outside world, the world where my mother was not and had not been for so many years that now it seemed as if her dreams were not the substitute for reality—they were the only reality there was"

The house itself has become her construction. It moves in the night; the furniture changes; "Did not the windows look out upon a different sea or a different street? We all knew these changes in the night, and we should have grown as accustomed to them as to the sound of the wind." Objects appear that prove her guests were real: a purple feather fan no one has seen before, riding gloves on a table, sequins on the floor. Vera allows that Catherine may have left the gloves herself long ago, that "she herself was that emissary she believed had so recently visited here". The evidence and the dream are made of the same material.

And the chapter states the hard fact without dissolving the dream: "For it was true, as must be finally conceded, perhaps by everyone except herself—she could not walk. She only thought she walked, for she was this sleeping lady." Both sentences stand. She cannot walk, and she travels everywhere.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

This chapter turns the grief pillar inside out. Catherine's sorrow is the sorrow of having had no sorrows:

"Any criticism of my mother's life must take into account, she herself had always said and Mr. Spitzer agreed, this sorrowful fact, the lack of sorrow. The lack of sorrow was a sorrow, too."

She has kept herself "vastly amused, sacrificing her life to her amusements", and the missed sorrows accumulate anyway. Her tears, Vera says, "were never for persons".

Mr. Spitzer's grief is the opposite kind, and just as permanent. He mourns Peron, the twin who "had killed himself by jumping from the tallest building in a cloud as a star flashed, as a train whistled", and he will not let the mourning end:

"He did his best to keep his sorrow alive. He was like a man who, having made one mistake, must make it again and again for the rest of his life—perhaps knocking forever at the wrong door"

Two griefs live in one house: one that never began, one that will not stop. Neither moves toward resolution.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Witness in this chapter is mostly absent, and the absence is the point. Catherine cannot be reached: "If one spoke, she did not hear, even if one announced his coming, even if one blew a trumpet opening the heavens, for she heard a silent voice." When she cries out from inside her ear, no one answers: "She would scream and cry, though no one heard her—or rather, no one paid attention".

The household's failure to witness her is matched by her failure to witness Mr. Spitzer. She has "taunted him with his inadequacy so many times and had reproached him for his continued life". He has come to her house for years and has never been seen as himself.

What witness remains belongs to Vera, telling it. She records her mother's raving without correcting it and without contempt, and once, in the middle of the chapter, she speaks in her own voice: "Poor mother, dreaming her life away like this as if there were no other life!" It is a short line. It is the one place in the chapter where someone stays in the room with Catherine and feels what it costs her.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Catherine builds without moving. Vera lists the powers she believes she has, and the list is a catalogue of construction:

"Had she not built great bridges, often with only a sigh? Was she not mechanical, a great engineer, a great mathematical genius who, if her mathematics had been accurate, should have succeeded in the manufacturing industry in a way to elicit nothing but admiration?"

She builds cities "in lands which had been unpopulated, where only the raven had spoken". None of it stands outside her room, and she builds it again every night. Vera gives the plainest picture of what this building is:

"She was like a person who, in a bare room, imagines many things, chairs and tables and draperies, swans, roses."

The bare room is real. So is the furniture she imagines into it, in the only sense that matters to her. And the building wears her out: Vera notes that she was "almost exhausted, that her imagination might perish before she perished". Provisional structures have to be held up, and holding them up is work.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Catherine's life is all departure and no arrival. She takes the night train from Boston to New York, and the train passes through the Alps and the Gobi Desert, "where was no railway, no head light but a star, no engineer of consciousness". Wherever she goes, she comes back unchanged: "her goings and comings seemed, rather than separate events in time and space, simultaneities, for hers were many coexistences".

The novel's own philosophy of the unfinished appears in this chapter almost as a creed:

"That which had never been, it was only that which was, for it had neither beginning nor end. The love which had never come into being was a greater love than love, and there was greater passion in restraint than in passion, greater being in non-being, greater torment in no torment than in torment."

Catherine's only hope of completion is death, and even that she imagines as an opening rather than an ending: "She believed, however, that she would some day die, and then she would be happy, divinely happy, for then she would have found a key to the door. She would have found a keyhole, perhaps only a keyhole in a cloud."

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Catherine is interested in everyone the world sets aside. Vera asks:

"Was she not interested in both those who were spotted and those who were unspotted, those who were beautiful and those who were disfigured, even as she was?"

And the guests who actually came to her house, in the years before she went to bed, were the marginal ones. The fashionable invited guests stayed away because she was thought mad; "Those who might have been fascinated by the idle prospect, those who had already been subdued by life, the luckless, the ill-fated, ill-starred, ill-omened—they were not those who had been invited, of course—though they were those who had stayed."

Cousin Hannah stands at another margin, a woman outside the feminine conventions of her time. Vera lists what Hannah could not do: she was "unable to blush if she lost her skirt, unable to stagger or lean upon an escort's arm, unable to faint", unable to "do battle with a fan, a seductive look, a flirtatious wink". Catherine thinks Hannah's suffrage battles were ones "she was bound to lose", and the list of what Hannah refused shows what the category of a proper lady demanded. At the edge, the construction shows.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

For Catherine, the past does not pass. It collapses into the present whenever she turns toward it: "The years collapsed to a moment whenever my mother looked back. She could not tell whether a thing had been a star or a firefly."

Cousin Hannah, dead in "a forgotten year", keeps changing in Catherine's memory, and Catherine keeps competing with her: "But my mother still, even after Cousin Hannah's death, must continue her rivalries, now when there was no living antagonist". The dead rival is still a rival. On the night train Catherine even wonders whether one of the bandits riding beside her might be "old Cousin Hannah, an apostate from her great cause".

And the objects that turn up in her bedroom are the past rising through the floor of the present: "things which seemed to turn up from nowhere, things which seemed to whirl around her like the dance of the dead years".

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Catherine is lost inside the organ of hearing, and the chapter takes her at her word:

"The ear, my mother said, was that great continent no one had ever completely known or understood, and no one had ever completely explored, and Aristotle had said that the ear was the doorway to the soul."

Inside it she finds every sound of her life at once: train whistles, foghorns, harp strings, "voices without bodies and sounds without sense". Language has become noise she cannot leave, and her cry is the chapter's most famous line: "Let me out of my ear! Let me out of my ear! My God, she cried—is there no silence?"

Vera's own sentences are built to match. One of them runs through the whole country of the ear, room after room, and ends on "paths never returning upon themselves even when they return, paths through this lost eternity, this madness which was this ear where she was lost". And Catherine's speech, Vera notes, has its own logic: "Really, she could be quite maddeningly reasonable at the most unexpected moments, surprising everybody by her brilliance, even herself with her opaque mind." What can be said in her house is shaped by what she can hear, and she hears everything.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Catherine's sacred is a world of ordinary things carried to every planet. Vera gives her mother's belief in one sentence:

"My mother always thought that if one should ever reach another planet, one should find that there were already upon that other planet a mirror, a rose, a door, a mystery."

A mirror, a rose, a door: the most domestic objects stand beside the mystery as its equals. Even her night journeys are full of plain things made strange, station masters "swinging their blurred lamps which were the stars", rabbits in lace veils, gophers she calls citizens.

And at the center of all her motion Vera finds stillness: "Perhaps she was the whirlpool, but she was also the still center at the heart of the whirlpool." She is "a star burning under water, a night flame burning in a glass". The sacred here is not elsewhere. It is in the bed, in the woman who cannot walk, burning where she lies.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Three people meet at this chapter's crossing point, and none chose the meeting. Catherine has been shaped by her retirement from the world, by the dead she cannot stop entertaining. Mr. Spitzer has been shaped by Peron's death and by his love for her. Cousin Hannah, dead, still shapes Catherine's sense of what she herself did not do.

Vera sets out what an honest life would have meant for Catherine, and the passage reads like a road not taken:

"An honest life would have been the realization of her vexed dreams, these tumultuous moments. She would have slain her tragic hero. She would have died a tragic death. She would have died young"

She did not take that road. She lay down instead, and what she deposited in the house is the atmosphere Vera grew up in. Lying awake to the train whistles, Catherine hears what all sleepers hear, as Vera tells it: "the sense of loneliness when each soul, though many, is yet one soul alone, and what happens to one must happen to all". That is the crossing point: each soul alone, and none of them separate. The carrying continues in the bedroom where nothing moves.
