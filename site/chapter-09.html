Summary

Vera asks whether she caused Miss MacIntosh’s death simply by opening her eyes. She recalls their life together: drills in geography and history, the Chicago fire and the glaciers, embroidery hoops and hemmed handkerchiefs, Solomon the turtle dragging a geranium pot across the schoolroom, and the silver dollar watch Miss MacIntosh used to remind her that time was passing. Then comes her fourteenth birthday. Mr. Spitzer sends a party dress, but no guests come. Vera dances alone and declares she is dancing with Death, and Miss MacIntosh sternly corrects her. That night Vera lies awake wondering about the man she might marry and whether a true marriage could ever be dissolved.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a haunting reflection on a terrible deed, possibly the death of Miss MacIntosh, and Vera’s lifelong torment over whether she was responsible, even as a child. She contemplates whether the death was murder, accident, fate, or suicide, and wrestles with deep remorse and existential questions about life, death, and responsibility. This passage sets the tone of guilt, mystery, and fatalism that permeates the chapter.
  • Vera describes Miss MacIntosh’s upbringing in What Cheer, Iowa, emphasizing her tough, unprivileged childhood and strict, austere personality. Despite her cheerfulness, she is portrayed as a forceful, practical woman who imposes discipline and domestic training on Vera. This passage highlights the tension between Miss MacIntosh’s rigid worldview and Vera’s more dreamlike, rebellious nature.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s insistence on preparing Vera for a traditional female role (domesticity and marriage) is detailed extensively. She emphasizes sewing skills, housekeeping, thriftiness, and moral discipline, reflecting societal expectations of women at the time. Vera’s subtle rebellion against this fate is also revealed. This theme of constrained female roles versus yearning for freedom is central to understanding Vera’s inner conflict.
  • The presence of an ancient turtle named Solomon in their home, which Miss MacIntosh disapproves of, serves as a symbol. The turtle, slow and slothful, contrasts with Miss MacIntosh’s energy and discipline. It may represent endurance, ancient wisdom, or the burdensome nature of their isolated existence. This subtle symbolism enriches the atmosphere of the household.
  • When Vera inquires about Miss MacIntosh’s ovaries and later her eyebrows, Miss MacIntosh evades these personal questions with unrelated historical or geographical lectures. This avoidance illustrates the emotional distance and repression within their relationship, as well as Miss MacIntosh’s discomfort with intimacy and femininity.
  • Vera’s fourteenth birthday is depicted as a moment of transition and tragedy. Despite preparations and the gift of a party dress, there is no real celebration or guests, and Vera dances alone, imagining a partner. Miss MacIntosh’s stern disapproval and the absence of family highlight the loneliness and isolation. This passage symbolizes lost innocence and the beginning of the end in their lives.
  • Vera’s fanciful statement, “I am dancing with Death,” and Miss MacIntosh’s severe response underscore the omnipresence of death and the seriousness with which it is regarded. The discussion reveals their differing attitudes toward life and death, with Miss MacIntosh embodying stern reality and Vera caught between fantasy and despair.
  • In the darkness after the birthday, Vera contemplates the nature of love and marriage, intrigued and troubled by Miss MacIntosh’s assertion that spouses do not truly know each other until after marriage and then it is too late. Vera dreams of a passionate, adventurous union rather than a mundane, poor existence, signaling a deep yearning for meaning and escape from imposed roles.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • Vera asks whether she killed Miss MacIntosh “only perhaps by opening my eyes.” What does she mean?
  • Miss MacIntosh answers the question about her eyebrows with the Chicago fire. What is she refusing to say?
  • “The dream was always the lie.” Does the chapter agree with Miss MacIntosh?

Characters

  • Miss MacIntosh calls herself “the land-locked sailor” who “could not swim an inch.” What does her sea talk reveal about her?
  • Vera imagines Miss MacIntosh as a circus ring-master with a mustache and bushy eyebrows. Why give her a man’s face?
  • Mr. Spitzer chooses a dress “too mature” for Vera while thinking “of someone else.” Whom does he see when he looks at her?

Connections

  • Madge called twelve “the age of wisdom” in Chapter 7, and here Miss MacIntosh does the same. What changes for Vera at twelve, and what does not?
  • Vera’s dance with Death echoes Madge’s “dead child dancing” in Chapter 6. Why do the girls in this novel dance with the dead?
  • Miss MacIntosh warns that “no wife ever knows her husband until after she is married to him.” How does Madge’s marriage prove her right?

Reflection

  • “If I knew Love, I would not dream of Death.” “What makes you think they are not one?” Who is right?
  • Is it kinder to train a child for the life she will probably have, or for the life she dreams of?
  • Vera says she “could have dispensed with her company” for seven years and then loved her in one month. Why do people so often know someone only at the end?

Research: Characters

Vera Cartwheel

  • A young girl on the cusp of adolescence, navigating strict domestic training and her own inner dreams.
  • Exhibits a tension between obedience and rebellion.
  • Yearns for love, adventure, and a meaningful future beyond the mundane expectations set by Miss MacIntosh.
  • Reflects deeply on identity, mortality, and the mystery of human relationships.

Miss MacIntosh

  • Vera’s guardian, strict and practical, embodying discipline and austere values.
  • Holds strong beliefs about women’s roles, marriage, and responsibility.
  • Comes from a harsh, unprivileged background in What Cheer, Iowa.
  • Acts as a maternal figure but also as a controlling authority.
  • Is caring yet unyielding, knowledgeable yet evasive.

Mr. Spitzer

  • Gives Vera her first party dress.
  • He gives Vera an old Roman coin and goes to Buzzards Bay to trace a lost heir “who had died a decade previously and who claimed to be egregiously alive.”
  • Suggests a connection to the past and to Vera’s mother, Catherine.
  • His absence at the party emphasizes Vera’s isolation.

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s Mother)

  • On Vera’s fourteenth birthday she lies “in an unearthly coma,” entertaining her dead guests, “the many P’s,” from Pythagoras and Proteus to Pandora and pterodactyls.

Solomon the Turtle

  • Symbolic companion residing in the schoolroom.
  • Represents slowness, wisdom, and perhaps stagnation.
  • Miss MacIntosh disapproves of the turtle, reflecting her disdain for inactivity and sloth.

Research: Themes

Loss and Remorse

See: Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • Vera grapples with a haunting sense of guilt and loss, possibly linked to Miss MacIntosh’s disappearance or death.
  • Questions of responsibility and fate permeate the narrative.

Discipline and Domesticity

See: Order, Chaos, and Discipline

  • Miss MacIntosh’s rigorous training prepares Vera for the traditional female role of housekeeping and marriage.
  • Emphasis on practical skills (sewing, household chores) contrasts with Vera’s imaginative inner life.
  • The discipline is strict, yet there is an undercurrent of rebellion and dissatisfaction.

Dreams vs. Reality

See: Illusion and Reality · Dreams and Imagination

  • Vera’s dreams of adventure, romance, and the wider world conflict with Miss MacIntosh’s insistence on practicality and limitation.
  • The metaphor of the circus (ringmaster and bareback rider) illustrates the control Miss MacIntosh exerts over Vera’s life.

Mortality and Death

See: Death and Mortality

  • Vera dances “with Death,” and the inevitability of mortality is discussed seriously by Miss MacIntosh.
  • The chapter contemplates death’s universality and the human responses to it.

Marriage and Gender Roles

See: Women, Gender, and Marriage

  • Marriage is portrayed as the “normal state” and a natural institution sanctioned by God and nature.
  • Miss MacIntosh emphasizes the importance of preparation for marriage and warns against sexual dangers.
  • Vera reflects on the mystery of marriage, love, and the unknown nature of her future husband.

Isolation and Companionship

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Vera and Miss MacIntosh live in relative isolation, with only the ancient turtle Solomon as a companion.
  • Their relationship is marked by authority and tension but also moments of care.

Nature and Nautical Imagery

See: Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos

  • References to the sea, birds, fog, and weather create a vivid natural backdrop and symbolize change and uncertainty.
  • Nautical language reflects Miss MacIntosh’s character and worldview.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Sea and Water Imagery

See: The Sea and Water

  • The sea, waves, tides, fog, and shore recur repeatedly as symbols of life’s uncertainty, danger, and transition.
  • The sea represents the unknown, the subconscious, and the boundary between life and death.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s identity as a “land-locked sailor” who “could not swim an inch” but who constantly talks in nautical terms symbolizes a life caught between stability and the wild, uncontrollable forces of nature.

Bird Imagery, Especially Seagulls

See: Birds

  • Seagulls appear as recurring natural motifs connected with care and injury. They may symbolize vulnerability and healing, but also freedom and the natural world.
  • The death of the seagull “when its wound was healed” suggests a paradox of survival and loss, or the cost of recovery.

Embroidery Hoops and Sewing

  • Embroidery hoops and sewing serve as symbols of domesticity, discipline, and the preparation for womanhood and marriage.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s insistence on sewing straight basting stitches and hemming handkerchiefs reflects the imposition of order and control over Vera’s life.
  • The hoops themselves evoke a circus ring and suggest that Vera’s life is a performance under Miss MacIntosh’s strict supervision.

The Turtle Named Solomon

  • The ancient turtle living in the schoolroom functions as a symbol of slow, deliberate life and possibly wisdom or its apparent absence.
  • Miss MacIntosh despises the turtle for its slowness and sees no wisdom in it, associating it with sloth and ignorance.
  • The turtle’s carrying a clay flower pot with a geranium that smells of tobacco suggests a blend of nature and artifice, endurance, and perhaps the burden of life.

Time and Watches

See: Clocks and Watches

  • The silver dollar watch worn by Miss MacIntosh symbolizes the relentless march of time and the pressure of discipline.
  • Miss MacIntosh frequently refers to time as deceptive and insists on punctuality and the urgency of preparing for the future, reinforcing the theme of control versus Vera’s dreams.

Light and Darkness

See: Lamps, Candles, and Light

  • The interplay of light and darkness appears in the descriptions of the house and Vera’s inner world.
  • The house is described as cold and resembling a funeral parlor, emphasizing decay and death.
  • Vera’s dreams and secret thoughts are contrasted with the harsh reality imposed by Miss MacIntosh, symbolizing the tension between imagination and discipline.

Death and Dancing with Death

  • Death is personified as a companion Vera dances with, highlighting a close and intimate relationship with mortality.
  • The dialogue with Miss MacIntosh about Death emphasizes its inevitability and commonality but also Vera’s youthful fascination and defiance.

Marriage and Reproduction Imagery

See: Weddings and Funerals

  • Miss MacIntosh’s lectures on marriage, ovaries, and reproduction are laden with symbolic meaning about societal expectations, femininity, and the passage into adulthood.
  • The metaphor of flowers needing a man to be impregnated (not just wind) symbolizes the necessity of union and procreation in life, reflecting the cultural norms around marriage and gender roles.

The Party Dress and the Birthday Celebration

See: Clothing, Hats, and Skirts · Pregnancy, Birth, and Children

  • The party dress, described as filmy, sleeveless, and embroidered with seed pearls, symbolizes both Vera’s coming of age and the tension between innocence and emerging sexuality.
  • The empty birthday party and Vera dancing alone highlight loneliness, unfulfilled expectations, and the absence of community or connection.

Natural Elements and Geography

  • Miss MacIntosh’s frequent references to geography, natural phenomena, and history (e.g., Chicago fire, glaciers, grasshoppers, rivers) symbolize the structured, factual world she insists on teaching, contrasting with Vera’s inner dream world and rebellion.

  • “Was it murder or the key turning of itself in the lock?”: Vera asks the question of Chapters 7 and 8, now of Miss MacIntosh. See the Chapter 7 resources.
  • “Desire be to thy husband”: God tells Eve, “thy desire shall be to thy husband” (Genesis 3:16). See Genesis.
  • “All of us are mortal, and some of us are more mortal than others”: George Orwell’s Animal Farm (1945): “All animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others.”
  • “What Cheer, Iowa”: A real town in Keokuk County. Its name is said to come from Roger Williams’s greeting to the Narragansett, “What cheer, netop?” See Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest.
  • “She was just the cross I had to bear”: “Whosoever doth not bear his cross, and come after me, cannot be my disciple” (Luke 14:27). See The Bible.
  • “Opportunity, it has been said, knocks only once at the door”: John James Ingalls’s poem “Opportunity” (1891): “I knock unbidden once at every gate.”
  • “Sit not there dreaming of Triton among the minnows”: Triton is a Greek god of the sea who later evolved into a merman. He is the messenger of his father, the god Poseidon. Wordsworth, in “The World Is Too Much with Us,” would rather be a pagan and “hear old Triton blow his wreathèd horn.” Miss MacIntosh would have none of it. See Greek Mythology and Romantic Poets.
  • “Time marches on”: The March of Time newsreels (1935-1951) closed with “Time marches on!”
  • “naked as in that day when we came from our mothers’ wombs”: “Naked came I out of my mother’s womb” (Job 1:21). See The Book of Job.
  • “Hope dies hard in the human breast”: Miss MacIntosh turns Pope’s “Hope springs eternal in the human breast” (An Essay on Man, 1734).
  • “When, Vera Cartwheel, did the first Viking land upon this bleak New England shore?”: Leif Erikson reached North America about 1000. The Norse site at L’Anse aux Meadows, Newfoundland, was found in 1960, and New Englanders long claimed Vikings had landed there.
  • “can you locate its source?”: The Mississippi rises at Lake Itasca, Minnesota, named its source by Henry Schoolcraft in 1832.
  • “for we were put here to be tested and not found wanting”: The writing on Belshazzar’s wall reads, “Thou art weighed in the balances, and art found wanting” (Daniel 5:27). Miss MacIntosh sees life as a rigorous discipline, where even suffering and failure have their place but must be met with fortitude. The phrase underscores her belief in the necessity of accepting life’s challenges as part of one’s duty and not giving way to despair or weakness. See The Bible.
  • “Charles’s Wain, the Hair of Berenice”: Charles’s Wain is the Big Dipper. Berenice’s Hair is the queen’s hair offered to the gods and set among the stars. Cassiopeia sits in her chair. See Astronomy.
  • “Twelve hours in a day, twelve hours in a night”: “Are there not twelve hours in the day?” (John 11:9). See The Bible.
  • “breaking into two parts like the worm”: Flatworms can be cut in two and each half grows into a whole worm.
  • “a loving couple need not ratify its bond by marriage”: Common-law marriage, by living together as husband and wife, is still recognized in Iowa. See Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest.
  • “the apple was no sweet mackintosh”: The McIntosh apple, found in Ontario in 1811, carries Miss MacIntosh’s name. Genesis never names the fruit; the apple came from the Latin pun malum, “apple” and “evil.” See Genesis.
  • “God, Who had divided the light from the darkness and created great whales”: “And God created great whales . . . Be fruitful, and multiply, and fill the waters in the seas” (Genesis 1:21-22). See Genesis.
  • “had been found in the mouth of a dead salmon”: In Glasgow’s legend Saint Mungo recovered a queen’s ring from a salmon’s mouth, and Herodotus tells how Polycrates’s ring came back in a fish.
  • “the G which stood for Georgia”: Georgia is a female name from the Greek georgos, farmer, a fitting name for the earthbound Miss MacIntosh. Its masculine form is George.
  • “her bloomers, which were red jersey”: Amelia Bloomer promoted the loose trousers named for her in 1851.
  • “her underclothes had been made of bleached flour bags”: Poor families sewed clothes from flour and feed sacks, and by the 1930s mills printed the sacks in patterns for that use.
  • “the London Tower where the ravens were chained”: Legend says the kingdom will fall if the ravens leave the Tower of London, so their wings are clipped.
  • “the dark Satanic mills”: William Blake’s “Jerusalem” (1804). See Romantic Poets.
  • “The old ship would be entombed in an ice block with its captain still on board”: In the legend of the Octavius, a whaler found in 1775 drifting off Greenland, the captain sat frozen at his log.
  • “all the genealogies of the Moody Bible”: The Moody Bible Institute was founded in Chicago in 1886 by the evangelist Dwight L. Moody. It is a conservative, evangelical Bible college.
  • “the sons of Levi, Judah, Er, Onan, Issachar, the sons of Tola, Phuvah, Zebulun”: Miss MacIntosh makes Vera memorize the Bible’s genealogies for discipline. See The Bible.
    • Levi was the third son of Jacob and Leah in the Bible.
    • Judah was the fourth son of Jacob and Leah.
    • Er was the eldest son of Judah and a Canaanite wife (Genesis 38).
    • Onan was the second son of Judah. He spilled his seed on the ground (Genesis 38:9), an irony in a chapter about ovaries and seeds.
    • Issachar was the ninth son of Jacob.
    • Tola was a son of Issachar (Genesis 46:13), and he had six sons (1 Chronicles 7:2). A later Tola was one of the Judges of Israel (Judges 10:1).
    • Phuvah was another son of Issachar, like Tola. Zebulun was the last son of Jacob and Leah.
  • “the heave shoulder and the breast wave”: “The wave breast and the heave shoulder” are the priests’ portion of the sacrifice (Leviticus 7:34), and Vera turns the phrase around. See The Bible.
  • “Hannibal crossing the Alps with his elephants”: Hannibal crossed in 218 B.C. to attack Rome.
  • “in some legendary preadamite time”: Pre-Adamism is the belief that there were humans before Adam. Isaac La Peyrère argued in 1655 that men lived before Adam.
  • “the whole world on his back”: Vera says the turtle is so old that it might be the “World Turtle.” This myth is found in different parts of the world and different cultures. In Hindu myth the turtle Akupara holds up the world, and the Lenape and Haudenosaunee tell of the earth built on a turtle’s back, Turtle Island.
  • “Solomon, Miss MacIntosh objected, had had too many wives”: Solomon may be named after King Solomon of the Bible, who was regarded as a wealthy and wise king. Solomon “had seven hundred wives, princesses, and three hundred concubines” (1 Kings 11:3), and his temple had tables of gold, lamps, snuffers, and pomegranates (1 Kings 7). See The Bible.
  • “Wall Street or Threadneedle Street”: Wall Street is the banking center of the U.S., and Threadneedle Street is home of the Bank of England, “the Old Lady of Threadneedle Street.”
  • “My twelfth birthday, Miss MacIntosh said, was the dividing line”: Madge named twelve “the age of wisdom” too. See the Chapter 7 resources.
  • “Her red, marcelled hair”: The Marcel wave, set with hot irons, was invented by Marcel Grateau in Paris in 1872.
  • “Ovaries, I knew, were little brown pods filled with seeds”: Vera gives her own explanation of ovaries where there is a comparison to a school book which showed the anatomy of flowers. In a detailed drawing a flower has ovaries that look similar to the human female reproductive organs. In a flower the ovary holds the ovules, which become seeds. The anthers hold pollen.
  • “sixteen bleeding Greek metropolitans”: Greek metropolitans are archbishops who head a province of the Orthodox Church. Some were killed and became martyrs and saints. In 1922, when Smyrna burned, Metropolitan Chrysostomos was handed to a mob and mutilated to death, one bleeding Greek metropolitan the church made a saint.
    • The sixteen metropolitans may refer to the Greek genocide (1914-1922).
  • “the great Chicago fire”: It burned October 8-10, 1871, killed about three hundred people, and was blamed on Mrs. O’Leary’s cow, which shares a name with old Doc, Justice O’Leary.
  • “Where were those showers of grasshoppers?”: Rocky Mountain locusts swarmed the Plains in 1874-1877 in clouds that darkened the sun.
  • “Where were those seven years of lean? Who shall divide the wheat from the tares? Who hid his lantern under a bushel?”: Joseph’s seven lean years (Genesis 41), the parable of the wheat and the tares (Matthew 13:24-30), and the candle under a bushel (Matthew 5:15). See The Bible.
  • “There, but for God’s grace, go I”: “There but for the grace of God” is attributed to John Bradford in reference to a group of prisoners being led to execution. John Bradford was an English Reformer, a prebendary of St. Paul’s, and martyr. He was imprisoned in the Tower of London for alleged crimes against Queen Mary I. He was burned at the stake on July 1, 1555. Paul writes, “But by the grace of God I am what I am, and his grace toward me was not in vain” (1 Corinthians 15:10). This meaning fits into the broader themes of the novel around fate, suffering, and the search for meaning amidst human vulnerability.
  • “the thirteen year old son of a Portuguese fisherman”: Portuguese fishermen from the Azores settled New Bedford, Gloucester, and Provincetown.
  • “A blind violinist and a deaf dancer”: There are many blind, deaf, and mute people in the book. This could be a play on the Three Wise Monkeys. The Three Wise Monkeys are a Japanese folk representation of the proverb “see no evil, hear no evil, speak no evil.” They symbolize prudence and purity, and are often seen as a call for discretion or willful ignorance. The three monkeys are Mizaru (see no evil), Kikazaru (hear no evil), and Iwazaru (speak no evil). The concept originates in Japan, with statues of the monkeys found in temples and shrines. The proverb “see no evil, hear no evil, speak no evil” is often interpreted as a way to avoid negative experiences or situations by choosing to ignore them. In Japan, the monkeys are seen as attendants of Koshin, the God of Roads, and their message is used to teach prudence and purity. In the West, the proverb is often used sarcastically to criticize those who deliberately ignore wrongdoing. The idea of avoiding what is contrary to propriety is rooted in Confucius’s teachings. Some representations include a fourth monkey, Sezaru, who “does no evil.” One misses the train by seeing it but not hearing its whistle, the other by hearing but not seeing. See the Chapter 8 resources and Deafness and Sign Language.
  • “for tomorrow was another day”: Scarlett O’Hara’s last line in Gone with the Wind (1936): “After all, tomorrow is another day!”
  • “Early to bed and early to rise makes a man healthy, wealthy and wise”: The proverb praises rising before most others, early and consistently, so as to accomplish more in the day. This method has been recommended since antiquity. The philosopher Aristotle said a phrase very similar to the one Miss MacIntosh used. Within the context of religious observances, spiritual writers have called this practice the heroic minute, referring to the sacrifice which this entails. Benjamin Franklin printed the exact phrase Miss MacIntosh used in Poor Richard’s Almanack (1735). It is a saying that is viewed as a commonsensical proverb.
  • “this vale of tears”: “In hac lacrimarum valle,” from the hymn Salve Regina, after Psalm 84:6. See The Bible.
  • “It would be better to celebrate, not the day we were born into this world but the day we were born out of it”: “The day of death than the day of one’s birth” (Ecclesiastes 7:1). See Ecclesiastes.
  • “Was there even a dark angel at the gate? What horseman reining his horse at the door?”: Death rides a pale horse (Revelation 6:8). Lionel Johnson’s poem “The Dark Angel” (1893) is about a tempter who haunts the soul. See The Bible.
  • “Pythagoras who had a golden thigh”: Pythagoras was an ancient Greek philosopher. It is believed his teachings were metempsychosis, that every soul is immortal and after death enters another body, and musica universalis where the planets move according to mathematical equations and create an inaudible symphony of music. Legend says he showed a golden thigh at Olympia as a sign of his divinity. See Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres.
  • “Proteus who changed from fish to bird before her eyes”: Proteus was an early Greek, shape-shifting sea-god. In the Odyssey Menelaus holds him fast while he turns into a lion, a serpent, a leopard, water, and a tree. See Greek Mythology.
  • “Pandora’s bottomless box”: Pandora was the first woman created and her story is used to explain why there is evil in the world. In Hesiod it was a jar. Erasmus made it a box in 1508. Only Hope stayed inside, and Miss MacIntosh says, “Hope dies hard in the human breast.” See Greek Mythology.
  • “Pythian games”: The Pythian Games were held at Delphi every four years in honor of Apollo, two years after each Olympics. They included music, poetry, and dance, and women could participate in some of the events. See the Chapter 6 resources on laurel.
  • “the canker eats the rose”: “And loathsome canker lives in sweetest bud” (Shakespeare, Sonnet 35). See Shakespeare.
  • “There is vomit at all tables”: “For all tables are full of vomit and filthiness” (Isaiah 28:8). See The Bible.
  • “the ephemera who last but for a day”: Mayflies, order Ephemeroptera, live as winged adults for a day or less.
  • “Died Abner as a fool died”: David’s lament for Abner, murdered by Joab: “Died Abner as a fool dieth?” (2 Samuel 3:33). This phrase is part of a broader meditation in the chapter on the impermanence of human life, the certainty of death, and the idea that all distinctions between people are ultimately erased by death. It aligns with the novel’s recurring themes of mortality and existential reflection. See The Bible.
  • “One died in his full strength. Another died in bitterness of soul. How died the wise man?”: Job 21:23-25, and “how dieth the wise man? as the fool” (Ecclesiastes 2:16). See The Book of Job and Ecclesiastes.
  • “When thou diest, I shall die with you”: Ruth 1:16-17 reads, “whither thou goest, I will go . . . Where thou diest, will I die.” This symbolizes the depth of commitment and the tragic unity of two souls bound by love, loss, and the inevitability of death. See The Bible.
  • “Experience, as I have heard, is a much harder teacher”: Franklin: “Experience keeps a dear school, but fools will learn in no other” (Poor Richard, 1743).
  • “What makes you think they are not one?”: Love and death as one is the Liebestod, the love-death of Wagner’s Tristan und Isolde (1865). See Music and Composers.
  • “There are many gay deceivers”: A “gay deceiver” was an old name for a rake or seducer. Gay here could mean happy or may be in reference to homosexuals. The term gay to describe immorality or someone addicted to pleasure was in use in the 17th century. Gertrude Stein in 1922 used the word gay to specifically reference homosexuals. In 1950, the earliest reference found to date for the word gay as a self-described name for homosexuals came from Alfred A. Gross who used it in the June 1950 issue of SIR magazine. In a series of interviews with Susan S. Neville, Marguerite Young said she had lovers both male and female. There was a relationship where she would not marry him but said he could visit her every Tuesday for the rest of her life.
  • “the Islands of the Blessed”: Islands of the Blessed or the Fortunate Isles are the winterless earthly paradise of the heroes of Greek mythology. See Greek Mythology.
  • “the Islands of the Dead”: Arnold Böcklin’s painting Isle of the Dead (1880-1886), a rocky island of cypresses with a white-draped figure arriving by boat, inspired Rachmaninoff’s tone poem (1909). See Music and Composers.
  • “all local habitations and all names”: The poet “gives to airy nothing / A local habitation and a name” (A Midsummer Night’s Dream 5.1). See Shakespeare.
  • “Were not true marriages made in heaven”: “What therefore God hath joined together, let not man put asunder” (Matthew 19:6). See The Bible.
