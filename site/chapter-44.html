Summary

Over the years, Mr. Spitzer pieces together fragments of Hannah's hidden life from her papers: letters, unreadable languages, evidence of a double existence. He recalls her deathbed talk of skirts and petticoats, strange from the great champion of bloomers, and her cry that false modesty killed her love. He wonders whether heroes are driven by a secret cowardice, and whether those who never loved know most of love.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, Mr. Spitzer reflects on the paradox of Cousin Hannah, a great feminist and enemy of restrictive fashion, calling for her skirt as she was dying. This paradox highlights the complexities and contradictions in her character. Though she battled societal constraints, at the end she seemed to long for something she had fought to discard. This mystery sets the tone for the chapter's exploration of illusion versus truth and the human vulnerability beneath heroic veneer.
  • Mr. Spitzer's gesture of bringing her snowdrops, delicate white flowers resembling snow, and her agony increasing upon seeing them melt in her hand is a poignant metaphor. It suggests the fragility of life and love, the melting away of hope, and the uncontrollable forces that govern human fate, forces akin to natural phenomena like avalanches and winds, that Mr. Spitzer likens to a musician losing control of his baton.
  • Cousin Hannah’s dying lament reveals a deep personal grief underlying her public persona. Her repeated cry about finding her skirt is both literal and symbolic, implying a search for identity, love, or completeness that was never fulfilled. This passage underscores the theme of lost love as a motivating force behind her suffrage battles and personal suffering.
  • Cousin Hannah’s words that “False modesty killed my love” and the declaration that there was “no man in all those mountains” or “one star looking down” evoke isolation and the absence of external validation or support. This passage deepens the existential solitude of her experience and suggests that internal struggles, not external enemies, may have been her greatest challenge.
  • As she questions whether her love and the love she received were true or false, the suffrage captain’s dying confession reveals the complexity of human emotions and relationships. This ambivalence illustrates that heroism and love are not clear-cut or absolute but often involve contradictions and inner conflict.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s reflection on the possibility that the greatest heroes might be driven by secret cowardice and that this inner failure propels them toward greater battles is philosophically profound. It questions traditional notions of heroism and valor, suggesting that vulnerability and fear are inseparable from greatness.
  • The chapter repeatedly returns to death as a process of transformation rather than an end: “flowers turning into birds, birds turning into flowers,” and “death was this eternal transmutation.” This idea challenges conventional finality of death, proposing a mystical continuity that blurs life and death.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s struggle to understand the suffragette’s life, filled with secret letters, unintelligible languages, and mysteries “greater than the Sphinx,” symbolizes the inscrutability of human motivations and emotions. Her heart is portrayed as “in darkness and terrifying whirlwind,” emphasizing that even the most public figures harbor private enigmas.
  • The paradox that Cousin Hannah had “never experienced” love, death, or marriage in a conventional sense, yet was the confidante of many secrets and the recipient of many whispered confessions, raises profound questions about knowledge, experience, and empathy. It suggests that knowing about human emotions can be distinct from living them.
  • The closing reflections ponder the nature of love and death as inseparable and universal experiences that “all who lived had already died” in some sense, and that the greatest love might be ineffable and unexpressed. Mr. Spitzer’s thoughts on the fate of all lives being “increased by this great mystery of death” provide a somber, philosophical conclusion to the chapter.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Cousin Hannah / The Great Suffrage Captain

  • A once-celebrated suffragette whose public life was marked by courage and activism.
  • In private, she was vulnerable, haunted by lost love, and self-doubt.
  • Her death is poetic and symbolic, involving imagery of skirts, snowdrops, mountains, and clouds.

Mr. Spitzer

  • An old lawyer entrusted with handling Cousin Hannah’s affairs after her death.
  • Reflective, cautious, and rational, yet deeply affected by the mysteries he uncovers.
  • He struggles to reconcile the public image of Cousin Hannah with the private truths he learns.
  • Acts as Vera and interpreter of the suffragette’s life and legacy.

Research: Themes

Truth vs. Illusion

See: Illusion and Reality

  • The chapter opens with the idea that truth in life is rare but powerful, like granite and sunlight amidst illusions (moonbeams). Mr. Spitzer slowly pieces together the fragmented truth of Cousin Hannah’s life, which is elusive and fleeting.
  • The suffragette’s public persona versus her private reality is a central tension. Though once celebrated, her fame has faded, and her true self remains mysterious and partly hidden.

Love and Loss

See: Love · Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • Despite being a fierce fighter for women’s rights, Cousin Hannah is shown to be deeply haunted by a lost love, which shapes much of her life and final moments.
  • Her dying cries for her "love," "little snowdrop," and her search for a "skirt" symbolize a yearning for something lost, possibly innocence, identity, or a personal connection.
  • The narrative suggests that her suffrage activism was motivated by personal heartbreak, intertwining public struggle with private grief 455.

Heroism and Cowardice

See: Fear and Courage

  • The chapter challenges traditional notions of heroism, suggesting that great heroes may be driven by secret cowardice or inner failures 456.
  • Cousin Hannah is portrayed as both a heroic figure and a vulnerable woman who doubted herself at the end.
  • Mr. Spitzer debates the nature of courage, recognizing that even the smallest, seemingly weak acts can be heroic, such as a moth’s desire to reach a star.

Death and Memory

See: Death and Mortality · Memory and the Past

  • Death is portrayed as a transformative and mysterious force, an eternal transmutation where forms change but essence remains.
  • Mr. Spitzer's reflections suggest that death does not erase a person but deepens the mystery of their existence.
  • The chapter explores how memory is fragile; great lives and deeds can be forgotten, and the true nature of a person might never be fully known.

Mystery and the Human Heart

See: Faith, God, and the Supernatural

  • The suffragette is depicted as a repository of secrets and confidences, receiving the hidden sorrows and loves of others even as her own heart remains obscure.
  • The novel questions whether anyone can truly understand love, life, or ambition, suggesting these remain inscrutable mysteries.
  • The idea that knowing without experiencing, knowing love without loving, or death without dying, might be a deeper form of understanding is proposed.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Skirts, Laces, Petticoats

See: Clothing, Hats, and Skirts

  • Symbolize femininity, freedom, and perhaps the burden of societal expectations. Cousin Hannah’s paradoxical final request for her skirt contrasts with her life’s advocacy for shedding such encumbrances.

Snowdrops and Snow

See: Snow, Ice, and Winter · Roses and Flowers

  • Represent purity, fragility, and the coldness of death. The melting snowdrops in her hand symbolize fading life and lost love.

Mountains, Clouds, Icebergs

See: Snow, Ice, and Winter · Mountains · Clouds and Storms

  • Evoke the harshness and isolation of her inner world and struggles.
  • The iceberg imagery suggests overwhelming, unseen dangers and emotional coldness.

Music and Sound

See: Music and Instruments

  • Mr. Spitzer often parallels his thoughts to music, hearing the “roaring of the long-tongued surf” and “race-track music,” symbolizing the ongoing passage of time and memory.

The Whirlwind and Darkness

See: Lamps, Candles, and Light · Clouds and Storms

  • Represent confusion, chaos, and the unknowability of the human heart.

Research: Resources

  • Lucy Stone and other suffragists adopted the Bloomer costume, which consisted of a knee-length skirt worn over trousers or pantaloons, in the early 1850s. Stone loved the comfort and practicality of the outfit and wore it both at home and in public for about three years. However, the Bloomer costume attracted significant public scrutiny and ridicule, and critics of the women's rights movement often focused on the reformers' attire rather than their arguments for suffrage and other reforms. By late 1853, many prominent suffragists, including Elizabeth Cady Stanton, abandoned the Bloomer costume due to the negative attention it generated. Stanton persuaded Stone to follow suit. Stone resumed wearing long skirts, although she never again wore the restrictive corsets and voluminous dresses of the fashionable style. Other notable suffragists like Susan B. Anthony also stopped wearing Bloomers around the same time. Amelia Bloomer, the editor of The Lily newspaper and namesake of the costume, continued to wear it until 1858. Only Elizabeth Smith Miller, who had helped popularize the style, continued to wear bloomers longer, until 1861.
  • There is a reference to “heart of darkness” again (book by Conrad that examines the horrors of Western colonialism). The phrase encapsulates both the personal anguish of Catherine and the broader existential themes of decay, loss, and the search for meaning in the novel.
  • “For false was my true love’s heart.” is used in folk songs like, A Brisk Young Sailor Courted Me, having some similarities to Madge’s subplot. Blackamoor - Hannah’s conscience may be similar to the Black Coachman for Catherine. It signifies the painful recognition that her idealized love was fundamentally flawed or deceptive, contributing to her sorrow and the novel’s ongoing exploration of illusion versus reality in human connections.
  • “eternal transmutation” - Transmutation is change and alchemy is a process of elevation. There is a subtle difference between the two and both are associated with the idea of turning lead into gold. They’re often used as a metaphor for spiritual or personal growth. With transformation it is inevitable because life is in a constant state of flux. People are in a perpetual process of becoming. Another way of pointing to Thales of Miletus and Heraclitus. This concept aligns with the novel’s broader themes of illusion, identity, and the blurred boundaries between life and death, reality and dream. It highlights the paradox of permanence in impermanence or the idea that while individual forms or lives may end or change, the process of transformation is ongoing and eternal.
  • “bearding the sleeping lion in its den” means to attack someone on their own ground or subject. The context of the novel, this expression highlights Mr. Spitzer's role as a persistent figure who, despite his cautious and reserved nature, must face the daunting and often confusing realities surrounding the Cartwheel family and their complex, dreamlike world. It suggests his courageous, though sometimes reluctant, engagement with the mysteries and challenges he encounters, especially concerning the enigmatic and troubled lives of the characters like Vera, her mother Catherine, and Miss MacIntosh.
  • The dedication Young gave the novel is in this chapter “For all dead loves and all remembered things. I have travelled through many seas.” The phrase reflects the novel’s overarching themes of memory, loss, and the blending of reality and dream, emphasizing that the journey through these "seas" is both personal and universal, touching on the shared human experience of love and remembrance.
  • “Judas cock” may refer to the Book of the Cock/Rooster which was probably written in the 400s or 500s in Arabic or Greek and used by Ethiopian Christians who have and use the text during Holy Week activities. It describes the final three days of Jesus's life, including a sequence where he reanimated a previously roasted rooster that He sent to spy on Judas Iscariot when he (Judas) was plotting to betray Jesus. The rooster informed Jesus and his disciples of the plan. Among other events, the Book of the Rooster describes Saul of Tarsus' participation in the arrest, abuse and crucifixion of Jesus, the betrayal of Jesus by a female relative of Judas, a debate between Pilate and Herod about the culpability of Jesus, Mary's grief, John the Evangelist witnessing the crucifixion of Jesus, the conversations Jesus had with the two thieves he was crucified alongside Gestas (Awsēmobyā) and Demas (Salikonilidākki) and references to other miracles that Jesus performed during his life. The rooster may be based on the Ziz of Jewish mythology, a giant bird. Like the Gospel of Judas from the second century, the Book of the Rooster attempts to explain the betrayal of Judas before the Last Supper, an act which is traditionally seen as abrupt or lacking reason.
  • Beau joueur is a person who shows a spirit of fairness and generosity in sport.
  • A chanticleer is another name for a rooster. It is also a character name in various works of fiction such as Reynard the Fox and The Nun’s Priest’s Tale in the book Canterbury Tales.
  • Albert Einstein was a German theoretical physicist who is best known for the theory of relativity. Relativity was a revolutionary idea that would change our understanding of the universe, but they were rarely able to explain any further. He said in a 1929 interview that, “The meaning of relativity has been widely misunderstood. Philosophers play with the word, like a child with a doll. Relativity, as I see it, merely denotes that certain physical and mechanical facts, which have been regarded as positive and permanent, are relative with regard to certain other facts in the sphere of physics and mechanics. It does not mean that we have the right to turn the world mischievously topsy-turvy.”
  • “there was no such thing as safety—not even for her— certainly none for him” This sentence encapsulates the idea that safety, in the absolute sense, is an illusion for all humans, even for the seemingly steadfast Miss MacIntosh and the cautious Mr. Spitzer, as they face the inexorable forces of fate, death, and the unknown. It is similar to Helen Keller’s quote from The Open Door, “Security is mostly a superstition. It does not exist in nature, nor do the children of men as a whole experience it. Avoiding danger is no safer in the long run than outright exposure. Life is either a daring adventure, or nothing.”
