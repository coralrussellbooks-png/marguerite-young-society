To All My Darlings

Chapter 44

On Chapter 44 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 10, 2026

← Chapter 43 · Home · Chapter 45 →

Summary

Over the years Mr. Spitzer pieces together the truth of Cousin Hannah’s last days. The great bloomer girl, who spent her life urging ladies to take off their skirts, called for her skirt as she died. When he brought her snowdrops, they melted in her hand, and from that moment she began to fade. In her delirium she relived a climb in the snowbound mountains: her love, “my lovely bride,” turned back to find her skirt, out of a modesty Hannah calls false since there was no one to see, and was lost in the snow. Hannah blamed herself for letting go, and Mr. Spitzer understands at last that this lost love drove her suffrage battles and her wanderings. He wonders whether great heroes are driven by secret cowardice, whether the burning moth is braver than the star, and whether suffrage was only her mask. In her papers he finds letters in languages no one can read, many like love letters to a great love, and he remembers a sea shell he once found, written over: “For all dead loves and all remembered things. I have travelled through many seas.” Perhaps he knows most of love who never loved.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, Mr. Spitzer reflects on the paradox of Cousin Hannah, a great feminist and enemy of restrictive fashion, calling for her skirt as she was dying. This paradox highlights the complexities and contradictions in her character. Though she battled societal constraints, at the end she seemed to long for something she had fought to discard. This mystery sets the tone for the chapter’s exploration of illusion versus truth and the human vulnerability beneath heroic veneer.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s gesture of bringing her snowdrops, delicate white flowers resembling snow, and her agony increasing upon seeing them melt in her hand is a poignant metaphor. It suggests the fragility of life and love, the melting away of hope, and the uncontrollable forces that govern human fate, forces akin to natural phenomena like avalanches and winds, that Mr. Spitzer likens to a musician losing control of his baton.
  • Cousin Hannah’s dying lament reveals a deep personal grief underlying her public persona. Her repeated cry about finding her skirt is both literal and symbolic, implying a search for identity, love, or completeness that was never fulfilled. This passage underscores the theme of lost love as a motivating force behind her suffrage battles and personal suffering.
  • Cousin Hannah’s words that “False modesty killed my love” and the declaration that there was “no man in all those mountains” or “one star looking down” mean that no one was there to see her lover’s nakedness, so the modesty that sent her back for her skirt was false, and it killed her. Hannah’s grief turns to self-accusation: “Did I let go with my hand which should have clung to yours, even through all eternity or for one moment?”
  • As she questions whether her love and the love she received were true or false, the suffrage captain’s dying confession reveals the complexity of human emotions and relationships. This ambivalence illustrates that heroism and love are not clear-cut or absolute but often involve contradictions and inner conflict.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s reflection on the possibility that the greatest heroes might be driven by secret cowardice and that this inner failure propels them toward greater battles is philosophically profound. It questions traditional notions of heroism and valor, suggesting that vulnerability and fear are inseparable from greatness.
  • The chapter repeatedly returns to death as a process of transformation rather than an end: “flowers turning into birds, birds turning into flowers,” and “death was this eternal transmutation.” This idea challenges conventional finality of death, proposing a mystical continuity that blurs life and death.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s struggle to understand the suffragette’s life, filled with secret letters, unintelligible languages, and mysteries “greater than the Sphinx,” symbolizes the inscrutability of human motivations and emotions. Her heart is portrayed as “in darkness and in terrifying whirlwind,” emphasizing that even the most public figures harbor private enigmas.
  • The paradox that Cousin Hannah had “never experienced” love, death, or marriage in a conventional sense, yet was the confidante of many secrets and the recipient of many whispered confessions, raises profound questions about knowledge, experience, and empathy. It suggests that knowing about human emotions can be distinct from living them.
  • The closing reflections ponder the nature of love and death as inseparable and universal experiences that “all who lived had already died” in some sense, and that the greatest love might be ineffable and unexpressed. Mr. Spitzer’s thoughts on the fate of all lives being “increased by this great mystery of death” provide a somber, philosophical conclusion to the chapter.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “False modesty killed my love, she cried, and not I.” Whom does Hannah blame, and does she believe it?
  • “There was no man in all those mountains.” Why does Hannah call her love’s modesty false?
  • The snowdrops melt in Hannah’s hand, “from that moment she had begun to fade.” Why does Mr. Spitzer feel that his own magic did this?

Characters

  • Hannah calls her lost love “my lovely bride” and “White lady.” How does this change what is known of her?
  • Mr. Spitzer is “almost inclined to believe that she had never been a suffragette at all, that suffrage was her mask.” Is he right?
  • Mr. Spitzer comes “at that gratuitous moment after the end.” Why does his understanding always arrive late?

Connections

  • Young told Studs Terkel that Hannah came partly from an Alpine story of two bloomer girls who shed their skirts at a summit (Chapter 38). How does this chapter finish that story?
  • “Who knocks now at the ivory gates?” Recall the gates of false dreams in Chapter 41. Which of Hannah’s memories might pass through the ivory gate?
  • Mr. Spitzer hears “race-track music long after Peron’s last horse race.” How does each of the two ladies’ mourners keep a lost one alive?

Reflection

  • “Had not the greatest heroes been prompted by secret cowardice?” Can fear make a person brave?
  • “He had always thought that the burning moth was more heroic than the burning star.” Which is braver?
  • “Perhaps he knew most of love who never loved.” Can a person know what they have never lived?

Research: Characters

Cousin Hannah / The Great Suffrage Captain

  • A once-celebrated suffragette whose public life was marked by courage and activism.
  • In private, she was vulnerable, haunted by lost love, and self-doubt.
  • Her death is poetic and symbolic, involving imagery of skirts, snowdrops, mountains, and clouds.
  • Her lost love was a woman: “Ah, my lovely bride,” “White lady, where are you now?”
  • Mr. Spitzer comes to suspect “that she had never been a suffragette at all, that suffrage was her mask.”

Mr. Spitzer

  • An old lawyer entrusted with handling Cousin Hannah’s affairs after her death.
  • Reflective, cautious, and rational, yet deeply affected by the mysteries he uncovers.
  • He struggles to reconcile the public image of Cousin Hannah with the private truths he learns.
  • Acts as witness and interpreter of the suffragette’s life and legacy.

Research: Themes

Truth Versus Illusion

See: Illusion and Reality

  • The chapter opens with the idea that truth in life is rare but powerful, like granite and sunlight amidst illusions (moonbeams). Mr. Spitzer slowly pieces together the fragmented truth of Cousin Hannah’s life, which is elusive and fleeting.
  • The suffragette’s public persona versus her private reality is a central tension. Though once celebrated, her fame has faded, and her true self remains mysterious and partly hidden.

Love and Loss

See: Love · Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • Despite being a fierce fighter for women’s rights, Cousin Hannah is shown to be deeply haunted by a lost love, which shapes much of her life and final moments.
  • Her dying cries for her “love,” “little snowdrop,” and her search for a “skirt” symbolize a yearning for something lost, possibly innocence, identity, or a personal connection.
  • The narrative suggests that her suffrage activism was motivated by personal heartbreak, intertwining public struggle with private grief.

Heroism and Cowardice

See: Fear and Courage

  • The chapter challenges traditional notions of heroism, suggesting that great heroes may be driven by secret cowardice or inner failures.
  • Cousin Hannah is portrayed as both a heroic figure and a vulnerable woman who doubted herself at the end.
  • Mr. Spitzer debates the nature of courage, recognizing that even the smallest, seemingly weak acts can be heroic, such as a moth’s desire to reach a star.

Death and Memory

See: Death and Mortality · Memory and the Past

  • Death is portrayed as a transformative and mysterious force, an eternal transmutation where forms change but essence remains.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s reflections suggest that death does not erase a person but deepens the mystery of their existence.
  • The chapter explores how memory is fragile; great lives and deeds can be forgotten, and the true nature of a person might never be fully known.

Mystery and the Human Heart

See: Faith, God, and the Supernatural

  • The suffragette is depicted as a repository of secrets and confidences, receiving the hidden sorrows and loves of others even as her own heart remains obscure.
  • The novel questions whether anyone can truly understand love, life, or ambition, suggesting these remain inscrutable mysteries.
  • The idea that knowing without experiencing, knowing love without loving, or death without dying, might be a deeper form of understanding is proposed.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Skirts, Laces, Petticoats

See: Clothing, Hats, and Skirts

  • Symbolize femininity, freedom, and perhaps the burden of societal expectations. Cousin Hannah’s paradoxical final request for her skirt contrasts with her life’s advocacy for shedding such encumbrances.
  • Her lost love turned back on the mountain for her skirt: “I must find my skirt, my love. Farewell, farewell, my love.”

Snowdrops and Snow

See: Snow, Ice, and Winter · Roses and Flowers

  • Represent purity, fragility, and the coldness of death. The melting snowdrops in her hand symbolize fading life and lost love.

Mountains, Clouds, Icebergs

See: Snow, Ice, and Winter · Mountains · Clouds and Storms

  • Evoke the harshness and isolation of her inner world and struggles.
  • The iceberg imagery suggests overwhelming, unseen dangers and emotional coldness.

Music and Sound

See: Music and Instruments

  • Mr. Spitzer often parallels his thoughts to music, hearing the “roaring of the long-tongued surf” and “race-track music,” symbolizing the ongoing passage of time and memory.

Keys and Locks

See: Keys, Doors, and Locks

  • “Was there no key to open the human heart, no locksmith to cast the mold for this key, silver or gold, to open this enigmatic lock? Perhaps this mystery was the key.”

The Whirlwind and Darkness

See: Lamps, Candles, and Light · Clouds and Storms

  • Represent confusion, chaos, and the unknowability of the human heart.

Research: Resources

  • “There was granite, and there was sunlight”: Virginia Woolf set the biographer the same problem: “if we think of truth as something of granite-like solidity and of personality as something of rainbow-like intangibility,” biography must weld the two together (“The New Biography,” 1927). Mr. Spitzer is Hannah’s reluctant biographer.
  • “that great bloomer girl”: Lucy Stone and other suffragists adopted the Bloomer costume, which consisted of a knee-length skirt worn over trousers or pantaloons, in the early 1850s. Stone loved the comfort and practicality of the outfit and wore it both at home and in public for about three years. However, the Bloomer costume attracted significant public scrutiny and ridicule, and critics of the women’s rights movement often focused on the reformers’ attire rather than their arguments for suffrage and other reforms. By late 1853, many prominent suffragists, including Elizabeth Cady Stanton, abandoned the Bloomer costume due to the negative attention it generated. Stanton persuaded Stone to follow suit. Stone resumed wearing long skirts, although she never again wore the restrictive corsets and voluminous dresses of the fashionable style. Other notable suffragists like Susan B. Anthony also stopped wearing Bloomers around the same time. Amelia Bloomer, the editor of The Lily newspaper and namesake of the costume, continued to wear it until 1858. Only Elizabeth Smith Miller, who had helped popularize the style, continued to wear bloomers longer, until 1861 (Wikipedia). See Amelia Bloomer in the Chapter 42 resources. See Women’s Suffrage and Clothing, Hats, and Skirts.
  • “some old Triton’s horn”: See Triton’s shell in the Chapter 43 resources. See Greek Mythology.
  • “snowdrops delicate as a wraith of mist”: The snowdrop flowers through the snow at Candlemas and stands for hope, but an old English belief held that a snowdrop brought into the house foretold a death (Roy Vickery, Oxford Dictionary of Plant-Lore, 1995). See Roses and Flowers and Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “a musician whose baton had gotten loose from him”: Goethe’s “The Sorcerer’s Apprentice” (1797) starts a magic he cannot stop. Paul Dukas set it to music (1897), and in Disney’s Fantasia (1940) Mickey Mouse dreams that he conducts the stars and the waves. See Music and Composers.
  • “There was a false moon, and there was a false sun”: In icy mountain air, ice crystals make mock suns and mock moons beside the real ones. See the Chapter 24 resources. See Moon and Stars.
  • “There was not the eye of a sparrow”: “Are not two sparrows sold for a farthing? and one of them shall not fall on the ground without your Father” (Matthew 10:29). See The Bible.
  • “I must find my skirt”: This is the Alpine story Young told Studs Terkel, of two bloomer girls who shed their skirts at a summit, and the younger, going back for hers, was lost: “the lady lost her life for a skirt” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See the Chapter 38 resources. See Marguerite Young on Her Craft and Clothing, Hats, and Skirts.
  • “false as the heart of darkness and whirlwind”: There is a reference to “heart of darkness” again (Joseph Conrad’s Heart of Darkness, 1899, which examines the horrors of Western colonialism). The phrase encapsulates both the personal anguish of Cousin Hannah and the broader existential themes of decay, loss, and the search for meaning in the novel.
  • “For false was my true love’s heart”: The line echoes the false true love of folk songs like “A Brisk Young Sailor Courted Me,” having some similarities to Madge’s subplot. It signifies the painful recognition that her idealized love was fundamentally flawed or deceptive, contributing to her sorrow and the novel’s ongoing exploration of illusion versus reality in human connections.
  • “Did I not ask for you at every castle gate?”: Legend says the minstrel Blondel searched the castles of Germany for the imprisoned Richard the Lionheart, singing beneath each wall until the king answered from his tower.
  • “Who knocks now at the ivory gates?”: See the gates of horn and ivory, the gates of false dreams, in the Chapter 41 resources. See Homer’s Epics.

  • “Did I not drive a phaeton through a cloud?”: Phaëton drove the chariot of his father the sun and lost control of the horses, and Zeus struck him down (Ovid, Metamorphoses 2). See the Chapter 62 resources. See Greek Mythology.
  • “mountain-climbers with their St. Bernards”: See the dogs of the Great St. Bernard Hospice in the Chapter 31 resources.

  • “She was a man without a country”: In Edward Everett Hale’s “The Man Without a Country” (1863), Philip Nolan curses the United States and is sentenced to live at sea, never to hear his country named again. See American Writers.
  • “that poor mollusk who had no sea shell”: A mollusk’s mantle builds its shell and lines it with mother-of-pearl.
  • “eternal transmutation”: Transmutation is change and alchemy is a process of elevation. There is a subtle difference between the two and both are associated with the idea of turning lead into gold. They’re often used as a metaphor for spiritual or personal growth. With transformation it is inevitable because life is in a constant state of flux. People are in a perpetual process of becoming. Another way of pointing to Thales of Miletus and Heraclitus. This concept aligns with the novel’s broader themes of illusion, identity, and the blurred boundaries between life and death, reality and dream. It highlights the paradox of permanence in impermanence or the idea that while individual forms or lives may end or change, the process of transformation is ongoing and eternal. See Astrology, Alchemy, and Esoterica and Heraclitus and Flux.

  • “Who rung these changes?”: In change ringing, a band of bell-ringers rings a set of bells in every possible order without repeating. To ring the changes is to try every variation. See the bells in the Chapter 59 resources. See Music and Instruments.
  • “Had not the greatest heroes been prompted by secret cowardice”: In Conrad’s Lord Jim (1900), Jim jumps from the Patna, a ship he believes is sinking with its pilgrims, and spends the rest of his life trying to redeem that moment. See Fear and Courage.
  • “the burning moth was more heroic than the burning star”: See Shelley’s “The desire of the moth for the star” in the Chapter 35 resources. See Romantic Poets.
  • “all things being relative”: Albert Einstein was a German theoretical physicist who is best known for the theory of relativity. Relativity was a revolutionary idea that changed the understanding of the universe, though few could explain it. He said in a 1929 interview that, “The meaning of relativity has been widely misunderstood. Philosophers play with the word, like a child with a doll. Relativity, as I see it, merely denotes that certain physical and mechanical facts, which have been regarded as positive and permanent, are relative with regard to certain other facts in the sphere of physics and mechanics. It does not mean that we have the right to turn the world mischievously topsy-turvy” (“What Life Means to Einstein: An Interview by George Sylvester Viereck,” The Saturday Evening Post, October 26, 1929, 17).   • “the greatest captains and kings”: “The tumult and the shouting dies; / The Captains and the Kings depart” (Rudyard Kipling, “Recessional,” 1897).
  • “He preferred to acknowledge those who had always been ignored”: See Gray’s “Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard” in the Chapter 33 resources.
  • “bearding the sleeping lion in its den”: To beard the lion in its den means to attack someone on their own ground or subject. In the context of the novel, this expression highlights Mr. Spitzer’s role as a persistent figure who, despite his cautious and reserved nature, must face the daunting and often confusing realities surrounding the Cartwheel family and their complex, dreamlike world. It suggests his courageous, though sometimes reluctant, engagement with the mysteries and challenges he encounters, especially here the sleeping lion is Cousin Hannah, “this great anchorite of suffrage.” See The Bible.
    • David “caught him by his beard, and smote him, and slew him” (1 Samuel 17:35), and Scott’s “to beard the lion in his den” (Marmion, 1808). See the Chapter 31 resources.
  • “this great anchorite of suffrage upon the rock”: An anchorite withdrew from the world to live alone. Simeon Stylites lived for decades on top of a pillar in Syria.
  • “the shining toils of memory”: Toils are nets for catching game. Octavius says of the dead Cleopatra that “she looks like sleep, / As she would catch another Antony / In her strong toil of grace” (Antony and Cleopatra 5.2). See Shakespeare.
  • “there was no such thing as safety”: This sentence encapsulates the idea that safety, in the absolute sense, is an illusion for all humans, even for the seemingly fearless Cousin Hannah and the cautious Mr. Spitzer, as they face the inexorable forces of fate, death, and the unknown. It is similar to Helen Keller’s quote from The Open Door, “Security is mostly a superstition. It does not exist in nature, nor do the children of men as a whole experience it. Avoiding danger is no safer in the long run than outright exposure. Life is either a daring adventure, or nothing.” See Fear and Courage.
  • “polyandrous countries”: In Tibet, brothers have commonly shared one wife, a custom called fraternal polyandry.

  • “languages which could never be translated by the greatest translator”: Linear A, the script of Minoan Crete, has never been read. Michael Ventris deciphered Linear B in 1952, and Champollion read the hieroglyphs in 1822 (see the Chapter 2 resources).

  • “the long fingers of rosy light reaching through the clouds at dawn”: Homer’s dawn is “rosy-fingered,” rhododaktylos Eos, throughout the Iliad and the Odyssey. See Homer’s Epics.

  • “There was no open sesame”: See Ali Baba’s “open sesame” in the Chapter 52 resources.
  • “Judas cock”: The Judas cock may refer to the Book of the Cock/Rooster which was probably written in the 400s or 500s in Arabic or Greek and used by Ethiopian Christians who have and use the text during Holy Week activities. It describes the final three days of Jesus’s life, including a sequence where he reanimated a previously roasted rooster that He sent to spy on Judas Iscariot when he (Judas) was plotting to betray Jesus. The rooster informed Jesus and his disciples of the plan. Among other events, the Book of the Rooster describes Saul of Tarsus’ participation in the arrest, abuse and crucifixion of Jesus, the betrayal of Jesus by a female relative of Judas, a debate between Pilate and Herod about the culpability of Jesus, Mary’s grief, John the Evangelist witnessing the crucifixion of Jesus, the conversations Jesus had with the two thieves he was crucified alongside Gestas (Awsēmobyā) and Demas (Salikonilidākki) and references to other miracles that Jesus performed during his life. The rooster may be based on the Ziz of Jewish mythology, a giant bird. Like the Gospel of Judas from the second century, the Book of the Rooster attempts to explain the betrayal of Judas before the Last Supper, an act which is traditionally seen as abrupt or lacking reason (Wikipedia). See The Bible and Judaism.

  • “a great cone reversed, standing on its apex”: Dante’s Hell is a funnel, a great cone reversed, narrowing circle by circle to its point at the center of the earth (Inferno). See Dante.
  • “this old maid”: In Henry James’s The Bostonians (1886), the Boston suffragist Olive Chancellor loves the young speaker Verena Tarrant. Two women who made a home together came to be called a Boston marriage. See American Writers, Women’s Suffrage, and Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “suffrage was her mask”: See “this pale Don Juan beneath her mask” in the Chapter 43 resources.
  • “a great stone ear or chamber of echoes”: The Ear of Dionysius, a cave in the quarries of Syracuse, carries the smallest sound. Legend says the tyrant Dionysius listened there to his prisoners, and Caravaggio gave it the name in 1608.

Oblivion
The state of being forgotten, or of being unaware of everything.
Papyrus
Writing material the ancient Egyptians made from the stems of the papyrus reed.
Petticoat
An underskirt.
Phaeton
A light, open, four-wheeled carriage; also a name for some seashells.
Pigeon-hole
A small open compartment in a desk for papers.
Plaintive
Sad and mournful in sound.
Plumed
Decorated with feathers.
Polyandrous
Having more than one husband at a time.
Porphyry
A hard purple-red rock with embedded crystals, prized by Roman emperors.
Prism
A clear piece of glass with flat sides that splits light into the colors of the rainbow.
Quavering
Trembling, as a voice.
Ramification
A branching consequence.
Retrospect
A looking back on the past.
Scimitar
A curved Eastern sword.
Seminal
Seedlike; giving rise to later growth.
Sheik
An Arab chief or elder.
Skiff
A small, light boat for rowing or sailing. Young’s “skiff moon” is a crescent shaped like one.
Suffragette
A woman who campaigned for women’s right to vote. The word was coined by a London newspaper in 1906 for militant British activists. Most American activists called themselves suffragists. See Women’s Suffrage.
Tenacious
Holding fast.
Tenebrous
Dark, shadowy, obscure.
Transmutation
A change from one form or substance into another, especially the alchemists’ dream of turning base metal into gold.
Triton
A sea god of Greek myth, half man and half fish, who blows a conch shell.
Winding sheet
The cloth a corpse is wrapped in for burial.
Wraith
A ghost; a faint, pale shape.
