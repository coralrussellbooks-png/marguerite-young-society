Unit 20, pp. 545-576: Whistler and Dostoyevsky

← Unit 19 · Harp Song for a Radical: A History Companion · Unit 21 →

Background for this unit: The History in Harp Song for a Radical

In the book's strangest and most daring leap, Young moves from Debs's railroad magazine to St. Petersburg in the 1840s. An American engineer, Major George Washington Whistler, is building the czar's railroad with serf labor while a young writer, Fyodor Dostoyevsky, walks past the construction on his way to a socialist reading circle. Both roads end in Russia's iron order: Whistler dies there, and Dostoyevsky goes to Siberia. The episode prefigures Debs's own imprisonment.

Major entries

Major George Washington Whistler (pp. 546-554)

  • Who he was: A West Point graduate (1800-1849) who became one of America's first great railroad engineers. He worked on the Baltimore and Ohio and built locomotives at Lowell, Massachusetts.

  • In Russia: In 1842 Czar Nicholas I hired him to build the railway from St. Petersburg to Moscow, about four hundred miles, one of the great engineering works of the age. It was built largely by serfs under harsh conditions, and many died. Whistler died of cholera in St. Petersburg in April 1849, and the line opened in 1851.

  • The family: His wife, Anna McNeill Whistler, a devout North Carolinian (p. 547), is the woman in their son's painting Arrangement in Grey and Black No. 1 (1871), known as Whistler's Mother (p. 546).

  • How Young uses him: An American technician serving an autocrat, Whistler links the railroads Debs fired on to the czar's empire. Young sets his serf crews beside the American workers his railroads later employed.

  • Further reading: Albert Parry, Whistler's Father (1939).

James McNeill Whistler (pp. 546-550)

  • Who he was: The painter (1834-1903), born in Lowell and raised partly in St. Petersburg.

  • West Point and after: He entered West Point in 1851, when Colonel Robert E. Lee was superintendent (p. 548), and was dismissed in 1854 for failing chemistry. He later joked, "If silicon had been a gas, I would have been a major general." He worked briefly at the Winans locomotive works in Baltimore and then as a draftsman for the U.S. Coast Survey in Washington, where he doodled faces in the margins of his maps (p. 549). In 1855 he left for Paris and never lived in America again.

  • Further reading: Daniel E. Sutherland, Whistler: A Life for Art's Sake (2014).

Vissarion Belinsky (pp. 555-561)

  • Who he was: The most influential Russian critic of his generation (1811-1848), called "Vissarion the Furious" (p. 555). He championed realism and social justice in literature and moved from Hegelian philosophy to socialism.

  • Dostoyevsky and Gogol: Belinsky hailed Dostoyevsky's first novel, Poor Folk (1846), as a work of genius, then cooled toward him. In 1847 he wrote a furious open letter to Nikolai Gogol, author of Dead Souls (1842) and "The Overcoat" (1842), attacking Gogol's turn to religious conservatism. The letter circulated in manuscript and was banned.

  • Why it matters: Belinsky died of tuberculosis in 1848, just before the police would have arrested him. His letter to Gogol became the crime that sent Dostoyevsky to Siberia.

  • Further reading: Isaiah Berlin, "Vissarion Belinsky," in Russian Thinkers (1978).

Dostoyevsky and the Petrashevsky Circle (pp. 555-572)

  • The circle: From 1845, Mikhail Butashevich-Petrashevsky, a Foreign Ministry official, hosted Friday gatherings in St. Petersburg where young men discussed Fourier, socialism, and censored books. Young calls him a "chatterer and mountebank" (p. 561). Dostoyevsky, a former student of the Main Engineering School, which was housed in the Mikhailovsky Castle where Paul I had been murdered (p. 563, see Unit 2), read Belinsky's letter aloud there.

  • Arrest and mock execution: After the European revolutions of 1848, Nicholas I cracked down. Dostoyevsky and others were arrested on April 23, 1849, and held in the Peter and Paul Fortress (pp. 565-566). On December 22, 1849, they were led to Semyonovsky Square, dressed in execution shirts, and tied to posts before a firing squad. At the last moment a messenger read the czar's commutation, which had been planned all along.

  • Siberia: Dostoyevsky served four years at hard labor in the prison at Omsk, in western Siberia (p. 569), followed by army service. He drew on it in Notes from the House of the Dead (1860-1862), a book Debs admired (Unit 21).

  • Later work: His novel The Possessed (Demons, 1871-1872) was based on the 1869 murder of a student at the Moscow Agricultural Academy by a revolutionary cell (p. 555), a savage portrait of the radicals he had once joined.

  • Where the record differs: Young places Omsk in a province she calls "Armolensk" (p. 569). Omsk lay in western Siberia, in Tobolsk province. "Armolensk" may be a garbling of Akmolinsk, a region to the south.

  • Further reading: Joseph Frank, Dostoevsky: The Seeds of Revolt, 1821-1849 (1976) and The Years of Ordeal, 1850-1859 (1983).

Shorter entries

  • Czar Nicholas I (pp. 559, 574): Emperor of Russia from 1825 to 1855. He came to the throne crushing the Decembrist revolt and ruled by censorship and secret police.

  • The Decembrists (pp. 567, 570): Officers who rose in December 1825 for a constitution. Five were hanged and more than a hundred exiled to Siberia, where their wives famously followed them.

  • The Nevsky Prospect (pp. 554, 564): St. Petersburg's main avenue, the setting of Gogol's 1835 story of the same name.

  • The Slavophiles (p. 553): Russian thinkers who rejected Western models and exalted Orthodox Christianity and the peasant commune.

  • The Russian commune and American Indians (pp. 569-570): Young compares the Russian peasant commune, the Mormon cooperative, and Native American common ownership as forms of communism older than Marx.

  • Thomas Hobbes and Malthus (p. 545): Hobbes's Leviathan (1651) argued for absolute state power. Thomas Malthus's essay on population (1798) held that the poor would always outrun the food supply, an argument used against relief.

Allusions

  • "The Fall of the House of Usher" (pp. 558-559): Poe's 1839 tale of a decaying family and house, for czarist Russia.

  • Byron's "The Prisoner of Chillon" (p. 565): The 1816 poem of a man chained for years in a Swiss dungeon.

  • Poe's "The Raven" (p. 565): The 1845 poem, with its refrain "Nevermore."

  • Liszt and Ole Bull (p. 566): The piano virtuoso and the Norwegian violinist. Ole Bull toured America and founded a short-lived colony in Pennsylvania in 1852.

  • Cromwell and Charles Stuart (p. 575): The English revolution that executed a king.

  • Prospero (p. 556): Shakespeare's magician, for St. Petersburg dissolving in mist.
