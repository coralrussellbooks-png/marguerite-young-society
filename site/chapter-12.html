Chapter 12

Summary

It is past ten at night. Vera lies awake beside the Big Ben alarm clock, the one reliable clock in a house of clocks stopped one by one as Catherine withdrew to her bed. Wanting human company, she sets out for Miss MacIntosh’s bedroom. On the way she crosses the garden of the blind, planted by her grandfather for his blind wife to be touched and smelled rather than seen, and she feels a joy she will not know again: this first clear vision of the world will also be her last. She looks back on the child she was at seven, who bayed at the moon, heard the dead through a wireless in her head, and believed she was Chopin reborn, and on Miss MacIntosh, who brought her to her senses with a plain creed of daylight and duty. There were signs Vera did not read: Miss MacIntosh’s short breath, her blue lips, her threats to retire. The chapter closes on Catherine’s suspicion that Mr. Spitzer sometimes imagines himself to be his dead brother, betting on dark horses.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a meditation on time, describing the Big Ben alarm clock as “Old Reliable,” contrasted with other clocks in the house that have stopped at different hours, symbolizing a suspended or fragmented perception of time, linked with Catherine’s gradual withdrawal from the world. This sets the objective measurement of time (servants’ clocks) against subjective experience (stopped clocks), reflecting how personal grief or change distorts time.
  • Vera’s unplanned visit to Miss MacIntosh’s bedroom is described as a sporadic, almost dreamlike moment that is “blurred” in memory and “told only through a veil of oblivion,” emphasizing the elusive nature of memory and how absence reveals what is hidden. This passage sets the tone for the exploration of consciousness and the limits of human understanding.
  • A long, lyrical reflection on the human senses and the body’s design celebrates the unity and beauty of nature. The eye is described as the organ of sight placed perfectly in the skull, with hair acting as a protective and sensory element, and the skin as a “drum of all our senses.” This passage praises the human body as a masterpiece of nature and highlights the interconnectedness of all living things.
  • Vera finds herself in a garden created for a blind person, her grandmother. She insists the place “could surely in no way have influenced” her own clarity of vision, yet she wonders, “what is the influence of place?” The garden is full of sensory experience beyond sight: textures, smells, and forms without color. This metaphor illustrates the theme of perception beyond the visible, and the coexistence of joy and sorrow, as the garden’s sadness contrasts with Vera’s boundless happiness.
  • Vera contemplates how her earlier dreams and visions experienced as musical discourses with ghosts, reincarnations, and fantastical experiences contrast with the harsh reality taught by Miss MacIntosh. The nursemaid’s admonition to accept that “nothing had happened” and that mortals are not omniscient introduces a sobering perspective. This tension between dream and reality, imagination and practicality, is a central philosophical conflict in the chapter.
  • Miss MacIntosh represents a grounded, no-nonsense worldview where she emphasizes moral clarity, the distinction between what is real and unreal, and the rejection of dreaming. Her views on love are practical, seeing it as “greatest love which could see another’s face in daylight and hear another’s voice.” She embodies endurance, frugality, and a refusal to indulge in illusions, contrasting with Vera’s imaginative tendencies.
  • Near the end, Vera sets out Miss MacIntosh’s creed in the face of divine mystery: the world is a flawed creation, and God has “been filled with the greatest remorse” over its imperfections. Humans should attend to their own lives and not “meddle in His remote business,” highlighting themes of humility and acceptance of the unknowable.
  • The long passage on hair (“The most beautiful ladies of history had been noted for their hair”) and the warning that “the first vision would also be the last” both prepare for Chapter 17, when Vera finds Miss MacIntosh bald.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “Though time was an illusion, it was not the illusion of servants.” Who can afford to stop the clocks?
  • The details come “through a veil of oblivion which is itself a medium of memory.” How can forgetting be a way of remembering?
  • “Without eyelids, one would stare at the face of one’s love, even as one stares at death.” Why does Vera join love and death here?

Characters

  • The blind grandmother is “the most immediate of persons,” with no fancy at all. Why is the garden of the blind planted for her, of all people?
  • Miss MacIntosh “held my attention by yelling louder than the dead, louder than the waves.” What did she save Vera from, and what did it cost?
  • She “had not two faces.” Is that true of anyone in this novel?

Connections

  • Miss MacIntosh’s prophecy that Vera might be “a poor little shoemaker’s humble wife” (Chapter 9) now has her as a “wooden wife.” What has changed?
  • The thimble carved “Juliet, 1899” returns to the Romeo and Juliet dream of Chapter 6. Who was Juliet?
  • The twelve white swans recall Chapter 11’s “one wild swan more.” Why do bewitched brothers keep appearing?

Reflection

  • “What is the influence of place?” Are there places that hold sorrow?
  • Was Vera at seven, who preferred the dream to “the cause and effect sequences of the waking life,” wrong?
  • Is happiness more fragile when it cannot see what is coming?

Research: Characters

Vera Cartwheel

  • Reflective, imaginative, and sensitive.
  • Struggles between embracing imagination and dreams and confronting reality.
  • Experiences moments of joy and freedom but also faces the harsh truths of life and mortality.
  • At seven she bayed like a dog, dug for bones, heard the dead, and believed she was Chopin reborn, until Miss MacIntosh “restored me to my senses.”

Miss MacIntosh

  • The practical, no-nonsense caretaker who keeps the “Old Reliable” clock.
  • Represents pragmatism, moral clarity, and a grounded worldview.
  • Cautions against illusion and flight of fancy; emphasizes duty and reality.
  • Her perspective contrasts with Vera’s imaginative nature.
  • Her short breath, blue lips, and stone-grey skin are signs Vera does not yet read.
  • She threatens to retire, taps Vera with a silver thimble carved “Juliet, 1899,” and saves soap coupons “to exchange for crockery.”

Catherine Cartwheel

  • Catherine is mentioned as retiring slowly from the world, symbolized by the stopped clocks.
  • She suspects Mr. Spitzer sometimes imagines himself to be his dead brother.

Vera’s Grandfather and Grandmother

  • The grandfather who planted the garden for his blind wife represents the effort to create a world accessible through other senses.
  • The grandmother’s blindness and her practical nature are contrasted with Vera’s imaginative vision.
  • The grandmother argued with “her voluble black coachman” about the clouds, and touched the stone faces only once she was blind. See The Black Coachman.

Mr. Spitzer

  • A figure with an ambiguous nature and identity.
  • Catherine suspects he sometimes imagines himself to be his dead brother (Peron), blurring reality and fantasy.
  • Catherine once danced with “pigeon-toed, ambiguous Mr. Spitzer, perhaps with two Mr. Spitzers, one who had died, one who had lived.”

Research: Themes

The Illusion and Reality of Time

See: Illusion and Reality · Time

  • Vera reflects on time as both a practical necessity and an illusion.
  • The Big Ben alarm clock symbolizes reliable, practical time, set for servants who must rise at certain hours.
  • Other clocks in the house are stopped at different times, symbolizing frozen moments or hesitation in the past, especially linked to Catherine’s retreat from the world.

The Life of the Imagination versus Reality

See: Illusion and Reality · Dreams and Imagination

  • Vera contrasts a “life of the imagination” with the waking world, pondering the significance of dreams and whether they hold more truth than reality.
  • Dreams are described as limitless and bridging life and death, whereas waking life is governed by cause and effect.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s pragmatic attitude opposes Vera’s dreamy perspective, emphasizing practical reality and cautioning against illusions.

The Role and Limits of the Senses

See: Eyes and Blindness · The Search for Meaning

  • There is an extended meditation on the human senses (sight, hearing, taste, smell, and touch) as the means by which humans experience reality.
  • Vera highlights the design and function of eyes, eyelashes, eyelids, and hair as protective and sensory mechanisms.
  • The garden of the blind symbolizes a world without vision, emphasizing other ways of perceiving such as touch (Braille, texture), and questioning how place and perception influence sorrow and happiness.

Freedom, Innocence, and Joy

See: Guilt, Innocence, and Justice · Freedom and Escape

  • There is a moment of profound joy and freedom as Vera walks out into the night, celebrating the beauty and unity of nature.
  • This freedom is described as innocence and clarity of vision, untainted by past sorrow or fear.
  • Vera feels connected to the natural world, sensing stars, flowers, and animals as real and meaningful.

The Limits of Human Understanding and Divine Mystery

See: Faith, God, and the Supernatural · The Search for Meaning

  • Vera reflects on the limitations of human knowledge, emphasizing that humans are not omniscient and should not try to tamper with God’s mysteries.
  • God is portrayed as remorseful over creation’s imperfections, but humans are advised to focus on their own lives rather than cosmic mysteries.

Love and Human Relationships

See: Isolation and Connection · Love

  • Miss MacIntosh’s perspective on love is pragmatic and grounded; love is about seeing and hearing another in daylight and tested by harsh experience.
  • She is portrayed as a plain and practical woman, devoid of romantic illusions or flights of fancy.

Eyes and Eyelashes

Moonlight and Mist

Hair

Research: Resources

  • “As I had missed my birthday party”: The birthday party that never happened in Chapter 10, where “we had not even cut the birthday cake.” See the Chapter 10 resources.
  • “the Big Ben alarm clock”: The Big Ben was the Western Clock Company’s alarm clock, sold from 1909 and the first alarm clock advertised across the country (1910). It takes its name from the great bell in London. See Clocks and Watches and Time.
    • Big Ben is the nickname for the Great Bell of the Great Clock of Westminster, and, by extension, for the clock tower itself, which stands at the north end of the Palace of Westminster in London, England. The clock is a striking clock with five bells.
  • “for our rising up and for our lying down”: “When thou liest down, and when thou risest up” (Deuteronomy 6:7). See The Bible.
  • “no Tritons dreaming around the clock, no ladies weeping over dead birds, no sporting cupids”: French mantel clocks were crowded with gilded sea gods, nymphs, and cupids. Triton, son of Poseidon, blows a conch shell. See Clocks and Watches and Greek Mythology.
    • Jean-Baptiste Greuze’s painting Girl Weeping over Her Dead Bird (1765).
    • Catullus wrote of his beloved Lesbia’s dead sparrow: “Mourn, O Venuses and Cupids.”
  • “It did not repeat the hours”: Repeater clocks and watches chime the last hour again at the pull of a cord or the press of a button, so the hour can be heard in the dark. See Clocks and Watches.
  • “though time was an illusion, it was not the illusion of servants”: Einstein wrote in 1955 that the distinction between past, present, and future “is only a stubbornly persistent illusion.” Servants still had to rise on time. See Time.
  • “each clock having been stopped at a different hour”: In Dickens’s Great Expectations (1861), Miss Havisham stops every clock at twenty minutes to nine, the hour she was jilted, and never leaves her room. Mourners once stopped the clocks at a death. See Clocks and Watches, Memory and the Past, and the Chapter 4 resources.
  • “Drink Coca-Cola”: Cardboard advertising fans printed with “Drink Coca-Cola” were handed out in churches and stores, especially in the South. See the Chapter 1 resources.
  • “that we are not as shadows”: “What shadows we are, and what shadows we pursue” (Edmund Burke, 1780), and “our days upon earth are a shadow” (Job 8:9). See Shadows and Plato and Platonism.
    • Prospero: “the great globe itself… shall dissolve, / And, like this insubstantial pageant faded, / Leave not a rack behind. We are such stuff / As dreams are made on” (The Tempest 4.1).
    • The theme of shadows runs through the novel. It brings to mind Plato’s work, The Allegory of the Cave, or Plato’s Cave. It is an allegory presented by the Greek philosopher Plato in his work, Republic. The Cave describes a group of people who have lived chained to the wall of a cave all their lives, facing a blank wall. The people watch shadows projected on the wall from objects passing in front of a fire behind them and give names to these shadows. The shadows are the prisoners’ reality, but are not accurate representations of the real world. The shadows represent the fragment of reality that can normally be perceived through the senses, while the objects under the sun represent the true forms of objects that can only be perceived through reason. The philosopher is like a prisoner who is freed from the cave and comes to understand that the shadows on the wall are actually not the direct source of the images seen. A philosopher aims to understand and perceive the higher levels of reality. However, the other inmates of the cave do not even desire to leave their prison, for they know no better life. See the Chapter 3 resources.
  • “The eyeball was made to see with”: William Paley’s Natural Theology (1802) made the eye his proof of design: “there is precisely the same proof that the eye was made for vision, as there is that the telescope was made for assisting it.” The book opens with a watch found on a heath. See Eyes and Blindness.
    • Darwin admitted that the evolution of the eye “seems, I freely confess, absurd in the highest possible degree,” then showed how it could arise by small steps (On the Origin of Species, 1859). See the Chapter 3 resources.
  • “the spot on a peacock’s tail”: Juno set the hundred eyes of the slain watchman Argus in the peacock’s tail (Ovid, Metamorphoses 1). See Greek Mythology, Birds, and the Chapter 5 resources.
  • “the hole through the needle”: “It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle, than for a rich man to enter into the kingdom of God” (Matthew 19:24). See the Chapter 3 resources.
  • “the brain was but the infoldings of the skin”: In the embryo, brain and skin both grow from the same outer layer, the ectoderm, which folds inward to form the neural tube.
  • “we were the masterpieces of nature”: “What a piece of work is a man! … the beauty of the world, the paragon of animals!” (Hamlet 2.2). See Shakespeare.
  • “the hair raising to warn us of our mortal danger”: “Then a spirit passed before my face; the hair of my flesh stood up” (Job 4:15). See Hair, Wig, and Baldness and The Bible.
  • “The most beautiful ladies of history had been noted for their hair”: Queen Berenice gave her hair to the gods for her husband’s safe return, and it was set among the stars as Coma Berenices. See Hair, Wig, and Baldness, Miss MacIntosh, and the Chapter 9 resources.
  • “Absalom was caught among the apple boughs and hanged by his hair”: In 2 Samuel 18:9, Absalom’s “head caught hold of the oak.” Vera makes it an apple tree. His heavy hair was cut once a year (2 Samuel 14:26). See Hair, Wig, and Baldness, The Bible, and the Chapter 5 resources.
  • “walks in the eye of the wind”: A sailor’s phrase for heading straight into the wind.
  • “dead men are usually found with their eyes open”: “Joseph shall put his hand upon thine eyes” (Genesis 46:4). The living close the eyes of the dead and once weighted them with coins. See Death and Mortality and Eyes and Blindness.
  • “Leonardo had forgotten to give eyebrows to his Mona Lisa”: The Mona Lisa (c. 1503-1519) is a half-length portrait by Leonardo da Vinci. The painting shows no eyebrows or lashes, though Giorgio Vasari (1550) praised its eyebrows, “the manner in which the hairs spring from the flesh.” See Eyes and Blindness and Hair, Wig, and Baldness.
  • “the garden of the blind”: Fragrance gardens for the blind were planted to be touched and smelled. The Brooklyn Botanic Garden opened one in 1955. Vera and Miss MacIntosh walk through this garden in Chapter 3. See Eyes and Blindness, Roses and Flowers, and the Chapter 3 resources.
  • “what is the influence of place?”: The Romans spoke of the genius loci, the spirit of a place. John Ruskin named the “pathetic fallacy” (1856), the habit of lending human feeling to nature.
    • “making the stones cry out”: “I tell you that, if these should hold their peace, the stones would immediately cry out” (Luke 19:40).
  • “the stumbling stone to the blind”: “Thou shalt not curse the deaf, nor put a stumblingblock before the blind” (Leviticus 19:14). See Eyes and Blindness and The Bible.
  • “the Braille texture of flowers”: Louis Braille, blinded as a child, published his raised-dot alphabet in 1829. See Eyes and Blindness.
  • “darkness moved on the face of the deep”: “And darkness was upon the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters” (Genesis 1:2). See Genesis.
  • “sweet william, cosmos, rose geranium, phlox, nasturtium, nicotiana”: Scented garden flowers. Rose geranium gives off its scent at a touch, and nicotiana, flowering tobacco, opens and perfumes the air at night. See Roses and Flowers.
  • “flowers of the old Jerusalem, flowers of the new Jerusalem”: “I am the rose of Sharon, and the lily of the valleys” (Song of Solomon 2:1), and the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down “as a bride adorned for her husband” (Revelation 21:2). See Roses and Flowers and The Bible.
  • “the first vision would also be the last”: Foreshadowing of Chapter 17, when Vera finds Miss MacIntosh bald. See Miss MacIntosh.
  • “the beauty of that horror more beautiful than life”: “For beauty is nothing but the beginning of terror” (Rilke, Duino Elegies, 1923).
  • “Mr. Chandelier or Mr. Chanticleer”: Chanticleer is the proud rooster of Chaucer’s “The Nun’s Priest’s Tale” and the fables of Reynard the Fox. Mr. Chandelier is Catherine’s companion from Chapter 2. See Mr. Chandelier, Birds, and the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “a place for everything”: “A place for everything, and everything in its place,” the old household rule. See Order, Chaos, and Discipline.
  • “her lips turned blue and her skin lost its natural color, turning to saffron”: Blue lips and short breath are signs of a failing heart or lungs, a warning Vera does not read. See Miss MacIntosh.
  • “saving even the soap coupons to exchange for crockery”: Companies such as the Larkin Soap Company gave coupons that buyers traded for dishes and furniture.
  • “a poor little shoemaker’s wooden wife”: Miss MacIntosh’s prophecy in Chapter 9 was “a poor little shoemaker’s humble wife.” See the Chapter 9 resources.
  • “Juliet, 1899”: Maude Adams played Juliet at the Empire Theatre in New York in May 1899, opposite William Faversham. A dream in Chapter 6 has “She was Juliet, and I was Romeo.” See Shakespeare and the Chapter 6 resources.
  • “baying like a dog at the moon”: “I had rather be a dog, and bay the moon, / Than such a Roman” (Julius Caesar 4.3). See Shakespeare and Moon and Stars.
  • “a rooster crowing when there was no rooster”: “Before the cock crow, thou shalt deny me thrice” (Matthew 26:34). See The Bible and Birds.
  • “a wireless was attached to my head so that I might hear the voices of the dead”: In 1920 Thomas Edison told interviewers he was working on a machine to speak with the dead. See Spiritualism and the Occult.
  • “the reincarnation of Chopin”: Chopin wrote some sixty mazurkas, the Polish dance. The polka is Czech, and he wrote none. See Music and Composers and the Chapter 10 resources.
  • “perhaps with two Mr. Spitzers, one who had died, one who had lived”: Joachim and Peron Spitzer, the twins. See Peron Spitzer and Twins and Doubles.
  • “twelve white swans like brothers or suitors bewitched”: Andersen’s eleven brothers turned into swans, and the bewitched swan maidens of Tchaikovsky’s Swan Lake (1877). See Fairy Tales and Folklore, Birds, and the Chapter 11 resources.
  • “should stir no feather in the air”: Lear holds a feather to Cordelia’s lips: “This feather stirs; she lives!” (King Lear 5.3). See Shakespeare, Death and Mortality, and the Chapter 5 resources.
  • “to be nothing but an old humbug”: “Death, thou shalt die” (John Donne, Holy Sonnet 10, “Death, be not proud”). See Death and Mortality.
  • “headless horsemen ride about in purple fog”: Washington Irving’s “The Legend of Sleepy Hollow” (1820). See Fairy Tales and Folklore and the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “filled with the greatest remorse”: “And it repented the Lord that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him at his heart” (Genesis 6:6). See Genesis and Faith, God, and the Supernatural.
  • “attend to our own knitting”: “Mind your own knitting,” the old way of saying mind your own business. “Study to be quiet, and to do your own business” (1 Thessalonians 4:11). See The Bible.
  • “The wisest man was a fool in God’s eyes, and there was a fool bound in the heart of every child”: 1 Corinthians 3:19 reads, “For the wisdom of this world is foolishness with God,” and Proverbs 22:15, “Foolishness is bound in the heart of a child; but the rod of correction shall drive it far from him.” The phrase captures the paradox of human existence: the striving for knowledge and meaning amid inherent limitations, and the acceptance that all are bound to folly in the grander cosmic scheme. See The Bible.
  • “We could not command the waters to stand still”: The waters of the Jordan “stood and rose up upon an heap” for Joshua (Joshua 3:16), and he commanded, “Sun, stand thou still” (Joshua 10:12). See The Bible.
  • “They were already moving”: Galileo is said to have muttered “And yet it moves” of the earth after he was forced to recant (1633).
  • “rather to endure ourselves than to test God”: “Ye shall not tempt the Lord your God” (Deuteronomy 6:16). See The Bible.
  • “the honest man was he who walked upright”: “He that walketh uprightly, and worketh righteousness, and speaketh the truth in his heart” (Psalm 15:2). See The Bible.
    • Diogenes is said to have walked Athens by day with a lamp, looking for an honest man.
  • “Love was undoubtedly greatest love which could see another’s face in daylight”: “For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face” (1 Corinthians 13:12). See the Chapter 10 resources on “One who could know us and yet love us.”
  • “her face was not, it was simply not her fortune”: The nursery rhyme’s milkmaid: “My face is my fortune, sir, she said.” See Fairy Tales and Folklore and the Chapter 10 resources.
  • “perhaps it was we who passed through time”: Austin Dobson, “The Paradox of Time” (1877): “Time goes, you say? Ah no! / Alas, Time stays, we go.” See Time.
  • “She could not soar upward on wings of words to heaven”: Claudius: “My words fly up, my thoughts remain below: / Words without thoughts never to heaven go” (Hamlet 3.3). See Shakespeare.
  • “she was a middle brow”: The word was coined in Punch in 1925, between highbrow and lowbrow. See the Chapter 3 resources.
  • “She had not two faces”: Janus, the Roman god of doorways and beginnings, looks two ways at once. See Twins and Doubles.
  • “put his bet on the dark horse”: The phrase comes from Disraeli’s The Young Duke (1831): “a dark horse, which had never been thought of.” See Horses and Carriages and Peron Spitzer.
  • “those of the tramontane regions”: Tramontane, from the Latin transmontanus, means “beyond the mountains,” here the lands beyond the Alps. It is also the name of a cold north wind.
