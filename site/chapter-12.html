Summary

It is after ten at night, and Vera lies beside the Big Ben alarm clock, the one reliable clock in a house of stopped clocks. Restless, she slips out into a garden her grandfather planted for his blind wife, made to be touched and smelled rather than seen. She moves through it with closed eyes in near ecstasy and meditates on Miss MacIntosh and all she taught. The chapter closes on Catherine's suspicion that Mr. Spitzer sometimes imagines himself to be his dead brother, betting on phantom horses he could know nothing about.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a meditation on time, describing the Big Ben alarm clock as "Old Reliable," contrasted with other clocks in the house that have stopped at different hours, symbolizing a suspended or fragmented perception of time, linked with Catherine's gradual withdrawal from the world. This symbolizes the tension between the objective measurement of time (servants’ clocks) and subjective experience (stopped clocks), reflecting how personal grief or change distorts time.
  • Vera’s unplanned visit to Miss MacIntosh’s bedroom is described as a sporadic, almost dreamlike moment that is “blurred” in memory and “told only through a veil of oblivion,” emphasizing the elusive nature of memory and how absence reveals what is hidden. This passage sets the tone for the exploration of consciousness and the limits of human understanding.
  • There is a lengthy and lyrical reflection on the human senses and the body’s design underscores the unity and beauty of nature. The eye is described as the organ of sight placed perfectly in the skull, with hair acting as a protective and sensory element, and the skin as a “drum of all our senses.” This passage celebrates the human body as a masterpiece of nature and highlights the interconnectedness of all living things.
  • Vera finds herself in a garden created for a blind person, her grandmother, and reflects on how this place, though designed for the absence of sight, paradoxically influences her own vision. The garden is full of sensory experience beyond sight: textures, smells, and forms without color. This metaphor illustrates the theme of perception beyond the visible, and the coexistence of joy and sorrow, as the garden’s sadness contrasts with Vera’s boundless happiness.
  • Vera contemplates how her earlier dreams and visions experienced as musical discourses with ghosts, reincarnations, and fantastical experiences contrasts with the harsh reality taught by Miss MacIntosh. The nursemaid’s admonition to accept that "nothing had happened" and that mortals are not omniscient introduces a sobering perspective. This tension between dream and reality, imagination and practicality, is a central philosophical conflict in the chapter.
  • Miss MacIntosh represents a grounded, no-nonsense worldview where she emphasizes moral clarity, the distinction between what is real and unreal, and the rejection of dreaming. Her views on love are practical, seeing it as “greatest love which could see another’s face in daylight and hear another’s voice.” She embodies endurance, frugality, and a refusal to indulge in illusions, contrasting with Vera’s imaginative tendencies.
  • The chapter ends with reflections on human limitations in the face of divine mystery. The world is described as a flawed creation, with God having “been filled with the greatest remorse” over the imperfections. Humans are advised to focus on their own lives and not “meddle in His remote business,” highlighting themes of humility and acceptance of the unknowable.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Vera Cartwheel

  • Reflective, imaginative, and sensitive.
  • Struggles between embracing imagination/dreams and confronting reality.
  • Experiences moments of joy and freedom but also faces the harsh truths of life and mortality.

Miss MacIntosh

  • The practical, no-nonsense caretaker who keeps the "Old Reliable" clock.
  • Represents pragmatism, moral clarity, and a grounded worldview.
  • Cautions against illusion and flight of fancy; emphasizes duty and reality.
  • Her perspective contrasts with Vera’s imaginative nature.

Catherine Cartwheel

  • Catherine is mentioned as retiring slowly from the world, symbolized by the stopped clocks.

Vera’s grandfather and grandmother

  • The grandfather who planted the garden for his blind wife represents the effort to create a world accessible through other senses.
  • The grandmother’s blindness and her practical nature are contrasted with Vera’s imaginative vision.

Mr. Spitzer (Joachim)

  • A figure with an ambiguous nature and identity.
  • Catherine suspects he sometimes imagines himself to be his dead brother (Peron), blurring reality and fantasy.

Research: Themes

The Illusion and Reality of Time

See: Illusion and Reality · Time

  • Vera reflects on time as both a practical necessity and an illusion.
  • The Big Ben alarm clock symbolizes reliable, practical time, set for servants who must rise at certain hours.
  • Other clocks in the house are stopped at different times, symbolizing frozen moments or hesitation in the past, especially linked to Catherine’s retreat from the world.

The Life of the Imagination vs. Reality

See: Illusion and Reality · Dreams and Imagination

  • Vera contrasts a "life of the imagination" with the waking world, pondering the significance of dreams and whether they hold more truth than reality.
  • Dreams are described as limitless and bridging life and death, whereas waking life is governed by cause and effect.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s pragmatic attitude opposes Vera’s dreamy perspective, emphasizing practical reality and cautioning against illusions.

The Role and Limits of the Senses

See: The Search for Meaning

  • There is an extended meditation on the human senses (sight, hearing, taste, smell, and touch) as the means by which humans experience reality.
  • Vera highlights the design and function of eyes, eyelashes, eyelids, and hair as protective and sensory mechanisms.
  • The garden of the blind symbolizes a world without vision, emphasizing other ways of perceiving such as touch (Braille, texture), and questioning how place and perception influence sorrow and happiness.

Freedom, Innocence, and Joy

See: Guilt, Innocence, and Justice · Freedom and Escape

  • There is a moment of profound joy and freedom as Vera walks out into the night, celebrating the beauty and unity of nature.
  • This freedom is described as innocence and clarity of vision, untainted by past sorrow or fear.
  • Vera feels connected to the natural world, sensing stars, flowers, and animals as real and meaningful.

The Limits of Human Understanding and Divine Mystery

See: Faith, God, and the Supernatural · The Search for Meaning

  • Vera reflects on the limitations of human knowledge, emphasizing that humans are not omniscient and should not try to tamper with God's mysteries.
  • God is portrayed as remorseful over creation’s imperfections, but humans are advised to focus on their own lives rather than cosmic mysteries.

Love and Human Relationships

See: Isolation and Connection · Love

  • Miss MacIntosh’s perspective on love is pragmatic and grounded; love is about seeing and hearing another in daylight and tested by harsh experience.
  • She is portrayed as a plain and practical woman, devoid of romantic illusions or flights of fancy.

Research: Resources

  • “The wisest man was a fool in God’s eyes, and there was a fool bound in the heart of every child.” - Proverbs 22:15 The phrase captures the paradox of human existence: our striving for knowledge and meaning amid inherent limitations and the acceptance that all are bound to folly in the grander cosmic scheme
  • Big Ben is the nickname for the Great Bell of the Great Clock of Westminster, and, by extension, for the clock tower itself, which stands at the north end of the Palace of Westminster in London, England. Originally named the Clock Tower, it was renamed Elizabeth Tower in 2012 to mark the Diamond Jubilee of Queen Elizabeth II. The clock is a striking clock with five bells.
  • The theme of shadows runs through the novel. It brings to mind Plato’s work, The Allegory of the Cave, or Plato's Cave. It is an allegory presented by the Greek philosopher Plato in his work, Republic. The Cave, describes a group of people who have lived chained to the wall of a cave all their lives, facing a blank wall. The people watch shadows projected on the wall from objects passing in front of a fire behind them and give names to these shadows. The shadows are the prisoners' reality, but are not accurate representations of the real world. The shadows represent the fragment of reality that we can normally perceive through our senses, while the objects under the sun represent the true forms of objects that we can only perceive through reason. The philosopher is like a prisoner who is freed from the cave and comes to understand that the shadows on the wall are actually not the direct source of the images seen. A philosopher aims to understand and perceive the higher levels of reality. However, the other inmates of the cave do not even desire to leave their prison, for they know no better life.

  • Tramontane is a classical name for a northern wind.
