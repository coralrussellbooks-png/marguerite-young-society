Chapter 72

On Chapter 72 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Esther Longtree · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Pillars: Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary · Unfinished Is Honest · The Margins Reveal the Truth · The Sacred Is Here

The Questions

Is Esther’s endless pregnancy an affliction to be pitied, or the book’s clearest image of a construction kept honestly open?

Are the stillborn children a failure to make something whole, or the truthful shape of a thing that refuses to close?

Why does the novel set its deepest wisdom at the dead end of a small town, in a waitress the settled world calls ugly and dumb?

What does Esther see from the edge that the center cannot?

By now Vera has watched Catherine seal her invention and Miss MacIntosh freeze her blueprint and Cousin Hannah wall herself in her cause, and has seen all three fail for the same reason: they finished. Esther Longtree is their counter-figure, the construction that will not close, and the book has been moving toward her.

Her everlasting pregnancy is Unfinished Is Honest made flesh. Forever expectant and never delivered, she is the unfinished held as honesty rather than confessed as failure. Her stillborn children are not proof that she has failed to make something whole. They are the novel’s insistence that the incomplete is the truthful shape, and that a thing kept open is worth more than a thing sealed. Where the sealed dream and the sealed creed and the sealed cause all failed because they were finished, Esther holds because she does not finish. This is Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary carried to its furthest point in the book.

She sees from The Margins Reveal the Truth. A waitress judged ugly and mulish, at “the dead-end of creation, being that being from which no mortal issue comes,” she stands where the seams show, and from the margin she sees what the settled center cannot. “The weight of memory and snow curved her spinal column”; she carries the sedimented past in her body and still sees clearly, because the edge is the clear-seeing place. And she carries The Sacred Is Here too, mothering everything within reach, rooted in the greasy-spoon world and the here-and-now body, finding the sacred underfoot and never in a promised beyond. She does not wait for a better place. She tends this one.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that treats Esther’s pregnancy as a pathology to cure or a tragedy to pity, and the reading that counts the stillborns as her failure. Most of all it refuses the pull toward closure, the wish that she would finally deliver, resolve, complete. It refuses, too, any settling of her into mere “compassionate acceptance,” which is a gentler and more consoling thing than what the chapter is doing.

The footing it offers is that Esther is Unfinished Is Honest standing upright. The unfinished is not the failure of the work. It is the honest shape of it. From the edge she sees the seams; in the ordinary she finds the sacred; and in staying pregnant, undelivered, she keeps the one construction the book most trusts, the one that will not close. She remains pregnant at the chapter’s end, and that is not the problem to be fixed. It is the answer, held open.

For Reflection

What in one’s own life is being held open that the world keeps calling unfinished business?

Is there wisdom the settled center has missed that only the edge can see?

What would change if the incomplete were treated not as failure but as the truthful shape of a thing still alive?
