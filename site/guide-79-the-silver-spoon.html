Chapter 78 gave us the accusation: Esther killed Rosemary in the womb, and was called Little Jackass, and grew up in the shadow of the perfect child who died. Chapter 79 goes further back still, to before Esther’s conception, and explains the mechanism by which she came to exist at all.

Her parents never consummated their marriage. Seven years passed. And then, over the course of those seven years, a large dark woman came regularly to their house (at night, sometimes knocking, sometimes whistling) and carried a tiny silver spoon between them. This is how Esther was conceived. Across a great arid distance. Through an intermediary. With an instrument.

The chapter also contains the most mythically charged episode of Esther’s childhood: on her seventh birthday, her mother takes her to visit the other woman. They make a difficult journey. They find the go-between dying, blind, nearly unrecognizable. And Esther, looking at this woman who smells of manure and old tulip bulbs, who wears men’s clothes and casts “a man’s shadow on the wall,” who slapped and pinched Esther’s mother to arouse life where there was none: Esther sees her as the father she never had.

What Actually Happens

Esther reconstructs her parents’ marriage as a structure of sustained non-connection: two people who “dreamed of heaven as something far away” and never reached each other. Her father, the large passive policeman, reading old baseball scores. Her mother, the tiny fragile pallid woman, unable to conceive without intervention. Between them: nothing that could produce a child. The other woman arrived. She was large and dark and somewhat grotesque, wearing a man’s hat and coat, her shadow falling “like a man’s shadow on the wall.” She smelled of manure and old tulip bulbs rotted by rain. She had a slow anger and a patience that spanned years. She carried a tiny silver spoon. She came at night, set an alarm clock, performed the rituals of conception across the arid distance between Esther’s parents. She slapped and pinched the mother to wake her body into possibility. She did this again and again, over seven years, until it worked.

Esther was born kicking and afraid, requiring instruments, producing also the dead twin Rosemary. The other woman was there for the birth too, or thereabouts. She is present at Esther’s origin in the way that a force is present, not warmly, not lovingly, but essentially.

The uncle, a pugilist who died of cancer and left his estate to a spiritualist medium, told Esther about all this “laughing until his face turned purple.” He confirmed the nickname: Little Jackass, given by the father himself, because of the unnaturalness of her conception. “The child of the unnatural mating.” Mulish from the start. On her seventh birthday, Esther’s mother takes her on a difficult journey to visit the other woman. The chapter presents this as a pilgrimage of sorts, marked by hardship: clothing lost, the natural and social world harsh against them. When they arrive, the other woman is dying. She is blind. She is monstrous in her decay. And Esther, standing before her, perceives her as the father she never had: the one who made her possible, the one whose will and method produced her life out of nothing. The go-between is cruel and strange and more wonderful and more beautiful, in some inner invisible way, than either of Esther’s actual parents.

The chapter closes with Esther reflecting on her gradual death, not a physical death but the slow fading of someone born without the foundation of natural love, who has spent her life trying to be good in a world that finds her awkward and wrong, who stands at the fence between life and eternity with arms akimbo and legs astraddle, belonging fully to neither side. She is the happy dead. She is cloud-like. She is driven to the edge of existence. She has a big leaking heart full of regrets and expectations.

She also, in this chapter, says she is Jesus Christ. She says this in the context of her endless pregnancy, her suffering, her bearing of what the world will not acknowledge. She does not elaborate. She has made her identification and moved on.

The Other Woman

She is one of the most extraordinary figures Young has created, and she arrives without a name. Large and dark and misshapen, wearing men’s clothes, casting a man’s shadow, smelling of earth and rot and rain. She is foreign: speaks with a strange accent, possibly carries what the narrative hints is an old crime or exile. She has flat dark hands capable of warmth but used mainly for slapping and pinching. She is methodical and patient in a way that has nothing to do with tenderness.

What she does is not insemination in any medical sense; it is stranger than that, mediated by a silver spoon, performed across the emotional and physical distance of a marriage that was never consummated. Young is not describing assisted reproduction as we would understand it. She is describing something that sits in the space between the literal and the mythological: a ritual of conception, performed by an intermediary, for parents who could not reach each other. The silver spoon is the chapter’s central symbol, and Young loads it with multiple meanings. The idiom “born with a silver spoon in one’s mouth” means to be born privileged, to inherit rather than earn. Esther is born with the silver spoon as her instrument of creation, not privilege but the cold mechanism of her existence. She is the child who was wanted enough that someone paid a strange woman to come in the night with a spoon, but not wanted in any way that translated into warmth or love or the ordinary comforts of being a child who is simply loved.

The other woman is more wonderful and more beautiful than Esther’s actual parents. This is Young’s most difficult observation about Esther’s origin: the figure who made her possible, who had the will and patience and physical power to produce life where there was none, is more alive in Esther’s imagination than either of the people who were supposed to love her. She is the dark father. She is the one who did the work.

The Seventh Birthday Visit

The pilgrimage Esther’s mother takes her on for her seventh birthday, to visit the woman who produced her life, is one of the novel’s most unsettling scenes, and one of its most mythologically resonant. Seven is the number of completion, of the sacred week, of fairy-tale transformation: the seventh year, the seventh son of a seventh son, the age at which, in various traditions, a child is held to reach the age of reason. On Esther’s seventh birthday, she is brought to see the origin of her own existence, and what she finds is dying. The other woman is blind and monstrous in her decay, barely recognizable as the powerful figure who came at night with her alarm clock and her slow anger. She is disintegrating. And Esther, at seven, looks at this disintegrating figure and sees her as a father. This is a precise and devastating reversal. The large passive policeman who gave Esther her nickname and read baseball scores and never came close to his wife is not Esther’s father in any sense that matters to her. The dying woman who smells of manure and wears men’s clothes and slapped her mother’s body awake; she is the father. She is the one who insisted on Esther’s existence. She is the will behind the conception. She is already leaving.

After this visit, something changes in Esther: every day will seem like that birthday. She is marked by the encounter in a way she cannot fully articulate: the knowledge that the person most responsible for her being in the world is dying, that she met her origin just as her origin was ending, that the father she recognized is a woman who is almost already gone.

Esther as Jesus Christ

She says it plainly, without elaboration: she is Jesus Christ. The identification is not blasphemy in Young’s framing; it is structural. Esther has been carrying what the world will not acknowledge: children the law has decided did not exist, suffering that has no official record, a pregnancy that has lasted beyond all reasonable duration. She has been accused of crimes she did not commit. She has been misunderstood and rejected by those around her. She is the bearer of suffering for others: the dying fathers’ illusions, the detective’s investigation, her mother’s grief for Rosemary.

Young has also told us that Esther is the mother of us all, and Mr. Spitzer is the father of us all. If Esther is both the mother of us all and Jesus Christ, she occupies simultaneously the role of origin and the role of sacrificial bearer of the world’s accumulated sorrow. She is Everywoman and the Redeemer simultaneously. Young is building a mythological structure around Esther that the novel never quite makes explicit, because to make it explicit would be to reduce it. The immaculate conception enters here too: To All My Darlings connects Esther’s unnatural origin (her parents who never consummated their marriage, the silver spoon as instrument, the third person as go-between) to the tradition of miraculous or unmixed conceptions. Saint Anne and Joachim, the parents of Mary, were similarly infertile and similarly received their child through divine intervention. Mary herself was conceived without the taint of original sin. The tradition of the child born out of the ordinary mechanisms of human reproduction (born through some other means, marked from conception for something the world will not understand) is the tradition Esther enters when she says she is Jesus Christ.

Gradually Dying

The chapter’s closing meditation on Esther’s gradual death is one of the most purely beautiful passages in her sequence. She is the happy dead. She stands at the fence between life and eternity, arms akimbo, legs astraddle, belonging to neither. She is cloud-like, illusive, driven to the edge of existence. Her heart is big and leaking. This is not suicidal ideation or despair in the clinical sense. It is the honest description of someone who was born without the foundation of natural love, who has spent her life in the space between what was supposed to be and what is. She is alive in every physiological sense (enormous, muscular, wind-burned, pregnant with the little shadow boxer) and she is also dying in the sense that every day subtracts from her something she cannot replace, because what was taken from her was taken before she existed and cannot be given back.

She tries to be good despite it. She has a maternal heart. She wants to love and be loved. She has the starlight and the wind and the water that her father’s lineage gave her. She is wilder than people think. She is Little Jackass. She was born kicking and she has not stopped.

What to Hold Onto

The other woman: large, dark, men’s clothes, men’s shadow, slow anger, alarm clock, silver spoon, seven years of night visits, slapping and pinching Esther’s mother awake. More wonderful and more beautiful, in some inner invisible way, than either of Esther’s parents.

The silver spoon: not privilege but mechanism. Cold instrument of a conception that happened across a great arid distance.

The seventh birthday pilgrimage to find the origin dying. The father Esther recognized in the dying woman. After this, every day will seem like that birthday. Esther saying she is Jesus Christ. Not elaborating.

The gradual death: not a dramatic end but a slow subtraction, the happy dead standing arms akimbo at the fence between life and eternity. Big heart, leaking. Still there.

And the little shadow boxer, still under her heart. Still two hearts. The child produced by the unnatural mating is still expecting the child who will be born.
