To All My Darlings

Chapter 46

On Chapter 46 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 5, 2026

← Chapter 45 · Home · Chapter 47 →

Summary

Mr. Spitzer comes to understand that Cousin Hannah had also been a secret mountain climber, perhaps the greatest ever known among ladies, of the Matterhorn, Mont Blanc, and mountains of consciousness, though she was ashamed of it and spoke of it only when dying. When she died, the room grew colder than the Arctic Alps. As her executor he takes her alpenhorn for his collection of instruments, and on the foggy beach he blows a few notes like a ghost calling his lost love. A ram steps out of the fog, and sheep follow him, and ever after lost men follow him through the grey cities. Her dying has brought him to reality and taught him the power of a dead love. He wanders the fog-bound city asking strangers whether they have seen her, and people take him for mad, until an old steeplejack lurches out of the fog and answers him in paradoxes: “I live because I am dead.” The chapter ends with Hannah’s last cry to the bright star of her love.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens by portraying Cousin Hannah not merely as a suffrage leader but as a “valiant wanderer” who climbed “formidable mountains and supernumerary peaks” beyond physical realms such as mountains “greater than mountains,” rising “Alp above Alp through consciousness” even after death. This metaphor suggests her spirit transcends earthly limits, linking her struggles and love to the eternal and the sublime. Her life is framed as an ascent beyond mortal boundaries, symbolizing a quest for immortal love and freedom.
  • Mr. Spitzer, acting as Cousin Hannah’s executor, finds and appropriates her alpenhorn, a heavy musical instrument twice her height. This alpenhorn is symbolic: though it cannot literally evoke the Alps in the lowlands, it serves as a poignant emblem of her spirit and the mountains she climbed. Mr. Spitzer reflects on how music arises from chaos and how the instruments of earth are linked to the heavenly realm, though none are truly “strung in paradise.” This passage explores the tenuous relationship between art, memory, and transcendence.
  • The novel muses on how ghosts in dreams never appear naked but are always clothed in some form implying that all of life and death is a form of “mask” or “clothing.” This metaphor conveys the idea that identity, beauty, and existence are layered, elusive, and perhaps illusory. Mr. Spitzer’s own music is described as a form of elegy or aubade that cannot quite awaken the dead but hints at the dreamlike quality of life and death.
  • A deeply philosophical passage appears as Mr. Spitzer contemplates the “power of a dead beauty, a dead love,” and the “sterility which begets... all beautiful things.” He asserts that no one truly knows the present moment because it is always “freighted with the past,” suggesting that beauty and existence are ghosts of themselves, transitory and elusive. The cloud over beauty is beauty itself, so the veil of mystery remains intact. This suggests an acceptance of ambiguity and the unknowable as essential to human experience.
  • Despite her achievements, Cousin Hannah is described as being ashamed of her mountain-climbing accomplishments, preferring oblivion over fame. Mr. Spitzer reflects on how many famous people value a moment of oblivion more than lasting glory, as oblivion is paradoxically both what they flee from and what they seek. This passage reveals a tension between public success and private vulnerability, heroism and cowardice, and the human desire for both recognition and escape.
  • A haunting voice from an old steeplejack (a tall, church-like figure) speaks to Mr. Spitzer, embodying contradictions like “I live because I am dead,” “I love because I love not,” and the idea that all who have loved have died and drowned. Time is depicted as a cloud crossing a clock face that tells no time. This passage powerfully captures the theme of existence as filled with paradox, where life and death, love and loss are inseparable, and where time itself is an illusion.
  • After Cousin Hannah’s death, Mr. Spitzer wanders the fog-bound city asking strangers if they have seen her, despite being perceived as mad. This quest symbolizes the human search for meaning, connection, and remembrance in a world of loss and forgetting. It also reflects on how memory is fragmented and how one can be simultaneously lost yet followed suggesting the persistence of influence beyond death.
  • The chapter closes with Cousin Hannah’s dying cry, calling for a “bright star of my love” to find the “lightless star,” signaling the mysterious journey beyond death. The imagery of mountains resurrecting before “the last great cataclysm” and her love “going down” evokes a cosmic, cyclical view of life and death, blending mythic and personal dimensions.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “Never yet had he been present at a climax.” Why does Mr. Spitzer always come before or after?
  • “The cloud upon the face of beauty was beauty itself. So never would he lift the cloud.” Why does Mr. Spitzer refuse to lift it?
  • “For that of which she should have been proud, she was ashamed.” Why would Hannah hide her greatest achievement?

Characters

  • Mr. Spitzer says Hannah “had brought him, by her dying, to reality.” What has he learned from her?
  • The men who call Hannah “a charlatan, a mere poseur, a mountebank” forget “her motive which had been her love.” Does knowing her motive change her deeds?
  • Who is the steeplejack, “tall as a church steeple,” who “fell the night she fell”?

Connections

  • In Chapter 44 Hannah’s love was lost in the mountains. How does knowing she was a great climber change that story?
  • Mr. Spitzer is “like an obsessed census-taker” who always finds “one missing,” as in Chapter 42. Who is the missing one now?
  • The steeplejack says, “All who have loved have drowned.” Where else in the novel do lovers drown?

Reflection

  • “It was oblivion from which they had fled, and yet it was oblivion toward which they fled.” Why might the famous long for oblivion?
  • “For who had ever known the present moment?” Is the present always freighted with the past?
  • Is a great deed lessened if no one remembers it?

Research: Characters

Cousin Hannah

  • A complex figure; a suffragette, a mountain climber, a symbol of liberation and love’s endurance, “perhaps the greatest mountain-climber unknown to history, surely the greatest ever known among ladies.” She is proud yet ashamed of her achievements, embodying contradictions of strength and vulnerability. Her death is portrayed almost mythically, with imagery of frost, mountains, and alpenhorns signaling her passage to another realm.

Mr. Spitzer

  • Witness and executor of Cousin Hannah’s estate, an old musician who is deeply introspective and symbolic. He is both observer and participant, grappling with loss, memory, and the meaning of life and death. His inquiries after Hannah’s death reveal his obsession and his quest for understanding the mysteries she embodied.

The Steeplejack

  • A steeple jack is a skilled tradesperson who works at great heights, primarily on tall structures like church steeples, chimneys, and towers, to perform repairs, maintenance, or construction. The profession has historical roots in the maintenance of ecclesiastical buildings.
  • This “old steeple-headed steeplejack or steeple jill” is the only one who remembers Hannah and says “that she was beautiful.”

Research: Themes

Death and Immortality

See: Death and Mortality

  • Cousin Hannah’s death is described with vivid cold and frozen imagery, suggesting a transition into an eternal, timeless state, but one that still holds mystery and significance. There is an ongoing meditation on what it means to die and what survives beyond death, particularly love and memory.

Love Beyond Mortality

See: Death and Mortality · Love

  • Hannah’s message is that love transcends mortal bounds, aiming for an immortal love. This idea recurs as Mr. Spitzer contemplates the power and persistence of love even after death, and the paradox that those who have loved have also died, and vice versa.

The Mountain as a Symbol

See: Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos

  • Mountains symbolize challenges, spiritual ascension, and the unknown. Cousin Hannah is portrayed as an unparalleled mountain climber not just of physical peaks like the Matterhorn or Mont Blanc, but of metaphorical mountains of consciousness and existence.

Memory and Oblivion

See: Memory and the Past

  • The chapter addresses how history, fame, and memory are fleeting. Cousin Hannah, despite her great achievements, is largely forgotten by the world, illustrating the tension between individual legacy and collective memory.

Music and Silence

See: Music, Sound, and Silence

  • Mr. Spitzer, a musician, reflects on music as a metaphor for life and death, where silence and sound intermingle. Music arises from chaos and holds a kind of ephemeral power, much like life itself.

Identity and Masks

See: Identity and the Self

  • The motif of masks and clothing in dreams suggests that identity is always layered, never fully naked or transparent. Ghosts and memories are always clothed in some form, and this applies to living beings as well.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Alpenhorn

See: Music and Instruments

  • Represents a call to lost love and memory; a connection to the mountains and the past. Its presence in a flat coastal landscape symbolizes the displacement of memory and the difficulty of preserving legacy in an unresponsive world.

Mountains and Snow

See: Snow, Ice, and Winter · Mountains

  • Mountains stand for the ultimate challenges and heights of spirit and consciousness. Snow and ice symbolize purity, death, and timelessness, as well as the coldness and isolation that often accompany great achievements or sacrifices.

The Dead Moon

See: Moon and Stars

  • A recurring symbol of deathless love and the eternal, especially in a world where stars have faded or fallen. It suggests a cosmic, timeless love that persists beyond human life.

Music and Bells

See: Music and Instruments

  • Music, especially the unplayed or unheard music, represents the ineffable aspects of life and death. Bells ringing without clear cause evoke mystery, transition, and the presence of the unseen.

Fog and Darkness

See: Fog and Mist · Lamps, Candles, and Light

  • Fog represents uncertainty, loss, and the blurring of reality and memory. The drifting fog and shadows evoke the liminal space between life and death and the difficulty of grasping truth.

Research: Resources

  • “the mountains which were greater than mountains”: The mountains in the chapter bear significant symbolic weight, representing the peaks of human endeavor, the mysteries of consciousness, and the epic nature of Cousin Hannah’s life and legacy within the novel. See Mountains.
  • “that great Matterhorn”: Matterhorn is a mountain of the Alps on the border between Italy and Switzerland. Edward Whymper’s party first climbed it in 1865, and four of the seven died on the descent.
  • “Mt. Rosa”: Monte Rosa is a massif of the Pennine Alps on the Swiss-Italian border. Its Dufourspitze is the highest summit in Switzerland.
  • “Mt. Blanc, Moensch”: Mt. Blanc is the highest mountain in the Alps. Moensch, or Monch, is a mountain in the Bernese Alps. See Mont Blanc in the Chapter 44 resources.
  • “Wetterhorn”: Wetterhorn is a peak in the Swiss Alps. Alfred Wills’s ascent in 1854 is often taken as the start of the golden age of alpinism.
  • “rising Alp above Alp”: “Hills peep o’er hills, and Alps on Alps arise!” (Alexander Pope, An Essay on Criticism, 1711).
  • “the same flowers may be found, white on white”: The same Arctic-alpine plants, such as mountain avens and moss campion, grow both in the far north and on the high Alps, left behind on the mountaintops when the Ice Age glaciers withdrew. See Snow, Ice, and Winter.
  • “the mirrors were rheumed with frost”: In Jewish mourning, and in many Victorian houses, the mirrors were covered after a death. See Judaism.
  • “to play the ranz des vaches”: The ranz des vaches is the call of the Swiss herdsmen to their cattle. Rousseau wrote that it was forbidden to sing it near Swiss soldiers in foreign service, since it made them so homesick that they deserted or died (Dictionnaire de musique, 1768). Rossini set it in the overture to William Tell (1829). See Music and Instruments.
  • “the alpenglow”: Alpenglow is the rosy light on high peaks just after sunset or before sunrise, when the valleys are already in shadow. See Mountains.
  • “the smuggler Charon’s boat”: Charon ferries the dead across the Styx for the coin placed in the mouth of the dead. See Greek Mythology.
  • “like statues upholding the rain-falling roofs”: Statues of women that hold up a roof are caryatids, as on the Porch of the Maidens of the Erechtheion in Athens.
  • “ghosts have seldom or never appeared naked in men’s dreams”: Hamlet’s father appears “in complete steel” on the battlements and later “in his habit as he lived” (Hamlet 1.4, 3.4). See Shakespeare.
  • “The great couturier has snipped his last thread.”: Atropos, the third of the Fates, cuts the thread of life. See Greek Mythology.
  • “his aubade for the evening star”: Aubade is a morning love song or song/poem about lovers separating at dawn. John Donne’s “The Sun Rising” scolds the dawn: “Busy old fool, unruly sun” (1633).
    • Venus is both the morning star and the evening star, called Phosphorus and Hesperus by the Greeks. Gottlob Frege used the two names, one object with two senses, in “On Sense and Reference” (1892). See Astronomy.
  • “the silver sheep’s bell which had once rung to the lost sheep”: “What man of you, having an hundred sheep, if he lose one of them, doth not leave the ninety and nine in the wilderness, and go after that which is lost, until he find it?” (Luke 15:4). See The Bible.
  • “lost like the Dog star”: Sirius, the Dog Star, rises with the sun in late summer, the dog days. See “before the day of the dog,” below. See Moon and Stars.
  • “For who had ever known the present moment?”: Augustine found that the present has no extent: “the present hath no space” (Confessions 11.15, trans. E. B. Pusey, 1838). See Augustine and Christian Theology.
  • “The cloud upon the face of beauty was beauty itself.”: The fourteenth-century Cloud of Unknowing teaches that God is known not by thought but by love, through a cloud that is never lifted in this life: “By love may He be gotten and holden; but by thought never” (chapter 6).
  • “If he knew not her omega, then how did he know her alpha?”: “I am Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the ending, saith the Lord” (Revelation 1:8). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “the age of Hugh De Capet”: Hugh Capet was King of the Franks from 987 to 996 and founded the Capetian dynasty.
  • “remembered in no stud book of mountain-climbing”: The General Stud Book, begun by James Weatherby in 1791, records the pedigree of every English thoroughbred. See Horses and Carriages.
  • “Alpine holy grass”: Holy grass, sweet with the scent of vanilla, was strewn before church doors on saints’ days in northern Europe.
  • “surely the greatest ever known among ladies”: Real suffragists climbed too. Annie Smith Peck made the first ascent of Huascarán’s north peak in 1908 and planted a “VOTES FOR WOMEN” flag on Coropuna in Peru in 1911. Fanny Bullock Workman of Massachusetts was photographed in the Karakoram about 1912 holding a newspaper headed “Votes for Women.” See Women’s Suffrage and Mountains.
  • “her granite was but a façade”: See Woolf’s “granite-like solidity” in the Chapter 44 resources.
  • “the croakings of frogs”: The chorus of Aristophanes’s The Frogs (405 BC) sings “Brekekekex koax koax.”
  • “before the day of the dog”: The Day of the Dog is a novel written by George Barr McCutcheon in 1904. It is a comedic novel. The story follows a Chicago lawyer, Crosby, who must get the widow Mrs. Delancy to sign estate papers, and Swallow, a bulldog who traps them both in a barn. The plot unfolds in a rural setting, highlighting themes of deception, misunderstandings, and social class interactions. The narrative follows Crosby as he travels to meet Mrs. Delancy at her brother-in-law’s farm to finalize legal matters concerning her husband’s estate. Upon arrival, he encounters Swallow, a fierce bulldog who guards the barn and prevents him from accessing the house. In a series of escalating comedic events, Crosby finds himself trapped and forced to engage with the dog, while simultaneously attempting to persuade Mrs. Delancy to reconsider her decision to abandon the legal claim to the estate. As the story progresses, the absurdity culminates in both characters finding themselves in a predicament that ultimately leads to unexpected revelations, personal growth, and a budding romance amidst the chaos. McCutcheon also wrote Brewster’s Millions which has been made into movies. It can be understood as a colloquialism for a day marked by difficulty or an unlucky day, emphasizing the themes of mortality and life’s hardships that permeate the chapter and the novel.
    • “The cat will mew, and dog will have his day” (Hamlet 5.1). The dog days of late summer are named for Sirius, the Dog Star, which appears earlier in this chapter. See Shakespeare.
  • “I am thine only love”: The phrase suggests a deep, exclusive bond or connection, likely representing the intense and often elusive nature of love in the novel. It could be interpreted as the voice of love itself speaking to a beloved, emphasizing that despite all the illusions, losses, and fragmented experiences of life, there remains one true and abiding love that transcends time and circumstance. Given the novel’s themes of mortality, loss, and the interplay between reality and dream, this statement might also reflect the idea that love persists beyond physical existence, echoing through memory, dreams, and perhaps even death. It embodies the yearning for connection and the hope that love, even if unfulfilled or shadowed by sorrow, has a lasting, ineffable presence.
    • The voice is a sand grain: “Because of me there grew this great pearl.” A natural pearl grows when an oyster coats an irritant with nacre, more often a parasite than a grain of sand. See Pearls and Jewels.
  • “apples of Hesperides”: The golden apples of the Hesperides, guarded by a dragon at the edge of the world, were Heracles’s eleventh labor. See Greek Mythology.
  • “like the light-winged cranes”: Pliny wrote that cranes “ballast themselves with coarse sand” before crossing the Black Sea, choose a leader, and post sentinels that each hold “a little stone in its claw” (Natural History 10.30, trans. John Bostock and H. T. Riley, 1855). See Birds.
  • “flying like a Chinaman’s cue”: The queue, a long braid, was forced on Han Chinese men by the Qing dynasty from 1645 as a sign of submission. “Chinaman” is now considered a slur.
  • “Have you seen my lady—or my lord?”: “I will rise now, and go about the city in the streets, and in the broad ways I will seek him whom my soul loveth… The watchmen that go about the city found me: to whom I said, Saw ye him whom my soul loveth?” (Song of Solomon 3:2-3). See The Bible.
  • “an old steeple-headed steeplejack”: “Steeple jill” is Young’s joke on Jack and Jill.
  • “dead as the balloonist shrouded in the garden”: See Sophie Blanchard, who died when her balloon caught fire over Paris, in the Chapter 42 resources.
  • “I live because I am dead”: The steeplejack’s speech functions as a lyrical meditation on the paradoxes of existence: life intertwined with death, reality entwined with illusion, and identity caught in flux. It encapsulates key themes of the novel by blending rich symbolism, natural imagery, and spiritual reflection, all delivered with a tone that balances acceptance of human frailty with a resilient embrace of life’s ongoing dance.
    • Teresa of Ávila: “Vivo sin vivir en mí, / y tan alta vida espero, / que muero porque no muero,” “I live without living in myself, and I hope for so high a life that I die because I do not die.”
  • “the surveyor with his plummet line”: “The Lord stood upon a wall made by a plumbline, with a plumbline in his hand” (Amos 7:7). See The Bible.
  • “The great steeplechase is over.”: The first steeplechase was run in Ireland in 1752, from the church steeple at Buttevant to the steeple at Doneraile, by Cornelius O’Callaghan and Edmund Blake. Here it is run by the clock’s “tall jockey” and “short jockey.” See Horses and Carriages and Clocks and Watches.
  • “much like the fly in the spider’s web”: “‘Will you walk into my parlour?’ said the Spider to the Fly” (Mary Howitt, “The Spider and the Fly,” 1829).
  • “he would have had his twin at his last hour”: To see one’s double was an omen of death. Shelley told of meeting his double weeks before he drowned in 1822.
  • “Ah, bright star of my love”: This may refer to John Keats’s sonnet “Bright star, would I were stedfast as thou art” (1819). This passage metaphorically captures the painful awareness of dying while longing for the beloved who is no longer fully present or alive, and the hope that love might find a path even through darkness and loss. See Romantic Poets.
