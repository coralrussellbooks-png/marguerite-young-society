Summary

Mr. Spitzer realizes that Hannah was also a secret mountain climber, of peaks real and of the mind. As her executor he takes her alpenhorn for his collection of instruments. He wanders a fog-bound city asking strangers whether they have seen her, and people take him for mad. A steeplejack answers him in paradoxes. The chapter ends with Hannah's last cry to the bright star of her love.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens by portraying Cousin Hannah not merely as a suffrage leader but as a "valiant wanderer" who climbed "formidable mountains and supernumerary peaks" beyond physical realms such as mountains "greater than mountains," rising "Alp above Alp through consciousness" even after death. This metaphor suggests her spirit transcends earthly limits, linking her struggles and love to the eternal and the sublime. Her life is framed as an ascent beyond mortal boundaries, symbolizing a quest for immortal love and freedom.
  • Mr. Spitzer, acting as Cousin Hannah’s executor, finds and appropriates her alpenhorn, a heavy musical instrument twice her height. This alpenhorn is symbolic: though it cannot literally evoke the Alps in the lowlands, it serves as a poignant emblem of her spirit and the mountains she climbed. Mr. Spitzer reflects on how music arises from chaos and how the instruments of earth are linked to the heavenly realm, though none are truly "strung in paradise." This passage explores the tenuous relationship between art, memory, and transcendence.
  • The novel muses on how ghosts in dreams never appear naked but are always clothed in some form implying that all of life and death is a form of "mask" or "clothing." This metaphor conveys the idea that identity, beauty, and existence are layered, elusive, and perhaps illusory. Mr. Spitzer’s own music is described as a form of elegy or aubade that cannot quite awaken the dead but hints at the dreamlike quality of life and death.
  • A deeply philosophical passage appears as Mr. Spitzer contemplates the "power of a dead beauty, a dead love," and the "sterility which begets... all beautiful things." He asserts that no one truly knows the present moment because it is always "freighted with the past," suggesting that beauty and existence are ghosts of themselves, transitory and elusive. The cloud over beauty is beauty itself, so the veil of mystery remains intact. This suggests an acceptance of ambiguity and the unknowable as essential to human experience.
  • Despite her achievements, Cousin Hannah is described as being ashamed of her mountain-climbing accomplishments, preferring oblivion over fame. Mr. Spitzer reflects on how many famous people value a moment of oblivion more than lasting glory, as oblivion is paradoxically both what they flee from and what they seek. This passage reveals a tension between public success and private vulnerability, heroism and cowardice, and the human desire for both recognition and escape.
  • A haunting voice from an old steeplejack (a tall, church-like figure) speaks to Mr. Spitzer, embodying contradictions like "I live because I am dead," "I love because I love not," and the idea that all who have loved have died and drowned. Time is depicted as a cloud crossing a clock face that tells no time. This passage powerfully captures the theme of existence as filled with paradox, where life and death, love and loss are inseparable, and where time itself is an illusion.
  • After Cousin Hannah’s death, Mr. Spitzer wanders the fog-bound city asking strangers if they have seen her, despite being perceived as mad. This quest symbolizes the human search for meaning, connection, and remembrance in a world of loss and forgetting. It also reflects on how memory is fragmented and how one can be simultaneously lost yet followed suggesting the persistence of influence beyond death.
  • The chapter closes with Cousin Hannah’s dying cry, calling for a "bright star of my love" to find the "lightless star," signaling the mysterious journey beyond death. The imagery of mountains resurrecting before "the last great cataclysm" and her love "going down" evokes a cosmic, cyclical view of life and death, blending mythic and personal dimensions.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Cousin Hannah

  • A complex figure; a suffragette, a mountain climber, a symbol of liberation and love’s endurance. She is proud yet ashamed of her achievements, embodying contradictions of strength and vulnerability. Her death is portrayed almost mythically, with imagery of frost, mountains, and alpenhorns signaling her passage to another realm.

Mr. Spitzer

  • Vera and executor of Cousin Hannah’s estate, an old musician who is deeply introspective and symbolic. He is both observer and participant, grappling with loss, memory, and the meaning of life and death. His inquiries after Hannah’s death reveal his obsession and his quest for understanding the mysteries she embodied.
  • A steeple jack is a skilled tradesperson who works at great heights, primarily on tall structures like church steeples, chimneys, and towers, to perform repairs, maintenance, or construction. The profession has historical roots in the maintenance of ecclesiastical buildings.

Research: Themes

Death and Immortality

See: Death and Mortality

  • Cousin Hannah’s death is described with vivid cold and frozen imagery, suggesting a transition into an eternal, timeless state, but one that still holds mystery and significance. There is an ongoing meditation on what it means to die and what survives beyond death, particularly love and memory.

Love Beyond Mortality

See: Death and Mortality · Love

  • Hannah's message is that love transcends mortal bounds, aiming for an immortal love. This idea recurs as Mr. Spitzer contemplates the power and persistence of love even after death, and the paradox that those who have loved have also died, and vice versa.

The Mountain as a Symbol

See: Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos

  • Mountains symbolize challenges, spiritual ascension, and the unknown. Cousin Hannah is portrayed as an unparalleled mountain climber not just of physical peaks like the Matterhorn or Mont Blanc, but of metaphorical mountains of consciousness and existence.

Memory and Oblivion

See: Memory and the Past

  • The chapter addresses how history, fame, and memory are fleeting. Cousin Hannah, despite her great achievements, is largely forgotten by the world, illustrating the tension between individual legacy and collective memory.

Music and Silence

See: Music, Sound, and Silence

  • Mr. Spitzer, a musician, reflects on music as a metaphor for life and death, where silence and sound intermingle. Music arises from chaos and holds a kind of ephemeral power, much like life itself.

Identity and Masks

See: Identity and the Self

  • The motif of masks and clothing in dreams suggests that identity is always layered, never fully naked or transparent. Ghosts and memories are always clothed in some form, and this applies to living beings as well.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Alpenhorn

See: Music and Instruments

  • Represents a call to lost love and memory; a connection to the mountains and the past. Its presence in a flat coastal landscape symbolizes the displacement of memory and the difficulty of preserving legacy in an unresponsive world.

Mountains and Snow

See: Snow, Ice, and Winter · Mountains

  • Mountains stand for the ultimate challenges and heights of spirit and consciousness. Snow and ice symbolize purity, death, and timelessness, as well as the coldness and isolation that often accompany great achievements or sacrifices.

The Dead Moon

See: Moon and Stars

  • A recurring symbol of deathless love and the eternal, especially in a world where stars have faded or fallen. It suggests a cosmic, timeless love that persists beyond human life.

Music and Bells

See: Music and Instruments

  • Music, especially the unplayed or unheard music, represents the ineffable aspects of life and death. Bells ringing without clear cause evoke mystery, transition, and the presence of the unseen.

Fog and Darkness

See: Fog and Mist · Lamps, Candles, and Light

  • Fog represents uncertainty, loss, and the blurring of reality and memory. The drifting fog and shadows evoke the liminal space between life and death and the difficulty of grasping truth.

Research: Resources

  • The Day of the Dog is a novel written by George Barr McCutcheon in 1904. It is a comedic novel. The story revolves around a humorous and somewhat absurd encounter between a lawyer, Mr. Crosby, and a stubborn bulldog named Swallow while he seeks to secure a signature from Mrs. Delancy on estate-related documents. The plot unfolds in a rural setting, highlighting themes of deception, misunderstandings, and social class interactions. The narrative follows Crosby as he travels to meet Mrs. Delancy at her brother-in-law's farm to finalize legal matters concerning her husband's estate. Upon arrival, he encounters Swallow, a fierce bulldog who guards the barn and prevents him from accessing the house. In a series of escalating comedic events, Crosby finds himself trapped and forced to engage with the dog, while simultaneously attempting to persuade Mrs. Delancy to reconsider her decision to abandon the legal claim to the estate. As the story progresses, the absurdity culminates in both characters finding themselves in a predicament that ultimately leads to unexpected revelations, personal growth, and a budding romance amidst the chaos. McCutcheon also wrote Brewster’s Millions which has been made into movies. It can be understood as a colloquialism for a day marked by difficulty or an unlucky day, emphasizing the themes of mortality and life's hardships that permeate the chapter and the novel.
  • “I am thine only love. I am thine only love, my dear of dears.” - Hebrews 10:22 The phrase suggests a deep, exclusive bond or connection, likely representing the intense and often elusive nature of love in the novel. It could be interpreted as the voice of love itself speaking to a beloved, emphasizing that despite all the illusions, losses, and fragmented experiences of life, there remains one true and abiding love that transcends time and circumstance. Given the novel's themes of mortality, loss, and the interplay between reality and dream, this statement might also reflect the idea that love persists beyond physical existence, echoing through memory, dreams, and perhaps even death. It embodies the yearning for connection and the hope that love, even if unfulfilled or shadowed by sorrow, has a lasting, ineffable presence.
  • The mountains in the chapter bear significant symbolic weight, representing the peaks of human endeavor, the mysteries of consciousness, and the epic nature of Cousin Hannah’s life and legacy within the novel.
  • Matterhorn is a mountain of the Alps on the border between Italy and Switzerland.
  • Mt. Rosa is a mountain in Colorado of the Rocky Mountains of the US.
  • Mt. Blanc is the highest mountain in the Alps. Moensch, or Monch, is a mountain in the Bernese Alps.
  • Wetterhorn is a peak in the Swiss Alps.
  • Aubade is a morning love song or song/poem about lovers separating at dawn.
  • Hugh De Capet was the King of the Franks.
  • Mr. Spitzer's existential struggle closely parallels the themes of Eliot's The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock, encompassing paralysis, duality, mortality, alienation, and the search for meaning in a fragmented, uncertain world. Both texts delve into the complexities of human consciousness, the burden of self-awareness, and the haunting presence of time and death.
  • The steeplejack's speech functions as a lyrical meditation on the paradoxes of existence: life intertwined with death, reality entwined with illusion, and identity caught in flux. It encapsulates key themes of the novel by blending rich symbolism, natural imagery, and spiritual reflection, all delivered with a tone that balances acceptance of human frailty with a resilient embrace of life’s ongoing dance.
  • “...Ah, bright star of my love, find your way to the lightless star. Find your way to one who has neither wings nor light. For I am dying now… and my love goes down.” - may refer to a poem by John Keats called Bright Star. This passage metaphorically captures the painful awareness of dying while longing for the beloved who is no longer fully present or alive, and the hope that love might find a path even through darkness and loss.
