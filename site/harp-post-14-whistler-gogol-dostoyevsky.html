Chapter 14. Whistler, Gogol, Dostoyevsky

Part of a reading of Harp Song for a Radical (1999), Marguerite Young’s life of Eugene Victor Debs.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 13 · The Unfinished Harp: Reading Harp Song for a Radical · Chapter 15 →

An ending in Russia, and an Afterword that finally speaks in Debs's voice

The last chapters of Harp Song for a Radical (pages 546 to 576) leave Indiana and never come back. Young tells the stories of the painter James McNeill Whistler's mother, of Whistler's father, who built a railroad for the czar, of Nikolai Gogol, and finally of Fyodor Dostoyevsky, arrested in 1849 for belonging to a Fourierist study circle and led out to be shot.

For a biography of Debs, it is a strange place to stop. For this book, it may be exactly the right one.

The mother in the painting

Anna Whistler is best known as the seated figure in her son's 1871 painting Arrangement in Grey and Black No. 1. Young notes that it was revered in pious homes and mocked in saloons, where sometimes "the face of Susan B. was substituted for hers" (546).

Young's Anna Whistler is a missionary. In Russia, where her husband, Major George Washington Whistler, was building the railroad from St. Petersburg to Moscow for Czar Nicholas I, she handed out Bible tracts to railroad workers and soldiers "right under the nose of the czar" (546). She believed that her husband's railroad was "God's mask" for her own mission of converting Russians (547).

The czar himself, in a story Young repeats, drew the line of the railroad with a ruler, showing Major Whistler "the way the railroad should be laid as straight and shining as a thread" (562). Serfs laid the rails. Strikes were forbidden.

Death before life

Young then turns to Gogol, whose novel Dead Souls satirized a Russia where serfs were bought and sold even after death. The critic Vissarion Belinsky had hoped the book would stir Russians toward reform. When Gogol retreated into religious conservatism, Belinsky wrote him a furious open letter. Young's summary of the Russian situation is a single line: "Death, it seemed, had preceded life" (558).

That letter led directly to Dostoyevsky's arrest. He read it aloud at a meeting of the Petrashevsky circle, a group of young men studying Fourier's utopian socialism. A police informer had been planted among them. Young makes the parallel explicit, comparing the informer to the Pinkerton agents "the American czars of railroads and mills and mines would employ" (560).

In the spring of 1849, the police rounded up the circle. Dostoyevsky and others were sentenced to death, taken to a square in St. Petersburg, and prepared for execution. At the last moment, a messenger announced that the czar had commuted their sentences to hard labor in Siberia, "through the infinite clemency of the czar" (568). The whole scene had been staged. Young calls it "this macabre joke" (568).

Why end here

Ruas wrote in his introduction that Debs's imprisonment is "anticipated by the unforgettably harrowing scene of Dostoyevsky being led to his near execution." That is the first reason for the Russian ending. The book that began with Debs in prison ends with another radical led out to die for belonging to a reading group.

The second reason is the date. Dostoyevsky was arrested in 1849, the year Daniel Debs arrived in New York and Weitling returned there. Major Whistler died of cholera in St. Petersburg that same April. The Russian ending loops back to the moment where Part Two began. It is The Past Doesn't Go Away made into a circle.

The third reason is the railroad. Dostoyevsky, Young notes, used to go down to watch the new St. Petersburg railway station being built (554). The railroad the czar drew with a ruler, the railroad Anna Whistler's husband built, and the railroad Debs would fire engines on are, in Young's imagination, one line.

Just before the Afterword, she brings in Thoreau's famous question from Walden about the railroad ties: "Did you ever think what those sleepers are that underlie the railroad? Each one is a man" (576). And then comes the book's last sentence before the Afterword. How long would Debs's elegiac sense last? "It would continue for as long as he lived and perhaps long afterward. As will be seen" (576).

"As will be seen." The book promises more, and there is no more.

The Afterword

The Afterword, only four pages long, is the one place in the book where Debs speaks at length. Young quotes his essays on the forgotten early labor organizers, the pioneer who "ended his career a nameless outcast" and "filled an unknown grave" (578). She quotes his faith: "No strike has ever been lost, and there can be no defeat for the labor movement" (578). And she quotes his list of the people who do the world's work, who "dug its canals, made its road beds, laid its rails and operates its trains" (578).

She notes that from his earliest reading, The House of the Dead, Dostoyevsky's account of his Siberian prison years, was among his favorite books, a world "he would see at large and magnified in America from the 1870s onward, long before he knew that he would be a prison bird" (577).

The book ends with Norman Thomas's image of Debs after his death, a tall man in a gray suit "walking the star belt," with "this world always half in darkness" (580).

What the lens shows

The ending is Unfinished Is Honest as structure. The book promises "as will be seen" and then stops. It is also the Crossing Point on a world scale. Russia and America, the czar and the railroad king, the Fourierist study circle and the Terre Haute lodge are all shown as parts of one century. Debs is the man standing where all these lines cross, though he is barely on the page.

Checking the record

  • Debs and the B&O. Young calls Debs a "former fireman for the old B & O" (550). Debs fired engines on the Terre Haute and Indianapolis line and, briefly, on a line out of St. Louis. He never worked for the Baltimore and Ohio.

  • The Russian dates. Accurate in the main. The Petrashevsky circle was arrested in the early hours of April 23, 1849. Major Whistler died in St. Petersburg in April 1849. The mock execution took place that December.

  • Whistler's Mother. Painted in 1871, as Young indicates.
