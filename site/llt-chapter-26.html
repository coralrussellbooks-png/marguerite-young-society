Chapter 26

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: Grief Doesn't End · The Sacred Is Here

The Questions

Mr. Spitzer, a lawyer, calls the law “erroneous and arbitrary” and longs for a world without it. Is that despair, or the one construction he refuses to seal?

He dreams of “silent music” and dissolved souls in a heaven where the dead are rejoined. Is that transcendence, or grief reaching for a beyond?

He is “living his brother’s life and dying his brother’s death.” What has grief made of his identity?

The Boston lady who is Cleopatra, the “castles of illusion,” what does Mr. Spitzer see in the sealed inventions of others?

Mr. Spitzer is the rare lawyer who sees the law as built. He “disparaged the fickle operations of the law,” calls the whole system “erroneous and arbitrary,” and hopes for a future with no need of it. Where another lawyer would take the law as the closest thing to bedrock, Mr. Spitzer holds it as one more construction, made and revisable and often unjust, a blueprint dressed as justice. He keeps open the one thing his profession is built to seal. The wills and deeds he works among, the “hidden past” filed in documents, are to him not the fixed record but a paper construction over a past that will not stay filed.

His grief has become the medium of his identity, and the chapter says so plainly: he is “living his brother’s life and dying his brother’s death.” He relives the moment of identifying the body; he thinks the dead man’s thoughts; the mourning does not close and does not stay outside him. This is Grief Doesn't End carried to the place the last chapter reached and held there, grief not as weather one stands in but as the substance one is made of, the dead brother woven through the living man until neither can be pulled clear.

And here grief tempts him toward a beyond. He dreams of “silent music” and “heavenly harmonies,” a heaven that is a state of mind where identity dissolves and the souls are rejoined, the upper world reached, as he puts it, only by first traveling through the lower. It is beautiful, and it is the pull the book is most wary of: the promised elsewhere where the dead are restored and the losses undone. Grief wants a beyond, a place where the brother waits and the dying-with completes. The Sacred Is Here sets its face against exactly this, meaning is here and not in a restored elsewhere, and Mr. Spitzer is caught between the two, the compassionate ordinary work of his days and the yearning for a silent music that would end them.

Around him the sealed inventions of others, the Boston lady who is Cleopatra, the eccentrics in their “castles of illusion,” show him what he is not doing. They have built their beyonds and moved in, sealed like Catherine. Mr. Spitzer feels the same pull and does not seal. He yearns for the upper world and keeps working in the lower, holds the “eternal paradox” open, carries an “almost cosmic serenity” that is not the peace of a sealed dream but the harder calm of a man who wants the beyond and stays in the ordinary anyway.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Mr. Spitzer’s longing for “silent music” and dissolved souls as its ideal, a transcendence to aspire to, and the reading that takes his disparagement of the law as mere cynicism. Neither the endorsed beyond nor the sour lawyer is the chapter’s Mr. Spitzer.

The footing is that Mr. Spitzer keeps open the one construction his profession exists to seal, holding the law as built and arbitrary rather than as bedrock. His grief has become the substance of his identity, the dead brother woven through him. And his yearning for a beyond, the silent music, the rejoined souls, is grief reaching for the promised elsewhere The Sacred Is Here resists, a pull he feels and does not obey, staying in the compassionate ordinary work while wanting the world that would end it. The law is built, grief can become one’s material, and the beyond is a temptation to be felt without being sealed into. Mr. Spitzer is left caught between the upper world he longs for and the lower one he keeps working in, the paradox held rather than resolved.

For Reflection

What construction that everyone treats as bedrock, the law, the record, the way things are, is actually built and revisable?

When grief reaches for a beyond where the lost are restored, is that faith, or a sealed dream one is being tempted to move into?

Can one want the elsewhere and still stay, honestly, in the here?
