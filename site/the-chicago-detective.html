The Chicago Detective

Part of To All My Darlings, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated November 12, 2026

Home

In the research: Chapter 73, Chapter 74, Chapter 75, Chapter 76, Chapter 77

Definition from research

Chapter 73

Listed as: The Chicago detective

  • A symbolic figure representing societal judgment, scrutiny, and the external pressures faced by Esther Longtree. Though he appears only indirectly through Esther’s thoughts and fears, his presence is significant and multifaceted.
  • The detective symbolizes the potential legal and moral condemnation that Esther fears. She imagines a scenario where she might be arrested, tried, and accused of murder for the stillborn children she has carried and lost over the years. The detective’s investigation represents society’s attempt to impose order and assign blame on Esther for the tragic outcomes of her pregnancies, despite her innocence in her own mind.
  • The detective is “old” and “worm-ridden,” and he is himself one of the fathers, “another old, sterile father,” hunting proof “that she had killed his three whom she had never borne or who were born without eyes.” He stands for the many fathers who impregnated Esther and took no responsibility for the children. The detective’s search for proof and evidence reflects a broader theme of male absence and desertion, with Esther bearing the burden alone.
  • Esther fears the detective’s inquiries, which might lead to public exposure of her condition and the stillborn children. She worries about appearing before a jury while visibly pregnant, the social humiliation involved, and the invasive nature of such an investigation. This fear adds to her isolation and suffering, emphasizing the harshness of societal judgment towards women in her position.
  • The detective’s efforts to find evidence of murder or wrongdoing are ultimately futile, as Esther has hidden the stillborn children in natural places (streams, fields, under snowdrifts) making proof elusive.
  • Esther’s silence and refusal to answer inquiries, partly motivated by kindness to the dying fathers and partly by self-preservation, contrast with the detective’s probing.
  • “the old Chicago detective was trying to find the proof”: England’s Act to Prevent the Destroying and Murthering of Bastard Children (1624) presumed that an unmarried mother who hid the death of her newborn had murdered it, unless she could prove “by one witness at the least” that the child was born dead; the penalty was death. It stood until 1803. In Massachusetts Hannah Piggen was reportedly the last woman hanged for concealing a birth, in 1785 (Wikipedia). Concealment of birth is still a crime in many states.

Chapter 74

  • The father of Esther’s children: three winters he found her unconscious in the fields, took her for a corpse, felt her pulse, and raped her.
  • He hunts murderers and now accuses Esther of murdering his children, though he himself raped her and sent her the name of an abortionist “who enjoyed police protection.”
  • He represents authority, suspicion, and the complexities of truth and guilt.
  • “a Chicago detective and specialist in murder mysteries”: The Chicago Detective can be understood symbolically as a representation of a police state or police corruption through several layers of meaning revealed in the novel.
  • “This Chicago detective was creating quite a problem even now”: The detective’s suit “was based upon a real case which happened in Chicago,” which Young read about in a New York paper, “and I wove it into my story” (Marguerite Young, interview with Kay Bonetti, American Audio Prose Library, May 1983).

Chapter 75

  • A figure of violence and threat; he raped Esther and later accuses her of murder.
  • “for it was not her business if his wife said that he was sterile”: The detective is married, and he and his wife each say the other is sterile.
  • “the soap salesman’s soap baby”: The Chicago detective once came “as a soap salesman in the winter meadows” (Chapter 74).

Chapter 76

  • An enigmatic figure who has solved countless murders, both loud and quiet, often using disguises and undercover tactics.
  • He is relentless and invasive, unable to avoid uncovering dark secrets wherever he goes.
  • His interest in Esther is personal, as he believes she has his children.
  • He is still hunting “the bleeding rapist” and says he found “his handiwork, Esther Longtree with her clothes torn and her mouth foaming”; yet in Chapter 74 Esther says the detective raped her, and here that he “had never touched her.”
  • His presence represents judgment, law, and the relentless pursuit of truth.

Chapter 77

  • Represents societal judgment and racial bias.
  • Obsessed with solving murders of white children but indifferent to black children’s deaths.
  • Seen by Esther as both a threat and possibly the real murderer: “This detective was a murderer.”
  • He “had raped her before she could cry out” and left her for dead, “the dung of blackbirds caked upon her face.”
