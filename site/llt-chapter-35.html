Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 35

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: The Past Doesn't Go Away

The Questions

Catherine builds the dead Cousin Hannah into a hero; Mr. Spitzer builds her into a cheat. Which one is she now?

Mr. Spitzer, who knows almost nothing about cards, is certain Cousin Hannah cheated. Even the open man has a sealed spot. What is his prejudice protecting?

The chapter admires Hannah’s boldness over Spitzer’s caution. But which of them was really living open?

If the dead are kept alive by memory, whose version of them lives?

Cousin Hannah is dead now, and the living build her two incompatible ways. Catherine idealizes her into a fearless hero who rode “naked through the gale”; Mr. Spitzer, despite an “abysmal ignorance of cards,” is convinced she was a cheat. Same woman, two constructions, the move the book made with Josh’s death and Beatrice’s reputation, now performed on a dead person kept alive in memory. Neither version is the recovered truth. Each is a build, held in place by the builder’s need, and Cousin Hannah, past all defending herself, is remade nightly in the mouths of the people who outlived her.

Mr. Spitzer’s certainty is the chapter’s quiet correction to any picture of him as purely open. He knows almost nothing about cards and is sure she cheated anyway; this is a sealed prejudice, a conviction held past his own ignorance, exactly the kind of frozen blueprint he elsewhere refuses. The man the book has been offering as the model of provisional, open living turns out to have his own locked corner. This is not a flaw in the reading but its completion: no one is purely open, not even Mr. Spitzer, just as no one is purely sealed, not even Catherine. Everyone is mixed, and the model of openness carries his own seal like everyone else.

The chapter’s surface admires Cousin Hannah’s boldness over Mr. Spitzer’s caution, her defiance over his timidity, and here the book’s deeper values run against its own surface. By now the reader knows that Cousin Hannah’s boldness was a sealed blueprint, a certainty extended into a wall, and that Mr. Spitzer’s caution is not mere passivity but the provisional, open living of a man who keeps his anchors light and never mistakes them for the floor. What looks like fearless freedom was the more sealed life; what looks like timid conformity was the more open one. The chapter tempts the reader to prefer the bold woman, and the book’s own terms reverse the preference.

And the dead live by whoever remembers them hardest. Catherine’s Hannah, the immortal defiant heroine, and Mr. Spitzer’s Hannah, the cheat, are both alive, both false, both sustained by the needs of the living. This is The Past Doesn't Go Away as afterlife: the dead are not gone but pressed into the present, shaped by the memory that keeps them, with no true Hannah underneath to correct the versions. She is now purely a construction, held open only in the sense that two irreconcilable builds of her coexist, unresolved, in the house she left.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes its admiration of Cousin Hannah’s boldness as the book’s verdict, freedom over conformity, and the reading that takes Mr. Spitzer as purely the open man against her seal. Neither the celebrated rebel nor the flawless model survives the chapter’s own terms.

The footing is that the dead Cousin Hannah is built two incompatible ways by the living, hero and cheat, each a construction held by the builder’s need, with no recovered truth beneath. Mr. Spitzer’s certainty that she cheated is a sealed prejudice held past his own ignorance, proof that even the model of openness has a locked corner, that everyone is mixed. And the chapter’s surface preference for boldness over caution runs against the book’s own values, since Hannah’s boldness was the sealed life and Mr. Spitzer’s caution the open one. No one is purely open or purely sealed, and the dead live as the living build them. The two Hannahs coexist unreconciled, and the chapter refuses to say which the real one was.

For Reflection

When someone is remembered two incompatible ways, is either the real them, and does the question have an answer?

Where is one’s own locked corner, the conviction held past one’s own ignorance?

Does the boldest-seeming life in a room always turn out to be the most open one?
