Chapter 25

  • The philosophy of death explores the meaning of death, our relationship to it, and the various ways it impacts human existence and understanding. It delves into questions about what happens after death, the nature of consciousness, and the ethical implications of death, including suicide and capital punishment. The Nature of Death: Dualism (the soul continues to exist after death, potentially in a separate realm or afterlife), Materialism (death is the permanent cessation of all bodily functions, including consciousness), Annihilationism (death is the end of all conscious experience, and nothing exists after death). Philosophical Perspectives on Death: Stoicism (death is not something to be feared, as it is a natural part of life and a release from suffering), Existentialism (like Kierkegaard and Heidegger, emphasized the importance of confronting our mortality to understand our own existence and the meaning of life), Nietzsche (proposed that individuals have the freedom to define their own death, suggesting it could be a conscious choice rather than an inevitable fate). Ethical Considerations: Suicide (philosophy engages with questions about the moral permissibility of suicide, including whether it's ever justifiable), Capital Punishment (the death penalty raises complex ethical questions about state-sanctioned killing and the value of human life), Euthanasia (issues related to assisted dying and the right to choose one's own death). The Meaning of Life: some philosophers argue that the awareness of our mortality can give deeper meaning to life, prompting us to live more fully and intentionally; others suggest that facing death head-on can help us appreciate the present moment and the beauty of existence. Fear of Death: the philosophy of death explores the psychological and emotional impact of fearing death, including its effects on our choices and behaviors; some philosophers believe that the fear of death is irrational and stems from our ignorance of what happens after death. Implications for Living: many philosophical perspectives on death suggest that accepting our mortality can lead to a more fulfilling and meaningful life; by contemplating death, we may be better equipped to appreciate the present moment, make conscious choices, and live a life aligned with our values.

Chapter 31

  • Heidegger’s philosophy “being-towards-death” is a fundamental aspect of human existence, highlighting the finite nature of our being and the importance of confronting mortality to live authentically. Humans are in the world and constantly engaging and interpreting it. Humans are unique in that they are aware of their own mortality and this shapes their existence. Heidegger distinguishes between authentic and inauthentic ways of living. Authentic living involves confronting the inevitability of death and making choices that are meaningful to oneself, while inauthentic living involves being swept away by societal norms and expectations. One must confront mortality in order to live more fully and purposefully, making the most of the time one has. Young articulates a richly poetic and existential meditation on death that embodies Heidegger’s philosophy of “being-towards-death.” Vera’s awareness of mortality disrupts ordinary existence, reveals the fragility and finitude of life, and through this confrontation, opens the possibility for authentic being. This chapter captures the essence of how the anticipation of death shapes human existence, infusing it with urgency, solitude, and profound insight, all central to Heidegger’s existential analytic.

Chapter 37

  • Life as a state of experience and potential - Socrates thought death might be a dreamless sleep or passage to another realm where one can converse with the dead. Existentialism believes death is an inevitable aspect of human existence and that it can lead to a heightened sense of self-awareness and responsibility. Epicureanism argues death cannot harm a person, as it’s either not yet experienced (while alive) or not experienced at all (once dead). Death is defined as the loss of the capacity to engage in vital activities and the cessation of consciousness. Wittgenstein said that death is not an experience in life. Schopenhauer believed that understanding death as the ultimate end of life can provide a perspective on the meaning of life itself. Freud thought the life instinct (Eros) and the death instinct (Thanatos) are fundamental forces in human behavior. The chapter depicts life as a state of ongoing experience and potential characterized by uncertainty, illusion, and transformation. It is not a fixed reality but a mutable and often paradoxical condition where life and death, truth and illusion, identity and multiplicity coexist and where human understanding is limited and provisional.

Chapter 39

  • “I AM” - which God says in Exodus 3:14. Jesus uses the phrase “I am” in seven distinct statements each emphasizing a specific aspect of His divine nature and purpose, connecting him to the God revealed in the Old Testament. Descartes “I think, therefore I am.” which then leads into Existentialism and Absurdism. Existentialism proposes the idea that people are free to choose their own meaning and are responsible for their choices, there are no inherent meaning or purpose to life and instead people are responsible for creating meaning, personal experience and subjective reality are used to shape understanding of the world, freedom, responsibility, choice, self-discovery and the absurdity of human existence are explored. Anais Nin did say that Young’s work was not absurdist because Young believed there was an inherent beauty to the world. It symbolizes the core being of a character or presence who asserts existence and identity beyond the chaotic flux of illusions and transformations depicted in the novel. It is a moment of profound existential affirmation amid the pervasive theme of identity's fragmentation and the quest for meaning.

Chapter 45

  • “Shall love be infinitely postponed? If infinitely postponed, then it is love.” may refer to Hegel. To deny something is to determine it, and to determine something is to negate it. Spinoza’s principle is “every determination is negation”. Hegel takes this one step further by suggesting that negation itself is a process of creation, and is even the essence of positive being. By denying a certain trait or characteristic, one is implicitly acknowledging the possibility of its opposite or the existence of a different reality. For Hegel, this power of the negative, or the act of negation, is what drives progress and development. Denying a particular concept allows for the possibility of a new one to emerge, incorporating both the original concept and its negation. This is often explained through the concept of the negation of the negation, where a denial of a denial leads to a new synthesis that transcends both the original thesis and the antithesis. From an existentialist perspective denying a predefined purpose or essence allows for the possibility of individuals to create their own meaning and existence. The line speaks to love as an infinite emotional state, one that transcends immediate gratification and endures through time, absence, and even death, resonating with the novel's larger themes of longing, loss, and the elusive nature of human connection.

Chapter 51

  • Existentialism: The paragraph touches upon themes of mortality, the subjective experience of time, and the search for meaning. Mr. Spitzer reflects on the fading boundary between life and death, the retrospective understanding of the heart, and the possibility of moving from death to life. These reflections suggest an awareness of the fleeting nature of existence and the individual's struggle to find purpose in a world seemingly devoid of inherent meaning. The questions posed, such as "why should the second journey seem longer than the first?" highlight the subjective and often paradoxical nature of human experience.

Chapter 51

  • “Seldom did he meet with anyone who did not suffer from some form of complex illusion beyond necessity” The paragraph begins by stating that most people suffer from "some form of complex illusion beyond necessity," suggesting a fundamental questioning of what constitutes reality. The examples of the old woman believing she's a birdcage and the piano tuner declaring foreheads "hollow" highlight the subjective and often distorted nature of individual perceptions. The recurring motif of blindness serves as a powerful metaphor for the limitations of human perception. The blind portrait painter raises questions about the nature of art and representation: Can true beauty or understanding be captured without sight? The reference to a blind war veteran visiting art galleries with a seeing-eye dog further emphasizes the limitations and interpretations of reality. The idea of deaf music critics "hearing" errors without actually hearing the music is paradoxical. It suggests that judgment and understanding can be based on something other than direct sensory experience, perhaps on intellectual analysis, intuition, or an awareness of structure and form. This challenges the conventional notion of sensory experience as the primary source of knowledge. Mr. Spitzer reflects that there might be "only one great dream informing all dreamers and all dreams." This concept aligns with philosophical ideas about a collective unconscious or a universal mind, where individual experiences are interconnected and share a common source. This also questions the nature of reality as an individual or shared experience. The paragraph questions whether time even matters when considering happiness. Is happiness something that exists outside of time, or is it bound by its constraints? The narrative suggests happiness is fleeting, like a snowflake. Time is further explored through the contrast between Mr. Spitzer and his brother. Mr. Spitzer's life has been so long that he can't remember certain things, while his brother's life was cut short. This contrast highlights the different ways in which time can be experienced and how it shapes our understanding of life and death. The observation that "three minutes of happiness seemed perhaps all there might be or less than there had been" in most lives suggests a pessimistic view of human existence. However, it also implies that happiness is a rare and precious commodity, to be cherished when it is found. The paragraph concludes by stating that "the greatest happiness might come through the sorrow of waiting, of postponement." This paradoxical statement suggests that the anticipation of happiness, or even the acceptance of sorrow, can be a source of fulfillment in itself. It also hints at the idea that happiness and sorrow are intertwined, and that one cannot exist without the other. The paragraph also touches on Plato's Theory of Forms. Plato believed that the world we perceive is merely a shadow of a higher realm of perfect Forms. The blind individuals, the deaf critics, and the deluded people Mr. Spitzer encounters highlight the imperfect and distorted nature of our sensory experiences. The idea that all dreamers share one great dream can be seen as a reflection of Plato's concept of the Form of the Good. This Form is the ultimate source of truth and knowledge, and it is what all individuals are striving to reach. The shared dream, therefore, could be interpreted as a connection to this Form. The paragraph reflects existentialist themes, particularly the absurdity of existence and the search for meaning in a meaningless world. The characters' illusions, their distorted perceptions, and the fleeting nature of happiness all contribute to a sense of the inherent absurdity of human life. Despite the seeming meaninglessness of existence, the paragraph also implies a sense of individual responsibility. Mr. Spitzer, as a lawyer dealing with the affairs of the dead, is tasked with making sense of their lives and legacies. This responsibility highlights the importance of individual choices and actions, even in the face of an indifferent universe.

Chapter 51

  • The last paragraph grapples with the elusive nature of reality. The initial fears of the "naked valet" and the "mad couturier" who clothes him with "the colors of a dream" suggest the influence of external forces shaping our perception of self and the world. This aligns with philosophical discussions about appearance versus reality, as seen in Plato's Allegory of the Cave, where humans perceive only shadows of true forms. The line, "For the dream of life, even like that of death, must ever contain within it an element of the unrealized, the unrealizable —else all would be complete," suggests that imperfection and the unattainable are integral to existence. This resonates with existentialist thought, which emphasizes the inherent incompleteness and absurdity of human life. The description of Mr. Spitzer "dreaming like the heart of God upon a world never created, uncreated, this world dreaming itself even when there was no counterpart" suggests a world that is in a constant state of becoming, and perhaps only exists within the mind of the dreamer. Mr. Spitzer's fear of being evicted from the "citadel" and being born into a world where he misses fundamental sensory experiences (smell of the rose, sound of birds) speaks to the existential anxiety of losing one's sense of self and belonging. The idea of a mother who is a "bearded man armored" challenges traditional notions of gender and identity. This can be linked to philosophical discussions about the social construction of gender and the fluidity of identity. The image of the "old jockey who dreamed that his dead horse lived" and the subsequent shock of realizing the dream's reality highlights the power of the unconscious and the irrational. This aligns with psychoanalytic theories, particularly those of Freud, which emphasize the influence of unconscious desires and repressed memories on human behavior. The phrase "We lived by an imperfect concatenation, even as we died—by echoes, tremblings, reverberations, sourceless thoughts" suggests that life is a series of disjointed and fragmented experiences. This aligns with Buddhist philosophy, which emphasizes the impermanence of all things and the illusory nature of the self.

Chapter 52

  • Mr. Spitzer wonders if doubt should move mountains “which faith had not moved." This is a direct inversion of the common saying that faith can move mountains. This highlights a core philosophical tension between believing in something despite a lack of evidence (faith) and questioning everything (doubt). - Matthew 17:20. The image of the suffragette searching for a lost skirt, leading to the death of her lover, suggests the absurdity of human pursuits. The pursuit of something seemingly trivial (the skirt) leads to tragic consequences, mirroring the existentialist idea that life is inherently meaningless, and humans create their own meaning through their actions. The description of the skirt as "white lace gleaming through snow and its little flowers which were like the memories of little loves" blurs the lines between reality and memory. It suggests that our perceptions of reality are shaped by our past experiences and emotions, leading to a subjective and potentially distorted understanding of the world. The mention of the suffragette crying "Long live woman! Vive la femme! Viva la donna!" in many languages, "perhaps even in Sanskrit which was the language written upon a silent snowflake," touches on the philosophy of language. It suggests that language can be a powerful tool for expressing universal concepts (like the importance of women) but that there are also limits to what language can convey. There are "secret languages never to be translated into words," implying that some truths are beyond the reach of verbal expression. The reference to, "the suffragette who had lost her apostle" could also be referencing the loss of an ideal form. Platonism is the belief that everything we see is a shadow or "lesser" version of a perfect form. The loss of her apostle, who could be a student or follower, is also the loss of what the suffragette sees as a perfect version of herself. The next paragraph emphasizes the individual's choice and action in the face of an indifferent or absurd universe. The suffragette's decision to dress as a man can be seen as an assertion of her freedom and rejection of societal norms. The fantastical images of footmen and golden stairways highlight the human tendency to create meaning and beauty in a world that may ultimately be meaningless. The contrast between Cousin Hannah's practical clothing and the elaborate attire of other climbers could be interpreted as a reflection of the philosophical debate between idealism and materialism. The suffragette's focus on functionality suggests a more materialistic worldview, while the other climbers' emphasis on appearance could be seen as representing idealism. The repeated blurring of the lines between mountains and clouds, and the shifting perceptions of the moon, raise questions about the nature of reality and the limitations of human perception. The statement, "the moon had floated most amazingly distending worlds," suggests that reality is not fixed but is shaped by our individual perspectives. The image of the footman cutting a golden stairway from a frozen lake to the mountains of the moon could be an allusion to Plato's Allegory of the Cave. The stairway could represent the philosopher's ascent from the darkness of the cave (the material world) to the light of the sun (the realm of Forms). The mountains of the moon could symbolize a higher realm of knowledge and understanding. The paragraph's emphasis on nature, individual experience, and the search for spiritual meaning aligns with the themes of Transcendentalism. The suffragette's mountain climbing could be seen as a metaphor for the individual's journey to transcend the limitations of the material world and connect with a higher power or truth. The mention of "horizons unknown as the stars floated" could also be interpreted in a nihilistic way, suggesting that there are no ultimate goals or destinations and that all human endeavors are ultimately meaningless. At first it's a whimsical and imaginative description of mountain climbing, then, beneath the surface, it explores fundamental philosophical questions about freedom, reality, perception, and the meaning of life which invites the reader to think about their own philosophical reflections.

Chapter 52

  • Mr. Spitzer, reflecting on the suffragette's life, wonders if "All might have been imaginary in this life as in this death." This relates to philosophical discussions about the nature of reality, perception, and the extent to which our experiences are shaped by our minds. The search through the suffragette's belongings is a search for truth and understanding, but the paragraph suggests that this search may be futile. "Surely her entire life could not have been a game of hide-and-seek, for although she was hiding—who was seeking for her?" This touches on the philosophical problem of epistemology, or how we can know what is true, and whether objective truth is even attainable. Mr. Spitzer reflects on the suffragette's death and its meaning. He wonders if death might be a "miracle," and questions whether she has found her love in death. This aligns with philosophical inquiries into the nature of death, the possibility of an afterlife, and the meaning of existence in the face of mortality. The paragraph suggests that time may not be linear or objective, but rather a subjective experience. "All might have been an indeterminate moment or one beyond this calendar of days, hours, years. And what calendar was ever kept in the long night?" This idea relates to philosophical discussions about the nature of time, its relationship to consciousness, and whether time is an objective reality or a human construct. The paragraph also presents love as a paradox, questioning whether it can ever be fully realized or understood. "There was the homeward music of the past and the music of the future which was never to be, and there was the music which could not be—and the music which was always to be..." This paradox is a recurring theme in philosophy, particularly in discussions about the nature of desire, attachment, and the limitations of human relationships. It is filled with dualities and opposing forces: life and death, reality and imagination, love and loss, male and female. This relates to philosophical concepts of duality, dialectics, and the idea that reality is composed of opposing forces that are constantly interacting. It questions the value of individual experience and the search for meaning. This aligns with philosophical discussions about existentialism, individualism, and the search for meaning in a seemingly absurd world.

Chapter 52

  • “Mr. Spitzer had trembled like a gambler for whom there would always be another shuffle” and the next paragraph. These two paragraphs use rich, poetic imagery and metaphor to explore themes of uncertainty, decay, memory, and intertwined fates. Mr. Spitzer is depicted as a liminal figure caught between reality and illusion, past and present, life and death, whose reflections on the suffragette reveal the elusive nature of truth and identity in the novel. The suffragette herself is a haunting presence (both fragile and magnetic, real and spectral) whose life and legacy resist simple comprehension, embodying the novel's complex interplay of reality and dream.The opening lines immediately establish a world governed by chance. Mr. Spitzer trembling "like a gambler" suggests a life where outcomes are unpredictable and determined by forces beyond individual control. This aligns with existentialist themes of the absurdity of existence and the lack of inherent meaning. The "faceless card" and "star of no known number" symbolize the limits of human knowledge. We can never fully grasp the forces shaping our lives, and there will always be elements of the unknown. This resonates with the existentialist emphasis on the limits of reason. The line "Yes, there was an external world, he knew now as it departed" is crucial. It indicates a loss of faith in an objective reality. The world is not fixed but is constantly slipping away, emphasizing the subjective experience. "He gave his belief when there was no reason for his belief" highlights a rejection of rationalism. Spitzer's faith is not based on evidence or logic but on something deeper, perhaps a Kierkegaardian "leap of faith" into the absurd. "For he reserved his credulity for the grand occasions eliciting the skepticism of others. Their skepticism was his faith" is a complex statement. It suggests that Spitzer finds meaning in what others reject. This could be interpreted as a form of contrarianism, or as a deeper understanding that truth often lies in challenging conventional wisdom.

Chapter 52

  • The Swiss accusations against Cousin Hannah highlight the ethical problem of unintended consequences. Even if her actions were driven by noble intentions, such as seeking her lost love or advancing suffrage, they allegedly resulted in harm to others, such as causing snow-slides or burying trains. This relates to consequentialism, which evaluates actions based on their outcomes. There is a tension between progress and destruction. Cousin Hannah "uncovered some things" but "covered others," raising questions about whether progress inevitably involves loss or harm. This relates to dialectical thinking, where progress emerges from the conflict between opposing forces. The Swiss fear of Cousin Hannah potentially disrupting the stability of their nation reflects a conflict between individual freedom and the collective good. The Swiss are concerned about their community and way of life. This relates to political philosophy and the social contract. Mr. Spitzer questions the reliability of perception and the possibility of truly knowing anything. The mountains may be clouds, and the clouds may be mountains, highlighting the subjective nature of reality. This relates to skepticism and the questioning of empirical knowledge. The newspaper reports of the suffragette's adventures are presented as "blurred" and open to interpretation. This emphasizes the role of the observer in shaping reality and the difficulty of establishing objective truth. Mr. Spitzer's belief "when there was no reason for his belief" and his reservation of "his credulity for the grand occasions eliciting the skepticism of others" suggests a rejection of conventional notions of truth based on empirical evidence or logical reasoning. This relates to philosophical movements like existentialism, which prioritize individual experience and meaning-making. The notion that Cousin Hannah's actions could cause "reverberations to eternity if not to infinity" implies a belief in the interconnectedness of all things and the far-reaching consequences of even seemingly small actions. This aligns with holistic philosophies that emphasize the unity of existence. The idea that "time and the dream were never married in her" suggests a rejection of linear time and an embrace of a more fluid, dreamlike reality. This relates to philosophies that challenge conventional notions of time, such as those found in process philosophy. Mr. Spitzer considers the possibility that Cousin Hannah might "ferry snow-crowned mountains of passage to another world" and "take all to that world where there was no mourning," suggesting a belief in transcendence or the possibility of escaping the limitations of earthly existence. These philosophical inferences are not presented as systematic arguments but rather as fragmented reflections that emerge from Mr. Spitzer's consciousness as he grapples with the legacy of Cousin Hannah.

Chapter 52

  • “Perhaps it was she who was the master of keys” grapples with the question of what happens after death. Mr. Spitzer contemplates whether the soul ("spirit") needs a physical exit, like a chimney, if the conventional routes ("doors and passage-ways") are closed. This reflects philosophical inquiries into the soul's immortality and its separation from the body. The idea that "death was unknown to those who loved, and death was unknown to those who loved not, and death was unknown to those who died" suggests that death remains an enigma regardless of one's experiences or beliefs. It's a state beyond comprehension, aligning with existentialist themes of the unknown and the limits of human knowledge. The paragraph questions whether our senses provide a complete picture of reality. The idea that "Even the closed door was not closed, for should death be better known than love? Not all the senses were ever sealed at once" suggests that there are realities beyond our immediate perception. The image of the butterfly escaping through the ear, "even when it was a door of the silence," implies that even in the absence of sound, there are avenues for experience or existence. The description of Mr. Spitzer as a night watchman in the "starry universe" raises questions about the nature of reality. Is he a real figure, or is he merely a figment of someone's imagination? His light awakening "other sleepers, those he never knew" suggests the interconnectedness of existence, even if we are unaware of it. The suggestion that he perhaps "had intended to lock and not to unlock" introduces the possibility that his actions may have unintended consequences, highlighting the limits of human control and the role of chance. The final lines emphasize Mr. Spitzer's role as the "only mourner among men," suggesting a profound sense of isolation and the burden of grief. His mourning for himself implies a recognition of his own mortality and the inevitability of loss. This connects to existentialist themes of individual responsibility and the search for meaning in a seemingly meaningless world.

Chapter 52

  • Mr. Spitzer's meticulous accounting and listing is directly tied to his struggle with his identity, particularly his relationship with his deceased brother. He seeks a perfect balance, a mirrored reflection where everything in his life corresponds to his brother's, suggesting a desire to understand himself through this dualistic lens. The fear of "no differing counterpart" speaks to the existential anxiety of lacking a defined self, relying on another for definition. This resonates with philosophical concepts of identity formation, the "self" versus the "other," and the influence of external relationships on self-perception. Thinkers like Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir explored similar themes in existentialism. Spitzer's obsessive listing and indexing of even the most mundane objects (pearl buttons, rusted nail heads, etc.) reflects a deep-seated need to impose order on a chaotic world. He's trying to find patterns, connections, and a "common denominator" to make sense of existence. This aligns with the philosophical pursuit of metaphysics: trying to understand the fundamental nature of reality. The futility of Spitzer's efforts mirrors the inherent difficulties in finding absolute order or meaning in a world often perceived as absurd or chaotic (a key theme in existentialism and absurdism). The questions about the sail ("Was the sail unwinding the same as the winding sail?") touch on the philosophical problem of identity over time. Does something remain the same if its state changes? This is further complicated by the cyclical nature of the tides and the wagon wheels, implying a sense of eternal return or repetition. This connects to Heraclitus's famous saying, "No man ever steps in the same river twice," emphasizing the constant flux and change in the universe. The paragraph also hints at Nietzsche's concept of eternal recurrence. Spitzer's attempts to capture reality through lists and measurements are ultimately limited. His fear that "all the weight, as he had often complained, was on this weightless star" alludes to the disparity between the tangible and intangible, the concrete and abstract. He can list the physical properties of objects, but he cannot capture their essence or meaning. This is related to epistemology, the study of knowledge and its limitations. The paragraph suggests the impossibility of fully knowing or representing reality, as there will always be something missing or lost in translation. The description of Spitzer's actions creating "division as in division there was creation," suggests that in every act of creation, there is also an act of destruction. This mirrors the belief that every action has an equal and opposite reaction. This is connected to the concept of the "Tao" which states that everything is composed of two opposites, and that without the other, it cannot exist.

Chapter 52

  • Mr. Spitzer's search through the house and trunks represents a quest for knowledge and understanding of the suffragette's life. The fact that he searches for "he knew not what" suggests that the nature of knowledge itself is uncertain. The "magnetic doors" that open on their own imply that some knowledge is revealed passively, not through active seeking. The idea that there were many differences between the suffragette's last sleep and her first sleep suggests a changing reality, shaped by experience and time. The "tumultuous life" alters the nature of her being. The reference to a "windowless seraglio or cave or darkened tower" relates to the mind as a prison or a space of limited perception. The idea of the "supernumerary love, a wife unknown" introduces the concept of hidden aspects of the self or unconscious desires. The paragraph delves into the nature of love and its mysteries. The "blind god of love" refers to the idea that love is irrational and indiscriminate. The image of the "turban-winder winding fields of consciousness" suggests that love can lead to a loss of individual identity and a merging of consciousnesses. The phrase "in the unwinding there should be no memory and no woman and no man" implies a fading of individual existence and a potential descent into nothingness. This reflects themes of nihilism and existentialism where existence is seen as meaningless and absurd. The individual's fading suggests a loss of self and meaning.

Chapter 68

  • "there was only one definition of reality, that it was real" emphasizes a tautological but deeply existential assertion: reality is defined by its own existence as experienced or known, regardless of its form. "even the unreal things were real," points to an inclusive, perhaps subjective or phenomenological, understanding of reality where things dismissed or marginalized by society (ignored, rejected, unloved) are nonetheless part of the fabric of reality. This echoes themes of illusion versus reality, and the permeability between them, which run throughout the novel. Philosophically, this passage represents a theme often associated with existentialism and phenomenology: that reality is defined by experience and perception rather than by fixed, external definitions. It also touches on the fluidity of reality, blurring lines between dream and waking life, appearance and essence, presence and absence. The passage conveys that reality is not limited to the tangible or socially accepted but includes dreams, illusions, forgotten or rejected beings, and all forms of existence or perception. It challenges rigid dichotomies and invites a broader, more inclusive understanding of what is real.

Chapter 70

  • "There is no return but the return of what is different, I suppose, and that by establishing relationships where none exist, we merely deceive ourselves into believing that life as a whole has a hidden, constant meaning like the unheard music of the spheres." The passage reflects a philosophical meditation on the unavoidability of change, the human compulsion to find or impose meaning, and the possibility that such meaning is not inherent but constructed. It challenges the belief in a fixed cosmic order and suggests that what we perceive as meaningful patterns may be self-deceptions, resonating with existentialist skepticism and the impermanence stressed in ancient and modern philosophies.
