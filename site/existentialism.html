Definition from research

Chapter 25

  • “What death was this which was no death?”: The philosophy of death explores the meaning of death, our relationship to it, and the various ways it impacts human existence and understanding. It delves into questions about what happens after death, the nature of consciousness, and the ethical implications of death, including suicide. The Nature of Death: Dualism (the soul continues to exist after death, potentially in a separate realm or afterlife), Materialism (death is the permanent cessation of all bodily functions, including consciousness), Annihilationism (death is the end of all conscious experience, and nothing exists after death). Philosophical Perspectives on Death: Stoicism (death is not something to be feared, as it is a natural part of life and a release from suffering), Existentialism (thinkers like Kierkegaard and Heidegger, in Being and Time, 1927, emphasized the importance of confronting our mortality to understand our own existence and the meaning of life), Nietzsche (proposed that individuals have the freedom to define their own death, suggesting it could be a conscious choice rather than an inevitable fate: “Die at the right time,” in “On Free Death,” Thus Spoke Zarathustra, 1883). Ethical Considerations: Suicide (philosophy engages with questions about the moral permissibility of suicide, including whether it’s ever justifiable).

Chapter 31

  • “Here lies one who shared our common lot of mortal frailty”: Heidegger’s “being-towards-death,” from Being and Time (1927), is a fundamental aspect of human existence, highlighting the finite nature of our being and the importance of confronting mortality to live authentically. Heidegger distinguishes between authentic and inauthentic ways of living: authentic living confronts the inevitability of death and makes choices that are meaningful to oneself, while inauthentic living is swept along by what “they” say and do (das Man). Mr. Spitzer’s epitaph names the common condition Heidegger describes: one who “shared our common lot of mortal frailty.”

Chapter 37

  • “How should death be different from life, my mother asked”: Life as a state of experience and potential: Socrates thought death might be a dreamless sleep or passage to another realm where one can converse with the dead. Existentialism believes death is an inevitable aspect of human existence and that it can lead to a heightened sense of self-awareness and responsibility. Epicureanism argues death cannot harm a person, as it’s either not yet experienced (while alive) or not experienced at all (once dead). Death is defined as the loss of the capacity to engage in vital activities and the cessation of consciousness. Wittgenstein said that death is not an experience in life. Schopenhauer believed that understanding death as the ultimate end of life can provide a perspective on the meaning of life itself. Freud thought the life instinct (Eros) and the death instinct (Thanatos) are fundamental forces in human behavior. The chapter depicts life as a state of ongoing experience and potential characterized by uncertainty, illusion, and transformation. It is not a fixed reality but a mutable and often paradoxical condition where life and death, truth and illusion, identity and multiplicity coexist and where human understanding is limited and provisional.

Chapter 39

  • “The personal pronoun I did not always mean”: “I AM THAT I AM” (Exodus 3:14). Jesus uses the phrase “I am” in seven distinct statements each emphasizing a specific aspect of His divine nature and purpose, connecting him to the God revealed in the Old Testament. Descartes wrote, “I think, therefore I am” (René Descartes, Discourse on Method, 1637), which then leads into Existentialism and Absurdism. Existentialism proposes the idea that people are free to choose their own meaning and are responsible for their choices, there are no inherent meaning or purpose to life and instead people are responsible for creating meaning, personal experience and subjective reality are used to shape understanding of the world, freedom, responsibility, choice, self-discovery and the absurdity of human existence are explored. Anaïs Nin wrote that Young’s work was not absurdist, because Young believed there was an inherent beauty to the world (Anaïs Nin, The Novel of the Future, Macmillan, 1968). It symbolizes the core being of a character or presence who asserts existence and identity beyond the chaotic flux of illusions and transformations depicted in the novel. It is a moment of profound existential affirmation amid the pervasive theme of identity’s fragmentation and the quest for meaning.

Chapter 45

  • “Shall love be infinitely postponed?”: may refer to Hegel. To deny something is to determine it, and to determine something is to negate it. Spinoza’s principle is “every determination is negation” (Spinoza, letter 50 to Jarig Jelles, 1674). Hegel takes this one step further by suggesting that negation itself is a process of creation, and is even the essence of positive being. By denying a certain trait or characteristic, one is implicitly acknowledging the possibility of its opposite or the existence of a different reality. For Hegel, this power of the negative, or the act of negation, is what drives progress and development. Denying a particular concept allows for the possibility of a new one to emerge, incorporating both the original concept and its negation. This is often explained through the concept of the negation of the negation, where a denial of a denial leads to a new synthesis that transcends both the original thesis and the antithesis. From an existentialist perspective denying a predefined purpose or essence allows for the possibility of individuals to create their own meaning and existence. The line speaks to love as an infinite emotional state, one that transcends immediate gratification and endures through time, absence, and even death, resonating with the novel’s larger themes of longing, loss, and the elusive nature of human connection.

Chapter 51

  • Existentialism: The paragraph touches upon themes of mortality, the subjective experience of time, and the search for meaning. Mr. Spitzer reflects on the fading boundary between life and death, the retrospective understanding of the heart, and the possibility of moving from death to life. These reflections suggest an awareness of the fleeting nature of existence and the individual’s struggle to find purpose in a world seemingly devoid of inherent meaning. The questions posed, such as “why should the second journey seem longer than the first?” highlight the subjective and often paradoxical nature of human experience.

Chapter 52

  • “Mr. Spitzer had trembled like a gambler”: This paragraph and the next use rich, poetic imagery and metaphor to explore themes of uncertainty, decay, memory, and intertwined fates. Mr. Spitzer is depicted as a liminal figure caught between reality and illusion, past and present, life and death, whose reflections on the suffragette reveal the elusive nature of truth and identity in the novel. The suffragette herself is a haunting presence (both fragile and magnetic, real and spectral) whose life and legacy resist simple comprehension, embodying the novel’s complex interplay of reality and dream. The opening lines immediately establish a world governed by chance. Mr. Spitzer trembling “like a gambler” suggests a life where outcomes are unpredictable and determined by forces beyond individual control. This aligns with existentialist themes of the absurdity of existence and the lack of inherent meaning. The “faceless card” and “star of no known number” symbolize the limits of human knowledge. No one can fully grasp the forces shaping a life, and there will always be elements of the unknown. This resonates with the existentialist emphasis on the limits of reason. The line “Yes, there was an external world, he knew now as it departed” is crucial. It indicates a loss of faith in an objective reality. The world is not fixed but is constantly slipping away, emphasizing the subjective experience. “He gave his belief when there was no reason for his belief” highlights a rejection of rationalism. Spitzer’s faith is not based on evidence or logic but on something deeper, perhaps a Kierkegaardian “leap of faith” into the absurd. “For he reserved his credulity for the grand occasions eliciting the skepticism of others. Their skepticism was his faith” is a complex statement. It suggests that Spitzer finds meaning in what others reject. This could be interpreted as a form of contrarianism, or as a deeper understanding that truth often lies in challenging conventional wisdom.

Chapter 59

  • “his tragic sense had been the greatest joy”: Compare Miguel de Unamuno’s The Tragic Sense of Life (Del sentimiento trágico de la vida, 1913), which finds the root of human feeling in a hunger for immortality that reason cannot satisfy.

Chapter 68

  • “there was only one definition of reality, that it was real”: The line emphasizes a tautological but deeply existential assertion: reality is defined by its own existence as experienced or known, regardless of its form. “even the unreal things were real,” points to an inclusive, perhaps subjective or phenomenological, understanding of reality where things dismissed or marginalized by society (ignored, rejected, unloved) are nonetheless part of the fabric of reality. This echoes themes of illusion versus reality, and the permeability between them, which run throughout the novel. Philosophically, this passage represents a theme often associated with existentialism and phenomenology: that reality is defined by experience and perception rather than by fixed, external definitions. It also touches on the fluidity of reality, blurring lines between dream and waking life, appearance and essence, presence and absence. The passage conveys that reality is not limited to the tangible or socially accepted but includes dreams, illusions, forgotten or rejected beings, and all forms of existence or perception. It challenges rigid dichotomies and invites a broader, more inclusive understanding of what is real.

Chapter 70

  • “the unheard music of the spheres”: The passage reflects a philosophical meditation on the unavoidability of change, the human compulsion to find or impose meaning, and the possibility that such meaning is not inherent but constructed. It challenges the belief in a fixed cosmic order and suggests that what we perceive as meaningful patterns may be self-deceptions, resonating with existentialist skepticism and the impermanence stressed in ancient and modern philosophies. Pythagoras taught that the planets sound a harmony too perfect for human ears. Compare Keats, in the ode Vera has already echoed: “Heard melodies are sweet, but those unheard / Are sweeter.”
