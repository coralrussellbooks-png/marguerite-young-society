Chapter 76: The Trial She Welcomes

Research notes: Chapter 76

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer · Tabitha · The Chicago Detective · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Duality and Doubles · Motherhood, Pregnancy, and Birth · Women, Gender, and Marriage

Symbols: Angels, Devils, and Myth · Pregnancy, Birth, and Children · Twins and Doubles · Weddings and Funerals

Philosophy: Freud and Jung · Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Greek Mythology · Homer's Epics · Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery · The History of Opium

This is the last chapter devoted to Esther Longtree’s extended monologue. Over Chapters 72 through 76, Young has given her more sustained attention than almost any other character in the novel outside of Vera herself, and what she has given her is not a resolved story but an accumulating one: more loss, more history, more ambiguity, more of the spectral children and the fathers who disappear and the detective who circles.

Chapter 76 brings Esther to a threshold. She has feared the trial the detective threatens. She has hidden everything she could hide. She has kept her silence with extraordinary discipline. In this chapter, she turns. She begins, strangely, to welcome the idea of the trial, not because she expects justice, but because the trial would at least be an acknowledgment that she exists, that what happened to her was real, that the quiet murders, the children who came and did not live, were worth the world’s attention.

She is also tired. Tired of the detective, tired of the fathers, tired of carrying everything alone. And she reveals, in passing, the darkest pocket of her history: she killed her twin sister in the womb.

What Actually Happens

Esther turns over her “successful conceptions” in her mind and concludes they may have been her worst mistakes: “her unfulfilled abortions, her many births.” She is baffled by her last triumphant pregnancy, the “diffused radiance” she feels. She still believes in it. She still feels two hearts. The detective pursues his quiet mysteries: the murders where no bodies are missed, the deaths that go unrecorded, the crimes that only he seems to notice. He uncovers murderers everywhere. He solves everything except his own case. A new loud crime will break, Esther thinks, and the quiet mysteries will remain quiet forever. This is how it always works: the spectacular displaces the invisible. The stillborn children hover around her, robed and bloodless, watching her with innocent smiles, sometimes applauding her act of intercourse as if cheering on the very process that produced them and failed them. This is one of the strangest images in the chapter: the dead children as audience, as witnesses to their own impossible origin.

She killed her twin sister in the womb, she says. She does not explain how, or whether this is literal or metaphorical, or whether she was conscious of doing it, or whether she simply survived and the other did not and she has always carried this as a killing. She notes the question of twins who murder: one twin who kills the other, then accuses the dead one of having done the murdering. She knows this structure. She wonders if she is the twin who killed or the twin who was wrongly accused. The detective’s historical parallel surfaces here: To All My Darlings mentions Robert Ledru, a French detective of the 1880s famous for solving crimes, who discovered through investigation that he was himself the murderer he had been hunting; he had killed while sleepwalking and had no conscious memory of it. The detective who is also the criminal. Young’s Chicago detective operates in this same inverted logic. Esther contemplates the trial with a new quality: she begins to welcome it. Mass infanticide, she supposes, is what she would be charged with. She finds, unexpectedly, a kind of comfort in the cosmic scale of her evidence: her body, nature, her memories, all the things that cannot be produced in a courtroom but that she carries absolutely. Let them try to prosecute what cannot be seen. Let the quiet mysteries finally make noise. She wonders why Cupid has missed her. She has loved. Her heart is broken. The god of love appears to have aimed elsewhere.

She closes knitting and unraveling a black hood: the color of night, the shape of birth, the garment she makes and unmakes because making it is the act of faith she can sustain and the child who will wear it is not yet here to receive it.

The Twin She Killed in the Womb

This is the revelation Young has been withholding for five chapters, and she delivers it with Esther’s characteristic obliqueness, not as confession but as fact, dropped into the middle of Esther’s ongoing meditation on guilt and innocence.

She killed her twin in the womb. This may be literal: vanishing twin syndrome, in which one twin is absorbed by the other early in pregnancy, is real and not uncommon, occurring in an estimated one in eight pregnancies that begin as multiples. The surviving twin has no consciousness of the process. Young gives Esther consciousness of it, or the feeling of consciousness, the retrospective conviction that something was done, that she survived at a cost.

It may also be metaphorical: the twin who did not survive, the self that Esther might have been if the first loss had not happened, the double she has been mourning all along without knowing it. The mud baby. The tall child. The little shadow boxer. All of them may be the twin: the other Esther, the one who did not make it out.

The twins who murder each other, one kills the other and then accuses the dead one of having done the killing, appears as a question Esther poses to herself. She does not answer it. The question is: which twin is she? The killer, or the one who is blamed for a killing she didn’t commit? She has been accused of killing the detective’s children. She has not killed anything. She may have killed her twin. She may have simply survived. She cannot tell the difference anymore, and possibly there is no difference to tell.

To All My Darlings notes that this twin dynamic echoes the story of Joachim and Peron Spitzer: the twin brothers mentioned elsewhere in the novel, where one killed the other and took his identity. The structure of twin-murder-and-impersonation runs through the novel’s mythology: the wrong person wearing the dead one’s face, the guilty party walking free under the innocent one’s name.

The Stillborn as Audience

Young’s most unsettling image in this chapter is the spectral children watching Esther during intercourse: robed, bloodless, hovering, sometimes applauding. They are witnesses to the act that produced them and failed them. They are the audience for the process that keeps generating new versions of them without ever producing a living one.

This image is disturbing and also, in Young’s precise way, logical. The stillborn have nowhere else to be. They cannot be anywhere except in Esther’s consciousness, because that is the only space they were ever given. They did not get a world. They got a mother. So they attend to her life with the complete attention of those who have nothing else.

They watch with innocent smiles. They applaud. They are not accusing her. They are witnessing. This is what Esther has always been unable to give them, a witness, and here they return the gift, imperfectly, from the wrong side.

Welcoming the Trial

The shift in this chapter, from fear of the trial to something approaching welcome, is one of the most significant movements in Esther’s arc. She has spent five chapters afraid of the jury of women who would judge her, the detective who would prosecute her, the public exposure of her hidden stillborn. In Chapter 76, she turns toward it. What has changed? Partly exhaustion: she is tired of the silence, tired of carrying everything alone, tired of being invisible. Partly a recognition that the trial, however unjust, would at least acknowledge that something happened to her: that the quiet murders were murders, that the children existed, that her body was not simply malfunctioning in private but was the site of a history that deserved attention. She also recognizes that her evidence is cosmic and unassailable (her body, nature, memory, the fullness of what she knows) even though none of it is admissible in a courtroom. Let them try to prosecute what cannot be seen. The quiet mysteries will probably remain quiet forever: some louder crime will come along and displace them. But for a moment, contemplating the trial, Esther imagines herself finally, loudly, in the record.

Knitting and Unraveling the Black Hood

The chapter’s closing image is Esther’s most enduring domestic act: she knits a black hood and then unravels it and then knits it again. The hood is the color of night. It is the shape of a beginning: the garment that covers the head at birth, that signals the start of a life.

She cannot finish it. Or she will not. Finishing it would mean the child is here to receive it, and the child is not here yet. So she knits and unravels, knits and unravels: the act of preparation maintained as an act of faith rather than an act of completion. This is Esther’s version of Penelope’s weaving: the work that is done and undone to keep the future open, to prevent the arrival of the wrong ending.

She is wilder than people think, the chapter notes. She runs. She laughs. She endures. Her life is bounded by routine and also marked by moments of freedom that no one from the outside has seen. She is not only what the detective’s investigation reveals. She is not only what the town’s gossip constructs. She is the woman who knits a black hood at night and unravels it at dawn and starts again, because the child is coming and she will be ready.

What to Hold Onto

The twin she killed in the womb, or did not kill, or simply survived: the question she cannot answer, the guilt she cannot separate from survival. The robed, bloodless stillborn, hovering around her, watching with innocent smiles, sometimes applauding. The dead as audience to the life that couldn’t give them a life.

The shift from terror of the trial to welcome of it: let the quiet murders finally make noise. Let the detective try to prosecute what cannot be seen.

Cupid who has missed her. She has loved. Her heart is broken. The god of love appears to have aimed elsewhere.

And Esther knitting and unraveling the black hood. Night, birth, faith, the work that is done and undone to keep the future open. The child is coming. She will be ready.

This is the last chapter that is wholly Esther’s. Carry her with you into what comes next.
