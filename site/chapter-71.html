To All My Darlings

Chapter 71

On Chapter 71 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 70 · Home · Chapter 72 →

Summary

Vera has not slept. She wears her mother's black velvet, meant as Catherine's shroud and never used, along with her lace and her pearls, which Catherine believed multiplied when lost. She thinks of Mr. Weed guarding his children through the night. She enters Dr. O'Leary's room, a clutter of mismatched women's shoes, medical instruments, a Yale diploma, and a calendar twenty years out of date. A woman shouts prejudices on the stairs. The Tavern Landlady, in thick dialect, tells her about everyone in town, including a waitress always expecting a baby. The maid, Tabitha, whispers good morning.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with reflections on the "Christian hangman," his routine duties, and the haunting memory of his wife Marie who hanged herself while pregnant. This passage explores themes of death, guilt, and the persistence of ghosts both literal and metaphorical. Mr. Weed’s fear of what he might find in the orchard, and the idea that Marie’s children play at hanging themselves, evoke a tragic cycle of violence and loss. Mr. Weed himself does not hang himself, suggesting a complex relationship with death and survival.
  • Vera reflects on wearing her mother’s necklace of real pearls, an inheritance described as indestructible and eternal, symbolizing a lost estate and continuity beyond material value. The story of the chauffeur, James, who returns thirteen pearls when twelve are lost, introduces the concept of infinity and the shadow of the past (Joachim as the "quiet, yawning twin"). This passage conveys themes of memory, loss, and the mystical nature of inheritance and identity.
  • Vera enters the doctor’s bedroom/office, described as chaotic, filled with scattered, unmatched women’s shoes, medical instruments, and broken objects. This disorder reflects the doctor’s madness and the blurred line between reality and fantasy. The doctor delivers imaginary babies in a barren countryside, and there is mention of a "pregnant man," a surreal image that challenges normality and points to widespread delusion.
  • The interaction between the doctor and a silent crawling woman, Sarah, his "little sister," is significant. Sarah’s presence as a tormentor and companion to the doctor, combined with his screaming and promises, illustrates his mental instability and the intrusion of childhood into his adult life. This relationship adds a poignant angle on loneliness and mental illness.
  • Vera studies the doctor’s extensive but disordered medical library, filled with eclectic and outdated books ranging from medical texts to children’s primers and political pamphlets. The doctor’s diploma from Yale hangs above the bed, marking a fall from professional authority into madness. The contrast between his past competence and present confusion emphasizes themes of decay and lost knowledge.
  • A confrontation with a half-dressed woman in metallic curlers introduces a sharp critique of social tensions: racism, anti-Semitism, political distrust, and the breakdown of community respect. Her tirade includes references to Hitler, Roosevelt, and racial purity, reflecting the deep social divisions and anxieties of the time and place. This passage grounds the surreal elements in harsh social realities.
  • The chapter ends with a description of the hotel lobby and the landlady, a large woman with a goiter, who speaks in a dialect mixing resignation and fatalism. She describes the household’s routines, the landlord, Tabitha the muted chambermaid, and the decay of the place, including the absence of the priest and the death of the old watchdog. This evokes themes of decline, abandonment, and the persistence of ritual and routine in the face of decay.
  • The final image of Tabitha kneeling to wash stones on the porch, her humble greeting "Good-morning," provides a moment of quiet humanity amid the chapter’s chaos and despair. This scene symbolizes cleansing, persistence, and perhaps hope or renewal in the "pale spring air".

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Mr. Weed (The Christian Hangman)

  • A man who has hanged many murderers but refuses to hang women or those far gone in pregnancy.
  • His wife, Marie, hanged herself during pregnancy, which haunts him deeply.
  • Mr. Weed is portrayed as a tragic figure, burdened by his past and haunted by ghostly children of the hanged.
  • Vera contemplates whether Mr. Weed might hang himself but concludes he waits for dawn to protect his children from danger 892.

Dr. Justice O’Leary

  • A once-respected doctor, now mad and living in a chaotic state.
  • His office/bedroom is cluttered with mismatched women’s shoes, birth instruments, and medical books, reflecting his mental disarray.
  • He delivers “imaginary babies” in a barren countryside, symbolizing futile or delusional efforts to bring life or hope.
  • He suffers from senility and hallucinations, including the presence of his dead sister Sarah and the chambermaid Tabitha.
  • Despite madness, he retains some medical knowledge and authority.
  • There are rumors of a “pregnant man” patient, blending reality and fantasy.

Tabitha

  • The chambermaid, also mentally unstable (“too crazy to be on relief”).
  • She cares for the doctor at night, hoping to get a baby by him.
  • She represents the marginalized and the caretakers in the chaotic environment.

Tavern Landlady

  • A large, fat woman with a goiter, representing a figure rooted in reality amidst chaos.
  • She speaks of the hotel’s management, the old watch dogs, the landlord, and the lack of interference in the hotel’s strange happenings.
  • Her speech is laced with local dialect and vivid imagery, adding texture to the scene.
  • Symbolically, the Tavern Landlady can be interpreted as representing the fading old America or traditional American society.
  • As a former circus fat lady, she recalls a form of popular entertainment and spectacle that had its heyday in the earlier part of the 20th century and before, symbolizing a bygone era of American culture rooted in simpler, rural, or small-town life.
  • The Landlady’s association with the old watch dog, who was a devout Catholic attending church even during harsh weather, and the mention of the priest who died in the landlord’s arms, indicates a decline of traditional religious and communal values. This suggests a metaphor for the waning influence of organized religion and community cohesion in mid-20th century America.
  • The Tavern Landlady is a symbol of resignation, decay, and perseverance. She embodies the physical and moral deterioration of the hotel and its inhabitants but also carries a quiet intelligence and a grudging acceptance of her circumstances. Her speech and presence vividly evoke the liminal, haunted atmosphere of the hotel lobby and the larger community, where life persists amid ruin and silence.

Vera Cartwheel

  • An observer and participant, reflecting on the surreal and tragic elements of the setting.
  • Vera, the narrator, emerges as a deeply contemplative and psychologically complex figure, marked by a blend of wakefulness, memory, and a searching, somewhat haunted awareness.
  • Vera is awake in the cold, grey morning, unable or unwilling to sleep despite the silence and stillness around her. Her state is described as "stiff with wakefulness, with ceaseless memory" and "wide awake, alert, watchful," showing a restless mind that is both physically and mentally active rather than at rest. This wakefulness seems not merely physical but also symbolic, representing a state of consciousness probing the mysteries and uncertainties of her surroundings and her own psyche.
  • Throughout the chapter, Vera’s thoughts drift into rich, imaginative flights and reflections. She muses on the Christian hangman and his tragic personal history, pondering whether he might hang himself or wait for dawn because of his children playing at hanging themselves. These ruminations blend reality with dreamlike speculation, demonstrating her tendency to think metaphorically and symbolically. Vera also reflects on her mother’s pearls, linking them to ideas of loss, infinity, and the elusive nature of reality and memory.
  • Vera is portrayed as a traveler or outsider in this strange, desolate Indiana hotel and its surroundings. She is "some time past the ambiguity of dawn," wearing travel-stained clothes and carrying her mother’s lace and pearls, symbols of a "lost estate" and an unrealized past. Her presence in the hotel is that of someone searching for something: wild flowers of spring, beauty, or perhaps a deeper meaning amid the decay and disorder around her. This sense of quest is tinged with uncertainty and disillusionment, as the landscape and people she encounters are far removed from her expectations or hopes.
  • Vera is a keen observer of the strange and unsettling characters she encounters, especially the doctor, Dr. O’Leary, and the hangman. She notes the doctor’s madness and the surreal disorder of his office and life, filled with instruments, scattered shoes of various types and eras, and imaginary patients. Vera reflects on the blending of reality and fantasy, the doctor’s second childhood, and the presence of "imaginary babies" and a "pregnant man," indicating her awareness of the blurred lines between sanity and madness, reality and hallucination.
  • Vera listens to the angry widow who confronts her, accusing her of being a "tramp girl" who kept her awake all night, and to Tabitha, the muted chambermaid who quietly carries out her duties. Despite these confrontations and the vivid descriptions of the inhabitants’ misery and anger, Vera seems somewhat detached as an intruder or observer rather than a participant. She tries to explain herself logically but acknowledges the complexity and strangeness of the place and its people.
  • Vera’s narrative voice often becomes poetic and philosophical, contemplating themes of time, memory, loss, and the impermanence of life. She notes how the hotel room expands during sleeplessness into a vast landscape of memory and future, blending space and time. Her thoughts on the pearls, on the dreamlike quality of the world, and on the symbolic meaning of the doctor's books and instruments show a mind attuned to deeper existential questions.
  • Vera is a searching, sensitive, and somewhat alienated narrator, caught between wakefulness and dreams, reality and fantasy, memory and the present. Her observations are rich with symbolism and emotional depth, portraying her as a figure grappling with the mysteries of life, madness, and the passage of time in a world that is both concrete and surreal.

Catherine Cartwheel

  • Catherine is a figure deeply immersed in a subjective, dream-like reality, detached from the conventional world. Her character is defined by her peculiar philosophies, eccentric habits, and a luxurious but illusory existence that profoundly shapes Vera's own journey and desires.
  • Catherine is often referred to by Vera as "my sleeping mother", suggesting a state of perpetual dreaming or detachment from waking life. Her physical presence is limited; she "had not walked for many years because of the birthmark staining her cheek and because of her fear of the wavering floor, the sea sweeping in, and her dead past". This immobility further anchors her to her internal world, within her great, "sea-blackened house with gold towers and cornices", which, despite its grandeur, is described as being "gone", implying its existence is more in memory or illusion than current reality.
  • Her unique philosophical outlook is evident in her beliefs about her "real pearls," which, she claimed, "could not be lost or stolen or sold. Whatever the human, unhuman situation might be, those real pearls could not really be lost, and I should wear them, for they were her lost estate". She believed that losing pearls paradoxically led to their multiplication, creating "infinities of pearls," a concept she explained as "just simple arithmetic, simple addition". This belief system defies logic, suggesting a mind that operates on its own fantastical rules. She also held a profound view of existence, stating that it was "probably not... eternity, something everlasting, this being so temporary a world, the world already gone. Only the shadow... ever does come here".
  • Catherine's eccentricity is vividly illustrated through her interaction with a thief who broke into her house one winter night. Far from being alarmed, she "smilingly greeted him, extending her ivory-colored hand, asking him how far he had come, and was the world truly dead, and had offered him drugged sherry". She engaged him in a languorous conversation, "sympathizing with her that she was rich, just as she had sympathized with him that he was poor". In a grand, yet impractical, gesture, she offered to "finance a Revolution" with a blank check, contingent on the thief naming "an exact sum needed and an exact date," which, caught in her "shimmering enchantment," he could not do. Her generosity extended to outfitting the thief in an aristocratic gentleman's costume, including a "high silk opera hat," "long white motor coat," and "white flowers behind his ears," symbolizing the "coming Revolution" and making him "the melting snowman upon the first day of spring". This act demonstrates her theatricality and her desire to transform reality according to her own grand, symbolic visions.
  • Vera carries the tangible and intangible legacy of her mother. She wears Catherine's "black velvet which had been her abandoned shroud," her "lace collar and the lace cuffs," and the "necklace of pearls". These items are not just mementos but symbols of her mother's unique existence and Vera's connection to that past. However, Vera's own desire for "something quite plain and poor and bare" and her search for "order, as if it were a beautiful island in the midst of life" suggest a yearning for a reality distinct from her mother's opulent, yet ultimately vanished, dream-world. Catherine Cartwheel, therefore, represents a captivating, almost mythical past for Vera, a world of intricate illusions and profound detachment, which Vera both inherits and seeks to transcend.

The Italian Thief

  • The Italian thief appears in Vera's memories of her mother Catherine's stories. He breaks into her mother's sea-blackened house one winter night, expecting it to be empty, but is surprised to find her there. Instead of robbing her, he is charmed by her and ends up talking with her until morning.
  • The thief is easily taken aback by Catherine's presence and charm. Instead of sticking to his original plan, he is willing to engage in conversation and accept her hospitality.
  • He sympathizes with Vera's mother being rich, just as she sympathizes with him being poor.
  • He does not name an exact sum or date for the Revolution, and he allows Catherine to outfit him in ridiculous clothes.
  • The thief is ultimately unsuccessful in his original goal of robbing Catherine, and he leaves with nothing of value, only a bizarre costume.
  • He is a figure in Catherine's dreamlike world, who is transformed from a thief into a "pale, aristocratic gentleman" destined to be "the melting snowman upon the first day of spring". This suggests that he represents the potential for transformation and the blurring of reality and illusion.
  • The thief, described as wearing an extravagant costume, "a high silk opera hat with a furry rim, a long white motor coat, glass medallions, ribbons, white trousers, white shoes, white flowers behind his ears" is outfitted by Catherine as a "pale, aristocratic gentleman" and is connected symbolically with the coming Revolution and the melting snowman of spring, implying a transition or transformation fraught with sorrow.
  • This figure is portrayed in a way that suggests a complex symbolism beyond just a literal character: he is an "Italian thief" who is served with luxury and kindness, almost unreal in the context of the snowy, desolate setting, and his presence is tied to themes of loss, dreams, and political upheaval. Catherine’s narrative about the thief financing a Revolution but unable to name an exact sum or date further evokes the ambiguity and uncertainty of political change.
  • While the text does not explicitly state that the Italian thief represents Italy in World War II, the symbolic elements strongly suggest an allegorical reading. Italy, during and after World War II, was a nation undergoing profound social and political turmoil and revolution, transitioning from fascism toward a new order. The thief’s aristocratic garb and his association with both crime and revolution could metaphorically reflect Italy’s complex role both as a participant in conflict and as a nation facing internal upheaval and rebirth.
  • The Italian thief may symbolically represent Italy’s ambiguous and troubled position around the time of World War II, embodying themes of loss, transformation, and the unpredictable nature of revolution as seen through Vera’s dream-like, poetic narration. The Italian thief can be read as a symbolic figure representing Italy in the wartime and revolutionary context, reflecting the larger motifs of change and sorrow threaded through the narrative.

The Woman in Curlers

  • The woman in metallic curlers emerges as a striking, intense figure who confronts Vera with anger and a sense of moral indignation. Her appearance and demeanor are vividly described: she is "half dressed," with her face marked by a contrasting complexion, one cheek lit by a "high red light," the other ashen which emphasizes her disturbed, volatile state. She approaches Vera blocking her path, "her mouth opening, closing," and her face "contorted with a cold rage," suggesting deep frustration and emotional turmoil.
  • This woman accuses Vera of being one of "those tramp girls," implicating her in keeping a "decent woman awake all night." Her repeated insistence on Vera’s shamefulness reveals her rigid moral stance and perhaps a fear or resentment of those she perceives as disrupting social order or propriety within the hotel. Her anger is not merely personal but tied to a broader anxiety about the chaos and unrest around her, symbolized by "couples screaming," "hitch-hikers," "ice-skaters," and "marathon dancers" who disturb her peace and the sanctity of "marriage bonds." This expresses her profound desire for stability and respectability in a world she sees as increasingly wild and disrespectful.
  • The woman reveals her own vulnerable position: she is a widow, isolated and grieving, with a husband who "died before he ever came to bed," heightening her sense of loneliness and despair. Her bitter outburst against "the other doctor, Simeon...the dirty Jew," and her invocation of Hitler and racial and religious prejudices reveal her bigoted attitudes and the pervasive social tensions in the community. This further complicates her character, showing how her personal suffering is entangled with broader social fears, xenophobia, and political resentments that color her worldview.
  • Despite her harshness toward Vera and others, the woman’s fury and bitterness also suggest a deep-seated fear of abandonment and loss of control. Her rage can be seen as defensive, a way to assert agency in a situation where she feels powerless. The fact that she knows Vera and quickly labels her indicates a community that is closely knit but fraught with judgment and suspicion.
  • The woman in curlers is portrayed as a complex character embodying anger, fear, loneliness, and prejudice. She stands as a symbol of the social unrest and personal despair pervading the novel's setting, and her confrontation with Vera reveals the tensions between individual desires and communal expectations in a fragile, decaying world.
  • The woman in metallic curlers who confronts Vera on the stairs of the hotel is depicted with a face "contorted with a cold rage," half-dressed and aggressively barring Vera's path. She accuses Vera of being one of the "tramp girls" who keep a decent woman awake all night, and she voices vehemently racist and anti-Semitic views, blaming Jews, communists, and Roosevelt for various social ills, and asserting white supremacist beliefs, including a denial of African Americans ("There are no Negroes in this county... We don’t want mulattoes"). She even endorses Hitler's ideology, saying "Hitler is right," and rants about conspiracies involving Jews and communism.
  • Symbolically, this woman can be interpreted as representing a segment of mid-20th century American society characterized by reactionary, xenophobic, and racist attitudes that were prevalent especially in certain rural or less urbanized areas before the Civil Rights Movement culminated in legislative changes by 1964. Her vehement defense of "traditional" social orders, expressed through racial purity, anti-communism, and religious intolerance, echoes the nativist, segregationist, and anti-Semitic sentiments that persisted in parts of the United States, particularly in the South and Midwest, during the early-to-mid 1900s. This character embodies the cultural anxieties and social resistance to change that would be challenged by the movements for civil rights, integration, and social reform leading up to and following 1964.
  • Her role as a gatekeeper figure barring Vera who might symbolize change, outsider perspectives, or marginalized identities underscores the entrenched social barriers and hostility faced by those who threatened the status quo. The woman's aggressive posture and speech serve as a stark personification of intolerance and oppression in the historical context before the transformative legislation of the Civil Rights Act of 1964.
  • The woman in curlers symbolically represents the reactionary, racist, and xenophobic elements in American society prior to 1964, embodying the cultural resistance to social progress and equality that defined much of U.S. social history before the Civil Rights era reforms.

Simeon the Doctor

  • He is described through the lens of local prejudice and suspicion, particularly voiced by a woman in the hotel who vehemently refuses to go to him for medical care. This woman expresses strong antisemitic views, calling Simeon "the dirty Jew" and accusing Jews of taking everything and running the world. She recounts that "Hitler ran him out of Germany" and refuses to let Simeon "paw all over her".

Research: Resources

  • The pearls are repeatedly connected to Vera's mother and her legacy. They represent the "lost estate" of her mother, something precious yet intangible that Vera inherits and wears, symbolizing the remnants of the past and the enduring connection to her mother's life and identity. The pearls are described as "real pearls which... would bring no more than cultivated pearls" in the market, suggesting a contrast between their material and symbolic value. The pearls also symbolize continuity amid loss and the passing of time. Vera’s mother believed that the pearls could not be lost or stolen, and that their number paradoxically increased with loss, implying an infinite or eternal quality beyond physical reality. This suggests a theme of immortality or persistence beyond death, where loss in one sense begets gain in another "the more pearls there were" despite losses in life. The novel often blurs the line between dream and reality, and pearls, like many other images, are part of this interplay. Pearls are associated with the ephemeral and the eternal, with illusions and truths mingling. They appear alongside motifs of opium dreams, ghosts, and phantoms, symbolizing the fragile and shifting nature of existence and memory. The pearls are linked to the theme of identity, as something that persists yet changes, much like Vera’s mother herself, who is simultaneously present and absent, real and dreamlike. The pearls become a metaphor for what remains of a person’s essence when all else has faded or been lost. The imagery of pearls dripping or cascading like tears, linked to the mother’s lifeless but radiant hands, suggests mourning and the cycle of life and death. Pearls are connected to sorrow ("the pity, pity, pity of nothing, of something"), highlighting their role as symbols of grief and the preciousness of what is gone.
  • Vera pictured Marie hanging and her unborn baby hanging by its umbilical cord. Pregnant Black women were among the many victims of lynching during the era of racial terror in the United States. While the exact number is not known, the case of Mary Turner is one of the most widely documented instances of this particularly brutal violence. In May 1918, a white farmer named Hampton Smith was killed by one of his Black employees, Sidney Johnson, in Brooks County, Georgia. An enraged white mob began a week-long rampage, killing several Black people, including Mary Turner's husband, Hayes Turner. After her husband was lynched, a grieving Mary Turner, who was eight months pregnant, publicly denounced the murder and threatened to have the mob members arrested. Her statement enraged the mob, which turned its fury on her. On May 19, 1918, a mob of hundreds of people captured Turner and dragged her to Folsom Bridge over the Little River. There, they tied her ankles, hung her upside down from a tree, and set her on fire. While she was still alive, a man cut open her abdomen with a knife, causing her unborn baby to fall to the ground. A mob member then stomped the baby to death. Turner's body was then riddled with hundreds of bullets. No one was ever held accountable for the murders of Mary Turner and her unborn child. The grotesque nature of her lynching revealed the level of dehumanization and impunity with which Black women were treated during this period. Her case was widely publicized by the NAACP and became a symbol in the anti-lynching movement. The lynching of Black women is a less-documented aspect of racial terror, but historians have uncovered evidence that it was not uncommon. The violence against Black women, especially pregnant women like Mary Turner, served as a tool of white supremacy to terrorize the Black community and suppress any form of dissent. The sexualized and gendered violence inflicted upon women's bodies was also used to communicate power and control. Many instances of women being lynched were underreported or not documented at all. The details of Mary Turner's lynching only became widely known because of the investigation by Walter F. White of the NAACP and the subsequent national anti-lynching campaign. The murder of Mary Turner remains a potent and horrific symbol of the racial and gender-based violence that was inflicted upon Black women during the era of racial terror.
  • The thief’s being dressed up and changed but melting with the spring sounds familiar to the Frosty the Snowman song written by Walter "Jack" Rollins and Steve Nelson, and first recorded by Gene Autry and the Cass County Boys in 1950 and later recorded by Jimmy Durante. A book and short film were also released that year based on the song. A group of children find a hat and place it on Frosty's head. Frosty laughs and plays with the children until the hot sun threatens to melt him. Frosty says goodbye to the children, reassuring them he’ll be back someday. Although it is generally regarded as a Christmas song, the original lyrics make no mention of the holiday. The song supposedly takes place in White Plains, New York, or Armonk, New York. Armonk has a parade dedicated to Frosty annually.
  • "Revolution" serves as a metaphor for a transformative change or upheaval Catherine imagines or aspires to finance. It aligns with the novel’s broader themes of disillusionment, social change, and the tension between dreams and reality. The Revolution here can be interpreted as a symbol of radical change (personal, social, or political) that Catherine contemplates amidst her dreamlike, drug-induced state and the decaying world around her. The promise to finance a Revolution is less about a concrete political movement and more about Catherine's fantasy or symbolic gesture of power, influence, and change in a world she perceives as lost or slipping away.
  • "After all, he was an Italian thief" By specifying that the thief was Italian, Catherine invokes a common ethnic stereotype: at that time and in many literary works, Italians were often portrayed as charming, passionate, and sometimes criminal or roguish figures. The label "Italian thief" carries connotations of romanticism, cunning, and a certain exotic, theatrical flair. In the story, this adds a layer of color and character to the thief, distinguishing him from a generic burglar and situating the encounter within a broader cultural imagination. Catherine’s world is heavily dreamlike and infused with fantasy, illusions, and theatricality. The thief’s Italian identity fits this atmosphere, lending a sense of drama and exoticism to the otherwise quiet, snow-blanketed house. It underscores the surreal quality of the encounter: where a criminal becomes a guest, sipping sherry and talking about revolutions, dressed in the finery Catherine provided. By choosing to highlight the thief’s nationality and by outfitting him as a "pale, aristocratic gentleman" with a silk opera hat, white motor coat, and white flowers behind his ears, Catherine asserts a kind of control and agency over the narrative. She transforms the thief from a mere criminal into a figure of aristocratic elegance and revolutionary potential, playing with identities and expectations. The Italian thief is not just a criminal but a symbol of possibility, subversion, and transformation. Catherine is herself a figure caught between reality and dream, between past grandeur and present decay. The Italian thief episode reflects her tendency to blur lines between fact and fantasy, to rewrite narratives with theatrical embellishments. By stressing "Italian," she situates the episode in a world of cultural richness and romantic possibility, contrasting with the harsh realities outside her doors. Catherine’s emphasis on "he was an Italian thief" is a deliberate narrative choice to evoke a particular image and mood: that of a charming, exotic, somewhat romanticized figure who becomes part of her dreamlike, theatrical domain. It reflects her complex psychological state and her inclination to infuse her recollections with drama, fantasy, and symbolic meaning, transforming a criminal intrusion into an almost magical encounter.
  • The scattered women’s shoes, all mateless, are highly symbolic. Shoes generally suggest journeys, paths, or the presence and absence of people, and the fact that these shoes are mateless highlights a sense of incompleteness, loss, or fragmentation. Each shoe appears to come from a different era and place (white canvas tennis shoes, green opera pumps, old women’s laced shoes, buckskin shoes with buttons, gold Grecian sandals) suggesting a collage of many lives, times, and experiences, all disconnected and isolated in this one room. This could symbolize the scattered, fragmented nature of the doctor’s mind or the fragmented realities of the women who come to him: real or imaginary. The shoes' variety and solitary nature imply absence: where are the wearers? The doctor deals with pregnancies and deliveries, but many of his "patients" are imaginary or unreal, and the mateless shoes evoke the idea of incomplete or failed births, lost children, or the ghostly presence of women who are not fully there. The shoes are remnants or traces, much like the doctor’s practice itself: delivering "imaginary babies" in a "barren countryside where nothing grew." This barren setting and the doctor’s madness emphasize infertility, loss, and the failure of creation or procreation. The doctor’s clothes hanging alone, despite his complaint of having only one suit, add to the sense of mystery and absence.   • What’s Wrong with Democracy? Critiques of democracy existed across a wide range of historical periods and came from diverse intellectual and social movements. These arguments addressed everything from the flaws of ancient Greek democracy to the systemic exclusions within American democracy before the Civil Rights era. Philosophers like Plato warned that in a democracy, the masses, driven by ignorance and emotion, could become susceptible to self-serving demagogues. This "rule by the mob" could lead to rash, unjust decisions, such as the execution of Socrates in ancient Athens. Later, thinkers like Thomas Hobbes argued that democracy leads to chaos and civil war, advocating for a strong, singular sovereign to prevent societal collapse. Some classical and 19th-century thinkers, including Plato, Thomas Carlyle, and John Ruskin, argued that society should be led by a knowledgeable elite rather than the common people. This perspective asserted that only a select few possessed the wisdom and virtue necessary to govern effectively, and that the majority of people were ill-equipped for the complexities of statecraft. As democracy expanded during the Industrial Revolution, critics like Carlyle, John Ruskin, and William Lecky argued that it produced anarchy and chaos within modern industrial society. They believed that democratic capitalism prioritized profit and individual self-interest over social stability and the public good. Commentators like Matthew Arnold expressed concern that democracy led to a flattening of culture and a decline in intellectual standards. He feared that the "average" tastes of the masses would displace high culture and that society would suffer from a lack of moral and intellectual leadership. Before the passage of the Civil Rights Act in 1964, critics pointed out the hypocrisy of American democracy, which systematically excluded many of its own citizens. This was particularly evident in the Jim Crow South, where discriminatory laws like poll taxes and literacy tests disenfranchised millions of African Americans and poor whites. The history of American democracy has been marked by periods of deep instability and division. Scholars point to the 1890s and the aftermath of the Compromise of 1877, which ended Reconstruction, as periods of "backsliding" where racial conflict produced significant damage to democratic ideals. This history was built on the foundation of racial exclusion, as some historians note that democratic norms only took hold among white people after racial equality was removed from the agenda. Especially during the Gilded Age (1865–1915), the rise of powerful, and often corrupt, political machines led many Americans to question the health of their democracy. Critiques focused on the manipulation of the voting process, the influence of money in politics, and the resulting feeling that elections were no longer fair or truly representative of the people. The failure of some interwar and post-WWII democratic governments, such as France's Fourth Republic, was often cited as a failure of democracy itself. Frequent changes in government and a fractured party system led many to conclude that democracy was inherently unstable and inefficient. The Civil Rights Act marks a significant turning point in the realization of American democratic ideals.
