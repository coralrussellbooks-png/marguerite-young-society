Research notes: Chapter 18 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 18

Characters: Miss MacIntosh

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

Miss MacIntosh is simultaneously and irresolvably multiple throughout these pages. She is nursemaid and rapist, woman and man, the familiar and the terrifying stranger. The narrator explicitly names this:

"It was neither man nor woman but some angelic demon, the flesh and blood with features missing, with senses missing, and yet with something more than sense had provided."

This is not metaphor for confusion. It is ontological statement. Miss MacIntosh does not have a hidden true self that the night reveals. Rather, the night reveals that selfhood was always this: plural, simultaneous, irresolvable. The red-headed nursemaid of the daytime was not a lie concealing the bald figure of the night. Both are equally real, equally constructed, equally partial.

The narrator's own self is equally multiple. She is the child who trusted, the witness who sees, the lover who grieves, the guilty one who stumbled on a secret. These selves do not reconcile:

"I am not sure now that Miss MacIntosh did say all I remember her as saying in that night's passage when nothing seemed certain."

Memory itself produces a plural narrator: the one who experienced and the one who reconstructs, and neither can claim priority.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

This pillar is perhaps the chapter's most explicit preoccupation. The text does not simply say that illusion was present and is now stripped away. It says something more radical: that the stripping away is itself another illusion, that there is no bedrock beneath.

"That night, the curtain of illusion had been stripped away, it seemed, even as if the illusion were the only reality, and our self-deception was the only rose."

The phrase "it seemed" is doing enormous work here. The revelation is not stable. And the formulation (the illusion were the only reality, and our self-deception was the only rose) does not mourn this. The rose does not become less beautiful because it is made of self-deception. It becomes differently beautiful. More honestly beautiful.

Miss MacIntosh herself insists on this from within the encounter:

"Nothing that you think is happening is happening! Nothing that you think is true is true. Nothing that you see is real!"

But this is not a reassurance. It is a philosophical position delivered in extremis, by a bald woman who has just committed violence, which makes it simultaneously more and less credible. The text refuses to arbitrate. The cave has no outside. There is only, as the text says, "a larger cave more carefully lit."

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The chapter is saturated with a grief that has no object and no resolution; it is simply the atmosphere in which everything occurs. Miss MacIntosh's litany of losses is not organized toward catharsis:

"Oh, where's my old heart? Oh, where's my hair?"

"Oh, how ephemeral are we! Oh, how transient were we, my child! The earth has known us not. Our mothers and our fathers never knew us."

These are not cries that expect answers. They are the accumulation of grief as weather: the permanent condition of existing in time, of loving things that change, of being a body that loses its hair, its breast, its youth, its certainty. The missing left breast is not a dramatic revelation but one more item in the inventory of what is gone. It joins the hair, the heart, the Iowa of her childhood, the red wig on the chair post.

The narrator's grief is equally structureless:

"I knew even then, as she held me in her arms, that our proximity was doomed, that we must part, that she must always leave me."

The grief is already present before the parting. It is not produced by loss. It is the climate of attachment itself.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator's fundamental stance throughout this chapter is that of the witness who stays in the room. She does not flee, does not rationalize, does not rush toward resolution. She continues to look at the bald head even as Miss MacIntosh insists there is nothing to see:

"I am not sure now that Miss MacIntosh did say all I remember her as saying... Perhaps, in retrospection, every lost event must be changed by the illumination larger than that of any instant."

This is the ethics of witnessing made explicit: the witness knows their own perception is imperfect, knows memory distorts, and continues to witness anyway. The refusal to simplify is the act.

The chapter's extraordinary length (its refusal to summarize the night, its insistence on rendering every turn of Miss MacIntosh's voice, every sensation of the encounter) is itself a formal enactment of this pillar. Young does not write: "That night I discovered Miss MacIntosh was bald and something terrible and tender happened." She stays in the room for pages, refusing the shortcut.

The narrator also witnesses her own cruelty:

"For I had been cruel to her, and I had discovered her essential baldness."

The witness does not exempt herself. Seeing fully includes seeing one's own participation in what is seen.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The chapter begins and ends in uncertainty, but it is not the same uncertainty. The narrator at the opening says she cannot be sure what happened; the narrator at the close is still uncertain but has built something from the uncertainty: a way of being charitable, a mark on her forehead, a commitment to remembering.

Miss MacIntosh herself enacts recursive construction in her monologue. She returns again and again to the same proverbs, the same geographical questions, the same invocations of Iowa, but each return is slightly altered by what has accumulated:

"Where, oh, where’s Miss MacIntosh, that old fool we used to see here?"

She asks after herself in the third person. She is building a version of herself even as she loses the materials. The building is never finished. The proverbs keep starting over.

The promise of the morning (that everything will return to normal, that the schedule will resume) is explicitly a recursive gesture:

"In the morning, everything would be all right, shipshape... two simple wayfarers in a thorny way, two grey pilgrims climbing the naked rock."

This is not delusion presented for mockery. It is the human necessity of returning to the beginning with what one has learned, of building again knowing the building is provisional.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter's formal structure is itself an argument for this pillar. Sentences extend for half a page. Qualifications multiply. The narrator explicitly refuses to conclude:

"I am not sure now that Miss MacIntosh did say all I remember her as saying."

This phrase appears twice, bracketing the central encounter. It is not a disclaimer. It is an ethical position: the experience is too large for the sentence that would contain it. To conclude would be to do violence to what actually occurred.

Miss MacIntosh's monologue is structurally incomplete; it circles, interrupts itself, asks questions it cannot answer, shifts register from the cosmic to the domestic without warning. This is not presented as her failure. It is presented as the honest shape of a consciousness confronting its own limits:

"Stuff, fiddle, and balderdash—she suddenly exclaimed... what was she saying?"

She loses the thread of her own argument. The unfinished sentence is the honest one.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Miss MacIntosh is the chapter's central evidence for this pillar. She exists at the edge of every category: woman/man, familiar/terrifying, sane/mad, alive/dead ("Long ago, I died and was not buried"), clothed/unclothed, haired/bald. Her marginality is not incidental to her meaning; it is her meaning.

"Who was that old rapist if not Miss MacIntosh, she who had warned me against him."

This sentence performs the ontology of the marginal: the category of rapist and the category of nursemaid who warned against rapists collapse into the same person. The categories themselves are revealed as constructions. Miss MacIntosh is not the exception to the rule. She is the evidence that the rule was always a construction.

The missing breast is another marginal marker: the body that does not conform to what a woman's body is supposed to be, the body that has been altered by forces the text does not explain, the body that is "that knob where the breast was missing". This is not presented as tragedy or as medical fact. It is presented as one more way in which Miss MacIntosh exists at the edge of the categories we use to organize persons.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The chapter's treatment of memory and the past is precisely sedimentary. The past does not recede. It accumulates beneath the narrator and continues to press upward:

"There is no new experience which shall not have emerged from the old. There is no surprise but of our own slow-wittedness, our own failures in understanding that the thing was always present."

The bald head was always there beneath the red wig. The violence was always there beneath the lessons. The grief was always there beneath the routine. The night does not introduce something new. It reveals what was already deposited in the sediment.

Miss MacIntosh's own monologue is sedimentary: layer upon layer of proverb, geography, scripture, folk wisdom, personal lament, all pressing against each other without hierarchy:

"Dead men tell no tales... God pity us. God pity the small fishes and the great whales beached on this beach."

These are not random. They are the accumulated layers of a consciousness formed by a particular culture, a particular time, a particular body of losses. The proverbs are not wisdom. They are sediment: the compressed residue of everything that was handed down to her.

The mark left on the narrator's forehead is the chapter's most precise image of sedimentary time:

"There, that will teach you to remember me."

The past is not behind. It is inscribed on the body, pressed into the skin, carried forward as physical fact.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter is acutely self-conscious about what language can and cannot do. The opening meditation is essentially a treatise on language's failure:

"Memory is surrounded by the unknown, the void, and there is so much that we have not heard, much that we have not seen... Memory sometimes provides the one flower more than ever blossomed. Memory sometimes omits the only flower there really was."

Language here is not a neutral vehicle. It adds flowers that were not there and removes the ones that were. The narrator's language knows its own location; it speaks from the position of retrospection, of uncertainty, of someone who was young and is no longer, of someone who was there and cannot fully recover what being there meant.

Miss MacIntosh's language is equally self-aware of its own constructedness. Her proverbs are explicitly marked as borrowed, as received, as coming from "the World Almanac or farmers' journals". She speaks in a language that was handed to her, and she knows it:

"Stuff, fiddle, and balderdash—she suddenly exclaimed... what was she saying?"

She catches herself speaking in received language and rejects it, then has nothing to replace it with. The honest language names its own location. The location here is: a woman who has lost her hair and her breast and her certainty, speaking in the only language available to her, which is not adequate to what she is.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter lives entirely within the immanent: within the bald head, the false teeth clicking, the missing breast, the cold hands, the wet kiss on the eyelids. There is no gesture toward a beyond. The transcendence, when it appears, points deeper into the ordinary:

"Our nakedness is God's nakedness."

This is not a metaphor for spiritual elevation. It is a statement of immanent transcendence: the naked, bald, one-breasted body of an old nursemaid is the sacred. God is not above this scene. God is bald in this scene.

The chapter's repeated invocations of God are not prayers directed outward. They are recognitions of the sacred within the wreckage:

"God pity poor sailors with their faces upturned on the leaden waters at dawn. God pity the poor streetwalker whose heart is virginal... and pity us all, for we are all alike in our being mortal and lost."

The pity is not divine condescension from above. It is the quality of attention that the immanent sacred requires: the willingness to see the streetwalker, the sailor, the bald nursemaid, and not look away. The sacred is here, in the grey light, in the spotted head, in the false teeth clacking.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This pillar finds its most concentrated expression in the chapter's final image and in Miss MacIntosh's last speech. The narrator is constituted by what was deposited before she arrived, by Miss MacIntosh's lessons, her proverbs, her violence, her tenderness, her baldness. And she is constituting the ground that comes after:

"Ever since, in a moment of extreme revelation when I have seen that people are not exactly what they have seemed to be, I have tried to be charitable."

The mark on her forehead is the crossing point made visible: the place where Miss MacIntosh's time crossed the narrator's time and left a physical trace that carries forward.

Miss MacIntosh's final words make the structural obligation explicit:

"I have lost my hair in your service. When I came here, I had all my own hair, a thick mane of it, pale gold hanging down my back... I am no older than the day I was born. Age is the most difficult thing to achieve."

She is simultaneously the ancestor depositing into the sediment and the child who has not yet grown old. She is where time crosses itself. The carrying continues: the narrator will carry the mark, the charitable habit, the memory of the bald head, the knowledge that nothing is what it seems, forward into a future Miss MacIntosh will not see.

The chapter ends not with resolution but with this structural obligation intact and unresolved. The ancestors are in the sediment. The carrying continues.
