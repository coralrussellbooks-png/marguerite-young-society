Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 73

← Chapter 72 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 74 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Esther Longtree · Joe Goldberg

The dying fathers write to Esther in this chapter, and the failure of the record to hold a woman's experience runs beneath the interrupted birth. As each man nears death he seeks assurance about the child she never brought to term, and Esther's silence in answer is protective and it is the book's erasure turned into a woman's own strategy, a mute refusal to hand these men a comfort the truth cannot give. She thinks of sending them tangible proof, a hand or a foot, and the impossibility of the gesture, the child reduced to a fragment that could be mailed, is the interrupted birth at its bleakest, motherhood proven by a piece.

The old doctor advises her to keep silent to protect male vanity, and the counsel names the whole arrangement, a woman's truth suppressed so a man's story can stand, and Esther cannot recall the father of the "little shadow boxer" or the champion featherweight at all. The forgetting is the failure of the record run backward, the man's memory dominating while the woman's experience blurs, and her solitude is the loneliness of a body that carries what no one will witness.

Joe Goldberg, the dying featherweight champion, writes to her full of nostalgia and regret, wanting a legacy through the child, and his fading strength beside Esther's endurance sets male physical triumph against male frailty, the fighter undone while the woman goes on. The graveyard where she remembers him, the encounter shrouded in violation and half-memory, is the dissolving boundary between life and death that Esther lives on, intimacy and trauma and the dead all folded into one ground she cannot fully recover.

The old mule, barren and mournful, stands for the sterility the town pins on her, and the "birth of nothing" she suffers again is the interrupted birth in its purest form, labor without a child, agony with no arrival. The white feather drifting at the chapter's close is loss and ambiguous hope at once, and Esther nurses her own fist among the lost children, a woman whose motherhood is real and unwitnessed, kept at the threshold the book reserves for everyone it will not let the world forget.
