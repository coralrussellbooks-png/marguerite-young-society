Unit 4, pp. 49-80: Paris, Brussels, Marx

Background for this unit: The History in Harp Song for a Radical

This unit carries the German exiles from Heine's deathbed in Paris to Marx's confrontation with Weitling in Brussels in 1846, then brings Weitling across the Atlantic to New York. The central event is the split between Weitling's prophetic, Christian communism and Marx's "scientific" version, a quarrel that shaped American socialism for fifty years.

Major entries

Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels (pp. 52-69, 78-79)

  • Who they were: Marx (1818-1883) was born in Trier, studied philosophy in Berlin, and married Jenny von Westphalen. Expelled from Paris in 1845, he moved to Brussels, then settled in London after 1849. Engels (1820-1895) was the son of a textile manufacturer and managed his family's mill in Manchester, which let him support Marx for decades.

  • Why it matters: Together they wrote The Communist Manifesto (1848). Engels's The Condition of the Working Class in England (1845) drew on Manchester. Marx wrote for Horace Greeley's New York Tribune from 1852 to 1862, his largest audience in his lifetime, which Young touches on at p. 61.

  • The family: Young follows the Marx household in poverty and grief. Three children died young: Heinrich Guido in 1850, Franziska in 1852, and Edgar in 1855 (p. 59). Daughters Laura and Eleanor survived and became socialists.

  • Further reading: Jonathan Sperber, Karl Marx: A Nineteenth-Century Life (2013).

The Brussels confrontation of March 1846 (pp. 67-69)

  • What happened: On March 30, 1846, Marx, Engels, Weitling, and others met in Brussels in the Communist Correspondence Committee. Marx demanded to know what doctrine Weitling was preaching. When Weitling defended stirring workers with moral appeals, Marx struck the table and shouted that ignorance had never helped anyone.

  • The witness: The Russian traveler Pavel Annenkov recorded the scene. Young draws on him for the goblets jumping on the table (p. 69).

  • Why it matters: It marked the break between the older, religious, conspiratorial communism of the artisans and Marx's theory of class struggle rooted in economics. Weitling lost, and within two years the League of the Just had become Marx's Communist League. In America the two strands kept competing, and Debs's own socialism, moral and Christian in tone, sits closer to Weitling than Young's reader might expect.

  • Further reading: Wittke, The Utopian Communist (1950), chapter 8.

The League of the Just and the Communist League (pp. 66-67)

  • What it was: A secret society of German artisans founded in Paris in 1836, with branches in London and Switzerland. Weitling was its leading theorist in the early 1840s.

  • What happened: In June 1847, in London, it reorganized as the Communist League and commissioned Marx and Engels to write its program, which became the Manifesto.

  • Where the record differs: Young mentions the International Workingmen's Association in this setting (p. 67). That body, the First International, was founded later, in London in 1864.

Weitling's first American journey, Kriege, and the League of Deliverance (pp. 72-78)

  • What happened: Hermann Kriege, a German radical in New York, invited Weitling in 1846 to help edit his paper, the Volks-Tribun. By the time Weitling arrived the paper had folded (p. 73). Weitling founded the Befreiungsbund, or League of Deliverance, in 1847 and began traveling among German workers in Philadelphia, Cincinnati, St. Louis, and elsewhere.

  • Kriege: Hermann Kriege (1820-1850) linked German radicals to the American land reform movement of George Henry Evans, which demanded free homesteads from public land. Marx and Engels issued a circular condemning him in 1846.

  • Why it matters: This is the first organized socialist politics among German-American workers, a generation before the Socialist Labor Party. The benefit societies Young describes (burial insurance, a building and loan, a singing society, p. 76) were the actual fabric of immigrant working-class life.

  • Further reading: Bruce Levine, The Spirit of 1848: German Immigrants, Labor Conflict, and the Coming of the Civil War (1992).

John Jacob Astor (pp. 79-80)

  • Who he was: Born in Walldorf, Germany, in 1763, he came to America in 1784. Family legend says he arrived with seven flutes to sell. He worked for a fur dealer, built the American Fur Company, and then bought Manhattan real estate.

  • Why it matters: At his death in 1848 he was the richest man in the United States, worth roughly twenty million dollars. His Astor House hotel (1836) was New York's grandest. For Young he is the German immigrant who succeeded by the opposite path from Weitling, and the symbol of the "money king" (p. 80).

  • Further reading: Axel Madsen, John Jacob Astor: America's First Multimillionaire (2001).

Shorter entries

  • Ludwig Börne (pp. 51-62): German-Jewish radical journalist in Paris (1786-1837), author of Letters from Paris. Heine's 1840 book on him offended Börne's companion Jeannette Wohl, and her husband Salomon Strauss fought a duel with Heine in 1841.

  • Heine and the Marx family (p. 52): Heine is credited with saving the Marxes' baby daughter Jenny during convulsions. Young places the visit in Brussels, but the episode belongs to Paris in 1844, and Heine did not visit the Marxes in Brussels.

  • George Eliot and Matthew Arnold on Heine (pp. 55-57): Eliot's essay "German Wit: Heinrich Heine" (1856) and Arnold's essay "Heinrich Heine" (1863) and poem "Heine's Grave" introduced him to English readers.

  • Alfred Meissner (pp. 64-65): Austrian poet who visited the dying Heine and wrote a memoir of him.

  • Red Sefchen (p. 55): The executioner's niece in Düsseldorf, Heine's youthful love, as he told it in his memoirs.

  • Proudhon's System of Economic Contradictions (p. 62): Proudhon's 1846 book, which Marx attacked in The Poverty of Philosophy (1847).

  • Karl Grün (p. 62): A "true socialist" whose sentimental socialism Marx and Engels mocked.

  • Ludwig Simon of Trier and Wilhelm Löwe (pp. 61-62): Members of the Frankfurt Parliament of 1848-1849 who went into exile. Löwe presided over its last session in Stuttgart. Simon attacked the dead Heine in a New York German paper, which Marx resented.

  • Joseph Weydemeyer (pp. 68, 78): Marx's ally who emigrated to New York in 1851. He published Marx's Eighteenth Brumaire there in 1852 and later served as a Union officer.

  • Robert Owen in Paris, 1848 (pp. 76-77): Owen went to revolutionary Paris and posted addresses, including a "Dialogue Between France, the World, and Robert Owen" (p. 77).

  • Weitling's Guarantees of Harmony and Freedom (p. 77): His main work (1842), a plan for a communist society run by elected councils of experts.

  • Charles Dickens (pp. 71-72): His American Notes (1842) and Martin Chuzzlewit (1843-1844) satirized American boasting and greed.

  • John Humphrey Noyes and Oneida (p. 80): Noyes founded his community in Putney, Vermont, fled adultery charges in 1847, and resettled at Oneida, New York, in 1848 (Unit 5).

  • Horace Greeley (pp. 61, 74): Editor of the New York Tribune and patron of Fourierism, known for his white hat.

Allusions

  • Diderot and Sterne (p. 49): The French encyclopedist and the English author of Tristram Shandy, both digressive writers Young admired.

  • The Tower of Babel (pp. 70-71): Genesis 11, for the confusion of tongues among the exiles.

  • Acheron (p. 64): A river of the Greek underworld.

  • Lazarus and the Kaddish (p. 64): The dying poet's poems of Lazarus, and the Jewish prayer for the dead.

  • Gulliver and the Lilliputians (p. 54): From Swift's satire.

  • Hamlet, Ophelia, Yorick (p. 79): The Marx family's love of Shakespeare.

  • Mephistopheles (p. 54): The devil in Goethe's Faust.
