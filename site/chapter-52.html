Chapter 52

On Chapter 52 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 6, 2026

Summary

Mr. Spitzer remembers closing the estate of the old suffragette, who reminds him of his own duplicity and duality. From old newspapers he learns that her wildest legends were true: she lost her lady on a mountain peak when the girl went back for a white skirt embroidered with forget-me-nots, challenged a prime minister to a duel, chained herself to a lamp-post, flew over the Alps in a plane with canvas wings until the Swiss drew up claims against her for the League of Nations, fought a golden eagle, and came back from the Holy Lands in black skirts and a turban. Searching her house by lamplight, he finds ten thousand shoe horns, an acorn in an iron safe, telephones that do not work, and forty trunks that open only after long trials with forty keys, with always one key or one lid left over. The trunks hold wedding gowns and veils, and no bride’s slippers. Believing she had wished to be the bride, he leaves the gowns out of his inventory to keep her secret. At dawn he finds she has turned a page of the book she was reading when she died, perhaps Eloise et Abelard, and he closes her eyes with two pennies, one in memory of the sun and one in memory of the moon.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a complex metaphorical description of mystery, time, and identity. Mr. Spitzer reflects on the suffragette’s lost love and her elusive, shadowy identity, “No name, no face told him her identity in cloud fusions as none told him who he was now.” This sets the tone of searching for meaning in a life shrouded in ambiguity.
  • Mr. Spitzer sees himself as connected to her through shared duplicity and duality, unable to condemn her without condemning himself. He describes himself as “one already dead in the midst of life” and the “locksmith in a street of dreams where there was neither key nor lock.” This reflects the theme of lost identity and the search for self in a confusing world.
  • Cousin Hannah is portrayed as a figure who chased a lost love, symbolized by the lost skirt on a mountain peak, a metaphor for incomplete or thwarted love: “the old suffragette had returned without her love who had gone to retrieve her skirt, surely no dog would ever find.” This image resonates through the chapter as a motif of longing and unattainable fulfillment.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s research reveals her as a woman of many paradoxes and adventures: a mountain climber, balloonist, flyer, traveler through deserts and Holy Lands, who lived beyond time and space: “She had gone to Holy Lands as to Death’s seraglio guarded by eunuchs black and white where were locked so many beautiful girls with their faces veiled.” Her life is portrayed as almost mythical, full of contradictions and mysteries.
  • The chapter meditates on the nature of time, memory, and existence. Cousin Hannah’s life is described as one without clear chronology, “with little relationship to chronology, for hers were the chronologies of all dead loves, lost loves, doomed efforts.” This connects to the idea that love, life, and death are intertwined in a timeless dance.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s physical search through her estate, opening trunks, examining keys, finding wedding gowns, veils, and other artifacts, symbolizes an attempt to unlock the mysteries of her life and, metaphorically, the mysteries of love and existence itself. The trunks “with their lids sealed by rust and great flames” and the abundance of bridal gowns and veils suggest the elusive and fragile nature of identity and memory.
  • Mr. Spitzer questions not only her identity but his own, reflecting on the blurred lines between reality and illusion, living and dead: “He questioned who he was as he questioned who she was, and doubtless this would always be the case.” This highlights the existential uncertainty permeating the chapter.
  • The suffragette is likened to a bride who has hidden away or been lost, a figure of unfulfilled destiny and love: Perhaps “she had abandoned the bridegroom at the church door.” This symbolizes the elusiveness of love and the human condition of searching for something lost or unattainable.
  • There is a recurring tension between what is real and unreal, the dream and reality, memory and oblivion. Mr. Spitzer reflects that “Perhaps the unreal and the real were one—or there was only this mask of the stars, and there was no face behind the stars.” This blurring of boundaries questions the nature of existence itself.
  • The chapter closes with a meditation on the limits of language and knowledge: “He was not certain of the book’s title now when all writing had faded ... no language contained truth, for the truth was that which could not be expressed.” This encapsulates the chapter’s theme of the ineffability of the greatest mysteries of life and love.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “Should doubt move mountains which faith had not moved, or should doubt be joined with faith when the mountains moved?” What does Mr. Spitzer come to believe, and when?
  • “Yes, there was an external world, he knew now as it departed.” Why does he believe in her adventures only after her death?
  • “There was always one lid more than there was a key, one key more than there was a lid.” What does the leftover key or lid stand for?

Characters

  • Mr. Spitzer leaves the wedding gowns out of his inventory. What does that choice say about him as a lawyer and as a mourner?
  • “She reminded him of his duplicity and his duality so that he could not condemn her unless he condemned himself.” How does Cousin Hannah mirror Mr. Spitzer and his dead twin?
  • Cousin Hannah preached against marriage and urged ladies to “divest themselves of excess luggage,” yet she kept forty trunks of bridal gowns. Was she the bride or the bridegroom, and does it matter?

Connections

  • The Swiss drew up a bill of claims against her for the League of Nations. How does Young turn one woman’s freedom into a threat to a nation’s mountains?
  • She chained herself to a lamp-post to protest “that woman was a chattel, a thing, a possession, a mere skirt.” How do coverture and the vote shape her story and her secret?
  • Why might Young end the chapter with Eloise and Abelard, Tristram and the white Yseult, “lovers never mated”?

Reflection

  • “How much more successful she had been in her failure than if only one door had opened to her knock.” Can failure leave a life larger than success?
  • “No language contained truth, for the truth was that which could not be expressed.” Is there truth beyond words?
  • He was “her only mourner among men—and he was mourning for himself.” Is all mourning partly for oneself?

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • A reflective, almost spectral figure, the investigator of the old suffragette’s estate. He embodies duality and duplicity, often questioning his own identity and existence, blurring lines between life and death, reality and dream. His role is to manage and close the estate of the old suffragette, but his journey is deeply philosophical and metaphysical.

Cousin Hannah

  • A mysterious woman shrouded in symbolism; she is a traveler, a rebel, and a figure of lost love and elusive identity. Known for her suffrage activism, she is also depicted as a wanderer through physical and metaphysical realms of mountains, deserts, and clouds. She carries the paradox of being both bride and bridegroom, representing the complex intersections of gender, love, and death.

Peron Spitzer

  • Mr. Spitzer’s dead twin is present in every list he makes, for he is “mindful always that he was two persons, himself and his dead brother, and that what appeared in one column should appear in the other.” His greatest fear is “that there might be no differing counterpart.”

Catherine Cartwheel

  • Recalled as “the opium lady” who entertains only imaginary guests, when Mr. Spitzer finds a golden teapot circled by twelve tiny golden cups as if Cousin Hannah had been entertaining someone.

Research: Themes

Mystery and Identity

See: Identity and the Self

  • The chapter is permeated with questions of identity, both Mr. Spitzer’s and the suffragette’s. Mr. Spitzer struggles to know who he is or whether he is a ghostly twin. The suffragette’s identity remains veiled, indicated by symbols like veils, masks, and cloud fusions.

Love and Loss

See: Love · Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • Central to the narrative is the motif of lost love, symbolized by the suffragette’s lost skirt and her search for love that may never be found or may be forever elusive. Love is portrayed as inseparable from death, an eternal quest that transcends time.

Life, Death, and the Afterlife

See: Death and Mortality

  • The chapter explores the boundaries between life and death, suggesting that death may multiply uncertainties and dissolve distinctions, such as gender roles and personal identities. Death is not an end but a continuation or transformation, with echoes of the past and shadows of lost loves lingering.

Time and Timelessness

See: Time

  • Time is fluid and ambiguous; events are reported out of chronological order, and the notion of “when” is elusive. The suffragette’s life and death exist in a timeless realm where beginnings and ends blur.

The External and Internal Worlds

See: Illusion and Reality

  • The suffragette’s adventures traverse both external landscapes (mountains, deserts, clouds) and internal, psychological realms. Reality and dream intertwine, with the external world mirroring internal emotional and spiritual states.

The Search and the Unattainable

See: The Search for Meaning · Keys, Doors, and Locks

  • Mr. Spitzer’s search through the suffragette’s estate, her memories, and her secrets is a metaphor for the human quest for meaning, often futile and always incomplete. The imagery of keys, trunks, and locked doors symbolizes mysteries that cannot be fully unlocked.

Women, Gender, and Marriage

See: Women, Gender, and Marriage · Women’s Suffrage

  • Cousin Hannah fought “for the overthrow of man” and urged all ladies to “divest themselves of excess luggage,” yet her forty trunks hold only wedding gowns. Mr. Spitzer cannot tell whether she dreamed of being the bridegroom or the bride, and he decides the world need not know.

Stars, Moons, and Celestial Bodies

  • Used to depict guidance, mystery, the unreachable, and the eternal. The moon, often mirrored or sunken, symbolizes reflection and illusion.

Birds and Butterflies

Music and Silence

Mountains

Research: Resources

  • “Perhaps she should be the husband and not the bride”: This carries deep symbolic meaning about the fluidity and ambiguity of roles, identities, and expectations in life, especially in relation to death and the uncertainties it brings. The narrator suggests that the woman might take on the role traditionally assigned to the husband rather than the bride. This reversal points to the breakdown of traditional social and existential roles under the overwhelming reality of death and uncertainty. Death, as a great equalizer and source of existential doubt, multiplies uncertainties in life and offers no permanent, unchanging foundation (“no firm foundation stone or unchanging lintel” or the lintel being a metaphor for the stable doorway or threshold in life). The passage implies that death disrupts the fixed roles and certainties relied on in life, including those of gender and social position. It suggests a world where the usual distinctions such as husband and bride, beginning and end, life and death, dissolve or become unstable. In such a world, the woman might embody the traditionally masculine role, symbolizing strength, authority, or active agency in the face of life’s precariousness. This line expresses a profound meditation on the instability and ambiguity of life and death, and the shifting nature of identity and roles when confronted with mortality and the unknown. Death is not just an end but a force that challenges all certainties and social constructs. This interpretation is supported by the broader themes in the novel, where identities and realities are often fluid, and characters struggle with the boundaries between life and death, reality and dream, self and other.
  • “those feathered goose barnacles”: Medieval naturalists believed that the barnacle goose hatched from the goose barnacle, a stalked shellfish that clings to driftwood, because no one had seen the geese nest; they breed in the Arctic. Gerald of Wales described the birds hanging from timber by their beaks in his Topographia Hibernica (1188), and John Gerard pictured geese dropping from a barnacle tree in his Herball (1597).
  • “Should doubt move mountains which faith had not moved”: Mr. Spitzer wonders if doubt should move mountains “which faith had not moved.” This is a direct inversion of the common saying that faith can move mountains. This highlights a core philosophical tension between believing in something despite a lack of evidence (faith) and questioning everything (doubt). Jesus told his disciples, “If ye have faith as a grain of mustard seed, ye shall say unto this mountain, Remove hence to yonder place; and it shall remove; and nothing shall be impossible unto you” (Matthew 17:20, KJV). The image of the suffragette searching for a lost skirt, leading to the death of her lover, suggests the absurdity of human pursuits. The pursuit of something seemingly trivial (the skirt) leads to tragic consequences, mirroring the existentialist idea that life is inherently meaningless, and humans create their own meaning through their actions. The description of the skirt as “white lace gleaming through snow and its little flowers which were like the memories of little loves” blurs the lines between reality and memory. It suggests that perceptions of reality are shaped by past experiences and emotions, leading to a subjective and potentially distorted understanding of the world. The mention of the suffragette crying “Long live woman! Vive la femme! Viva la donna!” in many languages, “perhaps even in Sanskrit which was the language written upon a silent snowflake,” touches on the philosophy of language. It suggests that language can be a powerful tool for expressing universal concepts (like the importance of women) but that there are also limits to what language can convey. There are “secret languages never to be translated into words,” implying that some truths are beyond the reach of verbal expression. The reference to, “the suffragette who had lost her apostle” could also be referencing the loss of an ideal form. Platonism is the belief that everything seen is a shadow or “lesser” version of a perfect form. The loss of her apostle, who could be a student or follower, is also the loss of what the suffragette sees as a perfect version of herself. The next paragraph emphasizes the individual’s choice and action in the face of an indifferent or absurd universe. The suffragette’s decision to dress as a man can be seen as an assertion of her freedom and rejection of societal norms. The fantastical images of footmen and golden stairways highlight the human tendency to create meaning and beauty in a world that may ultimately be meaningless. The contrast between Cousin Hannah’s practical clothing and the elaborate attire of other climbers could be interpreted as a reflection of the philosophical debate between idealism and materialism. The suffragette’s focus on functionality suggests a more materialistic worldview, while the other climbers’ emphasis on appearance could be seen as representing idealism. The repeated blurring of the lines between mountains and clouds, and the shifting perceptions of the moon, raise questions about the nature of reality and the limitations of human perception. The statement, “the moon had floated most amazingly distending worlds,” suggests that reality is not fixed but is shaped by individual perspectives. The image of the footman cutting a golden stairway from a frozen lake to the mountains of the moon could be an allusion to Plato’s Allegory of the Cave. The stairway could represent the philosopher’s ascent from the darkness of the cave (the material world) to the light of the sun (the realm of Forms). The mountains of the moon could symbolize a higher realm of knowledge and understanding. The paragraph’s emphasis on nature, individual experience, and the search for spiritual meaning aligns with the themes of Transcendentalism. The suffragette’s mountain climbing could be seen as a metaphor for the individual’s journey to transcend the limitations of the material world and connect with a higher power or truth. The mention of “horizons unknown as the stars floated” could also be interpreted in a nihilistic way, suggesting that there are no ultimate goals or destinations and that all human endeavors are ultimately meaningless. At first it’s a whimsical and imaginative description of mountain climbing, then, beneath the surface, it explores fundamental philosophical questions about freedom, reality, perception, and the meaning of life which invites philosophical reflection. See Plato and Platonism.

  • “the greatest St. Bernard who ever lived”: Barry (1800-1814), the most famous of the rescue dogs kept by the Augustinian canons of the Great St. Bernard Hospice in the Swiss Alps, is said to have helped rescue forty people, and the canons and their dogs saved more than two thousand travellers over two centuries. His mounted body is in the Natural History Museum of Bern, which notes that the stories of his carrying a child on his back and of his death at the hands of a soldier who took him for a wolf are legends (Natural History Museum of Bern, “Barry”). Later Cousin Hannah sleeps in shepherds’ huts with “an old St. Bernard sleeping at her feet, a Red Cross dog who had not retrieved her”; the Red Cross was founded in Geneva in 1863.
  • “It was a white skirt embroidered with forget-me-nots”: Forget-me-nots are charming, low-growing plants, known for their small, delicate blue flowers with yellow or white eyes. They typically bloom in the spring and are often found in gardens and natural settings. Forget-me-nots are widely associated with true love, remembrance, and enduring friendship. They are often given as gifts or used in remembrance ceremonies. The name “forget-me-not” is derived from a German legend about a knight who, while picking the flowers for his beloved, fell into a river and drowned, tossing the flowers to her with the cry “Vergiss mich nicht!” (Forget me not).

  • “had thought of satyrs and garlanded fawns”: Satyrs in Greek mythology are male nature spirits, companions of Dionysus, with the ears and tail of a horse or goat; the Roman fauns, half goat, followed the god Faunus.   • “some who were the beardless boys”: Many young people served as soldiers in the Civil War, with estimates suggesting that around 10% of Union soldiers were under 18.   • “Mr. Spitzer had trembled like a gambler”: This paragraph and the next use rich, poetic imagery and metaphor to explore themes of uncertainty, decay, memory, and intertwined fates. Mr. Spitzer is depicted as a liminal figure caught between reality and illusion, past and present, life and death, whose reflections on the suffragette reveal the elusive nature of truth and identity in the novel. The suffragette herself is a haunting presence (both fragile and magnetic, real and spectral) whose life and legacy resist simple comprehension, embodying the novel’s complex interplay of reality and dream. The opening lines immediately establish a world governed by chance. Mr. Spitzer trembling “like a gambler” suggests a life where outcomes are unpredictable and determined by forces beyond individual control. This aligns with existentialist themes of the absurdity of existence and the lack of inherent meaning. The “faceless card” and “star of no known number” symbolize the limits of human knowledge. No one can fully grasp the forces shaping a life, and there will always be elements of the unknown. This resonates with the existentialist emphasis on the limits of reason. The line “Yes, there was an external world, he knew now as it departed” is crucial. It indicates a loss of faith in an objective reality. The world is not fixed but is constantly slipping away, emphasizing the subjective experience. “He gave his belief when there was no reason for his belief” highlights a rejection of rationalism. Spitzer’s faith is not based on evidence or logic but on something deeper, perhaps a Kierkegaardian “leap of faith” into the absurd. “For he reserved his credulity for the grand occasions eliciting the skepticism of others. Their skepticism was his faith” is a complex statement. It suggests that Spitzer finds meaning in what others reject. This could be interpreted as a form of contrarianism, or as a deeper understanding that truth often lies in challenging conventional wisdom. See Existentialism and Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “a foot lover might have loved her”: Podophilia, also known as foot fetishism, is a sexual interest or attraction to feet.     • Coverture was a legal doctrine in common law systems where a married woman’s legal existence was subsumed into that of her husband’s, essentially making her a legal non-entity. Under coverture, a married woman (feme covert) had no independent legal rights, meaning she couldn’t own property, enter into contracts, sue or be sued, or control her own earnings. This concept, rooted in the idea that husband and wife were one legal entity, with the husband as that entity, has significantly shaped legal and social landscapes, particularly for women. William Blackstone put it plainly in his Commentaries on the Laws of England (1765): “By marriage, the husband and wife are one person in law: that is, the very being or legal existence of the woman is suspended during the marriage.” This concept formed the core of coverture, asserting that husband and wife were a single legal entity, with the husband as the representative. Coverture has roots in feudal Norman customs and English common law. It was a fundamental aspect of the legal system in early America, shaping social and legal norms. Coverture influenced not only legal practices but also societal expectations and gender roles. As the women’s rights movement gained momentum, coverture was increasingly criticized as oppressive, leading to reforms. The Married Women’s Property Acts, passed in the United States from 1839 (Mississippi) and 1848 (New York) onward, began to modify or dismantle coverture by granting women greater control over their property. Coverture was gradually weakened and eventually eliminated through legislation and legal reforms in the United States. While coverture is no longer in effect, its legacy continues to shape legal and social discussions, particularly concerning gender equality. Some legal scholars argue that vestiges of coverture persist in contemporary legal frameworks, particularly in areas like real estate and tax law. Mr. Spitzer’s inability to determine which character she identifies with (Tristram or Yseult) highlights the ambiguity and complexity of human identity and desire. The weighted eyes with pennies, “one in memory of the sun, one in memory of the moon,” symbolizes the end of perception, the inability to see or experience the world, and the finality of death. The description of the world as “one level of sheeted silver and gold” suggests a flattened, perhaps illusory, reality. This relates to philosophical ideas about the nature of appearance versus reality, and the possibility that the perceived world is merely a construct or a shadow of a deeper truth. Mr. Spitzer’s reluctance to awaken the dead for fear they might die again connects the idea that it is better to leave things be than to interfere with the natural order of life and death. This connects to philosophical concepts of acceptance and the inevitability of death.
  • “Eloise et Abelard”: The twelfth-century philosopher Peter Abelard and his student Héloïse secretly married, were parted when her uncle had Abelard castrated, and lived out their lives in the cloister, joined afterward by their letters. Young names them again among parted lovers in the Chapter 65 resources.
    • Peter Abelard was a medieval French scholastic philosopher, leading logician, theologian, teacher, musician, composer, and poet. In philosophy he is celebrated for his proposed logical solution to the problem of universals via nominalism and conceptualism and his pioneering of intent in ethics. Often referred to as the “Descartes of the twelfth century,” he is considered a forerunner of Rousseau, Kant, and Spinoza. He is sometimes credited as a chief forerunner of modern empiricism. In Catholic theology, he is best known for his development of the concept of limbo, and his introduction of the moral influence theory of atonement. He is considered (alongside Augustine) to be the most significant forerunner of the modern self-reflective autobiographer. He paved the way and set the tone for later epistolary novels and celebrity tell-alls with his publicly distributed letter, The History of My Calamities and public correspondence. In history and popular culture he is best known for his passionate, tragic love affair and intense philosophical exchange with his brilliant student and eventual wife, Héloïse d’Argenteuil. After they secretly married, Abelard sent Héloïse to the convent at Argenteuil; her uncle and guardian Fulbert, believing she had been cast off, had Abelard ambushed and castrated, and both entered religious life. Roscellinus and others made fun of him. Their son was named Astrolabe. (Wikipedia) See the problem of universals in the Chapter 49 resources.
    • Héloïse d’Argenteuil was a French nun, philosopher, writer, scholar, and abbess. Héloïse was a renowned “woman of letters” and philosopher of love and friendship, as well as an eventual high ranking abbess in the Catholic Church. She achieved approximately the level and political power of a bishop in 1147 when she was granted the rank of prelate nullius. She is famous in history and popular culture for her love affair and correspondence with the leading medieval logician and theologian Peter Abelard, who became her colleague, collaborator, and husband. She is known for exerting critical intellectual influence upon his work and posing many challenging questions to him such as those in the Problemata Heloissae. Her surviving letters are considered a foundation of French and European literature and primary inspiration for the practice of courtly love. Her erudite and sometimes erotically charged correspondence is the Latin basis for the bildungsroman genre and serve alongside Abelard’s Historia Calamitatum as a model of the classical epistolary genre. Her influence extends on later writers such as Chrétien de Troyes, Geoffrey Chaucer, Madame de Lafayette, Thomas Aquinas, Choderlos de Laclos, Voltaire, Rousseau, Simone Weil, and Dominique Aury. She is an important figure in the establishment of women’s representation in scholarship and is known for her controversial portrayals of gender and marriage which influenced the development of modern feminism. When Abelard gave a rationalistic interpretation of the Trinitarian dogma he was charged with heresy. Theologia by Peter Abelard refers to his three treatises on the Trinity, written in the 12th century. These works, titled Theologia “summi boni,” Theologia Christiana, and Theologia “scholarium,” explore the nature of God and the Trinity using dialectical reasoning. Theologia Christiana is considered the most influential of the three. However, Abelard’s Theologia was also controversial, with the first version being condemned as heretical and burned in 1121. Abelard employed logical arguments and philosophical analysis to examine theological concepts, particularly the Trinity. He adopted the Boethian sense of theologia as discourse about the divine nature, according to a study from Oxford Academic. The core of his work revolves around understanding the nature and relationship of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Abelard’s approach, which emphasized reason and challenged traditional interpretations, led to conflicts with Church authorities. His works were condemned at the Council of Soissons (1122) and the Council of Sens (1141). Abelard proposed that Christ’s death was primarily a demonstration of God’s love, rather than a satisfaction of divine justice. This view is now known as the moral influence theory. Despite the controversies, Abelard’s work had a lasting impact on medieval philosophy and theology, particularly in the development of scholasticism. (Wikipedia)
    • The Letters of Abelard and Heloise are two series of passionate and intellectual correspondences apparently written in Latin during the 12th century. The purported authors, Peter Abelard, a prominent theologian, and his pupil, Heloise, a gifted young woman later renowned as an abbess, exchanged these letters following their ill-fated love affair and subsequent monastic lives. The letters reveal the personal and intellectual relationship between Abelard and Heloise, and provide an intimate glimpse into the societal context of 12th-century Europe. They’ve played a significant role in the development of Western epistolary literature, attracting attention from historians, literary scholars, and general readers alike. The Letters of Abelard and Heloise also serve as primary source documents on questions of medieval gender roles, love, and monastic life. (Wikipedia)
  • “Was she Tristram, or was she the white Yseult?”: Tristram (Tristan) and Iseult (Yseult, Isolde) is a chivalric romance told in many versions since the twelfth century, the tragedy of the illicit love between the Cornish knight Tristram and the Irish princess Iseult, wife of his uncle King Mark. The white Yseult is Iseult of the White Hands, the Breton princess Tristram marries for her name; in the version of Thomas of Britain and many later tellings the marriage is never consummated, so she too is a lover never mated. Matthew Arnold’s Tristram and Iseult (1852) is in three parts, “Tristram,” “Iseult of Ireland,” and “Iseult of Brittany,” and gives the last part to the white-handed wife, whom Arnold, unusually, makes the mother of Tristram’s children.
  • “two pennies, one in memory of the sun”: Coins were laid on the eyes of the dead to keep the lids closed, a custom often linked to the Greek obol placed in the mouth of the dead as Charon’s fare across the Styx. Mr. Spitzer has just pictured himself as “Charon’s ferry boat.” See Greek Mythology.
