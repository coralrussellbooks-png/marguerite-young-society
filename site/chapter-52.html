Summary

Going through Hannah's estate, Mr. Spitzer opens trunk after trunk of wedding gowns, with no bride's slippers among them. He finds letters in languages he cannot read, her lost skirt embroidered with forget-me-nots, and evidence that she lived as Hannah in Boston and as Hamid in the East. Her book lies open to the tale of Tristram and Iseult. He resolves to keep her secret. He closes her eyes and weights them with two pennies, one for the sun and one for the moon.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a complex metaphorical description of mystery, time, and identity. Mr. Spitzer reflects on the suffragette’s lost love and her elusive, shadowy identity, "No name, no face told him her identity in cloud fusions as none told him who he was now". This sets the tone of searching for meaning in a life shrouded in ambiguity.
  • Mr. Spitzer sees himself as connected to her through shared duplicity and duality, unable to condemn her without condemning himself. He describes himself as "one already dead in the midst of life" and the "locksmith in a street of dreams where there was neither key nor lock". This reflects the theme of lost identity and the search for self in a confusing world.
  • Cousin Hannah is portrayed as a figure who chased a lost love, symbolized by the lost skirt on a mountain peak, a metaphor for incomplete or thwarted love: "the old suffragette had returned without her love who had gone to retrieve her skirt, surely no dog would ever find". This image resonates through the chapter as a motif of longing and unattainable fulfillment.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s research reveals her as a woman of many paradoxes and adventures: a mountain climber, balloonist, flyer, traveler through deserts and Holy Lands, who lived beyond time and space: "She had gone to Holy Lands as to Death’s seraglio guarded by eunuchs black and white where were locked so many beautiful girls with their faces veiled". Her life is portrayed as almost mythical, full of contradictions and mysteries.
  • The chapter meditates on the nature of time, memory, and existence. Cousin Hannah’s life is described as one without clear chronology, "with little relationship to chronology, for hers were the chronologies of all dead loves, lost loves, doomed efforts". This connects to the idea that love, life, and death are intertwined in a timeless dance.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s physical search through her estate, opening trunks, examining keys, finding wedding gowns, veils, and other artifacts, symbolizes an attempt to unlock the mysteries of her life and, metaphorically, the mysteries of love and existence itself. The trunks "with their lids sealed by rust and great flames" and the abundance of bridal gowns and veils suggest the elusive and fragile nature of identity and memory.
  • Mr. Spitzer questions not only her identity but his own, reflecting on the blurred lines between reality and illusion, living and dead: "He questioned who he was as he questioned who she was, and doubtless this would always be the case". This highlights the existential uncertainty permeating the chapter.
  • The suffragette is likened to a bride who has hidden away or been lost, a figure of unfulfilled destiny and love: Perhaps "she had abandoned the bridegroom at the church door". This symbolizes the elusiveness of love and the human condition of searching for something lost or unattainable.
  • There is a recurring tension between what is real and unreal, the dream and reality, memory and oblivion. Mr. Spitzer reflects that "Perhaps the unreal and the real were one—or there was only this mask of the stars, and there was no face behind the stars". This blurring of boundaries questions the nature of existence itself.
  • The chapter closes with a meditation on the limits of language and knowledge: "He was not certain of the book’s title now when all writing had faded ... no language contained truth, for the truth was that which could not be expressed". This encapsulates the chapter’s theme of the ineffability of the greatest mysteries of life and love.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • A reflective, almost spectral figure who is both narrator and investigator. He embodies duality and duplicity, often questioning his own identity and existence, blurring lines between life and death, reality and dream. His role is to manage and close the estate of the old suffragette, but his journey is deeply philosophical and metaphysical.

The Old Suffragette

  • A mysterious woman shrouded in symbolism; she is a traveler, a rebel, and a figure of lost love and elusive identity. Known for her suffrage activism, she is also depicted as a wanderer through physical and metaphysical realms of mountains, deserts, and clouds. She carries the paradox of being both bride and bridegroom, representing the complex intersections of gender, love, and death.

Research: Themes

  • Mystery and Identity The chapter is permeated with questions of identity, both Mr. Spitzer’s and the suffragette’s. Mr. Spitzer struggles to know who he is or whether he is a ghostly twin. The suffragette’s identity remains veiled, indicated by symbols like veils, masks, and cloud fusions.
  • Love and Loss Central to the narrative is the motif of lost love, symbolized by the suffragette’s lost skirt and her search for love that may never be found or may be forever elusive. Love is portrayed as inseparable from death, an eternal quest that transcends time.
  • Life, Death, and the Afterlife The chapter explores the boundaries between life and death, suggesting that death may multiply uncertainties and dissolve distinctions, such as gender roles and personal identities. Death is not an end but a continuation or transformation, with echoes of the past and shadows of lost loves lingering.
  • Time and Timelessness Time is fluid and ambiguous; events are reported out of chronological order, and the notion of “when” is elusive. The suffragette’s life and death exist in a timeless realm where beginnings and ends blur.
  • The External vs. Internal Worlds The suffragette’s adventures traverse both external landscapes (mountains, deserts, clouds) and internal, psychological realms. Reality and dream intertwine, with the external world mirroring internal emotional and spiritual states.
  • The Search and the Unattainable Mr. Spitzer’s search through the suffragette’s estate, her memories, and her secrets is a metaphor for the human quest for meaning, often futile and always incomplete. The imagery of keys, trunks, and locked doors symbolizes mysteries that cannot be fully unlocked.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

  • Clouds and Snow Represent mystery, concealment, and transformation. They are often described as moving, shifting, and obscuring identity and reality.
  • Skirts and Veils The suffragette’s lost skirt embroidered with forget-me-nots symbolizes lost love and identity. Veils and skirts also signify the blurred boundaries of gender and self.
  • Keys, Locks, and Trunks These represent the attempt to unlock secrets, memories, and truths. Mr. Spitzer’s fumbling with keys and trunks underscores the impossibility of fully understanding or revealing the suffragette's life.
  • Stars, Moons, and Celestial Bodies Used to depict guidance, mystery, the unreachable, and the eternal. The moon, often mirrored or sunken, symbolizes reflection and illusion.
  • Birds and Butterflies Symbols of transformation, fleeting beauty, and the soul’s journey. The butterfly sailing out of the ear (a door) suggests escape or transcendence.
  • Music and Silence Music represents life’s rhythm, emotion, and unspoken realities; silence and muteness indicate death, absence, or the ineffable.

This concept formed the core of coverture, asserting that husband and wife were a single legal entity, with the husband as the representative. As the women's rights movement gained momentum, coverture was increasingly criticized as oppressive, leading to reforms. Coverture was gradually weakened and eventually eliminated through legislation and legal reforms in the United States. While coverture is no longer in effect, its legacy continues to shape legal and social discussions, particularly concerning gender equality. Some legal scholars argue that vestiges of coverture persist in contemporary legal frameworks, particularly in areas like real estate and tax law. The brothers shipped the wreckage back to Dayton, and the aircraft never flew again. Orville later restored it and displayed it on several occasions. The Flyer joined the Smithsonian Institution's collection of historic aircraft in 1948 after the end of a long and bitter dispute between Orville and the Institution over its refusal to recognize the Flyer as the first successful airplane. Today, it is on display in a place of honor in the National Air and Space Museum in Washington, D.C.

  • Mr. Spitzer's meticulous accounting and listing is directly tied to his struggle with his identity, particularly his relationship with his deceased brother. He seeks a perfect balance, a mirrored reflection where everything in his life corresponds to his brother's, suggesting a desire to understand himself through this dualistic lens. The fear of "no differing counterpart" speaks to the existential anxiety of lacking a defined self, relying on another for definition. This resonates with philosophical concepts of identity formation, the "self" versus the "other," and the influence of external relationships on self-perception. Spitzer's obsessive listing and indexing of even the most mundane objects (pearl buttons, rusted nail heads, etc.) reflects a deep-seated need to impose order on a chaotic world. He's trying to find patterns, connections, and a "common denominator" to make sense of existence. This aligns with the philosophical pursuit of metaphysics: trying to understand the fundamental nature of reality. The futility of Spitzer's efforts mirrors the inherent difficulties in finding absolute order or meaning in a world often perceived as absurd or chaotic (a key theme in existentialism and absurdism). The questions about the sail ("Was the sail unwinding the same as the winding sail?") touch on the philosophical problem of identity over time. Does something remain the same if its state changes? This is further complicated by the cyclical nature of the tides and the wagon wheels, implying a sense of eternal return or repetition. His fear that "all the weight, as he had often complained, was on this weightless star" alludes to the disparity between the tangible and intangible, the concrete and abstract.
