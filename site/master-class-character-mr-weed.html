Mr. Weed: The Life Made From a Knot

← The Tavern Landlady · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Doctor Justice O'Leary →

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Titus Bonebreaker · Mr. Weed · Vera Cartwheel

The other man signing in at the Tavern, the one who takes so long at the register, is "the Christian hangman, Mr. Weed, a respectable citizen with a large family," on his way home from a hanging. He is the landlord's twin at the counter. The landlord ties the knot that marries people and swears it cannot be undone. Weed ties the knot that ends them. Between the two of them the whole trade of the book stands at the front desk, one man binding and one man severing, and by the time the night is over it is not easy to say they were doing different work.

His day job is death and his real living is a dairy farm. The hangman, the landlord lets slip, is secretly a prosperous farmer with a model dairy, and he ties the noose only because, as the phrase goes, "he got paid to tie it." "Every hanging represented an improvement on the farm." He calls himself "the soul of tolerance," condemns nobody, and dresses for a hanging in the same good suit he wears to church, because to him the two are equally official occasions. He is proud of his method. He "rehearses every hanging," practices with a sandbag the exact weight of the man, and holds that "the rehearsal is more important, from my point of view, than the actual hanging." He is, in his own mind, a merciful technician, offering a painless drop as a Christian kindness.

And he never once meets the person he kills. He writes each condemned man a form letter asking for "his weight and measurements and his permission to tie the official knot," and beyond that he "never talked to any of the parties concerned." This is the thing that makes him more terrible than any butcher in his own stories. He spends his life in the room with death, closer to it than anyone, and he sees no one. He reduces each man to a weight, a drop, a knot, and calls the reduction mercy. Where Mr. Bonebreaker fled the real the instant it stood revealed, Weed does the opposite and never has to flee, because he arranged never to look at the person in the first place. He is attention emptied of contact, procedure standing in for witness.

He is also where the book says its own deepest thing out loud, and it comes out as dread. The most terrifying murders, Weed believes, are the subtle ones, "like murdering a mouse in your dreams, and then the next morning when you got up, you found a mouse with a crushed skull in your coat pocket." That is the whole novel in one sentence, spoken by the hangman: the thing imagined becomes the thing that is real, and the body is found in the pocket. Where the mother dreams a chauffeur into being and the landlord makes coffee cups speak, Weed dreams of a killing and wakes up to a corpse. He knows, better than anyone in the book, that a thing made in the mind does not stay in the mind, and it does not comfort him. He once stood in his own orchard, saw small bodies hanging in the peach tree, nearly cut a branch to beat his children with, and only then realized he had been "walking in his sleep" and that the branch was from the tree where their mother died.

Because there is a death he did not manage cleanly, and it will not close. His first wife, Marie, hanged herself one snowy night, fearing she had conceived again, and when he went to cut her down he was "a split second too late." He was late, he cannot stop saying, because "he had stopped to put on his galoshes." Out of that split second he has built a riddle he will never answer, "Was the hangman a murderer because he put on his galoshes?" The man who makes death painless and efficient for strangers botched the one death that was his own, called it "an unskillful hanging," and it is the reason he "cannot sleep at night." Her children play at hanging themselves in the orchard. His whole professional calm is built over the one knot he tied too slow, and the calm does not reach it.

His surface, like everyone's in this book, is made of the dead. He wears "a dead man's hat." His Parker pen was a souvenir. He carries a tiny globe-shaped watch on a black ribbon, given to him by a man who shoved his own family off a cliff, and the watch stopped the instant the car went over, so that among all the broken things "the crystal was not broken." He keeps the exact moment of a murder wound down in his pocket, the smooth face intact over the catastrophe, and he takes it out to show, because "he was determined to show us everything." He collects the relics of other people's deaths the way Miss MacIntosh keeps a fled man's umbrella, and he displays them with a technician's pride, the stopped watch that tells no time and keeps perfect time, the surface that holds while everything under it is smashed.

And he is not even one man. When Vera cannot square the confessing, dancing, endlessly talking hangman with a respectable dairy farmer, the landlord explains that the rambler was "the hangman's shadow, who got loose after every hanging" and said the things the close-mouthed hangman never would, and then closes the split back up, "the two were one." So Weed performs the book's divided self as helplessly as everyone else, the silent farmer and the loosed shadow, one person and two. By the time his feet are dancing their soft tattoo on the ceiling over Vera's room, she is no longer sure he was ever there at all, and she asks the question the book asks of all its figures, "Had I dreamed the hangman?"

The connection for a writer is the darkest turn in the whole set, because Weed holds the maker's power and feels it as guilt. To bring the imagined to life is the same act as finding the dead mouse in the pocket the next morning. The book has spent itself insisting that dreaming makes real, that a sleeping stranger can be given a whole marriage and a cried name a whole death, and Weed is the figure who says the quiet cost of that power plainly, that what is made in the mind leaves a body. He is also the anti-witness in his purest form, not the man who runs from the real like Bonebreaker but the man who stares at death for a living and never sees a single person, who takes the weights and measurements and ties the knot and calls it mercy. A writer can do exactly that, reduce people to their uses, collect them as material and souvenirs, tie the neat knot and never talk to any of the parties concerned. That is the hangman's craft, and it is efficient, and it improves the farm. The book sets it beside the other kind of making, the kind that stays in the room and looks at the person, and it lets Weed dance alone on the ceiling all night, a good father who cannot sleep, to show what the tidy version costs.
