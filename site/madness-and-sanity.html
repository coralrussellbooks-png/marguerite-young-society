Definition from research

Chapter 2 · Madness and Sanity

  • Catherine’s mental state is ambiguous; she is at times lucid, at times delusional.
  • Opium and drugs regulate her condition, creating a "scheduled madness" that preserves her life.
  • Her madness is a form of self-preservation and rebellion against a brutal world.

Chapter 7

  • “A fool, Lear in the storm”: In Shakespeare’s King Lear, the “storm” is both a physical and metaphorical force. Lear is exposed to a violent natural storm on the heath, symbolizing his internal turmoil and the chaos of his kingdom after he disowns his loyal supporters and his loving daughter Cordelia. This exposure forces him to confront his own mortality and the suffering of others, leading to a shift in his perspective and, ultimately, a descent into madness. It is a metaphorical description that situates old Doc in the tradition of tragic figures overwhelmed by chaos, emphasizing themes of madness, vulnerability, and existential struggle that run through the novel.
  • “Crazier than fifty mad men playing at badminton in an insane asylum”: Despite the shared space and activity, each player struggles individually while staff, guards, and others look on for their amusement. The phrase underscores the sense of profound disorientation and madness that pervades the novel, particularly embodied in old Doc, caught between life and death, reality and dream.
  • “All these old cretins”: Cretinism, stunted growth and mind from a lack of iodine, was common in the Great Lakes “goiter belt” until iodized salt came to Michigan in 1924.
  • “because it didn’t have its right mind. It was an idiot”: Idiot, imbecile, and moron were official medical categories, ranked by the eugenicist Henry Goddard in 1910.
  • “grew up with the water on her brain”: Hydrocephalus. Before shunts were developed in the 1950s, children with it were often called idiots and put away.

Chapter 17 · Fear and Madness

  • The setting at midnight, the eerie descriptions, and the “zero hour” evoke a sense of dread.
  • Vera’s fear escalates into a violent struggle, suggesting a descent into madness and trauma.
  • The chapter explores how strange events can “leave us mad” or shatter sanity.

Chapter 24 · Madness and Sanity

  • The novel delves into how prolonged exposure to Catherine’s dreams affects the servants, leading some to lose their grip on reality and adopt bizarre identities or behaviors (e.g., thinking they serve Wellington or living among cuckoo clocks).
  • This theme raises questions about the fragile line between sanity and madness in a closed, surreal environment.

Chapter 39 · Illness and Madness

  • Catherine’s opium use and mental state are central to her experience.
  • She is aware of her insanity and the limitations imposed by it.
  • Madness is depicted as both a torment and a source of her vivid imagination and insight.

Chapter 54 · Duality of Identity and Madness

  • Mr. Spitzer oscillates between his own identity and that of his brother, sometimes confused about who he truly is. The chapter explores the thin line between sanity and madness, with Joachim embodying order and Peron chaos, yet both are reflections of each other.

Chapter 56 · Madness and Sanity

  • Madness is presented ambiguously as both a form of death and a form of life, sustaining illusion and preventing the advance of time.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s mental state deteriorates as he questions who controls him, who “tailored” his existence, and whether he is merely a puppet or a phantom.
  • His inability to distinguish between reality and imagination is a symbol of his psychological collapse.

Chapter 69 · Isolation and Madness

  • The doctor’s madness and isolation are emphasized through his anguished monologues and the strange, disruptive presence of Sarah.
  • The landlord and Vera observe these events as somewhat detached witnesses, enhancing the sense of alienation.

Chapter 70 · Madness and Sanity

  • Mrs. Hogden’s behavior is perceived as madness, yet she retains a shrewd awareness and control.
  • The narrative questions who is truly mad: the woman or those rejecting her.
  • Madness is portrayed both as a social stigma and a complex psychological state.

Chapter 71 · Madness and Reality

  • Dr. O’Leary’s madness blurs the lines between reality and hallucination.
  • The presence of imaginary babies and patients reflects the struggle to grasp meaning in a chaotic world.
  • Vera questions their own perception, wondering if they too are dreaming.
