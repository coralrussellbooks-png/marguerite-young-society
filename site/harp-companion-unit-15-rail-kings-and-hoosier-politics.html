Harp Song for a Radical

Unit 15, pp. 353-392: Rail kings and Hoosier politics

← Unit 14 · Harp Song for a Radical: A History Companion · Unit 16 →

Background for this unit: The History in Harp Song for a Radical

In the late 1880s Debs's magazine turns sharper. He quarrels with the engineers' brotherhood, attacks George Pullman and Jay Gould, and records wreck after wreck. Young then turns to Indiana's political theater: Riley and Nye on tour, Senator Daniel Voorhees, and the Harrison family, whose rising son would become president.

Major entries

Engineers versus firemen: Peter M. Arthur (pp. 353-358)

  • Who he was: Peter M. Arthur (1831-1903), Scottish-born, was grand chief engineer of the Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers from 1874 until his death. He ran the richest and most conservative of the railroad brotherhoods.

  • The conflict: The engineers considered themselves an elite and refused to support strikes by lower-paid crafts. Young notes Arthur's position that engineers could keep working if the "lower class" of firemen struck (p. 353). Debs resented this caste line between men who died in the same wrecks.

  • Why it matters: The engineers' refusal to back other crafts was a main reason Debs left the brotherhood system in 1893 to found an industrial union for all railroad workers.

  • Further reading: Paul Michel Taillon, Good, Reliable, White Men: Railroad Brotherhoods, 1877-1917 (2009).

George Pullman and the town of Pullman (pp. 359-361)

  • Who he was: George Mortimer Pullman (1831-1897) made his fortune building and operating luxury sleeping cars. Between 1880 and 1884 he built the company town of Pullman south of Chicago, with brick housing, a library, and a church, all owned by the company.

  • Debs's early critique: Years before the 1894 strike, Debs attacked Pullman in the magazine: "Pullman means purple and fine linen, sumptuous living" for the owner, and for his workers "no independence" (p. 359). He listed the grievances of Pullman car employees: "spotters" watching them, fines cutting pay, and blame for errors in berth diagrams (p. 360).

  • Why it matters: The model town became a symbol of paternalism. When Pullman cut wages in 1893-1894 without cutting rents, his workers struck, and Debs's American Railway Union refused to handle Pullman cars nationwide.

  • Further reading: Almont Lindsey, The Pullman Strike (1942).

Jay Gould (pp. 354-355, 361, 364)

  • Who he was: The most hated financier of the Gilded Age (1836-1892). He looted the Erie Railroad with Jim Fisk, tried to corner gold in 1869, which caused the "Black Friday" panic, and built a western railroad empire around the Missouri Pacific and the Wabash.

  • His war with labor: The Knights of Labor won a strike against his Wabash line in 1885, and the Knights' membership soared. In 1886 Gould crushed the Knights in the Great Southwest Strike. He is credited with boasting, "I can hire one-half of the working class to kill the other half." Young mentions a proposed "Knights of Capital" to fight the unions (p. 361).

  • Further reading: Maury Klein, The Life and Legend of Jay Gould (1986).

Daniel W. Voorhees and Edward Hannegan (pp. 385-391)

  • Voorhees: Terre Haute's Democratic leader (1827-1897), called "the Tall Sycamore of the Wabash." He defended John Brown's raider John E. Cook in 1859 and was a leading Copperhead in Congress during the war. As a senator from 1877 to 1897 he championed cheap money and the building of the Library of Congress. His Senate clashes with John J. Ingalls of Kansas were famous for their insults (pp. 388-389).

  • Hannegan: Voorhees grew up in Fountain County, where he knew Edward Hannegan (1807-1859), a senator known for the expansionist cry over Oregon, "Fifty-four forty or fight!" In 1852 at Covington, drunk, Hannegan stabbed his brother-in-law, who died. Lew Wallace, the county prosecutor and later author of Ben-Hur, declined to prosecute (p. 387). Hannegan died of an overdose of morphine and whiskey in 1859.

  • Further reading: Leonard S. Kenworthy, The Tall Sycamore of the Wabash: Daniel Wolsey Voorhees (1936).

Benjamin Harrison (pp. 388-392)

  • Who he was: Grandson of President William Henry Harrison, an Indianapolis lawyer, Union general, senator (1881-1887), and president (1889-1893).

  • Connections: He defended the officials in Milligan's damages suit (Unit 10), commanded a militia company against the 1877 strikers (Unit 19), and signed the Sherman Antitrust Act in 1890 (p. 389). That law, aimed at monopolies, was first used heavily against unions, including Debs's in 1894.

  • The stolen body: In 1878 the body of Harrison's father, John Scott Harrison, was stolen from its grave at North Bend, Ohio. Searchers found it hanging in a chute at the Medical College of Ohio in Cincinnati (pp. 391-392), a scandal that exposed the trade in corpses for anatomy classes.

  • Further reading: Charles W. Calhoun, Benjamin Harrison (2005).

The Chatsworth wreck (pp. 364-365)

  • What happened: On August 10, 1887, an excursion train of the Toledo, Peoria and Western bound for Niagara Falls crossed a burning wooden trestle near Chatsworth, Illinois (p. 364). The bridge collapsed and more than eighty people died.

  • Why it matters: It was among the deadliest wrecks of the century. The coroner's jury blamed a section boss rather than the railroad (p. 365), which was typical. Debs used such cases to argue that the companies, not the workers, should answer for safety.

Shorter entries

  • John Perego (p. 364): A fireman who crawled through scalding steam to stop the engine of President Cleveland's special train after the engineer was killed, as the magazine reported in October 1887.

  • Charles A. Dana and the New York Sun (p. 363): Dana's Sun was one of the most hostile papers to labor in the 1880s.

  • Chauncey Depew (p. 363): The New York Central's lawyer and president, a famous after-dinner speaker, later a senator.

  • Elliott F. Shepard (p. 369): Son-in-law of William H. Vanderbilt and a crusading Presbyterian publisher. Young calls him a brother-in-law.

  • Austin Corbin (p. 372): President of the Reading Railroad in the late 1880s. Debs wrote that men like him had "Russianized the anthracite coal regions of Pennsylvania."

  • Labor Day (p. 370): First celebrated with a New York parade in 1882 and made a federal holiday by a law signed on June 28, 1894, in the midst of the Pullman crisis.

  • The American Federation of Labor (p. 371): Founded in 1886 under Samuel Gompers, a federation of craft unions.

  • Myron Reed in Denver (pp. 370-371): As pastor in Denver, Reed preached for organized labor and against the Pinkertons.

  • Debs's organizing trips (pp. 373-374): Debs later recalled being put off trains as an "agitator" and dropped in a snowbank at Elmira, New York.

  • Art Young (p. 365): The socialist cartoonist (1866-1943) of The Masses, who was tried under the Espionage Act in 1918.

  • Riley and Nye's tours (pp. 375-381): The poet and the humorist toured together from 1886 to 1890 and published Nye and Riley's Railway Guide (1888). Riley's poem "Regardin' Terry Hut" (pp. 383-385) was one of his Hoosier dialect pieces.

  • Major Charles Holstein (p. 379): Riley boarded with the Holstein family on Lockerbie Street in Indianapolis from 1893 until his death.

  • Maria Halpin (p. 390): In the 1884 campaign, Grover Cleveland admitted he might be the father of her son. Opponents chanted "Ma, Ma, where's my Pa?" and Cleveland told his managers, "Tell the truth."

Allusions

  • The Gate of Tears (p. 366): Bab el-Mandeb, the strait at the mouth of the Red Sea, from Thomas Moore's poetry.

  • Phidias, Raphael, Michelangelo (p. 357): Masters of sculpture and painting.

  • Judas Iscariot (p. 365): The betrayer of Christ, for union traitors.

  • Morpheus (p. 360): The god of dreams, for sleeping cars.

  • Cardinal Wolsey (p. 385): Henry VIII's minister, and Voorhees's middle name.

  • Hogarthian (p. 389): Like William Hogarth's satirical prints of London's poor.
