Chapter 73: The Stillborn and the Fathers

← Chapter 72 · Guide Home · Chapter 74 →

Content note: The Esther Longtree chapters (72 to 81) deal with stillbirth and pregnancy loss, rape, infanticide, racist violence including lynching, and cruelty to children. This chapter describes stillbirths and the hidden remains of infants.

Research notes: Chapter 73

Characters: Abigail Porter · Coleman Bluebough · Esther Longtree · Joe Goldberg · Phoebe · The Chicago Detective · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Duality and Doubles · Identity and the Self · Motherhood, Pregnancy, and Birth · Music, Sound, and Silence

Symbols: Birds · Pregnancy, Birth, and Children · Silence and Deafness · Twins and Doubles · Weddings and Funerals

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Greek Mythology · Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery

Chapter 72 gave you Esther Longtree in her fullness: the little shadow boxer, the defiant hope, the philosophical reach toward the cosmos. Chapter 73 goes deeper into the particular. Into the texture of her loss. Into the faces, or the fragments of faces, of the children she has carried and buried. Into the men who wrote her letters at the end of their lives, asking about children who were never born whole.

This is a chapter about what silence costs, and what it protects.

You should know before you begin that some of what Esther describes here is disturbing: an encounter in a graveyard that was not consensual, a labor that produced nothing, a life of grief conducted almost entirely in secret, inside a body that everyone can see is pregnant and no one can see is also a graveyard. Young does not look away from any of this. Neither should you.

What Actually Happens

Esther continues her account, which is both monologue and meditation, both addressed to Vera (who is presumably nearby, or imagining this, or somehow receiving it) and addressed to no one, to the air, to the stillborn who whisper in her ears.

She describes the children she has carried as fragments: “an eye, an ear, a long gold hair, a fingernail, a cobweb,” a snowflake. Not whole babies, or not always. Sometimes something more nebulous than a baby, something that was almost a child and then wasn’t. She has buried them everywhere: in streams, under snow, in tree stumps, in the autumn leaves. The undertaker has complained that she brings him no business, because she hides the evidence. This is not indifference; it is the opposite. She cannot bear to hand them over to strangers.

She describes the fathers: the traveling men, the salesmen, the transients. Most are weak, sterile in some essential way she cannot diagnose. As they die, they write her letters. They remember her vividly. They ask: did the child live? They want to know they have left something behind. Esther, advised by her old drunken doctor to preserve the dying man’s vanity and peace, does not answer. She keeps their illusions intact. The truth (that there is no child, or that what arrived was a snowflake, a cobweb, a breath of something that did not take) is not something she can give them. It is also not something she can fully bear herself.

The Chicago detective appears, circling. He is investigating something (possibly the disappearance of the stillborn children, possibly other crimes) and Esther fears arrest, fears trial, fears standing before a jury of women who will judge her without understanding her condition. She has done nothing wrong. She has also hidden everything.

And then there is Joe Goldberg.

Joe Goldberg, Champion Featherweight

He is the one father Esther cannot quite remember. This is the novel’s precise reversal: the weak fathers, the sterile salesmen, the men who barely registered in her life; she remembers them clearly. The strong one, the champion, the featherweight boxer who was at the peak of his existence when he knew her; he is lost in clouds, like the weight of a feather in her mind. Young is telling you something here about how memory works: it keeps what hurt least, most clearly, and buries what was most overwhelming. Joe Goldberg wrote her long, anguished letters as he wasted away from tuberculosis. He remembered her as the greatest victory of his life: greater than any fight. He remembered meeting her in a graveyard on a foggy, windy night. He remembered knocking the nose off a stone angel. He remembered taking her against her will. He wrote asking for remembrance, for forgiveness, for confirmation that his child lived. Esther remembers the graveyard. She does not remember him clearly. She cannot anchor his face.

What Young is doing here is both precise and painful. Joe Goldberg assaulted Esther. He knows this; his letter asks for forgiveness, which implies acknowledgment of what he did. He has, in his dying days, turned it into something else in his memory (the “peak of his existence, his greatest victory”) which is what men who have done this often do. Esther cannot remember him clearly because the experience did not belong to her in any way that would make a clear memory. It was done to her. The blur is honest.

She does not answer his letter.

The Birth of Nothing

One of the chapter’s most quietly devastating passages describes a labor: Esther endures all the pain of childbirth (the contractions, the hours, the full physical breaking) and delivers nothing. Her body goes through the complete experience of birth and the child is not there, or is there as a feather, a white drift, something that might be a message from the silence or might be the absence of a message.

She lies alone afterward. She does not call anyone. She does not bring the nothing to the undertaker. She gets up. She goes back to the Greasy Spoon. This is the most extreme form of what Esther carries: not just grief for the children who came and did not live, but the labor itself spent on absence. Her body has done everything right. Everything has gone through the correct motions. And still: nothing. Young refuses to explain this medically or symbolically. It simply happens, the way loss simply happens, without adequate cause or sufficient reason.

The Silence

The old drunken doctor’s advice to Esther is one of the chapter’s key moments of pragmatic wisdom: don’t answer the dying fathers. Let them believe the child lives. What good does the truth do? They are dying. Their belief in the child is all they have. Give them that. Esther follows this advice. She does not answer the letters. She does not confirm or deny. She keeps the stillborn hidden. She carries the truth of her situation inside a body that broadcasts it to anyone who looks (she is visibly, obviously pregnant; she has been obviously pregnant for years) and still no one in the town knows what she actually knows. They have theories. They have suspicions. They have the undertaker’s complaint. They do not have the truth.

This silence is Esther’s great act. It protects the dying. It protects her. It costs her enormously. She is the only one who knows the full inventory of loss (every fragment, every snowflake, every cobweb that was meant to be a child) and she has agreed, by her silence, to be the only keeper of that knowledge. There is no one she can tell. The stillborn whisper in her ears. She listens to them and says nothing outward.

The Accusation

The Chicago detective is, in this chapter, still circling: still a threat rather than a fully materialized presence. But the threat is specific: Esther fears that her hidden stillborn children will be read as evidence of murder. That she will stand trial for the deaths of children she did not kill, that she loved, that she buried with her own hands in places she chose because she could not bear to hand them to strangers.

This is one of the novel’s most quietly accurate observations about how women’s grief is treated. Esther has lost every child she has carried. She has suffered the full physical and emotional catastrophe of this over and over. And the world around her (embodied in the detective, in the undertaker, in the jury of women she imagines judging her) reads her behavior not as grief but as guilt. She has hidden the evidence. She has refused to explain herself. She must be guilty of something.

She is guilty of nothing except surviving.

The Stillborn as Fragments

Young’s most distinctive choice in this chapter is the way she describes what Esther delivers: not always whole babies, but sometimes only their parts. “An eye, an ear, a long gold hair, a fingernail, a cobweb.” A snowflake.

This is, on one level, medically recognizable: very early losses sometimes produce only fragments. But Young is also doing something larger. The stillborn as fragments accumulate into a portrait: if you arranged all of Esther’s losses side by side, you would have, between them, something that adds up to a child. All the eyes, all the fingernails, all the gold hairs. They are dispersed through years and seasons, buried in different places, but they belong together. They are, taken together, the child that Esther has been waiting for all along. The little shadow boxer, still moving inside her, is the one who might be whole.

The Epizeuxis

Beginning in this chapter, Esther’s speech develops a new feature: she repeats words three times in immediate succession. Young, Young, Young. Gone, gone, gone. Baby, baby, baby. This repetition is called epizeuxis in rhetoric, the insistent restatement of a word for emotional emphasis, and it gives Esther’s voice a new quality, something between a stutter and an incantation. It sounds like grief working through language, like a mind returning compulsively to the same unbearable point. It sounds like someone trying to make a word real by saying it enough times. It sounds like someone trying to make something stay. It is also, as To All My Darlings notes, potentially comic in other contexts. Young holds both registers simultaneously: the grief is real, the repetition is also slightly absurd, Esther is both tragic and eccentric, and that combination, not one or the other, is what makes her one of the great characters in this novel.

What to Hold Onto

The stillborn as fragments: an eye, an ear, a fingernail, a cobweb. Each one a child that was almost. All of them together, a child that might still come.

Joe Goldberg’s letters, vivid with a memory Esther cannot share. The strongest father, barely a feather in her mind. The blur is the honest part.

The birth of nothing. Her body going through every motion, the hours, the pain. And then: a white feather, drifting down. She gets up. She goes back to work. The silence she keeps for the dying fathers. The enormous cost of letting them believe. The doctor’s advice: preserve their peace. What good does the truth do?

And the little shadow boxer, still there, still moving. Still two hearts.
