She is gone, she is lost, she is found, she is ever fair. Sir Walter Raleigh, The Ocean's Love to Cynthia

The Entry Point: A Headless Visitor in Chapter 11

Sir Walter Raleigh appears in the novel twice by name. In Chapter 53 he is one of the men who lost their heads and “had played cards with his executioners.” His great entrance is in Chapter 11, among the distinguished visitors Catherine receives in her opium-dream bedroom, as Vera tells it. He arrives in his historically documented seed-pearl suit. He removes his white plumed hat. He also removes his head. He “asked that he be addressed by his intimate name, Ocean or Water, for he had crossed many waters. He was also, as his head explained, the head of a society of night devoted exclusively to atheistic propositions, such as that man had existed before Adam, and God is nothing but an idea in the mind, and God is dog spelled backward.”

This is not a casual literary cameo. Every element is exact. Elizabeth I called Raleigh “Water,” a play on how his name was pronounced. She was his Cynthia, his moon, his organizing principle, and he was her Ocean: the body that could not stop moving toward her. His great unfinished poem, usually dated to his imprisonment in the Tower of London in 1592 after Elizabeth discovered his secret marriage, was titled The Ocean's Love to Cynthia. More than five hundred lines survive of a work he never finished, a meditation on an ocean that has lost its moon, still moving, still pulled, still devoted to something that has withdrawn its light. The poem was written after disgrace. The execution came later, in 1618, fifteen years after Elizabeth's death. Young's Raleigh is headless: already past the end of his story, still carrying it.

The atheism detail also matters. In 1592 the Jesuit Robert Parsons attacked what he called Raleigh's “School of Atheism.” Scholars later named the circle the School of Night, after a line in Shakespeare's Love's Labour's Lost, and placed in it Thomas Harriot, Christopher Marlowe, and George Chapman. The informer Richard Baines charged Marlowe with saying that Moses was a juggler and with reading “the Atheist lecture” to Raleigh. Young's Raleigh leads his society of night from inside Catherine's bedroom, which is also a kind of Tower of London: the place of confinement where the most dangerous thinking happens, where the exiled self keeps writing its unfinished poem.

The Poetic Chain: Marlowe, Raleigh, Williams

There is a three-part poetic exchange behind Raleigh's visit in Chapter 11, traced in To All My Darlings, and it sounds again in Chapter 40, where Cousin Hannah's many languages include the “Greek shepherd singing to his flock.” It forms one of Miss MacIntosh, My Darling's deep literary architectures. Christopher Marlowe wrote The Passionate Shepherd to His Love, an invitation to an idealized pastoral existence: come live with me and be my love. Raleigh wrote The Nymph's Reply to the Shepherd, an almost line-by-line refutation: the flowers fade, the rivers rage, a honey tongue hides a heart of gall, time drives the flocks from field to fold. No, the nymph says. Youth fades. Your promises are not adequate to what time does.

Raleigh's reply is the clear-eyed exposure of the illusion. It strips the pastoral fantasy of its timelessness. It does what Miss MacIntosh does: it points to the plain fact underneath the beautiful promise. But The Ocean's Love to Cynthia is something else entirely. It does not stop at exposure. It builds anyway, and it is left unfinished. In that poem Raleigh knows that the pastoral was an illusion, knows that Cynthia has withdrawn, knows that he has been ruined, and keeps writing anyway. He cannot stop. The ocean does not stop moving because the moon has risen behind a cloud.

William Carlos Williams added a third term to the exchange in the twentieth century. His poem Raleigh Was Right opens: 'We cannot go to the country / for the country will bring us no peace.' Williams confirms Raleigh's refutation: the pastoral is not available. The country is not an escape. There is no going back to the idealized elsewhere. But Williams says this as a modern poet, after industrialization has made the pastoral not just philosophically hollow but geographically inaccessible. He has been agreeing with Raleigh across three centuries. The conversation is ongoing. It does not resolve.

Another Raleigh poem may sit behind Chapter 45, where Cousin Hannah has “wandered like some penitential palmer with seashells on his hat to show that he had prayed in the Holy Land.” Raleigh's “The Passionate Man's Pilgrimage” begins its own pilgrimage with “Give me my scallop-shell of quiet,” as To All My Darlings notes for that chapter.

This three-part exchange is the architectural spine underneath the novel's treatment of What Cheer, Iowa. Miss MacIntosh comes from What Cheer: a name that is itself a refutation of the pastoral. What cheer? Not much. She carries the plain Midwestern interior as her credentials against illusion. She is the Nymph's Reply embodied: no, the roses fade, the rivers are just rivers, the time is what the clock says. But she drowns in the ocean anyway. The ocean has the last word, as it always does in Raleigh's unfinished poem.

The Novel as Unfinished Poem: Ocean Imagery as Architecture

The publisher's description of the novel says it directly: “Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is written with oceanic music moving at many levels of consciousness and perception.” Anaïs Nin, in her review “One Writer's Version of a Complete Universe” (Los Angeles Times, January 9, 1966), gives Young's own view: “the author's feeling is that if one is to follow the full swelling of the wave of imagination one must bring back to the shore the wave which carried you.” The novel does not describe an ocean. The novel is structured as one. What the Raleigh thread reveals is that this ocean structure is not a metaphor Young invented. It is an inheritance: the great unfinished poem of devotion to something that no longer governs you, still moving, still tidal, still pulling toward the shore that is not there.

The Moon and the Ocean: Gravitational Devotion

The key to the Cynthia/Ocean dynamic in the novel is not romance. It is gravity. The moon does not choose to pull the ocean. The ocean does not choose to be pulled. This is devotion that is structural, not willed: the most honest kind, and the most pitiless. The novel returns to this configuration across its entire length.

Chapter 17, as Vera rides Falada on the night of her fourteenth birthday: “the moon, dimly radiant but distant, invulnerable to our assault, the moon which draws the waves in its wake and has no life of its own.” This is the Cynthia figure exactly. Elizabeth governs Raleigh not through will but through what she is. She does not choose to pull him; it is gravitational. And the moon has no life of its own: it is reflective, not generative. The queen is a surface. The ocean is the one doing the moving, the one with the interior life, the one suffering.

Chapter 18, the same night: Miss MacIntosh's head looms above Vera, “that head which was chalk-colored as the dead moon which draws, in collaboration with all the planets, the spring tide in its wake.” The dead moon governs more powerfully than the living one. After Miss MacIntosh disappears into the ocean, she becomes a governing absence. The tides of the novel's consciousness keep moving toward her even when she is gone. This is the exact situation of The Ocean's Love to Cynthia: written after disgrace, still addressed to the moon that has withdrawn.

Chapter 55: half asleep, Mr. Spitzer hears “the buzzing, snoring sound of a distant sea, the winter sea which scarcely moved as if the moon had lost its power to pull the tide,” and only gradually learns that the sound is himself, turning in his sleep. Even the stillest sea in the novel is pictured through the moon's pull, the one thing no one in it can imagine away. Raleigh keeps writing toward a queen who is no longer listening. He cannot stop. He does not finish. The poem ends in a fragment because the pull does not end.

Chapter 82: Mr. Spitzer thinks “of his own circumstance of which he was certainly not the master, just as he was not the master of the tides, the scallop moon drawing the waves.” This is the novel's clearest statement of the Cynthia principle applied to Mr. Spitzer, the novel's central consciousness across its later chapters. He is water. He is pulled. He did not choose this and cannot alter it. The question the novel asks of him is not whether he can stop being the ocean but whether he can do anything generous with that condition.

The House and the Ocean: Mourning as Architecture

The Cartwheel house has its back to the ocean. In Chapter 70, Vera says it plainly: “No wonder my mother had black curtains at the back of the many-windowed house. My mother was in mourning for the ocean.” This spatial arrangement is one of the novel's most compressed Radical Immanence statements. The house turns away from the very thing it is built next to, which is also the thing that determines everything about it: the salt air, the damp, the tidal rhythm, the light off the water. Mourning for the ocean while living beside it: this is not a contradiction in Young's world. This is the accurate description of what it is to grieve a constitutive loss.

In Raleigh's poem, the ocean mourns Cynthia while Cynthia is still in the sky. The withdrawal of favor is not the same as disappearance. The moon is still there. The ocean can still be pulled. But the relationship is over, and the ocean keeps moving anyway, oriented toward something that no longer acknowledges it. The house with its back to the ocean is doing the same thing: maintaining its orientation in relation to what it has turned away from. The back of a house still knows where the ocean is.

Miss MacIntosh maintains the house's relationship to the ocean in an opposite and equally revealing way. In Chapter 22 she talks as if they are at sea constantly: all hands must be on board, we must plow the ocean, heave ho my hearties, all hands to the ropes. She has never been to sea. She wears a bathing suit she cannot swim in. She stands at the edge of tidal pools with her black umbrella. She uses the ocean as a metaphor for domestic routine while being constitutionally unable to enter it. This is the Nymph's position: knowing the ocean is there, refusing the pastoral invitation to dissolve into it, maintaining the plain dry shore of common sense against the pull. And then she walks in.

The Ocean Floor: We Were What We Mourned

The novel's most devastating ocean passage comes in Chapter 47, in Mr. Spitzer's long night, as his sleep lies upon him “wave upon wave.” The lost souls go down, “all but his own sinking to the ocean's floor.” Then: “this sunken continent for which we mourned, not knowing that we were what we mourned, that we were on the ocean's floor.”

We are not separate from what we are grieving. We are not observers of the loss; we are inside it, on its floor, part of its sedimentation. Raleigh's poem makes the same move: the ocean does not look at itself from outside. The ocean is the grieving. In the novel, the ocean floor is not a destination but a condition. You are already there. You have been there. The mourning is not for something you have lost but for what you are.

The passage continues: “If all were lost, how could one be lost?” This is the logical conclusion of the ocean-as-universal-condition argument. If every soul is on the floor, then the floor is also the world. There is no above from which one could fall. The novel's oceanic structure is not an image of drowning but of already having drowned, which turns out to be the same as living. The ocean floor is where the novel lives.

Miss MacIntosh's Death: The Nymph Enters the Ocean

“The sun and the moon shone in the same pearly sky when she walked into the ocean.” This sentence comes in Chapter 65, the chapter that tells of her walking into the sea. Sun and moon in the same sky: the time of day and the time of night simultaneously present, which is the condition of the novel at all times, which is also the condition of any consciousness holding the living and the dead together without separating them.

Miss MacIntosh is the Nymph throughout the novel: the one who says no to the pastoral invitation, no to illusion, no to the dissolving of the self in oceanic dreaming. She is the reality principle dressed in a black bathing suit. And she walks into the ocean. She gives herself to the undertow. The Nymph's Reply, that careful refutation, ends with the body entering exactly what it refused. Raleigh was right: the country will bring us no peace. But we go anyway. We cannot help it. We are pulled.

In the last chapter, Vera sets the two views side by side. Miss MacIntosh “did not seem then to see what I saw, the beautiful malignancy of the ocean, of always moving waters like the dreams sweeping over the dreams, for from her point of view, the ocean would be safe, the best place for the keeping of treasures, of pearls. No harm could come from the sea if one lay under it, not over it, if one could but give one's self up and be washed clear.” This is the novel's deepest Radical Immanence move. The one who organized Vera's life by pulling it is gone, and Vera is still moving. The grief is not about Miss MacIntosh. The grief is the movement itself, recognized now for what it always was: the ocean's own nature, not borrowed from anything else.

Oceanus: The Ship That Sank with the Evening Star

Among the catalog of lost ships in Chapter 42 is one named Oceanus, which comes right after the ships “sailing into port with no one on board, the table set with plates of gold”: “Oceanus which sank with the evening star, rose not with the morning star though the captain gave good hope.” In Greek cosmology, Oceanus was the primordial river encircling the world, the source of all waters. In the novel's lost-ships catalog, Oceanus is one wreck among many, failing to rise with the morning star despite hope.

This is Raleigh's poem compressed to a single image. The evening star and morning star are both Venus: one name for the same planet seen at different times of day, the “one star” Esther Longtree knows in Chapter 73. The ocean sinks with the evening star and does not rise with the morning star even though the captain gave good hope. The captain gave good hope: Raleigh gave the queen everything, kept faith, waited, gave hope. It did not rise. The ship's name is the thing it could not survive being.

The Radical Immanence Reading: What the Ocean Thread Does

The Ocean's Love to Cynthia thread is not a source study. It is a demonstration of how the first five pillars operate at the level of formal structure, not just content.

Pillar 1 (Not One Self): the ocean is not a single body of water. It is every body of water simultaneously, every consciousness that ever moved toward something it could not stop moving toward. Mr. Spitzer and Vera and Catherine and Miss MacIntosh are all aspects of the same ocean: different surfaces, the same gravitational condition.

Pillar 2 (Reality Is Constructed): the pastoral of Marlowe's shepherd is the novel's clearest example of the beautiful lie. Come live with me and be my love: every character in the novel has been invited into some version of this. Every one of them knows the Nymph's Reply. All of them go anyway. The illusion does not become less operative when it is known to be an illusion. This is not failure. This is the constitutive condition.

Pillar 3 (Grief Doesn't End): Raleigh's poem is not a poem about having grieved. It is a poem written inside grief, still grieving, in the Tower, with no end in sight. The Ocean's Love to Cynthia is an unfinished poem because grief of this kind does not arrive at a conclusion. The novel is structured the same way. It does not resolve. It accumulates. It keeps moving.

Pillar 4 (Witnessing Is the Core Ethical Act): Vera is the novel's witness, watching the ocean, watching Miss MacIntosh, watching Catherine behind the black curtains that turn the house from the sea. The witness is not outside the ocean; the witness is on the shore, which is also inside the ocean's reach. Every shore is the ocean's territory in weather. Vera's witnessing does not protect her from the tidal pull. It is the tidal pull: the inability to look away from the thing that has organized your life.

Pillar 5 (Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary): the novel builds itself the way the ocean builds itself, by returning. Wave after wave covering the same ground, each pass slightly different, laying down sediment, uncovering it, laying it down again. Raleigh's poem returns to Cynthia obsessively, circling back to the same images of loss from different angles. The 1,198 pages of Miss MacIntosh, My Darling do the same thing. This is not repetition as failure. This is the ocean's formal logic.

The Unfinished Poem

The Ocean's Love to Cynthia was never finished. Whether Raleigh meant it as a fragment, whether he continued and the continuation was lost, whether imprisonment broke the sustained act of composition: we do not know. What survives is a poem that circles its subject without arriving, that names its grief without resolving it, that addresses a moon that is no longer listening in the same formal registers it used when the relationship was intact. It is the most honest love poem in the English language: honest because it does not pretend to be over, honest because it does not pretend to continue.

Young sets this poem inside her novel as its deep water. The novel's oceanic structure, its refusal to resolve, its devotion to characters who cannot stop moving toward what they have lost or never had: all of this flows from the same source. The ocean does not stop because the moon withdraws. The moon has no life of its own. The water has always been what was doing the moving.

Raleigh was right. We cannot go to the country. The country will bring us no peace. But the novel's last word on Miss MacIntosh is not this refusal. It is Vera, in the last chapter, remembering Miss MacIntosh wading through the surf, asking: “And yet shall I not always remember her?” The answer is built into the question. The tide draws the question out whether the question wants to go or not. It was never a choice.
