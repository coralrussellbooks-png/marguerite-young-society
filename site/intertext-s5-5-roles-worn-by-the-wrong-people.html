5. Roles Worn by the Wrong People

Part of Intertextuality in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Previous · Intertextuality in MMMD Home · Next →

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Madge Capehorn · Miss MacIntosh

Philosophy: Hinduism

Allusions: Napoleon and Wellington · Shakespeare

Definition from research

Young keeps handing famous roles to people who do not fit them, and the mismatch carries the meaning. The strategy takes several forms.

Crossed gender. The bus-driver, guarding his uncut hair, "was Penelope weaving this web, not Ulysses" (chapter 5). Cousin Hannah is cast again and again in male heroic roles. She stands "one arm crossed before her bosom like Napoleon" and is "that Jason who had not found the golden fleece" (chapter 42). Looking at her in death, Mr. Spitzer cannot settle even that: "Was she Tristram, or was she the white Yseult?" (chapter 52). Esther Longtree, imagining a virgin birth, decides that "she was Jesus Christ or anybody holy" (chapter 79).

Doubled roles. Two Boston spinsters each believe herself the reincarnation of Cleopatra, and Mr. Spitzer serves as lawyer to both (chapter 26). His brother's house holds "the twin George Washingtons fording the twin Delawares" (chapter 55). Mr. Spitzer cannot distinguish "between the ravings of one imaginary Napoleon and another imaginary Napoleon" (chapter 54).

Great lines in wrong mouths. Calderón's famous line, that life is a dream, never appears on a stage or in a sage's mouth. Miss MacIntosh cries it in her collapse (chapter 18). A drunken country doctor says it after delivering a real baby he believed was a dream (chapter 77).

Parted lovers misread. The same list of lovers turns up three times: Romeo and Juliet, Paul and Virginia, Eloise and Abelard, Tristram and Yseult. Madge remembers her friend as Juliet (chapter 6). Mr. Spitzer uses the list to ask what Hannah was (chapter 52). Miss MacIntosh reads it as a warning, "reminding her of the increase of American divorces" (chapter 65).

In each case the role no longer fits its original shape. A mythic identity, once assigned, turns out to be portable, shareable, and reversible. Young does not treat this as error. The doubled Cleopatras and the crossed Penelope show that identity in her novel is a costume that can be worn by anyone, sometimes by two people at once. The famous role survives the transfer, and the person wearing it becomes stranger and more interesting.
