Angel in the Forest

Chapter 14. The Sacred Without Transcendence: Young’s Secular Theology

Part of a reading of Angel in the Forest (1945), Marguerite Young’s history of the two failed utopias at New Harmony, Indiana.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 13 · Angel in the Forest: A Reading in Fourteen Chapters

Both utopias in this book believed in transcendence. Rapp’s people believed in a heaven above, a New Jerusalem descending from the sky. Owen’s people believed in a perfected future ahead, a New Moral World that reason would build. One pointed up and one pointed forward, but both located the real, the meaningful, the sacred, somewhere other than here. And both failed. The obvious conclusion, the one a colder writer would draw, is that meaning was a delusion all along, that with heaven and progress both exposed as lies there is nothing left but mud and malaria and the indifferent river. Young does not draw that conclusion. This is the most important thing about her, and the hardest to say without sounding sentimental, so I will try to say it carefully. She removes both guarantees, the God above and the Progress ahead, and what she finds underneath them is not emptiness. It is the ordinary world, and it turns out to be enough. Not enough as a consolation prize. Enough, full stop.

The footprint

Consider the angel’s footprint. Somewhere in old Harmony the Rappites left a slab of stone with a footprint pressed into it, said to be the mark of an angel who descended to the earth. By 1940 the weather had worn part of it away, so an odd-jobs man from twenty miles up the river was hired, at fifty cents an hour, to chisel it back into shape. Young watches him work. He chews a straw. He wears a hat with holes cut for air. He has no opinion on whether an angel was ever really there, but he reasons, sensibly enough, that if there had been no angel he would not be putting the footprints back, would he? The work means shoes for his baby, whose toes stick out. Everything in this scene is available for mockery, and a lesser writer would take it: the manufactured relic, the credulous restoration, the whole apparatus of a miracle being re-carved by a man who needs the wage. Young does not mock it, and if you read her carefully you see why. The footprint is real. It is an actual stone with an actual shape cut into it, and people actually come to look. The meaning of the footprint is made, not found, made by the Rappites who first carved it, made again by the man re-carving it now, made a third time by the pilgrim who stands before it. Young’s whole theology is in her refusal to say that a made meaning is a false one. The meaning is real precisely because it is made, here, by people, in this world, with a chisel, for shoes. There is no angel. There is a footprint, and there are the human hands that keep it, and that is not less than the sacred. It is where the sacred actually lives.

The golden rain trees

Or consider the golden rain trees. The Owenites planted them as a sign of the New Moral World that was coming. The New Moral World did not come. But in 1940, in the shops on Brewery and Church Streets, you could still buy the trees, six shoots for twenty-five cents, “guaranteed to grow in any temperate latitude.” The sign outlived the thing it was meant to signify. The utopia is dust and the tree is still in bloom, still for sale, still growing wherever the climate allows. There is a entire argument in that fact, and Young lets the fact carry it. The Owenites thought the trees pointed forward to a redeemed future. The trees did not care about the future. They were only ever trees, and being only trees, they persisted, while the redeemed future that was supposed to give them meaning quietly failed to arrive. And here is enormous. The trees did not lose their meaning when the New Moral World failed. They gained a different one. They became themselves, ordinary golden-flowering trees a person can plant in a yard for a quarter, and that ordinary fact is more durable than any moral world, and quietly more beautiful. The sacred survived, not in the sign’s pointing forward, but in the tree’s staying put.

A God that cannot see itself

Behind all of this stands the one theology in the book that Young clearly loves, the heresy of Frederick Rapp, the pantheist hidden inside the community that denied the flesh. He believed God was everything, and therefore that God was “a huge animal without eyes,” because there is nothing outside God for God to look at, “not even one small, wet violet.” This is Young’s own sense of the sacred, handed to a minor character to say. A God with no outside is a God that cannot transcend anything, because there is nowhere beyond it to stand. A God that is everywhere is a God with no special address in the sky, no elsewhere it points to. It is immanent all the way down. It is the willow and the horse-chestnut and the wet violet, and it is nothing in addition to them, nothing behind them, nothing they are a sign of. The sacred here is not a light shining through the world from somewhere else. It is the world, attended to closely enough that its own strangeness becomes visible. Not one small, wet violet is left out, and not one small, wet violet points anywhere but at itself.

The ferry

You can find this theology already at work in the book’s first paragraph, before a word of history has been spoken, if you know to look for it. The book opens on a ferry. “You come to old New Harmony by a creaking ferry.” There are sandpipers migrating between the two shores, building not nests but holes in the mudbanks, so that the hills seem coated with golden birds. There are, likely enough, two blind mules riding along as fellow passengers, with translucent logs of fallen birch tied behind them. The ferryman, likely enough, will never say a word. A barge turns at the bend of the hills, the bargeman standing with his legs spread wide against a cloudless sky. Read as scene-setting, this is atmosphere, a slow and pretty way into the book. But it is not scene-setting. It is a demonstration. Young is showing you, in the first thing she does, what her whole method comes down to: this quality of attention, this willingness to look at a blind mule and a silent ferryman and a mudbank full of ordinary birds with the same seriousness a believer brings to an altar. The sacred is already fully present on that ferry, before any angel, before any utopia, before any word of history. It is present in the attention itself. Nothing has to descend from the sky to make the crossing matter. The looking is what makes it matter, and the looking is available to anyone.

The crossing point

There is a particular feeling that Angel in the Forest is built to produce, and it belongs to the eleventh pillar, the crossing point. It is the sensation of suddenly seeing the structure you have been standing inside all along. You are on the ferry, in the ordinary present, in 1940 or in your own year, and the whole weight of what made this moment possible comes up through the ground beneath you: the utopians and the Indians one layer below them, the language you are thinking in, the trees somebody planted, the footprint somebody carved and somebody re-carved. And at the same time you feel, faintly, the weight of what you are laying down for whoever crosses this river after you. You are not passing through the world. You are the place where its past and its future cross. Young does not state this as a doctrine. She produces it as an experience, by the slow accumulation of attended-to ordinary things, until the reader feels the crossing point rather than being told about it. That is what the book is for. That is the sensation it was built to leave you holding.

What she gives us to hold

So here is the question the book is quietly answering, and here asking directly. After you take away God, and after you take away Progress, after both the heaven above and the perfect future ahead have been shown to be lies people build and then die inside of, what is left to hold onto? Young’s answer is one word, and it is not a grand word. Attention. That is all, and it is everything. The sacred is not somewhere else, waiting to redeem this world. It is here, in the wet violet and the blind mule and the re-carved footprint and the tree you can buy for a quarter, and it becomes visible only when someone bothers to look at it honestly and without turning away. This is harder than believing in heaven, because heaven does the work for you and attention you have to do yourself, every day, at whatever the wage. And it is more sustaining than believing in Progress, because Progress is always about to arrive and attention is available right now. The two utopias reached past the world for their meaning, and the world killed them for it. Young stayed in the world, on the ferry, looking, and found that the world, looked at closely enough, does not need anything beyond it. The footprint is real. The tree is still growing. The violet is still wet. All the reader has to do which is also the hardest to do is look.
