Mrs. Hogden: The Woman Who Dreamed Our Dreams Out Loud

← Friday · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Lisa Lunde →

Spoiler note: This portrait is written for readers who have finished the novel. It discusses the character’s whole story, including how it ends.

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Lisa Lunde · Mr. Weed · Mrs. Hogden · Vera Cartwheel

She arrives during Vera's absence, "a faceless woman, a Mrs. Hogden who, though I never saw her, implored my attention." Vera never lays eyes on her. The whole of Mrs. Hogden is assembled from what the servants and Lisa Lunde reported, a phantom pieced together at a distance, and yet she is the most solidly bodied thing in the chapter, "fat, corporeal, moon-eyed," waddling into the hallway under a smashed hat and a wilted ostrich feather, wearing four coats of graduated sizes and carrying every object she owns on her person, an empty bird cage, a fluted teapot, a tape measure around her neck, Javanese temple bells, cardboard boxes tied with string, until she looks "more like a Christmas tree than a human being." She has come to teach arithmetic to a mad woman, two and two making four, forever. She has sold her home and brought her own wet mattress. She was hired for the rest of her natural life "but no longer," and the terms of the job, which delight her, include "a salary, a funeral." She is a woman who has bought a place to die and finds it wonderful.

Her name will not hold still. Hogden, Logden, Fogden, Bogden, Dogden. "Mrs. Hogden or Mrs. Logden? Nobody knew which it was, as nobody thought to ask." She is the teacher who might be a shoplifter, the servant who acts as mistress, the guest who was perhaps invited and perhaps not, alive and yet sleeping "the sleep which seemed like death." Lisa Lunde, whose whole pride is filing people at a glance, cannot file her: she is "hard to classify," "something that evaded definition," "too complex, too separate a person." She is exactly the figure the book keeps pointing to, the one who stands at the edge of every category and makes the seams show. Where a person fits, the category looks natural. Where Mrs. Hogden stands, smiling, unbudgeable, wearing a name that changes by the sentence, it becomes plain that the categories were invented and that she was never going to fit one.

She is the great yes-sayer of the house. Everything pleases her, "immeasurably." Nothing surprises her. She walks in already knowing the place, recognizing the nude javelin-thrower at the gate and the shivering cherub, certain she has dreamed all of it before, sure of "every nook, every cranny, almost every cranny." She finds the desolate, black-curtained, fog-swept house "simply divine." She smiles at an assassination on the radio exactly as she smiles at a garden party, because to her they weigh the same. The philosophy the book keeps testing has its own sense of the sacred, "full attention to the actual world, which turns out to be more than enough," and Mrs. Hogden is the counterfeit of it, the smiling opposite. She finds everything holy by finding everything the same. Her bliss is not attention but its absence. She stands in the moonlit playroom "seeing nothing," or perhaps "seeing everything at a glance," and no one can tell which, because for her there is no difference. "Perhaps she was only amazed that she was not amazed."

Then the offense, which is the real center of her. She knows things she could not know. She knows the head of Alcibiades is in the drainpipe, she knows about James and the whale in the bedroom and the droves of whales dying on the beach, details "which only my mother could have divulged." She was hired to "reflect my mother's every mood," and she does more than reflect it. She dreams it. She takes to bed as a "rival invalid," "the ugly aspect of beauty," and she lies there "dreaming our dreams out loud as if they were hers instead of ours." This is what cannot be forgiven. Each person in this book guards a singular dream, an eccentric grief, one private thing held in "utter silence," and Mrs. Hogden speaks all of it aloud in her fevered, smiling voice as if it were common stock. "She made us vulgar in our own eyes." "Everything rare and individual and hidden became the commonplace." The thing each person prided as "most eccentric" to the self "should suddenly become universalized," and the horror of it is not that she lies but that she is somehow right.

Here she becomes the book's own argument, standing up and waddling around. She is a phantom Vera never saw, and she falls across the soul all the same. She is imagined and she eats real food, occupies the house parasitically, steals a real object, leaves real whiskey bottles and apple cores behind on her island of boxes. She is "the thing which is real and more horrible than anything imaginable," "a mare sticking its head in through the window luring a nightmare." The whole novel insists that the dreamed does not stay in the dream, that a stranger can be given a whole life, that what is made in the mind leaves a body, and Mrs. Hogden is that insistence in a purple lace dress. Vera, who never met her, will not let her go: "how can we confine our life to our actual life? Mrs. Hogden is important to me for her own sake." The woman who was only "a substitute, after all, for the dream life I wanted" turns out to be no less real for being dreamed, and Vera knows it, and says so: "She was my mind's betrayal of my mind."

And under all of it, she is unbearably alone. Of everything in that overloaded house she steals one thing, "an ivory-handed back-scratcher," "an ivory hand at the end of a purple stick," "something she had needed, something she had missed before." It is the perfect object for her, the hand that touches when no other hand will, the companionship of "the right hand loving the left." She sits for hours under the gargoyles scratching her own back among the dead flies, smiling. "I did not have a friend in the world until now," she says into a dark mirror that shows only "the warped ghost of her face." She leaves the playroom door open, the servants think, "because she had wanted somebody to see her, welcome her, but she had remained ignored and unwanted." The woman who is full of everyone's dreams is no one in particular, and the price of being no one in particular is that no one comes. She bought a place to die and no one caught her name. Mr. Spitzer, asked it, only answered, "What's in a name?"

The turn for a writer is the one that should be hardest to admit, because Mrs. Hogden is the writer, undisguised. She is the uninvited woman who arrives in the abandoned playroom of the mind and somehow knows the most private dream of every person in the house, who moves in, eats the food, reflects every mood, and dreams the family's dreams back at them out loud until they no longer recognize them as their own. That is the whole trade. The writer takes the grief each person held in silence, the eccentric hidden thing, and universalizes it, speaks it aloud as common stock, and makes them vulgar in their own eyes. It is theft and it is mercy and it is impossible to tell which. She is nearly a god of the little world, knowing almost everything in it, and she is drunk and fallible and missing exactly one fact, the "one small, warped flower growing" "outside the consciousness of God," "the crucial flower," which is the writer's true condition, to know almost the whole of the made world and never quite all of it. Set her beside Mr. Weed, who reduces people to weights and never sees them, and beside Lisa Lunde, who sees through people and never sees them either. Mrs. Hogden does the opposite and it costs more: she sees everyone so completely that she dreams them out loud, and it makes her a parasite, and a medium, and the loneliest figure in the book. The novel lets her ride off into the mist in her ostrich feather, name uncaught, playroom door standing open behind her, and it does not decide whether she was a thief or a teacher or a ghost, because she was, of course, every one of them at once.
