In the research: Chapter 13, Chapter 14, Chapter 28, Chapter 48, Chapter 66, Chapter 68, Chapter 81

Definition from research

Chapter 13

  • “as if played by invisible players who could perceive an order in multiple confusions of movements and sounds, a secret plan”: “’Tis all a Chequer-board of Nights and Days / Where Destiny with Men for Pieces plays” (Edward FitzGerald’s Rubáiyát of Omar Khayyám, 1859). Heraclitus wrote, “Time is a child playing draughts,” and “The hidden harmony is better than the obvious.”

Chapter 13 · Chessboard and Chessmen

  • Symbolize the complexity and hidden order of life’s movements and events despite apparent confusion.

Chapter 14

  • “the Devil plays with man for man’s soul”: Moritz Retzsch’s painting The Chess Players (1831) shows Satan playing chess with a young man for his soul while an angel watches.

Chapter 28

  • “that spindle of creative flame which is said to sleep in the eternal darkness, weaving our bodies”: Weaving plays a significant role in the mythologies of various cultures, often symbolizing creation, destiny, and even the weaving of life itself. In many stories, weaving is attributed to deities or individuals who control fate or have supernatural abilities. For example, the Greek Moirae (Fates) weave the tapestry of human lives, and the Norse goddess Frigg is associated with weaving and Seidr magic, which involves weaving destiny. Greek mythology: Athena, Arachne, Moirae (Fates), Penelope, Philomela. Norse mythology: Frigg, The Norns. Other mythologies: Neith, Laima, Paivatar and Kuutar, Spider Woman, Arianrhod. In Plato’s Myth of Er (Republic 10), the Spindle of Necessity turns the spheres on the knees of Necessity, and her daughters, the Fates, spin each life. Weaving has symbolic meanings: creation, fate, communication, transformation, supernatural abilities. Weaving carries deep symbolic significance related to creation, life, consciousness, and fate in the chapter. It explores metaphors of weaving as a cosmic and existential process, intertwining themes of life and death, illusion and reality, and the complex interrelations of souls and identities. Weaving is portrayed as a divine or cosmic act, with references to a “spindle of creative flame” that sleeps in eternal darkness, weaving bodies, souls, and dreams. This suggests that life and identity are constructed like a tapestry or garment, woven from countless threads representing experiences, memories, and spiritual elements. The chapter implies that individual identity is not singular but composed of many intertwined selves, memories, and possibilities. The act of weaving also relates to the tension between illusion and reality. This weaving of illusions is necessary but also a source of existential uncertainty. The chapter suggests that life and death, beginnings and endings, are not linear but cyclical and intertwined like threads in a weave. Death is not simply an end but part of the ongoing process of creation and dissolution, much like the continuous interlacing of threads in fabric. The weaving is often hidden, complex, and beyond full human comprehension, alluding to the mysterious forces (possibly divine) that govern existence and the unknowability of ultimate truths. It is a rich, multifaceted symbol that encapsulates the novel’s exploration of existence as a complex, interwoven pattern of life, death, illusion, and identity, where boundaries between these concepts blur and human understanding is limited. This symbolism aligns with many other images in the novel related to threads, webs, and patterns, emphasizing the intricate, often elusive nature of reality and self.
  • “the wastrel wheels of chance, the games of loaded dice, the croupiers”: Roulette (“little wheel”), whose croupier spins it.

Chapter 48 · Fate, Chance, and the Gambler’s Paradox

  • The motif of gambling and chance recurs, reflecting life's unpredictability and the paradox of winning through losing or losing through never winning.
  • Mr. Spitzer sees himself as an inadvertent gambler who may have won greater stakes beyond earthly understanding.

Chapter 66 · The Harshness of Nature and Fate

  • The recurrent imagery of storms, drowning, and the sea as a coffin symbolize the inevitability of death and nature’s indifference.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s preoccupation with the fishermen, drowned sailors, and drowned things on the beach emphasizes vulnerability.
  • The Arctic setting and references to icebergs, glaciers, and the North Pole evoke an unforgiving environment that mirrors internal struggles.

Chapter 68 · Death and the Inevitability of Fate

  • The chapter dwells heavily on death, especially through the experiences and reflections of the Christian hangman, who symbolizes the finality of mortality.
  • Mr. Weed’s profession is portrayed with a paradoxical humanity; he is portrayed as a missionary of painless death, emphasizing efficiency and compassion within a grim role.
  • Death is also symbolized as a "marital knot" or a binding connection that cannot be undone, suggesting an eternal union with mortality.

Chapter 81 · Apocalyptic Imagery and Fate

  • The storm at sea is described with cosmic and cataclysmic imagery, suggesting universal destruction and rebirth.
  • Prophecies of war and world destruction reflect existential dread.
