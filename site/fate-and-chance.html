Definition from research

Chapter 13

  • “as if played by invisible players who could perceive an order in multiple confusions of movements and sounds, a secret plan”: “’Tis all a Chequer-board of Nights and Days / Where Destiny with Men for Pieces plays” (Edward FitzGerald’s Rubáiyát of Omar Khayyám, 1859). Heraclitus wrote, “Time is a child playing draughts,” and “The hidden harmony is better than the obvious.”

Chapter 13 · Chessboard and Chessmen

  • Symbolize the complexity and hidden order of life’s movements and events despite apparent confusion.

Chapter 14

  • “the Devil plays with man for man’s soul”: Moritz Retzsch’s painting The Chess Players (1831) shows Satan playing chess with a young man for his soul while an angel watches.

Chapter 28

  • “that spindle of creative flame which is said to sleep in the eternal darkness, weaving our bodies”: Weaving plays a significant role in the mythologies of various cultures, often symbolizing creation, destiny, and even the weaving of life itself. In many stories, weaving is attributed to deities or individuals who control fate or have supernatural abilities. For example, the Greek Moirae (Fates) spin, measure, and cut the thread of human lives, and the Norse goddess Frigg is associated with weaving and seiðr magic, which involves weaving destiny. Greek mythology: Athena, Arachne, Moirae (Fates), Penelope, Philomela. Norse mythology: Frigg, The Norns. Other mythologies: Neith, Laima, Päivätär and Kuutar, Spider Woman, Arianrhod. In Plato’s Myth of Er (Republic 10), the Spindle of Necessity turns the spheres on the knees of Necessity, and her daughters, the Fates, spin each life. Weaving has symbolic meanings: creation, fate, communication, transformation, supernatural abilities. Weaving carries deep symbolic significance related to creation, life, consciousness, and fate in the chapter. It explores metaphors of weaving as a cosmic and existential process, intertwining themes of life and death, illusion and reality, and the complex interrelations of souls and identities. Weaving is portrayed as a divine or cosmic act, with references to a “spindle of creative flame” that sleeps in eternal darkness, weaving bodies, souls, and dreams. This suggests that life and identity are constructed like a tapestry or garment, woven from countless threads representing experiences, memories, and spiritual elements. The chapter implies that individual identity is not singular but composed of many intertwined selves, memories, and possibilities. The act of weaving also relates to the tension between illusion and reality. This weaving of illusions is necessary but also a source of existential uncertainty. The chapter suggests that life and death, beginnings and endings, are not linear but cyclical and intertwined like threads in a weave. Death is not simply an end but part of the ongoing process of creation and dissolution, much like the continuous interlacing of threads in fabric. The weaving is often hidden, complex, and beyond full human comprehension, alluding to the mysterious forces (possibly divine) that govern existence and the unknowability of ultimate truths. It is a rich, multifaceted symbol that encapsulates the novel’s exploration of existence as a complex, interwoven pattern of life, death, illusion, and identity, where boundaries between these concepts blur and human understanding is limited. This symbolism aligns with many other images in the novel related to threads, webs, and patterns, emphasizing the intricate, often elusive nature of reality and self.
  • “the wastrel wheels of chance, the games of loaded dice, the croupiers”: Roulette (“little wheel”), whose croupier spins it.

Chapter 37 · Order and Chance

  • “Order, had she not always said, was a mad man’s dream, the maddest dream of all.”
  • She asks “how should we live except by accident, for were we not already dead.”
  • The music shakes “the enamelled heavens and the earth like a dice box,” and she sees “stars marked like dice.”

Chapter 48 · Fate, Chance, and the Gambler’s Paradox

  • The motif of gambling and chance recurs, reflecting life’s unpredictability and the paradox of winning through losing or losing through never winning.
  • Mr. Spitzer sees himself as an inadvertent gambler who may have won greater stakes beyond earthly understanding.

Chapter 56

  • “but who was the spinner?”: Clotho, one of the three Fates, spins the thread of each life, Lachesis measures it, and Atropos cuts it.

Chapter 59

  • “that old cue-ball musician who was writing a symphony for musical billiard balls”: Composers have long played with chance. The Musikalisches Würfelspiel, a dice game for composing waltzes, was published under Mozart’s name in 1792, and John Cage composed his Music of Changes (1951) by tossing coins according to the I Ching. Mr. Spitzer’s verdict: “And if chance, it was not music.”
  • “Chance might be the overwhelming factor of the universe”: The chapter delves deeply into the philosophy of chance and randomness as it relates to the characters’ experiences and the larger metaphysical structure of the novel. The chapter, primarily through the perspective of Mr. Spitzer, explores the tension between order and chaos, certainty and uncertainty, and how chance governs much of human life and beyond. Mr. Spitzer reflects on the human tendency to seek order and predictability in life, yet he recognizes the profound role of chance and the limits of human control. Despite our efforts to impose logic, mathematics, or law, reality often eludes these frameworks, revealing itself as a chaotic, unpredictable flux. This is emphasized in the discussion of how Mr. Spitzer’s attempts to organize his life and memories are constantly disrupted by unforeseen events and errors beyond his control. The chapter highlights the dual nature of existence, where opposites coexist: life and death, truth and falsehood, light and shadow, order and chaos. Mr. Spitzer himself embodies this duality, being a man torn between certainty and doubt, reality and illusion, the living and the dead. The interplay of chance affects these states, suggesting randomness is not merely external but intrinsic to the self and consciousness. Chance is portrayed as a force that shapes reality, both creating and destroying. The metaphor of dice and gambling recurs as an allegory for life’s unpredictability and the illusion of control. Mr. Spitzer’s brother’s gambling life contrasts with his own cautious nature, underscoring differing responses to randomness: one embracing chance, the other attempting to manage or deny it. Chance extends beyond human affairs into cosmic and metaphysical dimensions. Mr. Spitzer contemplates the vastness of space, the birth and death of stars, and the fragmentation of existence, all governed by forces beyond human comprehension or control. The randomness inherent in the universe mirrors the unpredictability in individual lives and destinies. The chapter suggests that chance affects identity itself, with Mr. Spitzer questioning his own selfhood and existence. The fluidity of identity is likened to shifting shadows, reflections, and illusions, where personal continuity is disrupted by forces beyond control. Chance influences memory, perception, and even the boundary between self and other. While chance brings uncertainty and disorder, there is also a sense of inevitability and surrender. Mr. Spitzer’s reflections convey a tragic acceptance of randomness as a fundamental aspect of existence, where attempts to impose total order are ultimately futile. This acceptance is tinged with melancholy but also a recognition of the limited human role in the cosmic design. The metaphor of music recurs in the chapter, with chance associated with the unpredictable notes, rhythms, and silences that compose the cosmic and personal “music.” Time itself is seen as an artificial construct, with moments overlapping, fading, and recurring in a way that defies linear understanding. Chance governs the flow and interruption of this music, reflecting the random and cyclical nature of existence. It presents chance and randomness not merely as external phenomena but as essential, pervasive forces that shape reality, identity, and the cosmos. Through Mr. Spitzer’s anguished and reflective voice, chance emerges as both a creative and destructive principle that defies human attempts at mastery or prediction. The philosophy articulated here embraces ambiguity, duality, and the limits of knowledge, suggesting that randomness is inseparable from life and death, order and chaos, memory and identity. This nuanced treatment aligns with the novel’s broader themes of illusion, mortality, and the search for meaning amidst uncertainty. Chance is both a source of suffering and a condition for the possibility of life and experience, challenging the characters and readers to reconsider assumptions about control, fate, and reality itself.

Chapter 66 · The Harshness of Nature and Fate

  • The recurrent imagery of storms, drowning, and the sea as a coffin symbolize the inevitability of death and nature’s indifference.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s preoccupation with the fishermen, drowned sailors, and drowned things on the beach emphasizes vulnerability.
  • The Arctic setting and references to icebergs, glaciers, and the North Pole evoke an unforgiving environment that mirrors internal struggles.

Chapter 68 · Death and the Inevitability of Fate

  • The chapter dwells heavily on death, especially through the experiences and reflections of the Christian hangman, who symbolizes the finality of mortality.
  • Mr. Weed’s profession is portrayed with a paradoxical humanity; he is portrayed as a missionary of painless death, emphasizing efficiency and compassion within a grim role.
  • Death is also symbolized as a "marital knot" or a binding connection that cannot be undone, suggesting an eternal union with mortality.

Chapter 81 · Apocalyptic Imagery and Fate

  • The storm at sea is described with cosmic and cataclysmic imagery, suggesting universal destruction and rebirth.
  • Prophecies of war and world destruction reflect existential dread.
