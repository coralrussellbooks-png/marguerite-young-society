Summary

A mouse has gotten into Mr. Spitzer's papers, eating his chords and nesting in old lawsuits. Mr. Spitzer catalogs the selves he might be: the trumpeter or the trumpeter's cry, a conductor, a lamplighter, a lawyer to starfish, a man who has stamped documents with the wrong seals for years. In his wanderings he meets people lost in their own illusions, such as a woman who believes she is a birdcage. He concludes that every dream of life must leave something unrealized, or all would be complete.

Key passages

From the research

  • The opening imagery describes Mr. Spitzer’s mantle seeming "to grow older as he grew older" and winding "like the shell of mother-of-pearl around his consciousness, unconsciousness," setting a tone of his blurred existence between awareness and oblivion. The moon shining at his back and the sunlight dashing into his "dead face" symbolize this liminal state. His uncertainty about whether he is the conductor, the lamp-lighter, or the oarsman striking waves of darkness conveys his fragmented identity and roles in the cosmic or metaphysical order.
  • Mr. Spitzer is depicted as a musician whose music is "ever of the silence flowing under the sound of accidental or incidental music," and as the "spectre dreaming upon the spectre," stressing the ephemeral, elusive quality of his existence and work. The analogy of being a "lawyer to starfish" or a "lawyer to a dead coachman" or a musician who "struck two chords together with one motion of his dead, jeweled hand" suggests his role as an intermediary between the living and the dead, the real and the unreal.
  • The chapter extensively explores the blurring of selfhood, with Mr. Spitzer unable to distinguish between being the subject or object, the key or the lock, the spark or the flame. This is encapsulated in the lines: "He did not know whether he was the trumpeter or the trumpeter’s cry, the cry or the echo". The theme that "life had ever been a minority" and the idea that reality might be a dream or illusion, where distinctions between living and dead, real and unreal, are uncertain, pervade the text.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s existence is described as a product of error, a flaw in creation that "had permitted this creation," with references to a "missing keyboard" or "a string missing in the harp of God," symbolizing cosmic imperfection and his own role as a flawed instrument of fate. The passage about his stamping documents with the seal of the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad by mistake, and the consequences of these errors, highlights his confusion and the absurdity of legal and worldly order in this shadowy realm.
  • The chapter suggests that all dreamers share "one great dream informing all dreamers and all dreams, one great dreamer dreaming through us," emphasizing the unity and indistinct boundaries of consciousness. The encounter with a woman carrying an umbrella painted with zodiac signs, whose twirling supposedly controls time, symbolizes the fragile and mutable nature of reality and temporality in Mr. Spitzer’s world.
  • Mr. Spitzer mourns his brother’s death, yet the brother is "deathless," and his own life is extended by this mourning. The paradox that "the greatest happiness might come through the sorrow of waiting" and that happiness and death are intertwined is poignantly expressed.
  • Various animals and objects are used symbolically: starfish, butterflies, fireflies, moths, dragonflies, snow geese, and reindeer; each representing facets of identity, transformation, illusion, or memory. The old woman who believed she was a reindeer and was found with a golden arrow through her breast dramatizes the merging of identity and myth, fear and fate.
  • He is "the doctor who had lost his patient," (echoes Old Doc from the beginning of the story) "the lawyer who had lost his client," and the "old postman going his rounds," a figure lost in time and space, trying to uphold order in a realm of chaos and oblivion.
  • The chapter closes on Mr. Spitzer’s ambiguous state, living "by mourning the death of the deathless one," and his realization that "there was no threshold between world and world," symbolizing his liminal existence.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • A liminal figure: Neither fully alive nor dead, trapped between worlds.
  • A dreamer and a witness: He records wills and testaments but struggles to understand his own life and identity.
  • A solitary figure: Despite his loneliness, he interacts with many others’ illusions and dreams.
  • A man of paradox: His existence is filled with contradictions: he lives by dying, acts as a lawyer for the dead, and is a musician who cannot play.

Mr. Mouse

  • Mr. Mouse is presented as a figure who actively disrupts order and control, particularly in relation to Mr. Spitzer. He gnaws on legal documents, musical manuscripts, and other important papers in Mr. Spitzer's possession. This suggests that Mr. Mouse embodies a force that undermines the very foundations of Mr. Spitzer's world – law, art, and history.
  • The mouse's actions are described in chaotic terms: "jumbling symphonies," "eating all his chords," and "torn his music to ribbons". This reinforces the idea that Mr. Mouse represents a force of disorder and chaos that is actively working to dismantle Mr. Spitzer's carefully constructed world.
  • Despite his destructive tendencies, Mr. Mouse is also portrayed as a kind of "impresario". He shakes the Great Dipper of stars, pouring out new and accidental constellations. This suggests that Mr. Mouse is not simply a destructive force, but also a creative one, capable of generating new and unexpected forms of order.
  • This duality of destruction and creation is a key aspect of Mr. Mouse's character. He tears down the old, but in doing so, he makes way for the new. He is a force of change, even if that change is initially disruptive.
  • It's possible to interpret Mr. Mouse as an aspect of Mr. Spitzer's own psyche. The mouse's actions could be seen as a manifestation of Mr. Spitzer's subconscious desires or fears. The fact that Mr. Spitzer "opposed to Mr. Mouse’s will his will" suggests an internal conflict between order and chaos within Mr. Spitzer himself.
  • The description of Mr. Mouse sleeping on a bed made of scraps of old lawsuits and wearing the papers lining Mr. Spitzer's hat further supports the idea that Mr. Mouse is closely connected to Mr. Spitzer's inner world.

  • Chaos theory is the study of how seemingly unpredictable and complex systems, like weather or ecosystems, can have underlying order and patterns, even though they are highly sensitive to initial conditions. It highlights that small changes in these conditions can lead to vastly different outcomes over time, a concept often referred to as the "butterfly effect". Chaotic systems are often nonlinear, meaning that small changes in inputs don't necessarily produce proportional changes in outputs. This can lead to complex and unpredictable behavior. A tiny change in the starting point of a chaotic system can cause a large difference in its eventual state. The classic example is the "butterfly effect," where a butterfly flapping its wings in one location might eventually contribute to a tornado forming elsewhere. While chaotic systems appear random, chaos theory suggests that there are often underlying patterns, feedback loops, and self-organization within them. Fractals, geometric shapes with self-similar patterns at different scales, are often used to model chaotic systems. They capture the complexity and irregular nature of these systems. Chaos theory has applications in various fields, including weather forecasting, fluid dynamics, biology, economics, and even social sciences.

  • Benjamin Franklin was an American polymath: a writer, scientist, inventor, statesman, diplomat, printer, publisher and political philosopher. Among the most influential intellectuals of his time, Franklin was one of the Founding Fathers of the United States; a drafter and signer of the Declaration of Independence; and the first postmaster general. Born in the Province of Massachusetts Bay, Franklin became a successful newspaper editor and printer in Philadelphia, the leading city in the colonies, publishing The Pennsylvania Gazette at age 23. He became wealthy publishing this and Poor Richard's Almanack, which he wrote under the pseudonym "Richard Saunders". After 1767, he was associated with the Pennsylvania Chronicle, a newspaper known for its revolutionary sentiments and criticisms of the policies of the British Parliament and the Crown. He pioneered and was the first president of the Academy and College of Philadelphia, which opened in 1751 and later became the University of Pennsylvania. He organized and was the first secretary of the American Philosophical Society and was elected its president in 1769. He was appointed deputy postmaster-general for the British colonies in 1753, which enabled him to set up the first national communications network. Franklin was active in community affairs and colonial and state politics, as well as national and international affairs. He became a hero in America when, as an agent in London for several colonies, he spearheaded the repeal of the unpopular Stamp Act by the British Parliament. An accomplished diplomat, he was widely admired as the first U.S. ambassador to France and was a major figure in the development of positive Franco–American relations. His efforts proved vital in securing French aid for the American Revolution. From 1785 to 1788, he served as President of Pennsylvania. At some points in his life, he owned slaves and ran "for sale" ads for slaves in his newspaper, but by the late 1750s, he began arguing against slavery, became an active abolitionist, and promoted the education and integration of African Americans into U.S. society. As a scientist, Franklin's studies of electricity made him a major figure in the American Enlightenment and the history of physics. He also charted and named the Gulf Stream current. His numerous important inventions include the lightning rod, bifocals, glass harmonica and the Franklin stove. He founded many civic organizations, including the Library Company, Philadelphia's first fire department, and the University of Pennsylvania. Franklin's life and legacy of scientific and political achievement, and his status as one of America's most influential Founding Fathers, have seen him honored for more than two centuries after his death on the $100 bill and in the names of warships, many towns and counties, educational institutions and corporations, as well as in numerous cultural references and a portrait in the Oval Office.   • The Ben Franklin effect is a psychological phenomenon in which people like someone more after doing a favor for them. An explanation for this is cognitive dissonance. People reason that they help others because they like them, even if they do not, because their minds struggle to maintain logical consistency between their actions and perceptions. The Benjamin Franklin effect, in other words, is the result of one's concept of self coming under attack. Every person develops a persona, and that persona persists because inconsistencies in one's personal narrative get rewritten, redacted, and misinterpreted. Benjamin Franklin, after whom the effect is named, quoted what he described as an "old maxim" in his autobiography: "He that has once done you a kindness will be more ready to do you another, than he whom you yourself have obliged." Franklin explains how he dealt with the animosity of a rival legislator when he served in the Pennsylvania Assembly in the 18th century: “Having heard that he had in his library a certain very scarce and curious book, I wrote a note to him, expressing my desire of perusing that book, and requesting he would do me the favour of lending it to me for a few days. He sent it immediately, and I return'd it in about a week with another note, expressing strongly my sense of the favour. When we next met in the House, he spoke to me (which he had never done before), and with great civility; and he ever after manifested a readiness to serve me on all occasions, so that we became great friends, and our friendship continued to his death.”
  • Boethius was a Roman senator, consul, magister officiorum, polymath, historian, and philosopher of the Early Middle Ages. He was a central figure in the translation of the Greek classics into Latin, a precursor to the Scholastic movement, and, along with Cassiodorus, one of the two leading Christian scholars of the 6th century. The local cult of Boethius in the Diocese of Pavia was sanctioned by the Sacred Congregation of Rites in 1883, confirming the diocese's custom of honoring him on the 23rd of October. Boethius was born in Rome a few years after the forced abdication of the last Western Roman emperor, Romulus Augustulus. A member of the Anicii family, he was orphaned following the family's sudden decline and was raised by Quintus Aurelius Memmius Symmachus, a later consul. After mastering both Latin and Greek in his youth, Boethius rose to prominence as a statesman during the Ostrogothic Kingdom, becoming a senator by age 25, a consul by age 33, and later chosen as a personal advisor to Theodoric the Great. In seeking to reconcile the teachings of Plato and Aristotle with Christian theology, Boethius sought to translate the entirety of the Greek classics for Western scholars. He published numerous transcriptions and commentaries of the works of Nicomachus, Porphyry, and Cicero, among others, and wrote extensively on matters concerning music, mathematics, and theology. His Arithmetica, geometria, et musica became the classical quadrivium or the basis of the liberal arts curriculum in the Middle Ages. This grouping emphasizes the interconnectedness of these disciplines, with arithmetic and geometry as foundational, and music was understood as a manifestation of mathematical principles. These disciplines were seen as pathways to understanding the underlying mathematical structure of the universe and were considered essential for a well-rounded education.
  • Arithmetica dealt with the properties and relationships of numbers. It was not just about calculation but also about understanding the nature of numbers themselves.
  • Geometria focused on the study of shapes, spatial relationships, and the measurement of the physical world.
  • Musica was not just about the art of musical performance, but rather the mathematical study of musical harmony, ratios, and proportions. It explored the mathematical relationships between musical sounds.
  • Though his translations were unfinished following an untimely death, it is largely due to them that the works of Aristotle survived into the Renaissance. Despite his successes as a senior official, Boethius became deeply unpopular among other members of the Ostrogothic court for denouncing the extensive corruption prevalent among other members of government. After publicly defending fellow consul Caecina Albinus from charges of conspiracy, he was imprisoned by Theodoric around the year 523. While jailed Boethius wrote On the Consolation of Philosophy, a philosophical treatise on fortune, death, and other issues which became one of the most influential and widely reproduced works of the Early Middle Ages. He was tortured and executed in 524, becoming a martyr in the Christian faith by tradition.
  • Diogenes the Cynic was an ancient Greek philosopher and one of the founders of Cynicism. Renowned for his ascetic lifestyle, biting wit, and radical critiques of social conventions, he became a legendary figure whose life and teachings have been recounted, often through anecdote, in both antiquity and later cultural traditions. Born to a prosperous family in Sinope, his life took a dramatic turn following a scandal involving the defacement of coinage, an event that led to his exile and ultimately his radical rejection of conventional values. Embracing a life of poverty and self-sufficiency, he became famous for his unconventional behaviors that openly challenged societal norms, such as living in a jar or wandering public spaces with a lit lantern in daylight, claiming to be "looking for a man". Diogenes advocated for a return to nature, the renunciation of materialism, and introduced early ideas of cosmopolitanism by proclaiming himself a "citizen of the world". His memorable encounters, including a legendary exchange with Alexander the Great, along with various accounts of his death, have made him a lasting symbol of philosophical defiance to established authorities and artificial values.

  • Sir Karl Raimund Popper was an Austrian–British philosopher, academic and social commentator. One of the 20th century's most influential philosophers of science, Popper is known for his rejection of the classical inductivist views on the scientific method in favor of empirical falsification, and for founding the Department of Philosophy at the London School of Economics. According to Popper, a theory in the empirical sciences can never be proven, but it can be falsified, meaning that it can (and should) be scrutinized with decisive experiments. Popper was opposed to the classical justificationist account of knowledge, which he replaced with "the first non-justificational philosophy of criticism in the history of philosophy", namely critical rationalism. In political discourse, he is known for his vigorous defense of liberal democracy and the principles of social criticism that he believed made a flourishing open society possible. His political thought resides within the camp of Enlightenment rationalism and humanism. He was a dogged opponent of totalitarianism, nationalism, fascism, romanticism, collectivism, and other kinds of (in Popper's view) reactionary and irrational ideas, and identified modern liberal democracies as the best-to-date embodiment of an open society.
  • Popper's Key Ideas:
  • Falsification: Popper argued that scientific theories should be falsifiable, meaning that they should be capable of being proven wrong through empirical testing. A statement or belief that cannot be falsified is not scientific, even if it seems profound.
  • Conjecture and Refutation: Popper saw scientific progress as a process of conjecture (making bold guesses or hypotheses) and refutation (attempting to disprove those hypotheses through testing).
  • The Open Society: Popper advocated for an "open society" that embraces critical thinking, tolerance, and the willingness to challenge established ideas.
  • Objective Knowledge: Popper believed in the existence of objective knowledge, but stressed that our access to it is always provisional and subject to revision.
  • The Problem of Induction: Popper rejected the idea that we can arrive at certain knowledge through induction (generalizing from specific observations). He argued that no matter how many confirming instances we have, we can never be sure that the next observation will not contradict our generalization.
  • Historicism is the belief that history unfolds according to fixed, predictable laws or patterns leading towards a predetermined endpoint. This perspective often fuels an “obsession with the past” by seeking to discern these laws and trends from past events in order to predict and even shape the future.
  • Historicists misunderstand the nature of scientific laws and the possibility of prediction. Popper claimed that genuine scientific laws are unconditionally valid and do not depend on specific initial conditions. Historicists mistakenly elevate historical trends to immutable laws, leading to unconditional properties about the future course of history, rather than conditional scientific predictions.
  • Popper argued that the growth of human knowledge significantly influences the course of history. Since predicting future scientific discoveries or advancements in knowledge is impossible, predicting the future course of human history is also impossible.
  • Human history is a unique and non-repetitive process. Knowledge gained from past events may not necessarily be applicable to predicting the future as historicists assume.
  • The belief in historicism is politically dangerous. It underpins totalitarian ideologies by suggesting that a future, ideal state of society is predetermined. This belief can lead to dangerous attempts at utopian social engineering, where societies are radically reshaped based on an assumed historical blueprint, often disregarding individual freedoms and generating unforeseen negative consequences.
