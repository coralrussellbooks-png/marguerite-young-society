To All My Darlings

Chapter 51

On Chapter 51 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 6, 2026

← Chapter 50 · Home · Chapter 52 →

Summary

Puffing along the shore with his mantle growing around him, Mr. Spitzer cannot tell whether he is the conductor lifting his baton, the lamp-lighter, or the oarsman striking light from the waves of darkness. He is the doctor who has lost his patient, the lawyer who has lost his client, lawyer to a starfish, a dead coachman, and the moon in the cloud, and the old postman knocking at every door. He fears being right more than being wrong, removes the stones from a road and puts one back lest he change rivers in their course, and trusts the past more than the future. He signs illegal documents as if they were legal and stamps wills with the wrong seals, once with the seal of the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad, while Mr. Mouse gnaws his lawsuits and eats his chords. Respecting other people’s mirages because he is a mirage himself, he meets a lady whose twirling zodiac umbrella moves time, an old woman who thinks she is a bird cage, deaf music critics who hear every error, two pianists who play in perfect time only when a thousand miles apart, and an old woman who believed she was a reindeer and was found with a golden arrow through her breast. He lives by mourning the death of the deathless one, and he concludes that the dream of life, like that of death, must ever contain the unrealized, else all would be complete.

Key passages

From the research

  • The opening imagery describes Mr. Spitzer’s mantle seeming “to grow older as he grew older” and winding “like the shell of mother-of-pearl around his consciousness, unconsciousness,” setting a tone of his blurred existence between awareness and oblivion. The moon shining at his back and the sunlight dashing into his “dead face” symbolize this liminal state. His uncertainty about whether he is the conductor, the lamp-lighter, or the oarsman striking waves of darkness conveys his fragmented identity and roles in the cosmic or metaphysical order.
  • Mr. Spitzer is depicted as a musician whose music is “ever of the silence flowing under the sound of accidental or incidental music,” and as the “spectre dreaming upon the spectre,” stressing the ephemeral, elusive quality of his existence and work. The analogy of being a “lawyer to starfish” or a “lawyer to a dead coachman” or a musician who “struck two chords together with one motion of his dead, jeweled hand” suggests his role as an intermediary between the living and the dead, the real and the unreal.
  • The chapter extensively explores the blurring of selfhood, with Mr. Spitzer unable to distinguish between being the subject or object, the key or the lock, the spark or the flame. This is encapsulated in the lines: “He did not know whether he was the trumpeter or the trumpeter’s cry, the cry or the echo.” The theme that “life had ever been a minority” and the idea that reality might be a dream or illusion, where distinctions between living and dead, real and unreal, are uncertain, pervade the text.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s existence is described as a product of error, a flaw in creation that “had permitted this creation,” with references to a “missing keyboard” or “a string missing in the harp of God,” symbolizing cosmic imperfection and his own role as a flawed instrument of fate. The passage about his stamping documents with the seal of the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad by mistake, and the consequences of these errors, highlights his confusion and the absurdity of legal and worldly order in this shadowy realm.
  • The chapter suggests that all dreamers share “one great dream informing all dreamers and all dreams, one great dreamer dreaming through us,” emphasizing the unity and indistinct boundaries of consciousness. The encounter with a woman carrying an umbrella painted with zodiac signs, whose twirling supposedly controls time, symbolizes the fragile and mutable nature of reality and temporality in Mr. Spitzer’s world.
  • Mr. Spitzer mourns his brother’s death, yet the brother is “deathless,” and his own life is extended by this mourning. The paradox that “the greatest happiness might come through the sorrow of waiting” and that happiness and death are intertwined is poignantly expressed.
  • Various animals and objects are used symbolically: starfish, butterflies, fireflies, moths, dragonflies, snow geese, and reindeer; each representing facets of identity, transformation, illusion, or memory. The old woman who believed she was a reindeer and was found with a golden arrow through her breast dramatizes the merging of identity and myth, fear and fate.
  • He is “the doctor who had lost his patient,” (echoes Old Doc from the beginning of the story) “the lawyer who had lost his client,” and the “old postman going his rounds,” a figure lost in time and space, trying to uphold order in a realm of chaos and oblivion.
  • The chapter closes on Mr. Spitzer’s ambiguous state, living “by mourning the death of the deathless one,” and his realization that “there was no threshold between world and world,” symbolizing his liminal existence.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “A flaw in the creation had permitted this creation.” How can a flaw be the reason anything exists?
  • “He had made of a dead kite-flyer his premise, of a muted bell-ringer his theory, of an old lamp-lighter his love.” What is Mr. Spitzer’s philosophy?
  • “His effort to go straight had made him crooked.” Why does every straight road turn circular for him?

Characters

  • Mr. Mouse gnaws Mr. Spitzer’s lawsuits and symphonies, and yet “the greatest impresario was the mouse.” Is the mouse his enemy or his collaborator?
  • Mr. Spitzer “would remove every stone from a road” and then put a stone back. What does his caution protect?
  • The old woman who believed she was a reindeer “was found in bed with a golden arrow driven through her breast.” What does her fate say about illusions that come true?

Connections

  • In Chapter 50 Mr. Spitzer feared “that one should be one.” Here he “might have dropped dead if he had discovered… that his brother lived.” Why is success what he fears?
  • The opium lady travels the world by night with a black coachman whose face she has never seen. Where have the coachman and the night journeys appeared before?
  • Mr. Spitzer stamps wills with the seal of the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad and imagines a world of all trains that have passed away. How do trains and stations carry the dead through the novel?

Reflection

  • “In most lives, three minutes of happiness seemed perhaps all there might be.” Is that too little, or enough?
  • “He had learned to respect other people’s mirages.” Should one?
  • “For the dream of life, even like that of death, must ever contain within it an element of the unrealized.” Is what remains unrealized a loss?

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • A liminal figure: Neither fully alive nor dead, trapped between worlds.
  • A dreamer and a witness: He records wills and testaments but struggles to understand his own life and identity.
  • A solitary figure: Despite his loneliness, he interacts with many others’ illusions and dreams.
  • A man of paradox: His existence is filled with contradictions: he lives by dying, acts as a lawyer for the dead, and is a musician who cannot play.

Mr. Mouse

  • Mr. Mouse is presented as a figure who actively disrupts order and control, particularly in relation to Mr. Spitzer. He gnaws on legal documents, musical manuscripts, and other important papers in Mr. Spitzer’s possession. This suggests that Mr. Mouse embodies a force that undermines the very foundations of Mr. Spitzer’s world: law, art, and history.
  • The mouse’s actions are described in chaotic terms: “jumbling symphonies,” “eating all his chords,” and “torn his music to ribbons.” This reinforces the idea that Mr. Mouse represents a force of disorder and chaos that is actively working to dismantle Mr. Spitzer’s carefully constructed world.
  • Despite his destructive tendencies, Mr. Mouse is also portrayed as a kind of “impresario.” He shakes the Great Dipper of stars, pouring out new and accidental constellations. This suggests that Mr. Mouse is not simply a destructive force, but also a creative one, capable of generating new and unexpected forms of order.
  • This duality of destruction and creation is a key aspect of Mr. Mouse’s character. He tears down the old, but in doing so, he makes way for the new. He is a force of change, even if that change is initially disruptive.
  • It is possible to interpret Mr. Mouse as an aspect of Mr. Spitzer’s own psyche. The mouse’s actions could be seen as a manifestation of Mr. Spitzer’s subconscious desires or fears. The fact that Mr. Spitzer “opposed to Mr. Mouse’s will his will” suggests an internal conflict between order and chaos within Mr. Spitzer himself.
  • The description of Mr. Mouse sleeping on a bed made of scraps of old lawsuits and wearing the papers lining Mr. Spitzer’s hat further supports the idea that Mr. Mouse is closely connected to Mr. Spitzer’s inner world.

Research: Themes

Illusion and Reality

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Mr. Spitzer exists in a liminal state where reality and illusion blur. The text repeatedly questions what is real and what is false, suggesting that all experiences might be dreamlike or illusory.
  • The motif of mirrors, shadows, and reflections emphasizes the uncertainty of identity and perception.
  • The idea that life and death, subject and object, love and loss are intertwined illusions challenges the reader to reconsider fixed realities.

Life, Death, and Immortality

See: Death and Mortality

  • The chapter meditates on death not as an end but as a continuation or transformation, death and life flow into each other.
  • Mr. Spitzer acts as a lawyer or doctor to the dead, a figure who navigates the borderland between the living and the dead, the conscious and unconscious.
  • His existence is marked by paradoxes: he is dead yet alive, the dreamer and the dreamed, the musician and the silent instrument.

Identity and Multiplicity

See: Identity and the Self

  • Mr. Spitzer is described as a man of many parts, many identities, reflecting a fragmented self that cannot be pinned down to a single essence.
  • There are references to his “dead twin,” suggesting a duality or multiplicity of selves.
  • His confusion over who he is (lawyer, doctor, musician, lamp-lighter) which symbolizes the fluidity and elusiveness of identity.

The Role of the Lawyer and the Musician

See: Music, Sound, and Silence

  • Mr. Spitzer’s profession as a lawyer handling wills and testaments serves as a metaphor for dealing with endings, legacies, and the passage from life to death.
  • He is also a musician, but one whose music is silent, unplayed, or forgotten, symbolizing unrealized potential and the fragmentation of harmony in life.
  • The blending of these roles points to the complex interplay between order (law) and chaos (music, dreams).

The Cosmos and Nature as Metaphor

See: Nature, the Sea, and the Cosmos

  • The imagery of stars, moons, seas, waves, and skies recurs throughout, symbolizing the vastness and mystery of existence, and Mr. Spitzer’s small but luminous place within it.
  • Natural elements like sand, shells, fireflies, and snowflakes are used to reflect fragility, ephemerality, and the beauty of fleeting moments.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

The Mantle and Cape

See: Clothing, Hats, and Skirts

  • Mr. Spitzer’s mantle or cape is described as growing with him, winding like mother-of-pearl around his consciousness and unconsciousness, symbolizing the weight of existence and the intertwining of life and death.

The Baton / Lamp-lighter’s Pole

See: Lamps, Candles, and Light · Music and Instruments

  • The baton he lifts symbolizes control and orchestration, but he doubts whether he is a conductor or merely a lamp-lighter or oarsman, indicating his ambivalence about his role in the universe.

The Star / Moon / Light

See: Moon and Stars · Lamps, Candles, and Light

  • Light and celestial bodies symbolize knowledge, life, and death. Mr. Spitzer is associated with a “supernumerary star,” a star beyond the normal count, symbolizing his unique, marginal existence.

The Mouse

  • The mouse gnawing on legal and musical papers represents decay, destruction, and the futility of human endeavors against time and oblivion.

The Umbrella with Zodiac Signs

See: The Umbrella

  • Represents fate, time, and cosmic cycles, as well as the interconnectedness of personal destiny with universal forces.

Mr. Spitzer’s consciousness is fragmented and overwhelmed by countless signals and impressions, some clear, some obscure, and the semaphores are that confusing signaling around and within him, which he tries to interpret amid the fog and chaos of his mind and life.

  • “he might change rivers in their course”: Mr. Spitzer’s fear is the butterfly effect of chaos theory: small, subtle causes yielding large, unpredictable consequences in a complex, interwoven reality. Chaos theory studies systems, like weather or ecosystems, so sensitive to their starting conditions that a tiny change can lead to vastly different outcomes, though underlying patterns, feedback loops, and self-organization remain (Wikipedia). The meteorologist Edward Lorenz named the effect in a 1972 talk, “Predictability: Does the Flap of a Butterfly’s Wings in Brazil Set Off a Tornado in Texas?” Ray Bradbury had already imagined it in “A Sound of Thunder” (1952), where a time traveler steps on a butterfly and changes history. “For the chaos had its pattern.” See Time, Physics, and Chaos Theory and Order, Chaos, and Discipline.
  • “ferried by a Charon of sea shells”: Charon is the ferryman of Hades in Greek mythology; he returns later in the chapter, reaching “with his oar upon the darkening waters.” See Charon in the Chapter 46 resources. See Greek Mythology.
  • “the prisms of Mr. Chandelier”: See Mr. Chandelier. See Mr. Chandelier.
  • “Was life a legal fiction”: A legal fiction is something the law assumes to be true for its purposes though it may not be literally true. Mr. Spitzer asks whether life itself is such a fiction, taken on faith but unprovable either way. The imaginary house with its ivory porches no waves should ever reach is a fragile, beautiful, perhaps illusory construct, complete-seeming but isolated and fading, “its lights already lost.” What seems real and substantial may be a delicate illusion that cannot withstand change and mortality. See Guilt, Innocence, and Justice.
  • “scattering clouds of ink like the ink of the squid”: Squid and octopus release ink in blobs held together by mucus, pseudomorphs, that keep roughly the shape of the animal and draw a predator’s attack while the animal escapes.
  • “scattering sand over his paper from a ram’s horn”: Before blotting paper came into wide use in the nineteenth century, writers dried wet ink by sprinkling sand from a pounce pot or sander.
  • “great windhovers sleeping on the wing”: The windhover is the kestrel, which hangs on the wind; “I caught this morning morning’s minion, kingdom of daylight’s dauphin, dapple-dawn-drawn Falcon” (Gerard Manley Hopkins, “The Windhover,” written 1877, published 1918).   • “some old lazar with his tinkling bell”: Lepers in the Middle Ages had to warn of their approach with a bell or clapper; lazar comes from the beggar Lazarus, “full of sores” (Luke 16:20). See The Bible.
  • “a resurrecting groundhog who had not noticed the presence of his shadow”: By folk belief, if the groundhog sees his shadow on February 2 there will be six more weeks of winter, and if he does not, an early spring; the Punxsutawney, Pennsylvania, Groundhog Day began in 1887.
  • “his circular eye which seemed to turn in his forehead”: The third-eye is a mystical and esoteric concept of a speculative invisible eye in the middle of the forehead which has perception beyond ordinary sight. In Hindu tradition it is the ajna chakra between the brows, and the third eye of Shiva burns with the fire of destruction. See Hinduism.

  • “Who knew what was real?”: Descartes in Meditation I is skeptical of knowledge. If a person cannot know if they are dreaming when they have an experience then a person may not have knowledge of the physical world. The Wisdom Paradox is the more a person learns the more he is exposed to what he does not know. The variety of ways to see the world is infinite. In the philosophy of consciousness there are generally two camps: dualism (the conscious mind or conscious mental state is non-physical in some sense) and materialism (the mind is the brain and is caused by neural activity). “I see so manifestly that there are no certain indications by which we may clearly distinguish wakefulness from sleep” (René Descartes, Meditations on First Philosophy, Meditation I, 1641, trans. Elizabeth S. Haldane, 1911). See Descartes and Dualism.
    • Mysterianism, named by Owen Flanagan in 1991 and argued by Colin McGinn, holds that the human mind may be too limited to solve the mind-body problem, since the cognitive capabilities of all organisms are limited by biology. William James found this liberating in “Is Life Worth Living?” (1895), where a dog in a laboratory cannot grasp the purposes of the men around it, and wrote, “I believe rather that we stand in much the same relation to the whole of the universe as our canine and feline pets do to the whole of human life” (Pragmatism, 1907, lecture 8). See William James and Pragmatism.

  • “the glassy cricket singing in the chimney fire”: Charles Dickens’s The Cricket on the Hearth (1845) is a fairy tale of a household guarded by the cricket chirping by the fire.
  • “Man might be the finest musician”: A person may have great gifts, but without connection, engagement, or the response of others those gifts fall silent, like an instrument no one plays, “an empty wooden box like the coffin of music strung with a few cat-gut strings.” Life gains meaning through being expressed, “even if played upon only by the fingers of the wind,” and the line laments the silence after death, part of the novel’s interplay of presence and absence, sound and silence.   • “He had made of a dead kite-flyer his premise”: These may refer to Ben Franklin, Boethius, and Diogenes. The muted bell-ringer may also be Quasimodo, the deaf bell-ringer of Victor Hugo’s The Hunchback of Notre-Dame (1831).
    • Benjamin Franklin (1706-1790) was an American polymath: printer, writer, scientist, inventor, statesman, and diplomat, one of the Founding Fathers, a drafter and signer of the Declaration of Independence, and the first postmaster general. His kite experiment of 1752 showed that lightning is electricity, and Anne Robert Jacques Turgot said of him, “Eripuit fulmen cœlo, mox sceptra tyrannis” (“He snatched lightning from the sky and the scepter from tyrants”). At some points in his life he owned slaves and ran “for sale” ads for slaves in his newspaper, but by the late 1750s he began arguing against slavery, became an active abolitionist, and promoted the education and integration of African Americans. He invented the lightning rod, bifocals, the glass harmonica, and the Franklin stove. (Wikipedia)
    • The Ben Franklin effect is the finding that people like someone more after doing him a favor, explained by cognitive dissonance. Franklin quoted an “old maxim” in his autobiography: “He that has once done you a kindness will be more ready to do you another, than he whom you yourself have obliged,” and told how he won over a rival legislator by borrowing a rare book from him. (Wikipedia)
    • Boethius (about 477-524) was a Roman senator, consul, and philosopher who sought to translate Plato and Aristotle into Latin; largely through his translations the works of Aristotle survived. His works on arithmetic, geometry, and music, with astronomy, formed the quadrivium, the core of the medieval liberal arts, in which music was the mathematical study of harmony, ratio, and proportion rather than performance. Imprisoned by Theodoric for defending a fellow consul against charges of conspiracy, he wrote On the Consolation of Philosophy, a treatise on fortune and death, before he was tortured and executed in 524. (Wikipedia) See Aristotle.
    • Diogenes the Cynic (about 412-323 BCE) rejected wealth and convention, lived in a large jar, called himself “a citizen of the world,” and carried a lit lantern in daylight, saying he was looking for a man (Diogenes Laertius 6.41). He is the old lamp-lighter. (Wikipedia) See Lamps, Candles, and Light.
  • “if reality had been a horse”: Mr. Spitzer thinks that if reality was a horse or a ship he would only know one small part of the thing. This is similar to the Blind Men and the Elephant parable whose moral is that humans have a tendency to claim absolute truth based on their limited, subjective experience and they ignore others’ limited, subjective experiences which may be equally true. The parable is told in the Buddhist Udana (6.4) and in John Godfrey Saxe’s poem “The Blind Men and the Elephant” (1872). See Buddhism and Horses and Carriages.
  • “Had he given birth to his dead twin”: See Peron Spitzer. See Peron Spitzer and Twins and Doubles.
  • “all riding for the Holy Grail”: See the Holy Grail in the Chapter 47 resources.
  • “Perhaps he was twelve cloaks”: Mr. Spitzer cannot define himself; he shifts between roles and images. Twelve cloaks suggest concealment, disguise, and many layers of identity, the number perhaps recalling the twelve apostles or the twelve signs of the zodiac. The islands of flowers in the flood are beauty and fragility amid chaos, isolated havens like the pockets of meaning he makes in a world of flux. The stalled sail is stagnation, the sail breaking into flame sudden and violent change. The ram’s horn, the shofar, is a call to awakening, remembrance, and the divine, and “metamorphosed” makes him not a fixed entity but a being always changing. See Judaism.
  • “a man of many gates, gates of ivory, gates of gold”: “There are two gates through which these unsubstantial fancies proceed; the one is of horn, and the other ivory. Those that come through the gate of ivory are fatuous, but those from the gate of horn mean something to those that see them” (Homer, Odyssey 19, trans. Samuel Butler, 1900). See Homer’s Epics and Dreams and Imagination.
  • “he trusted the past more than he trusted the future”: Mr. Spitzer’s uncertainty about identity, reality, and knowledge fits Popper’s view that knowledge is provisional: “Perhaps all definitions failed where each was each and each was the other.” He is “a man of many parts, many branches, a man of many faded horizons,” an identity that defies simple categorization, as for Popper knowledge is never complete. His trust of the past comes from uncertainty about the present and future rather than from belief in the past’s truth, and Popper would caution against blindly accepting any authority, including the authority of the past. He knows he may be “the decoy and not the man,” “deceived by many facades,” his perceptions unreliable, and he has found “the impossibility of committing suicide, for he had died merely by living, by continuing from moment to moment.” In the book, Open Society, Popper warned of the dangers of idealizing the past and assuming there is some perfect past that society can return to and make everything better. For example, the idea that the 1950s were perfect and that society could just go back to that time. Or the Dark Enlightenment bros wanting to go back even further to The Gilded Age and its perceived “perfection.”
    • Sir Karl Popper (1902-1994) was an Austrian-British philosopher of science who rejected the classical inductivist view of the scientific method in favor of empirical falsification: a theory in the empirical sciences can never be proven, but it can be falsified, and should be scrutinized with decisive experiments. Scientific progress is conjecture and refutation, bold guesses tested by attempts to disprove them; objective knowledge exists, but access to it is always provisional; and no number of confirming instances can make an induction certain. He defended liberal democracy and the open society, which embraces critical thinking, tolerance, and the willingness to challenge established ideas, and opposed totalitarianism, nationalism, and fascism (The Open Society and Its Enemies, 1945). (Wikipedia)
    • Historicism is the belief that history unfolds according to fixed laws toward a predetermined end. Popper argued that historicists mistake historical trends for laws; that since the growth of knowledge shapes history and future discoveries cannot be predicted, the future course of history cannot be predicted; that history is unique and non-repetitive; and that historicism is politically dangerous, underpinning totalitarian ideologies and utopian social engineering that disregard individual freedom (The Poverty of Historicism, 1957). (Wikipedia)
  • “Two tenants wrangled in an empty house”: Mr. Spitzer is in constant conflict with himself: the empty house is the body, and the wrangling tenants, body and soul, suggest no fixed self or objective truth about who he is. He would sue for “a false spring,” an illusion not to be trusted, and he is caught “between two worlds, between two lives, between two deaths.” Gorgias’s three claims run through the passage: he cannot know himself; the drifting mirrors show that what could be known could not be communicated; he mixes up greetings and farewells. “He had already gone, and yet he remained,” the paradox of existence in a world where nothing truly exists, and his confusing his conscience with his consciousness shows a world without objective value, where accuracy becomes irrelevant.
    • Gorgias (about 483-376 BCE) was a Greek sophist and rhetorician from Leontinoi in Sicily. W. K. C. Guthrie writes that like other sophists he traveled from city to city, giving public exhibitions at Olympia and Delphi and charging fees for his instruction, and that “a special feature of his displays was to ask miscellaneous questions from the audience and give impromptu replies” (A History of Greek Philosophy, vol. 3, 1969). He has been called “Gorgias the Nihilist,” though how well the epithet fits is disputed, and he carried rhetoric from Sicily to Athens. (Wikipedia)
    • In his On Non-Existence, as Sextus Empiricus reports it (Against the Logicians 1.65-87), Gorgias argued that nothing exists; that if something did exist, it could not be known; and that if something could be known, it could not be communicated.
  • “the great planetary wheel turning like a ferris wheel”: George Ferris built the first Ferris wheel for the Chicago World’s Fair of 1893. Fortune’s wheel is older: in Boethius, Fortune says, “I turn the wheel that spins. I delight to see the high come down and the low ascend” (The Consolation of Philosophy 2.2, trans. W. V. Cooper, 1902). See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “A flaw in the creation had permitted this creation”: In the Kabbalah of Isaac Luria (sixteenth century), the vessels that held God’s light shattered at the creation, the shevirat ha-kelim, the breaking of the vessels, so that the world was made through a break. Mr. Spitzer’s errors “had never evolved from an original sin,” Augustine’s doctrine of the sin inherited from Adam. See Judaism and Augustine and Christian Theology.
  • “a golden-footed compass point moving through a cloud”: “He took the golden compasses, prepared / In God’s eternal store, to circumscribe / This universe” (John Milton, Paradise Lost 7.225-227), the figure William Blake drew in The Ancient of Days (1794).
  • “a bridge made of the wings of birds”: In the Chinese legend of the Cowherd and the Weaver Girl, the lovers are parted by the Milky Way and may meet once a year, on the seventh night of the seventh month, on a bridge made of magpies’ wings.
  • “He had learned to respect other people’s mirages”: Because he exists only as a mirage himself, Mr. Spitzer has learned to respect the illusions of others, accepting that each person constructs a reality that is deeply personal and meaningful. Yet he dreams of “that blind Eros” for whom there is no time and no face of love, a love beyond the individual and beyond life and death, relying on no recognition of subject or object. The only light he sees by shines “through his finger-tips lighting clouds and fogs,” insight from an intuitive, almost mystical source, turning “old trees to trees of fire… as if a star had passed where there was no star.” The shore beyond is “bordered by no sea,” inviting and forbidding, a place of catastrophe, “whether it was death or love. And perhaps they could not be divided.”
    • Eros is a concept in ancient Greek philosophy referring to sensual or passionate love. It has been used in philosophy and psychology as an equivalent to life energy. “Love looks not with the eyes, but with the mind; / And therefore is wing’d Cupid painted blind” (A Midsummer Night’s Dream 1.1). See Greek Mythology and Shakespeare.
  • “Never should he see his brother’s face”: Mr. Spitzer can see his brother only “through all the mazed images of the dead, through all the differences.” He lives through death no more miraculously than “some crazy old gambler who had thrown his last dice,” and he tries “to go legal,” as biased as “a left-handed portrait painter in a world of right-handed portrait painters.” “His effort to go straight had made him crooked,” and his way is “finally circular.” He practices only “in the courts which were paved with the moonstones of lost cases under a firmament of lost stars,” and he prefers “the seething silence to the sound, preferring mystery to exposure, preferring a cloak to nakedness.” See Peron Spitzer and Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “trying to practice law where there was no law and no lawyer”: The rule of law holds that everyone, the government included, is accountable to laws that are publicly known, equally enforced, and independently judged: “no one is above the law.” Mr. Spitzer practices where there is no law at all. See Guilt, Innocence, and Justice.
  • “old lawsuits for heart’s balm”: Heart balm suits, for breach of promise to marry or alienation of affections, let a jilted or betrayed lover sue for damages; Indiana abolished them in 1935, and many states followed.
  • “perhaps had walked off the roof like his poor brother”: Here Peron walked off the roof of the building and not through the window as earlier, which brings to mind the 1929 stock market crash and the stories of investors and brokers jumping from buildings. The wave of such suicides is largely an urban myth, amplified by sensational newspapers and the dark humor of the time; John Kenneth Galbraith called it a legend (The Great Crash, 1929, 1955). There were suicides: a brokerage firm clerk and a member of the New York Mercantile Exchange jumped to their deaths in the financial district in the weeks after the crash, others died by gas, gun, or poison, and the suicide rate rose as the Depression deepened, from 17.0 per 100,000 people in 1929 to 21.3 in 1932. Winston Churchill, in New York at the time, wrote of a man who fell from a building near his hotel (Wikipedia). Although Miss MacIntosh pays a visit to Wall Street and invests “imaginary money,” it is not mentioned if Peron or Joachim invested in Wall Street. See Suicide in History and Literature and Peron Spitzer.
  • “that darkened glass through which he might view his own eclipse”: “For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face” (1 Corinthians 13:12). Eclipses were watched through smoked or darkened glass. See The Bible.
  • “perhaps a great President’s seal to indicate a buried nation”: The President’s seal is used to mark correspondence from the President to Congress and is a symbol of the presidency itself.
    • President Monroe was the fifth president of the United States.
    • President Garfield was the twentieth president of the United States. He was shot in July 1881 and died that September.
    • The Grand Union Army may refer to the Grand Army of the Republic which is a fraternal organization composed of veterans of the Union Army, Navy and Marines who served in the American Civil War. See The Civil War.
    • A seal of the New England Lighthouse Underwriters, there is no reference for this specific company. An underwriter is a person or company that underwrites an insurance risk.
    • Boston Life and Trust, there is no reference to this specific company. It is most likely a life insurance and underwriter type of company.
    • A scratch sheet is a racing sheet listing the day’s entries and the horses scratched from them.
    • Society for the Protection of Mariners’ Widows, there is no reference to this specific organization, however, there are several organizations still in existence today that financially support seafarers, fishermen and their dependents.
    • A hook and ladder company is a fire company whose truck carries ladders and hooks; a Hook and Ladder Society would be a firemen’s benevolent society.
    • Old Lamp-lighters’ Annuities was most likely an organization that supported lamplighters when it was a profession.
  • “drank the waters of nepenthe”: Nepenthe is a drug described in Homer’s Odyssey as banishing grief: Helen drugs the wine “with an herb that banishes all care, sorrow, and ill humour” (Odyssey 4, trans. Samuel Butler, 1900). A stirrup cup is a last drink taken in the saddle before riding off. See Homer’s Epics.
  • “the seal of the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad”: Baltimore and Ohio Railroad was the first common carrier railroad and the oldest railroad in the United States. Chartered in 1827, it merged into the Chessie System in 1972 and into CSX in 1987, so it has since become extinct, as Mr. Spitzer half expected.
  • “passenger pigeons who were already of another world”: See Martha, the last passenger pigeon, in the Chapter 49 resources. See Martha the Passenger Pigeon.
  • “some old, dreaming tortoise with the stars upon his back”: In Hindu myth Vishnu as the tortoise Kurma bears the world on his back, and for the Lenape and other peoples North America is Turtle Island, the earth on a great turtle’s back. See Hinduism.
  • “when he was crossing Beacon Hill”: Beacon Hill is a historic neighborhood in Boston, Massachusetts, and the location of the Massachusetts State House; “Beacon Hill” stands for the state government much as Capitol Hill does in Washington. Federal-style rowhouses, narrow streets, and brick sidewalks run through it, and it is one of the more desirable and expensive neighborhoods in Boston. (Wikipedia)
  • “the crab and the bull and the ram and the goat”: There are 12 Zodiac signs but Young lists only 8: Cancer, Taurus, Aries, Capricorn, Gemini, Scorpio, Aquarius, and Virgo. The eight zodiac signs on the umbrella symbolize the mysterious, ambiguous, and encompassing nature of cosmic truth and fate. The missing four are the lion, the scales, the archer, and the fishes, and Mr. Spitzer asks, “but where was his firefly, and where was his mouse?” See Astrology, Alchemy, and Esoterica and The Umbrella.
  • “by mental telepathy”: J. B. Rhine’s card-guessing experiments at Duke University made telepathy a subject of laboratory study (Extra-Sensory Perception, 1934).
  • “two pianos in the silence”: John Cage’s 4′33″ (1952), first played by the pianist David Tudor, is four minutes and thirty-three seconds in which the performer plays nothing, so that the music is the sound of the room. See Music and Composers.
  • “like Gabriel blowing his horn”: The Bible says “the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised” (1 Corinthians 15:52) but does not name the trumpeter; Gabriel’s horn is later tradition. See The Bible.
  • “His head roared, and his eyes were blurred by visions”: This paragraph references two philosophies.
    • Idealism - The paragraph suggests that reality is a construct of the mind. Mr. Spitzer’s “increasing oblivion as to the distinctions between one individual and another” indicates a blurring of the lines between separate entities, hinting at a unified consciousness or a singular underlying reality. The mention of stripping away “dreams, errors, masks of reality” to reveal a singular “night” implies that individual perceptions obscure a deeper, more fundamental truth.
    • Existentialism: The paragraph touches upon themes of mortality, the subjective experience of time, and the search for meaning. Mr. Spitzer reflects on the fading boundary between life and death, the retrospective understanding of the heart, and the possibility of moving from death to life. These reflections suggest an awareness of the fleeting nature of existence and the individual’s struggle to find purpose in a world seemingly devoid of inherent meaning. The questions posed, such as “why should the second journey seem longer than the first?” highlight the subjective and often paradoxical nature of human experience. See Existentialism.
  • “the squawking of a fire bird”: Igor Stravinsky’s ballet The Firebird (1910) is the tale of a magical glowing bird of Russian folklore. See Music and Composers.
  • “the sky was filled with mock moons”: Mock moons, paraselenae, are bright spots on either side of the moon made by ice crystals in high clouds. See Astronomy.
  • “had joined a majority”: To join the majority is to die, after the Latin abiit ad plures, “he has gone to the many” (Petronius, Satyricon 42).
  • “there was only Mr. Spitzer coming with his knock”: There is “nothing dependable.” Everything repeats and doubles before Mr. Spitzer’s eyes, and the boundary between life and death dissolves: he is “already dead or was of two worlds,” perhaps not even “this visitor the dying saw,” and there is “no threshold between world and world.” “We were all transmuted while we lived”: change is inherent to existence, regardless of death. Another world might be “this world which was itself these phantoms and these dreams dreaming themselves,” a form of idealism, and “only one great heartbeat sounding through all of silence” suggests a monistic unity, torn by “the silks of the universe being torn in two and many splittings.”
  • “The copestone was missing like the arch of the sky”: “The stone which the builders refused is become the head stone of the corner” (Psalm 118:22). See The Bible.
  • “a mandarin with a long cue”: The queue was forced on Han Chinese men by the Manchu Qing rulers in 1645 on pain of death, and Western caricature later made it a racist emblem of the Chinese. See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “the silks of the universe being torn in two”: “And, behold, the veil of the temple was rent in twain from the top to the bottom” (Matthew 27:51). See The Bible.
  • “We were all transmuted while we lived”: “We shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed, In a moment, in the twinkling of an eye” (1 Corinthians 15:51-52). See The Bible.
  • “Seldom did he meet with anyone who did not suffer from some form of complex illusion beyond necessity”: Most people Mr. Spitzer meets suffer “some form of complex illusion beyond necessity”: the old woman who believes she is a bird cage, the piano-tuner declaring foreheads “hollow.” Blindness and deafness become metaphors for the limits of perception: the blind portrait painter, the blind war veteran in the galleries of Europe, the deaf music critics who hear every error without hearing a note, judgment resting on something other than the senses. “One great dream informing all dreamers and all dreams” recalls a collective unconscious or universal mind, or Plato’s Form of the Good, the source all strive toward behind the shadows of sense. Happiness is fleeting as a snowflake; “three minutes of happiness seemed perhaps all there might be,” and “the greatest happiness might come through the sorrow of waiting, of postponement.” The passage holds the absurdity of existence and, through Mr. Spitzer’s work for the dead, the responsibility of making sense of their lives. See Plato and Platonism, Freud and Jung, and Deafness and Sign Language.
  • “tapping every forehead with his tuning fork”: “We are the hollow men / We are the stuffed men” (T. S. Eliot, “The Hollow Men,” 1925).
  • “He had once known a blind war veteran”: The veteran was real: “actually I met a World War II veteran in the hotel lobby in Rome with his seeing eye dog, and he was having a marvelous time doing the art galleries of Europe. He listened to the lectures. He heard the footsteps upon stone” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See Joachim Spitzer.
  • “the most famous music critics were the deaf”: Beethoven was nearly deaf when his Ninth Symphony was first played in 1824, and he had to be turned around to see the applause. See Music and Composers and Deafness and Sign Language.
  • “had travelled with a black coachman”: See The Black Coachman and the opium lady’s night travels in the Chapter 33 resources. See Catherine Cartwheel and The Black Coachman.
  • “she would never be married until she married the emperor of China”: The last emperor of China, Puyi, abdicated as a child in 1912.
  • “what infinite paradoxes there were in the lidded or the lidless box”: The box, lidded or lidless, holds the contradictions of existence: the lidded box closed, limited, secretive; the lidless box open but vulnerable and uncertain. Things contained and hidden, truths revealed and concealed, presence and absence, life and death are intertwined in ways that defy simple resolution, and Mr. Spitzer is caught in these paradoxes as he tries to reconcile his fragmented identity.
    • The lidded box may recall Schrödinger’s cat, Erwin Schrödinger’s 1935 thought experiment: a cat sealed in a box with a device that has a 50 percent chance of releasing poison is, by the Copenhagen interpretation of quantum mechanics, both alive and dead until the box is opened and observed. Schrödinger meant it to show the absurdity of applying quantum superposition to everyday objects. (Wikipedia) See Time, Physics, and Chaos Theory.
    • Eugene Wigner imagined in 1961 a friend inside a laboratory who sees a measurement while Wigner, outside, must treat friend and laboratory as hovering in a blur of possible states, so that the two seem to occupy distinct realities. In 2019 physicists tested a version with entangled photons and found that two observers could hold irreconcilable records of the same event (Massimiliano Proietti and others, “Experimental test of local observer independence,” Science Advances, 2019). QBism, developed by Christopher Fuchs and others from the early 2000s, holds that each person’s quantum states are personal beliefs built from his own interactions with the world, and the shared reality emerges from those collisions. In perception, a 2022 study found that what is seen at any moment blends with what was seen over the previous fifteen seconds or so (Mauro Manassi and David Whitney, Science Advances, 2022). See Time, Physics, and Chaos Theory.
  • “Most especially she had feared a man in a red coat”: English fox hunters ride in red coats, called hunting pink.
  • “a golden arrow driven through her breast”: Artemis, the huntress, is “of the golden arrows” in Homer, and when Actaeon saw her bathing she turned him into a stag and his own hounds tore him apart (Ovid, Metamorphoses 3). See Greek Mythology.
  • “an old man who had been cuckolded”: The cuckold was said to grow horns, so the antlers on her brow are the cuckold’s horns.
  • “He feared the naked valet who had clothed him”: The “naked valet” and the “mad couturier” who clothes him “with the colors of a dream” are outside forces shaping the perception of self and world, as in Plato’s cave, and Thomas Carlyle’s philosophy of clothes in Sartor Resartus (1833-1834) makes the body and the world garments of the spirit. “For the dream of life, even like that of death, must ever contain within it an element of the unrealized, the unrealizable—else all would be complete”: imperfection and the unattainable are part of existence, as existentialist thought holds. Mr. Spitzer fears eviction from the womb, the “citadel” where he heard “the beating of another heart above his heart,” and birth into a world where he will miss the smell of the rose, and the mother who is “a bearded man armored” unsettles fixed ideas of gender. The old jockey who dies of shock when his dream proves real shows the power of the unconscious, as in Freud. “We lived by an imperfect concatenation” echoes the Buddhist teaching of impermanence and the illusory self. See Freud and Jung, Buddhism, and Clothing, Hats, and Skirts.

Vocabulary

Abyss
A bottomless pit or chasm.
Alderman
A member of a city council.
Amphora
A tall two-handled jar of ancient Greece and Rome.
Arcana
Secrets; mysteries known only to the initiated.
Barnacled
Crusted with barnacles, small shelled sea creatures that cling to rocks, pilings, and the hulls of ships.
Bellrope
A rope pulled to ring a bell, such as one that summons a servant.
Bole
The trunk of a tree.
Calker
Also caulker. A worker who seals the seams of a wooden ship to make it watertight.
Carousel
A merry-go-round. Earlier, a tournament in which horsemen performed exercises and displays.
Casket
A small, often ornate box for jewels or valuables. In American usage, also a coffin, so Young’s “casket containing no jewel” carries both meanings.
Celestial
Heavenly; of the sky.
Centaur
In Greek mythology, a creature with the head and upper body of a man and the body and legs of a horse.
Chameleon
A lizard that can change the color of its skin.
Chandelier
A branched hanging light.
Chattels
Movable possessions.
Cherubim
Winged angelic beings. In art they are often shown as chubby winged children. The singular is cherub.
Chivalry
The code of the medieval knight; courtesy and protection shown by a man toward a woman.
Citadel
A fortress, usually on high ground, that protects a city.
Combers
A nautical term for long, curling waves that roll in and break on a beach or reef.
Concatenation
A linked series.
Conch
A large sea snail with a spiraled shell that can be blown like a horn. Held to the ear, it seems to hold the sound of the sea.
Congealed
Thickened or stiffened, as with cold.
Contrariety
Opposition; contrariness.
Copestone
The top stone of a wall or building; the crowning touch.
Counterpane
A bedspread.
Couturier
A designer and maker of fine clothes for women.
Crusader
A soldier in the Crusades, the Christian military campaigns to take the Holy Land, fought between 1096 and 1291.
Defunct
No longer living or in existence.
Demented
Out of one’s mind.
Diffused
Spread out widely.
Divan
A long, low couch without a back or arms.
Drove
A herd or flock driven or moving together.
Elegy
A poem or song of mourning.
Empearled
Adorned with pearls, or having a pearly sheen.
Ethereal
Light, airy, and delicate, as if not of this world; heavenly.
Evanescent
Quickly fading or vanishing.
Expiration
A last breath; death.
Extricate
To free from a trap or difficulty.
Firmament
The sky or the heavens, imagined as a solid dome overhead, as in Genesis 1.
Fitful
Starting and stopping; irregular.
Fluted
Marked with long rounded grooves, like a column.
Gauze
A thin, transparent fabric.
Hieroglyph
A picture-sign of a writing system, as in ancient Egypt.
Impresario
The manager of an opera or concert company.
Inarticulate
Unable to express oneself clearly.
Incalculable
Too great to be counted.
Incandescence
The giving off of light by something heated until it glows.
Intestate
Having made no will.
Iridescence
A rainbow shimmer of colors that change with the angle of view.
Keel and list
To keel over is to capsize, with the keel, the ship’s backbone along the bottom, turning upward. To list is to lean to one side, usually from water taken on.
Laconic
Using very few words.
Lazar
A diseased beggar, especially one with leprosy.
Legatee
One who receives a legacy.
Lepidopterist
One who studies butterflies and moths.
Liquescent
Melting or becoming liquid.
Lumbering
Moving heavily and clumsily.
Mandarin
A high-ranking official of imperial China.
Mantle
A loose sleeveless cloak; anything that covers or envelops.
Medusa
A jellyfish in its free-swimming, bell-shaped stage, named for the Gorgon of Greek myth whose hair was snakes.
Minaret
A tall, slender tower of a mosque, from which the call to prayer is given.
Mirage
An optical illusion, common in deserts, in which hot air bends light so that distant water or cities seem to appear.
Mock moon
A bright spot beside the moon made by ice crystals; a paraselene.
Mollusk
A soft-bodied creature, such as a snail or clam, usually with a shell.
Molt
To shed hair or feathers.
Moonstone
A milky, pearly-blue gemstone.
Mother-of-pearl
The shining, rainbow-colored lining of some shells, used for inlay.
Mottled
Marked with spots or blotches of different colors.
Myopic
Nearsighted.
Nacreous
Like mother-of-pearl; iridescent.
Nebula
A cloud of gas and dust in space; once any hazy patch of light among the stars, including galaxies.
Nepenthe
In Homer’s Odyssey, a drug from Egypt that Helen puts in the wine to banish grief and sorrow. Anything that brings forgetfulness of pain. See On Nepenthe (Ch 82).
Oblivion
The state of being forgotten, or of being unaware of everything.
Obtuse
Slow to understand.
Opalescence
A milky, shifting iridescence like that of an opal.
Opalescent
Shimmering with changing colors, like an opal.
Paradox
A statement or situation that seems to contradict itself and yet may be true.
Pinion
A bird’s wing, or the outer part of it.
Pinnacle
The highest point; a tall, pointed mountain peak or the slender spire on a building.
Plaintive
Sad and mournful in sound.
Prism
A clear piece of glass with flat sides that splits light into the colors of the rainbow.
Pugilist
A boxer.
Pulverized
Ground to powder.
Refulgent
Shining brightly; radiant.
Regatta
A series of boat races.
Retrospect
A looking back on the past.
Rose window
A round church window of stained glass, with tracery spreading from the center like petals.
Scalloped
Edged with a series of curves like the rim of a scallop shell.
Sceptre
A staff carried by a ruler as a sign of power. Also spelled scepter.
Scintillation
A spark or flash.
Scratch sheet
A racing sheet listing the day’s entries and the horses scratched.
Semaphore
A signaling apparatus with arms or flags.
Seminal
Seedlike; giving rise to later growth.
Sequin
