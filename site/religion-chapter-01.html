Chapter 1

On Chapter 1 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Christianity and Religion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 23, 2026

Characters: Moses Hunnecker · Vera Cartwheel

Allusions: American Religious Movements · Angels and Archangels · Genesis · The Bible

“an emanation of my over-active imagination”: Blaise Pascal thought imagination was powerful but often led people astray. In his Pensées he called it “that deceitful part in man, that mistress of error and falsity, the more deceptive that she is not always so” (Pensées 82, trans. W. F. Trotter). Vera, doubting the bus-driver’s family as her own invention, fears the same trick of the mind. He also argued that believing in God is more beneficial than not believing in God, which is known as Pascal’s wager.

Moses Hunnecker makes "many calls upon God, the angels, the archangels, angel Gabriel." Gabriel is the messenger angel of Scripture, the one who announces the births of John the Baptist and of Jesus.

Noah's Ark appears as an allusion to Genesis 6 through 8, the flood, the vessel, and its landing on Ararat.

"the first creation when only the spirit of God had moved upon the deep" reaches back to Genesis 1:2, the Spirit of God hovering over the formless, dark waters before order and light are made.

"a cloud of pearl-breasted seagulls, all crying with angelic voices" turns ordinary birds into a chorus of angels, one of the novel's habitual liftings of the plain world toward the holy.

"My soul thirsts for God, for the living God" is Psalm 42:2, the psalmist's plain hunger to stand again in God's presence.

"your whole body will be full of light" comes from Matthew 6:22, the eye as the lamp of the body: a single, clear devotion fills a life with light, a divided one with darkness.

“deep calling to deep”: Psalm 42:7 reads, “Deep calleth unto deep at the noise of thy waterspouts: all thy waves and thy billows are gone over me.” The same psalm opens with the soul’s thirst: “My soul thirsteth for God, for the living God: when shall I come and appear before God?” (Psalm 42:2, KJV). Vera repeats the phrase three times in this chapter, and it captures her spiritual hunger and the existential tension between life, death, and the hope for a transcendent encounter that will end her wandering and questioning.

The book invokes the Jehovah's Witnesses, a nontrinitarian, millenarian denomination known for door-to-door preaching, for refusing military service and blood transfusions, and for calling their body of belief "The Truth." They reject the Trinity, the immortal soul, and hellfire as unscriptural, and hold the wider world to be corrupt and under Satan's sway.

"God is the loneliest of all," and "no God but what we dreamed, and there is no train," is one of the book's bleakest lines. The first phrase pictures a God isolated in his own divinity, unreachable, close to Christ alone on the cross. The second doubts God outright, suggesting he may be only something dreamed. The missing train completes the mood: no clear route out, no assured road to salvation.

The preachers who cry of "harvests of dragons' teeth and reaping the whirlwind" draw on Hosea 8:7, "sow the wind, and reap the whirlwind," the prophet's warning that empty, faithless action ends in ruin.

“the salt of common sense”: The phrase recalls Matthew 5:13, “Ye are the salt of the earth,” said of the plain and faithful. Miss MacIntosh’s gospel is common sense: “Common sense is the finest sense.” See Common sense (Ch 23).

Vera names her whole errand in one line, "the search for life, for love, for truth that does not fail," a phrasing that leans on 1 Corinthians 13, where love takes its joy in the truth.

“a dead girl’s love letters scattered on the floor of her empty bedroom”: The chapter does not name the dead girl. One reading takes her to be Miss MacIntosh, the “dead steersman,” Vera’s lost companion and moral guide who had disappeared or died, whose memory drives Vera’s search for life, love, and truth. She may also be one of the many lost souls Vera met in the harbor cities, each of whom sends her on the journey. “the palm leaves crossed above the marble mantel piece” Palm leaves traditionally symbolize victory, peace, or martyrdom, especially in Christian iconography. Their presence “crossed” above a cold, marble mantelpiece evokes a memorial or shrine, reinforcing the idea of death and remembrance, perhaps hinting at a spiritual or solemn aspect of the lost figure. “her rosary hanging on a brass bedpost” The rosary is a symbol of faith, prayer, and the search for spiritual meaning or solace. Its placement on the bedpost of an empty room suggests a clinging to faith or hope in a place now vacant, emphasizing spiritual yearning amid loss. “because of her suicide”: This stark fact grounds the previous symbols in tragedy and finality. The suicide adds emotional weight and explains why these relics are scattered and why the search is urgent and poignant: there is a desperate need for understanding or closure. “because of a deaf musician” This figure introduces the theme of communication and isolation. A deaf musician cannot hear the music he creates or the world around him, symbolizing a disconnect or failure of connection, similar to the unanswered questions Vera faces. “because of a drunkard’s celestial dream of childhood” This phrase suggests a vision or hope, however fragile or impaired, that persists despite hardship or degradation. The drunkard’s “celestial dream” implies a longing for innocence, transcendence, or redemption. “because of the answers not heard” This line expresses frustration and yearning for understanding, the elusive nature of truth, or the silence in response to crucial questions. It conveys the theme of searching for meaning in a world that is often unresponsive or inscrutable. “because of a blind man’s groping for his coffee cup at an all-night quick-lunch stand on the fog-shrouded waterfront of that great harbor city” This vivid image symbolizes the human condition of blindness or ignorance, fumbling in darkness or uncertainty. The fog-shrouded waterfront amplifies the atmosphere of mystery, obscurity, and searching for clarity or sustenance (coffee cup) in a confusing and cold environment. “as he had asked of his companion” This question encapsulates the existential yearning for illumination, understanding, hope, and spiritual awakening. “When shall the light, Peter, enter my soul?”: 1 Peter 2:9 highlights that believers are called out of darkness into God’s light. 2 Peter 1:19 compares the prophetic message to a light in a dark place, offering hope for a new spiritual dawn. 1 Peter 5:10 assures believers that after suffering, God will restore and strengthen them. 2 Corinthians 4:6 speaks of God’s light shining in hearts, providing knowledge of His glory. Psalm 43:3 is a prayer asking God for His guiding light and truth. Micah 7:8 expresses hope that even in darkness, the Lord will be a light. Ephesians 5:14 encourages those who are spiritually asleep to wake up and receive Christ’s light. The blind man’s question is a metaphor for the quest for spiritual illumination and the deep human need to find light within the soul, to overcome both literal and figurative darkness. “The light of the body is the eye: if therefore thine eye be single, thy whole body shall be full of light” (Matthew 6:22, KJV). The phrase suggests that when one has a clear and true vision (healthy eyes), it positively affects the whole self, filling it with light which is understood as awareness, understanding, and vitality.

"When shall the light, Peter, enter my soul again?" takes up the recurring biblical image of God's light returning to the soul after a stretch of darkness.

"the world's greatest juggler, performing for the Lord's sake and glory" echoes 1 Corinthians 10:31, doing whatever one does for the glory of God rather than one's own.
