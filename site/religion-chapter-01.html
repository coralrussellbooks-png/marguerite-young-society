Characters: Moses Hunnecker · Vera Cartwheel

Allusions: American Religious Movements · Angels and Archangels · Genesis · The Bible

Definition from research

The book invokes the Jehovah's Witnesses, a nontrinitarian, millenarian denomination known for door-to-door preaching, for refusing military service and blood transfusions, and for calling their body of belief "The Truth." They reject the Trinity, the immortal soul, and hellfire as unscriptural, and hold the wider world to be corrupt and under Satan's sway.

Moses Hunnecker makes "many calls upon God, the angels, the archangels, angel Gabriel." Gabriel is the messenger angel of Scripture, the one who announces the births of John the Baptist and of Jesus.

"the first creation when only the spirit of God had moved upon the deep" reaches back to Genesis 1:2, the Spirit of God hovering over the formless, dark waters before order and light are made.

"a cloud of pearl-breasted seagulls, all crying with angelic voices" turns ordinary birds into a chorus of angels, one of the novel's habitual liftings of the plain world toward the holy.

The preachers who cry of "harvests of dragons' teeth and reaping the whirlwind" draw on Hosea 8:7, "sow the wind, and reap the whirlwind," the prophet's warning that empty, faithless action ends in ruin.

"God is the loneliest of all," and "no God but what we dreamed, and there is no train," is one of the book's bleakest lines. The first phrase pictures a God isolated in his own divinity, unreachable, close to Christ alone on the cross. The second doubts God outright, suggesting he may be only something dreamed. The missing train completes the mood: no clear route out, no assured road to salvation.

"the world's greatest juggler, performing for the Lord's sake and glory" echoes 1 Corinthians 10:31, doing whatever one does for the glory of God rather than one's own.

"When shall the light, Peter, enter my soul again?" takes up the recurring biblical image of God's light returning to the soul after a stretch of darkness.

Vera names her whole errand in one line, "the search for life, for love, for truth that does not fail," a phrasing that leans on 1 Corinthians 13, where love takes its joy in the truth.

Noah's Ark appears as an allusion to Genesis 6 through 8, the flood, the vessel, and its landing on Ararat.

"My soul thirsts for God, for the living God" is Psalm 42:2, the psalmist's plain hunger to stand again in God's presence.

"your whole body will be full of light" comes from Matthew 6:22, the eye as the lamp of the body: a single, clear devotion fills a life with light, a divided one with darkness.
