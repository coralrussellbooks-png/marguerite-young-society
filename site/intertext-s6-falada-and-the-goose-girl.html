Falada and "The Goose Girl"

Definition from research

Category: optional. The pony's name points to the Grimm tale, and knowing the tale deepens the passages, but they read clearly without it.

In the Brothers Grimm's "The Goose Girl," a princess travels to her wedding with her maid and her talking horse, Falada. On the way the maid forces the princess to change places with her and marries the prince as a false bride. She has Falada killed, but the horse's head is nailed beneath a dark gateway, and each morning it speaks the truth to the real princess as she passes. In the end the truth comes out.

Vera's black pony is named Falada. On her fourteenth birthday she rides him through the night, "Falada, his dish eyes shining like two moons" (chapter 16), and "He wore no saddle, and yet the rider did not part from the horse." At the height of the ride she says, "I was the headless rider." In chapter 18, when Miss MacIntosh's disguise is uncovered, Vera imagines their roles reversed, "as if I myself had become Falada, as if she were now the rider."

The novel is full of false brides and displaced identities: the bus-driver's wife who may not exist, Esther Longtree's imagined pregnancies, Miss MacIntosh's wig. Falada's severed head, which goes on telling the truth after death, is the figure of a witness. Naming the pony for him marks Vera as a girl who will spend her life trying to tell the true bride from the false.
