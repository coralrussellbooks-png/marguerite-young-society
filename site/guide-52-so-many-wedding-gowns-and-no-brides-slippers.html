Chapter 52: So Many Wedding Gowns and No Bride's Slippers

← Chapter 51 · Guide Home · Chapter 53 →

Research notes: Chapter 52

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

Themes: Dreams and Imagination · Illusion and Reality · Memory and the Past · Music, Sound, and Silence · Women, Gender, and Marriage

Symbols: Clothing, Hats, and Skirts · Keys, Doors, and Locks · Weddings and Funerals

Philosophy: Augustine and Christian Theology

Allusions: Women's Suffrage

Mr. Spitzer opens the trunks.

Trunk lid after trunk lid. He has been working through Cousin Hannah's estate for chapters now: the papers, the alpenhorn, the keys that fit no locks, the documents sealed by rust and flame. And now the trunks. And what he finds, in trunk after trunk after trunk, is wedding gowns. Sheets of silk. Veils of lace. “Porous silks so thin that the least touch might cause them to fall into nothingness.” “Many bridal gowns and no bride’s slippers.”

To All My Darlings pauses here and asks all the right questions: had Cousin Hannah secretly wanted to marry even though she had publicly opposed the institution? Had there been forty suitors she backed away from at the last moment? Had there been forty weddings where she was the one who did not appear? Or, the guide ventures this quietly, had there been one, only one, the bridegroom left at the church door, the love killed by false modesty, and all these gowns were the accumulated evidence of the life she had prepared for and could not bring herself to live?

Young does not answer. The trunks are the answer, or as close as you will get. The gowns are the secret. The no slippers are the secret. The secret is not a fact but a shape: the shape of a life organized around a door it never walked through.

What actually happens in Chapter 52

Mr. Spitzer goes through Cousin Hannah's estate and finds what is there to find. He finds the wedding gowns. He finds letters in languages he cannot read. He finds the “skirt embroidered with forget-me-nots”: her lost skirt, the one she called for in the delirium, the one she had sent someone to retrieve from a mountain peak and that person had not returned. He finds evidence of a woman who was both bride and bridegroom, who crossed every gender boundary the culture had placed in her path, who lived simultaneously as Hannah in Boston and Hamid in the East.

He finds her book open to page seven: Tristram and Yseult, the chivalric romance of illicit love, the story of the love that was magnificent and impossible and survived its own prohibition only to end in death. Matthew Arnold's poem about it has seven sections. Page seven of any text is where the story has just begun and you do not yet know what will be lost.

He reflects that “all might have been imaginary in this life as in this death.” He wonders: “surely her entire life could not have been a game of hide-and-seek, for although she was hiding—who was seeking for her?”

He almost turns to her, at this moment. Almost reaches for her even though she is dead, as one who might be rescued by the person you thought you were rescuing. He does not turn. He does not reach. He keeps the secret. Young does not tell us exactly what the secret is. She tells us its shape, and its weight, and the fact that Mr. Spitzer has decided it will remain a secret, and that this decision is the one meaningful thing he has been able to do for her in a life organized around failing to reach her.

The wedding gowns

Think carefully about what it means to find this many wedding gowns in a dead woman's trunks.

The obvious reading: she wanted to marry and couldn't, or didn't, and kept the evidence of that wanting in locked trunks because there was nowhere else to put it. The suffrage captain who publicly had no time for love, no time for feminine wiles, no time for the domestic arrangements of conventional womanhood; she had, in private, accumulated the material evidence of exactly the domestic arrangements she publicly refused.

But Young complicates even this. The gowns are numerous. They suggest not one failed marriage but many approaches to the threshold, each one turned away from, each one packed into a trunk and locked away. The silk so thin it would fall into nothingness at a touch: the same fragility that characterizes the love itself: real, present, accumulated, but unable to survive contact with the world.

And no bride's slippers. The gown without the slippers is the ceremony without the completion. The bride dressed but not shod for the walk down the aisle. The preparations were made and then arrested at the last item. The slippers are what take you forward, what move you from the vestibule into the ceremony. Cousin Hannah never put on the slippers. She put on everything else. She could not take the final step.

The skirt and the forget-me-nots

The lost skirt surfaces again here with its full detail: it was white lace, embroidered with forget-me-nots, the “little flowers which were like the memories of little loves.” It had blown away on a mountain peak or been left there, and someone had gone to retrieve it and not come back. The skirt was retrievable. The person sent for it was not.

Forget-me-nots are the flowers of remembrance: their name comes from a German legend of a knight who drowned while picking them for his beloved, crying Vergiss mich nicht as he went under. The flowers Cousin Hannah embroidered on her skirt are the flowers of the love that went under while trying to bring her something she wanted. This is the story of false modesty killing the love: she wanted the skirt, someone went to get it, the getting killed them, and she came back without either the skirt or the lover.

Young gives you this detail without insisting on it. The forget-me-nots on the lost skirt. The name of the flowers as the injunction of the dead. Don't forget. She didn't forget. She put the injunction in the embroidery and wore it until she lost it, and then called for it at the end of her life, and now Mr. Spitzer holds it in his hands, the lace so thin it might fall into nothingness at a touch, the flowers still there, still saying: don't forget.

Tristram and Yseult, page seven

She had left her book open at page seven, which is where Matthew Arnold's poem about Tristram and Iseult begins the section where Tristram is dying, tended by his wife Iseult of Brittany, while the other Iseult (Iseult of Ireland, the one he truly loved, the one he was not allowed to love) has been summoned but has not yet arrived.

The Tristram legend is the great medieval story of a love that violated every social contract and could not be renounced. Tristram and Iseult of Ireland loved each other through marriage and separation and years of prohibition, until they were dying, and even then the love did not release them. Young places Cousin Hannah at this precise page: the moment of the deathbed wait, the summoned lover who has not yet arrived, the wife who is present but who knows she is not the one who was truly loved.

Whose page is this? Is Cousin Hannah Tristram, dying and waiting for the forbidden love to arrive? Is she Iseult of Ireland, the true love who was kept away? Is she Iseult of Brittany, the legitimate wife who was always the second choice? Young leaves the book open on page seven without assigning Cousin Hannah a role in the story. She may be all three. She may have read the same page so many times that the book opened there of its own accord, the spine broken at the place her grief lived.

Perhaps she should be the husband and not the bride

Young gives Mr. Spitzer this thought: “perhaps she should be the husband and not the bride, for should not death multiply life’s uncertainties and propose no firm foundation stone or unchanging lintel”?

This is the novel's most direct engagement with Cousin Hannah's gender, not just the masculine virtues she claimed, not just the Hannah/Hamid/Great Moor multiple identities, but the question of which role she occupied in the love she could not live. Bride or bridegroom. Both roles are present in the trunks: the wedding gowns are for a bride; the alpenhorn, the iron keys, the campaigns, the decisive way she lived are for someone who functions as a husband. She may have been both, or neither, or she may have spent her life moving between them without being able to settle in either.

Death, Young writes, multiplies life's uncertainties. It does not resolve them. The great social fictions (husband, bride, the firm foundation stone and unchanging lintel of the marriage ceremony) are revealed by death to have never been as stable as they pretended. The lintel shifts. The stone erodes. The certainties that organized a life turn out to have been provisional all along.

The locksmith in a street of dreams

Mr. Spitzer describes himself in Chapter 52 as the “locksmith in a street of dreams where there was neither key nor lock.”

He is the executor. He has the keys to Cousin Hannah's estate. He has spent chapters fumbling with keys that fit no locks, opening trunks that answer no questions, certifying documents that reveal no definitive truth. He is the person whose professional function is to unlock, and he is operating in a space where the very categories of key and lock have dissolved into dream.

The locksmith without a lock is the legal professional without the rule of law, the musician without the instrument, the lamplighter without the stars. He has all the equipment of a person with a purpose. The purpose has been revoked by the environment it was meant to operate in. He is perfectly equipped for a world that does not correspond to his equipment.

And yet he continues. He opens trunk after trunk. He finds wedding gowns. He keeps the secret. The locksmith in the street of dreams does not stop being a locksmith simply because there are no locks. He goes on trying the keys, one after another, in the doors of the place where Cousin Hannah's life was kept.

No language contained truth

The chapter closes on this: “no language contained truth, for the truth was that which could not be expressed.”

Young has been working up to this formulation for the entire novel, but it lands with particular weight in Chapter 52 because the chapter has just shown you what the truth looks like when it cannot be expressed: it looks like trunk after trunk of wedding gowns and no bride's slippers. It looks like a book open to page seven. It looks like a “skirt embroidered with forget-me-nots” that fell into nothingness when Mr. Spitzer touched it. The truth is there. It has a shape. It has a weight. It has a texture: the porous silk, the lace that might collapse. It simply cannot be said in words, because words would make it something that can be argued with, qualified, revised, denied. In the trunks, it cannot be denied.

Mr. Spitzer's decision to keep the secret is, among other things, a decision about language: there are no words that would do what these trunks do. He cannot tell Catherine what he found, not because the information is too painful but because the translation would falsify it. Some truths are only true as objects. The wedding gowns are the truth. The words for them are not.

What to hold onto

Chapter 52 is where the novel's great secret settles into its final form, not revealed as a fact but disclosed as a shape. Cousin Hannah kept wedding gowns. She left her book open to the deathbed page. She called for a skirt embroidered with the flowers of remembrance. She was both bride and bridegroom, both the love and its prohibition, both the suffrage captain who had no time for domestic life and the woman who had packed the evidence of domestic longing into trunk after trunk, sealed with rust and flame.

What Mr. Spitzer keeps is not just her secret. He keeps the space of her contradiction: the space where a person's public commitments and private desires never resolved into a single coherent life, and yet the life was genuinely and magnificently lived. The locksmith in the street of dreams keeps the keys that open no locks, because the keeping is the only form of love still available to him, and love (even by indirection, even without a lock to fit) is what he has always been.
