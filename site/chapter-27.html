Summary

Vera grows exasperated with the adults who circle their problems endlessly. Mr. Spitzer carries calling cards and an old passport so that, if he is found dead, he will be identified as himself and not his brother. He wears Peron's accurate wristwatch, and has lately rediscovered his own identical watch, broken and stopped near the time of Peron's death, and wears it too. He is always late and always caught between streams of traffic. A stranger once asked him on a crowded street whether he wanted to die. He said no while nodding yes, and was led safely across.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with a reflection on Catherine's persistent engagement with "ancient people" and her habit of living in an imaginative, surreal world rather than facing reality. She imagines herself as historical or fantastical figures (e.g., Catherine the Great) and engages in imagined dialogues, addressing absent entities. This highlights her detachment from actual life and the dreamlike, opium-induced nature of her thoughts. It sets the tone for understanding her as a figure caught between illusion and reality, embodying a perennial lack of faith that paradoxically makes everything possible for her.
  • In contrast to Catherine, Mr. Spitzer is portrayed as being more aware of his difficulties and realities. Yet, he is burdened by his isolation, preferring neutrality and distance from the competitive world. His existence is described as "intermittent," marked by memory lapses and confusion, symbolized by the possibility of walking "through a wall of shimmering glass" or forgetting destinations. This motif emphasizes his fragile grasp on reality and self-identity, as well as his struggle with time and memory.
  • The chapter deeply explores the symbolism of time through the motif of watches. Mr. Spitzer wears his dead brother’s fine, accurate watch, contrasting it with his own broken and rusted timepiece. The brother's watch marks "the course of the inaccurate planets" and embodies an elegiac music in memory of the brother. This duality of watches reflects the tension between order and chaos, memory and loss, and the elusive nature of time itself. The mention of the brother's death and Mr. Spitzer's confusion about timing around this event underscores the theme of temporal dislocation and the impossibility of fully grasping or controlling time.
  • Mr. Spitzer reflects on the artifice of time, comparing it to Catherine's opium dreams and the "great coffin of stars" that might contain his love. This passage philosophically questions the reality of time, suggesting it is an artificial construct. The imagery of "jumping headlong into time with screaming stars" conveys a sense of fatalism and the overwhelming force of existence that defies human control.
  • Despite his solemn reflections, Mr. Spitzer is conscious of his external dignity, fearing ridicule and the loss of respect. The passage describing the humor in a "fat man slipping on a banana peel" humanizes him, showing his vulnerability and self-awareness. His concern with maintaining dignity despite physical and mental frailty illustrates a profound human struggle against decline and absurdity.
  • The motif of musical discord parallels the confusion and disorder in Mr. Spitzer’s life. This metaphor extends to his experience of reality, where signals, timing, and order are unreliable. The image of being caught between two traffic streams or crossing streets at wrong lights symbolizes his dislocation in time and place. The stranger who asks if he "wanted to die" and then guides him through chaotic traffic encapsulates his precarious existence, teetering between life and death, clarity and confusion.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • A man burdened by his awareness of his difficulties and haunted by memories and illusions.
  • He is isolated, secretive, and struggles to reconcile his inner world with external reality.
  • His interactions with the Catherine’s and his own sense of time and self are central.
  • Mr. Spitzer rarely leaves his home except for business and is burdened by his fragmented memory and difficulties.
  • He wears his deceased brother’s watch as a memento and a symbol of time and loss.
  • Mr. Spitzer experiences dislocation in time and space: arriving early or late, encountering sudden weather changes, and experiencing shifts in scenery.
  • Mr. Spitzer struggles with the notion that life, time, and events are illusions, yet he continues to strive for dignity and order in his existence.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s clothes are always described and contain items from other people (Peron and Jock).

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • A woman lost in opium dreams and fantasies, disconnected from reality.
  • She imagines herself in surreal, historical, or symbolic roles, often interrupting conversations with remarks addressed to absent figures.
  • Her lack of faith paradoxically makes "all things possible" for her.
  • Catherine insists on assigning significant interpretations to minor details and ignores major events, suggesting her detachment from reality.

Research: Themes

Isolation and Alienation

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Mr. Spitzer prefers solitude and neutrality, finding the competitive world burdensome.
  • Catherine is isolated in her opium-induced fantasies and mental state.
  • Both characters exhibit a disconnection from ordinary social interactions and time.

Perception of Reality and Imagination

See: Illusion and Reality · Dreams and Imagination

  • Catherine confuses or replaces reality with elaborate fantasies (e.g., imagining herself as Catherine the Great).
  • Mr. Spitzer’s reality is complicated by his own fragmented memory and sense of identity.

Time and Memory

See: Memory and the Past · Time

  • Time is portrayed as fluid, unreliable, and artificial.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s broken and inherited watches symbolize the fractured nature of time and memory.
  • The struggle with remembering and forgetting is a persistent motif, especially in relation to Mr. Spitzer’s brother's death.

Death and Existence

See: Death and Mortality

  • The idea of death is ever-present, especially concerning Mr. Spitzer’s brother.
  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates his own mortality and the notion of life as an illusion or transient state.

Dignity and Self-Image

See: Identity and the Self

  • Mr. Spitzer is deeply concerned with preserving his dignity and avoiding ridicule.
  • His physicality (notably his size) and the fear of losing control over his image are recurring concerns.

Music and Harmony

See: Music, Sound, and Silence

  • Mr. Spitzer finds solace in musical metaphors: the "Pythagorean harmonies," "music of the silent spheres," and the discordant experiences of life.
  • Music represents both order and chaos, reflecting the tension in Mr. Spitzer’s internal state.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

  • Watches (Mr. Spitzer’s and his Brother’s, Miss MacIntosh also has a watch given to her by her brother)
  • The brother’s unbroken, elegant watch contrasts with Mr. Spitzer’s broken, dust-clogged timepiece.
  • Watches symbolize the passage and distortion of time, memory, and connection between the brothers.

Opium Dreams and Fantasies

See: Opium

  • Catherine’s opium-induced visions illustrate altered perception and escapism.
  • These dreams blur the line between reality and imagination.

Phantasmagoric Facts

  • Catherine’s interpretation of Mr. Spitzer’s life is described as “phantasmagoric,” highlighting the surreal and distorted nature of her view.

Invisible Glass Wall

See: Mirrors and Reflections

  • Metaphor for Mr. Spitzer’s barriers between himself and the external world, also referencing an incident where he “walked through a wall of invisible glass,” symbolic of his fragile state.

Traffic and Crossings

See: The Bus and the Journey

  • Recurring images of Mr. Spitzer being caught in dangerous or confusing traffic symbolizes his existential confusion and vulnerability.

Research: Resources

  • Avoirdupois is a system of weights based on a pound of 16 ounces.
