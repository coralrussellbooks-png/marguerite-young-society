To All My Darlings

Chapter 27

On Chapter 27 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 26 · Home · Chapter 28 →

Summary

Vera grows exasperated with the adults who circle their problems endlessly. Mr. Spitzer carries calling cards and an old passport so that, if he is found dead, he will be identified as himself and not his brother. He wears Peron’s accurate wristwatch, and has lately rediscovered his own identical watch, broken and stopped near the time of Peron’s death, and wears it too. He fears nothing so much as the loss of his dignity, yet he is proud of walking through a glass wall and of being declared dead. He is always too early or too late, and always caught between streams of traffic. A stranger once asked him on a crowded street whether he wanted to die. He said no while nodding yes, and was led safely across.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with Vera’s exasperation at “these ancient people,” forever turning upon the same spiral problems, and at her mother’s habit of living in an imaginative, surreal world rather than facing reality. Catherine imagines herself as historical or fantastical figures (e.g., Catherine the Great) and engages in imagined dialogues, addressing absent entities. This highlights her detachment from actual life and the dreamlike, opium-induced nature of her thoughts. It sets the tone for understanding her as a figure caught between illusion and reality, embodying a perennial lack of faith that paradoxically makes everything possible for her.
  • In contrast to Catherine, Mr. Spitzer is portrayed as being more aware of his difficulties and realities. Yet, he is burdened by his isolation, preferring neutrality and distance from the competitive world. His existence is described as “intermittent,” marked by memory lapses and confusion, symbolized by the possibility of walking “through a wall of shimmering glass” or forgetting destinations. This motif emphasizes his fragile grasp on reality and self-identity, as well as his struggle with time and memory.
  • The chapter deeply explores the symbolism of time through the motif of watches. Mr. Spitzer wears his dead brother’s fine, accurate watch, contrasting it with his own broken and rusted timepiece. The brother’s watch marks “the course of the inaccurate planets.” Timing the breakers with it becomes “his elegiac music in memory of his brother.” This duality of watches reflects the tension between order and chaos, memory and loss, and the elusive nature of time itself. The mention of the brother’s death and Mr. Spitzer’s confusion about timing around this event underscores the theme of temporal dislocation and the impossibility of fully grasping or controlling time.
  • Mr. Spitzer reflects on the artifice of time, comparing it to Catherine’s opium dreams and the “great coffin of stars” that might contain “the body of her love.” This passage philosophically questions the reality of time, suggesting it is an artificial construct. The image of Peron “jumping headlong into time with screaming stars” conveys a sense of fatalism and the overwhelming force of existence that defies human control.
  • Despite his solemn reflections, Mr. Spitzer is conscious of his external dignity, fearing ridicule and the loss of respect. The passage describing the humor in a “fat man slipping on a banana peel” humanizes him, showing his vulnerability and self-awareness. His concern with maintaining dignity despite physical and mental frailty illustrates a profound human struggle against decline and absurdity.
  • The motif of musical discord parallels the confusion and disorder in Mr. Spitzer’s life. This metaphor extends to his experience of reality, where signals, timing, and order are unreliable. The image of being caught between two traffic streams or crossing streets at wrong lights symbolizes his dislocation in time and place. The stranger who asks if he “wanted to die” and then guides him through chaotic traffic encapsulates his precarious existence, teetering between life and death, clarity and confusion.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “It was this lack of faith which made all things possible to her.” What does Catherine’s unbelief give her?
  • Why does Mr. Spitzer carry calling cards, an old passport, and his silent music?
  • “Almost any fool, including himself, could tell him that there was no time.” Then why does he wear two watches?

Characters

  • Peron’s watch is accurate, and Joachim’s own is broken. Which brother keeps time?
  • Mr. Spitzer is “proud” of walking through glass and of being told he was dead. Why?
  • Asked if he wanted to die, he said no “at the same time shaking his head in the affirmative.” Which answer is true?

Connections

  • Miss MacIntosh’s “silver-dollar watch” is the kind Mr. Spitzer says “should have done for” Peron. What does the plain watch say about each of them?
  • The false signal lights, “the red where there should be the green,” return from Chapter 24. Who sees them first?
  • His brother “had not been Mr. Spitzer,” and the desk clerk said Mr. Spitzer was dead (Chapter 25). How does a man prove who he is?

Reflection

  • Does time heal all wounds, “even the wounds of time”?
  • Why is a fat man slipping on a banana peel funny, and to whom?
  • What would you carry so that the world would know who you were?

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • A man burdened by his awareness of his difficulties and haunted by memories and illusions.
  • He is isolated, secretive, and struggles to reconcile his inner world with external reality.
  • His interactions with Catherine and his own sense of time and self are central.
  • Mr. Spitzer rarely leaves his home except for business and is burdened by his fragmented memory and difficulties.
  • He wears his deceased brother’s watch, and under his cuff on his left wrist his own broken one, as a memento and a symbol of time and loss.
  • Mr. Spitzer experiences dislocation in time and space: arriving early or late, encountering sudden weather changes, and experiencing shifts in scenery.
  • Mr. Spitzer struggles with the notion that life, time, and events are illusions, yet he continues to strive for dignity and order in his existence.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s clothes are always described and contain items from other people (Peron and Jock).
  • He carries calling cards, an out-dated passport, and his silent music, so that if he is found in a city morgue, “all men should know that he had not been his brother.”
  • He fears “the loss of his dignity” above all, yet is proud of walking through glass and of the clerk who “insisted that he was dead,” “thinking of the joke he had played.”

Peron Spitzer (Joachim’s twin brother)

  • A man of “blatant tastes” who “had worn loud checks” and preferred “low company, lapsed souls, void characters, dice-rattlers.”
  • He “laughed at Mr. Spitzer’s silence,” yet left a fine, accurate watch, which he seems to have “most thoughtfully removed” before he jumped.
  • The twins “had always suffered, except for this final accident, the same accidents, one not falling unless the other fell.”

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • A woman lost in opium dreams and fantasies, disconnected from reality.
  • She imagines herself in surreal, historical, or symbolic roles, often interrupting conversations with remarks addressed to absent figures.
  • Her lack of faith paradoxically makes “all things possible” for her.
  • Catherine insists on assigning significant interpretations to minor details and ignores major events, suggesting her detachment from reality.
  • She enjoys “her present sense of omniscience” about Mr. Spitzer’s goings and comings, yet ignores him “in favor of God knew whom.”

Research: Themes

Isolation and Alienation

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Mr. Spitzer prefers solitude and neutrality, finding the competitive world burdensome.
  • Catherine is isolated in her opium-induced fantasies and mental state.
  • Both characters exhibit a disconnection from ordinary social interactions and time.

Perception of Reality and Imagination

See: Illusion and Reality · Dreams and Imagination

  • Catherine confuses or replaces reality with elaborate fantasies (e.g., imagining herself as Catherine the Great).
  • Mr. Spitzer’s reality is complicated by his own fragmented memory and sense of identity.

Time and Memory

See: Memory and the Past · Time

  • Time is portrayed as fluid, unreliable, and artificial.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s broken and inherited watches symbolize the fractured nature of time and memory.
  • The struggle with remembering and forgetting is a persistent motif, especially in relation to Mr. Spitzer’s brother’s death.

Death and Existence

See: Death and Mortality

  • The idea of death is ever-present, especially concerning Mr. Spitzer’s brother.
  • Mr. Spitzer contemplates his own mortality and the notion of life as an illusion or transient state.

Dignity and Self-Image

See: Identity and the Self

  • Mr. Spitzer is deeply concerned with preserving his dignity and avoiding ridicule.
  • His physicality (notably his size) and the fear of losing control over his image are recurring concerns.

Music and Harmony

See: Music, Sound, and Silence

  • Mr. Spitzer finds solace in musical metaphors: the “Pythagorean harmonies,” “music of the silent spheres,” and the discordant experiences of life.
  • Music represents both order and chaos, reflecting the tension in Mr. Spitzer’s internal state.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Watches

See: Clocks and Watches · Time

  • Mr. Spitzer wears both his own watch and his brother’s. Miss MacIntosh also has a watch, her “old dollar watch which was given to her by her dead love” (Chapter 22).
  • The brother’s unbroken, elegant watch contrasts with Mr. Spitzer’s broken, dust-clogged timepiece.
  • Watches symbolize the passage and distortion of time, memory, and connection between the brothers.

Opium Dreams and Fantasies

See: Opium

  • Catherine’s opium-induced visions illustrate altered perception and escapism.
  • These dreams blur the line between reality and imagination.

Phantasmagoric Facts

See: Illusion and Reality

  • Catherine’s interpretation of Mr. Spitzer’s life is described as “phantasmagoric,” highlighting the surreal and distorted nature of her view.

Invisible Glass Wall

See: Mirrors and Reflections · Chapter 25 resources

  • Metaphor for Mr. Spitzer’s barriers between himself and the external world, also referencing an incident where he “walked through a wall of invisible glass,” symbolic of his fragile state.

Traffic and Crossings

See: The Bus and the Journey · Chapter 24 resources

  • Recurring images of Mr. Spitzer being caught in dangerous or confusing traffic symbolize his existential confusion and vulnerability.

Calling Cards and Passport

See: Identity and the Self

  • His calling cards, out-dated passport, and scroll of silent music are what he carries so that the world will know him from his brother.

Further notes from the text

  • The chapter opens with Vera’s exasperation at “these ancient people,” forever turning on the same spiral problems, and at her mother imagining she is Catherine the Great in a bed with pillars of twisted amethyst rather than facing the living sunlight.
  • Mr. Spitzer objects less to Catherine’s “phantasmagoric facts” about his life than to her interpretation of them: she insists that minor details matter most and major events not at all.
  • His life, which should be continuous, is “intermittent.” He takes the wrong train, gets off at the wrong city, “the wrong station, the wrong star,” though he is painfully accurate and covered with exact reminders to himself.
  • Almost any fool, he admits, including himself, could tell him there is no time, that it is all artifice like Catherine’s opium dreams, and yet Peron’s watch is a convenient timepiece for a poor lawyer and musician.
  • Peron’s watch was made for clocking horses. Mr. Spitzer now uses it to time the breakers coming in with their foaming manes and tails, thinking of the long horse race of life.
  • His fear of losing his dignity runs alongside a frank admission that a fat man slipping on a banana peel is the funniest thing there is. See Comedy in Chapter 27.

Research: Resources

  • “perhaps Catherine the Great sleeping in a bed with pillars of twisted amethyst”: Catherine II, Empress of Russia (reigned 1762-96). Catherine Cartwheel shares her name. See Catherine Cartwheel.
  • “her mind distilled by the juices of languorous poppy flowers”: Opium is the dried latex of the opium poppy, Papaver somniferum. See The History of Opium and Opium.
  • “it was this lack of faith which made all things possible to her”: It inverts Mark 9:23, “All things are possible to him that believeth.” See The Bible.
  • “the Pythagorean harmonies, the muffled planetary voices, the music of the silent spheres”: The music of the spheres. See Pythagoras and the Music of the Spheres and the Chapter 26 resources.
  • “even the company of Mr. Chandelier”: Catherine’s companion of the candles. See Mr. Chandelier.
  • “likely to walk through a wall of shimmering glass”: The glass wall of Chapter 25. See the Chapter 25 resources.
  • “his high silk hat and black necktie and rosebud with maidenhair”: Maidenhair fern (Adiantum) was the usual green set behind a buttonhole rose. See Clothing, Hats, and Skirts and Roses and Flowers.
  • “unfinished epithalamiums and dirges and vast recessionals”: Epithalamiums are songs that celebrate a marriage, while dirges are a lament for the dead. Spenser wrote his “Epithalamion” (1595) for his own wedding, and Kipling’s “Recessional” (1897) is remembered for “Lest we forget!” See Music, Sound, and Silence.
  • “for the tongue-less nightingales”: Philomela, whose tongue Tereus cut out, became the nightingale (Ovid, Metamorphoses 6). See Greek Mythology and Birds.
  • “in a city morgue among the drowned women and the fallen men”: It reverses the “fallen woman” drowned in Thomas Hood’s “The Bridge of Sighs” (1844): “One more Unfortunate, / Weary of breath.” See the Chapter 26 resources.
  • “time healing, he believed, all wounds”: The proverb is old. Chaucer has “As tyme hem hurt, a tyme doth hem cure” (Troilus and Criseyde, c. 1385).
  • “storks’ nests on the roof-tops”: In northern Europe a stork’s nest on the roof brought luck, and storks brought babies. Hans Christian Andersen’s “The Storks” (1838). See Birds and Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “Any old silver-dollar stop watch should have done for him”: Miss MacIntosh’s “silver-dollar watch,” which she always wears (Chapters 3, 9, and 22). Ingersoll’s Yankee (1896), sold for a dollar, was “the watch that made the dollar famous.” See Clocks and Watches and Miss MacIntosh.
  • “the course of the inaccurate planets”: “Planet” is from the Greek planētēs, “wanderer.” See Astronomy.
  • “searching through time for that which was already lost”: Proust’s In Search of Lost Time (À la recherche du temps perdu, 1913-27).
  • “her dream of some great coffin of stars”: “The table might easily be a coffin of stars” (Chapter 25). See the Chapter 25 resources.
  • “the groupings of beats or pulses into equal measures, accelerated or delayed”: Meter and tempo: accelerando and ritardando. See Music and Instruments.
  • “they had always suffered, except for this final accident, the same accidents, one not falling unless the other fell”: The twins, like Castor and Pollux in Chapter 25. See Twins and Doubles and the Chapter 25 resources.
  • “when not one wind would blow through the sails of a ship stuck at the far horizon”: “As idle as a painted ship / Upon a painted ocean” (Coleridge, “The Rime of the Ancient Mariner,” 1798). See Romantic Poets.
  • “the great breakers coming in with their foaming manes and tails”: Poseidon was god of the sea and of horses, and breaking waves are still called “white horses.” See Greek Mythology, Horses and Carriages, and The Sea and Water.
  • “more accurate than the sundials in the windy gardens”: The sundial motto “Horas non numero nisi serenas,” “I count only the sunny hours.” See Clocks and Watches.
  • “had died a thousand deaths”: “Cowards die many times before their deaths; / The valiant never taste of death but once” (Julius Caesar 2.2). See Shakespeare.
  • “the old desk clerk had insisted that he was dead”: The end of Chapter 25. See the Chapter 25 resources.
  • “there is nothing funnier than a fat man slipping on a banana peel”: Henri Bergson’s Laughter (1900): “A man, running along the street, stumbles and falls; the passers-by burst out laughing.” We laugh, he argued, at “something mechanical encrusted upon the living.” The banana-peel fall was a vaudeville and silent-film staple. See Comedy in Chapter 27.
  • “his elephantine avoirdupois”: Avoirdupois is a system of weights based on a pound of 16 ounces. The word comes from Old French aveir de peis, “goods of weight.”
  • “these loud noises ringing in his dead or deafened ear”: His “dead ear” of Chapter 25. See Silence and Deafness.
  • “the holding over of one or more tones of a chord into the following chord”: In music, a suspension is a note held over from one chord into the next. It clashes, then resolves, and here the resolution never comes. See Music and Instruments.
  • “caught ponderously in a revolving door”: Theophilus Van Kannel patented the revolving door in 1888.
  • “just when the red light turned to green”: The first electric traffic signal was installed in Cleveland in 1914. See the Chapter 24 resources.
