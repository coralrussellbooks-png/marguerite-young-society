Chapter 71: The Morning After

On Chapter 71 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of A Guide to Reading Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 70 · Guide Home · Chapter 72 →

Research notes: Chapter 71

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Justice O’Leary · Marie Weed · Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Weed · Tabitha · The Tavern Landlady · Vera Cartwheel

Symbols: Clocks and Watches · Clothing, Hats, and Skirts · Pearls and Jewels · The House

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · Judaism · Lynching and Racial Violence · Music and Composers · Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery · Romantic Poets · The History of Opium

Morning comes to the Tavern. Vera has not slept.

She is stiff with wakefulness, “with ceaseless memory,” wearing her mother’s black velvet (which was meant to be Catherine’s shroud, never used) and her mother’s lace and her mother’s pearls. She is “some time past the ambiguity of dawn.” She gets up and goes out into the hotel, and what she finds there is the morning-after version of everything the night already told her: the same people, the same wreckage, now visible in grey light instead of heard through thin walls.

This chapter moves Vera through the Tavern’s spaces (Dr. O’Leary’s room, the corridor, the hotel lobby) while she thinks about Mr. Weed, remembers Catherine’s stories, encounters a woman screaming prejudice on the staircase, and finally finds the Tavern Landlady, large and goitered and speaking in a dialect thick as Indiana soil, who tells her everything she needs to know about the people in this place. Chapter 71 is a chapter of inventory: of what remains, who persists, and what the daylight reveals that the dark only suggested.

What Actually Happens

Vera wakes and thinks, first, of Mr. Weed: wondering if he has hanged himself in the night. She concludes he has not: he waits for dawn because his children play at hanging themselves, and he cannot leave them unguarded. This is his particular punishment, his particular vigil.

She thinks of her mother’s pearls, which she is wearing. Catherine believed the pearls “could not be lost or stolen”: that they were her “lost estate,” something real beyond market value. She also believed, with her characteristic defiance of arithmetic, that losing pearls made them multiply: the more that were lost, the more there were. The chauffeur James once returned thirteen pearls when twelve had been lost. Vera wears them now as an inheritance of this logic. She goes to Dr. O’Leary’s room. It is open, or she goes in: the chapter is not fastidious about how Vera arrives in places she observes. The room is a museum of disorder. Mismatched women’s shoes are scattered everywhere (“white canvas tennis shoes, green opera pumps, old” laced shoes, “buckskin shoes with buttons, gold Grecian sandals”) all without their mates, from different eras, belonging to no one currently present. Medical instruments are scattered among the shoes. His Yale diploma hangs above the bed. A calendar on the wall is stuck twenty years ago. And the books: shelves of them, a library that has lost the thread connecting one volume to the next: medical texts beside children’s primers beside political pamphlets beside obscure adventure novels beside Keats. The doctor is there, senile and leaning on his cane, his old eyes frozen, delivering imaginary babies in a barren countryside where nothing grows. There is a rumor, which the novel floats and does not confirm, of a pregnant man: a patient who perhaps is real, perhaps is the doctor’s hallucination, perhaps is both.

Sarah, the doctor’s dead sister, or the presence that haunts him by that name, is crawling on the floor. Tabitha the chambermaid moves silently through all of this, as she does every morning, hoping, the Landlady will later tell Vera, to get a baby by the doctor. She is “too crazy to be on relief.” She is here instead.

On the staircase, Vera is confronted by a woman in metallic curlers, half-dressed, her face contorted with cold rage. She accuses Vera of being a “tramp girl” who kept “a decent woman awake all night.” Then she goes further: the Jewish doctor Simeon is a dirty Jew who won’t touch her; “Hitler ran him out of Germany” and Hitler was right; Roosevelt is ruining the country; “there are no Negroes in this county” and no one wants mulattoes; the communists and the Catholics are running everything. She is a widow whose “husband died before he ever came to bed,” and her grief and her politics have hardened into each other until they are indistinguishable.

Vera descends to the lobby. The Tavern Landlady is there: large, fat, goitered, a former circus fat lady, speaking in dialect, “clombed the stairs,” “bedses,” “husbane”, with a folksy resignation that contains, nonetheless, everything Vera needs to know. The landlord is fine. Tabitha is mute and here every morning. The old watchdog who used to attend Mass is dead. The priest died in the landlord’s arms. The hotel sinks every night and comes back every morning. Outside, Tabitha kneels on the porch washing stones in the pale spring air.

Mr. Weed’s Vigil

The chapter opens with Vera still thinking about the Christian hangman, and the question she poses is quiet and precise: does he hang himself? She concludes he does not, and the reason is the children. Marie’s children (his children, the children of the woman who hanged herself from the peach tree) play at hanging themselves in the orchard. He cannot leave them. He waits for dawn to keep watch.

This is Young’s understanding of how grief actually works: not as a thing that passes but as a vigil. Mr. Weed cannot die because the people who carry his death inside them are still alive and still performing it, still rehearsing what their mother did, and someone has to be awake in case they go too far. He has made himself necessary by surviving. He is kept alive by the children’s need for a witness.

The image also completes the echo the novel has been building: Marie hanged from the peach tree, the children playing at hanging beneath it, Mr. Weed walking past on his way home with his Jew’s harp, taking the shortcut through the orchard. Everything that happened is still happening in that orchard. He still takes the shortcut.

Catherine’s Pearls

Vera wears her mother’s pearls through the chapter, and the pearls carry a philosophy. Catherine believed that real pearls, hers, “could not be lost or stolen.” They were her lost estate: what remained of a life that had otherwise dissolved into opium and illusion. She also believed, with the arithmetic of a woman who has stopped obeying mathematics, that losing pearls made them multiply. The chauffeur James returned thirteen when twelve were lost. There are now infinities of pearls, she said. Simple addition.

This is Catherine’s most compressed statement of her worldview: that loss generates abundance, that the real thing cannot be destroyed, that the shadow is more permanent than the substance. It is delusional and it is also a kind of wisdom that the novel treats seriously. Vera inherits it. She wears the pearls as a lost estate and a logical proposition simultaneously.

The pearls also carry mourning. They drip like tears. They are linked throughout the chapter to what remains of Catherine (her shroud-dress, her lace, her logic) now worn by Vera as she walks through a hotel full of people who are also, in their ways, wearing the remnants of people who are gone.

Dr. O’Leary’s Room

The shoes are the chapter’s central image. One shoe from every era, one shoe without its pair, scattered across the floor of a doctor’s office: “white canvas tennis shoes, green opera pumps, old women’s” laced shoes, “buckskin shoes with buttons, gold Grecian sandals.” They come from different times. They all lack their mates. Their wearers are nowhere.

Dr. O’Leary is an obstetrician, or was, before the “hardening of the artery of the brain” retired him from practice. The shoes are the shoes of women who came to him at their most vulnerable: pregnant, waiting, hoping. Some of those pregnancies ended well. Some did not. The shoes remain; the women are gone. The doctor, now delivering imaginary babies in a barren countryside where nothing grows, is the afterimage of a practice that has dissolved into hallucination.

His Yale diploma hangs above the bed. His calendar is twenty years out of date. His library, and Young gives us the titles with her usual care, mixing the medical and the absurd: obstetrics texts alongside children’s primers, a pamphlet on the diseases of coal miners beside Keats’ “Ode on a Grecian Urn” beside “The Causes of Ejaculatory Praecox Among the Great American Elks”, is the library of a mind that has stopped sorting by category and now holds everything together in one undifferentiated mass. It is both comic and genuinely sad. He was educated at Yale. He is now delivering imaginary babies, naked as death, in a barren field.

The rumor of the pregnant man floats through the chapter without resolution. Couvade syndrome (sympathetic pregnancy, in which an expectant father experiences some of the same symptoms as his pregnant partner) is real. Whether the doctor’s pregnant man patient is real, imaginary, or something in between is, characteristically, left open.

The Woman in Curlers

She is one of the most uncomfortable passages in the novel, and Young does not soften it. The woman confronts Vera on the staircase and delivers, in concentrated form, a litany of the prejudices that have organized a certain segment of American life: antisemitism, white supremacy, anti-communism, anti-Catholicism, xenophobia. She invokes Hitler as correct. She insists there are no Black people in the county and no one wants any. She refuses to see the Jewish doctor Simeon, who she says Hitler ran out of Germany rightly. Roosevelt is the enemy. The communists and the Catholics are running everything. This is not fringe material for its time and place; it is a reasonably accurate inventory of the views held by a significant portion of the American Midwest before the Civil Rights era. She is also a widow whose “husband died before he ever came to bed,” and she is terrified and furious and completely alone. Young gives her the prejudices and the grief simultaneously, not as explanation or excuse but as coexistence: this is what a person can contain. The hatred is real. The loneliness is real. They have grown together until she cannot separate them. Vera does not argue with her. There is nothing to argue with. She waits until the woman is done and then continues downstairs.

The Tavern Landlady

She is a new figure: the landlord’s wife, or the person who runs the hotel while the landlord manages its philosophy. She is large, fat, goitered: the Goiter Belt, the region of the upper Midwest where iodine deficiency was endemic before iodized salt, is her natural territory. She is a former circus fat lady, which means she was once spectacle, once exhibited for pay, once an American entertainment. Now she is here, behind the desk, in dialect.

Her speech is rendered phonetically: “clombed the stairs,” “bedses,” “husbane,” “weller and weller,” “axd,” “agone.” Young is giving her a voice that is entirely particular, entirely local, entirely hers: rural Indiana, working class, a woman who has watched this hotel since before it started sinking and expects to watch it after it sinks some more. She carries no illusions about the place. She simply states what is: the old watchdog attended Mass in all weathers and is now dead. The priest died in her husband’s arms. The hotel sinks every night. Tabitha washes stones every morning. Life persists.

She is, in her way, the clearest-eyed person in the Tavern. Not because she has Miss MacIntosh’s commitment to the cold surface of things, but because she has the practical intelligence of someone who has been too many kinds of things (circus exhibit, hotel keeper, widow of a community’s faith) to mistake any one version of herself for the whole truth.

Tabitha

The chambermaid. She is “too crazy to be on relief” (welfare). Relief is the Depression-era term for government assistance, later called welfare. To be too crazy for relief in the 1930s or 1940s meant to be too mentally ill to qualify for the minimal social support the state offered to the destitute. She is here instead, washing stones on the porch, caring for a mad doctor at night in the hope of getting a baby by him. She speaks one word in the chapter: “Good morning.” She says it to Vera as she kneels in the pale spring air. It is the most human sentence in the chapter.

What to Hold Onto

The mateless shoes. One from every era, every woman gone, the doctor delivering imaginary babies in a barren field while his Yale diploma watches from the wall. Catherine’s pearls and their impossible arithmetic: lose twelve, receive thirteen, multiply into infinities. Wear the loss as inheritance.

Mr. Weed’s vigil in the orchard. He does not hang himself because the children are still playing at it, and someone has to stay awake.

The woman in curlers and the full inventory of her fears. Young does not editorialize. She lets it stand: this is what some people contain.

The Landlady, ex-circus fat lady, goitered, in dialect, describing the hotel’s nightly sinking as a matter of fact. The hotel sinks. It comes back. The stones get washed. Good morning.
