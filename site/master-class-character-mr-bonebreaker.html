Mr. Bonebreaker: The Life Made From a Flight

← Lorena · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Solomon →

Characters: Lorena · Miss MacIntosh · Mr. Titus Bonebreaker · Vera Cartwheel

Lorena was the woman Miss MacIntosh loved. Mr. Bonebreaker was the man she was going to marry, and he is the other thing she meant when she said there had been no man in her life, "or else there was but the sorriest pretense, and that was my tragedy." He is the male half of the buried past that Vera draws out of the nursemaid, a Gospel salesman in Chicago who preached that the true self must stand revealed, and who ran the instant she revealed it.

He found her at the lowest point of her life. She had just come through her first operation, "the loss of her left breast," and was back at work setting pins in a bowling alley while the wound was barely healed, weak, reeling in the street with a murky water settled on her sharp mind. Mr. Bonebreaker "saw her reeling" and judged that "she must be a drunken woman or lost soul, such as he was always looking for." He mistook the devastation of a body for the stagger of a sinner, and closed on her, a salvation salesman working a mark. He put his arm through hers over her resistance, told her there was "little time," to "join now," and swept her along in the rain under his umbrella, quoting scripture, gathering "the stray lamb to the fold." The engagement began in his misreading, and in his hurry to close it.

He was a taker before he was anything else. It was his specialty, the book says, to "poke and pry with his long black umbrella, looking for lost souls in shadowed doorways," and also for "stubs of pencils, key rings, keys," because he had "the habit of taking whatever he found." Souls and pencil-stubs were the same catch to him. He gathered everything and could not tell one thing from another, a lost woman from a lost key. Born in the Ozarks, an umbrella-mender's son whose mother died giving him birth, sallow and stoop-shouldered and beaked, he ran the streets crowing the end of the world, always at hand, always "little time," a doom he sold as pressure and was punctual for year after year while it never came.

Then she gave him the truth, and she gave it to him on his own terms. He had preached, from the Bible, that the wife should stand before her husband "as the inner man revealed." So she obeyed him exactly. She invited him to her room and stepped out from behind a curtain with the wig off and her clothes off, bald and one-breasted, "denuded, shining, bare, but real," her fists clutched over the missing breast as if to ward off a blow. This was the inner self revealed, the thing his own gospel demanded. If he had meant a word of what he preached, if he had felt for her the compassion he claimed to feel for drunkards and murderers, he would have declared that "he would love her in the tomb," that "she was beautiful as snow." He did not. He became "like a crazy, snared bird," groaned, turned, and ran.

His flight is the truest sermon he ever gave. He ran down three flights of stairs and out, bareheaded into the falling hailstones "exactly as if it were, after all, creation's end," so that the apocalypse he had been selling arrived at last, and it was only the end of him. The Bible tracts fell out of his coat as he fled, and when she gathered them off the floor she found them mixed with Chamber of Commerce pamphlets, "See Chicago and live," the mayor sweeping the city clean, the new skyscrapers dwarfing the old, so that his gospel and his boosterism were the same paper, salvation and real estate sold out of one coat. The man who preached the revealed soul could not stay in the room for one revealed body.

He left his umbrella on her bed, and that is what remained of him. It was not even his. He had "plucked this umbrella from a dead man," a scavenger's find, and now he abandoned it a second time, fleeing so hard he forgot it. She kept it. Not to remember him, she said, but "to remind her of herself," and she carried that dead man's black umbrella for the rest of her life, the one she is never without. Her whole surface is built of what her lost loves left behind, the wig she wears in Lorena's black and then in memorial red, and the umbrella of the man who ran, a relic twice abandoned that shelters her still. Grief in this book is a thing kept in the hand.

He is Lorena's exact opposite at the one place that matters. Both of them saw the bald head. Lorena "had known, laughing behind her fan," and the love was not what she left over, and Bonebreaker knew and fled at once. The dancer under the spotlight could hold the bare truth, and the salesman of God could not. The bald, one-breasted body is the real thing under every made surface in the book, and Miss MacIntosh stood forth and offered it, and the measure of the two people who loved her is simply which one stayed in the room.

The connection for a writer is written into the scene, because Bonebreaker preaches the very thing the book asks of everyone and then fails it in front of the reader. His gospel, "the inner man revealed," is witnessing the whole ethic of staying with what is actually there and not looking away. The people who fail in this book are never the ones who suffer. They are the ones who stop paying honest attention, who cannot bear the real when it stands in front of them under a bare electric bulb. Bonebreaker is that failure given a name and a Bible. He collected souls the way he collected pencil-stubs, never seeing a single one, and when a real person finally stood revealed exactly as he had demanded, he became a snared bird and ran into the hail. A writer is asked to be the other kind, the one who does not run when the wig comes off, who stays for the bald head and the missing breast and calls them beautiful as snow because they are real. Lorena stayed and laughed. Bonebreaker fled and left a dead man's umbrella. The book knows the difference, and it never once forgives him for being the salesman who sold the revealed soul and could not look at one.
