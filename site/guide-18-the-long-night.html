Chapter 18: The Long Night

← Chapter 17 · Guide Home · Chapter 19 →

Content note: This chapter strongly suggests that the young Vera has been sexually assaulted, and it shows an adult frightening a child into silence.

Research notes: Chapter 18

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Duality and Doubles · Memory and the Past

Symbols: Twins and Doubles

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Chapter 18 is one of the most important chapters in the entire novel, and also one of the most difficult to hold in your hands. It is the night after the revelation, stretched out to its full impossible length, Miss MacIntosh talking and talking in the dark, holding Vera in her arms, talking until dawn.

You may find it overwhelming. That is the point. Vera is overwhelmed. Young is giving you the experience of being inside that room, inside that night, without the mercy of distance or summary.

Read it slowly. Let the proverbs accumulate. Let the voice shift. There are things said in this chapter that the rest of the novel will spend hundreds of pages unfolding.

What actually happens in Chapter 18

The night continues. Miss MacIntosh holds Vera in the crook of her arm, her bald head brushing against Vera’s cheek, and she talks. She talks in two registers, sometimes simultaneously: the familiar Miss MacIntosh of proverbs and schedules and common sense, and another voice underneath, raw and grieving and ancient, crying out about ephemeral life, the missing breast, the hair that was never hers, the kiss that was never given.

She tries to persuade Vera that nothing happened. She promises that by morning, the wig will be back, the red hair gleaming, everything restored to its usual place. She scolds Vera for entering without knocking. She threatens her: “if ever I told what I had seen, she would kill me, for it was nothing another should see.”

By dawn, Miss MacIntosh is asking Vera geography questions again. What is “the chief industry of northern Michigan?” Where is Egg Harbor? Where is the Battle of Shiloh? The night is being packed away.

The chapter ends with Miss MacIntosh rocking Vera toward sleep, whispering that she lost her hair in Vera’s service, that when she came she had her own hair, a thick pale gold mane. “Tempus fugit. Will you believe me when I say that I am not so old as you think?”

The two voices

Young built Miss MacIntosh’s voice in Chapter 18 on two frequencies running at once, and you need to hear both of them to understand what the chapter is doing.

The first voice is the one we know: proverbs, schedules, common sense, almanac facts, moral instruction. It surfaces throughout the night, trying to reassert normalcy. “One dupe would be as impossible” as one twin. “The old ways are best because they have been tried.” We will run a mile and knock down tenpins and say our lessons. This voice is performing recovery, insisting with increasing desperation that what happened can be folded back into the ordinary life of the household.

The second voice breaks through in the long crying passages. “Oh, how ephemeral are we! Oh, how transient were we, my child! The earth has known us not. Our mothers and our fathers never knew us.” God pity the left breast already gone. God “pity us all, for we are” “sinned against more than we are sinning. I can go no farther.” Where is my hair? This is not the Miss MacIntosh of the schoolroom. This is someone much older and more sorrowful, someone who has been carrying something alone for a very long time, and who is, in this one night, in this dark room, with this one child, briefly allowed to put it down.

The two voices are not in conflict. They are the same person. Young has spent seventeen chapters showing you the first voice. Chapter 18 shows you the second. Both are true. Neither is the whole truth.

The secret man and the secret woman

Early in the chapter, Vera arrives at a thought that is one of the most careful things in the novel. She decides that what loomed above her in the darkness was not a “man but only such a man as is hidden in the woman, just as there must also be, in every man, a woman secret and recessive, the forever fading image of the other self.”

Young is reaching here toward something the language of 1965 could barely name, and she does it not by naming it but by making it universal. Every person contains their opposite. The secret self that society will not accommodate doesn’t belong only to Miss MacIntosh; it belongs to everyone, hidden at different depths, pressing differently against the surface. Miss MacIntosh’s secret is simply more visible now, because her wig is on the chair post.

This is why Young cannot be reduced to a simple reading of this chapter. She is not saying Miss MacIntosh is a man. She is not saying Miss MacIntosh is a woman disguised. She is saying that Miss MacIntosh is a person whose inner life exceeded the categories available to contain it, and who spent decades building a persona of radical ordinariness as the only shelter available. The plainness was armor. The proverbs were a wall. And underneath, all her life, that second voice.

The threat and the tenderness

Two things happen near the end of the chapter that seem to contradict each other, and Young puts them almost side by side.

First, the threat: she would kill me if I told. “It was nothing another should see. She had never seen it herself except in a mirror.” This is Miss MacIntosh afraid: afraid with the specific fear of someone whose survival has depended on concealment for so long that the concealment has become the self. The threat is real. It is also the most naked moment of vulnerability in the chapter, because it reveals exactly how much is at stake for her in Vera’s silence.

Then, almost immediately: “she rocked me back and forth in her arms.” She kissed Vera’s eyelids and eyes, her hair, her palms, the soles of her feet. She said she had lost her hair in Vera’s service. That she was no older “than the day she was born.” “Age is the most difficult thing to achieve.”

Threat and tenderness. Concealment and confession. The attempt to erase the night and the refusal, at the same time, to fully let go of it. Young holds these things together without resolving them, because they cannot be resolved. This is what it is to be a person whose secret has been discovered by someone they love.

Memory as the chapter’s frame

The chapter opens and closes with Vera reminding us that she is not sure what Miss MacIntosh actually said. “I am not sure now that Miss MacIntosh did say all I remember her as saying.” “Memory is surrounded by the unknown.” “Memory sometimes provides the one flower more than ever blossomed. Memory sometimes omits the only flower there really was.”

This framing matters. Everything in Chapter 18 is Vera’s memory of a night that happened when she was fourteen, filtered through years of reflection, shaped “by the illumination larger than that of any instant.” The proverbs, the confessions, the geography questions, the rocking and the kissing and the threat: Vera is certain these happened. She is not certain of the exact words.

Young is being scrupulous about the nature of memory while also trusting the emotional truth of it. We may not have the transcript. We have the experience. And the experience left “the heavy, claw-shaped mark of” Miss MacIntosh’s hand on Vera’s forehead, which is the one thing Vera knows was real: “there, that will teach you to remember me.” There, that will teach you charity.

What to watch for as you keep reading

Light spoilers: This section hints at what happens later in the novel. Skip it if you want to meet the story fresh.

Miss MacIntosh will leave. The novel has been telling us this since Chapter 12, when she warned Vera that she might some day retire. Now you understand what departure means for Miss MacIntosh, and what it will mean for Vera. It will not be the retirement of an old nursemaid. It will be the disappearance of the only person who could confirm Vera’s reality.

The wig on the carved chair post. Young places it there and does not take it away. When Miss MacIntosh asks where her red wig is (and then answers that it is on the chair post, in plain sight, while she herself is right here) she is describing the situation exactly. The disguise is visible. The person is visible. They are both present. They are not the same.

“A woman, a wig-maker, and a wild horse are the three things never to be trusted.” This proverb appears in Miss MacIntosh’s mouth in the long night, and it may be the only moment in the novel where she makes a joke about herself. Or it may not be a joke at all. Either way, Young put it there deliberately.
