Chapter 48: A Long Rehearsal for the Silence

← Chapter 47 · Guide Home · Chapter 49 →

Research notes: Chapter 48

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Light and Darkness

Symbols: Keys, Doors, and Locks · Lamps, Candles, and Light · Moon and Stars · The House

Philosophy: Augustine and Christian Theology · Marguerite Young on Her Craft · Phenomenology · William James and Pragmatism · Wittgenstein

Allusions: Astronomy · Exploration and the Poles · Judaism · The Bible · Women's Suffrage

Mr. Spitzer is becoming something in Chapter 48 that he was not quite yet in the chapters before it: a figure of the threshold. Not the man who watches death from the outside (the cautious lawyer, the silk-hatted mourner, the second-remove observer) but a man who has moved so close to the border between living and dead that the border is no longer clearly visible from where he stands.

“This life was a long rehearsal of music for the silence.”

This is the chapter's governing metaphor, and it is one of the novel's most beautiful and most exact formulations. Life is not the performance. Life is the rehearsal: the practice run, the preparation, the running-through of material that will ultimately give way to the silence it was always approaching. The silence is not nothing. It is the thing the music was working toward. It is where everything unplayed finally arrives.

What actually happens in Chapter 48

Mr. Spitzer moves through an old house full of mysterious sounds, barred doors, keys that do not fit any lock, trunks and boxes whose contents he cannot fully read. He is the executor still, but the executor's work has become something stranger: he is navigating a space that feels less like a house than like a territory between states, a place where the entrance is known and the exit cannot be found.

He reflects on his brother, on the contrast between them. Peron burned brightly. Peron lit matches. Peron tempted fate and lost it and perhaps won something larger in losing it, something beyond earthly comprehension. Joachim was afraid of burning, “afraid of sudden incandescence, afraid of” the match that might light something that could not be extinguished. And now he moves through the house of the dead woman, holding the keys that open nothing, while the stars fade in the windows above him.

He imagines himself as a lamplighter: the person who goes through the city each evening lighting the gas lamps, and each morning extinguishing them. But his lamplighting is cosmic: he lights and extinguishes stars. He is angry at the idea that someone else might take his place in this duty. This is one of the chapter's strangest and most revealing moments: the revelation that Mr. Spitzer has claimed a cosmic role for himself, the role of the one who manages the lights of existence, and that the possibility of being replaced in this imagined responsibility disturbs him more than almost anything else.

The lamplighter of stars

Young's image of Mr. Spitzer as a lamplighter of stars draws on a tradition that runs from the literal lamplighters of nineteenth-century cities (the men who went through the streets at dusk with long poles, touching flame to gas mantle, and returned at dawn to extinguish them) to Saint-Exupéry's lamplighter in The Little Prince, the most faithful of all the prince's planetary inhabitants, performing his duty without question even as the planet spins faster and faster and the duty becomes increasingly absurd.

Young's version is more melancholic. Mr. Spitzer is not absurdly faithful. He is possessive. He has assigned himself the management of lights that are not his to manage (the fading stars of other people's lives, including Cousin Hannah's) and he resents the implication that this work might be taken from him or done by someone else. This is the conservative's deepest fear rendered cosmically: the world changing in ways that make his particular skill set, his particular role, his particular form of faithfulness, obsolete.

And yet. The lamplighter is also the person who makes it possible to see in the dark. He carries the light. He knows which lamps are failing and which still burn. In the long night of the novel's mourning, Mr. Spitzer is the person keeping track of what is still lit and what has gone out. That is not nothing. It is, Young suggests, the only form of love available to a man like him.

Consciousness as sporadic

“Consciousness was ever that which was sporadic, fluttering, not constant even in this life, just as sustained attention was impossible.”

This is Young's most direct statement about the nature of awareness, and it applies to Mr. Spitzer's experience with particular precision. He does not exist in a state of continuous consciousness. He is intermittent. He flickers. There are moments of acute perception (the deathbed, the papers, the alpenhorn, the skirt laid over the body) and then long stretches of the second remove, the minute-late reception of experience, the off-key singer that is his entire relationship to the present moment. The philosophical resonance here is with William James's stream of consciousness, except that James's stream is continuous even if turbulent, while Young's consciousness is more like the behavior of fireflies, or like St. Elmo's fire, appearing at the tips of pointed things and then going dark. You cannot sustain it. You cannot force it to stay lit. You can only be present when it flickers on and try to remember what you saw.

This is the experiential condition the novel has been describing in everyone (in Catherine, in Vera, in Cousin Hannah at the end) but in Mr. Spitzer it is most fully articulated. His consciousness is sporadic. His attention is intermittent. This is not a deficiency. It is the condition of being human, rendered visible in a man who has no talent for hiding it.

The stowaway

Mr. Spitzer doubts his own identity and profession in Chapter 48 with a new specificity: he feels like a stowaway. A hidden passenger. Someone who boarded under false pretenses and has been concealed in the hold, living off the ship's provisions, unseen by the proper passengers, sinking without awareness when the ship went down.

The stowaway is the perfect figure for someone who may be living another person's life, who slipped into an identity that was not assigned to him and has been riding it through decades, hoping no one checks. If Mr. Spitzer is Peron and not Joachim, then he is precisely a stowaway in Joachim's existence: the material twin in the immaterial twin's coat, the one who burned brightly borrowing the identity of the one who was afraid of matches.

And the stowaway sinks without awareness. He does not know the ship went down. He is still in the hold, still hidden, still alive in the only sense available to a person who has never fully declared themselves. Young does not deliver a verdict on this. She gives it to you as a possibility and lets it settle.

The evening star and the morning star

“Much as the evening star and the morning star.”

Venus appears twice in the sky: in the evening, low on the western horizon after sunset, and in the morning, low in the east before dawn. For centuries, observers took these to be two different stars (Vesper in the evening, Lucifer in the morning) before understanding they were the same planet seen from two positions in Earth's orbit. “The evening star and the morning star” are one.

Young uses this image for the twins, and for everything in the chapter that appears as two things and may be one: life and death, Peron and Joachim, the self that burned and the self that was afraid to burn. Two appearances, one planet. The dual name carries the full weight of the chapter's meditation on identity: the thing that sets and the thing that rises, the darkness and the dawn, may be the same thing moving through the sky. You called them different names. They were never different.

Lucifer, light-bringer, was in Roman mythology the personification of the morning star, the herald of dawn, before Christian theology absorbed him as the fallen angel. Young knows this etymology and deploys it precisely: the light-bringer is also the one who falls. The morning star rises and is also always falling toward the horizon. Every light in the chapter is provisional.

The house of barred doors

The old house Mr. Spitzer moves through in Chapter 48 (high-shouldered, full of windows and barred doors, every room opening onto another locked room, entrance known and exit impossible to find) is the chapter's governing architectural metaphor for the space between states.

He knows the entrance. He does not know the exit. This is the condition of someone who has, through the long years of his vigil, moved so close to the threshold that he is no longer clearly on either side of it. He can enter the house of the dead (the papers, the alpenhorn, the skirt, the keys) but he cannot exit through the other side to where the dead have gone. And he cannot fully return to the living world either, because the living world now contains Cousin Hannah's death and his brother's death and forty years of the second remove, and there is no version of it he can inhabit without the full weight of those absences.

“Man could not see his own end, for he passed beyond his end.” This is one of the chapter's central philosophical formulations, and it is vertiginous: you cannot witness your own death because by the time it happens, you have already gone past the point from which you could observe it. The self that would observe is the self that has ended. The observation is impossible because the observer has passed beyond. Mr. Spitzer, moving through the barred house, knows the entrance and cannot find the exit, and perhaps this is why: the exit is on the other side of the point where observation becomes impossible.

Boxes within boxes

“He who knew rhombs, circles, semicircles, boxes, boxes within boxes, parallelograms, octagons, octahedrons, ellipses, eclipses of light by darkness, darkness by light, darkness by darkness.”

Young gives Mr. Spitzer this inventory of forms, the full geometry of a trained and meticulous mind, and then immediately demonstrates that all this knowledge of shapes does not help him locate the exit. He knows every form. He cannot find the door. The legal mind, the Blackstone firm, the executor's precision with papers and keys; none of this equipment is adequate to the problem of being in a house that has no locatable exit.

The boxes within boxes are everything: legal documents containing other documents, trunks containing other trunks, lives containing other lives. Catherine's infinite house of many rooms. Cousin Hannah's double life, perhaps triple. The novel itself, which is always opening into another story inside the story, always finding another center inside the center that was supposed to be central. Young's architecture is boxes within boxes all the way down. Mr. Spitzer knows this. He has always known this. But knowing the shape of the thing does not tell you how to leave it.

It seemed better not to know

“It seemed better not to know that which was unknowable.”

This is Mr. Spitzer's accommodation with the mystery, not resolution, not transcendence, but a kind of practical peace with the limits of what can be known. He has sorted the papers. He has laid the skirt over the body. He has taken the alpenhorn into the fog-bound streets. He has asked every stranger whether they have seen her. He has found no master key that unlocks all the trunks. He has not found the exit.

And so: “it seemed better not to know.” This is not defeat. It is the acknowledgment that some rooms in the house are permanently barred, that some questions the novel asks are not questions it intends to answer, that the correct response to the truly unknowable is not more investigation but a particular kind of stillness in the face of it. Wittgenstein's silence. The suffragette's vow. The executor who stops sorting and simply sits with what cannot be sorted.

Young does not present this as wisdom exactly. She presents it as the best Mr. Spitzer can manage. In this novel, that is a form of grace.

What to hold onto

Chapter 48 is Mr. Spitzer at his most liminal: closest to the border, most uncertain which side he stands on, most fully present to the question that has been organizing his existence since his twin died. He is the lamplighter of stars. He is the stowaway who may not know the ship went down. He is “the morning star and the evening star”: the same planet appearing as two things, depending on where you stand and what time it is.

What to carry forward: this life as a long rehearsal for the silence. The house of barred doors that has an entrance but no visible exit. Consciousness as sporadic, fluttering, not constant: the firefly rather than the sustained flame. And the accommodation, provisional and honest, that “it seemed better not to know that which was unknowable.” The chapter ends without resolution because resolution is not what this chapter is for. It is for sitting with Mr. Spitzer in the house, in the dark, while the stars fade through the holes in his cape.
