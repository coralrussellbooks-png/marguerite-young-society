Research notes: Chapter 59 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 59

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

"He did not know whether he was one man in two worlds or two men in one world, two hemispheres, both North and South, two poles."

Mr. Spitzer's selfhood is irreducibly multiple. He exists as:

  • Himself and his dead twin Peron
  • The living man and the man who believes he is already dead
  • The body he inhabits and the skeleton he insists is not his
  • The silent musician and the man deafened by all music

The chapter does not present this multiplicity as a psychological disorder to be diagnosed and resolved. It presents it as ontological fact. When the text asks "For he might be his brother, and Peron might be Joachim", it does not treat this as metaphor or madness. It treats it as a genuine epistemological problem: one that cannot be solved because the self was never singular to begin with.

Most strikingly, the chapter extends this beyond the individual: "every man and every woman might have suffered a substitution like his own". The plural self is not Mr. Spitzer's peculiarity. It is the universal condition, merely made visible in him because he has not closed his eyes to it. The Pillar's insistence that multiplicity "is not a problem to fix — it is what you are" finds its most complete literary expression in Mr. Spitzer's refusal (or inability) to resolve which Mr. Spitzer he is.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

"Illusion of life — his brother having died that he might live"

"Reality is not what we find; it is what we make."

The chapter systematically dismantles the boundary between real and constructed at every level:

  • The trumpeter who seems poor but is rich
  • The magical doors that create "the illusion of a greater space"
  • The fog that makes Mr. Spitzer "faceless" and "semi-invisible"
  • The skeleton that may or may not be his own
  • The definition that is always wrong by the time it is correct

The philosophical move here is precise: "When the definition was correct, the thing defined should be incorrect. When the thing defined was correct, the definition should be incorrect." This is not skepticism in the classical sense; it is the recognition that reality and its representation are always out of phase, that the cave has no exit, only deeper chambers.

The fog is not incidental atmosphere. It is the novel's primary epistemological condition. Mr. Spitzer moves through a world where perception is structurally unreliable, not because he is broken, but because perception itself is construction all the way down.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

"Beautiful memories of memories thronged through his mind, as many as the dead, as many as the autumn leaves falling in an eternal autumn."

This is perhaps the pillar most completely realized in the chapter. Grief here is not a response to a specific loss. It is climate. Consider the accumulation:

  • The dead twin whose memory governs every charitable act
  • The musicians helped and lost and confused
  • The streets, houses, women, men, children "who had already fled before the storm,

before the storm of years"

  • The bells of drowned cathedrals
  • The cries of migratory birds, with "one bird less" always possible

The chapter's prose style itself enacts this pillar. The sentences do not end so much as accumulate: clause upon clause, image upon image, loss upon loss. There is no arrival at resolution because the grammatical structure refuses resolution. The grief is the weather the prose moves through, not a destination the prose is moving toward.

Most explicitly: "His notes were increasingly his own elegy, his memory of himself, the dead twin, his lost perceptions floating in the air." The elegy is not written for the dead. It is written by the surviving self about the surviving self. Grief has become self-referential, permanent, structural.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

"Though he had made many mistakes because of his sympathies, he hoped and believed that if he should be distinguished for nothing else in his sad life or in his death, he would at least be distinguished for his ability to make a great mistake which should be rectified only by a greater mistake."

The Witness does not fix. The Witness stays in the room. Mr. Spitzer's defining characteristic is his refusal to withdraw from the world despite its overwhelming complexity and his continual misreadings of it. He keeps tossing coins. He keeps following musicians. He keeps prodding with his cane among the mollusks and barnacles.

His witnessing extends to the morally uncomfortable:

  • He witnesses his own poverty and the trumpeter's hidden wealth without resentment
  • He witnesses the skeleton in the oyster bed: "the outline was indistinct... and there

were phosphorous sparks, strange gleamings under the wave, sudden movements of

that which had seemed dead", and does not look away

  • He witnesses his own dissolution, "he was not his body", without fleeing into denial

The chapter explicitly contrasts Mr. Spitzer's open eyes with those who close theirs: "darkness was their world, for they had closed their eyes". The ethical act is the open eye. The refusal to simplify, "he would at least be distinguished for his ability to make a great mistake which should be rectified only by a greater mistake", is the refusal to impose false resolution on irreducibly complex experience. This is the Witness as ethical practice.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

"Sometimes he thought he would write his prelude — but prelude to what? His prelude was his elegy, and his elegy was his prelude."

The recursive structure here is not metaphorical; it is the actual architecture of Mr. Spitzer's creative and existential project. He writes musical notes on his shirt cuffs, arranges fragments, returns to them, cannot complete them. He helps musicians find and lose themselves, knowing he is lost. He tossed coins to the trumpeter for years before discovering the deception, and then, after a period of caution, begins again.

The building continues despite knowing it is provisional:

"He had quite recently remarked that it was he who, having feared one grave, be it even lined with pearl, would sleep in two graves."

He builds toward death knowing he has already passed it. He composes music knowing it will not be completed. He makes arrangements and "obsequies" with great punctuality for appointments he cannot keep. The Pillar's assertion that "the building is never finished — that is not failure, that is how it works" is Mr. Spitzer's entire biography compressed into a single principle.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

"he had not yet arranged the ultimate music"

"The unfinished is not failure. It is honesty."

The chapter is saturated with unfinished projects:

  • The musical compositions that exist only as fragments on shirt cuffs and scraps of paper
  • The legal career that generates no income
  • The symphony for musical billiard balls that "could be played only in the exact arrangement of balls from game to game" and therefore can never be exactly repeated
  • The prelude that is also an elegy, the elegy that is also a prelude
  • The equation between life and death that "had failed, for the place had moved"

The billiard ball symphony is particularly sharp as philosophical illustration: "The ivory balls would not roll twice the same. No billiard-player could ever play the music twice." The demand for a repeatable, completable, definitive work is revealed as a demand that reality itself be different from what it is. "Unfinished Is Honest" is not a consolation for failure; it is the recognition that completion would require falsifying experience.

Mr. Spitzer's "ultimate music" is never arranged because the ultimate arrangement would require the world to stop moving. The ongoing sentence (the accumulating, clause-adding, never-concluding prose of the chapter itself) is the ethical form.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

"City of skull-faced men, dwarves with their feet on backwards, how many hallucinatory beings who might be real if Mr. Spitzer prodded with his cane."

"The marginal person is not the exception. They are the evidence."

Mr. Spitzer himself is the primary marginal figure: a fat man in a chimney hat and opera cape, whispering to himself, tossing coins at musicians who may or may not be what they seem, writing music on his shirt cuffs, convinced he inhabits a body that is not his own. He occupies no stable social category, not quite lawyer, not quite musician, not quite living, not quite dead.

But the chapter peoples the margins extensively:

  • The rich trumpeter who plays for coins on street corners
  • The derelict piano player who cannot distinguish keyboard from spinal column
  • The cue-ball musician writing a symphony for billiard balls
  • The skull-faced cavalier in white silks outside the Opera House
  • The man with the change purse strapped around his waist, mistaken for Mr. Spitzer

Each of these figures exists at the edge of categories: between poverty and wealth, between music and noise, between performance and begging, between life and death. Their marginality is not incidental. It is epistemologically productive: it is precisely at these edges that the constructed nature of the categories "poor musician," "fine gentleman," "living person" becomes visible. The cavalier who is simultaneously a professional musician and a figure who receives coins does not fit the category. The category is therefore revealed as a category.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

"The past does not recede. It accumulates beneath us, layer on layer." "streets, houses, women, men, even little children playing hopscotch in a dark street, though they had already fled before the storm, before the storm of years"

The temporal structure of the chapter is explicitly sedimentary rather than sequential. Time does not move forward in this chapter; it presses upward. The dead twin Peron governs every present action. The memory of musicians helped years ago shapes current caution. The bells of drowned cathedrals sound in the present. The skeleton in the oyster bed may be ancient or recent; it cannot be dated.

Most explicitly: "his great retrospective faculty, his memory of all those things which were no more but which persisted increasingly in his mind". The word "increasingly" is crucial. The past does not fade; it intensifies. It accumulates pressure.

The nerve impulse metaphor makes this literal: "these nerve impulses travelling...sometimes by such long roads that he would not react to a stimulation for several years, it taking several years for a message to reach his brain". The past is not behind Mr. Spitzer. It is inside him, still travelling, still arriving, still pressing through.

The image of the lighthouses going dark, "Some of the lighthouses were darkened. Some of the station lamps had gone out. Some of the stars shone no more", is not merely melancholy. It is a map of sedimentary time: the accumulated losses are still present as absences that shape the present, dark stations that were once lit, that once oriented navigation, that still constitute the landscape even in their darkness.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter performs this pillar in its very syntax. The prose's endless qualification, "perhaps," "doubtless," "it seemed," "he had sometimes thought," "or yet it might have been", is not stylistic excess. It is honest language naming its own location. Every assertion is hedged because the speaker knows he is constructing, not reporting.

The definitional problem is made explicit: "When the definition was correct, the thing defined should be incorrect. When the thing defined was correct, the definition should be incorrect." Language chases reality and always arrives a moment late, having shaped a target that has already moved.

The music Mr. Spitzer cannot write is itself a language problem: "he had not yet arranged the ultimate music" because the ultimate arrangement would require a language adequate to experience, and no such language exists. His notes on shirt cuffs, "fragments of notes... half notes, the quarter notes, the eighth notes", are honest language: partial, located, aware of their own incompleteness.

Even the chapter's title, "Chapter 59", participates in this. The chapter is one node in an enormous, famously unfinishable novel. The language of the chapter knows it is embedded in a structure too large to conclude.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

"The darkness was his illumination."

This pillar is perhaps the most theologically precise in its application to the chapter. Mr. Spitzer does not seek transcendence upward or outward. He finds the sacred downward and inward:

  • In the darkness rather than the light: "finding the darkness his only light"
  • In the bells of drowned cathedrals rather than standing ones
  • In the skeleton in the oyster bed rather than the living body
  • In the music he cannot complete rather than the music performed in concert halls
  • In the coin for Charon carried alongside ordinary coins

The sacred in this chapter is consistently located in:

  1. Threshold states: between life and death, between music and silence, between self and other 2. Marginal figures: the derelict musician, the blind sheep dog, the skull-faced cavalier 3. Ordinary materials: shirt cuffs, coins, fog, oyster shells, barnacles

"There were gleamings seen by night which were never seen by day — fish fins, fish eyes, iridescences, stars." The transcendent is not elsewhere. It is what becomes visible when you stop looking for the sunlit world and accept the luminous dark.

The white rose Mr. Spitzer wears, "the body mystical as the white rose he wore", is sacred not as symbol of an elsewhere but as immanent presence: the body itself, mystical, here, now, dying, gleaming.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

"his brother having died that he might live"

This is the pillar that most completely illuminates the twin structure of the chapter. Mr. Spitzer is not a self moving through time. He is the crossing point between Peron and Joachim, between the dead and the living, between the past that deposited him and the future he is constituting.

The chapter makes this structural:

  • He carries Peron's habits (writing on shirt cuffs) without having chosen them
  • He acts in "his faithless brother's persistent memory": the dead brother's will operating through the living body
  • He is constituted by losses he did not choose: "doubtless long ago at a time when he

had not been conscious of his suffering"

The Pillar's language, "constituted by what was deposited before you arrived, and constituting the ground the not-yet-born will stand on", maps precisely onto: "he had tried to make a symbolic equation between life and death... but he had failed, for the place had moved". The crossing point cannot be fixed because it is always moving. Mr. Spitzer is the movement, not the point.

Most profoundly: "Perhaps though he was dead, he had begotten himself." This is the Crossing Point in its most compressed form. The self that exists now was constituted by a death that preceded it. The obligation is not chosen; it is structural. He carries the dead. The carrying continues.
