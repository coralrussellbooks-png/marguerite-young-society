Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 63

← Chapter 62 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 64 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

The old dark is what Miss MacIntosh defends in this chapter, and the crossing point comes with the defense. Her long argument against the electric light arrives where it always arrives, at the old dark in which "a man had married a man, and a woman had married a woman, and no one had been the wiser," and the complaint about modern exposure is her own account of the seam the book writes from, darkness that let the categories blur and hurt no one, the light that sorts them again named as the loss. When she takes off the wig the bald head is under it, and the reveal is the self as mask lifted and the body as the place the self comes apart shown plain, the red hair that was Miss MacIntosh set down like an object beside the woman it belonged to.

She tells Vera what the baldness has cost her, the lovers who turned away, the loneliness of a "poor creature" the world would not have, and she says outright that she wishes she had been a man, that a man's life in a man's world would have been easier to carry. The wish is the crossing point spoken plainly, a woman reaching across the line the book keeps dissolving, and her wry line that "It is woman who makes man's life so short" turns the whole arrangement over with irony rather than complaint, gender relations named as a trap that catches both and blames the wrong one. Vera's answer is unconditional love for the bald, unadorned woman in front of her, the mask refused and the person underneath it loved instead.

Miss MacIntosh dances under the chandelier and calls on Vera to shed the "ballast stones" of appearance and social weight, and the dance is a woman's liberation from the roles that hold her down and it is the book's cosmic vacancy made bodily, the absence where her breast should be carried lightly and defiantly rather than hidden. Her long history of the umbrella, that emblem of a leveller and of equality, each woman carrying her own shelter, is female agency reclaimed from the ornamental, and her references to biblical and historical figures fold women's endurance and invisibility across time into her plain kitchen wisdom.

The chapter ends on the amputated breast and the bald skull she calls a "porcelain crown," and the two together are the body as the place the self comes apart shown without flinching and democratized, the skull under every face, the loss under every ornament. Her warning to Vera not to be deceived by illusion, to face the plain unvarnished person, is the grounded woman's morality and it is the book's whole method spoken in her voice, the mask lifted so the real can stand where it stood.
