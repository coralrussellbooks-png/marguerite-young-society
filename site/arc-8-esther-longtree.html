Arc 8: Esther Longtree (Chapters 72–81)

Part of Story Arcs, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated November 10, 2026

← The Tavern · Story Arcs Home · The Ending →

Content note: The Esther Longtree chapters (72 to 81) deal with stillbirth and pregnancy loss, rape, infanticide, racist violence including lynching, and cruelty to children. Chapter 74, with its account of rape, is especially difficult.

Spoiler note: This page covers Chapters 72 to 81. If you are reading along, read it after you finish Chapter 81.

Overview

At the Greasy Spoon, Vera listens to Esther Longtree, the everlastingly pregnant waitress: her stillborn children, the fathers, the Chicago detective, the mud baby, her twin Rosemary, her strange conception, Walter, and the little toy salesman.

Central figures: Esther Longtree · Vera Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Justice O’Leary · Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh

Chapter by chapter

Chapter 72

On an early spring morning Vera walks past the undertaker’s and the beauty parlor, which share one door, and a mule in a crocheted hood, into the Greasy Spoon, its walls painted with hollyhocks and Niagara. There she meets Esther Longtree, the passive policeman’s only daughter, a big, cross-eyed waitress with a growing bald spot and a deformed foot, who believes she is always pregnant, now with “the little shadow boxer.” Setting out seven cups for her stillborn, Esther tells of the children she has lost and hidden under leaves and snow, as many as the autumn leaves, and of their fathers, the salesmen passing through. She tells of wild mares pregnant by the wind on an island off Virginia; of old Dr. O’Leary, who calls her the immortal medical case; of her parents, who think her an old maid; of the lovebirds she keeps to hide the cries she hears at night; and of the layettes she has buried. At last she asks who killed cock robin and answers that all people killed him, never she.

Chapter 73

Esther Longtree, everlastingly pregnant, has given birth so often to nothing that she remembers her stillborn only as fragments, “an eye, an ear, a long gold hair, a fingernail, a cobweb,” hidden in hollow stumps, streams, and snowdrifts, and the undertaker complains that she brings him no business. Dying fathers, salesmen and insurance agents, write to ask whether their children lived, and on old Doctor’s advice she says nothing. The strongest of them, Joe Goldberg, the Jewish featherweight champion now wasting from tuberculosis in the Colorado mountains, writes of the night he raped her among the tombs of a hillside graveyard and calls her the greatest victory of his life; she remembers the graveyard but not him. An old grey mule, barren, stands by at every love. She fears a trial for murder, for the old Chicago detective, another of the fathers, is hunting proof that she killed his three. She remembers a labor alone under a barren dwarf apple tree, summer turning to winter in a single night, and the birth of nothing, the hardest birth to bear, while a white feather drifted down from some Arctic bird.

Chapter 74

Esther dreads the trial she expects next autumn, when the town’s women will crowd the courthouse to accuse her and she will be unable to produce the three children the Chicago detective says she killed. Her worst mistake, she thinks, was writing to him, a father who was not dying. Three winters, hunting a man who had killed several wives, he found her lying in the frozen fields and took her for a corpse; he felt her pulse, found her heartbeat, and raped her, leaving his eye-glasses, his calling card, a bone button, and a stillborn behind. He came once as a locksmith, once as a soap salesman, once as himself, and told her he was partly wax. When she wrote that she needed an abortion he sent her the name of an abortionist under police protection; now he accuses her of murdering his children. She sets her shame beside a man’s freedom to boast of his bastards, waxes and wanes like the moon, and speaks of the murder of her heart when she was young. A small, warped child’s hand lies across her womb, and her own childhood reproaches her.

Chapter 75

Vera listens to Esther all morning, perhaps through many mornings. Esther wonders whether she carries a child or a benign tumor, and admits that she told the Chicago detective, who raped her and left her to face the disgrace, that he would be a father when the child was already dead. Long ago, young and frightened, she knelt by the river beating the child in her womb; a fisherman who heard her screams helped her wrap the dead baby in a gunny sack and throw it into the river above the dam. Years later it comes back at night, a mud baby bigger in death than in life, a funeral wreath hanging from its navel, and in her dreams it is the policeman’s son: her father was the father. She sets out food on the window sill for a tall child who never comes in, nurses her own hand, and fits two wedding rings on her nipples. The detective now writes that he will prove she murdered his three children. She hopes the coming war, “killing all the men and some of the children,” will make one or two old murders matter less.

Chapter 76

Esther wonders whether her successful conceptions were her worst mistakes. The detective solves every mystery except his own. She says she killed her twin sister in the womb, and she recalls a story of twin brothers, one of whom murdered the other. She was fired from teaching school on false charges. She begins to welcome the trial, though she will answer only her own questions, and she is tired of all the men and all the fathers.

Chapter 77

Esther asks who killed the heavens and the earth and love itself. The detective sends her an old clipping about a mass murderess, an abortionist who killed unmarried mothers and their newborns. She was caught after killing a woman who was pregnant only in her dreams. Esther reveals that her first child was fathered by a Black man, which makes the detective's indifference to Black children personal. Sterile salesmen keep sending gifts for babies who may be dead.

Chapter 78

Esther was called Little Jackass from birth, because she was born kicking and stubborn. Her tiny, cold mother blamed her for the stillbirth of her twin, Rosemary, the perfect child, saying Esther kicked her out of the womb. Esther grew up lonely, often tied to a tree in the yard. Her father was a large, timid policeman who patrolled the graveyard to warn off lovers rather than arrest them. She wonders whether she herself is the shadow, the one who never truly lived.

Chapter 79

Esther's parents never consummated their marriage. For seven years a large, dark woman in men's clothes came to their house at night with a tiny silver spoon, until Esther and Rosemary were conceived. An uncle, a boxer, told her the story, laughing. On her seventh birthday, Esther's mother took her to visit the dying, blind go-between, and Esther saw in her the father she never had. Ever since, every day has been her birthday.

Chapter 80

Esther makes tiny clothes for no one and sets out crumbs for spirits. She tells of her first child, Walter. His father was a Black man who had escaped a lynching and came to her one night, then fled. Alone and frightened, she gave birth and pressed her thumb to still Walter's cry. Believing she had killed him, she hid him and ran. Yet she imagines him alive and growing somewhere, returning in visions to ask why she killed him and where his father is.

Chapter 81

On a rainy day, Esther hopes to marry at last. She remembers the little toy salesman: childlike, nearly toothless, drinking milk from a baby bottle, the son of a famous spy mother and a window-peeping father. He was the sole survivor of a shipwreck, drifting in a rubber lifeboat past the drowned while a shark circled. He told her the bad men would blow up the world one calm day, just for fun. She considers marrying him so the little shadow boxer will have a legal father, though she can no longer say who fathered him.
