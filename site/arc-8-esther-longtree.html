Arc 8: Esther Longtree (Chapters 72–81)

Part of Story Arcs, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated November 11, 2026

← The Tavern · Story Arcs Home · The Ending →

Content note: The Esther Longtree chapters (72 to 81) deal with stillbirth and pregnancy loss, rape, infanticide, racist violence including lynching, and cruelty to children. Chapter 74, with its account of rape, is especially difficult.

Spoiler note: This page covers Chapters 72 to 81. If you are reading along, read it after you finish Chapter 81.

Overview

At the Greasy Spoon, Vera listens to Esther Longtree, the everlastingly pregnant waitress: her stillborn children, the fathers, the Chicago detective, the mud baby, her twin Rosemary, her strange conception, Walter, and the little toy salesman.

Central figures: Esther Longtree · Vera Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Justice O’Leary · Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh

Chapter by chapter

Chapter 72

On an early spring morning Vera walks past the undertaker’s and the beauty parlor, which share one door, and a mule in a crocheted hood, into the Greasy Spoon, its walls painted with hollyhocks and Niagara. There she meets Esther Longtree, the passive policeman’s only daughter, a big, cross-eyed waitress with a growing bald spot and a deformed foot, who believes she is always pregnant, now with “the little shadow boxer.” Setting out seven cups for her stillborn, Esther tells of the children she has lost and hidden under leaves and snow, as many as the autumn leaves, and of their fathers, the salesmen passing through. She tells of wild mares pregnant by the wind on an island off Virginia; of old Dr. O’Leary, who calls her the immortal medical case; of her parents, who think her an old maid; of the lovebirds she keeps to hide the cries she hears at night; and of the layettes she has buried. At last she asks who killed cock robin and answers that all people killed him, never she.

Chapter 73

Esther Longtree, everlastingly pregnant, has given birth so often to nothing that she remembers her stillborn only as fragments, “an eye, an ear, a long gold hair, a fingernail, a cobweb,” hidden in hollow stumps, streams, and snowdrifts, and the undertaker complains that she brings him no business. Dying fathers, salesmen and insurance agents, write to ask whether their children lived, and on old Doctor’s advice she says nothing. The strongest of them, Joe Goldberg, the Jewish featherweight champion now wasting from tuberculosis in the Colorado mountains, writes of the night he raped her among the tombs of a hillside graveyard and calls her the greatest victory of his life; she remembers the graveyard but not him. An old grey mule, barren, stands by at every love. She fears a trial for murder, for the old Chicago detective, another of the fathers, is hunting proof that she killed his three. She remembers a labor alone under a barren dwarf apple tree, summer turning to winter in a single night, and the birth of nothing, the hardest birth to bear, while a white feather drifted down from some Arctic bird.

Chapter 74

Esther dreads the trial she expects next autumn, when the town’s women will crowd the courthouse to accuse her and she will be unable to produce the three children the Chicago detective says she killed. Her worst mistake, she thinks, was writing to him, a father who was not dying. Three winters, hunting a man who had killed several wives, he found her lying in the frozen fields and took her for a corpse; he felt her pulse, found her heartbeat, and raped her, leaving his eye-glasses, his calling card, a bone button, and a stillborn behind. He came once as a locksmith, once as a soap salesman, once as himself, and told her he was partly wax. When she wrote that she needed an abortion he sent her the name of an abortionist under police protection; now he accuses her of murdering his children. She sets her shame beside a man’s freedom to boast of his bastards, waxes and wanes like the moon, and speaks of the murder of her heart when she was young. A small, warped child’s hand lies across her womb, and her own childhood reproaches her.

Chapter 75

Vera listens to Esther all morning, perhaps through many mornings. Esther wonders whether she carries a child or a benign tumor, and admits that she told the Chicago detective, who raped her and left her to face the disgrace, that he would be a father when the child was already dead. Long ago, young and frightened, she knelt by the river beating the child in her womb; a fisherman who heard her screams helped her wrap the dead baby in a gunny sack and throw it into the river above the dam. Years later it comes back at night, a mud baby bigger in death than in life, a funeral wreath hanging from its navel, and in her dreams it is the policeman’s son: her father was the father. She sets out food on the window sill for a tall child who never comes in, nurses her own hand, and fits two wedding rings on her nipples. The detective now writes that he will prove she murdered his three children. She hopes the coming war, “killing all the men and some of the children,” will make one or two old murders matter less.

Chapter 76

Esther wonders whether her successful conceptions were her worst mistakes. She tells of the Chicago detective, who wears disguises and solves every mystery but his own, above all the “quiet, quiet mysteries” where no bodies are missed; he writes that he will track her to her grave if she does not produce his living children. He is still hunting the “bleeding rapist” whose handiwork he found in Esther, and now she says he “had never touched her.” Her stillborn children hover and applaud whenever she lies with a salesman, and a small brown hand closes her womb. She would rather face trial for mass infanticide than for one murder, says she once tried to kill herself, and confesses that in her mother’s womb she murdered her twin by kicking it out too early. Cupid has missed her, though her heart is broken by experience. Fired from teaching school on false charges, she knits and unravels a black hood for a child to be born white as snow, and she is tired of all the men and all the fathers.

Chapter 77

Esther asks who killed the heavens, the earth, and love, and decides that cock robin killed cock robin. She has scrubbed the restaurant clean, though the epileptic flour miller, who learned that no one can pass a thumb print down, walks the streets white with flour; her bedroom, with its empty cradle, could not bear the jury’s search. Once she found a rain-stained alphabet book in the hole where she had hidden a dead child, and every letter became an exhibit against her, from A, the agony, to N, “a Negro, the night, a noose,” and R, a rope. The white detective cares only for the deaths of white children: finding a murdered white girl and, farther on, a murdered Black girl in the same woods, he ran screaming and never told the police. He sends her clippings of old murders, among them an old woman abortionist who killed unmarried mothers and their newborns and was caught only after killing a woman who was pregnant in her dreams. Esther remembers that the detective raped her and left her for dead, sees the shadow of murder everywhere, and wonders why the sterile salesmen send ear muffs for babies who may be dead and growing.

Chapter 78

Esther was called Little Jackass from birth, because she was born kicking and stubborn. She grew up lonely, bullied at school for her deformed foot and crossed eyes, always stung by bees, always falling. Her tiny, cold mother, a pallid descendant of George Washington’s sister, hit her, tied her by a rope to a tree in the yard, and blamed her all her life for the death of her twin, Rosemary, whom Esther had kicked out of the womb months early, stillborn and buried under an unnamed pear tree; Rosemary would have been the perfect daughter. As a child Esther dug for her with a spoon and found only a thimble, beads, a broken doll, the toys of the dead. She loves her father, the passive policeman, who patrols the graveyard whistling to warn off the lovers and whose own mother spilled babies like leaves; she embarrasses her mother by telling that her father has a Negro streak and that her mother’s brother killed himself. She wonders whether she herself is the shadow, the one who never truly lived.

Chapter 79

Esther’s parents, the huge passive policeman and his tiny child-bride, never consummated their marriage. For seven years a large, dark, foreign woman in a man’s hat, coat, and shoes came at night to their kitchen, carrying a tiny silver spoon between husband and wife while the policeman read old baseball scores behind the stove, until Esther and Rosemary were conceived. When the mother went alone to the go-between’s mule farm to show her the pregnancy, the go-between took her into bed and held her down, and in that minute the mother saw that it was a man; afterward she tried and failed to get rid of the child. Esther’s uncle, a pugilist, told her the story, laughing. On her seventh birthday her mother, dressed in her old bridal clothes, led her all day through briers and marshes to the old mule farm, where the go-between lay dying and blind on a bed of chicken feathers, with Esther’s own crooked nose and dimpled chin: her father. Ever since, every day has been her birthday, and she has died with every moth.

Chapter 80

On a rainy afternoon in the dark restaurant, Esther tells Vera about Walter, her first-born. When she was a young girl, a stranger found her asleep in the summer fields, and she woke with him already inside her; at dawn she saw that he was a Black man, his face scarred where a white mob in the South had tried to hang him because he smiled at a white old maid’s rooster. He fled north for fear the white people would kill him. Hardly knowing what was happening to her body, she hid the pregnancy, went south, and gave birth alone in a dirty hotel room, where she pressed her thumb on the newborn’s throat, wrapped him in a sporting page, left him in a dresser drawer, and fled by train and bus. Yet she believes Walter lives, a Boy Scout raised by kind white people, and he follows her everywhere, asking why she killed him and where his father is. She sets out crumbs for the spirits, knits with invisible thread, and waits for the little shadow boxer to make up for the first.

Chapter 81

On a dark, rainy day Esther tells of the man she has chosen to be the legal father of the little shadow boxer: the little toy salesman, bandy-legged and pot-bellied, with three soft teeth, no tear ducts, and no taste buds, who drinks warm milk from a baby bottle and asks her to rock him to sleep. His mother, a world-famous beauty and spy, stole him from his father, a windowpeeper, and put him to sleep in dresser drawers. He tells her he is a murderer of women and little children because he alone survived a great storm at sea, rocking in a rubber life boat while the German captain conducted with his withered arm and everyone else drowned. Adrift in the calm, a dead child’s hand wearing a gold ring like his clung to his sleeve, a white shark that looked like his mother circled him, and he raved that the sea, the sky, and God were a Jewish conspiracy. He has not come lately. He whispered that the bad men would blow up the world some day of calm just for fun; if they do, Esther will go on pregnant with her dreams, and maybe the dead loved the dead.
