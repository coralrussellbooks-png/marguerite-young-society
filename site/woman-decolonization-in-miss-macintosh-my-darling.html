Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Decolonization in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling

← Young's contribution to feminist thought · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · A closing word →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh

Decolonization in literature is the work of dismantling the Eurocentric and imperial assumptions built into the canon, reclaiming the voices and narratives that colonial storytelling erased or subordinated. Young was not writing a decolonial program, and the book is not organized around one. Yet the pushback is real and it runs through the novel, sometimes as a pointed line against the Empire and its histories, and at its deepest as the same refusal that drives everything else in the book, the refusal to let any single account, imperial or historical or individual, stand as the whole.

The direct critique is plain enough. Miss MacIntosh condemns the "British Empire" and sets herself against "the exploitations of the natives and of the workers in the dark Satanic mills," and Mr. Spitzer's grief reaches "what America had done to passenger pigeons" and "to Indians in their lonely tepees." The passenger pigeon is not a decoration here. Martha, the last of her kind, mourned in the novel only by an old black porter, is the book's emblem of the erased, and setting the slaughtered pigeons beside the dispossessed of the tepees names colonial destruction and species extinction with one grief, the vanished kept at the threshold where the book keeps all its dead.

The novel also strips the authority from the West's founding stories. Columbus is dismissed as a "mere pretender," since "many an old sailor had already been washed up here and did not live to talk about it," and Cook and Peary would have done "better as fiction writers." This is the failure of the record turned on the record's proudest pages, the discoverer revealed as a latecomer to a place that was never lost, the explorer's claim exposed as a story told well enough to be believed. Young's suspicion of any account that presents itself as the whole truth, which runs through Catherine's dream and Spitzer's watches and the book's whole blurring of what is real, lands here on imperial history and dissolves its confidence.

Esther Longtree's story carries the racial injustice most directly. Her lover is a "Negro" who fled the South and its "white people," and the "white detective" who haunts her, "hating all things black," ignores the murders of "little black children" because he deems them "unimportant." The line the detective draws through justice, investigating the white child and abandoning the black one, is the intersecting erasure the book performs elsewhere with Martha and the mute and the coachman, the marginal life kept present precisely because the official world will not account for it.

Cousin Hannah folds the colonial reversal and the patriarchal one into a single figure. She rides "disguised as an Arab king," driving "the courtly Spanish cavalcades... before her like chickens," a reversal of the conquest narrative in which the European column is routed by a woman in the dress of the colonized, and her lifelong war for the "overthrow of man" and "paternal domination" is the crossing point meeting the anti-colonial inversion, gender and empire overturned in the same ride. Mr. Spitzer's conviction that the world "was woman's world from the beginning" sits beside her as the quiet form of the same claim, and Hannah's scorn for explorers who name their discoveries after their wives catches the patriarchal habit folded inside the colonial one.

The book's aversion to Western progress runs alongside. Miss MacIntosh's hatred of "electric lights" and modern convenience, and her disdain for the "civilized corruption" that overtakes the head-hunter once he is exposed to Western refinement, invert the civilizing mission outright, the refinement figured as the corruption and the so-called savage as the one it spoils. The reach is worth naming as a reach: this is the book's broad suspicion of a certain idea of progress as much as a developed postcolonial argument, and it works by feel and reversal rather than by program. It is real, and it is a temperament more than a thesis.

The deepest decolonizing move is none of these lines. It is the novel's refusal of the individual. "Every heart is the other heart. Every soul is the other soul," the book insists, and "the individual is the one illusion," and this is the illusion of the individual, the central pillar of the whole reading, stated in a form that happens also to be anti-colonial to its root. The sovereign, bounded, self-owning individual is the unit colonial order runs on, the figure who discovers and claims and names and owns, and when Young dissolves that individual she pulls the ground out from under the arrangement more completely than any single line against the Empire could. Her interconnectedness, the communal and holistic understanding of existence the book keeps returning to, is where her consciousness work and her decolonizing work turn out to be one work. She goes deep enough into the self that the self dissolves, and the self that dissolves is the one colonialism required.
