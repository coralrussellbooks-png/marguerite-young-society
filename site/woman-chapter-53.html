Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 53

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Two brothers hold this chapter, and their doubling is the pillar. Mr. Spitzer's identity fragments against Peron's, his delicate sensitivity set against the roles a man is handed, composer and gambler and mourner, and the fragmentation is the fragile masculine self and the plural selfhood the book carries in its central man. He is alienated, the boundaries between life and death and self and other gone blurry, missed connections and passing trains marking the openings a fixed self keeps missing, and Peron's "brilliant," many-colored life against Joachim's "colorless" one is the difference between two ways of being handed to men, neither of them whole.

The mourning cloak and the black keyboard set the chapter's key. Mr. Spitzer wants "equilibrium between opposing forces," life and death, acceptance and rejection, and the wish for balance is the book's refusal to let the binaries stand, the seam it writes from named in a man's grief. He wanders the city among "twin rooms and double arrangements," "twins" and "mirrors" everywhere, and the doubling is plural selfhood and the mirror in which a self is made against its opposite. His knowledge of gambling and sports and fighting fills male-dominated arenas with cards and dice and blows, and the absence of women from them marks the gendered division the book keeps noting, even as the fighters "broken by life" and the men who become "widows" of themselves undo the toughness those arenas were built to prove.

The chameleon and the "many-colored cloth" carry the crossing point. Identity here is performance, masks and disguises and assumed selves, and the political figure who was biologically female and lived as a man, who died and was buried in social ambiguity, is the crossing point at its most consequential, the policing of gender boundaries and the erasure of the gender-nonconforming staged in a single life. Men become "widows" of themselves, fighters become "madames" or "babysitters," and the porous line between masculinity and femininity runs through the whole chapter.

The arbitrariness of knowledge closes it. Identities shift, music and chess moves stand in for the fixed self, the "black etude" and the "black keyboard" mark mourning and the possibility of new composition beyond the old forms, and the fluidity is the book's rejection of the single, essential self. Peron's chameleon adaptability against Spitzer's "black velvet melancholy" leaves two brothers who are two faces of one unwillingness to be counted as one, and the chapter ends inside their doubling, a man composing his life out of a brother he cannot tell himself apart from.
