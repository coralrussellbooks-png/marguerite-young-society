Research notes: Chapter 40 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 40

Characters: Cousin Hannah

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

"She was many selves, many beautiful, volatile Psyches wandering about—and she was lying in bed, propped up among satin pillows...combing her hair in a psyche knot"

The mother does not have multiple selves as a symptom. She is multiplicity as ontology. The text refuses to resolve this into illness or metaphor. She simultaneously wanders the world and lies in bed. She is "both the fox-hunter and the fox". She cannot tell "whether she was the horse or the rider, the carrier or the carried".

The name Psyche is doing double work here (the soul, and the knot of hair, and the mirror) all at once, all irresolvable. The plural self is not presented as tragedy. It is presented as the condition of being her, full stop.

The chapter never attempts to consolidate her into one coherent subject. The narrator's voice itself fragments, moving between "my mother believed," "my mother remembered," "my mother dreamed", as if the act of narrating a plural self requires a plural narration.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

"Nothing could be explained except in this way — ignotum per ignotius — the unknown by the still more unknown, the mystery turning on the mystery."

There is no bedrock reality in this chapter. Every apparent fact dissolves into another layer of construction. The mother understands all languages she never learned. She communicates with Darius the Magnificent on the telephone. She greets strangers as cousins.

Crucially, the text does not frame these as delusions to be corrected. The question "Would anyone have known if reality was missing?" is asked without irony; it is a genuine philosophical proposition. Reality's absence might be undetectable. The cave has no exit. The mother's opium dreams and her waking perceptions are not distinguished by the prose because the text refuses the hierarchy that would make one more real than the other.

Even the explanation for her condition collapses into itself: "Had not opium caused her somnolence — but what kind of explanation was this, returning upon itself...Had not her somnolence been the cause of her taking opium". Cause and effect, reality and illusion, are mutually constructing. There is no ground floor.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

"Who else should know, when my mother was gone, the dead social season, the dead customs, the dead capital cities, the dead horses, the dead jockeys, the dead soil, the dead trees"

The word dead accumulates here like sediment, not as a single loss but as a climate of loss. The chapter does not describe grief as an event. It describes a world in which everything is always already in the process of dying or having died.

The mother's retirement from the world was not caused by one catastrophe:

"She had had no faith to lose, so change had followed change, and chance had followed chance."

This is grief without a precipitating wound: grief as the baseline condition of living in time. The dead social season, the dead horses, the dead jockeys: these are not elegized individually. They accumulate into a single weather system that is simply what the world is made of now.

And yet: "Almost all returned". The chapter holds both: everything dies, and nothing fully disappears. This is not consolation. It is the permanent weather described accurately.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

"She had beaten against my mother's frail defenses...causing my mother's head nearly to split into two, so great was the impact of all these disoriented impressions whirling and fluttering about her"

The narrator's stance throughout is one of sustained, unflinching witnessing. There is no diagnosis offered, no correction applied, no moment where the narrative voice steps back and says: here is what was really happening. The narrator stays in the room with the mother's experience and reports it on its own terms.

This is most visible in the treatment of Cousin Hannah, who may be a real person, a dream, a psychological projection, or a ghost, and the text refuses to decide:

"Cousin Hannah had passed over into the realm of the dream — or had she always been there"

The ethical act of the chapter is this refusal to simplify. The narrator witnesses without concluding. The prose itself (its length, its recursive qualifications, its refusal to arrive) enacts the practice of attention described in Pillar 4. To read this chapter is to be trained in witnessing.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

"My mother never had given up her belief that all of space could be wound, unwound like a bolt of silk."

The chapter is itself recursively constructed. It begins with Cousin Hannah, spirals outward through languages, history, dream figures, cosmology, and returns again and again to the mother in bed; each return slightly altered, slightly deeper, never arriving at a conclusion.

The mother's mind operates the same way:

"there should be a slight variation, a departure from the past, a change, a chance, a minuscular difference even without the necessity of death"

You return to the beginning with what you learned. The bolt of silk can be wound and unwound. The building (of self, of memory, of meaning) is never finished. The chapter demonstrates this structurally: it does not end so much as it suspends, mid-sentence, mid-thought, mid-construction.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

"an episode or summary which should be forever incomplete, for there should be a slight variation, a departure from the past"

This is perhaps the most explicitly stated pillar in the entire chapter. The mother's memories, her self, her story: all are framed as constitutively incomplete, and this incompletion is presented not as failure but as ontological honesty.

The demand for completion is identified as a kind of violence:

"Nor did she seem to wish to try to correct, in any genuine way, her errors — or imagine that there should be a golden mean between extremes, a sound mind in a sound body, a cure for nature's imperishable ills. Such an idea, if it had ever occurred to her, had been put away."

The "cure" (the completed self, the resolved narrative) is the thing she has correctly rejected. The ongoing sentence, the never-concluded chapter, the self that cannot be pinned down: these are not symptoms of disorder. They are integrity.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

"For a person of her presumed illiteracy and lack of a formal education...her knowledge was magnificent, astounding her by its brilliance"

The mother occupies multiple marginal positions simultaneously: bedridden, opium-dependent, socially retired, female, presumed illiterate, presumed delusional. The chapter systematically inverts every one of these marginalizations.

Her "illiteracy" becomes polyglot mastery of all languages including those never spoken. Her social retirement becomes a richer social life than any globe-trotter's. Her opium dreams become the site of genuine philosophical insight. Her madness becomes the evidence that the constructed nature of reality is most visible at the edges.

Cousin Hannah functions similarly: the eccentric suffragette, the woman with a man's voice, the one whose "furies" were intense, the one who was not a "dark stranger" but was treated as one. The marginal figures in this chapter are not exceptions to reality. They are its most honest representatives.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

She "heard rollings, pealings of thunderous hooves in the distance beyond all hearing—all being then as it was now."

That final clause, all being then as it was now, is the most compressed statement of sedimentary time in the chapter. The past does not recede. It presses up through the present as sound, as memory, as visitation.

The dead figures who populate the chapter (Alcibiades, Alexander, Hugh Capet, Dante, Diderot, the Wandering Jew) are not metaphors for the past. They are present interlocutors. Darius answers the telephone. Daniel Boone strides across the mortal stage. The mother converses with them in real time.

"even after so many years of the great captain's absence, my mother limpidly understood, as if no time had ever passed"

Time in this chapter is not a river moving in one direction. It is geological: layered, pressurized, with the oldest layers still actively shaping the surface. The ancestors are not behind her. They are beneath her, and they are speaking.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

"English being, as it was the one language she really knew, the only foreign language to her"

This extraordinary inversion, the mother's native tongue as her most foreign language, is the chapter's sharpest statement about language as construction. Language does not neutrally convey reality; it constructs the reality available to its speakers. The mother's fluency in languages she never learned and her alienation from the one she did learn suggests that all language is equally constructed, equally foreign, equally a medium that shapes what can be perceived.

The chapter's own language enacts this: the prose is deliberately excessive, self-qualifying, recursive, a language that names its own location as uncertain, that refuses to speak from nowhere. The lists of dead languages, the catalog of historical figures, the accumulation of images; these are not decorative. They are the text showing its own constructedness.

"Perhaps the most important thoughts were those which never found expression."

Language constructs what exists. What it cannot construct, the thought beyond expression, exists in a different register, one the chapter gestures toward but cannot, by definition, contain.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

"There were angels in all the angles — non angli sei angeli — angels on all the irregular roofs."

The sacred in this chapter is entirely immanent. It lives in the irregular roofs, the baroque mirrors, the coachman's whip, the long-bodied coach dogs barking at the wheels. The transcendent does not appear as a beyond; it appears as a depth within the ordinary.

"The figurehead knew more, the waters roaring at its mouth. For one thing, it always went first."

The figurehead (wooden, inanimate, marginal) knows more than the captain because it is fully in contact with the immanent world. It does not stand above the waves and command them. It goes first into them. The sacred here is not elevation but immersion.

The mother's opium dreams, her plural self, her conversations with the dead; these are not escapes from reality into transcendence. They are deeper penetrations into the immanent, into the layers of what is actually here, beneath the surface of the ordinary day.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

"Who else should know, when my mother was gone, the dead social season...and when my mother was gone...who should live in this closed, empty house, move through the still rooms, hear his echoing footsteps?"

The chapter ends (or rather, suspends) with this question hanging. The mother is the crossing point: she holds the dead social season, the dead horses, the dead jockeys, the languages of the dead, the visits of historical figures. She is the site where all of this accumulation becomes present tense.

And when she is gone, the question is not merely personal grief. It is structural: who will carry this? Who will know the dead customs, hear the coachman's whip, meet the mandarin on the stairway? The narrator, the "I" whose mother this is, is already constituted by what the mother carries. The chapter is itself the act of carrying forward.

"the dust returning always to the dust as the stars fluttered downward...though the dust was ever that which should reassemble into new and beautiful forms, so strangely like the old"

The crossing point is not chosen. The carrying is not chosen. The narrator did not choose to be made of this mother's sediment. But the writing of the chapter (the act of witnessing, preserving, transmitting) is the structural obligation being fulfilled in real time.
