Chapter 17: She Was Bald

← Chapter 16 · Guide Home · Chapter 18 →

Research notes: Chapter 17

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Falada · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: The Search for Meaning

Symbols: Hair, Wig, and Baldness

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Genesis · The Bible · The History of Opium

Here it is. The moment the novel has been building toward since Chapter 13.

Take your time with this chapter. It is not long, but it is dense with feeling, and Young is doing several things at once. She is delivering the revelation. She is showing you Vera’s wild, fourteen-year-old response to it. She is asking one of the novel’s deepest questions. And she is being very careful, in a way that first-time readers sometimes miss, not to tell you what the revelation means. That is your work. She is only showing you what Vera saw.

What actually happens in Chapter 17

Miss MacIntosh is standing by the window in the moonlight. The curtain that had concealed her head blows aside. And Vera sees that Miss MacIntosh is bald. Completely, coldly, stonily bald, her skull gleaming like an ivory ball, not one hair on her head.

Vera recognizes her instantly (by the broken nose, by something definite in her manner) and in the same moment refuses to accept what she sees. She cycles through shock, resistance, a wild kind of triumph, and then screams: “Miss MacIntosh, you are a man!”

Miss MacIntosh moves toward her. What follows is a fight, or what feels like a fight, or what Vera experiences as a fight, or what may be partly hallucination and partly real. Blows are exchanged. Vera is knocked down, lifted up, laid on the bed. She passes in and out of consciousness. She wonders if her assailant might be a sandbag dummy come to life, or a Roman gladiator, or the angel with whom Jacob wrestled at Peniel. The chapter ends the morning after. Vera’s braids are neatly plaited down her back. There are no scratches on her cheeks except perhaps those made by leafless branches.

The baldness

Young has seeded this moment across many chapters. The mirrors Miss MacIntosh avoids. The umbrella carried indoors. The windows thrown open in winter. The bundling up in hot weather. The bedroom bare of almost everything except a broken-down brass bed and a dressing table with three mirrors she never looks into. The one face, not two, she told Vera: one for the world, one for heaven. Only one.

All of it resolves here. Miss MacIntosh wore a wig. The practical, plain, red-haired woman who denied the existence of everything unreal was herself, at the most literal level, constructed. Her most visible feature (the red hair that gleams throughout the novel, that Vera describes as tawny as an August sunset) was not hers. The anchor of reality was wearing a disguise.

Young does not editorialize. She gives you the bald skull in the moonlight and lets you sit with it.

What Vera screams, and what it means

“Miss MacIntosh, you are a man!”

This is a fourteen-year-old's conclusion, delivered in shock and triumph and cruelty, and Young is careful to frame it that way. Vera herself immediately notes that had she stopped to think, she might have been silent. The laugh that follows is the laugh of someone who has been proved right about something she didn’t know she suspected.

What has Vera actually seen? A bald woman. A woman who is physically powerful (the amazing biceps, the muscular expansion, the skillful boxing) in ways that Vera associates with men. A woman who never looked in mirrors, never discussed her own body, kept herself covered even in summer, whose bedroom was bare of all the feminine furnishings of the house. A woman who Vera had privately imagined with a red, curled mustache, whom she had assigned the role of the bridegroom in her secret dream.

Young is writing in 1965, and she is writing about something the culture of 1965 had almost no language for. She does not give it language here either. She gives you Vera’s scream, and Vera’s scream is wrong in the way that a fourteen-year-old’s first understanding of something vast is always wrong: partially true, coarsely framed, certain in the way only the newly shocked can be certain.

What Young seems to be showing us is a person who built an entire identity (the practical nursemaid, the anchor of reality, the one who denied the existence of dreams) over something hidden so deep and so carefully that even the wig was part of the architecture. Miss MacIntosh’s insistence on the real, on the ordinary, on the surface of things: perhaps it was the most necessary insistence of her life. Perhaps the denial of illusion was her only way of surviving in a world that had no place for what she was.

The fight

The physical struggle between Vera and Miss MacIntosh is one of the strangest and most extraordinary passages in the novel, and Young is very careful about what she does and doesn’t tell us.

Did it really happen? Young gives you both possibilities simultaneously. Vera’s consciousness divides: “one who fought, one who lay still and watched it all.” The blows are described in the precise vocabulary of boxing (the left jab, the straight right, the rabbit punch, the Sunday punch) which is also Miss MacIntosh’s own vocabulary, the language of the sandbag dummy she had Vera box as a cure for daydreaming. Vera wonders if her assailant might actually be that sandbag dummy come to life. The next morning there are no scratches except possibly from branches.

Young is not being evasive. She is being accurate. This is what it feels like to have the foundation of your reality knocked out from under you: you experience it as a physical assault, as something that hits you, lifts you, lays you out. The body registers what the mind cannot yet process. Whether Vera’s knuckles actually connected with Miss MacIntosh’s face or whether the encounter happened partly in the space between shock and unconsciousness is a question Young leaves open on purpose.

What is not in question: Vera is changed. The sandbag dummy: the one Miss MacIntosh told her to box when she was being foolish, when she was daydreaming, when she needed to restore her circulation and her waning common sense: has fought back.

The question at the chapter’s center

In the middle of the fight, falling, Vera asks the question that is the novel’s central question, delivered here in its most compressed form:

“What shall we do when, fleeing from illusion, we are confronted by illusion? When falling from illusion, we fall into illusion?”

This is the question Young quoted in the novel’s front matter as the book’s guiding proposition, and here it arrives not as a philosophical abstraction but as something Vera is screaming into the dark while a bald woman in a moonlit bedroom deals her the rabbit punch.

Vera fled Catherine’s world of opium and hallucination by holding onto Miss MacIntosh. Miss MacIntosh was the real. And Miss MacIntosh, it turns out, was also a construction. The escape from illusion led into illusion. The anchor was wearing a wig. Where, then, is the real world?

Young does not answer. She gives you Vera’s braids neatly plaited down her back the next morning, and leafless branches that might account for the marks on her cheeks, and she moves on.

What to watch for as you keep reading

Miss MacIntosh does not disappear from the novel after this. She will be present throughout, in memory, in the search that drives Vera’s journey, in every question the novel asks about what is real and who gets to decide. But she will never again be what she was before this night. Vera will spend the rest of the novel understanding what she saw.

The morning after detail matters enormously. Vera’s braids are exactly as they should be. There are no marks. Life continues on its surface exactly as before. This is Young’s version of the morning after the impossible crossing of Lake Constance: you survived, you are here, everything looks the same. And nothing will ever be the same.

The sandbag dummy has been fighting back since Chapter 1. Notice, from here forward, every time Miss MacIntosh’s boxing lessons appear in Vera’s memory. The dummy was always a stand-in for something. Now you know what.
