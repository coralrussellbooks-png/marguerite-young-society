Research notes: Chapter 69 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 69

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

"Only my eyes were reflected in its depths, as if they had swam out of my face like two silver fishes detaching themselves from a porous underwater flower."

The narrator cannot see her own face in the mirror, only her eyes, swimming free of her. This is not a gothic flourish. It is a precise ontological statement: the self does not cohere into a single visible image. The self detaches from itself, observes itself from a distance, and cannot be reassembled.

Dr. O'Leary enacts the plural self even more dramatically. He is simultaneously:

  • A practicing physician making professional appointments
  • A man wrestling with a ghost or hallucination
  • A child mourning a dead sister
  • A drunk raving in a hotel room
  • A sailor, a soldier, a farmer: identities the landlord narrates as if they belong to different men

The landlord himself is explicitly plural: candy merchant, ventriloquist, sailor, soldier, hunter, Intelligence officer, circus husband. His body is a palimpsest of selves: tattoos layering over skin, a battleship behind chest hair, a woman's face in his palm that winks when he closes his fist. He is not one person. He is a walking archive.

The hangman is split from his own shadow, and the shadow is described as more interesting, more loquacious, more real than the hangman himself. The shadow says what the hangman will not. This is the plural self made architectural: one person occupies two ontological positions simultaneously.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

"Tillie," he had said, with an air of finality. Who was Tillie? "A young girl," he had answered, "a very young girl."

The landlord describes the same person as simultaneously an old woman with turkey buzzards and a young girl who will knit baby hoods. When the narrator presses him, he does not correct himself; he holds both descriptions as equally valid. There is no authoritative version. Reality is not discovered; it is asserted, and different assertions coexist without resolution.

The turkey buzzards become canaries, or the canaries are as big as turkey buzzards. The landlord's correction is casual, smiling, as if the distinction is minor. The line between the monstrous and the domestic is itself a construction, casually reversible.

Dr. O'Leary is described as having been killed in a traffic accident earlier in the evening, "he who even now if the bus-driver had been right should be pinned under a steering wheel in a flooded meadow", yet he is loudly, riotously alive in the next room. His death is simultaneously true and false. The text does not resolve this. It presents both realities and moves on. There is no sunlit world outside the cave. There is only the hotel corridor, where the dead practice medicine and the living cannot see their own faces.

Sarah herself is the supreme illustration: she died at age one and was buried in the vegetable garden, yet she is also an old woman with grey hair crawling on the floor, also a young beautiful woman with a wide forehead, also a ghost, also perhaps no one at all,,. She is all of these simultaneously, and the text refuses to adjudicate.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

"God damn it, Sarah, God damn it, Sarah!" That was the old refrain, I had heard even in my sleep.

This is grief as climate, not event. The doctor does not grieve Sarah in a moment of crisis; he has apparently been grieving her for decades, and the grief has not moved. It has accumulated into a permanent atmospheric condition that fills every room he occupies. The narrator herself carries a "small, faded pillow upon which I had always rested my head, pillow which had received my childhood tears". She has brought her grief with her, physically, as luggage. It is not behind her. It is under her head.

The hotel itself is a structure of accumulated grief: old people with hardening of the arteries, a legless newspaper man reporting news from ten, fifteen, twenty years ago, a doctor who cannot stop delivering a dead woman's baby. The building does not move forward. It sediments. Every room holds a grief that never resolved, only deepened.

The doctor's mourning is described as "like that of a wind rising and falling": weather language, precisely. Not a storm that passes. A wind that rises and falls and rises again. The honest life stops pretending otherwise, and O'Leary, drunk and raving, is perhaps the most honest character in the chapter precisely because he has stopped pretending.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator's position throughout this chapter is almost purely that of witness. She lies in her bed, unable to sleep, and she listens. She does not intervene in O'Leary's monologue. She does not go to him. She does not call the management. She does not explain or conclude. She receives.

"I was drifting toward sleep, but I was jerked back to consciousness by a quarrel between two persons in the bedroom next to mine."

She is jerked back: consciousness is not chosen here, it is imposed. The witnessing is not voluntary. It is structural. She cannot not hear. She cannot not receive what the hotel delivers to her.

The one moment she breaks this stance is devastating in its brevity:

"Go, Sarah!" I screamed.

This single intrusion, the only direct speech the narrator makes in the entire chapter, is immediately swallowed back into O'Leary's monologue. The witness screams once and returns to witnessing. The scream changes nothing. Sarah does not go. The doctor does not stop. The walls continue shaking. The ethical act was not the scream but the sustained attention before and after it.

The text itself performs the ethics of the witness: it does not summarize O'Leary, does not diagnose him, does not resolve whether Sarah is real or imagined. It stays in the room, recording the full texture of the experience without flinching from its grotesquerie: the stink of the dead, the cold legs, the toothless mouth, the sores.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The landlord's monologue is the structural embodiment of recursive construction. He begins to tell a story, loops back, corrects himself, adds a new layer, forgets his original point, arrives somewhere unexpected, and begins again. His account of the hangman is never completed; it is perpetually revised as he climbs the stairs,.

"The landlord had corrected himself, casually, smiling..."

The correction is not embarrassment. It is method. He builds, revises, rebuilds. The building is never finished. The landlord's entire biography, candy merchant to ventriloquist to sailor to soldier, is a recursive construction of identity, each version not replacing but layering over the previous one.

O'Leary's argument with Sarah follows the same recursive logic. He dismisses her, then addresses her again. He says she is the wrong woman, then continues speaking to her as Sarah. He says he has forgotten her, then demonstrates that he has forgotten nothing. He returns to the beginning with what he learned, which is that he has learned nothing, which is itself a form of knowledge.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter does not end with resolution. It ends mid-sentence, mid-disease list, mid-question:

"Was she her mother's only daughter?"

This is not a cliffhanger in the commercial sense. It is a philosophical commitment to the ongoing sentence. The demand for completion (for Sarah to be definitively dead or alive, for O'Leary to be definitively sane or mad, for the narrator to arrive somewhere) would be its own violence against the experience the text is rendering.

The legless newspaper man is perhaps the purest emblem of this pillar: he reports "all the news of what happened here ten, fifteen, twenty years ago". He has no legs, he cannot move forward, and so he reports backward, indefinitely. His room is "big enough for a man's head but not big enough for a man's body". He exists in a state of permanent incompletion, and the text treats this not as tragedy but as a form of integrity. He has an excellent head on his shoulders. He does his work.

The hangman's watch "was never wound": time is suspended, unmeasured, incomplete. The clock that should strike the hour does not strike. Completion is refused at the structural level. The chapter exists in a time that does not tick forward.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Every character in this chapter occupies the edge of a category:

  • The hangman is a prosperous farmer and model citizen who ties knots for pay; he is both executioner and agrarian, the violence of the state hidden inside the pastoral
  • The landlord is simultaneously a man of the world and a man who has never seen America: cosmopolitan and provincial at once
  • The legless man is a journalist without mobility, a reporter who can only report the past
  • Tillie is simultaneously old and young
  • Sarah is simultaneously dead and present, infant and crone, sister and lover,
  • The narrator occupies the Fire Escape: literally the marginal space, the emergency exit, the room that exists only in case of catastrophe

"I thought that if there should be a fire, all people would stream past me in their long nightgowns, their long hair burning like meteors as I lay still, unable to move."

The narrator imagines herself as the still center while the marginal becomes the main event: fire turning everyone into meteors, the emergency becoming the norm. The marginal person is not the exception. They are the evidence. The Fire Escape room is where the truth of the building lives: in the exit, in the emergency, in the space that exists only because the main structure might fail.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

"He reports all the news of what happened here ten, fifteen, twenty years ago."

The legless man is sedimentary time made flesh. He cannot move forward, so he reports backward. The past does not recede; it is actively reported, continuously present, delivered on a sled made of a child's toy and roller skates, announced by rusted sleigh bells.

The doctor's relationship with Sarah is the deepest expression of sedimentary time in the chapter. Sarah died, possibly, at age one. She was buried in the vegetable garden. Yet she presses up through the ground, through decades of the doctor's professional life, through the hotel floor, into his bed. The past is not behind him. It is beneath him, and it has broken through.

"I am aware that, if I started digging in the vegetable garden, I would come upon, not the skeleton of a dust-small infant but of an old, old woman."

This is sedimentary time made explicit: dig down through the layers and you find not what you expected but what time has made of what was buried, the infant has become the old woman, the past has aged in the ground. The sediment transforms what it holds.

The hotel itself is sedimentary: "this old Tavern which was going to wrack and ruin", filled with people whose stories are decades old, whose bodies carry the marks of previous lives, tattoos, bullet wounds, old hair in wire baskets, lipstick covered with tobacco flakes. Everything in the building is a deposit from a previous time.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The landlord's speech is the most explicit demonstration of language as construction. He does not describe reality; he builds it as he speaks, correcting himself, revising, asserting, withdrawing. When he says "They are canaries" after having implied turkey buzzards, he is not correcting a factual error. He is choosing which reality to construct in this moment, for this listener.

O'Leary's language is similarly self-aware of its constructive function. He addresses Sarah in a medical register ("When was the last time she had enjoyed sexual communion with her husband?"), then in a familial register ("my little sister"), then in a legal register, then in a theological register ("Jerusalem, Jerusalem"). Each register constructs a different Sarah and a different O'Leary. The language does not reveal the relationship; it constitutes it, differently each time.

The picture over the bed, "Forget me not", is language performing its most naked constructive function: an imperative directed at time itself, an attempt to use words to hold what time dissolves. It fails, of course. The room is full of forgotten things: blonde hair in a waste basket, lipstick under tobacco, sodden toilet paper. Language that pretends to speak from nowhere, the universal imperative of "Forget me not", speaks from the very particular somewhere of a narrow room in a failing hotel where everything is already forgotten.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The hangman's feet "were beating a light tattoo on the ceiling above my head."

The sacred does not descend from above in this chapter. It beats on the ceiling from the floor above. The transcendent is always just one story up, making noise, scraping like matches on wet tinder. It is immanent (present, nearby, embodied) but never fully accessible.

The picture of the silver church steeple "piercing a pink cloud and garlanded with blue flowers" hangs over the narrator's bed, but it hangs crooked when O'Leary's raving shakes the walls. The sacred image cannot maintain its vertical orientation in this building. It tilts. It participates in the disorder of the immanent world rather than presiding over it from above.

The doctor's invocations ("Oh, God, God damn it", "Jerusalem, Jerusalem", "Jesus Christ, my God") are not prayers directed outward to a transcendent God. They are eruptions of the sacred within the immanent, blasphemous and reverent simultaneously, the sacred living inside the profanity, inside the drunk old man's grief, inside the shaking hotel room. The sacred is here. It is the grey bus, the permanent grief, the plural self. It is O'Leary on his knees washing out dirty slop jars, which is simultaneously degradation and a kind of terrible devotion.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

"The bed having lived to the ripe old age of sixty in the city of St. Louis, you did, indeed, die of pneumonia at the tender age of one."

This extraordinary sentence (addressed to Sarah, or to the bed, or to both) collapses the distinction between object and person, between the narrator's present and Sarah's past, between the age of one and the age of sixty. The narrator is where time crosses itself. She lies in a bed that has accumulated sixty years of other bodies, in a room that holds other people's hair and lipstick and toilet paper, listening to a doctor mourn a woman dead for decades.

She did not choose this position. She was put in the Fire Escape room because there was a couple in the bridal suite. The obligation is structural, not chosen. She is constituted by what was deposited before she arrived: the hair combings, the sediment in the slop jar, the creaking rocking chair that moves as if a heavy body were in it.

And she is constituting the ground for what comes after: her witness, her screaming "Go, Sarah!", her carrying of the old pillow and its childhood tears; these become deposits in the sediment. The ancestors are in the room. The carrying continues. She cannot leave the Fire Escape room without having been changed by what pressed up through its floor.

"I am the only man in this town who remembers you."

O'Leary says this (man, though he is speaking to the narrator's consciousness as much as to Sarah) and it is the central statement of Pillar 11. Memory is not personal sentiment. It is structural obligation. To be the one who remembers is to be the crossing point, the place where the past and the present intersect, where the dead continue to press their claim on the living, where the infant Sarah and the old woman Sarah are the same sedimentary deposit pressing up through the vegetable garden into the doctor's bed into the narrator's sleepless night.
