Accountability, Harm, and What Comes After

A Companion Document to the Constitution

The Philosophical Foundation

The American carceral system is the largest in the world by total incarcerated population and among the highest in the world in its rate of incarceration per capita, the length of its sentences, and the scope of its criminal code. It is also the most racially stratified, the most expensive relative to its outcomes, and the least effective by its own stated measures of reducing harm and producing public safety. These are not accidental features of a system that is otherwise working. They are the predictable outcomes of a system built to serve purposes other than the ones it claims.

The carceral system presents itself as the natural response to crime, as simply what happens when people break the law, as the inevitable mechanism of a society that takes harm seriously. It is a construction. The decision to respond to harm with caging was made by specific people at specific historical moments for specific reasons, many of them having nothing to do with the reduction of harm and everything to do with the control of specific populations. The construction of crime itself, the decision about which harmful acts are crimes and which are not, which harms are prosecuted aggressively and which are treated as the cost of doing business, encodes the interests of the people who built the criminal code.

A system that requires the suffering it produces to be invisible cannot be a just system. The person in solitary confinement, the person in the immigration detention facility, the person serving a mandatory minimum sentence for a nonviolent drug offense, the person who cannot afford bail and sits in pretrial detention losing their job and housing while awaiting a trial that may acquit them: all of these are people whose suffering the system is designed to make invisible. The demand that the suffering be witnessed, named, and made the basis of accountability is not soft on crime. It is the precondition for any system that is actually oriented toward reducing harm.

What the System Is Actually Doing

The Racial Construction of Crime

The United States incarcerates Black people at five times the rate of white people. This disparity cannot be explained by differential rates of harmful behavior. The research is consistent: white and Black people use drugs at similar rates and commit similar rates of many offenses, but are policed, arrested, prosecuted, convicted, and sentenced at dramatically different rates. The disparity is produced at every stage of the system, from the deployment of police resources toward Black communities and away from white ones, to the charging decisions of prosecutors, to the bail decisions of judges, to the sentencing decisions that produce longer terms for Black defendants than for white defendants convicted of identical offenses.

This is accumulated loss built into the foundation of this nation, reproduced through the criminal legal system after direct forms of racial exclusion became constitutionally impermissible. The Black Codes after Reconstruction that recriminalized Black freedom. The convict leasing system that restored forced labor under the Thirteenth Amendment's exception for punishment of crime. The war on drugs that targeted crack cocaine, prevalent in Black communities, with penalties one hundred times harsher than powder cocaine, prevalent in white ones. The mass incarceration that followed, which by the 1990s had produced a rate of Black incarceration that exceeded apartheid South Africa. This is not history. It is the present condition of the system. The construction has been maintained because it continues to serve the interests it was built to serve.

What Incarceration Does and Does Not Do

The research on incarceration as a mechanism for reducing crime is extensive and largely consistent: it does not work the way the system claims. Incarceration has a modest incapacitation effect: the person who is caged cannot commit further offenses in the community while caged. But the deterrence effect, the idea that the threat of incarceration prevents people from committing offenses, is weak and highly conditional. People who commit crimes generally do not engage in the expected utility calculation that deterrence theory requires. They act under conditions of stress, poverty, addiction, trauma, and immediate circumstance that make the distant threat of incarceration less relevant than the immediate conditions producing the behavior.

The recidivism rate, the rate at which people who have been incarcerated commit further offenses after release, is consistently around two-thirds within three years. This means that two-thirds of the time, incarceration has not changed the conditions that produced the original behavior. The conditions that produce criminal behavior are well documented: poverty, trauma, addiction, educational failure, housing instability, lack of legitimate economic opportunity, community disinvestment. Incarceration does not address any of these conditions. It often makes them worse: the incarcerated person loses employment, housing, family connections, and any economic stability they had, and emerges into the same or worse conditions than the ones that produced the original behavior, now additionally burdened with a criminal record that forecloses many legitimate opportunities.

The communities from which incarcerated people are drawn and to which they return experience mass incarceration as a form of community devastation: the removal of working-age adults, the disruption of family structures, the economic contraction, the trauma transmitted to children who have a parent incarcerated. The research on children of incarcerated parents documents elevated rates of behavioral problems, academic failure, poverty, and eventual incarceration. The carceral system reproduces the conditions that produce it. This is not a failure of the system. It is the system working as designed.

What Accountability Without Caging Looks Like

Restorative Justice

Restorative justice is a framework for responding to harm that asks different questions than the current system. Not: what rule was broken and what punishment does the rule prescribe? But: who was harmed, what do they need, who is responsible for the harm, and what can the person responsible do to make it right? The framework is not new. It draws on indigenous practices of conflict resolution from cultures around the world, on community accountability traditions that predate the modern criminal legal system, on the understanding that harm is fundamentally relational and that the repair of harm requires addressing the relationship rather than simply punishing the individual.

The research on restorative justice outcomes is consistently more positive than the research on incarceration outcomes: higher rates of victim satisfaction, lower rates of reoffense, lower costs, and the repair of community relationships that the adversarial criminal process damages. Restorative practices have been implemented in schools, communities, and criminal legal systems at various scales, with results that challenge the assumption that punishment is the only serious response to harm.

Restorative justice requires that the person who caused harm see the full weight of what they caused, that the person who was harmed be seen and heard by the process rather than processed as a complainant, and that the community affected by the harm be present to the repair rather than excluded from it. The criminal trial as currently constructed does none of these things reliably. The victim is a witness for the prosecution, not a participant in determining what repair looks like. The person who caused harm sits through a process in which the harm they caused is often less visible than the procedural maneuvering around it. The community is the gallery.

Rehabilitation as a Constitutional Obligation

If the state takes a person's liberty, the state assumes an obligation to that person. The obligation is not only to hold them securely. It is to return them, to the extent possible, to the conditions that enable full participation in community life. This means education: the right to educational programs during incarceration, including post-secondary education, the ban on which was a policy choice with no penological justification and clear negative effects on recidivism. It means addiction treatment: the majority of people incarcerated for drug offenses are people with substance use disorders, and incarceration without treatment does not address the disorder, only removes the person from the community temporarily.

It means mental health treatment: the deinstitutionalization movement of the 1970s closed psychiatric hospitals without building the community mental health infrastructure that was supposed to replace them, and the resulting gap was filled by the criminal legal system, which is now the largest provider of mental health services in the country through jails and prisons that are profoundly ill-equipped to provide them. The person with a serious mental illness who cycles through emergency rooms, jails, and the street is not being served by any system. They are being processed by a system that has no idea what to do with them and responds with the only tool it has: incarceration.

It means housing: the person released from incarceration into homelessness is being released into the conditions most directly associated with reincarceration. The connection between housing instability and criminal legal involvement is one of the most thoroughly documented relationships in criminology. Housing first policies, which provide stable housing without preconditions to people with histories of homelessness and criminal legal involvement, produce consistent reductions in criminal legal involvement. The investment in housing is an investment in public safety that outperforms incarceration by every measure.

Solitary Confinement

Solitary confinement is torture. The research on the psychological effects of isolation is unambiguous: prolonged solitary confinement produces psychosis, severe depression, self-harm, and permanent psychological damage in people who had no prior mental health history. The United Nations Standard Minimum Rules for the Treatment of Prisoners, the Nelson Mandela Rules, define solitary confinement beyond fifteen days as cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment. The United States routinely holds people in solitary for months, years, and in some cases decades.

This constitution's freedom from cruel punishment provision bans solitary confinement beyond seventy-two hours. The enforcement of that ban requires independent oversight of correctional facilities, regular inspection by bodies with genuine authority to impose consequences, and the standing of incarcerated people to bring constitutional challenges without the procedural barriers the Prison Litigation Reform Act currently imposes. Incarcerated people in the United States have been systematically stripped of the legal mechanisms to report what is done to them. This constitution restores those mechanisms.

The Death Penalty

The death penalty is a constitutional violation under the freedom from cruel punishment provision. It is also a demonstrated failure by the system's own standards: it does not deter, it is applied with documented racial disparity, it has produced the execution of innocent people at a rate the Innocence Project has documented through DNA exonerations, and it costs more than life imprisonment when the full cost of the appellate process is included. The argument for its retention is not penological. It is retributive: the belief that some offenses require death as the appropriate response.

The demand that the person who caused harm suffer the maximum possible suffering is not the same as the demand that the harm be witnessed and repaired. Retribution is the attempt to resolve grief over a loss by producing an equivalent loss. It does not resolve the grief. The research on victims' families and the death penalty consistently shows that the execution does not produce the closure it promises. The loss remains. What the witness pillar asks instead is that we stay in the room with the grief rather than seeking its resolution through further harm.

Policing

Policing as currently practiced is the enforcement arm of the construction the criminal legal system maintains. The deployment of police resources is a political choice: the decision to heavily police some communities and not others, to prioritize some offenses and not others, to treat some encounters as opportunities for assistance and others as opportunities for control, reflects the values and interests of the people making the deployment decisions. Those decisions have consistently reflected the interests of the people with political power and subordinated the interests of the people without it.

The transformation of policing required by this document is not the abolition of any community safety infrastructure. It is the redefinition of what community safety requires and who is best positioned to provide it. The mental health crisis is not a policing problem: it requires mental health professionals. The domestic dispute is not reliably a policing problem: it requires social workers and the infrastructure of domestic violence response. The person in a drug crisis is not a policing problem: they require medical intervention. The call for service that requires an armed officer with the authority to use lethal force is a subset of the calls that currently receive that response. The appropriate response to the full range of community safety needs is a full range of community safety infrastructure, of which armed policing is one component deployed in the specific circumstances where it is actually appropriate.

Accountability for police violence requires genuine consequences. The qualified immunity doctrine, which shields officers from civil liability for constitutional violations unless the specific violation has been previously clearly established in court, is a political choice presented as legal doctrine: the protection of the dominant enforcement apparatus from the accountability mechanisms that apply to everyone else. It is repealed. Officers who use force are accountable to the same legal standards as anyone else who uses force. Departments whose patterns of conduct produce systematic constitutional violations are subject to federal oversight and intervention. The person harmed by police violence has the same legal standing to seek remedy as any other person harmed by any other person.

The Transition

The transition from the current carceral system to the one this document describes is the longest and most politically difficult of all the transitions the companion documents require. The carceral system has enormous institutional inertia, a powerful political economy built around its continuation, and decades of public messaging that has constructed the incarcerated person as the appropriate object of punishment rather than as a person whose circumstances the system has failed to address.

The transition requires the immediate implementation of the constitutional prohibitions: the end of solitary confinement beyond seventy-two hours, the abolition of the death penalty, the restoration of voting rights for incarcerated people. These are not gradual. They are constitutional requirements that take effect immediately.

The longer-term transition requires building the infrastructure the current system lacks: the mental health system, the addiction treatment system, the housing system, the educational and economic opportunity system that addresses the conditions producing harm rather than warehousing the people who act on them. It requires the scaling of restorative justice practices. It requires the rebuilding of communities that have been devastated by mass incarceration over decades. It requires the truth and reparative justice process of Article VII applied specifically to the history of racial construction in the criminal legal system.

None of this happens overnight. All of it is possible.
