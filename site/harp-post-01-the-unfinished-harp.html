Chapter 1. The Unfinished Harp

The Unfinished Harp: Reading Harp Song for a Radical · Chapter 2 →

Reading Marguerite Young's last book, Harp Song for a Radical, as a philosophy of the open ending

Marguerite Young spent roughly the last twenty-five years of her life writing a biography of Eugene Victor Debs. Debs was the locomotive fireman from Terre Haute who became the country's best-known socialist and went to prison twice for his convictions. Young did not finish the book. When she fell ill, she was writing the chapter on the Pullman strike of 1894, which sent Debs to jail for the first time. That strike was meant to close the book. She died in 1995. Knopf published what she left in 1999, edited by her friend Charles Ruas, as Harp Song for a Radical: The Life and Times of Eugene Victor Debs.

The book runs close to six hundred pages, and in all of them Debs never becomes the man most people have heard of. There is no Socialist Party and no presidential campaign. There is no Atlanta Penitentiary, and no courtroom speech with its famous line, "while there is a soul in prison, I am not free." The narrative follows him to about 1888. He is in his early thirties, a union officer and magazine editor with a wife, a house, and a local name. Then the book wanders off to Whistler, Gogol, and Dostoyevsky, and stops. The climax it was building toward survives as one paragraph in an Editorial Note at the back.

How the book reached print

Young wrote in near seclusion. Ruas explains that "no one was allowed in her apartment for years on end" while she was composing. During one of her hospital stays, her building was renovated, and "the different drafts of her enormous work became mixed together." Friends helped her collate a single draft, which Ruas photocopied. She kept writing and revising through her illnesses. At the end, her niece moved the contents of her New York rooms to a house in Indianapolis and rebuilt them down to the details, and Young was nursed there.

So the book in print is several things at once. It is Young's last intention, a reassembly by friends, and an edition shaped by an editor after her death. The seams show. The front matter calls the World War I law the "Alien and Sedition Act." The Editorial Note names Debs's union the "American Railroad Workers Union" instead of the American Railway Union. These are small slips, but they are reminders that other hands finished what she could not.

What kind of book this is

By ordinary standards, Harp Song is badly out of proportion. Heinrich Heine and the German utopian Wilhelm Weitling take up some sixty pages before Debs's parents ever reach Indiana. Then come the French Icarians, Brigham Young's Mormons, and Mark Twain's brother Orion. Later, the Pinkerton detectives and the Molly Maguires fill about seventy-five pages in which Debs nearly disappears.

Young did this on purpose. She told Ruas her method: "I simply build the landscape, the time-scape, and there's the little boy." Ruas describes the structure as "spokes radiating from a center," and says she deploys "a series of multiple consciousnesses" before arriving at the record of events. The digressions are the method.

Young also named her subject across every book she wrote. "I believe that all my work explores the human desire or obsession for utopia, and the structure of all my works is the search for utopias lost and rediscovered." Then she added the sentence this study is built on: "All my writing is about the recognition that there is no single reality. But the beauty of it is that you nevertheless go on, walking towards utopia, which may not exist, on a bridge which might end before you reach the other side."

The lens

This study reads Harp Song through a philosophy that runs through Young's work. It can be stated as eleven ideas, and several of them are visible from the first pages.

The unfinished as honesty. A book about a man who never reaches his destination was written by a woman who never reached hers. Ruas says the book "stands by itself, a complete creation, without having been entirely finished." The study takes that seriously. The missing ending is not only an accident. It shows how Young thought about any story that claims to be closed.

Time as layers. The past in Young's work is not behind her characters. It lies beneath them, pressed into the present like rock. That is why Heine's Paris and Weitling's wanderings sit under Terre Haute, and why Debs's father names his son after Eugène Sue and Victor Hugo. Young writes that Debs believed socialism "had been sleeping in the womb of time long before it was born." Calling any arrangement inevitable hides the people who built it, and Young's long detours put those builders back in view.

Seeing the invention. On her first page, Young describes capitalism as an institution treated "as sacred as if it had been handed down by God to man, probably along about the time of the eviction of Adam and Eve and the fencing of the Garden of Eden." That joke carries a whole argument. Money, property, and the law that jailed Debs are agreements held in place by repetition. Seeing them as built is the first step out from under their spell.

The plural self. Young's Debs is a crowd rather than a statue. He is the "Abel who would be branded Cain," a man with "ten hopes to your one hope and ten loves to your one love." He is gentle and stubborn, "one of the jackass Socialists who would not keep their mouths shut." Young never resolves him into one thing, and the study does not either.

The view from the edges. Ruas says Young's great subjects were "the misfit, the wrecked, the impossible, and the forgotten." Her book is crowded with failed communes, cranks, convicts, and prophets who were right too early. The people who do not fit the categories are the ones who see them most clearly.

Language as a building material. Young calls Debs "an Aesopian fabulist" who spoke in the worn coins of common speech. She believed Americans of every party shared a common tongue of poetry: "They all spoke in metaphors. And Debs was one of the great lovers and users of metaphors." Her own sentences, long and circling and piled with clauses, resist the flat voice that claims to speak from nowhere.

The sacred ordinary, and a tension inside it. Young places Debs's hoped-for world "here upon this earth and not upon some other star beyond time, beyond space." Still, many of her utopians promised a distant paradise that justified sacrifice now. The study watches that tension closely. Young seems to have known it well. She said that "out of every dreamer something good comes. It might not be heaven on earth, it might only be an eight-hour day." The eight-hour day is a very ordinary kind of sacred.

The remaining ideas arrive later in the book. The witness who stays in the room with pain returns when the narrative reaches Kate Debs, the strikes, and Dostoyevsky before the firing squad. Grief as permanent weather is there too. So is the crossing point, where each person stands between everyone who came before and everyone who will come after. That last idea may be the deepest reason Young spent her final decades on a railroad fireman from Indiana.

How this study works

The chapters follow the book in order, in fifteen parts. They begin with the prison scene that opens the book and end with the strike it never reached. Every quotation from Young or Ruas appears in quotation marks with a page reference. Where Young's history can be checked against later biographies, it is, because a writer who distrusted final answers deserves a reader who checks the facts too.

Young once said a mature person is someone who has "examined all the illusions of life" and yet goes on. Harp Song for a Radical is that kind of walk, on a bridge that ends in midair. This study follows it as far as it goes.
