Harp Song for a Radical

Part Two: The Bell and the Blind Leader

Part of a reading of Harp Song for a Radical (1999), Marguerite Young’s life of Eugene Victor Debs.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 25, 2026

← Part One · The Suffragette and the Socialist: Cousin Hannah and Eugene Debs · Part Three →

Hannah leads the army. Debs refuses to be Moses.

Late in Cousin Hannah’s story, a newspaper item surfaces. Hannah had gone skiing with a party of blind skiers who followed the sound of a leader’s bell down the mountain. What none of them knew was that the leader was blind too (Chapter 52).

The image is comic, and then it is not. The society’s essay “The Politics of Distrusting Leaders” reads it as a parable: followers know they depend on the bell but not that the ringer is blind too. Hannah herself was a leader of armies. She rode against “great turbaned Turks” (Chapter 31), crossed deserts, carried suffrage into Persia and the Alps. Her cause, the vote for women, was just. Young never mocks it. What the novel questions is the way Hannah held it. She acted as though she had “no use for further experience or knowledge.” Her certainty was “set above the peaks of time” (Chapter 30).

Young put her own politics plainly, in her conversation with Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs: “If you understand hallucination and illusion, you don’t blindly follow any leader.” In her novel, a leader who claims to stand on solid ground is either deceived or deceiving. Hannah is not a fraud. She is something more interesting and more dangerous: a good woman who believes she can see.

Debs gave Young the opposite figure. The same essay notes that Young’s long biography of Eugene V. Debs came from the same conviction as the novel: the only trustworthy public figure belongs to the people rather than standing above them. Debs said it himself: “I would not be a Moses to lead you into a promised land, for someone would lead you out again.”

This is the crux of the pairing. Hannah and Debs fought on the same side of the same century. Both were mocked, both were tireless, both crossed great distances for strangers. But Hannah rides out ahead and rings the bell. Debs stays in the crowd and refuses the bell altogether.

Young does not let Debs become a saint for this. In Harp Song he is the “Abel who would be branded Cain,” a man with “ten hopes to your one hope and ten loves to your one love,” and also, in the voice of his enemies, “one of the jackass Socialists who would not keep their mouths shut.” Young avoids a single portrait. That refusal is itself a kind of answer to the blind skier’s bell. A biography that will not make its hero into a leader cannot teach readers to follow him blindly.

Seen together, Hannah and Debs mark the two poles of Young’s political imagination. One crusader carries a system and a certainty. The other carries a refusal to be anyone’s system. The novel’s lesson, as the society’s essay puts it, is not to stop following every bell, which would leave a person alone on the mountain, but to remember the one ringing it may be blind.
