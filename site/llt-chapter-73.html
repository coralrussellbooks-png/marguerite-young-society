Chapter 73

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Esther Longtree

Pillars: Unfinished Is Honest · The Past Doesn't Go Away

The Questions

Esther has given birth to “an eye, an ear, a long gold hair, a fingernail, a cobweb, nothing.” What is a birth that delivers only a fragment?

She waits “for the visitant, whole and complete, the child to come.” Will the whole child ever arrive, and what if he never does?

She cannot remember how long she has been “pregnant, everlastingly.” What has happened to time in a body that never finishes?

If the child, come at last, “might be all the stillborn coming again,” what would completion even be?

The chapter gives the everlasting pregnancy its exact and terrible texture: Esther gives birth, but only to fragments. “How often had she given birth to nothing, an eye, an ear, a long gold hair, a fingernail, a cobweb, nothing.” The births are real and they deliver no whole thing, only a part, a piece, an eye or a hair or a cobweb, or nothing at all. This is Unfinished Is Honest at its most literal and most unsparing. The unfinished is not an abstraction here; it is a body that produces fragments, a making that never assembles into a made thing, an eye without a face, a hair without a head, the incomplete born again and again as incompletion.

And she waits for the whole child who does not come. Esther waits “for the visitant, whole and complete, the child to come, the child whom she could feel under her heart,” the finished one who would gather all the fragments into a single delivered life. The waiting is the chapter’s open construction: she has carried him “so long now, heavy, heavy weight,” and he does not arrive, and the not-arriving is not presented as her defeat but as her condition, the expectancy that never closes into delivery. She waits, and the waiting itself, held for years, is what she is, the child forever felt and never born.

Time has dissolved in a body that never finishes. Esther cannot remember whether she has been pregnant “for so many years, months, or days,” the calendar gone, the sequence collapsed. A body that never completes its one process has no way to keep time, because time is measured by things beginning and ending, and in Esther nothing ends. This is The Past Doesn't Go Away in the flesh: the past of every stillbirth is present in her now, “a memory of her stillborn” inhabiting “every slow act,” and the years pile up unmarked because nothing ever concludes to mark them. She is a duration without episodes, a pregnancy without a term.

The deepest turn is that completion itself is put in doubt. If the child ever came, Esther thinks, “he might be all the stillborn coming again or different from them.” The finished child, the delivered whole, might be nothing but the fragments returned, the incompletion arriving in another form. The chapter will not let completion be a clean opposite to the fragments; even the imagined whole is haunted by the stillborn, uncertain, maybe only the incomplete wearing the shape of the complete. So the waiting has no guaranteed resolution even in fantasy, and Esther’s condition is not a delayed wholeness but a permanent expectancy, the truest form the open construction takes in a body.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that treats Esther’s fragmentary births as pure horror to recoil from, and the reading that treats her waiting for the child as a wholeness the book means to grant her at last. Neither the horror nor the promised delivery is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that the everlasting pregnancy delivers fragments, an eye, a hair, a cobweb, the incomplete born again as incompletion, Unfinished Is Honest made a body; that Esther waits for the child who does not come, and the waiting itself, held for years, is her condition rather than her defeat; that time dissolves in a body that never finishes, a duration without episodes; and that even the imagined child might be only the stillborn returned, so completion itself is put in doubt. The unfinished is a body, the waiting is the life, and the whole is never guaranteed. Esther carries the child forever and never delivers him, and the chapter keeps the expectancy open.

For Reflection

What in one’s life keeps delivering fragments rather than the whole thing one waits for?

Can a permanent expectancy, a waiting that never resolves, be a way of living rather than a failure to arrive?

If the completed version, come at last, might only be the incompletion in another form, what is one really waiting for?
