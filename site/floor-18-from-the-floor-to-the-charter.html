Adapting the Constitution for State and Local Governance

A companion document to The Floor and the Framework

Introduction: The National Floor and the Local Work

The constitution described in The Floor and the Framework is a national document. It establishes the minimum: the floor below which no level of government may push any person, with no ceiling on the rights a state or city may add. But the floor is not the whole building. States have constitutions. Counties have charters. Cities have governing documents of their own. The work of translation matters because states and localities are often where the philosophy can be implemented first, and most concretely.

This document is structured around three questions that any state or local effort must answer before it can proceed:

  • What does the national constitution already establish as a floor that state and local governments are bound to honor, extend, and never contract?

  • What requires specific state constitutional language that the national document does not itself supply?

  • What requires specific municipal charter language for cities, counties, and localities operating within states?

A fourth section addresses a practical question the other three make necessary: what does actual engagement with state constitutional and local charter reform look like, and how does this philosophy enter that process?

The national document sets the floor. The state document builds the first story. The local charter furnishes the room. None of them is the whole building, and none of them is sufficient without the others.

One note on historical context that bears on the framing: state constitutions are in many respects more directly democratic documents than the federal one. Most are longer, more specific, and more frequently amended. Several states have constitutional provisions that go substantially further than the federal document on economic rights, environmental rights, and direct democracy. This is not an accident. State constitutions were often written or rewritten in periods of political mobilization when specific communities had the power to put their priorities directly into the governing document. The tradition of more expansive state constitutionalism is a resource, not a complication.

Part One: What the National Floor Already Establishes

The national constitution's floor provisions bind state and local governments directly. Any state constitution or municipal charter that falls below these provisions is unconstitutional on its face. The work at the state and local level begins with knowing what is already required and building upward from there, never treating the floor as a ceiling.

Equal Protection and Non-Discrimination

The equal protection provision of Article VII applies to every level of government without exception. No state law, no county ordinance, no municipal code may treat any person differently based on race, sex, gender identity, sexual orientation, religion, national origin, disability, or economic status. This is the floor. States may expand the categories of protected characteristics. They may never contract the list the national document provides.

State constitutional practice here has been instructive. Several states added sexual orientation and gender identity to their state equal protection clauses before the federal interpretation of the Fourteenth Amendment reached the same result. The state constitutional addition provided protection in that state that did not yet exist federally. The direction of travel in this framework is always expansion: the state that adds explicit protections for additional categories, for housing status, for immigration status, for weight and appearance, for political affiliation, is doing the work the national floor invites.

Voting Rights

The national constitution establishes the franchise at sixteen, automatic registration, the abolition of the Electoral College, and voting rights for incarcerated people and people under guardianship. These apply to all federal elections and bind state administration of those elections. State constitutions must align their own electoral provisions with these floors for state and local elections.

States have historically been the laboratories of voting reform. Ranked choice voting was adopted in Maine and Alaska before federal legislation addressed it. Automatic voter registration was pioneered at the state level. This framework's voting provisions are floors for federal elections and models for state electoral architecture. A state constitution that extends these provisions to all state and local elections, that adds same-day registration, that creates a state-level public campaign financing system, is building on the floor rather than merely meeting it.

Economic Floor: The Rights of Article VII

The right to adequate food, housing, healthcare, and education established in Article VII is a federal constitutional right enforceable in court. State governments that fall below this floor are in violation of the national constitution. But the federal floor is a minimum threshold. States have broad authority to exceed it: to establish more generous food assistance programs, more expansive housing rights, more comprehensive healthcare coverage, more fully funded public education systems.

The historical pattern is instructive here too. The Medicaid expansion under the Affordable Care Act was optional for states. States that refused it left their residents below the floor the national system offered. In this framework, that option does not exist: the federal floor is a constitutional requirement. But states that build above it, that establish state-level universal healthcare programs, that create state housing authorities with genuine resources, that fund education above the federal equalization baseline, are doing the philosophy's work at the scale where implementation is most immediate.

Direct Democracy Mechanisms

Article I establishes the people as the first branch at the federal level. The initiative, referendum, and recall mechanisms it creates are federal. State constitutions must provide comparable mechanisms for state governance. Twenty-six states currently have some form of citizen initiative process. This framework treats direct democracy not as a supplementary feature but as structurally required: the people who are governed must have direct mechanisms to govern.

The specific parameters of state initiative and referendum processes are state constitutional questions, addressed in Part Two. What the federal floor establishes is the principle: no state constitution is adequate if it provides no mechanism for the people to act directly on the laws that govern them.

Rights That Cannot Be Removed

The rights retained by the people provision of Article VII establishes that rights are not exhausted by what is listed. State constitutions that attempt to enumerate rights as a ceiling, to say these and only these are the rights the people have, are inconsistent with this principle. State constitutional rights provisions must be understood as floors and expansions, never as comprehensive lists that exclude what is not named.

Part Two: What Requires State Constitutional Language

State constitutions are not merely reflections of federal requirements. They govern the structure of state government, the organization of state power, and the specific rights and mechanisms that state residents have against their own state government. Several of the constitution's core provisions require specific state constitutional language that goes beyond what the federal document alone provides.

State-Level Direct Democracy

Federal initiative and referendum provisions govern federal questions. State initiative and referendum provisions govern state law. Every state needs its own specific constitutional language establishing:

  • The petition threshold for a citizen initiative (this framework uses two percent of registered voters at the federal level; state equivalents typically range from four to ten percent, calibrated to the state's voter population)

  • The majority required for passage (simple majority for statutes; supermajority for constitutional amendments, consistent with the federal sixty percent threshold)

  • The subjects excluded from initiative (this framework prohibits initiatives that remove rights from Article VII; state constitutions should prohibit initiatives that contract any right established in the state or federal constitution)

  • The referendum mechanism for reviewing legislative acts before they take effect

  • The recall process for state elected officials, including governors, legislators, and judges where applicable

  • The participatory budgeting provision, adapted to state budget scale

State constitutions have experimented with these provisions extensively. California's initiative process, which requires eight percent of the gubernatorial vote total as signatures, has produced both important expansions of rights and, through the same mechanism, attempted contractions. This framework's approach to this tension is constitutional rights as floors that initiative cannot breach: the initiative that expands rights is always available; the initiative that contracts them is void.

Ohio is a specific case worth attention for Mahoning Valley readers. The 2023 fight over Issue 1, in which the legislature attempted to raise the initiative threshold to sixty percent before a reproductive rights amendment could be voted on, demonstrated both the importance of the initiative mechanism and its vulnerability to manipulation by the legislature it is meant to check. A well-drafted state constitutional provision insulates the initiative threshold itself from legislative revision: it can be changed only by the same supermajority process that would change any other constitutional provision.

State Economic Rights: Building Above the Federal Floor

Several state constitutions already include economic rights provisions that go beyond the federal document. New York's constitution includes a provision requiring the state to provide for the care and support of the needy. Montana's constitution includes an explicit right to a clean and healthful environment. Hawaii's constitution includes a right to a public education. These state-level provisions matter because they are directly enforceable against the state government in state court, without requiring federal constitutional litigation.

A state constitution built on this framework would include explicit enforceable rights to:

  • Housing: not merely as a policy goal but as a justiciable right, with the state obligated to ensure no resident is in chronic housing instability

  • Healthcare: universal coverage through the state's healthcare system, with the state obligated to fill any gap the federal system does not cover

  • Education: fully funded public education from early childhood through post-secondary, with school funding equalized across districts by state law

  • A living income floor: whether through a state minimum wage tied to cost of living, a state earned income tax credit, or a state basic income pilot, the state constitution should specify the mechanism

  • Paid caregiving: state constitutional recognition of care work as economically productive labor, with state law required to establish minimum compensation and benefits for caregiving workers

State Environmental Rights

The Pennsylvania constitution's Article I, Section 27, adopted in 1971, establishes that the people have a right to clean air, pure water, and the preservation of natural, scenic, historic, and aesthetic values of the environment, and that the Commonwealth shall conserve and maintain them for the benefit of all the people including generations yet to come. This provision has been increasingly enforced by Pennsylvania courts to require the state to act on climate, to protect public lands from extraction, and to hold the state accountable for environmental harm.

A state constitution building on this framework would include:

  • An explicit right to a clean and healthful environment for current and future residents

  • Legal standing for state residents to enforce environmental rights in state court

  • A provision establishing the rights of specific natural features, with designated state trustees empowered to act on their behalf

  • A climate obligation tied to specific timelines consistent with scientific consensus, not subject to legislative revision downward

  • An environmental justice provision prohibiting the disproportionate siting of environmental harm in low-income and minority communities

For the Mahoning Valley, an environmental rights provision has specific and immediate relevance. The brownfields left by the steel industry, the ongoing air quality challenges in communities near industrial facilities, the Mahoning River's complicated recovery: all of these are the kind of accumulated environmental harm that a state constitutional provision with teeth could begin to address through litigation and state obligation.

State-Level Wealth and Taxation

Federal tax policy is federal. But states have their own tax systems, and this framework's insistence that concentrated wealth be taxed to fund the floor applies at the state level with full force. A state constitution built on this framework would include:

  • A progressive income tax provision (several states have flat income taxes locked into their constitutions; this framework requires that state constitutions permit, and ideally mandate, progressive structures)

  • A prohibition on regressive taxation structures that shift the burden from wealth to income from labor, from income to consumption

  • A property tax equity provision that prevents the property tax funding of education from producing the zip code lottery this framework identifies as the fundamental injustice of the current system

  • A wealth tax enabling provision, even if the specific rate is left to legislation, establishing the constitutional authority to tax accumulated wealth

State Judiciary and Accountability

The federal constitution's provisions for judicial independence and accountability apply to federal courts. State judicial systems are governed by state constitutional provisions, and they vary enormously. In Ohio, as in many states, Supreme Court justices are elected in partisan elections, creating the same campaign finance dynamics for judicial selection that this framework identifies as corrupting of legislative and executive accountability.

A state constitution building on this framework would include:

  • A merit selection system for state judges with fixed terms and competitive retention review

  • Mandatory recusal requirements for judges with financial interests in cases before them

  • An independent judicial oversight body with genuine authority to investigate and discipline judicial conduct

  • Public financing for judicial elections in states where election is retained

  • An explicit prohibition on the acceptance of gifts, travel, or other benefits by sitting judges

State-Level Historical Reckoning

The constitution's truth and reparative justice provision operates at the federal level. But the specific histories that require reckoning are often state and local histories. The specific decisions about where to put highways through Black neighborhoods, which communities received FHA loans and which were redlined, which counties maintained segregated schools longest: these were state and local decisions as much as federal ones.

A state constitution building on this framework would include:

  • A permanent state truth and historical accountability commission, funded through the state budget, with community governance and democratic oversight

  • Specific acknowledgment of the state's role in specific documented harms, named directly in the constitution rather than left to legislative discretion

  • An ongoing reparative obligation, with the specific mechanisms determined through the community-governed process rather than legislated in advance

  • An education obligation that connects directly to this provision: the state's schools are required to teach the state's actual history, including what the state did

Part Three: What Requires Municipal Charter Language

Cities and counties operate under state-granted authority. Home rule charters give municipalities the maximum authority a state grants to local governments to govern themselves without state legislative approval for every action. A home rule charter is a city's or county's constitution: it establishes the structure of local government, the rights of residents against that government, and the mechanisms through which residents govern directly.

This framework's most immediate implementation is often at the local level. The city that establishes a universal basic income pilot, the county that creates a community land trust authority, the municipality that implements participatory budgeting at meaningful scale: these are not waiting for the federal or state document. They are doing the work in the space the law currently allows, and they are creating the evidence base that state and federal adoption will eventually require.

Home Rule and Its Limits

Home rule authority varies enormously by state. In strong home rule states like Ohio, municipalities have broad authority to govern local affairs without state legislative approval. In Dillon's Rule states, municipalities have only the authority the state legislature explicitly grants. This framework's local implementation operates within whatever home rule authority the state currently provides, while the state constitutional work described in Part Two expands that authority over time.

The immediate practical question for any locality is: what can we do right now, within current state authority, through a home rule charter? The answer is usually more than most local governments attempt. Local charters can establish:

  • Local initiative, referendum, and recall mechanisms more accessible than the state process

  • Local non-discrimination ordinances that exceed state minimums (subject to state preemption, which Part Two's state constitutional work addresses)

  • Local economic floor provisions, including local minimum wages, local paid leave requirements, local anti-wage-theft enforcement

  • Local environmental standards, including local climate action plans with enforceable timelines

  • Local participatory budgeting requirements for defined portions of the local budget

  • Local community land trust authority and affordable housing obligations

  • Local public banking or public financial services provisions

  • Local police accountability structures, including civilian oversight with genuine authority

Local Direct Democracy

This framework's Article I is federal, but its spirit applies at every level. A municipal charter built on this philosophy would include:

  • A citizen initiative process for local ordinances, with a low petition threshold scaled to the local population

  • A mandatory referendum on any ordinance that restructures local voting rights, local governance powers, or that commits the municipality to major long-term financial obligations

  • A recall process for all elected local officials

  • A participatory budgeting process covering at least ten percent of the local discretionary budget, conducted through accessible multilingual in-person and digital processes

  • A citizen oversight board with genuine authority over local administration, not merely advisory standing

Local Economic Floor

Cities and counties are the level of government closest to where the floor either holds or does not. The person who is housing-insecure, food-insecure, or without healthcare is navigating local systems first: the local shelter, the local food bank, the local public hospital. A municipal charter built on this framework would establish:

  • A local right to shelter: the municipality is obligated to ensure that no resident sleeps outside without access to safe shelter, funded through the local budget and state and federal resources

  • A local food security program, operating through the local school system, community centers, and public facilities, available to any resident

  • A local healthcare access obligation, including a public option for primary care through local health departments or community health centers

  • A local living wage requirement for all municipal employees and all contractors doing business with the municipality

  • A local anti-displacement policy, including just-cause eviction requirements, right-to-return provisions for residents displaced by publicly subsidized development, and community benefit agreements for major development projects

Local Environmental Provisions

Municipal environmental provisions have been among the most creative and consequential local governance innovations of the past two decades. Pittsburgh adopted a local environmental rights ordinance modeled on the community rights movement. Several Ohio municipalities have passed resolutions recognizing the rights of local ecosystems, though state courts have sometimes preempted them. A municipal charter built on this framework would include:

  • An explicit local right to a clean and healthful environment for all residents

  • A local climate action plan with enforceable timelines, incorporated into the charter so that it cannot be reversed by a future council majority without a supermajority or a public referendum

  • Local community rights provisions for specific natural features: the local river, the local watershed, the local park system, with designated trustees empowered to act on their behalf

  • A local environmental justice provision prohibiting the siting of polluting facilities in communities already bearing disproportionate environmental burden, with a cumulative impact assessment required for any new permit

  • A local brownfield remediation obligation, funded through a local environmental restoration fund supported by assessments on new development

For the Mahoning Valley specifically, the brownfield provision is not abstract. The region has more brownfield acreage per capita than almost any other metropolitan area in the country. A local charter provision that establishes remediation as an ongoing municipal obligation rather than a project-by-project negotiation with developers would be both constitutionally significant and immediately relevant to community development.

Local Accountability and Transparency

This framework's transparency provisions apply to all levels of government. A municipal charter built on this philosophy would include:

  • Real-time disclosure of all lobbying contacts with elected and appointed officials, in a publicly accessible database

  • Mandatory disclosure of all financial interests of elected and appointed officials, updated annually and available to any resident

  • Campaign contribution caps for local elections, with public financing available to candidates who demonstrate grassroots support through small donations

  • A local inspector general or independent auditor with genuine authority, tenure protections, and the standing to investigate any municipal department or official

  • A community oversight board for local law enforcement with subpoena power, independent investigative authority, and the ability to impose discipline rather than merely recommend it

  • Open meeting requirements more expansive than state law minimums, with genuine accessibility provisions including multilingual interpretation, remote participation, and accessible venues

Local Historical Accountability

Local governments were the direct agents of many of the harms this framework's reparative justice provision addresses. Urban renewal programs that destroyed Black neighborhoods. Highway construction that severed communities of color from economic centers. Local police departments that enforced segregation. Local school boards that maintained separate and unequal systems.

A municipal charter built on this framework would include:

  • A permanent local truth and historical accountability commission, governed by the communities most directly affected, with a budget line in the annual municipal budget

  • A local reparative investment provision, directing a defined percentage of local tax revenue to the communities most directly harmed by documented local government decisions, for a period determined by the democratic process

  • A local curriculum provision, connected to the local school governance structure, requiring that local schools teach the local history honestly, including what the local government did

  • A public memorial and acknowledgment process, governed by affected communities, for the specific losses the local government produced

Part Four: What Engagement with Reform Efforts Actually Looks Like

This section addresses a practical question the other three make necessary: this constitution is a philosophical document with a specific publication strategy. It lives on Substack and as a free PDF. It is not lobbying for a specific piece of legislation, affiliated with a specific political organization, or timed to a specific electoral cycle. The question of how it enters actual reform processes is a real one.

The Landscape of State Constitutional Reform

State constitutional reform is rare and slow by design. Most state constitutions require a supermajority of the legislature plus ratification by the voters to amend. Full conventions, which would allow wholesale revision, are even rarer: most states require legislative referral to trigger a convention, which means the legislature must agree to the process before it begins. The exceptions are states with mandatory periodic convention referendums, where the voters are asked at defined intervals whether they want to call a convention.

The most active current state constitutional reform efforts include:

  • New York, which has a periodic mandatory convention referendum and has seen significant organizing around the question of whether to call one

  • Ohio, which has a history of constitutional initiative campaigns and where the 2023 fight over Issue 1 put the amendment process itself in the public eye

  • Several Western states where environmental rights provisions are being actively litigated and where new provisions are being organized

  • States where reproductive rights campaigns have produced constitutional amendments since 2022, demonstrating that specific single-issue constitutional campaigns can succeed even in difficult political environments

How Philosophical Documents Enter Reform Processes

This constitution is not a lobbying document, but the ideas in it are precisely the kind of intellectual infrastructure that reform movements need and often lack. The gap between 'we want to change this' and 'here is the language and philosophy that would accomplish that change' is where documents like this one do their work.

The specific pathways through which this kind of document enters real reform processes are:

Academic and Legal Infrastructure

Law schools with active constitutional law clinics, think tanks focused on state constitutional law, and academic centers for democracy reform are the institutions that translate philosophical frameworks into draft constitutional language. Organizations like the Brennan Center for Justice, the Center for Constitutional Transitions, and state-specific academic centers regularly publish model constitutional provisions and analyses of state constitutional reform options. This framework's provisions are candidates for this kind of academic translation: the philosophical argument is made, and the legal drafting work would turn it into model language.

Movement Organizations

The organizations most likely to engage with this framework directly are the ones working on specific provisions it addresses. Common Cause and the Brennan Center on voting rights and direct democracy. The National Housing Law Project and housing justice organizations on economic floor provisions. Earthjustice and state environmental law organizations on environmental rights. The ACLU on equal protection and criminal justice. These organizations work with philosophical frameworks when they encounter them, and they are always looking for the intellectual grounding that makes their specific campaigns legible as part of a larger argument.

This framework's distinctive contribution to these conversations is the philosophical grounding it provides for what these organizations advocate. The argument that the economic floor is not charity but the material precondition for the inner life, and that the inner life is not a luxury but the substance of what the constitutional project is for: this is not an argument any of these organizations is currently making, because it requires the philosophical apparatus that this project has been building. The conversation between the philosophy and the movement organizations is the place where the document enters real reform processes.

Local Government Innovation

Local government is where the most immediate implementation happens, and where the barriers are lowest. The city council that adopts a participatory budgeting process does not need a state constitutional amendment. The county that establishes a community land trust authority is working within existing state law. The municipality that creates a local inspector general with genuine authority is exercising home rule power it already has.

This framework is a resource for local elected officials and staff who are trying to do more than their current documents authorize, and for residents who are trying to hold their local governments accountable to a standard more demanding than the minimum the law requires. Local government innovation organizations like the National League of Cities and the National Association of Counties, as well as state-level municipal leagues, are the networks through which ideas about what local charters and ordinances can do circulate among practitioners.
