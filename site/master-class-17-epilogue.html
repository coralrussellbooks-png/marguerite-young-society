Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Esther Longtree · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

My hope with this survey of Marguerite Young's craft, is that readers will come away seeing Miss MacIntosh, My Darling more clearly, and that writers studying it will find not only a catalogue of the devices available to them but a demonstration of what those devices can be made to do when every one of them is bent to a single vision. The lesson the section has kept arriving at is that Young's techniques are not separable ornaments. Her repetitions, her sounds, her oppositions, her figures, her excess and her reticence, her broken syntax and her stopped clocks all perform the same few convictions, that reality and illusion do not divide, that the self is many, that the dead do not leave, that nothing resolves. A device, in her hands, is never decoration added to a meaning. It is the meaning, worked at the scale of the sound, the clause, the sentence, the chapter, until there is no gap left between what the prose says and what the prose does.

Young's contribution to writing

Marguerite Young's Miss MacIntosh, My Darling expands what the novel can attempt, and it is worth naming these enlargements, because they are the inheritance she leaves to writers willing to learn from her.

She demonstrates, first, that reality and dream can be rendered on one plane without ranking them, that a prose which refuses to sort the hallucinated from the actual can be more honest about consciousness than any realism, because consciousness itself does not sort its contents while having them. She shows that the divided, plural self, Spitzer and his twin, Catherine and her invented company, can be built into the very structure of a book rather than merely described in it, so that fragmentation becomes a form and not just a subject. She proves that poetic density can be sustained at novel length, that the lyric sentence need not be confined to the short forms, and that musicality carried across 1,198 pages produces an immersion no shorter work can reach. She offers, through Miss MacIntosh and Vera and Catherine and Esther Longtree, a range of female interiority (physical, visionary, laboring, grieving) rendered with a psychological depth and a refusal of easy judgment that the tradition had and rarely does extend to such women. She meditates on death and the persistence of the dead not as a theme visited but as a condition inhabited, giving the book a cosmic dimension that never leaves the domestic ground it stands on. And she builds, out of the bus driver and the waitress and the clam-digger and the deaf man, a polyphony that grants mythic scale to the American margins, making an epic out of the people the epic tradition had always left out.

What binds these contributions is the conviction this book has tried to trace: that form and meaning are one thing, that how a novel is made is inseparable from what it knows. Young could not live long enough to write the book on writing she said she would never write. She left the lesson in the architecture instead, for any reader patient enough to feel the wheel turn, and for any writer willing to learn how a novel can hold together when everything the tradition promised, the stable self, the linear time, the resolved ending, the single reality, has been let go.
