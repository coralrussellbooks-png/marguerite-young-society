Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 2

On Chapter 2 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 1 · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 3 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: Reality Is Constructed · Grief Doesn't End

The Questions

Is Catherine condemned for building a world of dreams, or for something else she does to it?

What is the difference between living in an invention and being sealed inside one?

When Catherine’s dreams “need carry no credentials other than that they were her dreams,” is that freedom, or a door locking from the inside?

Her grief built this world. Why does the grief never reach open air?

Catherine is the book’s first full portrait of Reality Is Constructed, and it has to be read with care, because what is wrong with her is not the thing it first appears to be. She has built a world: opium dreams, imaginary callers, a shuttered house by the sea where the clocks are stopped and the dead come to visit. The building is not the error. Everyone in this book builds a world, and the novel never once condemns the making of one.

What it shows instead is the sealing. Catherine has forgotten that her world was made. She holds that “things were not what they seemed” and that “her life was this play of illusion,” and she takes “nothing certain but uncertainty” as a settled floor rather than an open question. The clearest sign is her freedom from any check outside herself: she is “charmed to greet each shade, for her dreams needed carry no credentials other than that they were her dreams.” An invention that needs no credentials is one with the door locked from the inside. This is the caution the book turns on, and Catherine is where it is drawn first and most fully.

Her self is plural, her heart “not centralized but scattered through every nerve cell,” her body a “beautiful corpse” tended by a mind that has left it; and her time is layered rather than linear, an “eternal twilight” where days and nights blur and the dead keep their appointments. In an open construction this plurality and this layered time would be truthful, the ordinary condition of a self and of memory. Shut inside the dream, they harden into a tomb she mistakes for the only room there is. The same material that will make Mr. Spitzer porous makes Catherine sealed.

Grief is the engine of the house. Catherine builds against real loss, the husband who left, the death that emptied her, and grief of that size is meant to be carried. Grief Doesn't End would keep it in the open, permanent weather one lives inside without pretending it will pass. Catherine does the other thing: she builds the loss a sealed house and moves in, until she can say that “God was dead or sleeping as she was” and mean that the weather has been shut out for good. Her grief is not false. It is walled, and the walling of it is what fails.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that lifts Catherine’s way of living into a wisdom to adopt, embrace illusion, prefer the dream, make a private paradise against the disappointments of the world. It refuses just as firmly the opposite reading that writes her off as a drugged invalid with nothing to teach. Neither the endorsement nor the dismissal meets what the chapter is doing.

The footing is the distinction Chapter 1 opened and this chapter drives home. Living in an invention is the ordinary human condition, not the sin. Catherine’s danger lies in sealing it off: she forgot the world was made and locked the door, and her plural self and her layered time, real and even true, became a tomb because the construction was closed. The footing the book trusts is the reverse of hers, to see the construction and keep it open. What fails is not the dream. It is sealing it off from life.

For Reflection

Which of one’s own worlds is built, and is the door still open?

Where does comfort end and sealing oneself off begin?

What loss has one housed and moved into, rather than left it standing in the weather?
