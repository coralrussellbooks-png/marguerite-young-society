Reading Miss MacIntosh, My Darling for Its Structure

Marguerite Young was a student of Ronald S. Crane at the University of Chicago. Crane was a professor of English and a leader of the Chicago critics, who read a novel as a made thing, built like a poem. His own essay on Fielding, “The Concept of Plot and the Plot of Tom Jones” (1950), is still one of the best-known studies of how that novel is built. He gave his students an assignment that Young described in two interviews more than twenty years apart: the class read Tom Jones thirteen times to study its structure. Both times the count is thirteen.

From the Dalkey Archive interview:

We read Tom Jones thirteen times, doing an intricate study of its structure. Nothing could have been more valuable for my own writing, although it was one time more than I cared to read Tom Jones.

Marguerite Young, in Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs, “A Conversation with Marguerite Young,” Review of Contemporary Fiction (Fall 1989)

From the Roy Newquist interview:

We read Tom Jones thirteen times for its poetic structure. I have been grateful all my life for this beautiful discipline, for the adventure of spirit in regarding the novel as a poem and not merely a realistic or naturalistic document of some kind.

Marguerite Young, in Roy Newquist, Conversations (Rand McNally, 1967)

Henry Fielding's Tom Jones (1749) is one of the first great comic novels in English, organized around a structural principle that its surface actively conceals: everything that appears to be a digression is load-bearing, every subplot returns to the main plot, and the apparent disorder of the narrative conceals a meticulous architecture of cause and consequence beneath it. You cannot see this architecture on a first reading. You can barely see it on a third. Crane assigned thirteen readings because the structure of Tom Jones does not become fully visible until the reader has accumulated enough familiarity with every element to see how each element relates to every other.

Young absorbed this principle and spent eighteen years applying it to a novel that looks nothing like Tom Jones. Miss MacIntosh, My Darling has no ironic prefatory essays calling attention to its architecture. It does not announce its structure. It moves in spirals and returns and accumulations that feel, on a first reading, like digression. This is intentional. Young built a novel whose deep architecture is invisible until the reader has spent enough time inside it to begin to see how it is organized.

This series of chapters is the record of one reader spending enough time inside it.

What Young Said About the Structure

Young described the novel's architecture directly in the Dalkey Archive interview. The description is the clearest statement of structural intent she ever made about Miss MacIntosh, My Darling:

She is the central character with all the spokes of other characters radiating out from her. I always thought of Miss MacIntosh as the center of the wheel, the hub, then the spokes as the subsidiary, secondary characters, and the wheel as endlessly expanding like a universe.

Marguerite Young, in Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs, “A Conversation with Marguerite Young,” Review of Contemporary Fiction (Fall 1989)

The wheel with Miss MacIntosh at the hub and the other characters as spokes is the architect's description of the blueprint. Young did not offer this as an interpretation of the novel. She offered it as a description of how she built it. The chapters that follow take this description as their starting point and ask what the wheel actually contains: what the spokes are made of, what the hub's absence means when Miss MacIntosh is already gone from Vera's life on the novel's first pages, and what holds the wheel together when the center is gone.

Young also described the novel's structural tradition. When asked in the Dalkey Archive interview whether she considered herself experimental, she said:

I see myself as traditional even though I know you see my work as experimental. I don't really consider Sterne, Joyce, and Proust experimental either because the tradition of their writing goes back a long way. Traditional. The Grand Tradition. Clear back to Don Quixote.

Marguerite Young, in Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs, “A Conversation with Marguerite Young,” Review of Contemporary Fiction (Fall 1989)

And when asked why the novel has no beginning, middle, or end:

Because life has no beginning, middle or end.

Marguerite Young, in Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs, “A Conversation with Marguerite Young,” Review of Contemporary Fiction (Fall 1989)

These statements together define the project these chapters undertake. The novel is traditional, not experimental: its roots run back through Sterne and Cervantes to the oldest narrative traditions in the language. It has no beginning, middle, or end because it was built on a structural principle, the wheel, the hub-and-spoke, that does not depend on linear progression. And it was built by a writer who had been trained to read a novel thirteen times before she trusted her understanding of how it was constructed.

What This Series Does

The Deep Architecture series examines the novel's structural foundations: the philosophical and literary traditions Young embedded in the novel's architecture, the formal devices she used at the sentence and chapter level, and the patterns that become visible only across the novel's full length.

The chapters are organized by layer rather than by the order of the novel's chapters, in six Movements. Movement One looks at the wheel itself. Movement Two follows the pillars through philosophy, history, poetry, feminism, religion, and comedy. Movement Three takes up the cosmic layer: music, Dante's lower and upper worlds, William James, Schopenhauer, and Nietzsche. Movement Four looks at the formal devices: the Grey Goose bus, the windmill, the question mark, the quest that runs back four thousand years, and the names Young gave her characters. Movement Five traces the traditions the novel absorbs, from Raleigh and the Ancient Mariner to Scheherazade, Lady Macbeth, and Childe Roland. Movement Six asks what the novel teaches.

The pillars of Radical Immanence are named where the frame calls for them: in Movement Two, which follows the pillars through the traditions, and in the closing Radical Immanence Reading of an essay. Elsewhere the passages speak for themselves, and the pillars each essay touches are listed at the top of its page. For each pillar traced chapter by chapter through the whole novel, see The Wheel and the Spokes.

None of these structural elements are announced in the novel. Young did not label her chapters with their Dantean canticle or their Boethian musical category. She placed the derelict windmill in a flooded field in Chapter 6 and did not return to it explicitly until Chapter 19, and did not name Miss MacIntosh a windmill chaser until Chapter 65. She gave the Egyptian sailor directions through the labyrinth in Chapter 2 and did not name the minotaur as something not to fear until midway through those directions. She put the question mark in the novel nearly 4,000 times and distributed it in patterns that only become visible when counted.

This is what thirteen readings of Tom Jones taught her to do: to build a structure that the reader cannot see on the first encounter, that accumulates across the full length of the work, and that rewards the reader who stays long enough to feel its weight.

A Note on Evidence

Every architectural claim in this series is grounded in the novel. The series draws on Young's interviews and essays throughout, especially the Dalkey Archive interview with Fuchs and Friedman, the Roy Newquist interview, the Charles Ruas interview, and the essays collected in Inviting the Muses. Young spoke clearly about her work when asked the right questions. The interviews are primary sources and are treated as such.

The series also draws on direct analysis of the novel, including counts of the question mark distribution across all 82 chapters, word-by-word searches for motifs and allusions, and close reading of passages whose structural significance became apparent only through the accumulation of related findings elsewhere in the novel.

This is, in other words, an attempt to read Miss MacIntosh, My Darling the way Ronald Crane read Tom Jones with his students at the University of Chicago. Not thirteen times. But enough times to begin to see what Young built.

Young's Own Final Word on How to Read It

In her last conversation with Miriam Fuchs, at St. Vincent's Hospital in New York in June 1993, Fuchs asked Young how she would advise people to read Miss MacIntosh, My Darling. Young said they should not take it so seriously. They should read it for the humor.

Then she said:

They can always read it again, if they like it. At different periods in your life you see a different book.

Marguerite Young, in Miriam Fuchs, “A Brief Conversation with Marguerite Young,” St. Vincent's Hospital, New York, June 1, 1993, in Marguerite Young, Our Darling (Dalkey Archive Press, 1994)

The Deep Architecture series is one reader's account of what becomes visible after many readings, at one time of life, in one place. Other readers at other periods will see other books. Young expected this. She built the wheel so that each spoke could be entered from the rim, and the center would look different depending on where you stood.

The door is open. Come in.
