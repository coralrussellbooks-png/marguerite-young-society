Reading Miss MacIntosh, My Darling for Its Structure

Marguerite Young was a student of Ronald S. Crane at the University of Chicago. Crane was a professor of aesthetics who believed in the novel as a poem, and he gave his students an assignment that Young described twice in separate interviews, years apart, with consistent precision. The class read Tom Jones thirteen times, doing an intricate study of its structure. Young's account of this discipline appears in the Roy Newquist interview and again in the Dalkey Archive interview with Miriam Fuchs and Ellen Friedman. The counts match. The novel is the same. The assessment is the same in both accounts.

From the Dalkey Archive interview:

We read Tom Jones thirteen times, doing an intricate study of its structure. Nothing could have been more valuable for my own writing, although it was one time more than I cared to read Tom Jones.

Marguerite Young, Dalkey Archive interview with Miriam Fuchs and Ellen G. Friedman

From the Roy Newquist interview:

We read Tom Jones thirteen times for its poetic structure. I have been grateful all my life for this beautiful discipline, for the adventure of spirit in regarding the novel as a poem and not merely a realistic or naturalistic document of some kind.

Marguerite Young, Conversations with American Writers, Roy Newquist

Tom Jones by Henry Fielding is the first great comic novel in the English language, organized around a structural principle that its surface actively conceals: everything that appears to be a digression is load-bearing, every subplot returns to the main plot, and the apparent disorder of the narrative conceals a meticulous architecture of cause and consequence beneath it. You cannot see this architecture on a first reading. You can barely see it on a third. Crane assigned thirteen readings because the structure of Tom Jones does not become fully visible until the reader has accumulated enough familiarity with every element to see how each element relates to every other.

Young absorbed this principle and spent eighteen years applying it to a novel that looks nothing like Tom Jones. Miss MacIntosh, My Darling has no ironic prefatory essays calling attention to its architecture. It does not announce its structure. It moves in spirals and returns and accumulations that feel, on a first reading, like digression. This is intentional. Young built a novel whose deep architecture is invisible until the reader has spent enough time inside it to begin to see how it is organized.

This series of chapters is the record of one reader spending enough time inside it.

What Young Said About the Structure

Young described the novel's architecture directly in the Dalkey Archive interview. The description is the clearest statement of structural intent she ever made about Miss MacIntosh, My Darling:

She is the central character with all the spokes of other characters radiating out from her. I always thought of Miss MacIntosh as the center of the wheel, the hub, then the spokes as the subsidiary, secondary characters, and the wheel as endlessly expanding like a universe.

Marguerite Young, Dalkey Archive interview with Miriam Fuchs and Ellen G. Friedman

The wheel with Miss MacIntosh at the hub and the other characters as spokes is the architect's description of the blueprint. Young did not offer this as an interpretation of the novel. She offered it as a description of how she built it. The chapters that follow take this description as their starting point and ask what the wheel actually contains: what the spokes are made of, what the hub's absence means after Miss MacIntosh walks into the sea in the opening pages, and what holds the wheel together when the center is gone.

Young also described the novel's structural tradition. When asked in the Dalkey Archive interview whether she considered herself experimental, she said:

I see myself as traditional even though I know you see my work as experimental. I don't really consider Sterne, Joyce, and Proust experimental either because the tradition of their writing goes back a long way. Traditional. The Grand Tradition. Clear back to Don Quixote.

Marguerite Young, Dalkey Archive interview

And when asked why the novel has no beginning, middle, or end:

Because life has no beginning, middle or end.

Marguerite Young, Dalkey Archive interview

These statements together define the project these chapters undertake. The novel is traditional, not experimental: its roots run back through Sterne and Cervantes to the oldest narrative traditions in the language. It has no beginning, middle, or end because it was built on a structural principle, the wheel, the hub-and-spoke, that does not depend on linear progression. And it was built by a writer who had been trained to read a novel thirteen times before she trusted her understanding of how it was constructed.

What This Series Does

The Deep Architecture series examines the novel's structural foundations: the philosophical and literary traditions Young embedded in the novel's architecture, the formal devices she used at the sentence and chapter level, and the patterns that become visible only across the novel's full length.

The sections are organized by architectural layer rather than by chapter sequence. Each movement addresses one structural element: the wheel and its spokes, the Boethian musical system, the Dantean cosmological map, the bus as cosmic vehicle and the named passengers it carries, the quest traditions accumulated across four thousand years of literary history, the windmill as a motif threaded through the novel from Chapter 1 to Chapter 70, the question mark as a formal device distributed with structural intention, and the onomastic architecture of the names Young gave her characters.

None of these structural elements are announced in the novel. Young did not label her chapters with their Dantean canticle or their Boethian musical category. She placed the derelict windmill in a flooded field in Chapter 6 and did not return to it explicitly until Chapter 19, and did not name Miss MacIntosh a windmill chaser until Chapter 65. She gave the Egyptian sailor directions through the labyrinth in Chapter 2 and did not name the minotaur as something not to fear until midway through those directions. She put the question mark in the novel 3,998 times and distributed it in patterns that only become visible when counted.

This is what thirteen readings of Tom Jones taught her to do: to build a structure that the reader cannot see on the first encounter, that accumulates across the full length of the work, and that rewards the reader who stays long enough to feel its weight.

A Note on Evidence

Every architectural claim in this series is grounded in the novel. The series draws on Young's interviews and essays throughout, particularly the Dalkey Archive interview with Fuchs and Friedman, the Roy Newquist interview, the Charles Ruas interview, and the essays collected in Inviting the Muses. Young spoke precisely about her work when asked the right questions. The interviews are primary sources and are treated as such.

The series also draws on direct analysis of the novel, including counts of the question mark distribution across all 82 chapters, word-by-word searches for motifs and allusions, and close reading of passages whose structural significance became apparent only through the accumulation of related findings elsewhere in the novel.

This is, in other words, an attempt to read Miss MacIntosh, My Darling the way Ronald Crane read Tom Jones with his students at the University of Chicago. Not thirteen times. But enough times to begin to see what Young built.

Young's Own Final Word on How to Read It

In the last interview Miriam Fuchs conducted with Young before her death in 1995, Fuchs asked Young how she would advise people to read Miss MacIntosh, My Darling. Young said they should not take it so seriously. They should read it for the humor.

Then she said:

They can always read it again, if they like it. At different periods in your life you see a different book.

Marguerite Young, final interview with Miriam Fuchs, June 1993

The Deep Architecture series is one reader's account of what becomes visible after many readings, at one particular period of life, in one particular place. Other readers at other periods will see other books. Young expected this. She built the wheel so that each spoke could be entered from the rim, and the center would look different depending on where you stood.

The door is open. Come in.
