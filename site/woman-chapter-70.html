Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 70

← Chapter 69 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 71 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Mrs. Hogden · Musidora Tidings

A corporeal woman intrudes on Catherine's dream in this chapter, and plural selfhood and the book's practice with the erased carry it. Mrs. Hogden arrives as a "faceless woman," a suspected shoplifter burdened with "worldly goods," layered in packages and eclectic objects, and the accumulation she carries is the cultural and material baggage the book keeps loading onto its women, a self buried under what it has gathered. Against Miss MacIntosh's practicality she is the disordered and chaotic woman, the other model of female existence, and the two of them, the disciplined and the cluttered, are two shapes the same constraint takes.

She insists on teaching arithmetic, imposing logic on a household ruled by dream, and her failure to be accepted by the bedridden Catherine makes her a "rival invalid," one aging, uncelebrated woman contending with another for standing in a house that has no room for either. Her eccentric solitude, the talking to herself, the chess played against invented partners with real tears at the wins and losses, is plural selfhood scattered across imagined company the way Catherine scatters herself across imaginary guests, a woman multiplying herself because the world has left her alone, and her weeping over a game humanizes her against every dismissal of the mad woman.

Her grotesque physicality, the neglect of hygiene, the refusal to be an invisible or attractive woman, is the book's confrontation with the unattractive figure the world erases, a body that will not disappear despite its flaws. Her delusion that she is mistress rather than servant, her invented social life, is the desire for the respect and power denied an aging, impoverished woman, and the servants who will not disillusion her grant her, quietly, the agency the house denies her outright, real and dream as one country extended down through the staff who let her keep her dream.

When she is removed at last by male legal authority, the removal is the patriarchal control over a woman's body and space made literal, a woman filed out of the house by the same machinery that files the wills. The playroom she leaves behind, full of discarded objects and traces, is the marginalized woman's history reduced to leavings, and the coded chalk message she writes on her way out is her small assertion of self against erasure, a self insisting on continuance where the record would keep nothing. The chapter leaves her the way the book leaves all its erased, present and unaccounted for, gone from the house and not gone from it, kept at the threshold with Musidora and the coachman and Martha and the mute, one more woman the arrangement could not quite make disappear.
