Chapter 57: The Tailor's Bill

← Chapter 56 · Guide Home · Chapter 58 →

Research notes: Chapter 57

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer · The Great Tailor

Themes: Dreams and Imagination

Symbols: Cards, Games, and Chance · Roses and Flowers

Philosophy: Freud and Jung

Allusions: Gambling and Playing Cards · Suicide in History and Literature · The Civil War

The bill keeps arriving.

Mr. Spitzer has paid for Peron's funeral: paid for everything, the suit, the ceremony, the violinist with the empty sleeve, the track bugler. He went bankrupt paying. And yet the tailor's bill keeps coming in the mail, addressed to him, demanding payment for the black funeral suit he had made for his brother. He does not know who the tailor is. He does not know where the shop is. The address on the bill does not correspond to any street in any city directory he can find.

He begins to look for the tailor. He follows directions in his dreams. He follows directions in waking life. The directions contradict each other. The shop is not there. The street is not there. The bill keeps arriving.

Chapter 57 is a chapter about the debt that cannot be settled: the grief that sends bills long after you believe you have finished paying. It is also, in its dreamlike wandering through Boston's libraries and streets, one of the most quietly comic chapters in the novel: Mr. Spitzer seeking an address that does not exist, consulting city directories, encountering a Confederate soldier who insists the South won the Civil War, trying to find the soul's permanent address in a library reading room full of people who are equally lost.

What actually happens in Chapter 57

Mr. Spitzer receives the tailor's bill and attempts to pay it. The tailor is the Great Tailor (possibly the mad tailor from Chapter 56, the one who stitched him with basting threads) and the bill is for the black funeral suit worn by Peron at his burial. Joachim is the executor. He arranged the funeral. He paid for it. The bill should not be arriving. And yet it arrives, persistently, from an address that cannot be found in any directory.

He searches. He tries following the dream logic of the directions, knowing that dreams operate by opposites (dark is fair, life is death, man is woman) and allowing for reversals and contradictions in his navigation. He visits the library reading room, that last open space where anyone can stay as long as they cause no distraction, and consults city directories and old newspapers looking for the tailor's address. He finds nothing. The shop does not exist in the records. It may never have existed.

In the library he encounters the characters who populate the margins of sanity: a Confederate soldier who is certain the South won the Civil War and is living accordingly; others engaged in debates about the soul's existence and the nature of immortality. Mr. Spitzer wants to join the debate about immortality (he has opinions, he has longing for certainty) but holds back, afraid of rejection, afraid of being seen as the kind of person who takes these questions seriously in a reading room.

He leaves without finding the address. He watches the twilight sky, sees clouds shaped like skulls and hands and birds. He imagines a rose named after both brothers (red and white together, the two colors of the two men) and in his mind the rose changes from red to white. The bill will come again tomorrow.

The debt that cannot be settled

Young's choice of a tailor's bill as the emblem of Joachim's unpayable debt is characteristically precise. A tailor's bill is a mundane, legible, legal thing: it specifies what was made, for whom, at what price. It is a record. It is a claim. It demands response. And it keeps arriving, the same bill, addressed correctly, from an address that does not exist.

The debt the bill represents is not financial. Joachim paid the financial debt; he paid everything, until there was nothing left, until the twin house was gone. The debt the bill represents is the debt of grief, which is not a financial category and does not respond to payment. You cannot settle grief by paying the bill. The bill comes back. The executor who arranged the perfect funeral, who placed the poker chips in the grave and gave away the wardrobe and kept only his own threadbare suit; he cannot close the estate because the estate is not a legal matter. The estate is the relationship. The estate is what remains when a person is gone and the person who loved them is still here.

The tailor is the mad creator from Chapter 56: the one who stitched existence from the wrong materials with the wrong stitches. Now the creator is sending bills for his work. Mr. Spitzer, who was made by this creator, is being asked to pay for his own construction. The bill is for the suit that dressed the dead man. The dead man was his brother. He was his brother. The bill is for himself.

Dreams operating by opposites

Mr. Spitzer knows that dreams operate by opposites. Dark is fair. Life is death. Man is woman. North is south. The place you are looking for is never where the dream says it is; it is where the dream says the opposite thing is.

This is an old understanding, present in folk traditions and in Freudian dream interpretation: the manifest content of a dream (what it seems to be about) is the reversed or disguised form of its latent content (what it is actually about). Young gives Mr. Spitzer this knowledge and lets him apply it practically: he consciously allows for reversals when following the dream directions to the tailor's shop, accounting for the logic of the opposite, navigating by a map that he knows to be a mirror image of the territory.

And still he cannot find it. Because the tailor's shop is not in the dream's logic or in its reversal. It is in neither city and neither street. It is the shop that should exist but does not: the place where the unpayable debt was contracted, which has no address because its address is wherever Mr. Spitzer is. The debt travels with him. The bill arrives wherever he lives. The shop cannot be found because it is not a place.

The library and its inhabitants

Young's library reading room is one of the novel's most humanely observed spaces: the last open place where anyone can stay as long as they cause no distraction, where the homeless and the lost and the obsessively researching all share the same long tables and the same institutional light. Mr. Spitzer fits here. He is all three: homeless in the sense of having lost the twin house, lost in the sense of having lost his sense of which world he inhabits, obsessively researching the tailor's address.

The Confederate soldier who insists the South won the Civil War is the chapter's clearest figure of the alternative history that becomes necessary when the actual history is unbearable. He is living in a world where the outcome was different. His flags, his certainty, his reconstruction of events; these are not madness exactly. They are the logical endpoint of refusing to accept a historical fact. If you refuse the fact long enough and thoroughly enough, you need to build a replacement. The replacement is internally consistent. It just does not correspond to what happened.

Mr. Spitzer, listening, recognizes the man. Not because he agrees with him about the Civil War but because he understands the impulse: the construction of an alternative to the unbearable fact. The unbearable fact for Mr. Spitzer is Peron's death. He has not built an alternative history (he is too rigorous for that, too committed to the truth even when the truth fails him) but he understands the need that generates one. The Confederate soldier and the executor of Peron's estate are the same species of mourner. One built a new history. The other keeps paying a bill that cannot be paid.

The rose named after both brothers

Near the chapter's close, Mr. Spitzer imagines a rose: red and white together, named after himself and Peron, the two brothers unified in a single flower. The rose, in his imagining, changes from red to white. Peron's color bleeding into Joachim's. The reckless twin becoming the cautious one, or the cautious one absorbing the reckless one, or the two finally becoming inseparable in the form that horticulture occasionally achieves and that grief sometimes produces in the living who remain.

A rose named after both brothers would be a memorial. It would be the thing that persists when the persons have not: the named variety that growers catalog, that appears in gardens, that is neither fully red nor fully white but something that contains both. Young gives Mr. Spitzer this vision at twilight, in the sky full of skull-shaped clouds, as the practical search for the tailor's address has definitively failed. He cannot find the shop. He can imagine the rose. Both things are true, and the rose is the more useful of the two.

The penny with heads on both sides

Mr. Spitzer carries a penny with heads on both sides: a gambler's coin, a cheat's coin, a coin that cannot lose because it has no tails. He carries this talisman of certainty in an uncertain world: a coin that always comes up heads, that refuses the possibility of loss built into every genuine coin.

This is Joachim carrying Peron's logic. The two-headed penny is the gambler's tool, not the cautious lawyer's. It belongs to the world of the speakeasies and the racetracks and the 110-round boxing matches that Joachim has absorbed from his dead brother. He carries it as a charm against the chance that keeps arriving anyway: the chance that the tailor's bill brings in the mail, that the twin house's loss brought, that Peron's apparent suicide brought. The two-headed penny cannot prevent these things. It simply refuses, in its small stubborn way, to have a losing side.

What to hold onto

Chapter 57 is a chapter of futile searching and unexpected company. The tailor's shop does not exist. The Confederate soldier has built a better country in his mind. The debate about the soul's existence continues in the reading room without Mr. Spitzer's contribution, because he is afraid they will not want it.

What to carry forward: the bill that keeps arriving as grief's specific texture, the way it comes back with the same address, the same claim, the same demand for settlement that cannot be met. The dream logic that operates by opposites, which Mr. Spitzer knows and accounts for and which still does not lead him to the shop. The rose named after both brothers, changing from red to white in the twilight sky. And the two-headed penny: the inherited gambler's charm in the cautious lawyer's pocket, heads on both sides, refusing to lose.
