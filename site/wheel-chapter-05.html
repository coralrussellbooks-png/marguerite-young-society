Chapter 5

On Chapter 5 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of The Wheel and the Spokes.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 16, 2026

← Chapter 4 · Wheel and Spokes Home · Chapter 6 →

Research notes: Chapter 5 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 5

Characters: Vera Cartwheel · Moses Hunnecker · Miss Pebbles

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The bus-driver is irreducibly multiple. He is simultaneously:

  • The driver of an actual bus through flooding roads
  • A man lying in a four-posted bed at the old hotel
  • In his mother's mind, a baby still crying in her arms
  • Penelope, weaving rather than wandering
  • A potential Arctic whaler
  • A man the doctor might tell he had "died in his sleep two or three years ago"

The text does not resolve these into one. It insists on their simultaneity: perhaps he only thinks he is driving, while all the time he is at home in his four-postered bed and "he was just turning and tossing upon his feather mattress". Vera never adjudicates. The self here is not fragmented: fragmentation implies an original wholeness that broke. This self was always already plural, always already irresolvable.

The chapter names this multiplicity from inside the bus-driver's own thoughts: he is "only thinking he was the bus-driver", not that he is confused about who he is, but that identity is itself a provisional act of thinking, subject to revision, never settled.

Near the chapter's end he gives his name, as if to settle the question: "You know me, Moses Hunnecker, the bus-driver, my long hair streaming like a curtain over my eyes". Even the name arrives in the middle of an argument with a doctor who is not there.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter is architecturally committed to the undecidability of reality. The bus-driver insists the old doctor's patients are illusions, "there are none of these pregnant women you think there are", while a pregnant woman rides on the bus itself. The bus may or may not be moving:

"Perhaps he only thought he was the bus-driver with his rolling stomach, steering his old bus through the roaring floods while all the time he was at home in his four-postered bed in the bridal suite that was furnished with tables and chairs"

But crucially, the text does not offer the bed as the real answer and the bus as the illusion. Both are held with equal ontological weight. The doctor's "imaginary birth" and the bus-driver's four-postered bed are not contrasted with a stable reality; they are the only reality available. The bus-driver sometimes thinks that perhaps "the doctor was right, just because he was crazy, and it was the bus-driver who was always wrong".

The cave metaphor from the pillar is literalized here: the bus moves through permanent darkness, rain, and mist, past "vanished orchards" and "places which were no more but only in his imagination". There is no sunlit destination. There is only the larger cave of the journey itself, "more carefully lit" by the bus-driver's burning hair and the occasional "far light twinkled like a tired star".

The landmarks that might anchor a shared reality are gone: the sign-posts, "the Burma-Shave signs, the broken rail fences, the gate-posts". He drives on "with only his memory of the polar star, the sheep shed, the next turn". And he turns the charge of delusion on the doctor, who "was drunk in his imagination, imagining he was drunk on ether and starlight", though the bus-driver may himself be in his four-postered bed. The illusion-making is recursive. There is no outside from which to judge it.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The chapter's weather is not metaphorical. It is ontological. The rain does not stop:

"though now the rain seemed only to hover, pebbling the grey sky as with pebbles in a brook"

And later:

"'It ain't going to rain no more, no more,' the bus-driver sang in the midst of the driving rain, now pouring like a deluge."

This is grief as permanent weather made explicit: the song denying rain is sung inside the rain. The denial is not delusion; it is the human gesture of continuing to speak inside the grief that never resolves.

The losses accumulate without resolution: Moses Hunnecker's mother, who thinks her son is still a baby, the old doctor's patients who are dead and cannot pay, the "vanished orchards", the "filling station that was always closed", the barber shop covered in spiders' webs. Nothing is recovered. Nothing is mourned and then set aside. The text holds every loss simultaneously, layered, present.

Even the undertaker's trade has turned. He once did a side business in furniture for honeymooning couples, "but now things had gotten so bad, even he was going to seed, and there were no calls for tables or chairs, no calls but for the beds", the beds deep under the earth. And in the old hotel, deaf Miss Pebbles complains of being kept awake by things that are not there, among them "an old watch dog, he who had been shot to put him out of his misery, barking at a shadow". Death does not end the barking.

The bus-driver's political grief, his refusal to cut his hair until Roosevelt leaves office, is formally identical to every other grief in the chapter: it does not move toward resolution. It accumulates as hair accumulates, as snow accumulates on branches, as the dead accumulate beneath the living.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Vera occupies the position of pure witness in this chapter. She asks "How much longer?" and "Why don't you cut your hair?": minimal interventions that function not as attempts to change anything but as invitations for the monologue to continue. Vera does not judge, does not conclude, does not flinch from the bus-driver's drunkenness, his political rants, his terror, his grief.

She asks even though the sign above his head "urged that no passenger engage in conversation with the bus-driver whose eyes were always on the road". The rule is to watch and not interfere. Vera breaks it only twice, and only in ways that let him keep talking.

This is the ethics of witness enacted structurally. The prose itself refuses to simplify. When the bus-driver describes people buried alive:

"Sometimes only their hands reached out from their graves, their hands holding great clumps of human hair which they had torn out of their heads"

Vera does not soften this. Does not contextualize it as mere drunken rambling. Does not reassure the reader. The attention stays fully in the room with the image.

The witness position is also morally costly here: staying in the room with this bus-driver means staying with his misogyny, his political rage, his scorn for the pregnant women of the doctor's practice, his aggression toward a doctor who is not even there. The text does not resolve these into acceptable form. The witness holds them as they are.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The chapter's formal structure is recursive construction. The bus-driver circles back repeatedly:

  • To his hair
  • To the doctor
  • To his mother
  • To the Democrats
  • To the same broken landmarks: "the filling station that was always closed, the same old barber poles in the country of the bald-headed men"

Each return brings slightly different freight. The hair that begins as political statement becomes angel, becomes dead mother, becomes Penelope's web, becomes the thing that will keep growing in the grave. The building is never finished; each return adds a layer without resolving the previous ones.

The bus itself enacts recursive construction:

"we must detour a thousand miles, it seemed, to advance one, and we must always go back"

This is not failure of navigation. It is the structural condition of the journey. You build anyway. You return with what you learned. The detour is the road.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter's sentences formally embody incompletion. They accumulate clauses, pile image upon image, refuse to conclude:

"his hair drifting always around him, soft as a cloud, as a feather mattress and a soft pillow, as a bride in a bridal chamber, and his hair would not molt as would this mortal hair, and his hair would grow when nothing else grew"

The "and...and...and" construction is not stylistic excess; it is the honest form for a reality that does not conclude. To end the sentence cleanly would be to lie about what the sentence is describing.

The bus-driver's political oath is also formally incomplete; he swore not to cut his hair until a condition is met that cannot be met from within the journey. The oath has no completion point available. He knows this. He keeps the oath anyway. This is the ethics of incompletion: "keeping also even those promises he had not made".

Completion, for the bus-driver, means death. He tells the barber: "When you cut my hair, I said to him, I will be dead and not the man I was". And the old doctor's way of thinking, as the bus-driver reports it, makes the same point from the other side: "you died every minute that you lived, got a new skin every seven years, and you were born every minute that you died". To be alive is to be unfinished.

The unborn passenger, "one as yet unborn", is the figure of structural incompletion itself: present, counted, real, but not yet arrived.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Every figure in this chapter exists at the edge of categories:

  • The bus-driver: alive or dead? sleeping or driving? sane or mad?
  • The old doctor: practicing or not? his patients real or imaginary?
  • The pregnant woman on the bus: "everything about her artificial, everything but the one thing that mattered"
  • The unborn passenger: present but not yet born
  • Moses Hunnecker's mother: dreaming in the bridal chamber that she is young, with her baby in her arms

The bus-driver fears the doctor will tell him exactly this: that he "had died in his sleep two or three years ago and now only thought he was driving this bus". He is the marginal person in the most literal sense, on the margin between living and dead, and it is precisely this marginal position that makes the constructed nature of both categories visible.

The barber (bald, sharpening his razor on pumice stone) is the figure who enforces categorical norms (cut hair = normal man) and is himself the most extreme case of what he would impose. The marginal person reveals the arbitrariness of the center.

He answers the barber with his own theology of the margin: "God forgot to create some of us, and some of us created ourselves". The marginal person is not the exception to creation; he is evidence that creation is unfinished and partly self-made. The chapter fills its edges with such figures: old Miss Pebbles, "deaf as a stone in the midst of a rippling brook, turning her broken earphones off and on", and the old doctor in his long white nightgown, practicing in an empty office.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This pillar is perhaps most richly developed in the chapter. In the pillar's own words, the past does not recede; it accumulates beneath us, layer on layer. Here it presses upward.

The text gives us this literally in the image of the buried-alive:

"Sometimes, their graves opening in the spring floods and rains, they were washed up, years afterward, after the first funeral and the preacher's sermon, and then it could be seen that they who had been buried alive had torn out their hair, for their hair was in their hands, and their heads were bald"

The past erupts through the ground in the spring floods. The dead return not as ghosts but as geological evidence: their torn hair in their hands, their bald heads testifying to what happened beneath the surface.

The bus-driver's hair is itself sedimentary time; it has gone uncut since the oath he swore against the Democrats two or three years ago, accumulating political history, personal history, maternal history on his body. It is "already longer than it seemed": the past is always deeper than it appears.

The hair keeps time: "His hair had grown three inches since he left Persia at sunset", and it would grow three inches more before dawn. And in the old hotel his mother dreams she is young, with her baby in her arms and her young husband at her side, the sleepers separated only by "the thin partition between soul and soul". The past is not behind. It is in the next room, dreaming.

The road itself is sedimentary: the bus passes "the places which were no more but only in his imagination, of course, the cherry trees bending and breaking under their weight of snow". The vanished orchards are still there, pressing up through the present landscape. The bus-driver navigates by them even as he acknowledges they are gone.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The bus-driver's language is aggressively self-locating. He speaks from a specific position (Republican, his mother's son, drunken, long-haired, driving) and he knows it:

"I'm no Democrat but just the same old bus-driver I always was, my mother's son"

His political rants are not digressions from the philosophical content; they are the philosophical content. Language that pretends to speak from nowhere is exposed here by a speaker who cannot stop announcing where he speaks from. His location is his obsession.

The doctor's world is equally constructed but from a different position: the tools of medical authority (stethoscope, forceps, prescriptions) in an empty office, for patients who may not exist. His instruments construct a practice. The bus-driver's language constructs a self.

When he threatens to call the police, he admits he has no telephone, and then asks the chapter's plainest question about language itself: "What's a telephone? What's communication?"

The text's own language is self-conscious about its constructedness: the similes pile up and contradict each other ("soft as a cloud, as a feather mattress and a soft pillow, as a bride in a bridal chamber") in a way that draws attention to the act of comparison itself. Language here is not finding the right metaphor. It is accumulating metaphors until the act of metaphor-making becomes visible.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

This pillar is the chapter's spiritual center. The sacred is located precisely in the grey bus, the drunken driver, the flooding roads:

"the elegiac music of the spheres or with those heavenly harmonies no mortal ears could hear, the angels accompanying those spheres down the accustomed road, the great dolphins uplifting the stone boats of the dead upon the turbulent waves"

This music goes on in Vera's own heart, and the bus-driver, as Vera tells it, is not in tune with it, "for he was only, somewhat erratic, this wild-eyed bus-driver, driving his stuttering and wheezing bus through the roaring spring floods". Yet he opens the chapter singing "Oh, the old grey mare, she ain't what she used to was", and the heavenly harmonies and the drunken song travel the same road in the same bus. The angels and dolphins and music of the spheres are not elsewhere. They are in the bus.

The bus-driver's hair is his angel. His dead mother lives in him. The sacred is not above the grey miles; it is in the grey miles, in the permanent rain, in the drunken singing, in the burning eyes in the matted hair.

The chapter's most explicit statement of immanent transcendence comes in the bus-driver's prayer-like address:

"Giddy up, giddy up, little churchyard owls, little churchyard mice. Giddy up, little rabbits, little stars, for we are coming home"

He has just called them "little cherub dogs", and he ends, "and Papa is very drunk." The cherubs are addressed as if they are horses. The sacred is harnessed to the mundane. This is not blasphemy. It is the correct theological position of the chapter: the sacred lives here, in the harness, in the drunk voice, in the coming home.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The bus-driver is explicitly constituted by what was deposited before he arrived and is constituting the ground for what comes after. His mother deposited his hair: "His hair, of course, had come from the distaff side". The old doctor brought him into the world: "You brought me into this world, and now would you take me out of it?". He carries the promises he made before he could understand them: "those promises he had made when he was too young to know what the promises were".

And he is constituting the ground for the unborn passenger: the "one as yet unborn" who is already being carried, already counted among the four passengers, already present in the bus that may or may not be moving.

In his bed-dream the four passengers become "four angels at four bed-posts, Matthew, Mark, Luke, John": the four who carry the story forward. He did not choose the burden: "He was not responsible. He had not created the world nor the distance between one place and another". He carries everyone across it anyway.

The crossing point is literalized in the rear-view mirror:

"I can see what's coming from before, behind, having this rear mirror view"

This is the structural position of the crossing point exactly: you see what is coming by looking at what is behind. You are where time crosses itself. The bus-driver sees forward through the windshield and backward through the mirror simultaneously, not as a metaphor for time, but as the literal form of his navigation.

The chapter closes with the sky "suddenly reddened as if with a sudden but hallucinatory dawn": a dawn that is immediately "fading", that illuminates bare branches and distant hills before disappearing. This is the crossing point as image: the moment of illumination that is already passing, that shows us the sediment and the structure and the distance, and then withdraws, leaving us still on the road, still in the bus, still carrying the passengers, the living and the unborn alike. Moses Hunnecker drives on.
