Research notes: Chapter 5 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 5

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The bus-driver is irreducibly multiple. He is simultaneously:

  • The driver of an actual bus through flooding roads
  • A man lying in a four-posted bed at the old hotel
  • His mother's baby, still crying in her arms
  • Penelope, weaving rather than wandering
  • A potential Arctic whaler
  • A corpse who "died in his sleep two or three years ago"

The text does not resolve these into one. It insists on their simultaneity: "perhaps he only thought he was the bus-driver...and he was just turning and tossing upon his feather mattress". The narrator never adjudicates. The self here is not fragmented: fragmentation implies an original wholeness that broke. This self was always already plural, always already irresolvable.

The bus-driver himself names this multiplicity when he says he is "only thinking he was the bus-driver", not that he is confused about who he is, but that identity is itself a provisional act of thinking, subject to revision, never settled.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter is architecturally committed to the undecidability of reality. The old doctor's patients may or may not exist. The pregnant woman on the bus may or may not be real. The bus itself may or may not be moving:

"perhaps he only thought he was the bus-driver...steering his old bus through the roaring floods while all the time he was at home in his four-postered bed"

But crucially, the text does not offer the bed as the real answer and the bus as the illusion. Both are held with equal ontological weight. The doctor's "imaginary birth" and the bus-driver's "crazy dreams" are not contrasted with a stable reality; they are the only reality available.

The cave metaphor from the pillar is literalized here: the bus moves through permanent darkness, rain, and mist, past "vanished orchards" and "places which were no more but only in his imagination". There is no sunlit destination. There is only the larger cave of the journey itself, "more carefully lit" by the bus-driver's burning hair and the occasional "far light [that] twinkled like a tired star".

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The chapter's weather is not metaphorical. It is ontological. The rain does not stop:

"though now the rain seemed only to hover, pebbling the grey sky as with pebbles in a brook"

And later:

"'It ain't going to rain no more, no more,' the bus-driver sang in the midst of the driving rain, now pouring like a deluge."

This is grief as permanent weather made explicit: the song denying rain is sung inside the rain. The denial is not delusion; it is the human gesture of continuing to speak inside the grief that never resolves.

The losses accumulate without resolution: the mother who thinks her son is still a baby, the old doctor's patients who are dead and cannot pay, the "vanished orchards", the "filling station that was always closed", the barber shop covered in spiders' webs. Nothing is recovered. Nothing is mourned and then set aside. The text holds every loss simultaneously, layered, present.

The bus-driver's political grief, his refusal to cut his hair until Roosevelt leaves office, is formally identical to every other grief in the chapter: it does not move toward resolution. It accumulates as hair accumulates, as snow accumulates on branches, as the dead accumulate beneath the living.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The narrator of this chapter occupies the position of pure witness. They ask "How much longer?" and "Why don't you cut your hair?": minimal interventions that function not as attempts to change anything but as invitations for the monologue to continue. The narrator does not judge, does not conclude, does not flinch from the bus-driver's drunkenness, his political rants, his terror, his grief.

This is the ethics of witness enacted structurally. The prose itself refuses to simplify. When the bus-driver describes people buried alive:

"Sometimes only their hands reached out from their graves, their hands holding great clumps of human hair which they had torn out of their heads"

The narrator does not soften this. Does not contextualize it as mere drunken rambling. Does not reassure the reader. The attention stays fully in the room with the image.

The witness position is also morally costly here: staying in the room with this bus-driver means staying with his misogyny, his political rage, his contempt for the pregnant woman, his aggression toward the doctor. The text does not resolve these into acceptable form. The witness holds them as they are.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The chapter's formal structure is recursive construction. The bus-driver circles back repeatedly:

  • To his hair
  • To the doctor
  • To his mother
  • To the Democrats
  • To the same broken landmarks: "the same rail fences broken down and horse

fountains...the filling station that was always closed, the same old barber poles"

Each return brings slightly different freight. The hair that begins as political statement becomes angel, becomes dead mother, becomes Penelope's web, becomes the thing that will keep growing in the grave. The building is never finished; each return adds a layer without resolving the previous ones.

The bus itself enacts recursive construction:

"we must detour a thousand miles, it seemed, to advance one, and we must always go back"

This is not failure of navigation. It is the structural condition of the journey. You build anyway. You return with what you learned. The detour is the road.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter's sentences formally embody incompletion. They accumulate clauses, pile image upon image, refuse to conclude:

"his hair drifting always around him, soft as a cloud, as a feather mattress and a soft pillow, as a bride in a bridal chamber, and his hair would not molt as would this mortal hair, and his hair would grow when nothing else grew"

The "and...and...and" construction is not stylistic excess; it is the honest form for a reality that does not conclude. To end the sentence cleanly would be to lie about what the sentence is describing.

The bus-driver's political oath is also formally incomplete; he swore not to cut his hair until a condition is met that cannot be met from within the journey. The oath has no completion point available. He knows this. He keeps the oath anyway. This is the ethics of incompletion: "keeping also even those promises he had not made".

The unborn passenger, "one as yet unborn", is the figure of structural incompletion itself: present, counted, real, but not yet arrived.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Every figure in this chapter exists at the edge of categories:

  • The bus-driver: alive or dead? sleeping or driving? sane or mad?
  • The old doctor: practicing or not? his patients real or imaginary?
  • The pregnant woman: her condition real, "everything about her artificial,

everything but the one thing that mattered"

  • The unborn passenger: present but not yet born
  • The mother: dreaming she is young while her son drives a bus

The bus-driver names this explicitly regarding himself: "had died in his sleep two or three years ago and now only thought he was driving this bus". He is the marginal person in the most literal sense, on the margin between living and dead, and it is precisely this marginal position that makes the constructed nature of both categories visible.

The barber (bald, sharpening his razor on pumice stone) is the figure who enforces categorical norms (cut hair = normal man) and is himself the most extreme case of what he would impose. The marginal person reveals the arbitrariness of the center.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This pillar is perhaps most richly developed in the chapter. The past does not recede; it presses upward:

"The past does not recede. It accumulates beneath us, layer on layer."

The text gives us this literally in the image of the buried-alive:

"Sometimes, their graves opening in the spring floods and rains, they were washed up, years afterward...and then it could be seen that they who had been buried alive had torn out their hair"

The past erupts through the ground in the spring floods. The dead return not as ghosts but as geological evidence: their torn hair in their hands, their bald heads testifying to what happened beneath the surface.

The bus-driver's hair is itself sedimentary time; it has been growing since before the Democrats took power, accumulating political history, personal history, maternal history on his body. It is "already longer than it seemed": the past is always deeper than it appears.

The road itself is sedimentary: the bus passes "the places which were no more but only in his imagination, of course, the cherry trees bending and breaking under their weight of snow". The vanished orchards are still there, pressing up through the present landscape. The bus-driver navigates by them even as he acknowledges they are gone.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The bus-driver's language is aggressively self-locating. He speaks from a specific position (Republican, motherless, drunken, long-haired, driving) and he knows it:

"I'm no Democrat but just the same old bus-driver I always was, my mother's son"

His political rants are not digressions from the philosophical content; they are the philosophical content. Language that pretends to speak from nowhere is exposed here by a speaker who cannot stop announcing where he speaks from. His location is his obsession.

The doctor's language is equally constructed but from a different position; he speaks the language of medical authority (stethoscope, forceps, prescriptions) from an empty office, to patients who may not exist. His language constructs a practice. The bus-driver's language constructs a self.

The text's own language is self-conscious about its constructedness: the similes pile up and contradict each other ("soft as a cloud, as a feather mattress and a soft pillow, as a bride in a bridal chamber") in a way that draws attention to the act of comparison itself. Language here is not finding the right metaphor. It is accumulating metaphors until the act of metaphor-making becomes visible.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

This pillar is the chapter's spiritual center. The sacred is located precisely in the grey bus, the drunken driver, the flooding roads:

"the elegiac music of the spheres or with those heavenly harmonies no mortal ears could hear, the angels accompanying those spheres down the accustomed road, the great dolphins uplifting the stone boats of the dead upon the turbulent waves"

But this transcendence is immediately grounded: "for he was only, somewhat erratic, this wild-eyed bus-driver, driving his stuttering and wheezing bus through the roaring spring floods". The angels and dolphins and music of the spheres are not elsewhere. They are in the bus. They are the bus.

The bus-driver's hair is his angel. His dead mother lives in him. The sacred is not above the grey miles; it is in the grey miles, in the permanent rain, in the drunken singing, in the burning eyes in the matted hair.

The chapter's most explicit statement of immanent transcendence comes in the bus-driver's prayer-like address:

"Giddy up, giddy up, little cherub dogs...Giddy up, little rabbits, little stars, for we are coming home...and Papa is very drunk."

The cherubs are addressed as if they are horses. The sacred is harnessed to the mundane. This is not blasphemy. It is the correct theological position of the chapter: the sacred lives here, in the harness, in the drunk voice, in the coming home.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The bus-driver is explicitly constituted by what was deposited before he arrived and is constituting the ground for what comes after. His mother deposited his hair: "His hair, of course, had come from the distaff side". The dead doctor brought him into the world: "You brought me into this world, and now would you take me out of it?". He carries the promises he made before he could understand them: "those promises he had made when he was too young to know what the promises were".

And he is constituting the ground for the unborn passenger: the "one as yet unborn" who is already being carried, already counted among the four passengers, already present in the bus that may or may not be moving.

The crossing point is literalized in the rear-view mirror:

"I can see what's coming from before, behind, having this rear mirror view"

This is the structural position of the crossing point exactly: you see what is coming by looking at what is behind. You are where time crosses itself. The bus-driver sees forward through the windshield and backward through the mirror simultaneously, not as a metaphor for time, but as the literal form of his navigation.

The chapter closes with the sky "suddenly reddened as if with a sudden but hallucinatory dawn": a dawn that is immediately "fading", that illuminates bare branches and distant hills before disappearing. This is the crossing point as image: the moment of illumination that is already passing, that shows us the sediment and the structure and the distance, and then withdraws, leaving us still on the road, still in the bus, still carrying the passengers, the living and the unborn alike.
