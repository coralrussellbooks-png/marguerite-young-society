Philosophy: Hinduism

The novel does not argue Hindu thought either, but it leans the same way. Its sense that all beings share one essence, and that the separate self is a passing illusion, echoes the Hindu idea of Brahman, the single reality beneath every apparent thing, and its glancing talk of rebirth touches the same tradition.

The fuller count shows Young drew on Hindu scripture more closely than that, in her own wording and without names. Chapter 41 says of Catherine, "She was swifter than her own mind, swifter than her mercurial thoughts. She was far, yet she was near. She was within, yet she was outside." The Isha Upanishad says of the One that it "is swifter than the mind" and "is far and also It is near. It is within and also It is without all this" (4-5, trans. Swami Paramananda, 1919). Chapter 50 has "the door of truth which was covered with a golden disc and was never opened by man," the Upanishad's own prayer: "The door of the True is covered with a golden disk" (15, trans. F. Max Müller, 1879). Chapter 41 also has "an action in inaction," the Bhagavad Gita's wise man who "sees inaction in action, and action in inaction" (4.18), and the tree "rooted in clouds with its branches growing downward" (Chapter 52) is the Gita's eternal fig, roots above and branches below (15.1). The novel names "Sanskrit from a holy text printed on a snowflake" (Chapter 40), and its third eye (Chapters 14, 43, 51) comes from the same tradition.
