Angel in the Forest

Chapter 9. The Body the Utopia Cannot Hold

← Chapter 8 · Angel in the Forest: A Reading in Fourteen Chapters · Chapter 10 →

Two communities tried to build heaven on the same bend of the Wabash, one after the other, and both of them failed. The reasons usually given are organizational. Rapp’s people were held together by one old man’s will and could not survive without it. Owen’s people were held together by an idea, and the idea could not survive contact with actual farming. Both explanations are true. But Marguerite Young keeps her eye on something the organizational account leaves out, and she keeps it there with a patience that starts to feel like a thesis. Both utopias failed, in part, because neither one could find room for the bodies of the people living inside it.

This is easy to miss, because Young never states it as an argument. She states it as weather, as illness, as a list of what people ate and what killed them. But the accumulation is the argument. A community is a theory about human nature made concrete, and if the theory has no place for the body, the body does not go away. It goes on getting sick, getting old, wanting things, and dying. The utopia can refuse to speak about this. It cannot make it stop.

The Rappite body

Father Rapp’s Harmony ran on celibacy, and Young looks at what that costs without ever reaching for outrage. This is one of the harder things she does. It would be easy to write the Rappites off as monsters, or as fools. She does neither. She holds two facts together at once: this was a community of real spiritual intensity and staggering productivity, and it was killing its members at the level of the cell.

The productivity is not incidental to the celibacy. It flows from it. Young repeats Byron’s sly note that before the total edict, Rappite babies had arrived “never at harvest,” the implication being that harmony “was a form of economy.” Reproduction is inefficient. It distracts. It produces individuals, and individuals have wills, and a will is exactly the thing a perfectly harmonious economy cannot afford. So the community switches off reproduction the way you would switch off an expensive machine. The producer, Young writes, is forced to “lop off his own branch of the tree of life” so the corporation’s books can show a steady increase in earnings. That is the logic in one line. The body is a cost. Cut it.

The images pile up, and they are all bodily. The mills and furnaces of Harmony, “the power of which was derived from a treadmill operated by castrated dogs.” A town laid out with a cocoonery and a brewery and a hattery and, Young notes, “everything but a maternity ward.” The whole reproductive drive of the settlement redirected into labor, the dogs on the wheel a small exact figure for what has been done to everyone.

And then, when the body does assert itself, the story turns terrible. John Rapp, Father Rapp’s own son, could not keep the vow. His wife began to “swell out like the yeast of bread,” and there was no hiding it. Young describes John’s happiness with real tenderness. He had seen a droplet of milk on his wife’s breast, he whistled like a mockingbird, he was, she says, “the poor fool.” The community read that happiness as a crime against everyone who had stayed temperate. Father Rapp decided the offender must be punished. The punishment was not a flogging. It was emasculation, and the victim died “crying like a stuck pig,” somewhere near the piggery. The reason Young gives, in the community’s own logic, is the coldest sentence in the section: “Community must be preserved, at any cost to the finite destiny of finite man.”

This is what it looks like when a theory of human nature meets a human body and decides the body is the thing that has to give. The infant daughter survived, Young tells us, and grew up with a twinkle in her eye. The body wins in the long run. It always does. But the cost of the theory was a man’s life, and Young makes you sit with the fact that the community that killed him was, by every economic measure, a success.

The Owenite body

The Owenites were the opposite of the Rappites in almost every way, and their failure has the same shape. Where Rapp had one will, Owen had a program: a science of society, rational and generous and confident. What the program had no vocabulary for was the ground it stood on. New Harmony sat on broken river bottoms, and the breaking up of those bottoms released the germs of malaria, and people died. Babies died of whooping cough. The streets were mud. The dancers, walking home from the balls that filled almost every night, “were soon black from head to foot,” which is a lovely small joke and also a plain fact about mud and about bodies that have to walk through it.

Young gives the Owenite body a face, and it belongs to Mrs. Pears, who is miserable and correct about being miserable. Her eyes had gone bad from sewing, so bad that “she could hardly see to lift her hand before her face.” She missed Pittsburgh. She watched a neighbor lose a child, and watched the babies dying, and understood in her plain, unphilosophical way that the rational program had nothing to offer any of it. Owen’s system was addressed entirely to the mind. It had elaborate views on education and property and the formation of character. It had no answer for a woman going blind with a needle in her hand, or a child drowning in its own cough. The body was simply outside the frame. It was left to rot in the bottomlands while the committee debated the future of the human race.

The man in the storm

The clearest single image of the problem is old Greenwood, who Young says would have been “more at home among the Rappite candidates for heaven than among the Owenites,” and who does something during the second summer that nobody around him can explain. In a green-lit thunderstorm, with trees falling and lightning scrawling the sky, Robert Dale looks out the window and sees an old man walking the flooded streets, calm, going nowhere, carrying “a slender iron rod, ten or twelve feet long,” upright like a musket. Where was the war?

There was no war. Greenwood was old and in pain and he wanted to die, and when Robert Dale asks him afterward why he did not come in out of the rain, he says he thought it might be a chance to slip off and be laid quietly in the corner of the orchard. He did not want the responsibility of destroying himself, so he had prayed that God, who had struck the Rappite house and the young colt and the trees, might strike him too. If God had only seen fit to do it, he says, he would have been at rest that very minute, all his pains gone, no more burden to anyone or to himself.

This is the tenth pillar’s opposite and the ninth pillar’s proof. Greenwood is carrying a bodily truth. I am old, I hurt, I am ready to be done. And the community he lives in has no legitimate place to put it. New Harmony is a rational utopia. It does not have a language for a man who simply wants to stop. So the truth has to leave his body sideways, through an iron rod in a lightning storm, dressed up as an accident that God might arrange. When a community has no noun for what you are living through, you do not stop living through it. You are just erased from the official account of what is happening, and you have to smuggle your own experience out under cover of weather. Greenwood in the storm is a philosophy of language compressed into one drenched old man.

Contraband: the sacred body

And then, buried inside the Rappite section, the most surprising thing in the book. Frederick Rapp, the adopted son, was a pantheist inside a community built on denying nature. He believed, Young writes, that God is everywhere, inhering in “the feathery catkins of the willow, the pyramids of the horse-chestnut.” The whole physical universe was the body of God, and God was “a huge animal without eyes,” because there is nothing outside God for God to look on, “not even one small, wet violet.”

That theology does not point up toward heaven, or out toward some transcendent truth. It points down and in, into the actual willow, the actual violet. The divine is not the opposite of the physical world. It is the physical world, all of it, seen. This is the sacred exactly as Radical Immanence describes it: not a signpost to something beyond the ordinary, but the ordinary itself given its full weight. The bus, the cup of tea, the wet violet. Frederick found the tenth pillar a century and a half early, and he found it in the last place you would look, in the heart of a community whose entire economy ran on the conviction that the body is a cost and the physical world a snare.

That is why it reads like contraband. Frederick’s eyeless God is everything Father Rapp’s Harmony refused. Harmony said the body was the obstacle to the spirit and cut it away, castrated the dogs and the son alike. Frederick said the body of the world was where God actually lived. He kept carving his golden rose and playing his flute in the middle of a machine designed to prove him wrong, and Young lets his heresy stand as the truest thing anyone in the Rappite chapters believes.

What Young shows

There is a long tradition of arguing that utopias fail because they suppress the body, and it would be easy to file Young under it and move on. Plenty of critics have made the general case that any plan for a perfected society ends up at war with appetite, with sex, with the stubborn animal facts of the people it means to perfect. Young knows this. But she is not making the general case. She is doing something harder and more useful. She is showing you the specific bodies. John Rapp near the piggery. Mrs. Pears blind at her sewing. Hectorina Grant’s dead child. The dancers black to the knee. Greenwood in the rain with his rod. She does not argue that the body defeats the utopia. She just keeps the body in the frame until you cannot unsee it, and then the defeat is obvious.

Both communities had a theory of what a person is. Rapp’s person was a will to be dissolved into the general harmony. Owen’s person was a mind to be correctly educated. Neither theory had a place for the thing that gets malaria, wants its wife, goes blind sewing, grows old and hurts and wants to be laid in the orchard. And the thing without a place in the theory was the thing that was actually there, in the mud, the whole time.

Radical Immanence starts where both utopias refused to start. It says the body is not the obstacle to the sacred but the location of it. It says a philosophy that cannot describe an old man’s readiness to die is not more spiritual than one that can, only more dishonest. The people at New Harmony were not failing at utopia when they got sick and grieved and wanted things. That was the truth the utopia was built to deny. Young’s method is to refuse the denial, to stay in the room with the body until the body has had its say. The wet violet outlasts the harmony. The daughter with the twinkle in her eye grows up. The sentence does not end where the theory wanted it to.
