Chapter 62

On Chapter 62 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Woman in Young's Magnum Opus.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Characters: Joachim Spitzer

Mr. Spitzer writes his own will in this chapter, and the music that goes unheard is what he wills away. The dream opens it, rivers of fire and ice, old seals in "diamond necklaces with diamond crosses," seagulls turning into musicians with human faces, and the transforming dreamworld is real and dream as one country, the sea a place where instruments and animals and people trade shapes. Then the will: he leaves his compositions "to the musical world and even to indifferent ears and even to deaf ears," and the bequest to the deaf is the music that goes unheard handed forward on purpose, a legacy addressed to the very absence that will not receive it.

A pack rat steals and exchanges the objects and the manuscripts, and the theft is the book's erasure working on the record itself, women's unacknowledged and appropriated labor carried by a small thief in the walls. The music cannot be realized, thwarted by "atmospheric and environmental factors," and its titles name what will never sound, a "Pavanne for the Dead Polar Bear and the Entombed Butterfly," the unplayed piece mourning a loss it cannot perform. Its "music of the dead," its inscrutable codes and "counter-worlds," are the non-dominant expression the book keeps crediting, the composition that exists "apart from transitory sound" and defies the categories that would let it be heard.

The chapter's players are all broken in the same way, undone by the very art that defines them. The old horn-blower keeps playing though tuberculosis is killing him, devotion destroying the devotee; the clarinetist takes his own life over the fear of being "out of tune," the impossibility of a perfect self-presentation turned fatal; the old violinist dances and plays with no instrument at all, the music existing only where no one but Mr. Spitzer can find it. The second violinist is the sharpest of them, a man who "never touched the strings," who deceived the audience for years and was dismissed for a single false note, and his invisibility and precariousness are the book's erasure in a concert seat, the player present and unaccounted for until one error erases him entirely.

Mr. Spitzer identifies with "the sad dead millions," a single man carrying many voices, and the identification is the illusion of the individual stated over a will, the self that is not one distributing itself across everyone it mourns. The chapter closes on music as silence, on the beauty of what goes unplayed and unheard, and it leaves the whole apparatus of performance and audience set aside for a sound that needs no ear, the unheard song the book keeps composing for its erased, willed forward here to a world it trusts will never hear it and left, all the same, as the only legacy worth leaving.
