Chapter 8

Characters: Beatrice Fitzgerald · Homer Capehorn · Madge Capehorn · Moses Hunnecker · Vera Cartwheel

Pillars: Reality Is Constructed · The Past Doesn't Go Away

The Questions

The village is called a “conspiracy of illusion.” Who is conspiring, and what is being built?

Beatrice Fitzgerald is an ugly hag to one teller and a blue-blooded worker to another. Which is she, and does the question have an answer?

When Vera feels herself “pillowed upon the oozy tides of time,” what has happened to past and present?

Moses doubts hell, heaven, and the doctor alike. Is that despair, or the refusal of one more sealed authority?

The chapter names the built world plainly: the village is a “conspiracy of illusion.” Not a lie told by a villain but a thing made and held in common, an ordinary settlement revealed as a construction agreed upon and kept up. Reality Is Constructed gets one of its clearest labels here, and the word conspiracy catches the collective repetition that holds any built world in place.

Beatrice Fitzgerald is that construction in a single figure. To Madge she is an “ugly old hag” and a “common prostitute”; to Homer she is a hardworking woman with “blue blood.” Same person, two builds, and the chapter offers no third account to settle them. This is the move the road made with Josh’s death, now turned on a living reputation, and it deepens: a person is not only plural in herself but plural in the mouths that make her.

Time layers heavily here. Vera feels her life pillowed “upon the oozy tides of time,” relives past events, sinks into a present thick with what came before. The graveyard hill, the recurring dead, the decay of the old tin house are The Past Doesn't Go Away as landscape: the past is not behind the travelers but underneath them, soft and rising, and the journey moves through it without leaving it.

Moses doubts everything the settled world offers to steady it, hell, heaven, the doctor, the value of a cure. It sounds like despair, and the surface is bleak. But read from the edge it is also a refusal to seal any of those authorities into a final floor. Moses will not close the construction, even the consoling ones, and his morbid running talk keeps the scene from settling into any fixed account of what death is or means.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that asks which Beatrice is the real one, and the reading that takes the village’s bleakness and Moses’s doubt for the book giving up. Neither the settled fact nor the settled despair is available; both would seal what the chapter keeps open.

The footing is that the ordinary world is a conspiracy of illusion, built and kept by common repetition, and a person is made and remade in the telling. Time is sediment, not sequence; the dead rise into the present underfoot. And doubting every steadying authority is not nihilism when it is the refusal to seal a final floor. The built world is built, and the past is present. Beatrice is never resolved, the journey never arrives, and Moses talks on.

For Reflection

What settled place, seen closely, turns out to be a conspiracy of illusion held up by everyone agreeing to it?

Is there a person one knows chiefly through the incompatible ways others build her?

What does it feel like when the past stops being behind and starts being underneath?
