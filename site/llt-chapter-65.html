Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

The Questions

Miss MacIntosh is now “a storm center of mirages” who, “being nothing in herself,” is “a mirror to the dreams of others.” What is a solid self that turns out to be empty at the center?

She takes on “many moods and many dimensions” Vera “had hardly guessed.” Where was the plurality hiding in the plainest figure?

“Perhaps I believed in her only when she could no longer be believed.” Why does Vera’s faith arrive after the ground is gone?

Is the common-sense rock the fullest thing in the book, or the emptiest?

This chapter cuts deepest into Miss MacIntosh, and the cut is that the rock is hollow. She is now “a storm center of mirages,” and, “being nothing in herself, she might still be a mirror to the dreams of others,” “a mirror held up to a mirror when all images faded.” The figure offered as the one solid thing, the acme of common sense, turns out to be empty at the center, reflecting the dreams around her because she has no dream, no substance, of her own. The sealed blueprint is revealed as the emptiest of the book’s constructions: not a full solid self but a mirror, nothing in itself, giving back only what others bring to it.

Her plurality was hiding inside her plainness all along. She takes on “many moods and many dimensions such as, in the previously well-ordered days… I had hardly guessed,” the plural self surfacing at last in the one figure who denied it hardest. The woman who claimed to be single, solid, never dreaming, is as many as anyone, and the many were there under the surface the whole time, unguessed because the surface was so well-ordered. The book has said the plural self is universal; here it collects the last holdout, the plainest person, and finds her a crowd like the rest, her plainness only the lid that kept the crowd from showing.

Vera’s belief inverts, and the inversion is the chapter’s strangest truth. “Perhaps I believed in her only when she could no longer be believed.” The faith arrives after the ground is gone, the trust deepening exactly as the solid thing dissolves. This is not perversity but a recognition the book keeps circling: what one can truly believe in is not the sealed rock that claims to be bedrock but the open, dissolving, mirrored thing once it has stopped pretending to be solid. Vera could not really believe the rock while it stood as rock; she can believe the mirage once it admits it is a mirage. Belief becomes possible when the false solidity fails.

So the emptiest construction and the truest belief meet in her. Miss MacIntosh is nothing in herself, a mirror, a storm center of mirages, and this hollowness rhymes with the empty shell the book found in a sealed and finished self. But there is a difference the chapter holds: Miss MacIntosh’s hollowness is the sealed blueprint’s, the emptiness of a self that walled out its own dream and reflects only others, while the openness the book trusts is hollow in another way, refusing to cohere rather than walling out. Vera believes her, at last, not as the solid rock but as the honest mirror, the one who can finally be loved because she can finally be seen through.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that keeps Miss MacIntosh the solid center the book rests on, and the reading that takes her hollowness for mere pathos, a woman revealed to be nothing. Neither the solid center nor the empty nothing is quite what the chapter shows.

The footing is that the common-sense rock is empty at the center, a mirror to others’ dreams because it has walled out its own, the sealed blueprint revealed as the emptiest of the book’s constructions; that her plurality was hiding under her plainness the whole time, the last holdout found a crowd like everyone; and that Vera’s belief arrives only when Miss MacIntosh can no longer be believed as a rock, faith becoming possible when the false solidity fails. The sealed self is hollow, the plainest figure is plural, and belief follows the collapse of the pretended bedrock. Vera believes the mirage as mirage, and the chapter lets her love what she can finally see through.

For Reflection

Can a self that presents as the most solid turn out to be the emptiest, a mirror giving back only what others bring?

Where has plurality been hiding under one’s own, or another’s, well-ordered plainness?

Is there something one could only truly believe in once it stopped claiming to be solid?
