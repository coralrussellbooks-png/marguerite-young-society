The Plot

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Homer Capehorn · Joachim Spitzer · Jock Cartwheel · Justice O’Leary · Madge Capehorn · Miss MacIntosh · Moses Hunnecker · Peron Spitzer · The Stone Deaf Man · Vera Cartwheel

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is a dark and haunting novel set against a backdrop of mystery, tragedy, and psychological depth. It is an epic American coming-of-age story centered on a young woman, Vera Cartwheel, and it unfolds during a long bus ride across the Middle West as she travels toward What Cheer, Iowa, the birthplace of her lost nursemaid, Miss Georgia MacIntosh.

On the bus Vera encounters a handful of fellow travelers whose stories thread through her own. The driver, Moses Hunnecker, is an unmarried man of fierce superstitions, among them a vow never to cut his hair. The old doctor, who calls himself Dr. Justice O'Leary, was once a local physician who made house calls; now, according to Moses, his mind has failed, and he believes he is still delivering babies though he has long since retired. A young couple, Madge and Homer Capehorn, are also on the bus. Madge is pregnant and bitter, letting slip that the child may not be Homer's and that she did not marry the man she wanted; the marriage is a hollow arrangement, and the two are returning to Homer's small town to live with his mother. Vera listens to their talk and the small-town gossip it carries, paying particular attention to Madge and what she reveals about pregnancy, marriage, and disappointment.

Across the journey Vera recalls the adults who raised her and were meant to teach her how to live: her mother, Catherine; the man she knew as her father, Jock Cartwheel; her nursemaid, Miss MacIntosh; and the family lawyer, Mr. Spitzer, with his twin brother, Peron. As narrator, Vera examines each of these lives in turn and, by the end, decides how she will live her own.

Her mother, Catherine, is an opium addict who has taken to her bed. She rarely leaves it and lives almost entirely inside a world of drug-induced visions, attended by imaginary guests and phantoms of the dead.

Vera's care falls to Miss MacIntosh, whom Mr. Spitzer, the manager of Catherine's estate, hired after Catherine withdrew from the world. Miss MacIntosh is the novel's emblem of practicality. She drills Vera in facts and holds her to a strict schedule, working to instill discipline and a grip on reality that stands against Catherine's illusions.

But Miss MacIntosh carries deep wounds of her own. On the night of Vera's fourteenth birthday, Vera, wandering the estate in the dark, enters Miss MacIntosh's room and discovers her secret: beneath the wig, Miss MacIntosh is bald, her body marked by a mastectomy and a hidden history. Miss MacIntosh assaults her, and by morning behaves as though nothing has happened. Vera's innocence is gone. She confronts Miss MacIntosh, who hints at the death she is preparing for herself. Soon after, Miss MacIntosh walks into the sea at dawn, her items are found strewn along the beach. Her body is never recovered. Mr. Spitzer assumes she has simply gone away. Vera is inconsolable, and Catherine can offer her only the escape of the drug.

Mr. Spitzer, whom Vera knows as the faithful family lawyer, manages the estates of both Catherine and her formidable Cousin Hannah. He might have been the last adult to guide Vera, but he is consumed by grief over the early death of his twin brother, Peron, by suicide. Much of the novel follows Mr. Spitzer's endless philosophical questioning as he tries to make sense of life, death, and love. He was caught long ago in a love triangle: he loved Catherine, who did not love him but loved his brother Peron, who loved neither of them. After Peron's death he stayed at Catherine's side for the rest of her life. On the surface he appears to be the surviving brother, Joachim, the steadfast one who mourned.

The novel unsettles even this. In the end, as Catherine is dying, Mr. Spitzer confesses that he is in fact Peron, and that it was Joachim who died, that he has lived all these years inside his brother's memory and come each evening to her bedside out of a love he never declared. Catherine dies before she can hear him; she has already gone as he begins to speak. Whether this confession resolves the brothers' identities or only deepens their confusion is left open, which is fitting in a novel where identity is never stable and the dead move as freely as the living.

When Vera is grown, she escapes Catherine's house. Because Miss MacIntosh's body was never found, Vera lets herself believe her nursemaid may still exist, and she sets out for What Cheer, Iowa, which has become in her mind a magical place that will make sense of her life. She never reaches it. Instead she ends up in southern Indiana, the heart of the Middle West, where she stops for the night and, in a tavern, meets more strangers and hears more of their stories.

The novel gathers its conclusions here: what became of Mr. Spitzer after Catherine's death, and further pieces of Miss MacIntosh's buried life. In the end Vera chooses to marry a stone deaf man, accepting that there is no perfect moment and no perfect love to be had in this life. Her arrival at that acceptance is caught in the line she comes to: "Whatever is average is the dream. Whatever is hidden is the revealed."

Throughout the book, the same motifs return: death, lost innocence, and the blurring of reality and hallucination. The narrative dwells on the psychological wounds of its characters and is saturated with images of nature, the sea, the stars, and the passage of time, all reinforcing its themes of change, loss, and the search for meaning. It is a richly layered psychological and social drama, turning on the tensions between innocence and guilt, good and evil, consciousness and unconsciousness, reality and illusion, and on how elusive all of these become under close examination.

The novel does not follow a conventional plot. It works through fragmented memory, dream, and stream of consciousness, producing a sense of timelessness and disorientation, with the characters' thoughts looping back on themselves until the line between real and unreal dissolves. It is filled with musical imagery and seems at times to borrow musical forms, fugue and nocturne and elegy and overture, letting many voices intertwine into a polyphonic whole. Certain images and phrases recur, reinforcing the cyclical nature of memory and the characters' inability to escape their pasts. Again and again the characters and settings serve as symbols for deeper questions of truth, illusion, memory, and reality, with Miss MacIntosh herself standing as the emblem of the search for truth. The novel's vast scope, its crowd of characters and settings and historical periods, gives it an encyclopedic quality that deepens the sense of boundaries coming undone.
