On Purity Culture and the Unconsummated Marriage

To All My Darlings notes that Esther’s parents “had never consummated their marriage but had only dreamed of heaven as something far away,” and connects this to what is now called purity culture: the movement emphasizing abstinence until heterosexual marriage that uses shame, ritual (purity balls, rings, pledges), and strict gender roles to regulate sexual expression. Many raised in this culture report anxiety, fear, and profound difficulty with intimacy even within marriage: the training that made sex forbidden is not easily unlearned when permission is suddenly granted. Young is not writing about purity culture as such, which became named and visible decades after the novel was published, but she is writing about its psychological effects: two people who “dreamed of heaven as something far away,” who reached toward an intimacy they had been taught to refuse, who needed a third person with a silver spoon to produce the child they wanted.
