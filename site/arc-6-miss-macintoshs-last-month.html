Arc 6: Miss MacIntosh's Last Month (Chapters 63–66)

Part of Story Arcs, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated November 10, 2026

← Mr. Spitzer's Grief · Story Arcs Home · The Tavern →

Spoiler note: This page covers Chapters 63 to 66. If you are reading along, read it after you finish Chapter 66.

Overview

The novel returns to Vera and Miss MacIntosh. The wig comes off, and Miss MacIntosh tells her life: the childhood baldness, the orphans, Lorena, and Mr. Bonebreaker, who fled. Then the last lessons through a storm, and the September morning she walks into the sea.

Central figures: Vera Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Joachim Spitzer · Mr. Titus Bonebreaker · Catherine Cartwheel · Moses Hunnecker

Chapter by chapter

Chapter 63

At breakfast the morning after her dream, Vera tries to see Miss MacIntosh as she always has: the Rock of Ages, the plain, red-haired nursemaid of common sense who scolds the tide, hates the Emperor Constantine for changing the calendar, and rails against Benjamin Franklin and the electric lights that have robbed the poor of the dark. But Vera has seen her bald in the night. She strikes the table and demands the truth: Miss MacIntosh wears a wig and has neither eyebrows nor eyelashes. Miss MacIntosh answers in grief and wild humor, by way of Canute and Constantine, Jonas Hanway and the umbrella that made every man his own coachman, and the brothers who called her George, and at last she lifts off her red wig. She is bald, her left breast is gone, and she was broke when she first came, which is why they saved the electric lights. Vera promises always to love her. Miss MacIntosh tells her to eat her breakfast, that people are never quite what they seem, that nobody knows anybody, and that she suffers especially when there are rainbows.

Chapter 64

Solomon the turtle is gone; Miss MacIntosh has put him out of doors, and for the first time she calls Vera “darling.” She tells Vera she loved only once, a dark dancing girl named Lorena who laughed at her in the orphanage, became a fan dancer and stripper, was paroled to her care, and ran away again. For days afterward she keeps taking her wig off and putting it back. She tells her history: born bald in What Cheer, Iowa, the daughter of a poor shoemaker and coffin-maker; a doll’s toupee at three and a wig of hair stolen from the plow horses; boys fishing for her wig from the orchard boughs; a traveling phrenologist who gave her bald head the prize; a baptism in an icy river where the whole congregation saw her bare head; years in orphanages and a school for the blind; a forger at Alcatraz who sent her Christmas cards; a Chicago barber who could not grow one hair for a million dollars. Vera, who already knows that Miss MacIntosh will be committed to the deep, loves her more now than before. Their last month together has begun.

Chapter 65

As August turns to September, Miss MacIntosh, bald and half dressed, plays solitaire and mends a pair of black gloves; Vera reveals that these are the gloves she wore when she walked into the ocean one September morning, leaving her wig, her corset, and her umbrella on the beach. The umbrella was Mr. Bonebreaker’s, and Miss MacIntosh tells the story. Weak after losing her left breast and setting ninepins in a Chicago bowling alley, she was found reeling in the street by Titus Bonebreaker, a vegetarian Gospel salesman from the Ozarks who preached the end of the world through a trumpet and believed his grandmother’s story that he was the true King of Sweden. He called her the little sister who had no breasts, praised the hair she did not have, gave her his watch, and kept postponing their wedding. On the night before it, she stood before him naked and bald under an electric bulb. He fled, leaving his umbrella, and later married a foreign girl to give her child a name. Miss MacIntosh worked among the blind, drifted east, spent her savings in Buffalo on white clothes and an ashen wig, and stood alone among the honeymooners at Niagara Falls.

Chapter 66

Through a winter storm, Miss MacIntosh, dressed like a whaler, fires questions at Vera about the life of the eel, the calendar, the North Pole, and the states adjoining Kansas. In her last month she tells Vera her secrets: that her ovaries were removed when she was young; that on her vacations in New York she made an imaginary fortune at the Stock Exchange, for which she was sure Vera’s mother had her followed by a Pinkerton; and that there she met a wooden-legged Danish explorer who begged her to give him her money and come with him to the North Pole, then vanished without leaving his name. Vera wonders whether everyone, from Lorena to her own mother, always knew that Miss MacIntosh was bald. The chapter closes on her walk into the sea at dawn, her clothes and wig strewn on the beach, and Vera’s elegy for her, while old Friday barks by the waves.
