Chapter 41

On Chapter 41 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 40 · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 42 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer

Pillars: Not One Self · Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act · The Past Doesn't Go Away

The Questions

Catherine “did not know how many selves there were which came and went.” What kind of self cannot count itself?

“The one which did not return would be the immortal one. The immortal one would be the lost.” Why is the deathless self the missing one?

She holds there is “no difference between life and death…a difference which could never be resolved.” Is that wisdom, or the sealed dream refusing the last distinction?

She is lost inside her own ear, crying “Let me out of my ear!” What has her invention become when she cannot get out of it?

Not One Self here is not a freedom but a vertigo. Catherine “did not know how many selves there were which came and went,” her spirit moving “outside of her…going by many ways upon many errands,” a crowd too large and too scattered for its own member to number. This is the plurality the book has affirmed all along, but felt from inside a woman who cannot hold it, and the chapter gives it a devastating turn: “the one which did not return would be the immortal one. The immortal one would be the lost.” The self that never comes back is the deathless one, which means the deathless one is exactly the one who is missing. Plurality and grief meet in a single figure: to be many is to have one of the many always gone.

Her refusal of the last distinction is the sealed dream at its widest. There is, she insists, “no difference between life and death…a difference which could never be resolved,” and it would be easy to hear the book’s own openness in the line. But Catherine does not hold the distinction open the way Mr. Spitzer holds the butterfly and the moth open; she dissolves it, so that nothing can be lost because nothing was ever alive as against dead. This is the seal disguised as acceptance. Where the Witness stays with the unresolved, Catherine abolishes the terms, and abolishing loss is not the same as bearing it.

And the chapter shows her trapped inside her own construction. She is lost for years “inside her ear,” that “great continent no one had ever completely known,” walking its stairways and colonnades and crying “Let me out of my ear! … Where is the door?” The invention that was a paradise in the early chapters has become a labyrinth she cannot escape. This is what the sealed dream turns into once it is truly sealed: not a refuge but a maze with the door mislaid, the builder shut inside the building with no way out. The seal that kept loss out keeps her in.

The line the chapter turns on is quiet: “She travelled, but she never moved.” All the journeys, the night trains through impossible Alps and Gobi deserts, the carriage through the sleeping house, are motion that goes nowhere, a sealed world elaborating itself without ever opening. The Past Doesn't Go Away runs through it, the house moving in the night, the dead loves arriving, the furniture rearranging, but none of it is change; it is the same sealed room furnished over and over. Catherine is the book’s picture of what endless invention comes to when it is never let open: travel without movement, a crowd that cannot count itself, and one self always, immortally, lost.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Catherine’s dissolving of life and death for the book’s own openness, and the reading that takes her endless dream-travel for freedom. Neither the wise acceptance nor the liberated wandering is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that her plural self is a vertigo she cannot hold, and the immortal self among the many is precisely the lost one, so that plurality and grief are the same fact in her. Her refusal of the difference between life and death abolishes the terms rather than holding them open, which is the seal disguised as acceptance. And her invention has become a labyrinth she cannot escape, travel without movement, the builder shut inside the building. Dissolving loss is not bearing it, and a sealed dream is a maze, not a refuge. Catherine goes on travelling without moving, crying to be let out of her own ear, and the chapter leaves her there.

For Reflection

When the many selves cannot be counted, is that freedom or vertigo, and what decides which?

Is abolishing the difference between what is lost and what is kept the same as learning to bear loss?

What construction of one’s own, built as a refuge, has become a maze with the door mislaid?
