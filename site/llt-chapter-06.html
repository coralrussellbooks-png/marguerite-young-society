Chapter 6

On Chapter 6 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 5 · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 7 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Madge Capehorn

Pillars: Not One Self · The Past Doesn't Go Away · The Sacred Is Here

The Questions

Madge feels herself “breaking into two.” Is that a breakdown, or the plural self arriving in someone who wanted to stay one?

Why does Madge meet her own pregnant, changing body with artifice and dread rather than with the ordinary?

The bus doubles back and Madge feels she has lived this before. What has happened to time?

What built things, “wife,” “mother,” “woman,” is Madge mistaking for fate?

Madge experiences Not One Self as terror. She is “breaking into two, herself and the other, this face unknown,” and the breaking frightens her because she wants to be one sealed thing. The pregnancy makes the plurality literal, two lives in one body, but Madge cannot receive it as the ordinary condition; she feels it as a self coming apart. Where the book elsewhere holds that the individual is the one illusion, Madge is the person that truth happens to against her will, and her dread measures how badly she wanted the sealed single self the book calls illusion.

This is why she refuses The Sacred Is Here of her own body. Her changing, growing, laboring body is the plainest ordinary fact there is, and she meets it with artifice and dread, wanting the smooth sealed surface rather than the open changing one. Her pregnancy she has called “this dark valley from which she might never return.” The ordinary miracle within her is exactly what she cannot hold.

Time layers around her. The bus doubles back, “phantom shapes which came and went,” and Madge feels she has lived this before, the past pressing into the present until sequence dissolves. The decay everywhere, broken gates, derelict windmills, is The Past Doesn't Go Away made scenery: nothing is behind her, everything accumulates in a present already going to ruin.

And she is caught inside built things she takes for fate. “Wife,” “mother,” the apparatus of what a woman is supposed to be, are agreements held in place by repetition, and Madge lives them as an iron circumstance she is a victim of. She cannot see the agreement as an agreement, and so she cannot revise it; the invention has sealed around her from the outside, which is its own kind of locked door.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that treats Madge’s fragmentation simply as trauma to be healed back into wholeness, as if the sealed single self were the health she should return to. And it refuses the reading that takes her dread of the body as mere private neurosis rather than a refusal of The Sacred Is Here the book keeps testing.

The footing is that the plural self is the ordinary condition, and Madge’s terror is what it looks like to meet that condition while still wanting to be one. The changing body is The Sacred Is Here at its most undeniable, and trading it for artifice is refusing the ground itself. The roles she takes for fate are built, and naming them as constructions is the first crack of daylight, even if Madge never finds it. She does not arrive at any of this; she rides on, breaking, and the chapter honors the breaking without curing it.

For Reflection

When the single self one wanted to be comes apart, is that loss or arrival?

What ordinary fact of one’s own body or life is met with dread instead of tending?

Which roles that feel like fate are actually agreements that could, in principle, be revised?
