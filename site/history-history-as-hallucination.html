Definition from research

Vera's mother, Catherine Cartwheel, lives in opium dreams where history comes calling. Among her guests in Chapter 11 is an Egyptian pharaoh who is "Rameses I, II, III," each wrapping a different and older king. He learns he had been "partitioned into many selves without his knowing it, without his consent," so that "as Rameses III he had dreamed the dreams of Rameses I who was already dead."

This is the clearest statement of "Not One Self" in the novel, and it arrives disguised as a mummy. A historical name, which the list files as one person, turns out to be a stack of selves dreaming each other. The same pattern runs through the whole cast. Mr. Spitzer cannot be sure whether he is himself or his dead twin Peron. Cousin Hannah is described in Chapter 30 as "being both herself and a gentleman." Young uses history's dynasties and numbered kings because they already admit what her characters discover: one name has always held many.

The mother also greets Czar Kolokol, king of "the iron bells which, because of an engineering miscalculation, had never tolled" (Chapter 14). The list crowned him a real king. Young chose a monument famous for its silence. The opium world is full of grand history that never quite sounds.
